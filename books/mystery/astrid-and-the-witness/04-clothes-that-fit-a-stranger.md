## 4. Clothes That Fit a Stranger

The call came at 3:47 in the afternoon, which was wrong on its face. Lior Nakamura had learned long ago that the city kept business hours for its cruelties the way it kept them for everything else — bad news preferred daylight, preferred to catch you with your guard down and a mug of coffee halfway to your mouth. Night shift people got their horrors delivered neat, in the dark, where they could be filed away by morning. But this call came in the flat gray light of mid-afternoon, while Lior was sitting at their kitchen table trying to remember how to eat breakfast at an hour that felt like someone else's life.

"Mr. Nakamura? This is Detective Reyes again. Homicide." A pause, the sound of a door closing somewhere on her end. "Are you alone?"

"I'm alone."

"Good. I need you to come down to the precinct. Not for questioning — I want to show you something first. Before anyone else does." Another pause, longer this time. "And Lior — don't talk to anyone on the way. Anyone."

* * *

The Fourth Precinct occupied a block of poured concrete that had been ugly in three different architectural decades, and it smelled the way all police stations smell: burnt coffee, toner, and the faint metallic ghost of fear that no amount of cleaning ever fully lifted. Reyes met Lior in the lobby instead of making them sign in, which told Lior something before she said a word. She was out of uniform even though she never wore one — plain gray blazer, hair pulled back hard, eyes doing that thing they did when she was deciding how much truth a civilian could carry.

"You look terrible," she said.

"It's been four days since I found out a dead man was wearing my clothes. I've been sleeping great."

Something flickered across her face — not quite sympathy, more like the professional acknowledgment of a joke made in self-defense. She walked them down a corridor lined with corkboards and faded community-outreach posters, past a room where a man was weeping into a payphone that hadn't worked since 2011, and stopped at a door marked FORENSICS LIAISON.

"I'm going to show you photographs," Reyes said. "I need you to look at them properly, not the way civilians look at crime scene photos — fast, from the corner of the eye, hoping it'll go away. Can you do that?"

Lior thought about the dispatch board. About the nights spent holding a whole city's worth of moving metal in their head, tracking every cab like a chess piece, never flinching when a driver called in an accident or worse. "I close fares for a living," they said. "I can look."

Reyes opened the door.

The room beyond was small and windowless, dominated by a light table and a laptop connected to a projector that threw its image onto a blank stretch of wall. A forensic technician — young, freckled, with the careful neutrality of someone who'd learned early not to react to what crossed her screen — nodded once and clicked her mouse.

The ravine filled the wall.

Lior had seen the location before, of course, in the news footage that had played on loop for two days: the steep cut of drainage land behind the old rail spur, the blackberry bramble, the shallow gully where water ran only in spring. What the news cameras hadn't shown, what the anchors' voices had smoothed over, was the body itself — and now here it was, rendered six feet tall in clinical detail, photographed from every angle with the patient thoroughness of people who understood that the dead could only testify once.

He had been lying on his back, half-turned toward the slope, one arm flung above his head as if reaching for something just out of frame. The jacket was unmistakable. Lior knew it the way you know your own handwriting — the charcoal wool, the small burn scar near the left cuff from a radiator grate outside the Blue Door Diner, the way the collar sat slightly crooked because of a seam Lior had never gotten around to having fixed. The scarf too: deep green, fraying at one end, a gift from a person whose name Lior still couldn't say out loud without their chest tightening.

"That's mine," Lior heard themselves say. Their voice sounded very far away. "Both pieces. That's — those are mine."

"We know," Reyes said quietly. "That's why you're here."

The technician advanced the slides. Close-ups now. The jacket's lining, photographed inside-out. The scarf's weave under magnification. Then something new — a series of images Lior hadn't seen in any news report. The clothing laid out on a steel table in a lab, tagged, measured, lit from above like museum pieces.

"The victim is a male, approximately forty-one years of age," the technician recited, in the toneless voice of someone reading coordinates off a map. "One hundred seventy-eight centimeters, seventy-four kilograms. Your building records indicate you're—" she glanced at a printout, "—one hundred seventy-seven centimeters, seventy kilos. Close enough that in low light, face-down or face-away, in that jacket and scarf, a witness would make the identification without hesitation."

"In low light," Lior repeated. "Face-down or face-away."

"He was found at night," Reyes said. "By a jogger with a flashlight. From ten feet up the embankment, all she saw was dark wool and a green scarf. Her first statement to the responding officer was, and I'm quoting the transcript, 'It's the dispatcher. It's the dispatcher from the radio.'" Reyes let that sit in the air between them. "She listens to the scanner. She knows your voice. And for about ninety seconds, until the officer got close enough to see the face, everyone at that scene believed they were looking at you."

* * *

They put Lior in an interview room after that, but not the kind with the mirror and the bolted table — a smaller one, almost comfortable, with a window onto the parking lot and a vending machine humming in the corner. Reyes brought coffee neither of them drank. For a long moment she just sat, turning her paper cup in slow circles, and Lior watched her decide something.

"Off the record," she finally said. "And I mean that. If you repeat this to a reporter, to your lawyer, to your own reflection, I will deny it so thoroughly you'll think you dreamed it. Do you understand me?"

"I understand."

"The break-in at your building. Fourteen days ago. Your unit, third floor, no forced entry on the main door — someone came in behind a neighbor who doesn't pull the lobby door shut. Your apartment lock showed picking marks. Nothing taken except the jacket, the scarf, and a pair of gloves. No electronics disturbed. Cash left in the drawer. Jewelry — such as it is — untouched." She looked up. "You know what that pattern means."

Lior's mouth had gone dry. "Someone wasn't robbing me."

"Someone was shopping. Specifically. Selectively. They stood in your bedroom and chose the exact items that would make a body read as *you* from a distance, in the dark, to a frightened witness." Reyes set the cup down. "The forensics on the lock came back yesterday. The picking marks are consistent with a tension wrench and a standard hook — professional work, quick, no scratching around. Whoever did it has done it many times."

"And you're telling me this off the record because—"

"Because the official theory is still opportunistic burglary followed by coincidental homicide, and the official theory is what the file says, and the file is what survives." Her jaw tightened. "But I've been doing this nineteen years, Nakamura, and I am telling you, as one human being to another, that there is no coincidence here. Someone dressed a corpse in your clothes. That is not random. That is *authored*."

The word landed in Lior's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They heard it fall for a long time before it hit bottom.

"Why?" The question came out smaller than Lior intended. "I dispatch cabs. I write things in a logbook. I don't — I'm nobody. People don't get murdered in my place. People don't get *dressed* in my place."

"People don't usually get murdered in anyone's place. That's what makes it murder instead of accident." Reyes leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for the first time since the ravine, Lior saw something underneath the detective's armor — not warmth exactly, but a kind of grim solidarity, the look of someone who had carried cases like this before and knew what the weight did to a person. "Which brings us to the question I need you to answer honestly, because I think your life may depend on it. Think carefully. In the last month — two months — has anything come through your dispatch line that didn't fit? A fare that felt wrong. An address that shouldn't exist. A voice you recognized. Anything."

Lior opened their mouth to say no.

And then the memory surfaced, unbidden, the way water finds a crack: three calls in one week, all logged from the same landline in the Marsh District, all from the sagging Victorian on Corvair Street that Lior had driven past a hundred times and never once stopped at. All placed by a woman who never spoke. All requesting pickups for a passenger named — Lior had checked the log twice, three times, at four in the morning with the police report spread across their kitchen table — a passenger named Daniel Merrick.

The dead man.

"I have to check my logbook," Lior said.

Reyes studied them for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Lior's face made her nod slowly, like a doctor confirming a symptom she'd already suspected. "There it is," she murmured. "There's the thread."

"What thread? Detective, what aren't you telling me?"

"I'm telling you nothing, officially." She stood, collected both cold coffees, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "Unofficially, I'm telling you this: whoever did this wanted you dead or wanted you blamed, and either way, they know your routines, your building, your wardrobe. Which means they've been paying attention for a while. So start paying attention back. Check that logbook. And Nakamura—" she looked over her shoulder, and the fluorescent light caught the fatigue carved into her face, "—the victim's phone records came back this morning. Three calls placed from a residence on Corvair Street in the week before he died. The account holder is a woman named Astrid Voss. Does that name mean anything to you?"

The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a cab tilts when it takes a corner faster than it should.

"She's in my logbook," Lior said. "She's been in my logbook for years. I've driven past her house a hundred times. I've never met her. She doesn't speak. She just... calls."

Reyes held their gaze for three full seconds, and in that silence Lior heard the whole shape of it — the frame, the clothes, the ravine, the silent woman at the center of the web like a spider nobody had bothered to notice because spiders that don't speak don't scare anyone.

"Then maybe," Reyes said quietly, "it's time somebody finally answered her call."

* * *

Lior walked home the long way, through streets going amber with early evening, and the city had changed around them. That was the thing nobody warned you about — not that danger arrives like weather, sudden and visible, but that it retroactively rewrites everything you already lived through. Every unremarkable night shift became evidence. Every quiet street became a route someone had studied. The gray sedan parked outside the corner market — had it been there last week too? The man reading a newspaper at the bus stop with no bus due for twenty minutes — was he reading, or watching?

Lior climbed the stairs to their building and stood in the hallway outside their own door for a long moment before touching the handle. The lock looked ordinary. It had always looked ordinary. Now it looked like a promise someone else had already kept.

Inside, the apartment was exactly as they'd left it, and that was somehow worse. Someone had stood here. Had moved through these rooms with a flashlight and a purpose, opening drawers, weighing options, choosing. Lior walked to the closet and stared at the gap where the jacket used to hang — a slim absence among the other coats, a negative space shaped like a stranger's plan. The remaining hangers swayed faintly in the heat-vent draft, ticking softly against the rod like a clock counting down to something.

Fear sat in Lior's stomach, cold and specific. But beneath it, rising slowly through the cold like heat through floorboards, was something else — something that felt, recognizably, like the feeling they got at 2 a.m. when a case wouldn't close, when a fare didn't add up, when the numbers refused to balance and the only honest response was to keep pulling until the knot gave.

They weren't collateral. They had never been collateral. Somewhere in the machinery of this city, someone had opened a ledger — literal or otherwise — and written Lior Nakamura's name in it, in the column reserved for problems to be disposed of.

Fine. Lior pulled the dispatch logbook from the shelf, set it on the kitchen table under the lamp, and flipped to the week of the first call from Corvair Street.

Two could keep a ledger.

The pages fell open, and Astrid Voss's address waited there in Lior's own handwriting, patient as a buried thing.
