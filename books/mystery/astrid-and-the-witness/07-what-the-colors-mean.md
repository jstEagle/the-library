## 7. What the Colors Mean

The kitchen table had become a war room by the third night.

Lior sat in the yellow pool of a desk lamp they'd carried over from the apartment, the sagging Victorian's kitchen chair creaking under them, and spread Emil Voss's route cards across the wood in long, patient rows. There were hundreds of them — maybe nine hundred, maybe more — each one a rectangle of stiff cardstock gone soft at the edges from decades of handling. Each one bore a fare amount, a date, a pickup and drop-off written in Emil's small, upright hand. And each one was edged in color: a strip of paint or ink along the bottom border, blue or red or black or green.

Astrid sat across from them, hands folded around a mug of tea she never drank, watching Lior work with an attention that felt almost physical, like a hand resting on their shoulder.

"Okay," Lior said, mostly to themselves. "Let's say you're not just sentimental."

They'd started with the dates. That was the dispatcher's instinct — every shift sheet, every logbook, every night of Lior's working life was organized around time, and if Emil had been a driver for forty years, his cards should read like any driver's history: scattered fares, random addresses, the ordinary static of a city moving through its nights.

But they didn't.

Lior pulled out a legal pad and began writing dates down in columns, one column per color. The blue cards ran from 1979 to 1984 and then stopped entirely. The red ones clustered in the late eighties, thinned out through the nineties, vanished after 2003. The black cards were sparse, irregular, sometimes two years between them. But the green —

The green cards never stopped. The green cards ran unbroken from 1979 to 2011, the year of Emil's death, and there were more of them than all the other colors combined.

And the green cards weren't scattered across the city. Lior flipped through them one by one, laying them out, and the pattern emerged so cleanly it made the hair on their arms rise. The pickups changed — a diner on Corliss Avenue, a parking structure on Fifth, the marina office, a private residence on Halden Hill with no street number given, just *H.H.* — but the drop-offs repeated. Over and over, decade after decade, the same three destinations: the Kessler Building. A law office suite inside it, 1400. And once, memorably, a handwritten note where the address should have been: *the younger one's car.*

"Kessler," Lior said aloud.

Astrid's eyes came up.

"The name keeps showing up." Lior turned a green card around and slid it across the table to her. Dated March 1988. Pickup: the marina. Drop-off: Kessler Building, Suite 1400. Fare: $18.50. Tip: 40 percent. "Forty years of trips to the same building. Nobody takes a cab to the same office four hundred times unless they work there — and these aren't commuters. Look at the hours." Lior fanned out more cards. "Eleven p.m. Two a.m. Four-fifteen in the morning. These are the dead runs, Astrid. The fares nobody else wants."

Astrid picked up the card. She held it the way she held everything — carefully, with both hands, as though objects might startle — and her thumb moved slowly along the green border. Then she looked up at Lior, and something in her face shifted. It wasn't fear. Lior had seen fear on her the first night, when the police cruiser idled too long outside her house. This was older than fear. This was recognition, the look of someone who has been waiting thirty years for another person to see what she saw.

She set the card down. She reached beneath the tea tin at the center of the table — Lior had assumed it held tea; everything in this house was a disguise — and drew out a second stack of cards, smaller, worn nearly translucent with age, each one marked with a single word in Emil's hand.

*Kept.*

Lior's mouth went dry. "These are different."

Astrid nodded once, slow and deliberate.

There were perhaps sixty of them. Lior sorted them by date with fingers that had begun to tremble slightly, and the earliest was dated June 1979 — the same month as the first green card. The handwriting on this one was different. Younger. Less controlled. The fare amount was smudged, rewritten twice, as if Emil had been driving while he wrote it, or as if his hand hadn't wanted to record what he'd seen.

*Pickup: Corliss Ave. dock gate. Drop-off: none. Passenger declined destination. Paid triple to be driven nowhere for one hour. Cargo transferred trunk to trunk at pier 9. Witnessed by E.V.*

"Witnessed by E.V.," Lior read aloud, very quietly. "He was keeping receipts."

Astrid's hand closed over theirs. Her grip was surprisingly strong, the grip of a woman who had spent a lifetime lifting things nobody thanked her for, and she held it there until Lior looked up. Then she released them, took the pen from the table, and wrote on the back of a blank route card in her careful, schoolroom cursive:

*He drove nights because days belonged to other people. He saw what nights carry.*

"He saw too much," Lior translated.

Astrid's pen scratched again. *He saw exactly enough. He counted.*

Lior looked down at the sixty *kept* cards, then at the four hundred green ones, and the shape of it rose out of the table like a city rising out of fog. Emil Voss had spent three decades doing what Lior did every night — closing fares, solving puzzles, bringing order to chaos — except Emil's puzzle had teeth. He'd been paid to forget, trip after trip, year after year, and instead of forgetting he had written it all down in the only language no one would ever think to search: taxi paperwork. Fares that weren't fares. Tips that weren't tips. A ledger disguised as a dead man's hobby, filed in a shoebox in a widow's closet, sitting ten feet from a window that someone had shot out last week.

"They're payments," Lior said. The words came out flat with certainty. "The green cards. The tip percentages — look." They grabbed the legal pad, flipping pages. "Standard tip is fifteen, twenty percent. These run thirty, forty, fifty, sometimes a hundred percent. And the amounts — Emil rounded everything to the half dollar. Every single green card. No driver rounds every fare for forty years." Lior's pen moved fast now. "If the tip percentage encodes something — a multiplier, a count — and the fare is the base —"

Astrid tapped the table twice. When Lior looked up, she pointed at herself, then at the cards, then made a motion like turning pages.

"You know how it works."

Another tap. Yes.

*Emil showed me once,* she wrote. *Sundays. He sorted the colors and I made coffee and he told me stories about the passengers who never existed.* Her pen paused. *He said: if anything happens to me, the greens are the spine. Read the spine and you'll find the body.*

Lior sat back. Outside, the streetlight buzzed and flickered the way it always did, and somewhere down the block a dog barked twice and gave up. The house ticked and settled around them, old wood remembering the day's heat. On the wall above the sink hung a photograph Lior had noticed before without seeing: a young man in a driver's cap, arm slung around a laughing woman, both of them squinting into a sun that had set decades ago.

Emil Voss had known he was going to die. That was the thing Lior understood now, sitting in his kitchen, surrounded by his life's secret work. You don't write *witnessed by E.V.* on a fare card unless you understand that someday the witnessing will matter. You don't tell your wife *the greens are the spine* unless you've already imagined the version of events where you're not there to explain them.

"He protected you by leaving it where no one would look," Lior said slowly. "In plain sight. In grief. Who robs a widow's shoebox?"

Astrid's expression darkened. She wrote: *Someone did look. Last week. They took nothing. Now we know why.*

Because they couldn't read it. Because only two people alive understood the language — and one of them was mute, and the other was a night dispatcher whose clothes had recently been stolen off a corpse.

Lior gathered the green cards into a single stack, squared the edges, and laid them out in date order across the entire length of the table, a green river running through the kitchen. Sixty *kept* cards went alongside, white flags planted along the same timeline. And there — Lior's breath caught — the correspondence was exact. Every *kept* card fell within a week of a green card. Every payment followed a witnessed event. Emil hadn't just been keeping receipts. He'd been keeping *pairs*: the sin, and the price of silence, matched side by side for thirty-two years.

"We can build the whole chain," Lior whispered. "Names, dates, amounts. If the tip code breaks the way I think it does, we can reconstruct every payoff the Kesslers ever made — and prove it came from them, because Emil logged the pickup points. The marina. The warehouse. Halden Hill." They looked up. "That's the shipping company. That's the family fortune."

Astrid was already writing. Her pen moved with a steadiness Lior hadn't seen in her before, and when she turned the card around, the cursive was almost fierce:

*Adrian Kessler's father rode in this cab. So did his father's lawyer, his cousin, his foreman at the warehouse. Emil called them the green people. He said money leaves a color when it's dirty.*

Lior laughed — a short, disbelieving sound that had no humor in it. "Money leaves a color when it's dirty." They looked down the green river of cards stretching across the table, forty years of quiet corruption rendered in cardstock and half dollars, and felt something settle in their chest that had been loose and rattling since the night the police called about the body in the ravine. Fear was still there. It would always be there now. But underneath it, something harder had formed, something with edges.

Purpose.

Not curiosity. Not the pleasant click of a puzzle closing. This was the other thing, the rarer thing — the knowledge that the truth was *load-bearing*, that pulling it free would either hold the whole rotten structure up to the light or bring it down on top of them, and that either way there was no longer any version of the story where Lior Nakamura went back to dispatching anonymous fares and sleeping through mornings.

"I'm going to need your husband's garage records," Lior said. "Shift sheets, maintenance logs, anything with card numbers or dates cross-referenced. If the tips encode amounts, I need a baseline to decode against." They paused. "And I'm going to need to borrow your Sundays."

Astrid almost smiled. It was a small movement, quickly gone, but Lior would remember it — the first crack of light through thirty years of silence. She wrote one line and pushed it across the table:

*Sundays were ours. They can be yours too.*

Outside, far down the street, an engine turned over — soft, unhurried, deliberate. Lior glanced at the window out of habit, the dispatcher's reflex, cataloguing without thinking: sedan, gray, parked with its nose toward the house, headlights still dark.

It hadn't been there when they arrived.

Lior kept their voice level. "Finish your tea," they said quietly, gathering the green cards into their case with steady hands. "I think tonight we work at my place."

Astrid looked at the window, then at Lior, and nodded — and in her nod was everything Emil must have seen on his last ride home: the understanding that once you read the spine, the body knows you're reading.

* * *

They worked until nearly dawn at Lior's apartment, the cards spread across the floor because the table was too small, and by the time the sky over the ravine side of the city went pale, Lior had confirmed what the kitchen had promised: the green cards clustered around the Kessler shipping fortune like iron filings around a magnet. Payments rising through the eighties as the company grew. Payments spiking in years Lior could match, with a little library research, to harbor contract awards, to a fire at a competitor's warehouse, to a wrongful-death settlement that had never made the papers.

And at the top of the stack, dated eleven months ago — before the murder, before the frame, before any of it — a green card Lior had almost missed, tucked inside the *kept* pile where it didn't belong.

Fare: $200. Tip: 100 percent. Pickup: Kessler warehouse, loading dock C. Drop-off: none recorded. One line of Emil's handwriting, tighter than usual, pressed hard enough to emboss the card:

*Same passenger as '88. He asked if I remembered him. I said everyone remembers their drivers. He laughed. I did not.*

Lior read it three times. Then they looked at the date again, and the cold that moved through them had nothing to do with the morning air coming through the window.

Emil had died in 2011.

This card was written in someone else's hand — imitated, carefully, but wrong, the loops too even, the pressure of a forger who had studied the original without understanding it. Someone had planted a fake card in Emil's own archive, sometime in the past year, which meant someone knew the archive existed, knew where it lived, and had been close enough to touch it.

Which meant the shoebox hadn't been safe for a long time.

And whoever forged that card hadn't put it there to hide the ledger.

They'd put it there to test whether anyone was reading.
