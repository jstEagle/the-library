## 17. Ravine Road

The call came in at 2:47 a.m., and it was wrong in three ways before Lior had even finished writing it down.

First, the voice. Dispatchers learn voices the way sommeliers learn vintages — by the hundred, by the year — and this one was pitched a half-tone too smooth, too rehearsed, like a man reading his own alibi off an index card. Second, the address. 1400 block of Ravine Road. Nobody called for a cab to Ravine Road at three in the morning; there were no houses left on that stretch since the county bought out the lots after the landslide scare of '09, nothing but guardrail and gravel and the steep green drop into the ravine where a man in Lior's jacket had been found dead eleven weeks ago. Third — and this was the one that made Lior's hand go still on the pen — the caller had used the old booking code. The legacy fare code, the one Marisol's father had written into the dispatch software twenty years ago and never bothered to retire. A code no customer would ever know.

A code only someone inside the company could know.

Lior sat in the dispatch office with the phone cradled against their shoulder and the logbook open, and let the silence stretch just long enough to hear the caller breathe.

"Confirming," Lior said, keeping their voice flat as asphalt. "One passenger, Ravine Road access point, north side. Twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes is good," the voice said, and hung up.

Lior set the receiver down and looked at it for a long moment, the way you'd look at a snake that had somehow gotten into your kitchen and then left again without biting anyone. Then they pulled up the shift roster, because if someone inside the company had leaked the booking code, Lior wanted to know who had access tonight. The answer was: everyone, technically. The code lived in the system's guts. But only four people had been around long enough to remember it existed — Marisol, who'd taught Lior everything they knew about surviving night shifts; old Petey, whose hands shook too badly to drive anymore but who still covered the Sunday desk; Reyes, who wasn't company at all; and whoever had killed a man and dressed him in Lior's clothes.

Lior wrote the call into the logbook in their small, square handwriting — *2:47, Ravine Rd, no-show risk high* — and then did something they'd never done in six years of dispatching.

They picked up a set of car keys instead of handing them to someone else.

The cab smelled like every cab Marisol ran: pine air freshener gone brittle with age, coffee, the ghost of a thousand strangers' cigarettes. Lior sat behind the wheel in the empty lot and let the engine idle while they thought it through, because going out to Ravine Road alone at three in the morning, eleven weeks after a body had turned up there wearing their jacket, was either the stupidest thing they'd ever done or the only smart thing left to do. The ledger copies were already out — Reyes had his set, the *Herald*'s crime desk had theirs, defense counsel had theirs. Whatever happened to Lior now, the truth was loose in the world. That was the whole point of chapter eighteen of Lior's life, if you wanted to narrate it that way: give up control so the story can't be strangled.

But Kessler's people didn't know exactly what Lior had copied. And a dispatcher who could read a fake call in three seconds flat was also, apparently, a dispatcher who could be baited with one.

They went anyway. Not because they were brave — Lior had never once felt brave, only stubborn — but because Astrid Voss was sleeping in a motel room booked under the name *M. Delgado*, and every hour Lior spent being predictable was an hour someone spent deciding she was expendable. If the trap was for Lior, better Lior walk into it with eyes open than let it drift toward the house on Alder Street, toward the sagging porch, toward the woman who had pressed a dead man's route card into their hand like a key.

Besides. Lior knew something about Ravine Road that the caller didn't know they knew.

It had no cell coverage.

* * *

The access point was exactly where the maps said it would be: a gravel pull-off wide enough for two cars, a dented guardrail running along the lip of the drop, and beyond it the ravine itself, black and breathing, full of the sound of water moving somewhere far below. The county had planted saplings along the slope after the landslide — thin birches, staked and strapped, each one wearing its own little plastic tag like a hospital bracelet. In the headlights they looked like a row of patients waiting to be discharged.

No car. No person. Of course not.

Lior parked nose-out — first rule of night driving, always park so you can leave — and killed the headlights but kept the engine running. They checked the mirrors. Empty road in both directions, silvered faintly under a sky the color of dirty wool. They picked up their phone. One bar, flickering, dying. They watched it drop to zero and stay there, and felt the particular loneliness of a device becoming a brick.

So this was how it would happen. Not a courtroom, not a headline, not a dramatic confrontation across a table with a lawyer present. Just a gravel lot and a man walking out of the dark with a tire iron or worse, and later a report that said the dispatcher had gone out to Ravine Road alone, at night, emotionally unstable, clearly grieving, clearly confused — and hadn't it been strange, the way they kept showing up at crime scenes? The narrative was already written. Lior was supposed to supply only the body.

They put the cab in gear.

And that was when the second car came over the rise.

It came fast, no lights until the last second, and swung into the pull-off with the practiced economy of a vehicle that had done this maneuver before — blocking the exit, nose angled at the guardrail, boxing the taxi against the drop. High beams hit the windshield and turned the world white. Lior's hands did what six years of night shifts had trained them to do: stayed on the wheel, stayed calm, started counting. Two doors opened. Two sets of boots on gravel. One heavy, dragging slightly on the left — a bad knee or a bad conscience. One light and quick.

"Dispatcher!" the quick one called out, cheerful, terrible. "You came yourself. Marisol must be short-staffed."

Lior didn't answer. Answering was time. Time was distance. Through the white glare Lior could make out shapes now — big man by the driver's door of the sedan, smaller one circling toward the taxi's passenger side, and the geometry of it was simple and final: they wanted the door open, they wanted Lior out of the cab, because whatever happened next needed to look like an accident at the bottom of a ravine, and accidents require bodies outside of vehicles.

The passenger handle rattled. Locked. Good. Lior had locked it out of habit, the way they locked everything, the way they'd locked down their whole careful ordered life until a murdered stranger in their own jacket had come along and picked every lock they owned.

"Come on now," the cheerful voice said, closer, right at the glass. "We just want to talk about the route cards."

There it was. Confirmation, delivered free of charge: Dale Voss knew about the cards, knew they'd been decoded, knew the copies were moving — and had decided the cheapest remaining fix was one more body in one more ravine. Lior's heart was going hard enough to feel in their teeth, but underneath the fear, absurdly, the dispatcher's brain was doing what it always did: triaging. Passenger side blocked. Driver's side occupied. Rear doors — the old Crown Victoria cabs Marisol bought used from the city fleet had rear doors, and the man by the sedan was between Lior and the front of the car, which meant the rear driver's-side door was the longest throw, the worst angle, the option nobody guards because nobody uses it.

Nobody except a dispatcher who had spent years listening to drivers complain about which doors stuck.

Lior killed the engine. In the sudden silence the ravine got loud — water, wind, the two men's breathing. Then Lior threw the transmission into reverse, stood on the gas, and wrenched the wheel hard left, and the taxi screamed backward in a half-arc of gravel and burning rubber, rear quarter panel kissing the guardrail with a shriek of metal, missing the big man by a yard, missing the sedan's fender by inches, and for one glorious second the pull-off was open road ahead and both men were lunging through their own headlight beams like actors who'd missed their cue.

Then the taxi was moving down Ravine Road, dark, engine cold-blooded and complaining, and Lior realized with a lurch that the plan ended here. The men were already back in the sedan — headlights blooming in the mirror, gaining, because the sedan was newer and angrier and driven by someone who didn't care what he hit. Lior's phone was a brick. The nearest lit thing was the all-night diner on Route 9, four miles east, and the sedan would run them down in two.

So Lior did the thing they'd heard drivers joke about for six years and never once expected to do.

They killed the phone signal.

Not the phone — the signal. Every Marisol cab carried a transponder box under the dash, a fleet tracker with a kill switch hidden behind the fuse panel, installed back when her father worried about hijackings and retired the worry without retiring the hardware. Flip the switch and the cab went dark to dispatch, dark to GPS, a ghost in the system — the trick drivers used, allegedly, to take unpaid personal detours, the trick Marisol swore she'd fire anyone for using. Lior found the panel by touch, in the dark, at seventy miles an hour, because they had helped install the replacement boxes last winter when Petey's hands couldn't manage the screws, and their fingers remembered what their mind had filed away as trivia.

Click. The little amber light died. Somewhere in the dispatch office, a screen stopped showing a dot on Ravine Road. If anyone was watching — and someone was always watching — the taxi had simply ceased to exist.

The sedan behind them hesitated. Lior saw it in the mirror: brake lights flaring, confusion, the hunter losing the scent not because the prey outran him but because the prey stepped off the map. For ten seconds the headlights fell back. Ten seconds was a mile.

Lior took the next right, then the next, off the main road onto the service loop that followed the old rail spur — roads that existed in no rideshare app, roads Lior knew the way other people knew their mother's face, because knowing them was the job. Gravel gave way to cracked asphalt. The sedan's headlights swept past the intersection behind them, kept going, braked, reversed, and Lior was already two turns gone, engine idling low, running dark.

Now the harder part. A ghost car still needs fuel eventually, and a ghost driver still needs help, and the whole architecture of Lior's life — the desk, the radio, the logbook, the safe remove of dispatch — was built on the principle that you solve things by sending someone else. Tonight there was no one to send. Tonight the dispatcher was the fare.

Route 9 crossed the rail spur at a grade crossing two miles south, and beside the crossing, under the one working sodium lamp for miles, there was a wide gravel shoulder where truckers sometimes waited out their hours. Lior eased the taxi onto it, killed the lights, and waited — counting headlight pairs coming down the 9, sorting them the way they sorted everything: sedan, sedan, semi, pickup, and then, at 3:31 a.m., a cab. Marisol yellow, roof light glowing, moving eastbound at a law-abiding fifty-five.

Lior stepped out onto the shoulder and raised both arms, and did the oldest thing in the profession, older than radios, older than apps, older than the Kessler fortune and probably older than money itself.

They hailed a cab.

The yellow cab slowed. Braked. Pulled over with the wary courtesy of any night driver picking up a stranger on a dark shoulder, and the window came down, and Lior leaned in and said the words that would get them home:

"Company car, unit forty-one. I'm off the grid — don't ask. There's a gray sedan working Ravine Road looking for me, and I need you to take the long way to the diner on Ninth, and I need you to radio Marisol and tell her the night dispatcher says the ledger's already out, and she should get Astrid Voss out of that motel before sunrise."

The driver — a young woman Lior vaguely recognized from the Tuesday day shift, Dani, Dani-something, a face from the morning roll calls — stared at them for a full three seconds. Then she reached over and popped the locks.

"Get in," she said. "I heard about your shifts. Marisol told us all to watch for you." She pulled back onto the highway, and in the mirror Lior watched the dark road behind them stay dark. "Also, you're paying the fare. Company policy."

"It's my own company's car."

"Company policy," Dani repeated, with the serene immovability of someone reciting scripture, and Lior — adrenaline draining out of them in a slow, shaking tide, hands finally admitting how badly they trembled — laughed. It came out ragged and half-hysterical and real. They were alive. They were a passenger. For the first time in six years, someone else was driving, and the strangest part was how much it felt like relief.

At the diner, under buzzing fluorescent light, Lior fed quarters into the payphone bolted by the restrooms — the last payphone in the county, Marisol kept it because her father had installed it — and called the motel front desk using the booking name, and heard the clerk say, confused, "Miss Delgado checked out twenty minutes ago. Said her nephew was picking her up."

The quarters dropped from Lior's hand and rang on the tile like small bells.

Astrid had no nephew who drove. Astrid had one nephew, and he was currently a name in a decoded ledger, and Lior had just led him straight to her by calling in a panic from a traceable line — no. No, wait. Think. The motel call had come *after* the ambush; Dale's people had been on Ravine Road, not at the motel. Unless the ambush was the diversion. Unless the whole night had been choreographed — scare the dispatcher into running, flush the protector away from the witness, lift the witness while the protector was busy surviving.

Lior's mouth went dry. On the wall above the payphone, taped and yellowed, was a page torn from a phone book: local taxi numbers. Lior dialed the depot direct line with fingers that wouldn't quite obey, and Marisol answered on the first ring, awake, sharp, because Marisol was always awake and always sharp.

"Marisol. It's me. Is Astrid there?"

A pause. Paper rustling. Then Marisol's voice, carefully level, which was how Marisol sounded when things were very bad:

"She's here. She walked in the depot's back door fifteen minutes ago, alone, on foot, carrying a note." Another pause, longer. "Lior — the note's addressed to you. It's one word."

Lior closed their eyes. Behind them, through the diner window, dawn was still an hour off and the parking lot was empty, but somewhere out on Route 9 a gray sedan was turning around.

"What word?"

"'Run,'" Marisol said. "And underneath it — she wrote a second line. Small. Like she almost didn't want you to see it." The sound of Marisol covering the receiver, speaking to someone, then returning. "It says: *He knows Emil showed me where.*"
