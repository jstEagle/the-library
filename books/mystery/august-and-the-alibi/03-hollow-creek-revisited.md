## 3. Hollow Creek Revisited

The bus from the county seat took two hours and forty minutes, which gave August Whitlock plenty of time to reconsider every life choice that had led them to a seat upholstered in a fabric best described as "regret plaid."

They had told Dev they were going alone.

"You're going *alone*," Dev had repeated over the phone, in the tone of a man reading his own eulogy. "To the town where a confessed murderer lives. Alone. On a *bus*."

"It's a small town, Dev. The murderer is presumably eighty years old by now."

"Presumably. Great word. I'll carve it on your headstone. 'Presumably.'"

August had hung up smiling, which was the effect Dev usually had on them, and then spent the first hour of the ride not smiling at all, because the confession letter was in their bag, and it kept being there.

They'd read it eleven times now. Maybe twelve. The handwriting was careful, unhurried, the letters of someone who'd learned penmanship in an era when it mattered. *I drowned Lena Marsh in Hollow Creek on June 14th, 1984. She came to the water because I asked her to. Nobody pushed her in but me.* And then, beneath that, in the same steady hand: *More soon.*

More soon. As if this were a newsletter. As if August had subscribed.

The bus crested a hill and Hollow Creek appeared below them the way dying towns always do in autumn—suddenly and without apology. A main street four blocks long. A water tower with HOLLOW CREEK painted on it so many times the layers had gone geological. A mill at the far end, its windows dark, its smokestack standing over everything like a headstone nobody had gotten around to carving.

August had expected decay. What they hadn't expected was how *tidy* it all was. Lawns mowed. Porch flags out. A hardware store with a hand-lettered sign advertising a pie sale at the Methodist church. Towns that died of neglect looked neglected. This town looked *maintained*, the way a museum is maintained, or a stage set between performances.

The bus let them off in front of a diner called the Blue Plate, which served, according to the chalkboard, blue plate specials, which struck August as a business model with a certain circular integrity.

Inside, the diner smelled like coffee that had been coffee once and now aspired to be gravy. Eleven heads turned when August walked in. August counted them out of professional habit, then counted again, because ten of the eleven had turned back to their plates almost immediately—the practiced disinterest of people who'd been warned someone might come asking questions.

The eleventh kept looking. An old man in a feed cap, seated at the counter, who smiled at August with too many teeth.

"You'll be the podcast fella," he said.

"Person," August said automatically. "And I'm—"

"The one on the radio about dead folks." He said it cheerfully, the way you'd say *the one who does the weather*. "Ruthie Marsh said you'd be along. Told her she was a fool for talking to you." He patted the stool beside him. "Told her I'd be a fool not to listen in, though. Sit down. Coffee's terrible. That's how you know it's honest."

August sat. It seemed less dangerous than standing.

"I'm not here to cause trouble," they said.

"Course you're not." He poured the coffee himself, uninvited, from the pot behind the counter, with the ease of a man who'd been doing it since the last waitress quit in what may have been a previous administration. "Nobody ever is. Trouble's just what shows up anyway, like cats." He slid the cup over. "You'll want Ruthie. House on Sycamore, the one with the wind chimes made out of spoons. Can't miss it. Sounds like a kitchen falling down the stairs."

He turned back to his plate before August could ask his name, and August understood the interview was over, and also that it had never begun, and that both of those things had been arranged in advance.

* * *

The spoons announced Ruthie Marsh's house from half a block away—a bright, chaotic clatter that sounded, exactly as promised, like cutlery experiencing a structural collapse. The house itself was small and white, with a garden gone gloriously feral, zinnias staging a coup against the fence line.

Ruthie Marsh opened the door before August knocked.

She was small and straight-backed, somewhere north of seventy-five, with white hair cut short and eyes the pale gray of creek water in November. She looked August up and down once, thoroughly, the way you'd inspect a fish at market.

"Huh," she said. "You're younger than you sound."

"People say that."

"They say it about everybody on the radio. Radio makes everyone sound sixty and disappointed." She stepped back from the door. "Well? Come in before the neighbors get a good look at you. They've had forty years of practice looking, and they're very good at it."

The inside of the house was warm and crowded and smelled of cinnamon and old paper. Every horizontal surface held photographs. August's eyes went to them immediately, professionally, and stopped on one near the window: two teenage girls arm in arm on a dock, squinting into summer, one laughing so hard the picture had caught her mid-blur.

"Lena," Ruthie said, following August's gaze. Not a question. "You found her fast. Most people take a minute."

"She looks like you."

"She looked like our mother. I got our father's face, which God saw fit to make resemble a boot." Ruthie settled into an armchair and gestured August into the other one, across a coffee table bearing a single cup of tea already poured. Two cups. She'd known. Of course she'd known; the whole town had known before August's bus cleared the county line.

"Mrs. Marsh," August began.

"Ruthie. Mrs. Marsh was my mother-in-law and she was a horror." She folded her hands. "Ask your questions. I've waited forty years for somebody to ask them, so forgive me if I answer more than you bargained for."

August set down the recorder, asked permission, got it, and started gently—with Lena herself, who she'd been, what she loved. Ruthie answered easily here, warming like a stove. Lena had been funny. Lena had been the fast one, the bold one, the one who talked Ruthie into everything and talked them both out of trouble after. Lena had wanted to leave Hollow Creek for nursing school in the fall of 1984.

"She never made it to June 15th," Ruthie said. Her voice didn't waver. It had clearly done its wavering decades ago and come out the other side flat and hard as slate. "The coroner said accidental drowning. Said she slipped on the rocks. Said she'd been—"

She stopped. Picked up her tea. Put it down without drinking.

"Said she'd been drinking," August finished quietly. "It was in the report. Blood alcohol. It was used to explain why she didn't fight, didn't surface."

"Lena didn't drink." Ruthie's eyes came up sharp. "Not that June. She was training for her physicals. She wouldn't have touched a drop, and everyone in this town who knew her knew that, and every one of them nodded along anyway when the coroner said it." She smiled, and it was the worst smile August had ever seen, all teeth and no warmth, the smile of a woman who had been polite for forty years and had run completely out of the raw material. "That's when I understood how this place works. It doesn't decide what happened. It decides what we're all going to *say* happened. And then we say it until we die."

August's skin prickled. They'd interviewed hundreds of people in cold cases, and there was a register of grief they'd learned to hear underneath grief—and Ruthie had just spoken in it fluently.

"Tell me about the others," August said.

Ruthie went still. "Others."

"The drownings. Plural."

For a long moment the only sound was the spoons outside, clattering in the wind like applause from an empty theater.

"How do you know about the others?" Ruthie asked.

"Because your coroner retired to Cedar Falls," August said. "And last week he drowned in his bathtub. And because I've spent three days pulling county records, and records are like icebergs—what's visible tells you there's more underneath." They leaned forward. "Tommy Aldous, 1982, age sixteen. Danny Voss—" they watched her flinch at the name, filed it away— "1986, age nineteen. Both ruled accidental. Both in the creek. Both within two miles of where Lena died. Three drownings in four years, in a town of two thousand, in a stretch of water children swim in every summer. That's not bad luck, Ruthie. That's a pattern wearing a costume."

The old woman looked at August for a long time. Then she laughed—one short, cracked note, nothing amused in it.

"Forty years," she said. "Forty years I've been the crazy one. The sister who wouldn't let it go. Do you know what they call grief that won't sit down and shut up in a town like this? They call it *trouble*. And trouble gets your windows broken, and your husband passed over at the mill, and your phone calls not returned, until either you leave or you learn to whisper." She stood, abruptly, and went to the window full of photographs. "I whispered for forty years. You want to know the worst part?"

"Tell me."

"I think most of them don't even know what they're protecting anymore. It's not a person. It's older than whoever did it. It's the *saying*. The whole town saying the same wrong thing for so long that the truth would be rude now. You can't accuse a town of lying for forty years. It'd be impolite." She touched Lena's blurred laughing face with one finger. "Impoliteness is the only sin left around here."

August sat with that. Outside, the spoon chimes rang on, cheerful and deranged.

"Who benefited?" they asked. "From the saying. Who did the town close ranks around?"

Ruthie turned around slowly.

"You've been reading the files," she said. "So you tell me. Whose names show up on the rescue squad that pulled the bodies out? Whose names sign the coroner's paperwork? Whose family owned the mill, the bank, and half the main street, and whose son was sweet on Lena that spring?" She came back to her chair but didn't sit. "I'll give you a hint. It rhymes with *boss*."

"Voss."

"There it is." She lowered herself into the chair at last, suddenly looking every one of her years, and older, like a building whose scaffolding had finally been removed. "Harold Voss was mayor when Lena died. His brother signed the death certificate. His father owned the mill where half this town worked, including my husband, who never missed a shift again as long as he lived." She met August's eyes. "I'm an old woman telling stories, Mr.—Ms.—Whitlock. Whatever you are. I'm a grieving old woman and none of this is evidence. You should know that going in. Everyone here will tell you the same thing, eventually, in the same words, like they rehearsed it."

"Did they rehearse it?"

"Oh, honey." Ruthie smiled her terrible smile again. "Rehearsal implies somebody remembers learning it."

* * *

August walked the creek road at dusk, because they were constitutionally incapable of leaving a crime scene unvisited, and because some part of them—the part that had grown up moving through foster houses like a ghost auditing other families' happiness—had always needed to stand in the places where things ended.

Hollow Creek was low this time of year, running brown and quiet over flat stones, maybe eight feet wide at the bend where the old swimming hole widened out. A rope swing still hung from a sycamore, frayed and motionless. Somebody's kids had left sneakers on the bank. Ordinary water. Murderously ordinary water.

Three people had died here in four years, and the town had said *accident* three times, and meant it, or said meaning it, which amounted to the same thing.

August crouched at the water's edge and felt the familiar thrill rise—the hunter's hum, the sense of a lock beginning to turn—and then, for the first time in their career, felt something curdle inside the thrill. Because this wasn't a cold case anymore. Someone had sent them a confession. Someone was killing again, *now*, in bathtubs and quiet rooms, and every new fact August dug up was a fact someone might kill to keep buried. The hunt had always been a game played with the past. It had just stopped being one.

Their phone buzzed. Dev:

*Local news says retired coroner's autopsy is "inconclusive." Inconclusive!! For a guy found drowned in a DRY bathroom. Get out of there. Also Marisol asked if you're okay, which means she's worried, which means I'm worried. Also I ate your emergency granola bar. Sorry. Not sorry.*

August huffed a laugh despite everything, typed back *on my way tomorrow, buy more granola*, and pocketed the phone.

As they turned to go, they noticed it—a small thing, the kind of small thing their whole career was built on. At the base of the sycamore, half-tucked into the roots, someone had left flowers. Fresh ones. Zinnias, gone slightly wilted in the evening cool, their stems cut clean.

Zinnias. Like the ones rioting against Ruthie Marsh's fence.

Someone in this town was still tending the dead. Quietly. Regularly enough that the petals had begun to fade.

August stood in the gathering dark, listening to the creek say nothing, and understood that Hollow Creek had answered their real question without meaning to. The town wasn't hiding a killer.

It was keeping a vigil—for something it had decided, forty years ago, never to name.

Behind them, up the road, a car engine started. Headlights swept the trees, held on August for one long moment—

and drove slowly away.
