## 14. Rosa's Cold Case Wall

Rosa Ibarra's apartment smelled like coffee that had been reheated too many times and the faint, stubborn ghost of cigarette smoke from a habit she'd quit in 2011 and still talked about like a lover who'd done her wrong.

"You're going to have to take your shoes off," she said, holding the door open with her hip. "And you're going to have to promise me—out loud, like a witness on the stand—that nothing you see here goes into an episode."

August stepped out of their shoes onto a doormat that read WELCOME in letters worn to suggestion. "And if I don't promise?"

"Then you can stand in the hallway and I'll describe it to you through the door, badly, like a sports announcer who doesn't know the rules." She held out her hand. August shook it. Her grip was dry and strong and did not let go immediately. "Out loud, Whitlock."

"Nothing goes in an episode," August said. "Off the record. Scout's honor. I was never a scout, but I honor the concept."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said in this building all year." She released their hand and jerked her chin toward the interior of the apartment. "Come on. And fair warning—if you touch anything without asking, I will arrest you for something. I'll figure out what later. I'm a detective. It's what I do."

* * *

The wall was in the second bedroom, which had clearly once been intended as a nursery or an office and had instead become something closer to a chapel.

August had seen evidence walls before—in documentaries, in police stations, in the glossy reenactments their own podcast commissioned from production designers who made murder look tasteful. None of them had prepared them for this. This was not a wall built for an audience. This was a wall built by one person talking to herself at three in the morning, trying to make a shape out of noise.

Photographs covered every inch. Printouts of postmarks. Enlargements of handwriting, annotated in Rosa's small, furious capitals. A timeline ran along the baseboard on butcher paper, dates marching backward through decades like footprints leading away from a body.

"Jesus," August said quietly.

"He's not been much help," Rosa said. She leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, watching them the way a person watches someone else handle something fragile. Or dangerous. With Rosa it was hard to tell the difference, and August suspected she considered that ambiguity a professional asset.

August moved closer. The photographs were of envelopes—their envelopes. The twin letters that had started everything, photographed flat under raking light so the paper's texture showed like topography. Beside them, other envelopes August had never seen. Different handwriting, mostly, but arranged in clusters, connected by red string that would have embarrassed August if it weren't so obviously functional rather than decorative.

"How long?" August asked.

"Fourteen months before you got yours." Rosa came up beside them. Close, she smelled like coffee and hand sanitizer. "I caught a case in March of last year. Retired schoolteacher out past Fairview, found dead in his car at the bottom of a retention pond. Ruled accidental. Looked accidental. Except his daughter swore he'd gotten a letter he wouldn't show anyone, and he'd started sleeping with a baseball bat by the bed, which is not behavior consistent with a man planning to drive himself into a pond."

"And you pulled the letter?"

"I pulled the letter." She tapped one of the photographs—a crumpled envelope in an evidence bag, postmark blurred but legible. "Different handwriting than yours. But same paper stock. Same brand of envelope, same batch, near as I could tell from the manufacturing codes. And the same—" she hesitated, hunting for the word, "—the same *tone*. You've read yours enough times to know what I mean. That polite, almost apologetic quality. Like the writer is doing you a favor."

August knew exactly what she meant. They had read their own confession letter so many times they could recite it, and the recitation always snagged on the same detail: *I hope this brings you some peace, though I suspect it won't.* Courtesy folded around cruelty like a napkin around a knife.

"There are nine," Rosa said. "Nine letters I know about, including yours. Six deaths. Every single one ruled accident, natural causes, or unsolved-and-forgotten. Every victim over sixty. Every one of them connected to Hollow Creek, either born there or lived there long enough to leave roots." She looked at August sideways. "You didn't know about the others."

It wasn't a question, but August answered it anyway. "No. I thought—I thought it was him finishing something. Tying off loose ends from '84. That's what it looked like. That's what it *felt* like."

"It felt tidy," Rosa agreed. "That's what it was designed to feel like. Come here. I want to show you why tidy is a lie."

She led them along the timeline. Her finger, blunt-nailed and ink-stained, touched down on dates like a bird landing.

"March, last year. Gerald Pruitt, retired teacher, drowned in his car. Letter received by his daughter two weeks after the funeral—postmarked the day before he died. Then nothing for five months. Then September: Dolores Kwan, seventy-one, former county clerk's office, heart attack in her sleep. Her nephew gets a letter saying, quote, 'she kept things she shouldn't have.' Nothing for four months. January: Raymond Ostrander, sixty-eight, falls down his own basement stairs in Ohio. His brother gets a letter. February: Walter Finch, seventy-four, Hollow Creek native, house fire. His niece gets a letter. And then—" her finger stopped, pressed flat against the paper, "—April. Your letters. And then June, Coroner Ames in his bathtub, and the letter describing it before the police released a single detail."

August stared at the timeline until the dates swam. "There's a pattern in the gaps," they said slowly. "Five months, four months, one month, two months. It's accelerating."

"It's accelerating," Rosa confirmed. "Which tells us the writer is running out of time. People who are comfortable take their time. People who are dying, or being investigated, or being hunted—those people speed up." She turned to face August fully. "But here's the part that kept me up at night. Here's the part I need you to look at, because you're the only person I know whose entire brain is built for this and who isn't contractually obligated to ignore it."

She crossed to the far side of the room and pulled back a bedsheet that had been pinned over a section of the wall like a dust cover over furniture in a dead relative's house.

Beneath it was a second timeline. Smaller. Denser. And completely different.

"These are the victims' *finances*," Rosa said. "Property records, pension disbursements, land sales, court judgments. Gerald Pruitt owned eleven acres outside Hollow Creek that a development consortium tried to buy in 1987 and he refused. Dolores Kwan processed every deed filing in the county for thirty years—she handled the paperwork when the Voss family consolidated their holdings along the creek in the nineties. Raymond Ostrander was a surveyor. Walter Finch was a title examiner." She tapped each name as she went, and August felt the floor of the case tilt beneath them, gently at first, then all at once. "Every victim touched the land records. Every one. Either they refused to sell, or they filed the documents, or they surveyed the boundaries, or they examined the titles."

"The drownings were never the point," August said. The words came out of them flat and strange, like a sentence in a language they'd only just begun to learn.

"The drownings were never the point," Rosa echoed. "The drownings are the *story*. The confession letters, the anniversary, the whole gothic little ritual of it—that's the wrapping paper. It's beautiful wrapping paper, Whitlock. It got *you*, didn't it? Forty-year-old drowning, dramatic letters, a town full of secrets. You bit. I bit. Everybody bites, because the story is delicious." Her voice hardened. "But you know what nobody asks about a delicious story? *Who benefits.*"

August stood very still. In their chest, something was rearranging itself—the same sensation they'd had at fourteen, in their third foster home, when they'd realized the family photo albums had been curated, that the smiling Christmas pictures were a selection, that the truth of a household lived in what had been left out of frame.

"Harold Voss," August said.

Rosa's eyebrows went up. "Say more."

"The patriarch. Elias told me about him—or told me around him. He owns half the creek frontage. He's old, he's respected, he's the kind of man towns name things after." August turned back to the wall, scanning the financial timeline with new eyes. "If the drownings were the work of the town fathers—and everything points that way—then Voss isn't covering up a murder. He's covering up a *land grab*. Three people died because they were inconvenient, and forty years of property value compounded on top of their graves. And now he's old, and maybe he's sick, and every person left alive who touched those records is a thread that could unravel the whole inheritance."

"Now you're cooking," Rosa said. She sounded genuinely pleased, in the way of a teacher whose student has finally stopped showing their work wrong. "Here's my theory, off the record, unprovable, and possibly insane: Voss—or whoever is writing these letters—isn't confessing. He's *editing*. Every death removes a witness to the land transactions, not the drownings. The letters are theater to keep investigators like me chasing 1984 while the actual crime scene is a county deed office." She paused. "Which, incidentally, someone already broke into last month. You wouldn't know anything about that."

August kept their face admirably neutral. "I couldn't possibly comment."

"Mm." Rosa's mouth twitched. "The security footage showed a tall figure and someone shorter carrying a camera bag. I described it to myself as 'a journalist and their producer' and then I decided I hadn't seen it. We're even now."

"We're even," August agreed, and meant it, and was surprised by how much they meant it. There was something steadying about Rosa—her fury, her mess, her wall built at three in the morning. She was a person who had also chosen truth over comfort and paid rent on the choice every day.

They stood together in front of the uncovered section, shoulder to shoulder, looking at forty years of paperwork arranged like scripture.

"There's one more thing," Rosa said, and her voice changed, and August felt the change in their spine before they understood it in their ears. "The acceleration. Five months, four months, one month, two months. If the pattern holds, the next interval is weeks. Days, maybe. And there's one category of person left on my list who fits the profile—someone who touched the records, someone connected to Hollow Creek, someone young enough that killing her now, while she's traceable to the town, would close the loop permanently."

The room seemed to get quieter. August heard the refrigerator humming in the kitchen. Heard their own pulse.

"Rosa," they said carefully. "Who?"

"She works for you, August." Rosa turned and looked at them directly, and her eyes were not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Her grandmother was Lena Marsh's best friend. Which means her grandmother knew where the bodies were buried—maybe literally. And if the grandmother kept anything—letters, documents, anything Dolores Kwan ever filed—then the granddaughter is holding the last copy of the evidence." She let that sit. "Marisol Reyes. When does your crew next go to Hollow Creek?"

August's mouth had gone dry. "We haven't scheduled it. But the anniversary is—" they did the math automatically, horribly, "—six days away."

"Six days," Rosa repeated. She reached out and unpinned a blank index card from a box on the desk, wrote MARISOL REYES on it in her small furious capitals, and held it up to the wall beside the timeline, not yet pinning it. Holding it, like a question.

"So here's your choice, Whitlock, and I'm sorry, because I know what that crew is to you—I've listened to the episodes, I'm not made of stone. You can pull her off the story, keep her home, keep her blind, and hope the writer loses interest. Or you can use the fact that the writer is watching you, and feed them something false, and control the ground." She lowered the card. "But decide fast. Because if my math is right, whoever is writing these letters has already decided for you."

* * *

August drove home with the windows down despite the cold, letting the air scour them, thinking about Marisol laughing in the studio booth yesterday, headphones around her neck, teasing Dev about his caffeine intake, utterly unaware that her name was written on a detective's wall in furious capital letters.

At a red light, they pulled out their phone and opened the group chat. DEV, MARISOL, ELIAS. Their whole strange, stitched-together family, one thumb-tap away.

They typed: *Hey. Can everyone come to the studio tomorrow morning? Something's changed. I'll explain everything.*

They stared at the message for a long time. Then, beneath it, almost without deciding to, they typed a second line:

*Marisol — I need to talk to you first. Just you. Please.*

They hit send on both. The light turned green. Somewhere across town, in a quiet house, an envelope might already be sealed, addressed, stamped, waiting for the morning mail—and August Whitlock, who had spent their whole career arriving at the truth one step behind the body, was suddenly, desperately afraid of what it would mean to arrive one step ahead.
