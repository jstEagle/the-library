## 20. Live Broadcast

The hospital smelled like antiseptic and old coffee, and August Whitlock hated both of them equally.

Elias Crane was in room 412, propped up against a mountain of pillows that some nurse had arranged with more care than he'd ever arranged anything in his life. His left arm was in a sling. There was a bruise along his jaw the color of a storm cloud, and his reading glasses were folded on the bedside tray because he'd insisted on being lucid for this conversation, and Elias Crane without his glasses was a man who had decided to stop hiding.

"You're doing the thing," he said as August sat down in the vinyl chair.

"What thing."

"The thing where you rehearse a speech and pretend you're just visiting." He gestured at the water pitcher with his good hand. "Pour me one of those and then say what you came to say."

August poured. The water trembled slightly in the glass, which annoyed them, because their hands had been steady through a quarry confrontation forty-eight hours ago and apparently it was a hospital room that finally broke them.

"Harold Voss confessed," August said. "All three drownings. Lena Marsh, Dale Pruitt, Tommy Okafor. He talked for four hours. Rosa Ibarra had to change the tape twice."

"I heard."

"He used your name. In the confession. Said you knew. Said you helped bury the 1988 tape." August set the pitcher down. "The DA is deciding whether to charge you with obstruction. Forty years of obstruction."

"Yes," Elias said.

Not *I didn't*. Not *it wasn't like that*. Just *yes*, delivered the way he'd once corrected August's pronunciation of *Kierkegaard*—patiently, as if the truth were simply a word August had been misusing.

"They'll offer you a deal," August said. "Cooperate, testify about the town, the Voss family, all of it, and they'll go easy. Probable suspended sentence. You're seventy-four, Elias. No prosecutor in the world wants to put you in a cell."

"I know."

"But there's another version." August's throat tightened around the words. "The version where I sit on the tape. Where I tell the audience the story ends with Harold Voss, full stop, and nobody ever learns that my mentor—the man who taught me everything—took money from the Voss family in 1989 to make a problem disappear. The version where I protect you."

Elias looked at them for a long moment. Then he smiled, small and tired and so familiar that it hurt somewhere behind August's ribs.

"There it is," he said. "The choice. I wondered when you'd let yourself see it."

"Don't do that. Don't act like you planned this as a teaching moment."

"I didn't plan any of this." He shifted against the pillows, wincing. "But I've known since the second letter arrived that it would end here. With you in a chair, holding a decision like a hot pan. That's the job, August. That's what I trained you for, even though I never said it out loud. Someday you'd have to choose between the story and the person. And I always hoped—" He stopped. Started again. "I always hoped you'd choose wrong."

August stared at him. "You *what*?"

"I hoped you'd choose me. Lie for me. Bury it." He laughed, and it turned into a cough. "Because then I'd know I mattered more than the work. Selfish of me. But there it is." He recovered, wiped his mouth with the back of his good hand. "And now I'm going to tell you the thing I actually believe, the thing I should have told you twenty years ago in that community radio station when you were twenty-three and terrified and so hungry for the truth you could've eaten it raw: if you bury this, you'll never forgive yourself. And worse—you'll never forgive *me*. You'll visit me every Sunday until I die and every visit will be a small funeral for the person you thought I was. Whereas if you tell it—" he spread his hands, "—you'll hurt like hell for a year, maybe two. And then you'll still be able to look at yourself in the mirror. I've spent forty years not looking. Take it from me: the mirror wins eventually."

August's eyes were burning. They blinked it back down. "You could ask me. You could just ask me to keep quiet. It would be easier if you asked."

"No," Elias said softly. "It wouldn't. Because then you'd be keeping my secret instead of making your choice. And you, August Whitlock—" he tapped the bedside tray twice, a gesture August had seen a thousand times across a soundboard, meaning *cue*, meaning *this is the part that matters* —"you were never any good at keeping secrets. That's why I picked you."

* * *

Dev found them in the studio at six the next morning, already surrounded by cold pizza and legal documents.

Marisol had gone home to sleep—or to lie awake, more likely—and Dev had the particular gray-faced energy of a man who had been discharged from the ER at 2 a.m. with seven stitches above his eyebrow and a prescription he intended to ignore. The cut made him look rakish, which he would have enjoyed if he weren't so exhausted.

"Okay," he said, dropping into his chair and spinning it once, a nervous habit he'd never outgrown. "Lawyer says we're clear to broadcast. Rosa signed off—off the record, unofficially, in a parking garage, which is very her. The DA's office is furious but they can't stop us. First Amendment, public interest, blah blah blah." He rubbed his eyes. "August. Before you do this. Can I say something?"

"You'll say it whether I say yes or not."

"Fair." He turned the chair to face them fully. "I've edited every episode you've ever made. Nine years. I know your pauses better than my own heartbeat. And I'm telling you—if you go live and say Elias's name in connection with the cover-up, there's no edit button. Not for the audience, not for you, not for him. Are you sure?"

August looked at the soundboard. At the ON AIR sign, dark, waiting. At the mug on the shelf that Marisol had given them three birthdays ago: WORLD'S OKAYEST TRUTH-TELLER.

"When I was eleven," August said, "a caseworker told me that the trick to foster care was lowering your expectations. Keep your stuff in a garbage bag by the door. Don't unpack. Don't get attached." They picked up the mug, turned it in their hands. "I spent thirty years doing that with people. Keeping my stuff packed. And then you three happened, and Elias happened, and I unpacked. And now the whole point—" their voice caught, and they pushed through it, "—the whole point is that you don't get to only unpack the boxes you like. You take what's in all of them. Even the ugly ones. Especially those."

Dev was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and reached over and flicked a switch on the board—a pre-flight check, muscle memory.

"Levels are hot," he said. "Whenever you're ready."

* * *

They went live at 7:04 a.m., four minutes late because Marisol came running through the door at 7:02 with her coat half-on and her grandmother's rosary wound around her wrist, and August refused to start without her.

She slid into her seat, gave August a look that contained an entire conversation—*I know what you're about to do, I hate it, I'm here anyway*—and pulled up her faders.

"This is *Hollow Creek*, episode fourteen," August said. "The final episode. I'm August Whitlock. Normally I'd give you the polished version. The produced version. We're very good at that—my team is the best in the business, and we had a beautiful cut of this episode ready to go. Clean narrative. Satisfying ending. Bad man arrested, case closed, roll credits.

"I'm not going to play it."

In the glass, Dev closed his eyes briefly, then opened them and kept working.

"Two nights ago, in the flooded quarry outside Hollow Creek, Harold Voss attacked me. He confessed to killing three people between 1984 and 1988—Lena Marsh, Dale Pruitt, and Tommy Okafor. He confessed because he's dying, and because my podcast flushed out the last witnesses, and because he wanted to tie off loose ends the way his family has tied off loose ends for forty years. He is in custody. He will almost certainly die in prison. If the story ended there, it would be a good story. It might even be a true one.

"But it isn't the whole truth. And this show has only ever had one rule: the whole truth, or nothing.

"So here is the part that hurts."

August took a breath. Marisol's hand hovered near theirs on the console—not touching, just near, a lighthouse of a hand—and August told it.

They told the audience about the 1988 cassette. About young Elias Crane, twenty-six years old, walking into the Hollow Creek sheriff's office with a recording of everything he'd seen and heard the night Lena Marsh died. About how the tape vanished into a file cabinet, and how two years later a struggling journalist named Elias Crane received an envelope of cash and a phone call from a lawyer representing the Voss family interests, and how he took it. How he moved away, changed his name back to the one he'd been born with, built a career, and never spoke of Hollow Creek again until the day he hired a ferocious, broken, truth-starved kid from the foster system and taught them how to dig.

"Elias Crane did not kill anyone," August said. "Harold Voss killed three people, and a town full of decent, frightened people helped him forget it. But Elias took the money. Elias stayed silent. Elias watched me spend nine years tearing open other people's sealed cases while sitting on a sealed case of his own—and I have to believe, because I choose to believe, that he did it hoping I'd find it. Hoping I'd be the one to make him pay. Because he couldn't make himself do it."

Their voice cracked on the last sentence. They let it crack. Marisol's hand settled over theirs, brief and warm, then withdrew.

"Some of you trusted this show because we were willing to burn people who deserved it," August continued. "Today I'm asking you to trust us with something harder: burning someone who didn't deserve it, but who wasn't innocent either. Elias Crane is facing charges for obstruction. He has asked me—in writing, through his attorney—not to contest them. He wants to plead guilty. He says it's the first honest thing he's done in forty years, and he wanted to do it in public, where the Marsh family could hear it."

A pause. Dev held up a finger: incoming call. Rosa Ibarra, on the line, as agreed.

"Detective Ibarra is with us. Rosa—anything you want to add before we open the phones?"

Rosa's voice came through dry and steady as ever. "Just this. People are going to say the system failed because it took forty years. The system did fail. But it failed because systems are made of people, and the people chose comfort over courage, one at a time, for four decades. What finally cracked it wasn't a detective or a prosecutor. It was a podcaster with a cheap microphone and an inability to leave things alone." A pause, and August could hear the almost-smile in it. "Don't ever change, Whitlock. It's infuriating and it works."

* * *

The calls came in waves.

Some were furious—*how dare you drag your mentor, how dare you air this, some things should stay private.* Some were grateful—Lena Marsh's great-niece called from Ohio and cried on the air for two straight minutes while Marisol, tears streaming down her own face, rode the levels gently so the grief wouldn't clip. A retired teacher from Hollow Creek called to describe the town the way it had really been: the Voss mill employing half the families, the other half afraid, the creek running brown with dye waste some summers, everyone knowing and no one saying.

At 9:15, a call screen read ELIAS CRANE — HOSPITAL, ROOM 412.

August's heart stuttered. They looked at Dev. Dev shrugged helplessly—he hadn't patched it through; someone at the station had.

"Take it," Marisol whispered. "He's earned the mic."

August patched it through. "We have a caller. Go ahead."

"You always did talk too fast when you were nervous," Elias's voice said, reedy through the phone line but unmistakably himself. "I'm listening from bed. The nurses think I'm sleeping. I want to say one thing to your audience, and then I'll hang up before you can cut me off, which I know you're itching to do."

"I would never."

"You absolutely would. Here's my thing." A breath, winded. "Forty years ago I made a trade. My silence for my life. And I told myself for four decades that it was survival, and sometimes it even felt like survival. But it was cowardice wearing survival's coat. This young person"—and August could hear the smile—"whom I found half-feral in a community radio station, taught me, without meaning to, that the debt doesn't expire. That you pay it whenever you finally grow the spine. I grew mine too late to save anyone. Maybe telling this now saves someone else. That's the best I can offer. Goodbye, August. Do the next one better than I did."

The line went dead.

The studio was silent except for the hum of the board. Marisol had both hands pressed over her mouth. Dev had removed his glasses and was cleaning them on his shirt, which was what he did instead of crying.

August leaned into the microphone.

"That was Elias Crane. And that's the end of the broadcast portion of today's program—but not the end of the story. In our final episode, we'll hear from the families. From Hollow Creek itself. From the people who stayed silent and the ones who couldn't. Because a case doesn't end when the handcuffs click. It ends when the living figure out how to carry it." They glanced at Marisol, who nodded. "For Dev Okafor—"

Dev's head snapped up. August had never said his full name on air. Tommy Okafor's name sat in the air between them, forty years old and finally spoken by family.

"—who learned this week that his grandfather's cousin was one of the three; for Marisol Reyes, whose grandmother loved Lena Marsh enough to grieve her for sixty years in silence; and for Elias Crane, who is listening whether he admits it or not—thank you. This has been *Hollow Creek*. Truth costs. Pay it anyway."

August reached for the fader. Their hand hesitated over it—one breath, two—

Then Dev reached past them and pulled the channel down himself, gentle as closing a door.

"We're clear," he said quietly. "You did it."

August sat back. The ON AIR light blinked off. Outside the glass, morning sun was coming through the studio windows, ordinary and golden, falling across the soundboard, the cold pizza, the mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST TRUTH-TELLER.

Marisol put her arms around them first. Then Dev, stitches and all. August stood in the middle of it and let themselves shake, because the broadcast was over and the shaking had been waiting patiently for hours and deserved its turn.

It was Dev who stepped back first, frowning at his laptop.

"Huh," he said.

"What?"

"The mailroom just flagged a package. Hand-delivered, ten minutes ago. No return address." He turned the screen toward them. A photo of a plain envelope, sitting in a wire tray. August's name on it in careful, unfamiliar handwriting.

"It's over," Marisol said. "Voss is in custody. It's over."

August looked at the envelope on the screen and felt the old vertigo rise, gentle as water.

"Apparently," they said, "we're about to find out."
