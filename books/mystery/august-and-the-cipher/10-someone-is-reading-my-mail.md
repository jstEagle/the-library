## 10. Someone Is Reading My Mail

The locker smelled of pennies and old rain.

Mara noticed it the moment she swung the door open at 11:40 p.m., the way you notice a stranger's cologne in your own hallway—not the smell itself but its wrongness, its trespass. Her locker was the size of a breadbox, dented green steel with a combination she'd chosen herself, 14-26-8, her grandmother's birthday folded into something innocuous. Inside it lived the small archaeology of a night-shift life: a spare cardigan the color of wet slate, a thermos with a hairline crack that wept coffee down its seam, a paperback of tide tables dog-eared to March, and—folded into quarters, tucked beneath the thermos—the working copy of the cipher.

She had photocopied it twice and kept the originals elsewhere. She had photographed it and stored the images under a folder named *HVAC invoices*. She had done everything an invisible woman does to stay invisible.

But the thermos sat two inches left of where she'd left it, and the cardigan's sleeve, which she always draped over the shelf hook with the cuff turned back, hung straight. And the cipher—she lifted it with two fingers, careful not to smudge whatever prints might live there—was refolded along new lines. Creases crossing creases, like a scar over a scar.

Someone had opened her locker. Someone had read August Hale's last letter to Miss Wei, and then had put it back with the tenderness of a burglar making a bed.

Mara stood in the dispatch bay's fluorescent hum and let the cold move through her in stages: first the shoulders, then the sternum, then a long slow slide into the gut. Around her the night went on being ordinary. Unit 12 radioed in from the depot wanting his log signed early because his daughter had a recital. The harbor cam showed fog unspooling off the breakwater like gauze pulled from a wound. Somewhere a gull complained at nothing. The world did not know that the floor of Mara Zhou's life had just tilted three degrees.

*Who has the combination?*

She ran the list the way she ran a routing board—fast, clean, no sentiment. Maintenance had master keys, but maintenance came Tuesdays and this was Thursday, and the master key left a different mark on the latch, a bright scratch she'd have seen. Dispatch supervisors: one, Gerald Okafor, who kept his office dark by ten and whose handwriting on the shift logs had gone small and cramped lately, as if he were writing for someone other than himself. And then—

And then anyone who had watched her open it. That was the answer she didn't want. Fourteen months of nights, and she could not count the times she'd spun the dial with the bay half-full of drivers waiting on assignments, drivers killing time, drivers with nothing to do but watch the woman who never talked, never lingered, never gave them anything to watch. She had made herself a locked box in a room full of bored men and women, and she had assumed the lock mattered more than the audience.

Amateurs assume locks. Dispatchers know better. Everything is observable; the only question is who's logging it.

She closed the locker gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and went back to the board.

* * *

At 2:17 a.m. the scanner gave her the thing she would spend the rest of the night turning over like a stone with something alive under it.

Voss's voice, flat and tired, talking to the desk sergeant at the station: "Pull the tip line tape from yesterday afternoon. The letter that came in about the Hale exhumation—I want the envelope it came in, the postmark, everything."

A pause, static breathing. Then the sergeant: "It wasn't mailed, Detective. It was hand-delivered. Front counter, around four. No name."

"Read me the first line again."

The sergeant read it, and Mara's hand stopped moving on the routing board, pen hovering over a fare adjustment she would later have no memory of making.

*"Regarding the empty coffin of August Hale: check the taxi company records from 1951, and ask yourself why Harborline Dispatch was founded the same year a boy drowned at Old Harbor boathouse."*

Her sentence. Not word for word—word for word would have been forgery, and whoever wrote it was better than that—but her architecture. The two facts braided together, the year, the drowning, the company. It was the connection she had made alone in this chair at three in the morning ten days ago, staring at the 1951 ledger until the numbers swam, and she had told exactly no one. She had written it in her notebook, in shorthand of her own devising, and the notebook lived in her apartment behind a door with two locks.

Except.

Except she had drafted a letter. Of course she had—that was what she did, that was the whole machinery of her secret life, the anonymous letters that had quietly cracked three deaths the police had closed and shelved. She drafted them here, on shift, in the dead hours between calls, on the dispatch terminal's offline buffer where no one looked. She drafted them and then she deleted them, every keystroke, before printing the final clean copy at the library or the all-night copy shop on Delancey, paying cash, wearing the gray coat with the hood.

Had she deleted this one?

She tried to remember and found the memory maddeningly soft-edged, the way memories go when routine has sanded them. Ten days ago. Night shift. Rain. She remembered writing the paragraph—remembered the pleasure of it, the tight click of two facts fitting—and she remembered the copy shop, the bell over the door, the clerk who never looked up. Between those two memories lay a gap the width of a deletion command. Had she pressed it?

Or had someone else pressed it for her? Had someone stood behind her chair—or beside the printer, or at the terminal after her shift ended at six, when the day dispatcher, Pete, took over and spent his first hour doing crossword puzzles with the radio low?

The letter hadn't been sent through the tip line's mail slot. Hand-delivered, front counter, four p.m.—which was ninety minutes before her shift began. Which meant someone had carried her words across town in daylight, walked them into the police station, and set them down like a gift with no return address.

Giving Voss the thread. Or giving Voss *her*.

Because that was the other reading, the one that crawled up out of her stomach around three-thirty and refused to go back down: the letter didn't just point at Hale and 1951. It pointed at the pattern. Three anonymous letters, three closed cases reopened, and now a fourth communication about the case everyone in town was chewing on. Voss was already half-suspicious of her letters—he'd said as much on the phone, that warning wrapped around a request like wire around a live cable. A detective who suspected an invisible woman now held a letter that sounded like her, delivered by hand, about the exact mystery she'd been asking quiet questions around town.

She hadn't sent it. But the letter existed, and existence was enough. You didn't need to catch a dispatcher breaking the law; you just needed to make her visible, and visibility did the rest.

Someone was unwrapping her.

* * *

She finished the shift the way she did everything: flawlessly. Two medical runs routed ahead of traffic, a lost tourist walked to his hotel by voice alone, a drunk driver reported off the bluff road and intercepted before the curve. At 5:50 she wrote the handover notes in her small square hand, and at 6:02 Pete slouched in smelling of coffee and crossword ink, and she watched him the way she'd never watched him in fourteen months—noticed that he wore the same jacket three days running, that he logged in under her session without signing her out, that he glanced at the printer tray out of habit, the way everyone did, because the printer tray was where the world arrived.

"Quiet night," he said.

"Quiet," she agreed, and picked up her bag, and felt the cipher's absence in her locker like a tooth missing.

She did not go home. She drove—her own car, the one nobody associated with her because she so rarely used it in daylight—to the copy shop on Delancey and bought a coffee she didn't want, standing at the window watching the morning come up gray over the marina. And there she did the thing she was best at in all the world, the thing no one had ever thanked her for: she reconstructed a timeline.

Ten days ago, draft written, 3:10 a.m. Copy shop receipt, 4:22 a.m.—she kept receipts, she kept everything, that was the disease and the gift of her. Letter mailed at the collection box outside the post office, 4:40 a.m., the pickup flag already up because the truck came at five. So if she had mailed it, then the tip-line letter was a *copy*. Someone had gotten hold of her draft or her printed original and produced a second version, hand-delivered twelve hours later, stripped of anything that might trace back to a mailing.

If she had mailed it. If.

There was a third possibility, and she made herself look at it straight on, because looking away was how people died in the stories her grandmother used to tell: the possibility that she had deleted the draft, mailed nothing, and someone had *written the letter themselves*—someone who knew the connection, knew her style well enough to imitate it, and wanted it in Voss's hands wearing her fingerprints. Someone inside Harborline. Someone who had also been inside her locker.

Two intrusions in ten days. Locker and letter. Not random. A sequence. First they read her; then they spoke as her. Next they would—

Next they would do whatever people did when they finished building a false version of you and wanted the real one out of the way.

* * *

She went to the library when it opened, because the library was the one public place where sitting alone for hours read as virtue rather than suspicion, and because Mrs. Adeyemi at the reference desk asked no questions and answered all of them. Mara requested the microfilm of the *Gull Harbor Sentinel*, 1968, the obituary week, ostensibly to continue her research into Hale's reinterment. What she actually fed through the reader, frame by frame, was the society pages, and what she was looking for was faces.

Ruth Hale appeared twice—a young woman in a pale coat at the centennial planning meeting, sharp-featured, unsmiling, the family jaw visible even in newsprint. Mara studied her the way she'd study an unfamiliar vehicle on a route map: noting capabilities, likely destinations. The great-granddaughter who had commissioned the exhumation. Who had expected, if the rumors at the depot were true, to find something in that coffin besides ballast and ceremony.

But it was the third photograph that stopped her breath.

It ran beside a piece about the lighthouse benefaction, 1967, black-and-white and soft with age: August Hale at the harbor railing, mid-laugh, one hand raised in some gesture of address—and behind him, half-turned toward the camera, a young man in a work jacket holding a clipboard. Young, broad, squinting against the sun. The caption named only Hale and the alderman. But Mara had spent fourteen months memorizing the faces that moved through Harborline Dispatch, and the face above the work jacket was a younger version of one she saw every Tuesday and Friday when the supervisor came in to countersign the logs.

Okafor. Gerald Okafor, whose father had worked the docks in the fifties and sixties—everyone knew that much, it was depot lore, the old man had driven one of Harborline's first cabs before moving to the boatyard. Whose handwriting had gone small and cramped lately. Whose office light, she realized now, thinking back through weeks of it with forensic patience, did not always go dark at ten. Some nights it burned past midnight, and she had filed those nights under *paperwork* because filing things was what she did instead of feeling them.

He had been at the railing in 1967, twenty years old, at August Hale's shoulder. He was fifty-some now, and he had spent his whole life inside the company Hale built the year after the boy drowned.

Mara shut off the microfilm reader and sat in the machine's after-hum, watching dust turn in the light from the high windows, and let the shape of it assemble itself: a man guarding something older than his own career, finding her name—or her style, or her silence—woven through the one story that could bury his father's memory alongside Hale's marble one. A man with a master key ring on his belt and a reason to read her mail.

Or not Okafor at all. That was the poison of it. Every conclusion she reached now had a twin, a shadow-conclusion, and she could not tell which one cast which shadow. Paranoia, her grandmother's letters had called it once, in a lighter decade—*the tax you pay for knowing too much.* Wei had known too much too; it was practically the family inheritance.

Her phone buzzed against the table. A text from a number with no name attached, because she saved no names:

*Tip line got another one this morning. Same hand. This one mentions "the dispatcher who knows." Voss is pulling Harborline's personnel files.*

Mara stared at it for a long moment. Ruth. It had to be Ruth—they had traded numbers at the flats, cautiously, two women circling the same fire. Which meant Ruth was watching the tip line the way Mara watched the scanner, and the net was closing from both ends.

*The dispatcher who knows.*

Not her name. Not yet. But a name was only ever one records request away, and Voss was pulling files, and somewhere in a green steel locker in the dispatch bay her secrets sat folded in quarters behind a combination that had apparently never been a secret at all.

She gathered her coat. In the microfilm room's dimness she caught her reflection in the darkened screen—gray woman, gray coat, face like a closed ledger—and for the first time in her life she understood that invisibility was not a shield. It was a hiding place, and hiding places get searched.

Outside, the fog was burning off the marina, and the tide was going out, and she had eleven hours before her shift began—eleven hours to decide whether to walk back into that bay and pretend nothing had changed, or to change the game entirely.

She was still deciding when her phone buzzed again.

This time it was the dispatch line itself. Okafor's number.

"Mara." His voice was careful, courteous, wrong. "Come in early tonight, would you? There's something we should talk about. About your grandmother."
