## 4. The Anniversary Dinner

The invitation arrived on cream cardstock, embossed with the Ashcombe crest—a stag's head over crossed antlers, which struck me as a family that had never quite understood how heraldry worked—and I held it up to the light like a jeweler checking for flaws. There were none. That was the first thing worth noting about the Ashcombes: they did not do flaws. They did not do typos, or chipped china, or grief that hadn't been rehearsed at least twice in front of a mirror.

*Mr. and Mrs. Edmund Ashcombe request the pleasure of your company at the fiftieth annual commemoration of the founding of Ashcombe House.*

Fifty years. Which meant, if my math was right—and my math was always right when it mattered—the dinner had been held every August since 1974. Since the year Lila Voss walked out of the service entrance with her coat over her arm and was never seen again.

They'd thrown a party every single year since she vanished. I turned the card over. On the back, in smaller type: *Press credentials available upon application.*

Well. That was either an oversight or an invitation to a different kind of table.

I applied. Three days later, a laminated badge arrived with my name spelled correctly—Rosalind Nystrom, *Hearth & Heritage Quarterly*, a publication I'm fairly confident does not exist outside a Gmail account and a Canva template. The Ashcombes' people hadn't checked. Or they had checked, and didn't care, and that possibility sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone all the way down the long gravel drive.

* * *

Ashcombe House announced itself before you saw it. The drive curved through two miles of beech and chestnut, and then the trees simply gave up, as if even they knew better than to crowd the place, and there it was: honeyed limestone, three stories, wings stretching out on either side like a man spreading his arms to say *everything you see is mine.* It was beautiful the way a shark is beautiful. You admired it while understanding, somewhere below language, that it was built for one purpose only.

A valet took my car. A footman took my coat. A woman with a clipboard and the smile of someone paid by the hour took my press badge, examined it for exactly one second too long, and handed it back.

"Ms. Nystrom. Welcome to Ashcombe." Her eyes flicked up to mine, then away. "You'll want to stay out of the east wing. Restoration work."

"Of course," I said, and made a mental note to find out what was being restored in the east wing, because nobody ropes off a wing for restoration two weeks before their biggest social event unless they're restoring something they don't want photographed.

The great hall had been dressed for the occasion. Candles everywhere—real ones, which in a house this old said either confidence or contempt for the fire brigade. Round tables fanned out across the parquet, each set with white linen and crystal that caught the candlelight and threw it around like gossip. A string quartet played something baroque and bloodless in the corner. And everywhere, everywhere, were photographs.

That was the thing I hadn't expected. Framed images lined the hall on easels: the Ashcombes through the decades, stiff and glossy, the founder—Reginald, dead now these twenty years—looking like a man who ironed his face each morning. His son Edmund appeared in the later photos, growing from a sulky boy into a silver-haired man with his father's jaw and none of his warmth, if the old man had ever had any to begin with.

And in the earliest photograph, taken in the summer of '74, there she was.

Lila Voss stood at the edge of the frame, half-cropped, holding a tray of champagne flutes. She wasn't looking at the camera. She was looking at something just outside it—something to the left, near the terrace doors—with an expression I recognized because I'd spent four months staring at her payroll signature until I could have picked it out of a police lineup. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was the look of a person doing arithmetic. Counting exits.

Someone had chosen to include that photo. Someone had looked at fifty years of family history and decided the maid belonged in it, cropped to the shoulder, frozen mid-glance at a door.

I wondered which Ashcombe had made that choice, and what they thought they were saying by making it.

* * *

"Ms. Nystrom, isn't it?"

He materialized the way old money always does—not arriving so much as having already been there, waiting for you to notice. Edmund Ashcombe was seventy-something and wore it like a tailored coat: silver hair swept back, a face that had decided sometime in the eighties to stop moving, a glass of something amber held loosely at his side as though he'd forgotten it existed.

"Mr. Ashcombe. Thank you for the invitation."

"Thank *you* for coming." His smile was warm the way a heating vent is warm—you felt it without believing it came from anywhere alive. "We don't get many of your kind anymore. Print is dying, they tell me."

"They've been telling me that since I started. I've stopped taking it personally."

A laugh, brief and correct. He gestured toward the photographs with his glass. "You'll forgive the display. My mother started the tradition. She believed a house should remember itself."

"A house that remembers itself," I said. "Most people struggle to remember themselves."

"Don't they just." He studied me for a moment, and I felt the quality of the attention change—shift gears, like a car dropping from cruising speed into something lower and more deliberate. "Rosalind Nystrom. That's an unusual name. Scandinavian?"

"My grandfather's. He came over from Gothenburg with nothing but a suitcase and a suspicion of banks."

"And what brings a"—a fractional pause, the length of a heartbeat, no more—"heritage writer to a commemoration dinner? We're hardly heritage. We're just old."

"Just old" was possibly the most dishonest sentence spoken in that room that evening, and the room included a eulogy later, so that was saying something.

"I write about houses and the people history forgot," I said. "Servants, gardeners, cooks. The people who kept the place running while the portraits got painted. Your own staff photograph from '74 has eleven faces in it, Mr. Ashcombe, and I'd wager your family could name maybe three."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not anger—I'd have preferred anger. Anger is information you can use. This was closer to a door closing quietly in a distant room.

"Lila Voss," he said.

My pulse did something unprofessional. "Excuse me?"

"The maid. In the photograph. The one everyone forgets." He swirled his drink. "She left suddenly, that summer. My father was terribly put out—good staff were hard to keep, even then. You'll want to speak to Eleanor, I expect. Her sister. She was here briefly, after."

The floor did not tilt, though it considered it. *Eleanor.* The name from the ledger margins, the payments that ran for years after Lila vanished, the sister listed as deceased in 1975. He said the name like a man laying a card face-up on the table to see what I'd do.

"I didn't realize there was a sister," I said, keeping my voice at the temperature of the punch.

"Mm. Sad story. Left the area years ago. Lost touch entirely." He smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "But then, people leave Ashcombe all the time. They always have. It's a big house. Easy to get lost in."

The quartet finished its piece. The silence between movements lasted half a second too long, and in that half-second I understood something with total clarity: Edmund Ashcombe had known who I was before I walked through the door. Not the invented magazine. Not the press badge. *Me.* The podcaster. The woman asking questions about Lila Voss in public, episode by episode, week by week.

And instead of having me removed—which he could certainly have done—he'd invited me to dinner, handed me a name I shouldn't have known he knew, and told me, in the politest possible English, that people disappear here.

It was either a warning or bait. With men like Edmund Ashcombe, I suspected the distinction was academic.

"If you'll excuse me," I said, "I should mingle. It's a lovely event."

"It is," he agreed. "Do enjoy yourself, Ms. Nystrom. And do eat something. The kitchen has been with us thirty years—they'd be wounded otherwise." He raised his glass a fraction. "We're very loyal to our people here. And they to us. It's rather the whole point of the evening."

* * *

I mingled. I ate something—it was excellent, some kind of lamb, and I resented it for being excellent, because I wanted to hate everything in that house and the lamb refused to cooperate. I spoke to a county councilman who talked for nine minutes about drainage. I spoke to an elderly cousin who remembered Reginald as "strict but fair," which is what people say about men who were neither. I watched the family perform grief.

Because that's what the commemoration was, once you scraped off the candles. At eight o'clock, Edmund's wife—Margaret, serene and diamonded—rose and gave a toast to "the generations who built this house and those who keep its spirit." The guests raised their glasses. The quartet resumed. And the whole ritual moved with the smoothness of something performed annually, identically, for fifty years, a liturgy with the emotion sanded off it. Nobody cried. Nobody stumbled. Grief, at Ashcombe, was a choreography, and everyone knew the steps.

Except that the toast wasn't for the dead founder. Listen closely and Margaret's words were elastic enough to cover anything—to cover a missing maid, a missing business partner whose name appeared nowhere in any archive I'd searched, whatever else the house needed its ghosts to be. A commemoration dinner is a marvelous invention. It lets you honor the past without ever specifying which part of it.

I found myself standing near the terrace doors—the same doors Lila had been looking at in the photograph, because of course I'd checked, because I am nothing if not thorough in the least healthy way. Beyond the glass, the lawn fell away toward the tree line, and beyond the tree line, invisible in the dark, the lake. The lake where, according to a ledger entry I hadn't yet decoded, a delivery of "masonry supplies" had been billed in September of 1974 to a boathouse that contained no masonry.

"You're a long way from the canapés."

Tom—the gardener's grandson, though I wouldn't learn that for weeks yet—wasn't there. Instead it was a young woman in staff livery, refilling water glasses, watching me watch the dark.

"Just getting air," I said.

"There's air by the fountain too," she said pleasantly. "Nearer the lights." And she moved on, and I stood there feeling the shape of the sentence she'd left behind, which was: *we see you, and we'd prefer you inside, where we can count you.*

I went inside. I am brave, but I am brave in increments, and the increments were telling me to eat dessert.

* * *

Dessert was a lemon tart I also resented. Coffee followed. And coffee meant the end was near, which meant it was time for the thing I'd actually come to do.

The guest cloakroom was off the east corridor. The east wing—"restoration work," remember—branched off the east corridor. While the string quartet sawed through Vivaldi and the councilman explained culverts to a visibly dying woman, I slipped down the corridor under the universal camouflage of a woman looking for the ladies' room.

The east wing door was locked. Of course it was. But the corridor beside it held the household offices—housekeeper's room, still room, and a small panelled study where, through a gap in curtains that hadn't been drawn properly, I could see a desk. On the desk, a ledger. An actual, physical, leather-bound ledger, open, with a lamp burning over it at ten o'clock at night during a party.

I didn't go in. I want that on the record. Whatever else I am, I was not going to commit breaking and entering at a dinner where the host had already told me, twice, in two different grammatical constructions, that people who wander off at Ashcombe tend to stay wandered.

But I stood in that dark corridor for perhaps ninety seconds, close enough to smell the furniture polish and the old paper, and I memorized what I could see of the open page. Columns. Dates. Initials. And at the top of the page, in faded brown ink, a heading that made my breath go thin:

*Wages & Disbursements — Michaelmas 1974.*

September 1974. The month after Lila Voss walked out the service door. The month the masonry supplies went to the boathouse.

From the ballroom, the quartet began something new—Mozart, maybe, or a very convincing impersonation—and above the music I heard footsteps in the corridor behind me, unhurried, well-shod, and closing.

I turned, arranging my face into pleasant, harmless inquiry, and found Edmund Ashcombe standing at the end of the passage with a candle sconce throwing his shadow long across the floorboards, and he was smiling.

"There you are," he said warmly. "I was beginning to think you'd gotten lost."

And the terrible thing—the thing I'd turn over at three in the morning for weeks afterward—was that he didn't sound like a man catching an intruder.

He sounded like a host confirming his guest list.
