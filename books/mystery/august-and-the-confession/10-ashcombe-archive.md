## 10. Ashcombe Archive

The invitation came on cream cardstock with a wax seal, which is the traditional way of saying *we have more money than God and slightly better manners*. *The Ashcombe Estate cordially requests the pleasure of your company at its Annual Heritage Charity Tour*, followed by a list of trustees whose names read like a law firm that bills by the syllable.

I'd been on their radar since episode one. They knew I was coming; I knew they knew. The whole thing had the choreography of a duel conducted over canapés. You don't refuse an invitation like that. You press your best blouse, charge your camera—no, wait, cameras were forbidden, which is why I brought two—and you go.

"Charity tours are how old money launders its conscience," Dev said when I told him. He was eating cold noodles out of a takeout box at nine in the morning, which is either a cry for help or a lifestyle. "You get to walk through their beautiful rooms and feel grateful for it."

"I'm not going to feel grateful. I'm going to feel around."

"For what?"

"Smoke," I said, and he looked at me like I'd finally lost it, which was fair, because I hadn't explained about the smell yet.

Here's what I hadn't told him. Two nights earlier, I'd driven out to the estate's perimeter fence—the public road runs within sight of the east lawn, close enough to see the chimneys—and I'd sat there in the dark with my engine off, doing nothing illegal except thinking. And the wind had come off the house carrying something I recognized from my grandmother's woodstove: paper smoke. Not leaves. Not oak. Paper burns sweet and acrid, with an undertone like burnt hair, and there is no fireplace season in August.

Somebody at Ashcombe had been burning documents. In summer. At night.

Old houses keep archives the way sharks keep teeth—constantly, and in rows you don't want to inspect too closely. Ledgers, correspondence, staff records, legal papers, all of it filed away by generations of people who believed that paper was power and throwing it away was for peasants. If Lila Voss's name appeared anywhere outside a missing-persons file, it would be in that archive. And if someone was burning files, then whatever remained was exactly what they wanted me—or worse, the police—to never see.

So: the charity tour. Forty pounds a ticket, proceeds to the restoration of the orangery, and one podcaster with a handbag full of contraband.

* * *

The Ashcombe estate in daylight is a masterclass in controlled decay. The house is Georgian, honey-colored stone gone soft at the edges, with a portico supported by columns that have been repointed so expertly you can't tell which ones are lying about their age. The gardens were designed by someone whose name appears in guidebooks, and the lawns rolled down toward the lake—that lake—with the serene confidence of a landscape that has never once been asked to explain itself.

Our guide was a woman named Mrs. Calloway, silver-haired, tweed-skirted, with the crisp diction of someone who has spent forty years telling people not to touch things. There were fourteen of us on the tour. I counted twice. Retired couples, a pair of architecture students, a man who asked three separate questions about drainage, and me, wearing the expression of a woman deeply interested in plasterwork.

"The family has lived here since 1789," Mrs. Calloway announced, leading us through the great hall, where portraits of Ashcombes hung in chronological judgment. "The current Mr. Ashcombe—you may know him from his charitable work—is the fifth of his name."

I did know him from his charitable work. Edmund Ashcombe the Fifth, sixty-eight years old, chairman of four boards, donor of two hospital wings, and—as established in chapter seventeen of my research notes, currently unwritten—a man who inherited a cover-up along with the silver. He wasn't on the tour himself. He was, according to Mrs. Calloway, "abroad." Which meant either Geneva or the boathouse, depending on how much you trusted the word *abroad*.

We saw the ballroom, where the 1974 anniversary dinner had been held. We saw the library, twenty feet of shelving and a globe on a stand that opened to reveal decanters, because of course it did. And we saw, through a roped doorway we were explicitly told not to enter, a corridor lined with flat filing cabinets and document boxes stacked to shoulder height.

"What's through there?" asked the drainage man.

"The estate archive," Mrs. Calloway said, with the smoothness of long practice. "Household records, mostly. Nothing of interest to visitors, I'm afraid."

Nothing of interest. To visitors. Three words, each one doing honest work.

I lingered at the rope longer than politeness allowed, and Mrs. Calloway materialized beside me the way only career guides can, without appearing to move. "The orangery is this way, miss. The camellias are spectacular in August."

They were not spectacular. Camellias bloom in spring; these were green lumps in pots. But I thanked her and went, because the first rule of infiltration is that you never argue with the person holding the keys.

* * *

Lunch was served on the south terrace—quiche, salad, elderflower cordial, the cuisine of institutions that consider flavor a form of showing off. I ate half of it and spent the other half mapping.

Here is what forty minutes of loitering had taught me: the archive corridor ran behind the library, accessible from two doors—one roped, one marked PRIVATE—and ventilated, judging by the small high windows I could see from the garden path, by openings above the north wall. Those windows faced a service yard screened by yew hedges. And service yards, in my experience, have doors that are locked less often than anyone admits, because staff need to carry things.

The tour ended at four. The guests dispersed to their cars. Mrs. Calloway stood on the steps accepting compliments like tribute.

I had told Dev I'd be home by six. I had also told him, in the same breath, not to wait up, and he knows me well enough to understand that sentence as a weather report: conditions likely to change.

I left my car in the village lot, walked back along the footpath that skirts the estate's boundary wall—a right of way, posted with signs trying very hard to suggest otherwise—and let myself into the yew-screened service yard at ten past five, when the light was still good but the staff had thinned to whoever drew the evening shift.

The door into the undercroft was unlocked. I want that noted for posterity: I did not break anything. I walked through an open door into a cool stone passage that smelled of floor wax and, faintly but unmistakably, of that same sweet char I'd caught from the roadside. Recent. Days old, maybe less. It clung to the walls near a metal bin that had been emptied recently enough to leave a gray ghost-ring of ash on the flagstones.

Someone had burned paper *here*, indoors, in a bin, and then swept up. That's not tidiness. That's procedure.

The archive itself was everything I'd hoped and dreaded: order imposed by people who expected to live forever. Boxes labeled by decade in fading copperplate. Flat drawers of maps. A card catalog, actual cards, cataloging actual sins. I put on the cotton gloves I'd bought for exactly this moment—they sell them at the museum shop, which should tell you something about my life—and got to work.

Staff ledgers first. The household accounts ran in unbroken volumes from 1902 to the present, which is the kind of continuity that makes historians weep and murderers nervous. I found 1974 between a volume bound in green cloth and one in brown, and there it was, the week of the anniversary dinner, itemized in a bookkeeper's immaculate hand:

*Extra staff, dinner service — 14 heads.*
*Wine, cellar — 6 doz.*
*Lila Voss, wages — £11 4s.*

And then, dated eleven days after her disappearance, in the same hand:

*E.V., consideration — £50.*
*E.V., consideration — £25 (monthly, until further notice).*

My hands were not steady. I'll admit that. Fifty pounds in 1974 was nearly a month's wages for a maid, paid out to initials, eleven days after she vanished, followed by monthly payments *until further notice*. Until further notice turned out to mean March 1975, when the entries stopped—not because the money stopped, I realized as I flipped forward, but because the ledger changed format. From 1975 onward, the payments continued in a slimmer volume kept separately, filed under *Legal & Personal*, and those entries used full names.

I photographed everything. Phone camera, silent shutter, gloved hands, the works. Page after page of the household ledger, then the slim black volume from *Legal & Personal*, 1975, and there—on the third page, in ink gone brown with age—

*Eleanor Voss (d.), care of Halloway & Finch, Solicitors — £40 monthly.*

Eleanor Voss. Deceased. In parentheses, like a footnote, like an afterthought, like a clerical convenience. Listed as dead in 1975 and yet receiving payments routed through solicitors for—I turned pages, faster now—1976, 1977, 1980, 1988, the entries marching on year after year, a dead woman drawing a salary from the Ashcombe estate for decades.

Except Eleanor Voss wasn't dead. I knew that the way you know a thing in your spine before your brain catches up. Because Lila's diary—the real diary, sitting in a bank vault forty miles away—had mentioned her sister exactly once, in an entry from June 1974: *Ellie says I laugh too loud for service. She's probably right.* Present tense. Alive. And the anonymous letter, the one quoting phrases from pages no one should have seen, had been postmarked from Millbrook—which is two counties over, which is where I'd already pulled one Eleanor Voss from the electoral roll, age eighty-one, widow, no criminal record, no obituary, no grave.

A woman declared dead in 1975 does not collect wages for fifty years unless someone very powerful wants her invisible. And a woman who is invisible, and frightened, and watching a podcaster poke the hornet's nest on national audio platforms—well. A woman like that might write letters. Might quote diary pages she'd have known by heart, because her sister wrote them.

The pieces didn't just fit. They clicked, like a lock giving way.

I photographed the last page of the slim volume too, and it was there that the triumph curdled. The final entry was dated 2019—*E.V., final settlement, per instruction*—and beneath it, newer ink, a different hand, a single line: *Account closed. File retained.*

File retained. Except the air in the archive still smelled of burning, and the ash ring on the flagstones downstairs was fresh, and somewhere in this house there was a fire grate or a bin or a chimney flue that had recently eaten paper. Maybe the file was retained. Maybe what burned was everything *around* the file—the context, the corroborating pages, the entries that would have made my photographs legible to a prosecutor instead of just to me.

Or maybe what burned was proof of something worse. The partner who vanished in 1974 had never been found. Neither, officially, had anything else.

I was crouched over the 1980s volumes, photographing a run of payments that coincided—my stomach noted, without being asked—with the years Tom's grandfather worked the grounds, when I heard it.

Footsteps. Not on the flagstones below—in the corridor above, the roped one, moving slowly, with the unhurried gait of someone who owns the building and expects it to cooperate. Then a door. Then silence, the loaded kind.

I killed my phone screen and stood very still among the boxes, breathing through my mouth, listening to dust settle. Thirty seconds. A minute. The footsteps resumed, crossed overhead, faded toward the front of the house. A key turned in a lock—somewhere ahead of me, not behind. The PRIVATE door, maybe. Locking the archive corridor from the inside, the way you do when you're putting something away for the night.

Which meant the way I'd come in might be the only way out that wasn't watched.

I gave it five full minutes, then gathered my gloves, my phone, my nerve, and eased back down the undercroft passage, past the swept ash, through the unlocked door, across the yew-shadowed yard, over the wall, and onto the footpath with my heart going like a telegraph wire. Only when I reached the village lot did I let myself look at what I'd taken.

Forty-one photographs. Ledgers, payments, initials, names. A dead woman alive on every page.

And one photograph I didn't remember taking—taken, I realized, in my first fumbling minute before the gloves, when I'd shot a quick frame of the room itself to orient myself. In the corner of that frame, on top of a stack of boxes nearest the PRIVATE door, sat a single document box with a label in fresh marker, the ink still dark, the handwriting neat and modern:

*V. — personal effects — DO NOT DISCARD.*

Not *Voss*. Just *V*. As if even the label was afraid to say the whole name.

As if someone in this house had been keeping Lila's things—her things, *personal effects*—close at hand, for fifty years, in a box they couldn't quite bring themselves to burn.

I sat in my car in the emptying lot, engine off, and stared at that photograph until the screen dimmed. Then I called Dev.

"Tell me you got something," he said.

"I got a dead woman's pay stubs," I said. "And I think I know where Lila Voss's sister is hiding. Has been hiding. Since before I was born."

A pause. Noodles, presumably, abandoned. "Rosalind. If Eleanor Voss has been alive this whole time—"

"Then she's the last living link to 1974," I said. "And somebody at Ashcombe just locked an archive door behind them, and the air in there smells like smoke. Whatever they're cleaning up, they're doing it *now*. I need to get to Millbrook tonight."

"Tonight? Roz, it's—"

"Dev." I watched the sun drop behind the estate's tree line, gilding the chimneys, the lake, the whole beautiful lying face of the place. "She opened the door before I knocked once already. I'd rather not find out what happens the second time I'm late."
