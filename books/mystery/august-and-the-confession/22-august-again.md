## 22. August, Again

A year is a strange unit of measurement for grief. Too long to feel fresh, too short to feel finished. I've learned this the way I learn most things—by accident, at inconvenient moments, usually in a cemetery.

The one outside Harrowgate is small and tidy, the kind of churchyard where the grass gets cut by volunteers and the headstones lean like old men comparing aches. Eleanor Voss walked ahead of me down the gravel path, her cane ticking against the stones in a rhythm I'd come to think of as hers alone: tap, tap, pause. Tap, tap, pause. She'd told me once it was how she kept time when there was no clock worth trusting.

She stopped at a flat granite marker near the yew tree. No name. No dates. Just a rectangle of stone flush with the earth, purchased fifty years ago by a woman who understood that the safest grave is the one nobody looks for.

"She picked this spot herself," Eleanor said. "In a letter. 1976, it would have been. The last one." Her voice was steady the way a frozen lake is steady—you could walk on it if you were careful and light. "She said she wanted to be somewhere with a view of the water but not too close. She'd had her fill of water."

I didn't say anything. There's a version of me—the version from four years ago, the one who launched season four of *The Cold File* with a monologue about ambition and the oldest unsolved disappearance on record—who would have had a microphone out already. That woman treated grief like an unsecured crime scene: something you could move through freely as long as you didn't disturb the evidence. I've spent a year trying to make my peace with her, and mostly failing.

"Margaret Hale," Eleanor said now, testing the name aloud like a key in a lock she hadn't opened in a while. "That was her, at the end. Margaret Hale of Inverness. Church on Sundays, not because she believed but because she liked the singing. Made a chutney that could strip paint. Volunteered at the library." A pause. "She read mysteries. Can you imagine. Fifty years hiding from one and she read them like other women eat sweets."

"I can imagine," I said. "I'm told I'm one."

Eleanor laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped custody. Then she crouched, slowly, one hand braced on the cane, and laid something on the stone. A sprig of rosemary. And beside it, a hotel key tag, brass, worn smooth, stamped with a number I knew better than my own birthday: *Room 9.*

Lila's room. The one she'd been turning down the night everything changed.

"You kept that?" I asked.

"Fifty-one years," Eleanor said. "It was in her locker when they cleared it out. I signed for it. First lie I ever told on official paper—I said I was family." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and fierce and very old. "Turns out I wasn't lying after all. Just early."

* * *

We stood there a long time. Long enough that a robin got bored of us and left. Long enough for the August heat to press its thumb against the back of my neck and remind me what month it was—what week, almost to the day. The anniversary dinner had been held two nights ago at Ashcombe, and for the first time in fifty years, nobody had raised a glass to a lie.

I'd watched it happen from the edge of the marquee, not as an infiltrator this time but as something harder to define. A witness, maybe. Or a guest. Edmund Ashcombe—the last living conspirator, the inheritor of his parents' erasures—was still awaiting trial, remanded in a facility where he reportedly complained about the coffee. Tom had been promoted to head gardener, which felt less like a reward than a coronation; he walked those grounds now like a man who'd finally been introduced to his own grandfather. And the estate itself—God help it—was being converted into a heritage trust, its ledgers and account books open to researchers, its private archive lit for the first time by anything other than secrecy and a single smoking bulb.

Rosalind Nystrom, celebrated novelist and true-crime podcaster, had been invited to consult. I'd said yes before I understood why. I understand now. It's the same reason I'm standing in a churchyard holding a sprig of rosemary I bought at a petrol station, because Eleanor forgot hers and refused to turn around.

You don't get to choose what a story does to you. You only get to choose what you do next.

"So," Eleanor said, straightening up with the careful economy of the very old. "You're starting again. The podcast."

"How did you—"

"Tom mentioned it. He listens now. Says your voice sounds less angry." She gave me a sidelong look. "He says it like that's an improvement. I haven't decided."

"It might be," I admitted. "Or it might just be tiredness wearing a nicer coat."

"And what will you chase this time? Another body in a lake? Another family with good tailoring and worse secrets?"

"No." I'd turned the question over all winter, through the trial coverage and the book deal offers and the three a.m. sessions with my producer Dev, who had learned to recognize the look on my face that meant I was about to ruin our download numbers on principle. "No more cold cases. Not like that. Not—" I gestured vaguely at the yew tree, the flat stone, the whole apparatus of disappearance. "Not hunting. I keep thinking about what Lila wrote in the last entry. *Survival was always the confession.* Everyone in that story survived by becoming someone else. The maid became a ghost. The sister became a widow. The partner became a runaway. Even Mrs. Ashcombe became a grieving hostess and performed it so well her own son believed it."

"And?"

"And nobody ever got their names back. Except Lila, and only because we dug." I crouched down myself, knees complaining, and pressed my palm flat against the cool granite. "There are so many of them, Eleanor. The invisible ones. The maids and groundskeepers and kitchen girls who saw things and were never asked. The people in the margins of other people's ledgers. My whole career I treated them as sources. Evidence with pulse rates." I stood. "Season five isn't about finding the guilty. It's about finding the erased. Giving them their names back while they can still hear it—or their children can."

Eleanor was quiet for a moment. The cane ticked once against the gravel, an involuntary metronome.

"Do you know what she'd say to that?" Eleanor asked.

"Telling me off?"

"Thanking you." Eleanor's voice caught, just slightly, on the word. "In that letter that came through the poste restante—the one you read on air—she wrote that whoever finally asked the right questions should know it mattered. Not the answers. The asking." She touched my arm, briefly, with fingers like paper. "You asked, Rosalind. Late, clumsy, half-wrong much of the time—but you asked. Most people never even wonder about the woman who made the beds."

* * *

That evening I drove back along the coast road with the windows down, letting the salt air wreck whatever remained of my composure, and thought about beginnings.

Four years ago I'd sat in a rented studio with a script I'd rehearsed until it shone, and opened season four with these words: *"Some disappearances are accidents. Some are crimes. And some are performances—and the audience doesn't know it's watching one until the curtain comes down fifty years later."* Dev had loved it. Said it was hungry. He'd meant it as a compliment.

I'd been hungry, then. Hungry the way a fire is hungry, indiscriminate and self-justifying. The postman died because I was hungry. Tom risked his inheritance because I was hungry. Eleanor spent a year looking over her shoulder because a stranger's ambition had come knocking on her door with a recorder running. I've stopped pretending otherwise. Mercy, I learned—from Lila, from Eleanor, from the whole terrible architecture of that summer—isn't the absence of justice. It's justice that remembers the cost.

But renewal isn't amnesia either. I'm not going to stand here in my own epilogue and claim I've been cleansed. I still check the locks twice. I still flinch when a car idles too long outside my flat. I still have the warning note—*some glasses should stay raised*—in a drawer, because throwing it away felt like pretending the fear hadn't taught me anything, and it had. It taught me exactly what I refuse to do to anyone else.

So: season five. We're calling it *The Margins.* Dev fought the title for a week—he wanted something punchier, something with *Blood* or *Buried* in it—and then he listened to the first episode's rough cut and went quiet in that way he does, which is his version of surrender.

The first subject is a woman named Ada Kessler, a scullery maid at a country house in 1931 who testified at an inquest and then vanished from every record afterward. Nobody murdered her. Nobody staged anything. She just... slipped through, the way poor women do, into a life no archivist bothered to write down. We found her granddaughter in Manchester. She has photographs. She has a christening gown. She has a story that has never once been anyone's lead item, and next Thursday, at six p.m., it will be mine to tell—not as evidence, not as leverage, but as a name spoken back into the world.

Ada Kessler. Say it once and it stops being nothing.

* * *

Before I left Harrowgate, Eleanor walked me to my car. The churchyard was going gold at the edges, that particular August light that makes even gravestones look forgiving.

"One more thing," she said, and produced an envelope from her handbag. Old paper, soft at the corners. My name on it in handwriting I recognized from a diary I'd spent a year inside of. "This came for you. Forwarded twice—it took its time. She wrote it in 2018, a year before she died. She said she was writing it to whoever asked, whenever they asked, and that she hoped they wouldn't mind waiting."

My hands weren't entirely steady. "Have you read it?"

"Of course not." A pause. The ghost of a smile. "I've had fifty years of practicing patience. I wasn't going to fail the final exam."

I waited until I was home. Waited until dark, actually, with tea going cold beside me, because some letters deserve ceremony and I've become a person who believes that now.

It was short. Lila—or Margaret, or whichever name she was wearing when she wrote it—had beautiful, economical handwriting, the penmanship of someone who learned to write in service and never wasted a stroke.

*Rosalind,* it began, and I confess that seeing my name in her hand undid me more than any revelation of that entire year.

*If you are reading this, then someone finally followed the numbers in the margins, and I hope it was you, because you seemed like the sort who would feel bad about it afterwards, and that is the best quality a person can have.*

*I want you to know I was not brave. I was frightened every day for fifty years, and I hid anyway, and I want the girl who reads this someday—there is always a girl—to understand that frightened and gone are not the same thing. I stayed. I stayed in the only way I could.*

*Tell my sister she kept her promise. Tell the boy with the garden he is forgiven for being born late. And tell yourself, whoever you are when you find this: the questions were enough. You don't need the whole truth to do right by it. Nobody ever has.*

*August again. It always was my month.*

*L.*

I sat with it for a long time. Then I did the thing I've decided is my job now, the whole job, the only part worth keeping: I took out my recorder, set it on the table, and spoke her name into it—both of them, all of them, every name she'd earned or chosen or been denied—so that somewhere in the archive of the world, in the margins of somebody else's ledger, a maid from Room 9 exists in her own handwriting at last.

Then I opened a blank page, and began.

* * *

Season five premieres Thursday. But tonight, if you'll allow me one small vanity before the work resumes—tonight I'm just a woman in a kitchen with a cold cup of tea and a letter from the dead, listening to the robin on the sill, thinking that August, after everything, might be my month too.
