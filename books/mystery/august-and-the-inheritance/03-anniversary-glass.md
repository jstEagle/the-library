## 3. Anniversary Glass

The Halvorsen house wore its forty years like a costume it had outgrown. Chandeliers too bright, silver polished to a nervous shine, and everywhere the smell of lilies laid over something older — dust, or memory, or fear. Yara Marchetti stood at the edge of the dining room in a charcoal dress that read as *professional* and *forgettable*, which was the point. Estate counsel. A woman with a folder and no face.

Forty-one guests. She had counted twice.

The table stretched the length of the ballroom, set for the family and their nearest ghosts: business partners, a retired judge, cousins who hadn't spoken since the last funeral. At the head sat Magnus Halvorsen, seventy-three years old, robust as an oak that refused to fall, laughing at his own jokes. Beside him, his wife Ingrid. Down the table, scattered like pieces on a board, the children — Elias pale and sweating at position nine, exactly where Yara had told him to sit; Astrid, the eldest, all teeth and charm; Henrik, quiet, watchful, pouring wine for everyone but himself.

And at the far end, near the service door, Lena Voss. Ingrid's sister's daughter. The poor relation. The one who had spent thirty years being thanked and never once thanked properly. She had organized this dinner down to the fold of the napkins. She had also, if Yara's reading of her was right, organized three deaths before it — each one filed away neatly, each file left open on purpose, like a signature.

Yara had found the pattern in the paperwork, which was fitting. Everything about Lena Voss lived in paperwork.

* * *

The toast glasses came out at half past eight.

Yara had been in the butler's pantry an hour earlier, under the pretext of reviewing the trust documents "before the announcement." The household staff moved around her as though she were furniture, which she encouraged. And there they were: two crystal flutes set apart from the others on a silver tray, identical to every other glass in the room except to the eye that knew what to look for. A faint amber film inside the bowl, dried at the meniscus line. Something fat-soluble, slow, tasteless in wine. It would take Magnus Halvorsen somewhere between the toast and midnight, and by then he'd be alone in his study, and by morning it would be his heart, and by afternoon it would be a closed file with a small annotation in the margin.

She had swapped them. Two clean flutes from the same cabinet, same etching, same weight. The poisoned pair now sat wrapped in a linen napkin in her handbag, sealed in a glass jar because she did not take chances with other people's chemistry. Her hands had not shaken. That frightened her more than anything else — how easily her hands did what they did.

Now she watched the tray make its circuit. Lena Voss carried it herself. Of course she did. She moved through the room like water finding its level, smiling, dispensing glasses, saving the two special ones for the head of the table. Yara tracked her the way she'd learned to track everything: peripherally, patiently, letting the room tell its story.

Lena reached the head of the table. Set one flute before Magnus. One before Ingrid.

Then she stopped.

It was very small — the pause. Half a second, maybe less. But Yara had built a career on half-seconds, and she saw Lena's thumb move against the stem of Ingrid's glass, saw her tilt it a degree toward the light, saw the exact moment the arithmetic behind her eyes failed. The film should have been there. The film was not there.

Lena Voss looked up and scanned the room.

Yara let herself be found. Just for an instant — a glance held a beat too long, the ghost of a smile, the look of someone who knows. Then she turned back to her wine as if nothing had happened, heart beating hard and even, and listened to Magnus rise to his feet with his glass raised.

"To forty years," he boomed, "of putting up with all of you."

Laughter. Crystal chimed. Forty-one people drank to love and endurance, and not one of them drank poison, and across the length of the table Lena Voss smiled and applauded and began, quietly, to improvise.

* * *

Yara gave her twenty minutes.

That was deliberate too. Long enough for Lena to re-anchor, to circulate, to be seen being gracious. Short enough that the question would still be burning. When Yara finally drifted toward the service corridor, ostensibly looking for the ladies' room, she passed within arm's reach of her hostess's niece and murmured, "The cellar has better light for photographs. If you want to talk."

She didn't look back. She walked. Behind her, after a pause measured in heartbeats, footsteps followed — unhurried, composed, the footsteps of a woman who had decided to turn a liability into an asset.

The wine cellar ran under the east wing, stone and candle-warm, racked bottles sleeping in the dark. Yara had scouted it that morning: one entrance, thick door, no windows. A stage someone else had chosen long ago, perhaps. She stepped inside, turned, and waited.

Lena Voss came down the stairs and pulled the door shut behind her with a soft, decisive click.

"You're the lawyer," she said. "Marchetti. Ingrid hired you for the estate review."

"Ingrid hired me," Yara agreed. "Though I suspect you already know I was coming. You've known for nine days."

Something flickered in the other woman's face — not guilt. Recognition. The pleasure of meeting a worthy opponent.

"The retainer," Lena said slowly. "Elias swore he never sent it."

"He didn't. You did. My fee, paid in full, for a murder that hadn't happened yet. You wanted me here, watching, so that when Magnus died I'd be standing in the middle of it — the estate counsel with a history of finding murder where police find accidents. You studied my cases. All three. You left the files open where I'd find them, annotated in my own handwriting, so that when this ended, every road would lead to me." Yara took a step closer. "You didn't want to kill him quietly. You wanted to kill him *through* me."

"And yet," Lena said, "here we are. Alone. Which was either your mistake or mine." She glanced at the racks, the shadows, the door. "I assume the glasses?"

"Swapped this afternoon. The poisoned pair is in my bag, sealed. It will go to a laboratory tomorrow, along with everything else."

"Everything else," Lena repeated softly. Then she laughed — low, genuine, almost admiring. "Do you know what he did? Do you know what any of them did?" She touched a bottle without taking it down. "My mother. Ingrid's sister. She worked this house for nineteen years and when she got sick, they moved her to a facility two towns over and visited twice. Twice, Marchetti. Magnus signed the papers himself. He always signed the papers himself — that was his one talent, signing things that ruined people."

"The gardener," Yara said. "Twelve years ago. Fell from the north ladder."

"The ladder he'd reported as unsafe. Twice. In writing." Lena's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "The accountant who discovered the skimmed trust. The nurse who threatened to go public about the medication schedule. Each one wronged him. Each one died of gravity, or fatigue, or a bad heart. And each time, I kept the file. Open. Because a closed file means it's over, and none of it is over."

"So tonight was the end of the ledger."

"Tonight was the last entry." Lena turned to face her fully, and in the candlelight her composure was total, serene, terrible. "He signs his own death warrant tonight, one way or another. If not the glass, then—"

"Then what?" Yara said. "What's your improvisation, Lena? Because that's the part you haven't planned. You've never had to work without a script before."

For the first time, something moved behind the serenity. Yara felt the recorder in her clutch, running, its red light hidden, its microphone drinking every word — the confession, the pattern, the ledger, all of it landing on the recording the way evidence should: freely given, in full sentences, with names.

"I don't need a script," Lena said. "I have thirty years of practice and one locked door."

And Yara heard it — above them, through the stone, the soft scrape of the bolt sliding home.

Not from inside.

Footsteps crossed the floor overhead, unhurried, familiar, and stopped directly above the cellar stairs. Someone had followed them down. Someone had been listening at the door.

Lena's composure cracked into genuine surprise — the first honest expression she'd worn all night — and Yara understood, with cold clarity, that neither of them had written this scene.

Someone else had.
