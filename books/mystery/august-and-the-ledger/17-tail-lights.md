## 17. Tail Lights

The storage unit door rolled down with a sound like a held breath being released, and Ulyana felt the weight of the boxes they'd left behind settle onto his shoulders as if he were carrying them himself. Unit 12 had given up its secrets grudgingly — the missing middle section of the ledger, photographed page by page on Wren's phone, the county records stacked in mildewed cartons, and the evidence that someone else had been there before them: fresh cigarette ash ground into the concrete, a padlock that turned too easily, oiled recently by hands that weren't theirs.

"Photographs first," Wren had insisted, her voice low in that way she had when the archivist in her took over from the girl. "Paper burns. Phones can be backed up." She'd worked fast and thorough, kneeling on the cold floor in her wool coat, and Ulyana had stood watch at the mouth of the unit, watching the long corridor of storage doors stretch away into fluorescent dimness, feeling like a man standing in a throat.

Now it was late afternoon going to evening, October pressing its gray thumb against the sky, and the river district waited for them between here and home.

They walked out past the chain-link gate, past the dented sign reading RIVERVIEW SELF-STORAGE in letters gone brown with rust, and Ulyana did what forty years of homicide work had trained into his bones without his permission: he looked at the street.

The lot across the road held four cars. A pickup with a cracked windshield, frost on its glass — been there a while. A hatchback with a child seat in back, also frosted. A sedan so old its paint had gone the color of weak tea. And at the far end, nose pointed toward the exit, a dark gray Volvo with clean glass and no frost at all, though the temperature had been dropping since noon.

He didn't break stride. He didn't say anything yet. He counted instead — one Mississippi, two — and watched the Volvo's exhaust pipe in the corner of his eye. Nothing. Engine off, then. But clean glass meant recent arrival or recent departure, and either way it hadn't been there when they'd driven in an hour ago. He was almost sure of it. Almost.

"Wren," he said quietly, as they reached his own car, the old Buick with the bookshop's name stenciled on the door in peeling gold leaf. "Don't look up. Keep talking about the ledger."

She froze for half a second — he felt it through the air between them — and then she kept walking, her breath making small clouds. "The double-entry structure means every payment has a mirror entry somewhere," she said, pitched perfectly conversational, and he marveled again at how quickly she'd learned this, how naturally suspicion fit her. "If we find the matching column—"

"In the glovebox there's a map," he said. "Under the registration. Don't pull it out yet."

Her eyes flicked sideways, quick and controlled. "What are we doing?"

"Getting in the car normally. Driving normally. And finding out if I'm paranoid or right."

* * *

The Buick pulled out of the storage facility and turned south toward the river, and Ulyana watched the mirrors the way other men watched television. The tea-colored sedan came out thirty seconds behind them. The Volvo stayed put.

He relaxed a fraction. Then the sedan turned off at the feed store, and in the same breath the Volvo's headlights came on in the mirror, and his stomach dropped through the floor of the car and into the cold earth below.

"There," he said. "Gray Volvo. Been behind us since the lot."

Wren twisted in her seat before he could stop her, and he saw her do the thing young people did — actually turn and look, full face, guileless — and he winced. "Did they see me?"

"They saw you. Doesn't matter now. They know we know, or they don't care." He took the next right, not toward town but along the levee road, where the river ran wide and dark on their left and the warehouses stood shoulder to shoulder on their right, brick giants from August's freight-boom decades, most of them empty now, their windows blind with grime. "I want light and witnesses if this goes wrong, but this road doesn't have either. So we're going to be smart instead."

"Define smart."

"You're going to take out your phone and photograph everything you've got from that unit. Every page. Send copies to yourself, to me, and then—" he thought for a moment "—to Corinne Boone. She's earned the right to hold something, even if she never uses it. Then delete nothing, understand? If something happens to us tonight, those photographs need to exist in more places than one."

Wren's thumbs moved. The little shutter-click sounds filled the car, absurd against the drumming of his own pulse. Outside, the river slid past, black and patient, the same river that had given them Tobias Finch like a message in a bottle.

"He's speeding up," she said.

"I know."

The Volvo's headlights had grown in the mirror, swelling from points to orbs, filling the glass. The levee road was straight as a ruled line here, half a mile of nothing but warehouse walls and water, and Ulyana understood with cold clarity that whoever was driving had chosen this stretch on purpose, or knew it well enough to use it. He pressed the Buick harder. The old engine groaned but answered.

"What's the plan?" Wren asked, and he heard it in her voice — not panic, not quite, but the thin vibration underneath words that panic sends ahead of itself like a scout.

"The plan is the river district," Ulyana said. "Forty years ago this was all loading docks and rail spurs. There are more ways in and out of these blocks than anyone who didn't grow up here could count. We're going to lose them in the geometry."

"And if we can't?"

Ulyana checked the glovebox — flashlight, tire iron, the pistol he'd sworn off carrying since retirement and had started carrying again three days after Tobias Finch washed ashore. He didn't answer her question. She saw the gun and went quiet, and the quiet was worse than any plan.

* * *

He took them off the levee road at the old Cannery Street ramp, braking hard enough that the tires chirped, and swung into the grid of the river district proper. Brick canyon walls rose on both sides. The streetlamps here were the old kind, sodium-orange, spaced too far apart, pools of amber light with rivers of darkness between them. In the rearview, the Volvo ate the turn wider and faster, its grille flashing in a lamp's glow — and for one instant Ulyana saw the driver's silhouette, big-shouldered, gloved hands on the wheel.

Gloved. In October. His mind snagged on it and wouldn't let go.

"Left here," he said, mostly to himself, and took it, threading between a rusting flatbed and a dumpster, cutting through the alley behind the old Hollis Mercantile annex — the building whose stationery had started all of this, he realized, the irony arriving sour. The Buick's mirrors kissed the brick on both sides. Behind them, tires shrieked; the Volvo couldn't make the cut at speed and slewed wide, its bumper striking the dumpster with a clang that rang off the walls like a church bell.

"Ha," said Wren, breathless.

"Don't celebrate. They're angry now, and angry drivers get stupid, and stupid gets people killed." He hooked another turn, down a ramp that had once served the freight elevators, and killed his headlights in the sudden dark between buildings. The engine he left running, a purr he felt rather than heard. "Everyone thinks losing a tail is about speed. It isn't. It's about becoming unremarkable. A car that vanishes is interesting. A car that parks and becomes part of the scenery is invisible."

He nosed the Buick into the deep shadow beneath a loading canopy, behind a stack of pallets gone silver with weather, and shut the engine off entirely. Silence rushed in — river-silence, lapping water, wind worrying loose tin somewhere above, the far-off moan of a barge horn. Their breath fogged in the cold interior. Neither of them spoke. Ulyana kept his eyes on the alley mouth, a rectangle of sodium light maybe sixty yards away.

Two minutes passed. Three. On the fifth, the Volvo's engine note entered the world beyond the alley — slow now, hunting, crawling past the gap at perhaps ten miles an hour. The headlights swept the far wall opposite them, a pale blade sliding across brick, and Ulyana's hand found Wren's arm and squeezed: *don't move*. The light passed. The engine receded, turned somewhere, faded.

Then, maddeningly, it returned. Closer. The slow crawl came again, and this time the car stopped — he could hear it idling just outside the alley mouth, a low animal breathing. A door opened. Boots on asphalt. Footsteps coming into the alley, unhurried, deliberate, each one placed like a chess piece.

Ulyana eased the pistol off his lap and into his coat pocket, and put his other hand over Wren's where it gripped her phone, and felt her trembling stop — felt her go still and hard instead, like water deciding to be ice. He would remember that later. He would think, later, that in that moment she stopped being someone he was protecting and became someone standing beside him.

The footsteps came to within twenty feet of the Buick's hiding place and stopped. A flashlight clicked on. The beam played across the pallets, across the wall, across the underside of the canopy — and stopped short of the car by the width of a shadow, because the pallets' silvered stacks threw a blackness deeper than the night around them, and the man with the light wasn't patient enough, or wasn't sure enough, or was only ever supposed to scare them and not find them.

The light clicked off. The footsteps retreated. The car door closed. The engine pulled away, unhurried again, and faded into the ordinary sounds of a dying evening.

Neither of them breathed fully for another full minute.

* * *

They sat in the dark until the cold made the windows fog from the inside, and then Ulyana started the car and drove them out the opposite end of the block, looping wide and slow through streets he'd known since he was a boy delivering newspapers, until they surfaced near the public landing, where the riverfront opened up and the lamps burned honest and bright and a couple walked a dog under the willows, oblivious and blessedly ordinary.

Wren exhaled like a diver surfacing. "I have never," she said, "been so frightened in my life. And I grew up in Detroit."

"You hid it well."

"I was praying. It looked like composure from the outside."

He almost smiled. Then he saw it.

It lay in the gutter at the edge of the landing's gravel lot, half in the light of the nearest lamp, and it was the kind of thing his eye had been trained to catch for forty years — the object out of place, the detail that didn't belong to the scene. A glove. Leather, dark brown, good quality, dropped as if flung from a car window in passing. He got out and crouched over it without touching, and Wren came around the hood and stood above him, and together they looked down at it the way you look down at a snake.

"Yours?" he asked.

"No. Yours?"

"No."

The glove lay palm-up, fingers slightly curled, like a small sleeping animal. Embroidered on the cuff, in thread the color of old gold, was a crest: a shield divided quarterly, a river bend rendered as a silver wavy line, and above it three stars. Beneath the shield, stitched in a banner, a single letter.

**V.**

Ulyana knew the crest. Everyone in August knew it — it was carved over the door of Vance Shipping, it was embossed on the leather menus at the anniversary dinners, it hung in the county courthouse lobby in a gilded frame. He had seen it that very week on the committee's letterhead, rehearsing the town's hundred and fifty years of proud history.

He took out his handkerchief — an affectation he'd kept from his working years, and tonight it earned its keep — and lifted the glove by one finger, turning it. Inside the cuff, written in fading ballpoint in a careful clerk's hand, a size notation: *10½*. And on the palm, worn smooth in a distinctive crescent, the callus pattern of a man who gripped a steering wheel for a living, or a sidearm.

"Raymond Vance," Wren said quietly. "Deputy Raymond Vance. He was at the committee meeting Tuesday. He was standing at the back in uniform, and he was wearing gloves, and I remember thinking it was odd, because it wasn't cold in the hall."

Ulyana wrapped the glove in the handkerchief and put it inside an empty evidence bag he still carried in the trunk from a life he'd supposedly retired from — a bag that had held nothing but receipts for three years and now held the first thread leading anywhere. He tied it off and set it on the seat between them like a relic.

"It's not proof," he said. "A glove is not proof. Gloves get lost, gloves get borrowed, gloves get planted. Any defense attorney in the state will say we found it exactly where we wanted to find it, which is true, because we found it where someone dropped it, and we can't prove which of those things happened."

"But?"

"But it's a name." He looked out at the river, black and moving, carrying whatever it carried toward the sea. "For weeks this has been a shape without a face. A 'committee man.' An anonymous buyer. Cigarette ash in a storage unit. Now it has a name, and the name belongs to a family whose crest is on half the buildings in this town, and one of whom carries a badge."

Wren hugged her coat around herself, and in the lamplight her face was pale but steady. "What do we do with it?"

"We do what we should have done from the start," Ulyana said. "We don't confront him. Confrontation warns him. We watch him. We learn his nights, his routes, his alibi for the night Tobias Finch went into the water. And tomorrow morning, first thing, we make a copy of those photographs and put them somewhere no one can burn, flood, or steal." He paused, and heard himself say the next part, and let it stand. "And then we talk to Marsh. Because a glove with a crest on it is a thread, and threads lead to knots, and knots—" he looked at the bag on the seat "—knots are where the truth gets tied down. Or where people get hanged. Elias Boone taught this town that lesson already. Nobody else hangs."

The dog-walking couple had drifted farther down the bank. The barge horn sounded again, long and low, and the river answered with its silence. Ulyana started the Buick and pulled out of the lot, and in the mirror the landing shrank and vanished, and somewhere behind them in the dark grid of the river district a gray Volvo was hunting empty alleys for a car that had become scenery.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. A text from Marsh, sent twenty minutes ago, while they'd been holding their breath under a loading canopy:

*Heard you visited Riverview Storage. Whatever you found — don't bring it to me. Not yet. People are asking questions about YOU now, Ulyana. Real ones. Call me.*

Ulyana read it twice, and handed the phone to Wren without a word, and drove them both home through streets that suddenly felt mapped, measured, and owned.
