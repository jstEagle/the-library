## 20. Night Visitor

The shop at three in the morning was a different country.

Ulyana had learned its geography over the long hours of the stakeout — the way the moonlight through the front window laid pale bars across the poetry section, the way the old floorboards ticked as they cooled, each one a small clock winding down toward dawn. He sat in the reading chair by the biography shelves, a paperback of *The Count of Monte Cristo* open on his knee, unread for the past two hours. He'd chosen it deliberately, some private joke he wasn't sure he believed: a man who waited years for the right moment to reveal himself. Except Dantès had a plan. Ulyana had a chair, a thermos of coffee gone bitter hours ago, and a store full of hollowed books arranged just so on the shelf by the register — restocked that afternoon with fresh ledger fragments, baited like hooks in dark water.

He had turned off every light except the small brass lamp behind the counter, angled so its glow wouldn't reach the window. From the street, the shop would look empty. Dark. Asleep. He wanted whoever maintained the drop to believe exactly that.

His knees ached. His hip had gone numb an hour ago and come back with pins and needles that made him shift, wince, settle again. Retirement, Marsh liked to say, was just surveillance work you did on yourself. Ulyana had laughed at that once. He didn't laugh now. Now he understood that waiting was its own kind of violence — the slow erosion of certainty, minute stacking on minute until you began to doubt the thing you were waiting for would ever come, or worse, that it had already come and gone while you blinked.

Twice, cars had slowed outside. The first was teenagers, laughing, their voices trailing off down Water Street toward the pier. The second had idled for ninety seconds — long enough for Ulyana's pulse to climb into his throat — before pulling away with a soft diesel cough. He'd written down the partial plate anyway. Habit dies hard. It was the last part of the job that ever would.

At 2:47, he checked his watch by feel, tilting the face toward the lamp's spill without looking up from the book. Another hour, maybe two, and he'd have to decide whether tonight was a failure or simply the first night of many. Wren had argued for cameras; he'd argued against them. Cameras were evidence, and evidence could be seized, subpoenaed, seen by the wrong eyes before the right ones. Whatever came through that door tonight, he wanted to be the first to know it. He was tired of learning about his own life secondhand — from coroners, from landladies, from pages tucked inside books someone else had chosen to gut.

The bell over the door rang at 3:04.

It didn't jangle. It gave a single low chime, half-swallowed, the sound of someone who knew the bell existed and had tried to silence it with a cupped hand — and failed. Ulyana didn't move. He kept his eyes on the page, on the words that meant nothing, and listened.

Footsteps. Small ones. Careful ones. Not the heavy, deliberate tread he'd imagined for Raymond Vance, no cop's flat-footed walk, no hesitation at the threshold followed by the confident stride of a man who owned the room. These steps moved the way water moves when it finds a crack — testing, retreating, testing again. A breath, drawn sharp through the nose. The click of the deadbolt being eased back into place, which told him something too: whoever it was intended to stay a while, and did not want to be interrupted.

Ulyana set the book down on the armrest, spine up, marking nothing.

"I locked it," said a voice — young, female, fraying at the edges. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have. I just—" A swallow, audible across the dark shop. "I saw your shape."

He reached out and clicked on the reading lamp.

The girl flinched like he'd fired a gun. She stood between the history stacks and the window, one hand still raised near her throat, the other gripping a canvas tote bag so hard the straps bit into her knuckles. She was maybe twenty-five, brown-skinned, dark hair pulled back in a ponytail that had given up sometime around midnight, wisps escaping around her temples. She wore a gray hoodie zipped to her chin despite the mild night, jeans with river mud dried along the hems. Her eyes were enormous, wet, and moving everywhere at once — the door, the windows, the shadowed aisles, and finally, unwillingly, to him.

"You're not Vance," she said. It came out as accusation, then as relief, then as something close to collapse. "You're not— oh God. You're the detective. You're the one who owns the shop."

"Retired," Ulyana said quietly. He rose slowly, hands visible, palms out — an old instinct, older than retirement, the body language of a man who had spent thirty years talking people down from ledges and out of corners. "And I don't think I caught your name."

She didn't give it. She was staring at the shelf by the register now, at the row of hollowed books he'd restocked that afternoon, and whatever she saw there drained the last color from her face. "You know," she whispered. "You found them. That's why they're all—" She pressed the back of her hand against her mouth. "That's why you're here. You're fishing."

"I'm listening," Ulyana said. "There's a difference. Sit down, please. Or stand. Whatever keeps you closest to the door — I understand that. But you came here at three in the morning to a shop you thought was empty, which means wherever you were before this was worse than empty." He nodded toward the reading chair. "Nobody follows you?"

"No. I walked. Twice around the block first. Through the churchyard." She almost smiled at that, a terrible little flicker. "Figured if God was watching, He'd mention it to somebody."

Ulyana lowered himself back into the chair, leaving the distance between them undisturbed. Trust, he had learned, was not something you approached. It was something you made room for.

"They'll kill me," she said, conversationally, as if reporting the weather. Then her voice cracked clean through. "They'll kill me like they killed him. Tobias. I read the paper. I know what the river does with people nobody's looking for." She sank onto the edge of the armchair opposite his, perched there like a bird ready to bolt, the tote bag clutched in her lap. "My name is Priya. Priya Nair. I work — I worked — at the county records office. Filing. Digitization. Nobody knows I exist, which is exactly why they picked me."

"Picked you for what?"

Her eyes came up to his, and in them he saw the particular exhaustion of someone who has been carrying a secret long past the point where carrying it has become its own punishment. "The books," she said. "The hollowed books. I'm the one who fills them."

* * *

She talked in pieces, the way people do when the truth has been sitting in them so long it has grown roots and has to be pried out in sections.

It started, she said, eighteen months ago. A man she knew only as Mr. Aldous — gray suit, gray hair, a voice like a patient teacher — had approached her after Sunday service at First Church, of all places. He knew her name. He knew her mother was ill, knew the medical bills, knew she'd taken a second job shelving returns at the library. He was, he explained, part of a historical preservation committee — "a committee man," she repeated, and Ulyana felt the phrase land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well — and the committee needed help with a discreet archival project. Certain documents needed to circulate among certain interested parties. The bookshop on Water Street was the exchange point. She would place envelopes inside specific volumes on specific shelves. She would collect others. She would be paid four hundred dollars a month, cash, in an envelope left in her own mailbox, and she was never, ever to look inside the documents.

"And you didn't."

"For six months I didn't." Her hands twisted in her lap. "Then one night I was early, and the shop lights were on, and I panicked — I couldn't be seen coming out of here, Mr. Aldous was very clear about that — so I hid in the travel section until closing, and while I was hiding I opened one. Just one. Just to know what I was carrying." She looked at him, and her chin trembled. "Numbers. Columns of numbers. Names I recognized — Ashcombe, Vance, initials mostly, but I grew up here, everybody knows whose initials those are. And dates going back further than I've been alive. And one word that kept showing up, year after year, like a subscription. *Maintenance.*"

"Maintenance," Ulyana repeated softly. So the drop ran both directions — payments flowing in, instructions flowing out, all laundered through his shelves while he slept upstairs counting unsold stock. Eighteen months for Priya. Which meant the operation predated her, which meant Aldous had inherited the arrangement from someone, which meant the machinery was older than any single pair of hands. Institutions outlive their operators. He'd spent a career watching that truth do damage.

"I went to Mr. Aldous. I told him I wanted out." She laughed, a dry, airless sound. "He didn't threaten me. That's the worst part. He thanked me for my service, shook my hand, and said the committee appreciated discretion and hoped I'd continue to exercise it. And my envelope stopped coming, and I told myself it was over. For two weeks it was over." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Then Tobias Finch started coming into the records office. Pulling mercantile files. Old shipping manifests, fire reports from '85. He was so careful, but I file things, it's what I *do*, I notice what gets touched. And then he disappeared, and then the man in the river was wearing—" She stopped. Started again. "Mr. Aldous called me the next week. Same voice, same patience. Said the committee had a new arrangement and hoped I'd reconsider. Four hundred became eight hundred. And I said yes, because I thought if I said no, I'd be the next thing the river gives back."

The shop was silent except for the tick of cooling boards and, somewhere far off, the river itself — a sound Ulyana had stopped hearing months ago and heard now with new attention, like a colleague he'd mistaken for furniture.

"The clothes," he said. "Tobias was wearing mine. Stolen from my donation bin. Do you know anything about that?"

Priya shook her head — then paused, and the pause had weight. "There was one instruction. New, this spring. Mr. Aldous told me to take a bag of clothes from your bin — he said it was a test of the security camera the owner was rumored to be installing, that it was harmless, that the committee owned the building next door and had a right to check." Her face folded. "I took the bag. I put it in the drop locker at the bus station, like always. I told myself it was harmless." She looked up at him, stricken. "Two weeks later that man was floating in it. In *your* coat. They used me to dress a dead man, Mr. Sandoval. They used my hands."

Ulyana let the silence hold. Anger was available to him — hot, immediate, aimed at this frightened young woman who had carried envelopes through his shop for eighteen months while he alphabetized paperbacks ten feet away. But anger at Priya was a luxury, and a misdirection besides. She was a hinge, not a hand. Somewhere above her, above Aldous even, stood the person who decided what the river received.

"Tell me about Aldous," he said instead. "Everything. How he contacted you. Where. What he looked like. Left-handed? Wedding ring? Cologne? Did he ever slip — ever call you by another name, reference something he shouldn't have known?"

"He was careful. That's what scared me most — everyone else in this town is careless, they talk, they brag, but he was *careful*." She frowned, dredging. "Gloves, always, even in summer. But once — once he smelled like cigar smoke, expensive, the kind my uncle used to bring back from Havana. And once, on the phone, when he thought the line had dropped, I heard him say—" She closed her eyes, retrieving it. "*'The judge wants it settled before the dinner.'*"

The words entered the room and seemed to rearrange the furniture.

Ulyana kept his face still, kept his breathing level, the way he'd learned to decades ago in interrogation rooms where the suspect watched for exactly the flinch he was feeling now. *The judge.* August had one judge who mattered, one beloved retired jurist whose name was spoken with the fondness towns reserve for founding fathers and high school football legends. Bartholomew Crane. Host of tomorrow's anniversary dinner. Voice of the sesquicentennial toast. The town's conscience, rehearsing forgiveness.

And *settled*. Not resolved. Not handled. Settled — the word from the final ledger entry Wren had decoded only days ago. *Settlement — B.C., final.*

"B.C.," he said aloud, barely a breath.

"What?"

"Nothing." He leaned forward. "Priya. Listen to me carefully, because what happens in the next five minutes matters more than everything you've told me tonight. You've confessed to a crime — tampering, theft, at minimum. I'm not police anymore, but what you've said is still dangerous, and not because of me. Because of them. If Aldous learns you were here—"

"He won't. I made sure." But her voice shook, and they both heard the lie in it. She reached into the tote bag and drew out a slim bundle wrapped in a plastic grocery sack, tied with a knot that had been tied and retied until the plastic was white with stress. "The final pages. The ones from this spring. I copied them before I returned the originals — I photograph everything now, I keep copies in three places, I'm not stupid, whatever else I am." She held it out, then pulled it back an inch, holding his eyes. "If I give you this, you have to promise me something. Not protection — you can't protect anyone, look what happened to the last man who knew too much. Promise me you'll use it *before* the dinner. Tomorrow night. Because after the toast, after Crane stands up there and makes this whole town forgive itself out loud, it'll be too late forever. They'll have their anniversary. They'll have their story. And nobody — not the state police, not God himself — will be able to make August un-hear it."

Ulyana took the bundle. Through the plastic, he could feel the edges of paper, forty years of paper, the weight of a town's arithmetic. "I promise," he said, and found that he meant it the way he had never meant the oaths of his old career — not to a badge, not to a department, but to the woman shivering in his shop and the dockworker hanged in 1985 and the daughter who had told him he was late by forty years.

Priya exhaled, long and shaking, and for a moment she looked younger than twenty-five, younger than the trouble she was in, just a tired girl in a gray hoodie at three in the morning who had finally set down something heavy.

Then her head snapped toward the window.

Headlights swept across the glass — slow, deliberate, a car crawling past at walking speed. The beam crossed the poetry section, the biography shelves, the reading chair where Ulyana sat holding stolen pages, and for one suspended second he saw the reflection of his own face ghosted over the street outside, and beyond it the shape of a dark sedan easing by without brakes, without pause, patient as tide.

The taillights glowed red at the corner of Water Street. Held there. Three seconds. Five.

Then they turned, and the night swallowed them, and somewhere downstream the river kept doing what the river had always done for August — keeping its accounts, and waiting.
