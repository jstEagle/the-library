## 26. The River Gives Back

Wren Okafor had learned to read the river the way other people read a calendar. The August ran high in spring and low by late summer, brown-green in the bends and glass-clear over the gravel shallows, and everything it took from the town it eventually, grudgingly, gave back. Dock ladders. Fishing poles. A child's red wagon, rusted to lace. In the eleven years since she'd come to the county archives, she had catalogued the river's returns with the same patient hand she used for everything else — date, object, condition, location recovered — in a slim notebook she kept for no reason except that she was the sort of person who believed the town's small losses deserved a record too.

So it was Wren, not the police, who understood what it meant when Dale Pruitt came into the archives on a Thursday morning, dripping, hat in hand, and said, "Miss Okafor, you'd better come see what the river coughed up at the old weir."

She was elbow-deep in a carton of 1972 tax assessments. She set them down carefully, squared the lid, and reached for her coat.

* * *

The weir was a ruin of poured concrete a mile and a half downstream from the landing where Tobias Finch had been pulled from the water — a relic of the mercantile era, built by the same founding families whose names Wren now knew by heart, whose initials she had spent two weeks decoding out of a dead man's ledger. The water pooled there in a slow, silted eddy, and it was in that eddy, snagged against a fallen willow, that Dale had found the bag.

It was a dry bag, the kind fishermen used, scuffed and faded but sealed, its roll-top cinched with a brass buckle gone green. It hung in the willow branches like something deliberately hung there, though Dale swore the river had done it — had carried it down, turned it over, and set it in the branches the way the river did everything, without malice and without mercy.

"Has anyone opened it?" Wren asked.

"Wasn't mine to open," Dale said, which was a lie — it was entirely his to open, and everyone knew it — but she appreciated the lie. It meant he'd waited. It meant the town still, in its bones, understood that some things belonged to the record, and she was the record.

She knelt on the concrete apron and worked the buckle with numb fingers. The roll-top peeled back with a sigh of trapped air. Inside, wrapped in a fisherman's plastic sleeve and nested in a towel gone stiff with damp, was a notebook.

Not a ledger. A notebook — the cheap spiral kind a clerk buys by the dozen, its cover soft with water though the pages inside, she would learn, were nearly pristine. Tobias Finch had been a records clerk for nine years. Of course he had known about waterproofing. Of course he had known how documents survived, and how they died, and what the river did and didn't take. He had prepared for the water the way he had prepared for everything: quietly, thoroughly, alone.

Wren lifted the notebook out and did not open it on the weir. Some instinct, old and archival, told her the first page should be turned under good light, with gloves, with a witness. She looked up and found Ulyana Sandoval standing at the top of the concrete stairs, hands in the pockets of a coat that was, she noticed with a small private ache, not the one the drowned man had worn. He'd stopped wearing it. She'd noticed that days ago.

"Dale called you too," she said.

"Dale called everyone," Ulyana said. "I got here when I saw you drive past me going thirty in a thirty-five." He came down the stairs slowly, favoring the knee the way he always did when the morning was damp. He looked at the notebook in her hands for a long moment. "Is it his?"

"The bag's a fisherman's. But the towel—" she turned back a fold, showing the monogram, a small embroidered T — "is his. His landlady said he fished the weir on Sundays."

Ulyana crouched beside her, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The river went over the broken lip of the weir in a long brown ribbon, patient as ever, giving back what it owed.

"Archives," Wren said. "Now. Before anyone else with a badge or a checkbook hears about this."

* * *

The archives occupied the old Carnegie building on Courthouse Square, two rooms and a basement that smelled of cold stone and old paper, and Wren had keys to all of it. She locked the front door behind them, turned the sign to CLOSED — REPAIRS, a lie she had been telling a great deal lately, and carried the notebook to the worktable under the north window, where the light came in clean and gray and honest.

She gloved. She photographed the cover, the spine, the spiral binding, the water stain at the edges. She logged the time, the location of recovery, the name of the finder, in the slim notebook she kept for the river's returns. Then, and only then, she opened it.

The first page was a title page, in Tobias Finch's small, clerkly hand — the hand of a man who had written ten thousand filing labels and never once allowed himself a flourish:

*Property of T. Finch. If found, this book contains the truth about the August fire of 1985. Please take it to someone who will read all of it.*

Wren's throat tightened. She read it aloud anyway, because Ulyana was standing behind her and because some sentences are too heavy for one pair of eyes.

"He knew," Ulyana said quietly. "He knew he might not make it."

"He was a records clerk," Wren said. "He knew exactly how things got lost. He built this book to survive him." She turned the page.

The next forty pages were Tobias's reconstruction, and it was beautiful in the way that only obsessive, careful, unglamorous work is beautiful. He had begun with the shipping manifests of the Hollis Mercantile, 1983 through 1985 — the same manifests Wren and Ulyana had traced through the ledger's double-entry code, the same false weights and phantom destinations — and he had rebuilt the smuggling route the way an archivist rebuilds anything: from the residue it left in the paperwork.

He had drawn a map. Wren flattened the page with both palms and felt her breath go shallow. The route ran from the river docks up through the mercantile's warehouse, out on the night barges to the rail spur at Vance's Crossing — *Vance's Crossing*, she underlined it twice — and from there into the county interior. Crates listed as mill machinery going out heavy and coming back light. Crates listed as machinery going out light and coming back heavy. The mercantile had been a pump, Tobias wrote in the margin, *moving goods in both directions and skimming both ways, and every man on the committee knew, because every man on the committee took his cut.*

"Every man," Ulyana repeated. "He wrote *every man.*"

"He wrote it in pencil," Wren said. "Then he went over it in pen. He wanted it permanent." She kept turning.

The middle of the notebook was testimony — not sworn, not signed, but gathered. Tobias had interviewed people. A retired barge hand named Ossie, who remembered loading "mill machinery" at midnight. A woman whose father had kept the rail spur's switch lamp and had once been paid twenty dollars to look the other way on a Sunday in 1984. Each account was dated, sourced, cross-referenced to a document number in the county records — Tobias had cited his interviews the way Wren cited her holdings, and she felt something move in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite admiration but was, she realized, recognition. He had been one of hers. Not by training, not by employment, but by nature. A keeper of the true record, working alone, in a town that did not want one.

Then came the fire.

Tobias's reconstruction of the fire took eleven pages, and Wren read every one of them aloud while Ulyana stood at the window with his back to her, watching the square, watching for cars, watching for the thing they both knew was coming. Elias Boone had refused to sign the manifests. That much Corinne had told them; that much the ledger's coded entries had implied. But Tobias had found the next layer: the fire itself had been set to cover the refusal — not to punish Boone, but to erase the paper trail, the crates, the route, everything that a stubborn dockworker with a good memory might one day describe to the wrong person. The fire was the amendment. Elias Boone was not the crime's victim. He was the crime's *redaction.*

"Oh," Wren said, when she understood it, and the sound she made was small and terrible in the quiet of the archives.

The last pages were the payment record — Tobias's own reconstruction of the ledger's "maintenance" entries, matching hers entry for entry, year for year, forty years of quiet annuities paid to keep the verdict closed. And then, on the final written page, in pen, in a hand that had begun to shake — the letters slightly larger, the lines slightly less level, the handwriting of a man who knew what he was writing and who he was writing it against —

Tobias had left a note.

It was not long. It was a clerk's note, precise and unsentimental, and Wren read it once silently and then, because Ulyana asked her to, because her voice had failed on the first attempt, she read it aloud.

*If I am gone, the man responsible is Bartholomew Crane. Not because he lit the fire — I don't believe he did, and I have no proof that he did. Because he was the county prosecutor in 1985, and the manifests proving the smuggling crossed his desk three weeks before the fire, and instead of investigating he opened a file against Elias Boone. Because the maintenance payments in the ledger are drawn on an account I traced to the Crane family trust. Because in 1998, when I first pulled the 1985 manifests from county records, they were re-sealed within a week, and the only person with standing to seal them was Judge Bartholomew Crane. And because two weeks ago, a man I had never seen before stood outside my window and looked at me, and the next morning Judge Crane stopped me on the courthouse steps and asked after my mother, whom I have never mentioned to anyone in this town.*

*I have copied everything. The copy is where he will never think to look, because he has never once in his life looked at the river.*

*The ledger is the town's confession. This book is mine.*

*— T.F.*

The archives were very quiet. The north window let in the gray light, and somewhere on the square a truck door slammed, and Ulyana Sandoval did not move from the window for a long time.

"Where's the copy?" he said finally. "He says he made a copy. Where he'll never think to look — the river. The copy's in the river?"

"Or it *was*," Wren said. Her mind was already moving, fast, along the shelving of possibilities. "The dry bag. The bag was sealed, Ulyana. The notebook was in the sleeve, but the sleeve was loose — like something had been taken out and put back in a hurry. And the towel was folded under it, not over it. Someone found the bag before Dale did. Someone opened it, took the copy, and re-sealed it — badly — and put it back in the water, or hung it, or—" She stopped. She was holding the notebook so hard the spiral bit into her gloves.

"Or Crane's people found it first," Ulyana said, "and left the original because a dead man's notebook naming him is survivable, but only if the corroborating copy is gone."

"Or," Wren said, and her voice was steadier than she felt, "we're not the only ones racing, and whoever took the copy isn't Crane."

They looked at each other across the worktable, and neither of them said the name. *Vance.* The deputy whose family had given the rail crossing its name. The deputy with the thin alibi and the monogrammed glove and the debt to the judge that he had not yet found the courage to describe. The deputy who had agreed, three days ago, to give a statement — *after* the anniversary dinner, a delay that had sat in Ulyana's chest like a swallowed stone ever since.

"He said after the dinner," Ulyana said.

"I know what he said."

"If he found that bag — if he's been down here, in the river district, looking—"

"Then he has the copy," Wren said, "and every hour he holds it, he's choosing which man he is."

* * *

She spent the afternoon making copies of the notebook — photographing every page twice, printing three sets, sealing one set in the archive's fire safe behind the false shelf where the county kept its oldest land records, and sliding the second into an envelope addressed to the state police that she would mail herself, tonight, from the box on Delano Street, because she no longer trusted the courthouse mailroom and had not for two weeks.

Ulyana sat across from her the whole time, not helping, watching the square through the north window, and she understood that he was keeping watch, and that the watch had become the shape of his days, and that he had stopped pretending otherwise.

At four o'clock she logged the notebook into the archive's permanent holdings. Accession number 2024-118. *Recovered from the August River at the old weir by Dale Pruitt; identified as the property of Tobias Finch, dec.; contents: reconstructed record of the Hollis Mercantile smuggling operation and the fire of 14 March 1985.* She wrote *dec.* and her pen hesitated over the abbreviation, because she had written it ten thousand times for other people and never once for a man she had decoded a ledger with, a man whose citations she had admired, a man who had been one of hers.

"He testified," she said, when she finished. "That's what this is. It's not a notebook. It's a deposition. He knew he might not live to swear it in a courtroom, so he swore it to the record instead." She looked up. "The river gave him back, Ulyana. Now we have to be worthy of it."

Ulyana came away from the window at last. He looked older than he had in the spring, when he had first stood in her archive doorway with a torn ledger page in a plastic sleeve and a detective's hunger he was supposed to have retired. But his eyes were the same.

"Corinne should hear this first," he said. "Before anyone. Before Marsh, before the dinner, before the whole town. She waited forty years. She gets the first reading."

"Yes," Wren said. "And then—"

The phone on the archive desk rang. The old landline, the one number she had never given to anyone involved in any of this, the number that existed on a county directory page from 2011 and nowhere else.

She and Ulyana looked at it. It rang again.

Wren picked it up. "County archives."

The voice on the other end was low, ragged, and familiar — a voice she had heard give careful, lawyerly non-answers across a desk at the sheriff's substation, a voice that had said *after the dinner* and meant it and had perhaps stopped meaning it in the last three days.

"Miss Okafor," Deputy Raymond Vance said. "Is Sandoval there with you? Don't answer that. Just — tell him I found something in the river. And tell him I'm coming in. Tonight. Before the dinner." A pause, and then, quieter, in a voice stripped of everything but the choice itself: "Tell him I'm done choosing the wrong side of it."

The line went dead.

Wren set the receiver down slowly. Across the room, Ulyana had gone very still, the way he went still when a case turned — she had learned that about him, the stillness, the total attention.

"That was Vance," she said.

"I know."

"He found the copy. And he's coming in."

Ulyana nodded once, and outside the north window the gray afternoon was already failing toward evening, and somewhere across town a hall was being decorated, and a judge was rehearsing a toast about a town that forgives itself, and the river, having given up its dead man's testimony, ran on toward the sea without comment.

"Then we'd better decide," Ulyana said, "what we do with him when he gets here — because if Crane finds out Vance is coming in tonight, the anniversary dinner won't be the only thing that happens tomorrow."

Wren reached for her coat.
