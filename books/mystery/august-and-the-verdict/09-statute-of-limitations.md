## 9. Statute of Limitations

The call came at 7:14 on a Tuesday morning, which told me everything before Mr. Pellworth said a word. Insurance men do not call at 7:14 in the morning to deliver good news. Good news keeps banker's hours. Bad news is an early riser.

"Miss Brightwater." His voice had the particular smoothness of a man who has rehearsed his regret in the mirror. "I'm afraid the home office has reviewed the Verdict claim and determined that we need to accelerate our timeline. The claim window closes in fourteen days. After that, all evidence gets sealed pending litigation review."

"Litigation review," I said. "Whose litigation?"

"That's—well. That's rather the point of sealing it."

I sat down at my kitchen table, where my coffee was going cold beside a plate of toast I'd already forgotten to eat. Fourteen days. I did the arithmetic without meaning to—it's not a thing I can switch off, arithmetic or anything else—and arrived at the fact that two weeks from Tuesday was the ninth of August. August. Of course it was. This town had a sense of humor like a landmine.

"Pellworth," I said. "Who called you? Before you called me. Who got to you first?"

A pause long enough to drive a hearse through. "Councilman Harrow's office made an inquiry regarding our exposure. Entirely routine."

"Entirely routine inquiries don't come with deadlines attached."

"Miss Brightwater, I understand your frustration—"

"You understand nothing, which is why they pay me." I kept my voice pleasant. It takes very little effort for me to keep a voice pleasant; what costs effort is keeping it honest, and I've learned over the years that the two are rarely compatible on the telephone. "Fourteen days. Noted. Sealed evidence, noted. Anything else?"

He hesitated again, and then said, quietly, off-script, the way people do when their conscience catches up with them somewhere around the second cup of coffee: "For what it's worth, I think your report was excellent work. And I'd get copies of everything. Paper copies. Somewhere outside the county."

Then he hung up, and I sat there listening to the dial tone, which in this century means sitting there listening to nothing, which felt about right.

* * *

Here is the trouble with being the only witness to anything: there is no version of events that exists without you. No corroboration. No second set of eyes to say *yes, I saw it too, the stones were river-worn, the chisel marks were fresh.* Just me, and my head, and a town full of people with every incentive to suggest that my head is not to be trusted.

I have been doubted before. It comes with the territory. When you can recite a man's own signed statement back to him word for word, including the typo in the third paragraph, his first instinct is not gratitude. His first instinct is to wonder what else you noticed, and his second instinct is to start building the argument for why whatever you noticed doesn't count.

But this was different. This wasn't one man squirming in a deposition. This was a hundred years of institutional weight, a council, an archive, a founding legend carved into the courthouse lintel, and—as of last week—a paper trail being actively rewritten behind me by someone with keys to everything and a smile like a locked door.

Cordelia Moss had brought me tea. Cordelia Moss had produced a file that contradicted what I had read with my own eyes. And I had said nothing, because saying something would have meant admitting what I suspected, and I wasn't ready to be alone with that knowledge yet.

Well. Alone was apparently the theme of the season.

* * *

I spent Wednesday doing what I should have done the day the coffin came open: making copies.

Not photographs—photographs live on phones now, and phones live in clouds, and clouds are just other people's computers, and other people's computers have administrators, and administrators have phone numbers that Councilman Harrow knows how to dial. I wanted paper. Paper is stubborn. Paper burns, yes, but it also persists, and it can't be remotely edited by an archivist with a scanner and a dream.

The problem, I discovered by Thursday afternoon, was that paper couldn't hold what was actually in my head.

I want to be clear about what I mean. It isn't that the material was voluminous—though it was; I could reproduce the 1898 policy front and back, the ledger entries, the soil strata as they appeared when the gravedigger's spade turned them, the exact angle of the fresh chisel marks on the coffin's interior lid, forty-one degrees to the grain, left-handed stroke pattern. Any of that would fit in a notebook fine.

It was the connections that wouldn't fit. Memory doesn't store facts in neat columns; it stores them the way roots grow through clay, tangled, each one gripping six others. When I tried to write down *the ledger page torn out between the March and April 1899 entries*, the sentence kept wanting to accrete—the smell of camphor in the archive stacks, the way Cordelia's thumb had covered the binding when she handed me the substitute file, my grandmother's kitchen table and the smell of her coffee and her voice saying *paper lies, Larkie, paper lies all day long, so you hold the truth where nobody can burn it*—and suddenly I wasn't writing a case note anymore, I was writing a memoir, and I didn't have time for a memoir. I had thirteen days.

So I developed a system. I am, if nothing else, a woman who develops systems under pressure the way other people develop hives.

One notebook, cheap, spiral-bound, purchased with cash from the drugstore on Delancey, chosen precisely because it was unremarkable. Numbered pages—I numbered them myself, in pencil, because pencil says *working document* and ink says *evidence*, and I wanted anyone who found it to believe it was the former. Entries keyed not by date but by a cipher of my own devising, which sounds paranoid until you remember that someone had already been through my office touching nothing but my case notes. Deen had found the drawer open, the papers squared back into place with a tidiness that was itself a message: *we read you, we can reach you, we were gentle this time.*

The cipher took me most of Thursday night. By Friday morning I had eleven pages of dense, small handwriting that would look, to any casual reader, like the grocery lists of a lunatic. To me, each line unfolded into whole rooms. Page four, line nine: *L.B., 3/11, no marker, east row.* That single line held the entire shape of chapter three of this disaster—the gravedigger's grandson, his grandfather's deathbed refusal, the empty plot in the east row where a man had refused to lie among stones that lied.

I wrote until my hand cramped, and then I kept writing, because here is the thing I could not say out loud to Deen, or to Pellworth, or to anyone: I was writing the notebook for the version of events in which I was no longer believed.

That version was coming. I could feel it the way you feel weather change behind a closed window—not the storm yet, but the pressure. Harrow had laughed at me once already, that easy laugh of a man holding all the doors. *Who believes a memory without a page?* He hadn't asked it yet, not in those words, but it was coming, and when it came it would arrive wearing a suit and carrying a psychiatrist's vocabulary. *Confabulation. Hyperthymesia of a self-aggrandizing type. The subject demonstrates rigid certainty inconsistent with the fallibility of human recall.* They wouldn't call me a liar. Liars can be caught. They'd call me *unwell*, which cannot be caught, only pitied, and pity buries deeper than any grave in the Verdict cemetery.

So the notebook was insurance against my own mind. Think about that. My entire life, my memory has been the vault—impregnable, complete, the thing that made me feared in three counties. And now I was backing it up like a woman afraid of a hard-drive failure, except the failure mode wasn't corruption. It was credibility.

* * *

Deen came by Saturday with takeout and a face like a forecast.

"They posted the council agenda," she said, dropping containers on my table without ceremony, because Deen has never met a surface she considered too good for dumplings. "Special session. August ninth. Item three: 'Resolution regarding the disposition of centennial artifacts and closure of associated claims.'"

"The ninth. Same day the claim closes."

"Cute, right?" She sat across from me and watched me write for a while, which she does better than most people—watches without needing to fill the silence. Finally: "You know how this looks from out there, right? From the part of the world that isn't inside your head?"

"Tell me."

"It looks like a woman with a famous memory insisting everyone take her famous memory's word for it, while the actual documents—" she tapped the table, meaning the archive, meaning Cordelia's tidy substituted files, "—say otherwise. You're asking the town to believe the copy in your skull over the copies in the vault. People don't do that. People believe the vault."

"People believed the vault when it said August Verdict founded this town in glory," I said. "The vault has been lying for a hundred years. Somebody's been dusting the lies and calling it preservation."

"I know." She opened a container, didn't eat. "I'm not arguing with you, Ansel. I'm telling you what the jury pool looks like. Fourteen days isn't enough time to prove anything. It's barely enough time to make copies of the copies."

Which was, of course, exactly the point of the fourteen days. I saw it plainly then, the architecture of it, elegant and cruel: they weren't trying to stop me from finding the truth. They were building a box with a date on it, so that whatever I found after the ninth would be legally weightless—sealed, inadmissible, a madwoman's notebook full of cipher. The deadline wasn't bureaucracy. It was a weapon with a calendar face.

"Deen," I said. "If something happens—if I get discredited, or worse, or merely *inconvenienced* in some creative way—you take the notebook. There's a copy. Don't ask where. You give it to the state attorney, not the county. State. Promise me."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide not to argue about the *if something happens*, which was its own kind of promise. "You could leave town," she said instead. "Take the consulting fee, write it up as vandalism, let it go. Nobody would blame you. Most people would."

"Most people can forget," I said. "It's the one luxury I've never been able to afford."

* * *

Sunday night I finished the notebook—forty-three pages, ciphered, cross-referenced, wrapped in a plastic bag inside a tin inside a place I will not write down even here, old habits—and I sat at my kitchen table with the pen still uncapped, and I did something I hadn't planned.

I added a forty-fourth page. In plain English. No cipher.

*If you are reading this and I am not the one giving it to you,* I wrote, *then assume everything in the coded sections is true, and assume I knew it might cost me to know it. My name is Ansel Brightwater. I have a perfect memory. Whatever they tell you about what that means—whatever doctors, whatever lawyers, whatever friendly voices from the historical society—they will tell you it makes me unreliable. It makes me the opposite. It makes me the only person in this county who cannot be lied to successfully, which is why I must be made to seem unreliable, and quickly.*

I stopped. Read it back. It sounded half like testimony and half like a suicide note, and I hated both registers, so I added one more line:

*I am not unstable. I am inconvenient. Learn the difference.*

Then I capped the pen, and the apartment went very quiet, the specific quiet of a room containing one person and everything she knows.

And that was when I heard it—or didn't hear it, which is the same thing with certain people. A faintness in the hallway. The elevator's particular reluctance on my floor. And underneath it, threading through the gap under my door, unmistakable, patient, familiar as a hymn:

Camphor.

Somebody was standing in the corridor outside my apartment at eleven o'clock on a Sunday night, smelling of mothballs and old paper, and they were waiting—waiting to see my light go out, waiting to learn whether I kept the notebook on me or hidden, waiting, perhaps, simply to let me know that they could.

I did not turn off the light. I picked up the phone instead, and dialed the one number in this county I still trusted, and when Deen answered I said, quietly, "How soon can you get to the flooded shaft on the Verdict estate? Bring rope. And bring a second flashlight."

Because the strongbox was still wedged in that rock, and I had twelve days, and I was done being the only witness standing watch alone in the dark.
