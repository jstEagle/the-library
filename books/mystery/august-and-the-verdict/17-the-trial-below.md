## 17. The Trial Below

The water in the crypt came up to my ribs, and it was the temperature of a held breath.

I had expected the note to be a bluff. Cordelia Moss did not strike me as a woman who wasted paper, but people who spend decades curating lies develop a certain theatrical streak. Still, I went. I want that on the record, wherever this record ends up: I read *Bring the memory. Come alone*, and I dried my hair, and I put on my good coat, and I walked into it with my eyes open.

The bell tower of St. Ambrose-in-the-Hollow had been deconsecrated since before I was born—too expensive to maintain, too beloved to demolish—and so it stood at the edge of the Verdict estate like a tooth nobody could afford to pull. The flooded crypt beneath it connected to the mine shafts; I'd mapped the connection myself from the church records, and the church records, unlike the county archive, had never passed through Cordelia's hands.

A service door at the tower's base stood ajar. Through it, a stairwell climbed, and someone had left a lamp burning on the third landing. That was the invitation. That was the whole architecture of the evening: come up, come in, sit down.

I climbed.

The belfry room at the top had once housed the rope mechanisms and, presumably, the town's better intentions. Now it held two folding chairs, a card table, a hurricane lamp, my stolen notebook lying square and centered on the table like an exhibit, and Cordelia Moss and Councilman Harrow, waiting the way people wait when they've rehearsed.

Cordelia sat with her hands folded, her silver hair pinned with the same severe competence she brought to the reading room. Harrow stood by the empty bell frame, his coat still buttoned, as if he'd come straight from a council meeting and considered this one merely a continuation.

"Miss Brightwater," he said. "You're wet."

"It's a flooded crypt," I said. "I took the scenic route."

"We asked you to come alone."

"I am alone." I let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "Unless you count the eleven men you'd prefer I forget."

Cordelia's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a flicker, the way a catalog card slips out of alignment. She gestured to the second chair. I didn't take it. Standing kept my blood moving, and moving blood is thinking blood, and thinking was going to be the whole of my survival strategy tonight.

"You have something of mine," I said.

Harrow picked up the notebook and weighed it in his hand like a man appraising livestock. "We do. And you have something of ours. Or rather, you *are* something of ours—or you were meant to be. That's the tragedy here, Miss Brightwater. You were supposed to be one of us."

"Was I."

"The Brightwater memory," Cordelia said quietly. "It's not a fluke. It's a line. Your grandmother had it. Her mother before her. The town has always known how to find it, and how to use it." She unfolded her hands. "Lark refused. You don't have to."

There it was again—the offer inside the threat, the door held open onto a room I was apparently born to occupy. I made myself look at the notebook instead. My handwriting, my compressed shorthand, everything I knew about the ledger, the stones, the strongbox, the eleven names. Everything except what was actually in my head, which was more, always more, and which they couldn't steal because they'd never figured out where I keep things.

"What happens now?" I asked. Genuinely curious, or performing curiosity—I've found the distinction matters less than people think. "You've got my notes. You've got me. There's no one else who knows what I know. Why not simply—" I tilted my head toward the drop beyond the bell frame, down into the dark of the shaft below. "Solve everything?"

Harrow laughed, but it had a frayed edge. "Because dead investigators become martyrs, and martyrs get other investigators. Because your firm has a file open. Because Deen Okafor's sister has already called the county sheriff twice." He set the notebook down. "No. We don't need you gone. We need you *unreliable*."

And there it was. The word I'd been circling for weeks without naming.

"Unreliable," I repeated.

"A public recantation," Cordelia said. "A statement, signed, delivered at the centennial closing ceremony. That the exhumation findings were misread. That the coffin's contents were consistent with soil subsidence and historical record-keeping errors. That your subsequent investigation was conducted under—" she paused, choosing the word with a curator's precision— "*distress*. Grief, perhaps. Family history. A documented tendency toward confabulation."

"Confabulation," I said. "That's a lovely word. It means remembering wrong. It means the memory can't be trusted. It means a woman who has never once been wrong about a document can be made into a woman who was wrong about all of them." I looked between them. "You're not asking me to lie. You're asking me to be no one. To be the thing I've spent my whole life making sure I never had to be—which is a person whose word requires corroboration."

"Everyone's word requires corroboration," Harrow said. "That's how the world works."

"Not mine," I said. "That's how *your* town works. That's the whole machine, isn't it? For a hundred years, Verdict has run on the principle that whatever gets written down becomes true, and whoever controls the writing controls the truth. My grandmother wouldn't play, so she was driven out in 1974 and erased. Cordelia plays beautifully—she's been playing for thirty years. And me—" I smiled, and I felt it come out wrong, more teeth than warmth. "Me, you thought you could recruit. The perfect keeper. Born and bred."

Cordelia rose. Up close, in the lamplight, I could see what I hadn't let myself see in her office over tea: exhaustion worn down to the bone, the particular grayness of a person who has done a terrible thing for so long it has stopped feeling like a choice. "Ansel," she said. "Listen to me. I am the fourth keeper. Before me, my mother. Before her, a judge named Aldous Crane. We are not monsters. We are librarians of a fire. Every generation, we keep it contained, because every time it has gotten out, people have burned. Land lost, families ruined, the town itself nearly bankrupted in 1931 trying to suppress the first inquiries. What I'm offering you isn't disgrace. It's *mercy*. Recant, take the commission Harrow will fund, live your life. Or refuse, and I promise you the world will choose to believe you invented all of it. A photographic memory sounds like magic until a lawyer explains it's just a claim. Who believes a memory without a page?"

"Who indeed," I said.

And I stalled. Because stalling was the plan, and the plan was all I had.

"Tell me about 1974," I said. "If I'm going to make the biggest decision of my life, I want the full provenance. Provenance matters—it's the first thing you taught me, Cordelia, that first day in the archive. *Always ask where a document has been.* So: where has Lark Brightwater been? Where did she go when she left? What did she take with her?"

Cordelia's hands tightened on each other. "She took nothing. She was given a settlement and a bus ticket and instructions never to return."

"And the crypt," I pressed. "Sealed in 1974. Same year. That's a coincidence, surely."

"The crypt was sealed for structural reasons."

"With the strongbox inside it. With Lark's testimony inside the strongbox. Eleven widows' signatures, sworn statements about the collapse of '98—the collapse August Verdict caused by ordering the timbering skipped, the collapse he blamed on Lark's husband so the company wouldn't pay. You've read it. You've had fifty years to read it. Tell me, Cordelia—when you sealed your mother's work down there, were you burying evidence or preserving it?"

For a moment—one long, lamplit moment—I thought she might answer honestly. Her mouth opened. Harrow moved first.

"This is pointless," he said. "She's stalling."

"Of course I'm stalling," I said. "I'm a woman standing in a bell tower at night with two people who stole from me and threatened me. Stalling is the only rational activity available. But while I stall, let me ask one more question, Councilman, since you've been so generous with your time." I stepped closer to the table, close enough to see the water stain on my own notebook's cover. "The empty coffin. The river stones. Someone put them there—someone who wanted the lie dragged into the light after a hundred years. Cordelia told me the founding families kill to protect this. So who emptied the coffin? Because it wasn't either of you—you've spent three weeks trying to convince me nothing was ever wrong. And it wasn't Lark; she's been dead nine years. So who decided, in the town's centennial year, that the fire should finally burn?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and texture and a smell—camphor, faintly, still, always camphor somewhere in these old rooms.

Harrow glanced at Cordelia. It was a small glance, a half-second, but I have a photographic memory, and half-seconds are libraries to me. He glanced at her the way a man glances at a partner whose side of the arrangement he no longer trusts.

"You don't know," I said softly. "You don't know who did it. You've been managing me for weeks, and you don't actually know who opened that coffin, because it wasn't part of your plan. Something happened this summer that neither of you ordered." I picked up my notebook from the table—neither of them stopped me; taking it back was clearly part of their script, a prop returned before the final act. "Which means there's a player on this board I haven't met. And gentlemen—lady—that is the single most interesting fact I have collected all month."

"Put the notebook down," Harrow said.

"No," I said. "I think I'll keep it. You see, you're both operating on a theory of me that's twenty years out of date. You think I'm safe because I work alone, because I trust documents, because I have no allies worth the name. But here's what my grandmother taught me, the lesson underneath the lesson about paper: *paper lies, but paper also testifies.* Everything I've found exists twice—once in my head, and once somewhere you haven't found yet. The ledger page. The policy. The names. Copies, Councilman. In places that will open automatically if I stop checking in."

It was maybe sixty percent true. I watched them decide how much of it to believe, and I watched the arithmetic frighten them, and I understood suddenly that fear was the only currency I had and I intended to spend every coin.

"You're bluffing," Harrow said.

"Probably," I agreed pleasantly. "Care to bet your family name on it? Because I've memorized yours too. Chapter and verse. 1898 through last Tuesday."

Cordelia made a sound—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob. "Enough," she said. "Both of you. Ansel—go home. Think about the offer. You have until the closing ceremony Saturday. After that, the insurance claim closes, the evidence seals, and whatever you say afterward is just a woman talking." She turned away toward the stairs, dismissing me, ending the audience. "Take the east stair. The west one floods at high water."

I went, because going was part of the performance too. At the top of the east stair I paused, one hand on the cold stone, and looked back at the two of them in the lamplight—Harrow already speaking low and fast, Cordelia staring at the bell frame as though it might ring.

"One more thing," I said. "The camphor. Someone's been watching me for weeks—someone who smells of camphor, at the shaft, at the archive, probably at my car the night my notebook was taken. Neither of you smells of camphor tonight. So before Saturday, ask yourselves the question I'll be asking: who else knows the story well enough to guard it?" 

I descended into the dark, water sloshing at my knees, heart hammering out a rhythm I refused to call panic. Behind me the lamp burned on. Ahead, the tunnel ran toward the mine shafts, toward the strongbox, toward everything the town had drowned and everything it had kept.

And somewhere above me, faint through forty feet of stone and floodwater, I heard a sound I would remember for the rest of my life, however long the rest of it turned out to be:

the bell, ringing once, though there was no one in the tower tall enough to reach it—

and then, closer, much closer, from the black water ahead of me, a voice saying my name.
