## 19. Harrow's Fall

The state attorney's office in Marrow County occupies the third floor of a building that smells of wet carpet and institutional despair, which I have always found reassuring. Institutions that smell confident are the ones you should worry about. This one smelled like it had already lost several arguments and expected to lose more.

I arrived at nine-fifteen on Monday morning with two recorders, a sworn statement signed by eleven widows in 1899, and a memory containing roughly forty hours of conversations I would happily have paid to forget. Deputy Attorney Okafor—gray suit, gray eyes, the only person in that building who looked like she'd slept in the last decade—took custody of everything with the solemnity of a woman accepting a live grenade.

"You understand," she said, "that a recording made in a flooded crypt by an interested party, without consent notification, will be challenged."

"I'm counting on it being challenged," I said. "Challenge it loudly. Make them explain why Cordelia Moss and Councilman Harrow were standing in August Verdict's crypt at midnight demanding I recant."

Okafor almost smiled. It was the closest thing to joy I'd seen above the second floor. "And your memory. If the recordings are excluded—"

"Then you'll have my testimony, and they'll call me unstable, and we'll be right back where Harrow wanted us." I set the widows' statement on her desk, squared its corners out of habit. "But paper doesn't lie, Ms. Okafor. My grandmother taught me that. People lie *on* paper, which is different, and traceable, and eventually expensive."

She looked at the statement for a long moment—the eleven signatures, the notary's stamp from a justice of the peace who had died believing his work was forgotten—and something in her posture changed. The posture of a woman who had just realized the grenade was real.

"Go home, Ms. Brightwater," she said. "Don't talk to anyone. Don't leave town."

"Those instructions contradict each other," I said, "but I'll do my best."

* * *

Harrow did not go quietly. I want that on the record, since records have been the whole problem.

He learned the recordings had gone to the state attorney at approximately ten o'clock Monday morning, because Okafor is thorough and served notice promptly, and by eleven he was in the archive basement with a can of lighter fluid and forty years of Cordelia Moss's careful, criminal stewardship.

Here is what I know about what happened next, assembled from testimony, from security footage, and from the particular quality of silence that follows a man discovering he has been outmaneuvered by a dead woman's paperwork:

Cordelia had known this day would come. Not the day, perhaps, but *a* day. Every keeper does, she told me later, through the glass of the visiting room, in the tone of someone discussing weather. So she had done what keepers do: she had protected the record. But this time she had protected it from herself.

The ledgers Harrow doused in the basement reading room were forgeries. All of them. Cordelia had spent three years producing meticulous reproductions of every document worth destroying, aging them with tea and camphor—I finally understood the smell; it clung to everything she touched, a preservative's perfume, a curator's signature—and shelving them where an arsonist would look first. The originals had gone into a storage unit in Kettle Falls under the name of a woman born in 1898.

Harrow struck the match at 11:14 a.m. The sprinkler system—installed in 1998 over the objections of three councilmen, one of whom was named Harrow, a detail I found delicious—activated at 11:15. The fire died. The water damage was cosmetic. And Councilman Douglas Harrow stood in a puddle holding an empty lighter-fluid can while two deputies who had been waiting in the parking lot since 10:40 walked down the stairs.

He didn't run. That surprised me until I thought about it. Men like Harrow don't run; running is an admission that the rules no longer apply to them, and their entire lives are built on the certainty that the rules apply to everyone else. He stood there dripping and demanded to speak to whoever was in charge, and when they arrested him he said, clearly enough that the body camera caught it:

"This town was built on order. Somebody has to maintain it."

Eleven men died in a collapse he'd have called an act of God. A widow was driven out of her own name. And what he mourned, ankle-deep in sprinkler water, was *order*.

* * *

The charges came down like a long-delayed rain: arson, evidence tampering, obstruction, conspiracy, and—once Okafor's people finished tracing the money through the Verdict estate trust—two counts of fraud going back to land deals in the seventies that had nothing to do with any of us. Harrow retained the best lawyer in three counties, which lasted four days, until the lawyer read the widows' statement and quietly asked to withdraw from the case. I'm told his exact words were "I don't defend ghosts, and I especially don't defend the people who made them."

The founding families began to fracture the way old stone fractures—not all at once, but along lines that had always been there.

Councilwoman Petra Voss was first. She came to my office on Wednesday without an appointment, which for a Voss was roughly equivalent to arriving naked. She sat across from me with her hands folded and said, "My great-grandfather was Tomas Voss. He was one of the eleven."

"I know," I said. "He was twenty-nine. He had a daughter."

"He had a daughter," she repeated, as if tasting the words. "We were told he abandoned the mine. That he cut corners and killed his crew. That's what the ledger says. That's what the *plaque* says." She looked at her hands. "I've laid flowers on August Verdict's grave every Founders' Day since I was six years old. For the man who buried my family twice."

She gave a formal statement that afternoon. Two more council members followed within the week—one confessing to knowledge of the cover-up's maintenance, one claiming ignorance so fervently that Okafor flagged him for follow-up, since nobody protests innocence that specifically unless they're rehearsed.

Others fled. The Aldermans packed a moving van on Thursday night and were in Reno by Saturday; their house sold in nine days, which tells you something about how many people in Verdict wanted out and had been waiting for permission. Old Mrs. Calloway, ninety-one and sharp as broken glass, reportedly told her grandson, "If they come asking, tell them I remember nothing, which will be true, because I intend to drink until it is." She was, I'm told, the only member of the founding families having any fun.

And then there was the offer.

* * *

It came by letter, hand-delivered, on cream stock that I checked immediately against known forgeries. Old habit now. Everything gets checked.

*Ms. Brightwater — The undersigned descendants of the founding families, being eleven in number, hereby offer you full custodial authority over the Verdict municipal archive, its contents, endowment, and future, in perpetuity, effective upon your acceptance.*

Eleven signatures. They'd counted. Someone among them understood that the number mattered—that anything less than eleven would look like surrender, and eleven would look like penance. Whether it was penance or public relations, I genuinely could not tell you. Both, probably. Most human motives are both.

I sat with the letter for a long time. Outside my window, the town went about its business—the bakery, the hardware store, the church steeple above the bell tower where I'd nearly drowned in other people's history. They were offering me the keys to the lie. The whole apparatus. The chair Cordelia's mother had warmed before her, and Cordelia's mother before *her*, back and back into the dark.

They thought they were handing me power. What they were actually doing was what the founding families had always done: finding one person with a perfect memory and making the record her problem. Lark Brightwater refused in 1974. Cordelia accepted and rotted slowly for thirty years, curating a crime. And here was my invitation, in cream stock, with eleven signatures, dressed up as an honor.

I wrote *declined* across it and mailed it back certified, return receipt requested. If they were going to fracture, let them fracture in daylight, where I could count the pieces.

* * *

Deen came back to work on Friday. He looked thinner, and he flinched at the sound of the office door, and he didn't meet my eyes for the first hour, and then he put a coffee on my desk without a word and sat down across from me like nothing had happened, which was his way of saying everything.

"They held me in a cabin off the north road," he said finally. "Two days. They kept saying they just needed to know what you'd copied. I kept telling them I didn't know. Which was true." He turned the cup in his hands. "Ansel, I took the coordinates from your notes. I shouldn't have. I wanted to see the strongbox myself, get ahead of it, maybe—" He stopped. "Maybe find something that would make me useful instead of just the guy who fetches things."

"You were bait," I said. "They used you to get to me."

"I know." He looked up. "Doesn't change what I did."

"No," I agreed. "But you held the second recorder steady in those rafters for forty minutes with your hands shaking so hard I could hear it on the tape. That counts for something. Most things, actually."

He nodded, and drank his coffee, and after a while he said, "Your notebook. The one from your car. Harrow's lawyer has it—it's in the evidence chain now, logged and sealed. Okafor says it'll come back to you after trial." A pause. "He never read it, apparently. Said a notebook proves nothing. 'Who believes a memory without a page?'"

"He said that to me too," I said. "In a crypt. Right before I proved him wrong about pages entirely."

Deen smiled for the first time in two weeks. It was crooked and tired and real.

* * *

That evening I walked out to the cemetery. I hadn't meant to; my feet decided, and I've learned to let them, since they frequently know things before I do.

August Verdict's monument still stood over its empty grave—the granite obelisk, the founding dates, the inscription about vision and industry that now read like a threat assessment. The council hadn't decided what to do with it yet. There was talk of removal, talk of a corrective plaque, talk—always talk—of leaving well enough alone. Towns are slow to bury their founders twice.

The fresh dirt from the centennial exhumation had settled into a shallow depression. River stones all the way down, and beneath them, nothing. No founder. No bones. Just the shape of a hole where a story used to be.

I stood there as the light went amber and then gray, and I waited to feel triumphant. I had done the thing. I had taken the impossible case and pulled the thread and watched the whole rotten garment come apart. Harrow was in county lockup awaiting arraignment. Cordelia was cooperating fully, testifying to everything, calm as a librarian closing time. The widows' names were going to be read aloud in courtrooms and eventually, if Okafor had her way, carved somewhere permanent. Vindication. The thing investigators aren't supposed to admit wanting, and want anyway.

What I felt instead was tired, and cold, and strangely unmoored. Because vindication, it turns out, is a room you arrive at and find mostly empty. Eleven men were still dead. Lark Brightwater had still lost her name and her town and, eventually, her mind to the weight of what she remembered. Cordelia had still spent her whole life kneeling inside a lie, dusting it, protecting it, until conscience drove her to smash the display case—and even her rebellion had required a stolen notebook, a missing colleague, and a trap sprung on the one person foolish enough to walk into a flooded crypt alone.

Nobody wins these cases. Some people just stop losing.

A car door closed behind me. I knew the gait before I turned—Okafor, in her gray coat, hands in pockets, looking up at the obelisk with the expression of a woman auditing a very large invoice.

"You're supposed to be home," she said.

"My feet disagreed."

"They should get counsel." She came to stand beside me. "Arraignment's Thursday. Harrow's pleading not guilty, all counts. His new attorney is filing to exclude the recordings tomorrow morning." She paused. "And the Moss woman asked for one thing before her deposition. She wants to see you. Says she has something to give you that isn't in any evidence log."

"Should I be worried?"

"Cordelia Moss," Okafor said, "has spent thirty years deciding what survives. Whatever she wants to give you, I'd assume she chose it carefully." She turned back toward her car, then added, without turning around: "For what it's worth, Ms. Brightwater—the state doesn't need your memory anymore. We have the pages. Go home. Sleep."

I watched her taillights fade down the cemetery road, and I stood a while longer in the dark beside the empty grave, thinking about what a keeper chooses to hand over when the keeping is done.

Thursday felt a long way off.
