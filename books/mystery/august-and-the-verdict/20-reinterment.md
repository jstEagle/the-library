## 20. Reinterment

The crane arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Verdict had never been a town for ceremony so much as logistics—funerals scheduled around harvests, weddings around funerals—and a Tuesday was the most honest day we had.

I stood at the fence line of the old Verdict estate with my hands in my coat pockets and watched them rig the pumps. The flooded shaft had been draining for nine days, and the water coming up was the color of weak tea and old decisions. Deen stood beside me, his arm still stiff from where they'd kept him tied in the bell tower rafters, and he didn't say anything for a long time, which was how I knew he understood what this was going to cost me.

"You don't have to watch," he said finally.

"Yes, I do." I said it the way you'd say *the sun will rise*, because it wasn't a choice, it was physics. "That's rather the point of everything."

The state's forensic team worked the shaft for three weeks. I won't give you the details, because some things even a photographic memory should be permitted to file under *later*. What I'll tell you is this: eleven men went into that collapse in 1898, and eleven men came out of it in October, carried up in baskets by people wearing gloves and expressions I recognized from every funeral I'd ever attended—the particular solemnity of strangers doing their best work.

What surprised everyone—including, if I'm honest, me—was that there were eleven.

Cordelia had told us, in her confession, that August Verdict himself had ordered the men sealed in the lower gallery after the collapse, that the "mine accident" story required bodies that could be pointed at, and the real ones had been an inconvenience to the legend. She'd assumed, after a century, that there would be nothing left but suggestions. But the lower gallery had flooded early and stayed flooded, cold and airless, and the water had done what water does: it had kept its receipts.

Eleven men. Eleven sets of remains. The county medical examiner's office spent a month confirming what I could have told them from memory, because I had, in fact, already told them—from the ledger, from Lark Brightwater's sworn testimony, from the widows' statements recovered from the strongbox. Names, ages, next of kin. I'd recited the list so many times by then that Harrow's lawyer had objected during the preliminary hearing on the grounds that no witness could possibly know that much without having been present at the crime. The judge had looked at him for a long moment and said, "Overruled. That's rather the opposite of the problem."

* * *

The town council voted to bury them in the Verdict cemetery—of course they did; it was the only cemetery—but the argument was over where. There was a faction, led by two of Harrow's cousins who were scrambling to rebrand themselves as reformers before the indictments landed, who wanted a separate memorial plot, something new, something with a plaque and landscaping, something that would let the town say *look what we did* instead of *look what we did*.

The gravedigger's grandson settled it.

His name was Tobias Pell, and he was seventy-one years old, and he showed up at the council meeting uninvited, which in Verdict is the traditional method. He stood in front of the dais with his hat in his hands and said, "My grandfather dug graves in that cemetery for forty years and refused, on his deathbed, to lie in it. Said he wouldn't spend eternity keeping company with a lie. Well." He put his hat on. "The lie's been dug up now. Bury them with the others. Same ground. Same rows. They were townspeople. That's all they ever wanted to be."

Then he sat down, and the council, which had never in a hundred years been handed a moral position so clean, voted unanimous within four minutes.

They asked me to speak at the service. I want to be clear about what that request actually was, because I've thought about it a great deal since. It wasn't gratitude. It wasn't quite penance either, though there was some of that in the room when Councilwoman Voss called my office—it was fear. Half the founding families' descendants had learned, over the preceding months, that their names were attached to those eleven miners by blood or by benefit, and they had discovered that a woman with a perfect memory standing at a podium is a very different instrument than a woman with a perfect memory working alone in an archive. They wanted me inside the ceremony because a thing spoken at a funeral is finished, sealed, laid to rest alongside the dead. A thing spoken at a press conference has a way of growing appendages.

I said yes anyway. Not because I didn't see the maneuver—I see everything, that's my whole tragic condition—but because the eleven names deserved to be said out loud in daylight by someone who meant them, and I was, whatever else I was, available.

* * *

The morning of the service, it rained.

Not dramatically—this isn't a novel, whatever Cordelia used to say about my sense of narrative. It was just ordinary October rain, fine and gray, the kind that makes gravestones look freshly washed and makes umbrellas useless. The whole town came. I mean that literally. Four hundred and six people, and yes, I counted, because counting is what I do instead of breathing when I'm nervous.

They buried them in the oldest row, beside the graves of their wives and children, whose stones still stood with dates and endearments worn soft by weather. The wives had waited a hundred years in the same dirt as the man who'd ruined them, and nobody had ever thought that strange until now.

The minister read the twenty-third psalm, because ministers always do, and then the mayor—new mayor, the old one having discovered a pressing family obligation in another state—stepped up and gave three minutes of careful language about "a community reckoning with its past," and then it was my turn.

I walked to the podium under the rain awning and looked out at the town. I found faces I knew. Tobias Pell, front row, hat off, rain flattening his white hair. Deen, arms crossed, looking like a man guarding a door. Councilwoman Voss, weeping openly, which I hadn't expected. And near the back, apart from everyone, a woman in a dark coat whom I recognized from the visiting room at the county jail—Cordelia Moss, released on bail pending trial, come to stand at the edge of the thing she'd spent thirty years maintaining and eleven months destroying.

She nodded at me. Just once. I nodded back, because whatever she was, she had emptied that coffin on purpose, and conscience, even late conscience, even criminal conscience, deserves acknowledgment.

"I have a memory that doesn't let go," I said. "Most of my life I've treated that as a professional asset and a personal inconvenience. For the last year it's been something else. It's been a debt." I took out the paper I'd written the names on, held it up so they could see it, and folded it away again. "I wrote them down in case anyone doubted me. Nobody here doubts me anymore. So I'll do it the way paper can't."

And I said the eleven names.

Elias Crane. Samuel Ottery. Josiah Bell—no relation to the tower, though God knows the irony had been noted. Thomas Wren. Ambrose Fitch. Daniel and Matthew Sorrel, brothers, aged nineteen and twenty-two. Peter Halloway. Isaac Dunn. Cornelius Ashe. And last, because the ledger listed him last and because his widow's signature opened the testimony in the strongbox: Gabriel Brightwater. My great-grandfather. The man the town had blamed for the collapse he died trying to prevent.

I said each name slowly, and I said each one twice, because the old mining custom—which Lark Brightwater had noted in the margin of her testimony, in handwriting I knew better than my own—was to say a dead man's name once for the world and once for the man, so that somewhere he'd hear it and know he'd been counted.

When I finished, the rain kept falling, and nobody moved for a moment, and then Tobias Pell stood up and said, in a voice like gravel settling, "Counted," and the word went through the crowd like a current, four hundred people saying it back, not in unison exactly, but close enough. Counted. Counted.

I stood there and let it happen. I'd like to tell you I kept my composure. I'd like to tell you that, but my memory records everything, including the fact that I cried at a public podium in front of the entire town of Verdict, and I have decided, as of this writing, to keep that fact rather than edit it. A perfect memory means living with your own record too. I'm learning to sign off on it.

* * *

Afterward, while the crews lowered the caskets—one for each man, plain pine, the kind they'd have been able to afford in life—people came to me in ones and twos. This is the part I hadn't prepared for. I'd braced for hostility, for the cold shoulder of a town watching the person who'd pried open its favorite story. Instead they shook my hand. They told me small things: a locket passed down that no one had known the origin of, a middle name that had always seemed odd, a grandmother who'd gone quiet whenever the mine was mentioned. They brought me their fragments and asked me, tentatively, if I might know where they fit.

And I did. That was the terrible, wonderful thing. I knew where nearly all of them fit. A woman named Ruth Fitch showed me a photograph of a house and I told her, gently, that it had stood on what was now the bank parking lot, and that her grandfather had bought it in 1901 with settlement money, and she put her hand over her mouth and said, "So that's why Mama never liked banking there."

By four o'clock I was soaked through and hoarse and more connected to this town than I had ever been in thirty-odd years of living an hour away and knowing everything about it and being known for nothing. Invisibility, I'd once thought, was protection. It turned out visibility was something better than protection. It was company.

Deen found me at the cemetery gate as the crowd thinned.

"Voss is telling people the council wants to offer you Cordelia's job," he said.

I laughed. It came out wrong, rusty. "The archive job. Keeper of the record. They want to hand the vault keys to the woman who burned the vault open."

"They think it solves their problem. Put the memory on the payroll, get a little control over what it does next." He shrugged. "You'd be good at it."

"I'd be *terrible* at it," I said. "That's the problem. One keeper, alone, with all the truth—that's not a solution, Deen. That's the disease. It's how we got Cordelia, and before her Cordelia's mother, and before her—" I stopped, because the list went back a hundred years and I could recite every name on it, and some of them were carved on stones ten feet behind us.

"So what are you going to do?"

I looked back at the cemetery. The fresh mounds. The old stones. Somewhere in that row, a stone for Gabriel Brightwater that didn't exist yet but would, because I intended to pay for it myself and I intended to make sure the inscription was accurate down to the letter.

"I'm going to negotiate," I said.

Deen grinned. "There she is."

* * *

That evening, back at my office, I found an envelope in the mail slot. No return address, but I knew the handwriting before I'd fully registered the envelope—knew it the way you know a voice in the dark. Careful, upright, the penmanship of a woman trained to make copies that would outlast her.

Inside was a single sheet: the original 1898 insurance policy, the one that had started everything, payable to Lark Brightwater. The genuine article, pulled from whatever hiding place Cordelia had kept for it these long decades. On the back, in the same hand, one line:

*A record is only as honest as the number of people allowed to read it. Don't repeat my mistake.—C.M.*

I sat with it for a long time. Then I got out a fresh sheet of paper and began drafting terms—not a letter accepting the post, but a proposal for something that had never existed in Verdict, or maybe anywhere: an independent records commission, outside auditors, no single keeper, no single key. A structure designed so that no one—no archivist, no councilman, no family, no memory, however perfect—could ever again hold the town's truth alone.

It was going to be a fight. The council would resist; half of them still had surnames on the ledger. Cordelia would go to trial in the spring, and the town would have to sit through every ugly detail, and I would testify, and my memory would be picked at by lawyers hoping to find the seam where perfect recall comes apart.

But that was tomorrow's work. Tonight I had a policy made out to a woman who never officially existed, and I propped it on my desk where I could see it, and I memorized it one final time—not because I needed to, but because she had carried it for a hundred years, and somebody ought to carry it now on purpose.

Lark Brightwater. Counted.
