## 8. Trial by Letter

**MAREN**

The sixth letter arrived on a Tuesday, which by then felt less like a coincidence and more like a taunt. Tuesdays were the day Harlan used to mow Mrs. Kettleman's lawn, the day he'd come home smelling like cut grass and cheap deodorant and flop across the couch with his boots still on, and my mother would say *Harlan, the cushions*, and he'd say *Maren sat there first*, which was a lie, and I'd let him have it because he was my little brother and letting him have things was the whole job description.

Fifteen years of Tuesdays, and then this one, with an envelope in the mailbox addressed to me in handwriting I now knew better than my own doctor's.

I'd stopped opening them at the kitchen table. I took this one to the car, sat in the driveway with the engine off, and slit it open with a butter knife because I'd lost my letter opener somewhere between letter three and letter four, somewhere between *curious* and *haunted*.

*Maren,* it began. *You were in the water that night too, in a way. You were on the porch. You heard the second scream — the one nobody testified about, the one the sheriff's report says doesn't exist. Ask Jonah. He heard it too. He just doesn't know he heard it yet.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because my hands had started shaking on the second pass and I wanted to make sure the shaking wasn't editing the words.

The porch. I had been on the porch. I'd told the original investigators that, fifteen years ago — that I'd heard something around ten-forty, a sound carrying across the water, high and wrong, the kind of sound that makes you stand up without deciding to. They'd written it down as *possible animal noise, distant roadway, etc.* I remember the deputy writing *etc.* Like my brother's death had an *etc.*

Nobody had ever said *scream*. Nobody had ever said *second*.

I called Jonah. He picked up on the first ring, which told me he'd been sitting with the phone in his hand, waiting for exactly this call, the way a man waits for a diagnosis.

"It's Tuesday," I said.

"I know," he said. "I got mine an hour ago."

**JONAH**

My letter didn't come with a threat. It came with a schedule.

*Ten days,* it read. *That's how long until Toby Ellery stands in a courtroom and the state tries to make him into the thing you know he isn't. You know he isn't because you were there, Jonah. You've always been there. The question is whether you'll remember before the jury is sworn, or after, when it won't matter anymore. Grief is a filing system. Someone taught you that. Someone taught you everything. Tick tock.*

The handwriting, as always, was mine. Not similar — mine, down to the way I cross my sevens and the little hook I put on the tail of a *y* that no handwriting analyst would believe wasn't mine unless they watched me write in front of them. Whoever was doing this had studied my hand the way I studied case files: obsessively, lovingly, with an eye for the details the owner forgets.

There are exactly two explanations for that. Either I was writing these letters in some fugue state and mailing them to myself across town like a madman running a one-man pen pal program — a theory I'd tested and discarded around letter three, when one arrived while I was demonstrably in a diner being watched by a waitress who remembered my order better than my mother did — or someone had learned to *be* me on paper.

And there was only one person alive who'd had that much access to me.

I didn't let myself finish that thought. I'd been not-finishing it for a month, the way you don't finish the sentence *the smoke alarm is chirping because* when the alternative is climbing a ladder.

Maren came over at noon with her letter in a plastic sleeve. She'd started preserving them, which I found both grimly funny and deeply right. We are, apparently, the kind of people who archive our own torment.

"He mentions the porch," she said, laying it flat on my table between the coffee rings and the case files. "The second scream. Jonah, I *heard* something. I always heard something. They made me feel crazy for it."

"You weren't crazy."

"Then what was I?"

I looked at the letter, at my own handwriting staring back at me like a mirror with an attitude, and said the truest thing I'd said in fifteen years: "Alone. Which is its own kind of crazy, and the state of Maine owes you an apology for it."

She almost smiled. "Your letter. What's the threat?"

"Ten days. Trial. Tick tock." I slid mine across. "Whoever this is wants Toby free and me testifying, and they want both to happen in that order, and they're running out of patience."

"Or they're running out of *time*," Maren said quietly.

I looked at her. She had that expression people get when they've followed a thread further than you have and aren't enjoying the view.

"Say it," I said.

"The postmarks." She tapped my letter. "All six of yours, all five of mine. I lined them up. The first two came from the post office on Route 9. The next three from the annex downtown. But the last two —" she turned her letter over, showed me the circular stamp — "these came from the regional medical center's mail room. The one that serves the hospice wing."

The room got very quiet. Outside, a jay was committing some small crime in the maple tree, and I could hear it perfectly, because my memory has never once had the decency to blur.

Dale Pruitt had been in that hospice wing since June. Pancreatic, they said, the kind with a prognosis measured in seasons rather than years. I'd visited twice. He'd looked small in the bed, gray as ash, still cracking jokes with the nurses, still calling me *kid* even though I was forty-one and he was the one running out of clock.

Dale, who had taught me to file. Dale, who had taught me that grief is a filing system, that some drawers you close and some you seal and some you burn the whole cabinet over. Dale, who had been the sheriff the night Harlan Voss drowned, and who had — I now remembered, in the terrible slow way you remember a thing you've spent a lifetime not-remembering — sat with me on the dock afterward, put his coat around my shoulders, and said, *You didn't see anything, kid. You were home by nine. It's better this way.*

I had believed him. That was the part that made my teeth ache. I hadn't been coerced. I'd been *relieved*. A seventeen-year-old boy who'd watched a man die, whose memory was a cage with no door, had been handed a key by the one adult whose voice still had authority over him, and I had locked myself out and called it mercy.

"Jonah," Maren said. "You've gone somewhere."

"Yeah," I said. "I've gone somewhere I should've gone in August, fifteen years ago." I picked up my keys. "Drive me to the medical center. I don't trust myself to drive."

* * *

**MAREN**

He was quiet the whole way over, which for Jonah is a loud kind of quiet. I've known him four months and I already know his silences the way sailors know weather. This one was the bad kind — the front-moving-in kind.

The hospice wing smelled like lavender and institutional lemon, a scent that tries too hard and knows it. A nurse with kind eyes and a lanyard full of cartoon pins pointed us down the hall. "He's having a good day," she said. "He'll pretend he isn't. Don't let him."

Dale Pruitt was propped up in bed watching a fishing show with the sound off, which struck me as the saddest possible way to watch fishing. He looked smaller than the last time I'd seen him, which had been at the county fair, years ago, when he'd judged the pie contest and given my entry second place out of what he called *respect for the craft of losing gracefully*. Everyone loved Dale. That was the thing about Dale. Loving Dale was the town's easiest civic duty.

"Well, hell," he said when he saw us. "The investigator and the sister. If this is an intervention, I want it noted I've already quit everything worth quitting."

"Did you write the letters?" Jonah said.

No preamble. I'd have softened it. Jonah doesn't soften; he files.

Dale looked at him for a long moment. Then he did the thing I'll remember until I die, the thing that rearranged the whole town in my head like furniture in the dark. He smiled. Not a guilty smile, not a caught smile. A *proud* one. The smile of a teacher watching a student finally balance the equation.

"You finally read the whole file," he said.

"Answer the question."

"Which one? The one you asked or the forty you didn't?" He shifted against the pillows, wincing, waving off the wince like it was a waiter. "Sit down, both of you. I've got maybe three good conversations left in this body and I'd rather spend them telling the truth than playing twenty questions."

Jonah didn't sit. "The handwriting. It's mine."

"It *was* yours. Fifteen years ago, before your hand settled. I kept every report you ever drafted as a kid — you used to volunteer at the station, did you know that? Fourteen years old, alphabetizing evidence logs like it was a religious calling. I kept your forms. Practiced your hand for two years." He said it the way other men talk about learning to whittle. "Took a while to get the sevens right."

"Why?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Why now? Why this way? If you knew what happened to Harlan, you could have —"

"Could have what, sweetheart? Stood up fifteen years ago and said *I did it*?" He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on long enough that I reached for the water pitcher. He waved that off too. "I was the sheriff. My word was the whole legal system in this town. You think a jury convicts a sheriff on his own confession and a dead boy's *etc.*? They'd have given me a plaque." His eyes went to Jonah. "No. It had to come from outside. It had to come from someone whose memory couldn't lie forever. It had to come from you, kid. I just had to make you remember."

"You let Toby Ellery rot for fifteen years," Jonah said, and his voice had a crack in it I'd never heard, a fault line under the wry.

"I let a scared boy take a deal his own lawyer recommended, in a case nobody was going to reopen, because the alternative was a town that stopped trusting its sheriff and a witness who'd have spent his whole life inside a memory he couldn't survive." Dale's voice dropped, and for the first time it wasn't a performance. "I was wrong. I've had eight months in this bed to be wrong in every direction. So I fixed it the only way I know how. I built you a case, Jonah. I mailed you the evidence one piece at a time. And now you're going to do what I couldn't."

"Which is?"

"Put me on that stand. Ten days. I'll be alive for it — the doctors gave me that much, and I intend to be insufferable about spending it." He looked at Jonah, and then at me, and his eyes were wet and steady at the same time, which shouldn't be possible. "You finally read the whole file. Now read it *out loud*."

Jonah stood there for a long moment. I watched him do the thing he does — the filing, the sorting, the terrible arithmetic of a man who can remember everything and is only now learning what that costs.

Then he took out his phone, set it on the bedside table, and pressed record.

"Dale," he said. "Let's start with the night of August ninth. Take your time. We've got ten days, and for the first time in fifteen years, I'm not going anywhere."

Dale smiled again, and this time it wasn't proud. It was relieved.

"Well, then," he said. "Pull up a chair, kid. It's a long file."
