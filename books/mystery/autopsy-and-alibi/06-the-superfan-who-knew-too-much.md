## 6. The Superfan Who Knew Too Much

There is a particular kind of insomnia reserved for people who make their living from other people's tragedies. It's not the tossing-and-turning kind. It's the kind where you lie perfectly still at 3 a.m., staring at the ceiling, and your brain — that loyal, treacherous employee — replays the day's evidence in a loop with the volume turned up.

Mine had been running the same clip since the funeral home called: *Jane Doe, mid-forties, found in the drainage culvert off Halloway Road, dressed in clothing belonging to Isandro Whitlock.*

I'd stopped lying still around four. By five I was at my kitchen table with a laptop, a cold cup of coffee, and the forum.

Every long-running true-crime case breeds its own ecosystem. The Vale case had one of the healthiest — or sickest, depending on your tolerance — communities on the internet. The Marguerite Vale subforum had eleven thousand members, a wiki, a timeline maintained to the hour, and a hierarchy of regulars whose usernames were as familiar to me as my own byline. There was *HallowayRoad* who believed everything was the gardener. There was *PetraDidIt*, who had been banned twice and kept returning under variations like *PetraDefinitelyDidIt*. And there was *ValeWatcher*.

*ValeWatcher* had joined fourteen months ago. No avatar, no signature, no personal posts about their own life — which, in a community of people who narrated their breakfasts between theories, was itself a tell. They posted only case analysis. Long, meticulous, well-sourced analyses. The kind you'd expect from someone with archival access.

The kind you'd expect from someone reading over my shoulder.

I scrolled back through their history with my heart doing a slow, ugly percussion against my ribs. Fourteen months of posts. And there it was, timestamped nine days before the Jane Doe surfaced:

> *When the body finally shows up — and it will — look at what she's wearing. The housekeeper's account mentions Marguerite left in a blue coat and a patterned scarf. Whoever staged this will want the clothing to mean something. Watch for a scarf.*

A scarf. My scarf, as it happened, knotted around a dead stranger's throat.

I kept scrolling. Two months earlier, regarding the second detail — the one from my unpublished draft, the detail about the shoes placed facing the door:

> *The shoes matter more than anyone thinks. In the original statement, they were found pointing toward the front entrance. If someone is reconstructing the scene, they'll get that wrong or they'll get it deliberately right. Deliberately right would be the point.*

Deliberately right. That was exactly what Okonkwo and I had stood looking at in that culvert: shoes placed with a precision no accident produces.

And then, three weeks ago, the post that made my hands go cold on the trackpad:

> *Whitlock knows more than she's said publicly. Her final episode will change the shape of the whole case. Someone should ask her what she's holding back.*

Someone should ask her. I read it five times. It wasn't a threat, not technically. It was worse than a threat — it was intimacy. *ValeWatcher* knew there was an unreleased episode. Only four people on earth knew that: me, Theo, Camille, and Bram.

I sat in the grey light of my kitchen and did the arithmetic every paranoid person eventually does. Four people. One dead woman wearing my clothes. And a forum account that spoke about my work the way a stalker speaks about a lover — with total knowledge and total distance.

* * *

Okonkwo took some persuading. Actually, "persuading" is generous; what I did was show up at the precinct at 8:40 a.m. with printed screenshots and refuse to leave until she looked at them, which she eventually did in the interview room, across a table, with the door closed — her choice, not mine, though I noticed she made it quickly.

"Walk me through this," she said. "You think your killer has a fan account."

"I think my killer *is* a fan account."

"Those aren't the same thing."

"They are if the fan account predicted things only the killer could know." I laid the screenshots out in a row, like tarot cards. "Predicted the scarf. Predicted the shoes. Referenced an episode that doesn't exist publicly. This person has access to my research, Detective. Either directly or through someone who does."

Okonkwo studied the printouts for a long moment. She had a way of going very still when she was thinking, like a heron over water, and it was unnerving precisely because it gave nothing back.

"You said last week you'd audit your circle," she said. "How's that going?"

"Everyone's alibi is a little too clean."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the most honest answer I have." I tapped the screenshots. "This account logged in from somewhere. IPs, session data — the forum admin can pull it if law enforcement asks. Will you ask?"

She was quiet long enough that I heard the radiator tick. Then: "I'll request the records. But Whitlock —" and here she leaned forward, and the heron became something with teeth — "if this account turns out to be run by someone close to you, I need to know you'll tell me. Not protect them. Not investigate them yourself like some amateur detective in one of your own episodes."

"Would you believe me if I said yes?"

"No." She almost smiled. "But I'd respect the honesty."

* * *

The forum admin turned out to be a retired schoolteacher named Dev in Manchester, who responded to the police records request within a day and, unprompted, sent me a follow-up email that began *Dear Ms. Whitlock, I hope this finds you well, and I hope you don't mind a fan writing, I've listened to all forty-one episodes twice.* Dev was delighted to help. Dev also included, in a P.S., the IP history he'd already pulled himself because "it seemed urgent, given the news."

Every login from *ValeWatcher* traced back to the same block. Not a home address. A commercial node.

I cross-referenced it, and then cross-referenced it again because I didn't believe the first answer, and the answer didn't change: the defunct internet café on Corben Street. NetAccess. Eleven minutes' walk from the gates of Vale House.

An internet café. In 2024. I actually laughed out loud, alone in my studio, and the laugh came out wrong, too high, the laugh of a person standing too close to an edge.

NetAccess had closed two years ago according to the local paper's archive — squeezed out, the obituary-ish article said, by phones in every pocket. But the building still stood, sandwiched between a nail salon and a shuttered bakery, its faded sign still bolted above the door. And here was the thing that kept me staring at the map: whoever ran *ValeWatcher* hadn't just used the café. They'd used it exclusively, fourteen months, never once slipping onto a home connection, a library, a phone hotspot. That wasn't caution. That was ritual. That was someone who *liked* the location. Who maybe liked being eleven minutes from the house where it all began while they typed.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited and stayed — someone who wanted the trail to lead there. Someone who understood that a traceable IP near Vale House was itself a piece of staging. A clue placed as carefully as shoes facing a door.

I got my coat. The blue one, which felt like tempting fate, and then felt like refusing to be dictated to, which is a distinction I clung to all the way across town.

* * *

Corben Street smelled of rain and frying oil. NetAccess sat behind a chain-link gate with a REAL ESTATE INQUIRY sign zip-tied to it, windows papered over from the inside except for a strip at eye level where the paper had peeled. I pressed my face to the gap like a burglar in a children's book.

Inside: rows of empty desks, monitors gone grey with dust, chairs pushed in neatly as if the last customers had simply ascended. Except — I narrowed my eyes — not all of them. One chair, third row, window side, was pulled out at an angle. And the dust on its desk was disturbed in a way the others weren't. Smudged. Recent.

Someone had been using a dead café as a writing desk.

"Can I help you?"

I startled hard enough to bang my elbow on the gate. Behind me stood a small woman in a nail-salon smock, holding a cigarette she clearly wasn't supposed to smoke out there.

"Sorry — I'm researching. Was this place open long?"

"Eleven years. Died slow." She nodded at the building. "You a writer? We get writers. Well, we *got* one."

My pulse went from jog to sprint without passing go. "Got one?"

"Regular. Came most weeks, couple years now. Always the same machine, third row. Paid cash, bought the hour, never the coffee." She shrugged. "Quiet type. Kept to themselves."

"What did they look like?"

The woman's eyes did something careful then. People who live above shops develop a talent for knowing when a question has weight behind it, and hers suddenly had all of its weight showing.

"Hood up, mostly. Gloves, when it was cold." A drag of the cigarette. "Youngish. Small hands. That's all I'll say, and I didn't even say that."

Small hands. Youngish. I thanked her, wrote down nothing because I didn't trust my handwriting not to shake, and walked back down Corben Street feeling the pursuit energy curdle into something else. Because here was the problem with a suspect described as *youngish, small hands*: it fit half the people I knew, and it fit none of them better than it fit the person I least wanted it to fit.

* * *

Theo was at his desk when I got back to the studio, hunched over the mixing board with his headphones on one ear, which meant he was pretending to work through something difficult. Theo pretending to work was indistinguishable from Theo working, which had always been part of his charm and was, I noted with unease, also a useful property for other purposes.

"Where've you been?" he asked, not looking up.

"Corben Street."

That got him. His head came up, and for a fraction of a second — less than a second, a sliver — his face did something complicated. Not guilt exactly. Something adjacent to guilt. The expression of a man who has just heard a floorboard creak in a house he thought was empty.

"Corben Street," he repeated. "What's out there?"

"A dead internet café and a witness with good eyes." I hung my coat on the rack, watching him in the reflection of the darkened studio glass. "*ValeWatcher* logged in from there. Every session. Fourteen months."

"The superfan?" He said it lightly. Too lightly, the way people say things when they're buying time to decide how much surprise to display. "The one who emailed about the finale? I answered that one, remember. Told them the release date was internal."

"I remember." I did remember. I remembered everything about that email now, replaying it, searching it for the thing I'd missed. "You told them the date, Theo. Which means they knew there *was* a finale before anyone else did."

He turned back to the board, fiddled with a fader that didn't need fiddling. "Half the internet guessed we'd wrap after fifty episodes. It's arithmetic, not prophecy."

"It's not arithmetic." I kept my voice level, pleasant, the voice I use for interviews when I'm letting someone hang themselves gently. "The account referenced the *content* of the unreleased episode. The detail about the east wing. Nobody guessed that. Nobody could have."

Silence. The board hummed. Somewhere in the building a pipe knocked twice, like a polite ghost.

"Isandro." He swiveled to face me fully, and his face was open now, hurt, honest-seeming, and God help me, I wanted to believe it so badly it felt like vertigo. "Three years. Three years we've built this together. You think I'm leaking your research to some forum ghoul?"

"I think somebody is."

"Then let's find out who." He spread his hands. "Give me access to whatever you've got. I'll help you dig. That's what we do, right? We dig."

And that was the moment I understood something that made my stomach drop through the floor: I couldn't tell whether that offer was loyalty or strategy. The sentence worked perfectly either way. It was the most polished alibi yet — not an alibi of whereabouts but of character, and those are the hardest kind to break.

"Sure," I said, smiling. "We dig."

* * *

**Theo**

After she left, he sat at the board for a long time without touching it.

Then he opened his laptop, went to the folder he had promised himself a hundred times he would delete and a hundred times hadn't, and deleted it. Dragged it to trash, emptied the trash, held the keys down until the machine confirmed what he'd done. The progress bar filled. Done. Gone.

Except that wasn't quite true, and he knew it wasn't quite true, because the file had been copied once, months ago, by someone who'd asked to borrow his drive "for backup" and smiled while asking, and he'd handed it over without thinking, the way he handed Isandro coffee, the way he handed everyone everything, because being useful was the only armor he'd ever owned.

He stared at the empty folder view. Outside, rain started ticking against the studio window.

*They asked,* he reminded himself. *They asked and I said yes. That's not the same as—*

But it was, and he knew that too. He'd known it since the second body. He'd been paid — no, not paid, *funded*, the checks had come through the production account, legitimate-looking, deniable — and he'd told himself it was just a sponsor, just a patron of the arts, just someone who loved the show. Everyone loves the show. That's the whole problem with the show.

His phone buzzed on the desk. A message from a number with no name attached:

*Good. Now act normal. She trusts you. Keep it that way.*

He typed *who are you* and deleted it. Typed *I'm done* and deleted that too. Typed nothing at all, set the phone face-down, and put his headphones back on over ears that had stopped hearing music weeks ago.

In the empty folder on his screen, the cursor blinked in the dark like a small patient heartbeat.

* * *

I spent the evening in the archive room with the door locked, which was theatrical, since the real danger had a key, but theater keeps the nerves fed. I laid out everything: the forum posts, the café, the witness's small youngish hands, the funding records Theo had cheerfully handed over that afternoon — clean, all clean, sponsors verified, invoices tidy, a little *too* tidy, every document present and correct like a student who'd memorized the exam.

And underneath it all, one thread I hadn't pulled yet, because pulling it scared me more than the rest combined.

*ValeWatcher*'s earliest posts, fourteen months ago, weren't about the murders-to-come. They were about the money. Probate. The estate settlement of 1972. The dispersal of Marguerite Vale's assets among cousins who'd barely known her. *ValeWatcher* had begun, before anything else, as an accountant of grievance — itemizing, line by line, who profited from the erasure.

Which meant the killings might not be the beginning of the story at all. They might be the ending of a much older one, written in ledgers and sealed filings, in documents someone had died — or killed — to keep shut.

I pulled on my jacket and reached for the probate index.

If I wanted to know who *ValeWatcher* was, I first had to find out what they'd inherited.
