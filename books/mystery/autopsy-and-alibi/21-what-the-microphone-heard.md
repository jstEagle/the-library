## 21. What the Microphone Heard

The interview room at County Central smelled of burnt coffee and floor wax, and Camille sat in it like a woman waiting for a bus she'd already decided to miss.

They'd let me in because Okonkwo had learned, over the course of three corpses and one very long autumn, that there were doors I could open that warrants couldn't. She stood by the mirror with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of someone who'd stopped being surprised by me somewhere around the second body and had simply budgeted for it.

Camille looked smaller than she had a week ago, when I'd watched her being led out of Vale House with her wrists cuffed and her chin up, as if the whole thing were an award ceremony and she'd finally been recognized. Without the coat — my coat, the one they'd cut off her shoulders in the east wing — she seemed to have shrunk into herself. Her hair was flat. Her hands were folded on the metal table with the composure of someone who had rehearsed this.

"Isandro," she said. Not surprised. She'd known I'd come. Of course she had. She'd built me, in a sense, episode by episode, funding me through a donor account I still couldn't say aloud without my throat tightening.

"Camille."

"You look terrible."

"I've been told it's the lighting." I sat down across from her. The chair was bolted to the floor, which felt like a comment. "You wanted to see me."

"I wanted to ask you something." She leaned forward, and for a moment I saw the researcher I'd hired four years ago — the one who'd found the probate discrepancy in the Vale file that no one else had caught, who'd stayed late cataloguing cassette tapes with me, who'd laughed at my terrible jokes about dead heiresses. The person I'd worked beside every day while she was, apparently, also murdering people. That's the sentence I'll never get comfortable with. *Also.* As if murder were a side project. A hobby. Something she did between archiving and lunch.

"Ask," I said.

"When you make the last episode." Her voice was steady, but her thumb pressed hard against the back of her hand, whitening the knuckle. "The finale. You're going to tell them what I did. The confession tape is evidence, they'll want it aired, or they'll leak it, or some true-crime ghoul will buy it. And everyone will talk about me. The granddaughter who snapped. The broken adopted girl with the grievance. They'll make me the story."

"That's usually how it goes," I said quietly.

"I know how it goes. I've listened to your show. I've listened to forty shows like yours." She smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all week, and I'd spent the week looking at crime scene photographs of people I'd known. "I became a story once before, Isandro. In 1971. An unnamed infant in a sealed court record. A line item in a probate dispute. I was erased so thoroughly that even I didn't know I'd been erased until I was thirty-one years old and holding letters my adoptive mother should never have had." Her thumb stopped pressing. "So here's my ask. Air the whole truth. All of it. Not me. *Them.*"

"The family."

"The family. The lawyers who sealed the will. Judge Harrow, who signed the order the same month Aldous paid off his mortgage. St. Ambrose Maternity, which has no record of a birth that happened in its own building. The adoption agency that falsified the placement file. Fifty years of institutions doing exactly what institutions do, which is protect themselves and call it order." She spread her hands. "I'm not asking you to excuse me. I'm not stupid enough to think you would, and I'm not arrogant enough to want it. I'm asking you to be accurate. For once in this county's history, be accurate."

I thought about the nursery in the east wing. The preserved room, dust-sheeted and waiting, the crib with its chipped paint, the window Marguerite had supposedly fallen from — except she hadn't fallen, she'd died in childbirth in that room while her family decided downstairs whether the child was worth more alive or gone. I thought about the midwife's deathbed letter, the hospital ledger with its careful gap, the second will that named *the child of Marguerite Vale, born or unborn* and then vanished from the record like smoke.

I thought about how easy it would be to give them Camille. How clean. One broken woman, case closed, podcast downloaded two million times.

"I can't promise you anything," I said. "Okonkwo controls the evidence. The DA controls the trial. There will be a trial, Camille. People will talk about you regardless."

"People will talk regardless," she agreed. "But you decide what they talk *about*. You taught me that. Every episode, you chose the frame. The missing heiress, not the pregnant girl. The eccentric rich family, not the machine. You framed it their way for three years because their way was the only version anyone would give you." Her eyes were dry. I don't think she'd cried since the east wing; I think she'd used it all up decades ago, in some stranger's kitchen, being told she was adopted and special and lucky. "Frame it mine. Frame it *true*."

* * *

Okonkwo walked me out past the booking desk, and we didn't speak until we were in her car, which she'd parked where the security cameras were famously broken, because Rhea Okonkwo believed in privacy the way other people believe in God — privately, and with suspicion of anyone who performed it.

"She asked you to bury her," Okonkwo said.

"She asked me to stop making her the headline."

"And what are you going to do?"

I looked out the window at the parking lot, at the gray light coming down like a verdict. "What I should have done three years ago."

Okonkwo was quiet for a long moment. Then: "The confession recording. Legally, it's hers now — solicitor-client privilege doesn't apply, but she made it voluntarily to you, and her lawyer will fight any broadcast. It'll take months."

"I know."

"But if you were to build an episode that didn't need it," Okonkwo said, staring straight ahead, "an episode built entirely on documents — the sealed will, the probate payments, the maternity records, the judge's mortgage — documents that are already public record or already in my discovery file, which is technically supposed to be confidential but has developed a mysterious leak—"

"Rhea."

"—then no one could stop you, and no one could claim you compromised the trial, because you'd be saying nothing the prosecution isn't already going to say in open court." She turned to me then, and her face was carefully neutral, which for Okonkwo was practically a signed affidavit. "I'm not telling you to do this. I'm telling you I checked the weather, and the studio roof is fine, and I have no idea what you do in there."

"You're a good cop," I said.

"I'm a tired cop. Go home, Isandro. Write the thing."

* * *

It took eleven days.

Theo came back for it. That matters to me more than I can probably convey in prose, so I'll just state it: Theo, who'd been arrested on courier evidence manufactured to frame him, who'd spent two weeks under suspicion partly because I'd suspected him myself — Theo came back to the studio on the first morning and set two coffees on my desk without a word, and then sat down at his mixing board and said, "Okay. Structure. What are we doing?"

"We're doing the family," I said. "All fifty years. We're doing the will, the sealing, the money. We're doing the maternity ward and the adoption mill and the judge. We're doing every institution that touched this case and laundered it."

He nodded slowly. "And her?"

"Her name gets said once. At the end. In context. No confession audio. No psychology. No 'what drove her.' She's not a character study. She's a consequence."

Theo looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face made him turn to his board and start pulling faders. "Then we'd better get it right," he said. "Because if we're only saying her name once, it has to land."

We worked fourteen-hour days. Bram came out of retirement — actual retirement, three weeks of it — to verify every document against the physical archive, because if we were going to burn down fifty years of institutional respectability, we were going to do it with citations. He arrived with his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and a box of certified copies and the grim joy of a man who has finally been given permission to be pedantic at scale.

"Page forty-seven," he announced on day six, tapping a probate filing. "Aldous Vale's law firm billed the estate for 'records management services' the same week the second will was sealed. Fourteen hundred dollars. In 1971 that's a car."

"Put it in."

"It's already in. I put it in yesterday. I just wanted to say it out loud."

Camille's research — the work she'd done under her own name, before everything, the brilliant archival digging that had made me hire her — went in too. Credited. Fully credited, in the show notes and in the narration. If we were being accurate, we were being accurate all the way down.

The hardest part was the middle section: Marguerite herself. Eight months pregnant, confined to the east wing, attended by a midwife rather than a doctor because a doctor files paperwork. Dying in that preserved room while her cousins' lawyers met downstairs. I wrote it eleven ways. Theo kept sending it back. Too novelistic, he said. Too cold. Too much you, he said, meaning too much of my voice performing grief instead of delivering fact. On day nine I finally stripped it to the bones — dates, records, the midwife's letter read aloud in full, in her own words, recorded off a microcassette by a hospice nurse in 1998 — and it was devastating precisely because nobody was performing anything. The truth didn't need production value. It had waited fifty years. It could wait out my ego.

On the eleventh night, Theo played the final mix top to bottom, and the studio went quiet in that specific way studios go quiet when something is finished — not empty silence but held silence, the room itself listening.

Ninety-four minutes. The whole machinery of it: the will, the seal, the payment, the birth, the falsified registry, the adoption, the disinheritance of a child who legally had never existed, and then the long decades of everyone profiting from the erasure — the trust funds, the endowed wings, the charitable foundations with Vale names on them, all of it built on a sealed order and a dead mother and a living girl handed to strangers with her history amputated.

At the end, ninety-two minutes in, I said her name. Once. In context.

*Marguerite Vale's daughter lived. She was adopted out under a falsified name, raised without her history, and denied twice — once by the family that erased her, and once, decades later, by the inheritance that was stolen before she knew it existed. Everything else you've heard about this case — the mystery, the glamour, the tragedy — was written by the people who benefited from that erasure. This episode is the correction.*

Then, after a pause Theo insisted on and I'm glad he did:

*Her name is not ours to give. But her grandmother's is. Marguerite Vale. Say it like it's the first time.*

* * *

We released it on a Tuesday morning, unannounced, no trailer, no promo cycle. Theo titled it simply: *Episode 94: The Record.*

By Wednesday it had been downloaded more times than the previous three seasons combined. By Friday, the county clerk's office had received so many public records requests about the 1971 sealing order that they issued a statement, which was itself a small masterpiece of institutional panic, confirming that the order existed and declining to explain why it had taken fifty years and a podcast to surface it.

Judge Harrow was dead, but his chambers issued a statement anyway, distancing the current bench from "practices of that era." St. Ambrose Maternity — now a regional health system with a very good PR firm — announced an internal review. The state bar opened a file on the defunct law firm's surviving partners. Two of the charitable foundations quietly amended their names. Aldous Vale's obituary, published months ago, began circulating again with corrections appended beneath it, the digital equivalent of a headstone being revised.

Petra Vale called me once, at 11 p.m., and let it ring twice before hanging up. I understood. There was nothing left to negotiate. The belonging she'd offered me — the archives, the access, the seat at the table — had been real, and it was ash now, and some part of me grieved it even though I'd lit the match. That's the thing nobody tells you about burning bridges: sometimes you can see, from the far bank, exactly how beautiful the bridge was.

Okonkwo reported that the DA's office was "recalibrating" its approach — which, translated, meant that Camille's prosecution would now proceed alongside a much larger, much more embarrassing inquiry, and that the state would prefer, very strongly, that the whole thing be resolved quietly. Quietly, for once, meant fairly. Institutions move toward fairness the way glaciers move toward the sea: slowly, reluctantly, and only when the ground itself has shifted.

I visited Camille twice more before the trial. The first time she asked only one question: "Did you say my name?"

"Once. At the end."

She closed her eyes. "Good," she said. "Once is right."

The second time, she didn't ask anything. She just said, "Thank you," in a voice so small I pretended not to hear it, and we both let the pretense stand, because some mercies only work if they're deniable.

* * *

The night after the second visit, I went back to the studio alone. Everyone else had gone home. The building hummed its nighttime hum, and the ON AIR sign glowed its patient red, and I sat in the dark with the faders down and listened to nothing for a while, which is a thing I recommend to no one and cannot stop doing.

Three years ago I'd started chasing Marguerite Vale because she was a good story — a missing heiress, a sealed house, a cold file with teeth. I'd said that out loud, in episode one, with the confidence of a woman who believed stories were things you took from the world. It had taken me three bodies, one betrayal, one near-death in a sealed wing, and one impossible act of restraint to learn the other thing: that some stories aren't yours to take. Some stories are debts. You pay them by getting them right.

My phone buzzed on the console. A message from an unknown number, and I almost didn't look — I'd had my fill of anonymous messages to last several lifetimes — but something in the preview made me pick it up.

It was a photograph. Grainy, old, scanned from a print: a young woman standing at a nursery window with winter light on her face, one hand resting on the glass. On the back, in faded ink, someone had written a date — February 1971 — and a single line.

*She knew about the baby. Ask the gardener.*

Below the photo, the unknown number had typed one more message:

*I've been listening since episode one. I think there's one more person the record missed.*

I stared at it for a long time. Outside, the rain had started again, soft against the studio windows, and the red ON AIR sign reflected in the glass like a small persistent heart.

One more person the record missed.

I reached over and opened a new project file. Untitled. Empty timeline. Ninety-four minutes of silence, waiting to be filled.

"Well," I said, to the microphone, to the room, to whoever was listening. "Let's find out."
