## 1. The Retainer

The rain had been falling on Marrow Street since Tuesday, and by Thursday afternoon it had worked its way into everything — the awning's seams, the mortar of the brick, the ledger's soft corners, and finally, it seemed, into Beatrix Whitlock herself, so that she sat behind the counter of the Blackwood & Vale Bookshop — no relation, she'd told a hundred customers over the years, and always with a smile that had gone thin as tissue — feeling damp in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

She was counting bills.

Not money. Money would have been a pleasure, a luxury, an indulgence she could no longer afford. She was counting the other kind — the white and pastel rectangles that arrived in the window of the shop's brass mail slot and migrated, by some gravity of their own, to the drawer beneath the register, where they accumulated like sediment. Electric. Gas. The wholesaler. The wholesaler again, with a red stamp this time. The insurance man who had once been so courteous and now wrote in the clipped, apologetic tone of a man who had stopped expecting to be paid. She had them spread across the counter in a fan, and she was counting them the way other people counted rosaries, touching each one, saying nothing, letting the arithmetic do its quiet violence.

Four hundred and twelve pounds. That was what the fan came to, if you added the red-stamped ones twice, which she did, because honesty was a habit she'd never managed to break even when it hurt her. Four hundred and twelve pounds owed, against a till that held, if she was generous with the coins, sixty-three.

Outside, the street did what Marrow Street did in rain: nothing. The butcher's next door had closed at two. The florist across the way had turned her sign around weeks ago and hadn't turned it back. A man in a grey coat passed the window without looking in, and Beatrix watched him go with the particular attention of someone who had spent thirty years watching people pass windows, doors, alibis, and the scenes of things.

Retired, she reminded herself. You retired. You chose this.

The word *chose* sat in her chest like a stone in a shoe.

She was sixty-one years old, and her hands still remembered things her life had no use for — how to hold a torch so the beam wouldn't catch the glass, how to read a room's silences, how to stand in a doorway and know, before anyone spoke, whether the person inside was grieving or lying. Those hands were currently occupied with a pencil, totalling a column of shame, and she set the pencil down when the bell above the door gave its small, tired ring.

* * *

The man who came in out of the rain was wet in the way of someone who had owned an umbrella and forgotten it, or lost the argument with the wind. Medium height, medium years, a suit that had been good three winters ago and was trying its best. He stood just inside the door and dripped, and looked around the shop with the expression of a man who had come to a bookshop for the first time in his life and wasn't sure of the protocol.

"Are you open?" he asked, though the sign said so, and though he had, she would later learn, walked past the window twice before coming in.

"We're open," Beatrix said. "The books are for sale, the chair is for sitting, and the tea is free if you're buying something. House rules."

He almost smiled. Then he saw the fan of bills on the counter, and the almost-smile died of embarrassment, and she swept them into the drawer with a motion that was too quick to be casual and closed it with her hip.

"Detective Chief Inspector Whitlock," the man said. Not a question. He'd rehearsed it.

"Retired," she said. "It's on the sign. The little one, in the window. 'Formerly of the Met.' People find it charming. I find it necessary."

"Elias Crane." He crossed the floor and offered a hand, and she took it, and his was cold and damp and shook — not visibly, not the way a drunk's shakes, but the way a wire shakes when something is humming through it. "I'm a solicitor. Crane, Fenwick and — well. Crane and Fenwick, now. My practice is mostly probate and property. Conveyancing. Wills. The quiet end of the law."

"And yet," Beatrix said, "you've come to the loud end of a bookshop to find a retired policewoman. Sit down, Mr. Crane. You look like a man who's been standing at bus stops in his head all day."

He sat. She made the tea because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't trembling in sympathy with his, and because the ritual of it — the warming of the pot, the four minutes exactly — was the last piece of discipline she had kept from her working life. She set the cup in front of him and watched him hold it without drinking.

"Mr. Crane. I'm going to save us both some time. I don't do consultations. I don't do advice. I sell books, and I read them, and once a year I get asked to consult on a documentary and I say no. If you've read about me in the papers—"

"I haven't," he said, and then, with the honesty of the truly frightened, "I asked someone. A man at the court. He said if I needed something looked at properly, and quietly, I should find the woman who used to be called the Undertaker, because nothing stayed buried around her unless she wanted it to."

The nickname landed in the room and sat there. Beatrix looked at him for a long moment, at the wet shoulders and the humming hands and the eyes that kept going to the window behind him as though the street might produce something worse than rain.

"Who are you afraid for?" she asked.

* * *

He told it badly, and she let him, because the way a man tells a thing tells you the thing. He started with the weather, then with his practice, then with a client he could not name, and she let him circle for the better part of ten minutes before she said, gently, "The name, Mr. Crane," and he put the tea down untouched and said:

"Judge Marcus Vale."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere in the stacks, the old radiator ticked and knocked, its ancient argument with the pipes.

"The Judge Vale," she said. "The one currently—"

"Awaiting trial. Yes. Corruption. Alleged corruption." Crane's hands were flat on the table now, pressing down, as if the words might otherwise float away and take him with them. "It begins in six weeks. Six weeks tomorrow. And my client — Judge Vale — has received—"

He stopped. Beatrix waited. She had learned, in a long career, that the second silence was always more expensive than the first, and she let him pay it.

"He's received letters," Crane said. "Three of them. Anonymous. The first said he would be *judged*. The second said the trial was a farce and the real verdict was coming. The third—" Crane's voice did something complicated, dropped half an octave and lost its polish. "The third gave a date."

"A date."

"Four days before the trial opens. And a place. His study. And a method that I will not repeat aloud in a bookshop, Mrs. Whitlock, because it was — it was specific. It was the kind of specific you can't un-know."

Beatrix looked at him, and the shop seemed to get smaller around the two of them, the stacks leaning in, the rain pressing at the glass.

"And the police?" she said. "A threat against a sitting judge is not a quiet matter. There are protocols. Protection officers. This is not a problem you solve by walking past a bookshop twice and dripping on the floor."

"The police are the problem," Crane said, and then he heard himself, and went grey, and pressed his lips together as though he could staple the sentence shut. "I didn't say that. Forget I said that."

"I forget nothing," Beatrix said. "It's the only skill I've kept."

She let him sit in it for a moment. Then she said, "You believe the threat comes from inside. From the institution. Which is why you can't go to it, and why you've come to me — a retired officer with no badge, no file, and no obligation to anyone in a uniform." She folded her hands. "Mr. Crane, do you know what you're actually asking me?"

"I'm asking you to watch him."

"I know what you're asking. I'm asking if you know." She leaned forward. "You want me to guarantee a man's life for six weeks. Not protect him — I'm sixty-one years old and I shop at the Tesco on Halden Road. Guarantee him. You want a promise that Judge Marcus Vale will not be murdered before his trial begins. That is not surveillance, Mr. Crane. That is prophecy. And prophecy is the one thing they never taught me at Hendon."

"I'm not asking for a promise," Crane said. "I'm asking for the attempt. And I'm authorised to make it worth your attempt."

He reached into his jacket, and Beatrix — whose body still lived a decade behind her retirement — felt her pulse tick up one notch before she saw that what he produced was an envelope. Thick. Cream-coloured. The kind of envelope that had weight the way stones have weight, that you felt in the hand before you understood it.

He set it on the counter between them, and did not push it toward her, and did not look at it, and looked at nothing but his own cold tea.

"Ten thousand," he said. "A retainer. There will be more if — when you need expenses. Surveillance equipment. Whatever the work requires."

Beatrix looked at the envelope.

She looked at it the way she had once looked at evidence — from slightly to the side, letting the periphery do the honest work — and what she saw was not money. What she saw was the drawer beneath the register with its sediment of bills. The red stamp. The insurance man's apologetic hand. The landlord's letter from September that she had read eleven times and answered none. The boiler man's estimate, which had been a work of fiction in the tradition of the great Victorian novelists. She saw the shop — her husband's name on the lease, her husband four years gone and the lease outliving him the way debts do — and she saw the arithmetic that had been running in the background of her life for two years like a machine left on in an empty room: *four months, three months, by spring, by summer—*

Ten thousand pounds.

"Mr. Crane," she said, and her voice came out level, which cost her something, "why me? Genuinely. You have money — this kind of money buys protection, buys discretion, buys any number of firms whose invoices say *security consultancy* and mean exactly this. Why does it buy a woman in a damp bookshop instead?"

Crane's hands resumed their humming. He looked at the window, at the rain, at the grey coat of the street.

"Because of who sent me," he said. "Judge Vale asked for you by name."

The radiator knocked. The rain went on. Somewhere down the street a door slammed and the sound travelled the wet air like a shot that had missed.

"By name," Beatrix repeated.

"He said — and I am quoting him, Mrs. Whitlock, because I wrote it down, because I write everything down now, it's a habit I've developed lately — he said: *'Find Whitlock. If anyone will understand what it is to be afraid of the right thing, it's her.'*" Crane swallowed. "I don't know what it means. I assumed you would."

Beatrix sat back. The envelope sat between them, cream and heavy and patient as a coffin lid, and she was aware — with the old, cold professional clarity that surfaced in her like a fish in dark water — that she was being handled. That somewhere behind this nervous solicitor and his rehearsed lines and his envelope there was a mind that had chosen her, measured her, priced her. That the request was shaped like a key and she was shaped like the lock, and neither of those facts was an accident.

She was also aware of the drawer.

That was the thing about need, she thought. It didn't announce itself. It didn't come in out of the rain and drip on your floor and ask for anything. It sat quietly in a drawer under the register and counted itself, four hundred and twelve pounds at a time, and it waited, patient as sediment, for an envelope exactly like this one.

Pride said: *walk him out. Pride said: a man who won't go to the police about a death threat is a man whose problems are the police. Pride said: you spent thirty years learning what people look like just before they lie, and this man has been lying since the bell rang, gently, in pieces, the way frightened men lie, but lying.*

Need said nothing at all. Need just opened the drawer, in her mind, and let her see the red stamp.

"Six weeks," Beatrix said. "You want eyes on him for six weeks. His home, his movements, his visitors. A report to you, and to no one else. And if the letters' date arrives and nothing has happened—"

"Then you'll have earned every pound and my permanent gratitude," Crane said. "And if something does happen—" He stopped, and the humming stopped too, and for one moment his face went completely still, the way water goes still over something deep. "Then you'll be the only person in this city who saw it coming."

*The only person who saw it coming.* She would think about that sentence later, in a different room, under different light, and understand it had been a prophecy after all — just not the one either of them intended.

She drew the envelope across the counter. It was heavier than ten thousand pounds had any right to be. She did not count it. Counting it would have made it real, and she wanted, for one more minute, to be a woman who could refuse it.

"House rules," she said, standing. "You drink your tea before you leave. You tell me how to reach Judge Vale's people — a name, a number, someone who can let me get close without alarms. And Mr. Crane—" she held his eyes, and watched him hold hers back with the effort of a man holding a door shut against weather, "—the next time you don't tell me something, I'd rather you said so out loud. 'I can't tell you that' costs nothing. A lie costs me time, and time is the only currency I've got less of than money."

"I understand," Crane said. He drank the tea. All of it, in three swallows, like medicine. "There's a housekeeper. Mrs. Okafor. She's been with the judge twenty years. I'll telephone her tonight."

"Tonight," Beatrix said. "Then we begin tomorrow."

He left the way he'd come, into the rain, and the bell rang its small tired ring behind him, and the shop settled around her like a house settling, stacks and shadows and the tick of the radiator arguing with the pipes.

Beatrix stood at the counter with the envelope in both hands.

She told herself the story she needed, then, the way she'd told a hundred witnesses to tell theirs — plainly, in order, with nothing added. *A rich frightened man wants a bodyguard with a bookshop. Six weeks of watching a paranoid judge drink tea behind high walls. Easy work. Surveillance, not investigation. In and out, and the drawer gets empty, and the boiler gets fixed, and by spring the shop breathes again.*

She told it to herself the way you tell a child there's nothing in the dark, and for the same reason, and with the same success.

Because underneath the story, in the place where thirty years of instinct kept its own ledger, a different entry had already been made, in handwriting she recognized and could not yet read: *A judge who asks for a retired detective by name. A threat with a date on it. A solicitor who lies like a frightened man and fears the police. Nothing about this was sent to you, Beatrix. It was addressed to you.*

She opened the drawer, took out the fan of bills, and put the envelope beneath them — the heaviest thing in the drawer, heavier than the debts, heavier, she suspected, than it looked.

Then she turned the sign on the door to CLOSED, and stood a moment in the grey light, listening to the rain write its long, illegible sentence down the glass.

Four days early, the date on the third letter would come due. But she didn't know that yet.

She knew only that the shop smelled of rain and old paper, that the till held sixty-three pounds, and that somewhere across the city a man made of precautions was waiting for her — and that she had just been paid, in full, to keep him alive.

She turned off the lamp. The dark took the counter, the stacks, the fan of bills, and the envelope with its patient weight, and held them all equally, the way the dark holds everything: without judgement, and without mercy.
