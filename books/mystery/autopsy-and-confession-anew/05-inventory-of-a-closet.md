## 5. Inventory of a Closet

Marguerite did not sleep. That was not unusual — night shift had long ago rearranged his relationship with darkness, taught him to treat it as a working surface rather than a rest — but tonight the sleeplessness had a different texture. It was not the pleasant, humming wakefulness of a dispatcher holding a city in his head. It was the wakefulness of a man lying in his own bed in his own apartment, listening to the walls.

The police had released him at 4:40 in the morning. Detective Okafor had said, "Don't leave town," in the flat tone of someone reciting a form, and then, at the door, in a different tone entirely: "Check your things. All of them." She hadn't explained. She had looked at him the way dispatchers look at a fare who pays with a bill that feels wrong — not accusing, just weighing.

So at nine, with the sun doing its indifferent morning work against the blinds, Marguerite Crane stood in his own bedroom and began to take inventory.

He was good at inventories. It was, if he was honest with himself, the only kind of prayer he had ever practiced. A dispatcher's mind is a closet: everything hung in its place, everything countable, everything retrievable by a system that does not forgive improvisation. Twelve cabs on the night rotation. Fourteen drivers, counting part-timers. Three hundred and eleven streets he could draw from memory. He had built his whole self out of the certainty that things stayed where he put them.

He started with the closet because the police had started there.

The coat was gone. He had known that since Okafor laid the evidence photo on the table — the charcoal wool overcoat, the one with the frayed left cuff he kept meaning to have repaired, photographed on a dead man in a drainage culvert off Halden Road. Seeing it in the photo had been like seeing his own handwriting on a ransom note. But the shirt was worse. The shirt had been a pale blue oxford, and it had been gone longer, and he had told himself a lie about it weeks ago — *must have taken it to the cleaner's, must have lost it, must have* — the small administrative lies a man tells his closet when the closet starts disagreeing with him.

Now he stood before the open door and did what he did every night at the depot: he counted.

Shirts on the rod: nine. He had owned eleven. Two oxfords missing, then — the blue and a white one he'd forgotten about until this moment, which meant the white one had been gone long enough to be forgotten. Suits: three, all present, all in their dry-cleaning plastic like shrouds waiting to be needed. Sweaters: five of six. The missing one was the grey lambswool with the burn mark on the right hem, the one he'd never thrown out because — and here his mind did the thing it had been doing all night, the small sideways slip, the trapdoor opening in a floor he'd walked on for seven years — because the burn mark had come from somewhere he couldn't quite picture. He remembered owning it scorched. He could not remember the fire that had scorched it. He remembered the fact the way you remember a phone number from a city you no longer live in.

He wrote it all down. Pen on paper, because paper couldn't be edited by anyone but him. *Grey lambswool sweater, burn at hem. White oxford. Blue oxford. Charcoal overcoat.* Four items. He sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the list and felt the first real cold of the morning come up through the floorboards.

Because here was the thing about the coat. The coat had not been lost. The coat had been *taken*, and taken recently — he had worn it eleven days ago, to the funeral of old Tomás Reyes's mother, and he remembered hanging it back on its hanger because the left cuff had caught on the rod and he'd had to jiggle it free. He remembered the specific small annoyance of it. Which meant that sometime in the last eleven days, someone had stood in this room, opened this closet, and lifted that coat off its hanger with the care of a man selecting a costume.

*With the care of a man selecting a costume.*

The phrase arrived in his head fully formed and unwelcome, wearing its meaning like the coat had been worn. Not stolen. Costumed. A dead man in a drainage culvert had been dressed — by someone, for someone, for a purpose — and the wardrobe had come from Marguerite's closet, and Marguerite's closet had been open to a stranger for at least eleven days, and Marguerite had noticed nothing.

His pride, that perfect record, that never-losing-a-case certainty, turned over in his chest like a body.

* * *

He worked outward from the bedroom the way he'd work a grid search, room by room, and he made himself slow down. The apartment was small — third floor of a building on Corvus Street, two rooms and a kitchenette and a bathroom the size of a phone booth — and he had lived in it for six years and believed he knew it the way he knew the backs of the cabs: every scratch, every rattle, every place a fare could lose a wallet. He had believed that right up until this morning.

The kitchen gave him nothing at first, and then too much. He kept his spare keys in the ceramic bowl by the door — depot key, apartment key, the key to Sasha's place that she'd given him years ago with the words *for emergencies, and you'll know one when you see it*. Three keys. He counted them twice. Three. But the ring itself was wrong — he was almost sure of it, the way you're almost sure of a voice on the radio. The ring had been brass. This one was duller, cheaper, and there was a hairline scratch across the apartment key that he did not remember making. He held it up to the window light for a long time, turning it, feeling like a man reading tea leaves, and finally set it down and wrote in his notebook: *keys — possibly swapped. Possibly paranoid.*

The *possibly paranoid* was a kindness he extended to himself and did not believe.

The bathroom told him nothing except that he needed to replace the caulk, and he was grateful to it for the mundanity, the way you're grateful for a quiet hour on the board between crises.

Then he went to the hall closet, and the hall closet was where the morning stopped being a search and became an autopsy.

The hall closet was not really a closet. It was the place where the past went to be stored — boxes he had carried out of the old depot apartment after the fire, boxes he had never fully unpacked because unpacking them would have required deciding what they meant, and he had decided, seven years ago, that the meaning was *closed*. That was the word everyone used. Closed. The fire was closed. Elias was closed. Marguerite's own hospital stay was closed, a file shut with a rubber stamp, and he had built his life on the sound of that stamp coming down.

He pulled the boxes out into the light one at a time and counted them against the inventory in his head, and the inventory in his head, for the first time in his career, did not match.

Box one: winter things, mothballs, a radio he'd meant to repair. Present. Box two: books, water-stained at the bottom from some old leak. Present. Box three — the box with the tape on the lid where he'd written, in his own blocky hand, *MISC — KEEP*, which was the closest he had ever come to writing a confession about anything — box three was lighter than it should be.

He knelt on the hallway floor and opened it, and the smell that came up was the smell of seven years ago, and his body reacted before his mind did: a tightening in the throat, a flinch, a readiness to hear an alarm that had stopped sounding before dawn on a night he could almost — *almost* — remember.

Inside the box: a stack of photographs, a tin of screws, a folded map of the transit district. All present. And a gap. A clean, rectangular gap, pressed into the dust-fuzz of the box's floor, the size and shape of a manila envelope.

He knew what had been in the gap because he had put it there himself, six years ago, when he moved in: the folder of papers from the fire settlement. The insurance correspondence. The investigator's preliminary notes — the ones that had arrived before the investigation was closed, before the closing had swallowed everything else. He had never read them closely. He had told himself that was grief. Sitting on the floor of his hallway with the empty shape of the folder pressed into the dust beneath him, Marguerite allowed himself, for the first time, the other explanation: he had never read them closely because some part of him had been afraid of what they said, and someone had known that, and someone had come and taken them, and that someone had counted on the fact that Marguerite Crane did not open box three.

That was the part that made his hands go cold. Not the theft. The *knowledge*. Whoever had taken the coat knew his closet. Whoever had taken the folder knew his silences. This was not a burglar. A burglar takes the television and the jewelry. This was someone who had studied him — his habits, his avoidances, the architecture of the things he couldn't look at — and had reached into his life and removed exactly the items that would hurt, and had left the rest arranged, undisturbed, dust unbroken, so that he would not notice for eleven days or eleven years.

His home was not a home. It was a set, and someone else had been dressing it.

* * *

He called Sasha at eleven. She picked up on the second ring, which meant she'd been awake, which meant she'd been awake the way he'd been awake.

"Tell me about the coat," she said, before he said anything.

"You heard."

"Okafor called the depot. Asked who else had keys to your place." A pause, and in the pause he could hear her smoking, the long slow drag she took when she was thinking rather than calming down. "I told her nobody. That's the truth, right, Marguerite? Nobody has a key."

"Nobody has a key," he said. "But somebody has my coat, my shirts, a sweater with a burn on the hem, and a folder of settlement papers I haven't opened in six years. They were in my apartment, Sasha. Recently. The dust wasn't even disturbed."

The silence on the line went on long enough that he checked whether the call had dropped.

"Sasha."

"I'm here." Another drag. "Listen to me. Don't touch anything else. Don't clean anything. And Marguerite —" her voice did something it almost never did, something he'd heard only once before, the night of the fire, when she'd found him sitting on the curb in a blanket watching the depot burn and had crouched down and said *look at me, look at me, you're here, you're here* — "did you call a locksmith?"

"No."

"Call a locksmith. Change the locks today. Not tomorrow. And then get out of that apartment for the day, because you're going to sit there all afternoon staring at your closet and it's going to eat you alive, and I need my dispatcher tonight."

It was good advice. It was the kind of advice he gave other people every night, in a calmer voice, over a calmer radio. He did not follow it. He changed the locks — the locksmith came at noon, a bored man who asked no questions and charged too much, and Marguerite stood in the hallway watching the old deadbolts come out of the door like bad teeth — and then he stayed, because the apartment was the only witness he had, and you don't leave your witness alone.

* * *

The afternoon light moved across the floor and he moved with it, and by four o'clock he had done what he should have done at nine: he stopped searching for what was missing and started studying what remained.

Because the remaining things had been arranged. He was sure of it now, sure the way he was sure of a voice on the board. The photographs in box three — the ones of the old night crew, the ones from the depot's last Christmas party, the ones of — he paused over one, a group shot on the loading dock, everyone squinting into the sun, and there was Elias Vann at the edge of the frame, half out of it, laughing at something outside the photograph — the photographs had been re-stacked. The edges didn't align. Someone had looked through them and put them back in a hurry, or in a specific order, and Marguerite sat on his floor with the stack in his lap and tried to read the order like a dispatch log.

And there was one more thing. He found it at half past five, in the coat pocket of the jacket he'd worn to the police station — a jacket he had not worn in weeks, hanging in the closet among the suits, a jacket that had been present and accounted for all day. In the left inside pocket, folded into a small square, was a page torn from a memo pad. His memo pad. The yellow one on his kitchen counter, the one with the magnet on the back, the one he used for fare notes and grocery lists.

The paper was blank. Both sides. He held it up to the window, then to the lamp, looking for indentations, ghost-writing, anything. Nothing. Just a blank yellow square, torn with a clean edge, placed in a pocket he did not use, in a jacket he had not worn, in a closet that had been open to a stranger.

It was not a message. Or rather — and this was the thought that finally made him stand up and put on his shoes — it was not a message with content. It was a message with a *shape*. *We were here. We read everything. We took what we needed and left you a page, because a page is what you'll try to write this on, and we wanted you to know we'd already read it.*

Or it was simpler than that, and crueler: *we can put things in as easily as we take things out.* A blank page today. Tomorrow, something written on it in his handwriting. A note. A confession. A receipt for a coat.

Marguerite stood in the middle of his apartment with the blank page in his hand and understood, with the clarity that comes at the end of a long shift when the board finally goes quiet, that his home had stopped being a place where he lived and become a place where evidence was being manufactured. Every object in it was now ambiguous. Every drawer was a question. The locks he'd changed at noon were a lock changed on a door that had been open for eleven days, or eleven months, or — he thought of the settlement folder, of how long ago the dust had settled into that envelope's shape — possibly years.

The killer — and he made himself use the word, made himself stand in the word the way you stand in cold water — the killer had dressed a dead man in Marguerite's clothes. And then had come here, to Corvus Street, to the third floor, and had walked through the rooms of the man who would find the body's wardrobe, and had taken the papers that might have explained the fire, and had left a blank page like a tip on a table.

*You're on the list,* said the empty envelope. *And we know what you don't remember.*

He packed a bag. Not much — the notebook, the pen, the tapes he'd never had a reason to play, a change of clothes, the photograph of the night crew with Elias laughing at the edge of the frame. He would not sleep here tonight. He would not sleep here again, maybe, until he understood who had been sleeping here with him.

At the door he stopped, hand on the new deadbolt, and looked back at the apartment: the counted closet, the re-stacked photographs, the yellow memo pad on the counter with its pages riffling slightly in the draft from the open door, as if it were trying to tell him something in a language of blank paper.

Then he went downstairs and did what he always did when the world stopped making sense.

He went to work. And it was only in the cab, halfway across the bridge with the depot's lights coming up ahead of him like a stage, that he remembered the detail that stopped his breath: the locksmith had found no marks on the old lock. No picks, no scratches, no force.

Whoever had walked into his home had walked in with a key.

And Marguerite Crane, keeper of the perfect record, the man who never lost a case, sat in the back of a hired cab and asked himself the question he should have asked at nine o'clock that morning, the question that would open the next door and the one after it, all the way down:

Who, in seven years, had ever had a key to his life?
