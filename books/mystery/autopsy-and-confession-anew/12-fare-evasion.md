## 12. Fare Evasion

The tail made its first mistake at 11:47 on a Tuesday night, which was the kind of hour Marguerite Crane trusted more than most people.

Nothing honest happened at 11:47. The drunks had either made it home or given up trying. The lovers had gone quiet or gone loud. The city sat in that thin band of time between last calls and first deliveries, and in eleven years of dispatching the night fleet out of the Halloway Depot, Marguerite had learned that anyone moving through that band with purpose was either working or hiding. He was working. The sedan idling half a block behind him, dome light dark, engine running, was hiding.

He'd spotted it outside the all-night laundromat on Corvair Street, where he'd gone to pick up a fare who never came — a call that had come in clean, a woman's voice, tired and specific, an address and a name and a please hurry. The fare had never appeared. The address had been a boarded duplex with a porch sagging like a bad sentence. And when he'd pulled away, the sedan had pulled away too, three seconds later, patient as a bill.

Marguerite drove. He kept his speed lawful, his turns deliberate, and he did what he had done ten thousand times from behind the dispatch desk: he listened to the city's rhythm and looked for the note that didn't belong.

* * *

The thing about tails — and Marguerite knew this the way he knew the fleet's maintenance schedule, in his hands and not his head — was that most people were bad at following because most people had never spent a night listening to someone else's night. Dispatchers heard the whole city at once. They heard Driver 6 report a fender-bender on the viaduct and Driver 19 say she was taking her break at the pie shop and they heard, threaded through all of it, the shape of a city breathing. You learned where a car could go without being noticed and where it couldn't. You learned that a tail always thought in straight lines, because a tail was trying to keep a fixed point in view, and the city was not built of straight lines. The city was built of loops.

So Marguerite took a loop.

He turned off Corvair onto Delancey, ran it to the dead end at the freight yard, made the U-turn that every driver in the fleet had made a thousand times and that no one else ever made on purpose, and came back around the block. The sedan followed. It followed too well — no hesitation at the dead end, no fumbling for the turn, just the smooth, bored compliance of someone who had already driven this exact route in rehearsal.

*Okay,* Marguerite thought, and his hands were steady on the wheel, and his heart was doing something less steady, something like a drum tapped in another room. *You've done this before. You've done this on these streets.*

He tried the second trick, the one he'd taught rookies: the fare stop. He pulled to the curb outside the Blue Lantern diner, put his hazards on, and sat. A legitimate tail — a cop, a creditor, a jealous spouse — would drift past or park and wait. This one parked. Directly across the street. In a loading zone. Where anyone with a radio scanner and half a brain knew the night patrol units idled between rounds, which meant either the driver didn't know the city or didn't care who saw him.

Marguerite went into the diner, ordered coffee he didn't want, and watched through the glass. The sedan sat. After four minutes, its driver stepped out — a broad man in a dark coat, face turned away, and he did something that made the coffee go cold in Marguerite's hands before it ever reached his mouth.

The man spoke into a handheld radio. Not a phone. A radio.

Marguerite couldn't hear the words through the glass, but he knew the cadence. Everyone in the fleet knew the cadence. Short transmission, clipped, and then — this was the part that lifted the hair on his arms — the man paused, and the pause had a shape to it. It was the shape of someone waiting for an acknowledgment. *Copy that. Over.*

The company's night band. The frequency that lived in every cab's dash unit, that Marguerite himself had managed for a decade, that was supposed to be as private as a family's kitchen table. Whoever this was had keyed up on their channel or one adjacent to it, and had waited for a confirmation in the old style, the style from before cell phones, the style the company had used the year of the fire.

* * *

Marguerite paid for the coffee he hadn't drunk and walked back to his cab the way a man walks when he wants to be seen being casual. The sedan's driver was back inside his car. Marguerite got behind his own wheel, keyed his dash unit — the one he'd installed himself, the one with a switch that cut the transmitter without cutting the receiver — and said nothing. He just listened.

The night band was quiet. Driver 19 checking off her last run. The depot's automated weather report. Static like snow. And underneath it, so faint he might have invented it, a second carrier — someone holding a transmit button without speaking, a breath of open microphone, listening to the band the way he was listening. Waiting for him to talk.

Marguerite set the handset down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a thing that might be wired.

*They know the codes.* The thought arrived whole and terrible. Not just the frequency — the codes. The old call-sign structure, the acknowledgment protocol, the pause-and-wait. That system had been retired seven years ago, the month after the fire, replaced with digital dispatch and encrypted terminals. The only people who still thought in the old cadence were the people who had worked the night of the fire. Drivers. Dispatchers. And whoever had been listening to them.

He pulled out of the Blue Lantern's lot and made his decision the way he made all his decisions: at speed.

* * *

What followed was eleven minutes of the kind of driving Marguerite had never once done with a fare in the car and had, in fact, only ever done in his imagination, on slow nights, when the depot was empty and the map on the wall looked like a puzzle waiting to be solved. He ran the gauntlet of one-way streets behind the cannery where the signage contradicted itself. He took the underpass with the low clearance that only fleet drivers knew was passable in a cab if you hugged the left lane. He cut through the hospital's loading loop, waving at the security guard who knew him, and came out the exit marked *ambulances only*.

The sedan stayed with him. Not perfectly — it lost him twice, and both times recovered with a speed that told Marguerite there was a second car, or a third, or that the man behind the wheel had the same map in his head that Marguerite had. At the viaduct, with the river sliding black below and the sodium lights turning everything the color of old tea, Marguerite did the last thing on his list. He stopped in the middle of the bridge, hazards on, and got out.

He stood in the open, hands visible, and looked back the way he'd come. The sedan was two hundred yards back, stopped too, and in the long amber light Marguerite raised one hand — not a wave, not a salute, just an acknowledgment — and keyed the dash unit through the open door.

"This is Crane," he said, using the old call format, the one from the year of the fire. "Dispatch to mobile. I see you. Over."

Static. Long static. And then, in a voice processed by distance and speaker distortion into something almost gentle, almost familiar, a reply:

"Copy, Crane. Nice driving. Over."

Then the sedan reversed, executed a turn too clean for the space, and was gone, and Marguerite stood alone on the viaduct with the river running beneath him and his own heartbeat loud as a second engine.

* * *

He didn't go home. Home was a word he no longer trusted — the apartment with the missing coat, the missing shirt, the missing photographs, the closet with its gaps like pulled teeth. He drove instead to the one place that still felt like his: the depot, at 2 a.m., empty except for the night mechanic and the smell of diesel and floor wax.

He sat in the dispatch chair he'd sat in for eleven years and pulled the old codebook from the bottom drawer, the one nobody had opened since the conversion to digital. He turned the pages and felt the paranoia in his chest sharpen, honed by fear into something almost like craft. Because here was the thing he was only now letting himself think: the tail hadn't just known the old codes. The reply had used *his* call sign format. *Crane.* Not the fleet designation, not the digital ID — the personal sign, the one from the old system, the one that had been assigned to him the year of the fire and retired with everything else.

Whoever was hunting him wasn't following him. They were *dispatching* him. Sending him to empty addresses, herding him through the city with phantom fares, confirming receipt when he checked in — running him the way he had run a hundred drivers, patiently, professionally, with full command of the network.

And there was one more thing, one small terrible thing, which he saw only now, sitting in the chair with the codebook open on his knees. The voice on the viaduct had been processed, distorted, anonymized. But underneath the processing, underneath the speaker static and the distance, it had had a cadence he knew. A rhythm of speech he'd heard every night for years, before the fire took it away from him along with everything else.

He closed the codebook. Outside, the first of the dawn-shift drivers was pulling in, headlights sweeping the bay.

Marguerite picked up the handset, switched to the old band, and made a decision that would either save him or finish him.

"Sasha," he said quietly. "It's Crane. I need to talk. Not on the radio. Over."
