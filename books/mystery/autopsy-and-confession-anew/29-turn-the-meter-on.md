## 29. Turn the Meter On

Sasha Voss had been driving the night shift for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one law above every other: a cab is never just a car. It is an ear, an eye, a rumor with wheels. People said things in the back seat they wouldn't tell their priests. They cried into their collars at red lights on Delmore Avenue and made phone calls that ended marriages and made fortunes, and Sasha drove them all through it, silent as a confessional booth with a meter running.

So when she stood up on the folding chair in the back of Kowalczyk's Garage at four in the afternoon — the hour when the day drivers were clocking out and the night crew was drinking bad coffee before their shift — and looked out at forty-odd faces, she knew exactly what she had.

She had a fleet.

"Turn your meters off," she said. "For five minutes. I need you to listen."

The garage went quiet in stages, the way garages do — the hiss of the air compressor first, then the radio somebody had going by the vending machine, then the low murmur of men and women who had spent decades talking over engines and could read a room like a road map. Dmitri leaned against Bay 3 with his arms crossed. Old Ferreira sat down heavily on a stack of tires. Renata stopped wiping her windshield. Even the new kid, Osei, three weeks on the job, felt the temperature change and shut his mouth around whatever he'd been about to say.

"You've all heard what happened to Marguerite," Sasha said. "Warrants issued this morning. His face on the news between the weather and the sports. They're calling him a killer." She let that sit. "You've also heard what happened to Dr. Hale, who was going to testify, and to Investigator Brandt before him, and to Elias Vann, who was supposed to be dead seven years ago and wasn't. You've noticed, I hope, that every single one of those bodies was dressed in somebody else's clothes. Somebody still living. Somebody the killer wanted to talk to."

She reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out a folded sheet of paper, soft as cloth from handling.

"I'm not going to stand here and tell you Marguerite is innocent. I'm going to tell you something better. I'm going to tell you I don't care." A ripple went through the room. She held up a hand. "Nineteen years I've driven nights with that man's voice in my ear. He has found every drunk who wandered off into the river district, every runaway kid, every driver broken down on the interstate at three a.m. He has never once left any of us out there alone. Not once. And now he's out there alone, and the people hunting him are the same people who signed off on burning down our depot with two men inside it, and I for one would like to return the favor."

Dmitri spoke without uncrossing his arms. "The company's watching everything. Transponders, dash cams, the dispatch system itself. You know that. Half our cabs have cameras in them now."

"They do," Sasha agreed. "That's exactly the point."

She unfolded the paper.

"Here's what I know about surveillance, because I've had nineteen years to think about it while you were all asleep in your beds. A camera doesn't see. It records. A transponder doesn't think. It reports. All of it — every bit of it — flows back to one room, one system, one man whose job it is to find the signal in the noise. And there is only so much noise a man can hold in his head before the signal drowns."

She looked around the garage, meeting eyes. Ferreira's, watery and sharp at the same time. Renata's, steady. Osei's, frightened and hungry.

"So we're going to give him noise. We're going to drown him in it."

* * *

It took two hours to build, standing around the coffee urn with grease on their hands, and it was beautiful the way only working people's plans are beautiful — no elegance, no cleverness, just weight.

Every driver in the fleet would work the next night like no night in the company's history. Every fare accepted, every detour taken, every radio call answered — but doubled, tripled, mirrored. When Renata picked up a fare on Corliss Street, Dmitri would key the mic and report a pickup on Corliss Street too, a block off, a minute apart. When Ferreira ran a credit card, Osei would read out a card number over the radio, wrong in every digit but the rhythm. The dispatch logs would fill with phantom pickups, phantom drop-offs, phantom drivers reporting phantom passengers in phantom locations, until the record of that night became a fog so thick that no analyst alive could pull a single true thread out of it.

"And buried inside the fog," Sasha said, "we move real things."

Because that was the second half of it, the part she'd saved. The dispatch network was a broadcast system. Every cab heard every call. Which meant that anything spoken over that band went out to forty cars scattered across the city at once — and any one of those drivers could be recording, could be relaying, could be passing a message to a reporter, a lawyer, a cop. Detective Okafor had already made contact; Sasha had met her in the parking lot of a laundromat at dawn, two women passing a flash drive like contraband cigarettes. The ledger Marguerite had lifted from the executive archive was copied and safe. What remained was testimony, corroboration, the human scaffolding around the paper — and the company would be listening for exactly that kind of traffic.

So they'd bury it. Real calls hidden among a thousand false ones, coded in the old way, the way drivers had always talked around dispatchers when they wanted privacy — route numbers that meant names, street corners that meant warnings, the idle chatter of a shift that never stops talking.

"The company thinks the radio is theirs," Sasha told them. "It never was. It's ours. We built it, call by call, night by night. Tomorrow night we take it back."

Ferreira raised his hand like a schoolboy, which was his joke and nobody laughed anymore because he'd earned the right to it. "And if they fire us? All of us? Whole fleet walks off the board in one night?"

Sasha smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile.

"Ferreira, they're murdering witnesses and framing my best friend. Firing us is the least scary thing they can do, and honestly? Let them try. Who's going to drive their cabs? The day shift?" Scattered laughter, tired but real. "No. Here's what happens if you're scared, or if you've got kids, or if you just can't — you say so tonight, no shame, and tomorrow you drive your normal shift and hear nothing and know nothing. That's fine too. This only works if everybody in it chose it."

Nobody left. That was the thing Sasha would remember afterward, in the years when she told the story — that not one of them walked out of the garage. Osei, three weeks in, raised his hand and asked which channel the false traffic should run on, like he'd been doing it all his life.

* * *

They started at sundown.

Sasha took Cab 12 out of the lot at 6:04 p.m., and by 6:09 the radio was already singing. Renata called in a pickup at the airport that Renata was nowhere near. Dmitri reported a fare dispute at a hotel bar that had closed two years ago. Osei, bless him, had written himself a little script of plausible complaints — backseat too cold, driver took the long way, passenger threw up, passenger didn't pay — and delivered them in rotation with the flat boredom of a man reading a grocery list, which was exactly right, which was perfect, because nothing draws suspicion faster than drama and nothing soothes it like tedium.

By nine o'clock the dispatch log was a river. By eleven it was a flood. Sasha pictured the compliance office downtown — she'd never seen it, but she imagined it now with relish: fluorescent light, monitors, Mr. Whoever-He-Was staring at screens blooming with impossibilities, two pickups at the same corner ninety seconds apart, a cab logged on the north side and the south side in the same ten minutes, forty drivers generating the telemetry of sixty.

Let him dig. Let him try to find the true thread.

Because threaded through it, spaced irregular as heartbeats, came the real traffic.

At 9:22, Ferreira keyed his mic and said, "Dispatch, 7, I've got a fare heading to the old cannery district, says he knows a shortcut through Ash Lane." *Ash Lane.* Nobody lived on Ash Lane. Ash Lane was where Marguerite was sleeping that night, in the back of a borrowed cab behind a shuttered print shop. Every driver on the band heard it. Every driver understood.

At 11:40, Renata reported a passenger "leaving the hospital, quiet fella, headed toward the water." The hospital was code for Okafor. The water was code for the marina, where the detective had arranged to receive a second copy of the ledger from a courier who was actually Dmitri's niece, who thought she was delivering birthday presents.

At 12:15, Sasha herself called in a false pickup two blocks from the compliance office, just to watch the panic. She imagined chairs rolling back. She imagined phones lifted. She hoped whoever was listening choked on his coffee.

It was, she thought somewhere past midnight, idling at a red light with her meter running on a fare who was actually asleep in the back seat, the best shift of her life. Nineteen years of driving alone in the dark, and it turned out she'd never been alone at all. The whole city was strung with her people, forty points of light in forty cars, and every one of them lying beautifully, loyally, in unison, for love of a dispatcher who had once talked a terrified rookie named Osei through a diabetic passenger seizing in his back seat, and a veteran named Ferreira through the night his wife died, and Sasha herself through the night she'd driven Elias Vann away from the depot and had come back shaking and been told, gently, by a voice on the radio, *you did what you were told, Sasha, go home, sleep, I'll take the log from here.*

He had covered for her. He had rewritten the log to protect her, seven years ago, with pages he probably shouldn't have touched — and now those same clean cuts were being read as evidence against him.

So no. There was no version of tonight where Sasha Voss drove a normal shift.

* * *

It was almost two in the morning when the tone changed.

She was parked on a rise above the river with her thermos, running her part of the fog — she'd been calling in phantom fares near the rail yard for an hour — when Dmitri's voice came over the band, casual as anything, reporting a pickup at Fifth and Halloway.

Then Renata, thirty seconds later, also Fifth and Halloway.

Then Osei. Then Ferreira. Then a voice Sasha didn't recognize, gravelly, probably old Manuszak from the day shift who'd insisted on joining after all — Fifth and Halloway, Fifth and Halloway, five drivers in two minutes all picking up fares at an intersection where there was nothing but a shuttered church and a parking structure.

Her blood went cold, and then hot. That wasn't the plan. That wasn't noise — that was a beacon. Either someone in the fleet had cracked, panicked, was trying to warn the rest of them about something —

Or someone outside the fleet had gotten onto the band, and was using their own trick against them, flooding their channel with a message none of them had written.

Sasha keyed her mic, kept her voice flat, gave the counter-sign they'd agreed on for *trouble, go dark, say nothing.*

"Dispatch, 12. Correction on my last — cancel that pickup, meter malfunction, returning to base."

One by one, the phantom voices fell silent. The real drivers acknowledged and went quiet. The band settled into static, and static had never sounded so loud.

And then, threading up through it, faint and distorted, a voice Sasha had heard only once before, seven years ago, saying goodnight to her outside a burning depot — a voice that belonged to a dead man.

It said an address. It said it twice.

Then the carrier dropped, and the night went quiet as a held breath.

Sasha sat with her hand frozen on the microphone, the engine ticking, the river sliding black below her. Then she turned her meter off, put the cab in gear, and keyed the mic one last time, soft enough that only her people would catch it under the static.

"All units. All units. You heard it. You know the address." A breath. "Light 'em up, and let's go get our dispatcher back."

Forty engines, across the dark city, turned over in answer.
