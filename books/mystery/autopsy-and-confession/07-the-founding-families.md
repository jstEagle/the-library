## 7. The Founding Families

The archive room of the Harrow County Historical Society smelled like dust and old ambition. Vesper had been there forty minutes before the pattern surfaced, and when it did, it surfaced all at once, the way a body surfaces in water—slowly, then everywhere.

They had come for the 1990s, but they'd started earlier, because that was how you worked a town: you didn't ask who killed a man, you asked who owned the ground he was buried in. The founding families of Ash Hollow were four names, and the four names repeated like a chorus through every ledger, every land grant, every photograph of men in dark coats standing at the edges of things.

Calloway. Merrick. Voss. And Aldous—the family that had given the town its only bank and its only judge.

Vesper spread the photocopies across a folding table and laid the five witness statements from the 1994 file beside them. Then, one by one, they matched them.

Dana Calloway, age nineteen in '94. The Calloways had run the quarry concession since 1911.

Peter Merrick, twenty-two. Merricks owned half the riverfront and all of the goodwill at city hall.

Lena Voss, eighteen. The Voss name was on the hospital wing and the library annex.

And Ruth Aldous, seventeen. Daughter of Judge Martin Aldous, who had signed off on the case's single arrest—a drifter who'd left town before the trial and drowned in the Ohio River two years later, ruled accidental.

Four witnesses. Four alibis that interlocked like fingers laced together. Four children of the four families that had owned Ash Hollow since before it was Ash Hollow.

Vesper sat back in the plastic chair and let the vertigo come. It came the way it always did—not as a thought but as a tilt in the floor of the world, a sense that everything they'd been standing on was a floor someone else had built.

Because here was the thing Vesper had missed, or refused to see, back when the file was fresh and they were still young enough to believe a clean timeline meant a clean case: the victim hadn't belonged to any of it.

Tomas Reyes. Twenty-four. No family in the county. He'd arrived in the spring of '94 with a surveyor's license and a contract to assess the old quarry property for a development group out of Cincinnati. An outsider with a measuring tape, walking the land the way outsiders do—without knowing which fences mattered.

The file said robbery gone wrong. The file said Tomas Reyes was found at the drowned quarry on a Tuesday morning, and four upstanding young people from four good families each saw nothing, heard nothing, and could account for one another down to the minute.

Vesper had believed them because their stories were seamless. That was the trap, and thirty years later Vesper finally understood it plainly: seamless stories aren't told by innocent people. Innocent people are messy. Innocent people contradict themselves about what time they ate dinner. Only rehearsed testimony is perfect.

And now two of those four rehearsed witnesses were dead under ambiguous circumstances, and a third had died yesterday afternoon while Vesper was driving toward him with questions, and somewhere out there a confessor was working down the list with the patience of someone who had waited three decades already.

But the list wasn't the point anymore. The list was just the echo. The point was what Tomas Reyes had seen at the quarry that made four children of four dynasties need him dead—and what he'd found that made them keep the story stitched shut for thirty years.

Vesper gathered the photocopies, thanked the archivist, and walked out into a gray afternoon that felt thinner than air should feel.

* * *

The answer came to them instead of being sought, which was how the big answers always behaved.

Ruth Aldous called the bookshop on Thursday morning, while Vesper was shelving a return cart of poetry no one ever bought. The voice on the phone was composed, pleasant, the voice of a woman who chaired things—galas, foundations, committees for the preservation of historic districts.

"Detective Oyelaran. Or is it still detective? I understand you've retired."

"It's shopkeeper," Vesper said. "Mostly."

"Well. Shopkeeper, then. I'd like to buy you lunch. There's a place outside town, past the county line, where nobody knows either of our faces." A pause, smooth as poured cream. "I think we have something to discuss, you and I. About 1994."

Vesper looked at the confession letter, locked in the drawer beneath the register. At the photograph Marisol had pressed into their hands three nights ago, the one that proved the timeline was staged. At Theo, asleep in the back room on a mattress that cost more in dignity than dollars.

"You could just tell me over the phone," Vesper said.

"No," Ruth Aldous said, and for the first time something moved underneath the composure, something old and tired. "No, I really couldn't."

* * *

The place was called the Halfway House, which turned out to be literal—a roadhouse straddling the county line with a dining room full of truckers and a kitchen that smelled like fryer oil and forgiveness. Ruth Aldous was already seated in a corner booth when Vesper arrived, a woman of sixty-odd with silver hair cut short and a coat that probably cost more than the bookshop's monthly rent.

She didn't order until Vesper sat down. She ordered for both of them without asking, which told Vesper she'd done this before—bought people, or tried to.

"You've been in the historical society archives," she said, once the waitress was gone. "I know because I pay a young man there to tell me who asks about the nineties. You pulled the land grants. The family ledgers." She folded her hands. "So you already know who we are. Let me save us both the theater."

"I'd appreciate that."

"Tomas Reyes found something at the quarry. Something he wasn't supposed to find, because he was hired by an outside firm and outsiders don't know what they're walking into." Her eyes stayed level, but her hands had gone white where they clasped. "The quarry wasn't just a quarry. It never was. Our grandfathers used it for things that needed burying, and our fathers kept using it, and by 1994 there were drums in the north pit that would have ended every one of us if a state inspector ever read a soil report. Reyes took samples. He photographed the site. He was going to send it all to his firm in Cincinnati, and his firm was going to sell it to the highest bidder, and the highest bidder was going to be the state."

"So you killed him."

Ruth Aldous didn't flinch. That was the most frightening part. "I was seventeen. Dana was nineteen. We were told to be at the lake house that night, all four of us, and to remember the evening exactly as we were told to remember it, and we did, because we were children and our parents were the law in that town. Judge Aldous made sure the investigation went nowhere. The bank made sure the development group settled quietly. And the four of us—" her breath caught, just barely, "—the four of us spent thirty years being grateful and sick about it in equal measure."

"And now your co-witnesses are dying."

"Yes." For the first time, the composure cracked, and what showed underneath wasn't fear of Vesper. It was fear, plain and animal. "Dana first. Then Peter. Then Lena last week. Someone knows, Detective. Someone has always known, and now they're collecting."

"The letter," Vesper said. "Did you get one?"

Something flickered across her face—surprise, then a bitter almost-laugh. "So it's true. It named you?"

"It named me."

Ruth reached into her bag and set a checkbook on the table. Old-fashioned, leather-bound, absurd. She wrote with a fountain pen, unhurried, and slid the check across the formica. Vesper looked down at a number with enough zeroes behind it to buy the bookshop outright, the building, the block, maybe the street.

"That's for stopping," Ruth said. "Not for silence—I'm past believing silence can be bought, we proved that in '94. For stopping. Walk away. Let whoever is doing this finish the list, because the list ends with me, and frankly, Detective, I've made my peace with that. But if you keep pulling this thread, you won't just expose us. You'll expose the town. The bank, the bench, the hospital wing, the library annex—all of it built on that quarry. Hundreds of people who never touched anything will lose everything. Is that justice? Is that what you want?"

Vesper picked up the check. Held it between two fingers like something dead.

"Thirty years ago," Vesper said quietly, "a man came to your town to measure dirt, and your families decided his life weighed less than your name. Now you're sitting here telling me the price hasn't changed. Same currency. Different counter."

"It's not different," Ruth admitted. Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. It was never delayed, Detective. Justice in Ash Hollow was purchased. Up front. In full. We're just the installment plan."

Vesper tore the check in half. Then in quarters. Set the pieces on the table between them like a small white confession.

"I don't want your money," Vesper said. "I want the fourth name on your list. Because if you're next, I'd like to be there before the killer is—and this time, I'm not losing anyone by an afternoon."

Ruth Aldous stared at the torn pieces for a long moment. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and steady at once, the look of a woman stepping off a ledge she'd stood on for thirty years.

"There's something you should know first," she said. "About who's doing the killing. I figured it out last month, after Dana died, and I haven't slept since." She leaned forward, and the diner noise seemed to fall away around her. "Reyes had a sister. Nine years old in 1994. She was in the car that night—he'd brought her along because he couldn't afford a sitter. She was waiting in the parking lot at the quarry when it happened. She saw all four of us leave that lake house together, an hour after her brother stopped answering his phone."

The floor of the world tilted again, harder this time.

"She was nine," Ruth whispered. "Nobody deposed a nine-year-old. Nobody even knew she existed. And she grew up, Detective. She grew up and she learned patience, and she's been counting us down like rosary beads."

Vesper was already reaching for the file in their head, flipping backward through thirty years of names, looking for a girl who would now be thirty-nine, who had walked into their bookshop or written to them or stood close enough to touch—

"My God," Vesper said. "I know who she is. I've met her."

Ruth went pale. "Where?"

"At the funeral," Vester said, standing, grabbing their coat. "She was standing right beside me."
