## 8. Mother's Letters

The boxes had been in the attic of the cottage for three years.

Noor knew this the way she knew the dew point in the crypt and the exact number of steps from the muniment room to the vestry — as a fact of her architecture, unexamined, load-bearing. Three years since the funeral. Three years since she had carried the last of her mother's effects up the narrow stair and set them down among the insulation and the wasp nests and shut the door on them with a firmness that had felt, at the time, like grief's proper etiquette. You did not open them. Opening them would have meant deciding what to keep, and keeping meant choosing, and choosing meant admitting that a life could be reduced to inventory.

She had spent thirty years reducing other people's lives to inventory. She could not do it to Marion Brightwater's.

But tonight the cottage felt wrong to her. It had felt wrong since the morning they carried Thomas Reeve out of her crypt wearing her clothes, and every attempt to restore order — the alphabetized spice rack, the books re-shelved by height, the kettle descaled until it gleamed like an reliquary — had only sharpened the wrongness rather than soothing it. Detective Ferro's parting words kept circling: *Someone wanted you in that crypt, Ms. Brightwater. Or wanted you blamed for it.* Either way, someone had studied her closely enough to copy the mended cuff of her grey coat, the one her mother had sewn for her the winter before she died, the stitches small and even as typeset text.

Someone had looked at her life with an archivist's eye. And Noor understood, with a cold professional clarity, exactly what that meant: somewhere out there was a finding aid to Noor Brightwater, compiled without her consent, and she could not catalog what she refused to look at herself.

So at nine o'clock, with the rain coming down like static against the attic window, she climbed the stairs with a torch between her teeth and pulled the cord on the bare bulb.

* * *

There were seven boxes. Her mother had labeled none of them, which was unlike her; Marion Brightwater had labeled the jam jars. Noor knelt on a sheet of dust sheeting and opened the first one with a conservator's hands — lifting flaps from the corners, never tearing, supporting the base.

Clothes first. A cardigan that still smelled, impossibly, of cedar and something floral gone to powder. Then photographs, loose in a biscuit tin: Noor at eight, gap-toothed, squinting on a beach; Noor's father, who had left when she was four and whom the photographs treated with a careful absence, cropped edges, turned-over backs. Then a box of kitchen things no one would ever want — a lemon zester, a gravy boat chipped like a wounded soldier — and Noor found herself laughing, one short wet sound, because grief was absurd, grief was a gravy boat, grief was the fact that she could not throw away a lemon zester.

The fifth box was heavier than it should have been.

Under a folded tablecloth she found her mother's working papers. Not personal papers — working ones. Wage slips from a cleaning agency. A laminated badge, photo faded to a ghost: **MARION BRIGHTWATER — ST. ALDHelm'S CATHEDRAL — CONTRACT CLEANING STAFF**, the apostrophe dropped, the lamination yellowed and cracked along one edge. Noor held it under the torch light and felt the attic tilt slightly around her.

Her mother had worked at the cathedral.

Noor ran the arithmetic automatically, the way she ran all arithmetic. Noor had been born in 1990. In 1999 she had been nine years old, living with her mother in the flat above the chemist on Chandler's Row, a fifteen-minute walk from the close. She had spent her childhood in and out of the cathedral the way other children were in and out of parks — her mother cleaning there evenings after school hours, Noor doing homework in the north transept while the vacuum hummed down the aisles, learning the building's smells before she learned its history. Of course her mother worked there. Of course. It was not a secret. It was simply a fact so old and so domestic that it had sunk below the waterline of memory, the way the crypt's Roman foundations lay beneath the Norman floor.

And yet.

And yet in thirty years of cataloguing the cathedral's dead, in thirty years of knowing every name in every ledger, Noor had never once thought to ask what her mother had seen from the other side of the mop. Elsie Marsh had vanished in October 1999. Noor had been nine. Had she been sitting in the north transept with her spelling book the week the chorister disappeared? Had her mother been polishing brass two corridors from a girl's last known footsteps?

Noor sat back on her heels. The bulb swung faintly on its cord, making the shadows lean and right themselves, lean and right themselves, like a congregation rising and sitting.

At the bottom of the box, wrapped in a tea towel, was a bundle of letters tied with green garden twine.

* * *

They were not love letters. The first thing Noor established — because establishing things was how she kept her hands steady — was their provenance and character. Cheap lined paper, torn from spiral notebooks, some of it written in ballpoint pressed so hard the words stood in relief on the reverse like braille. All of it in her mother's hand. Not letters received but letters written — drafts, or copies, or letters never sent, addressed variously to *Mr. H*, to *the Diocesan Office*, and once, in capitals that gouged the paper, to *WHOEVER IS IN CHARGE*.

Noor untied the twine with fingers that had begun, treacherously, to tremble, and read them in chronological order, which was also the order of a woman coming apart.

The earliest was dated March 1999.

*Dear Mr. H,* it began. *I am writing again about the matter I raised in January. I know you said the accounts are audited and I should not concern myself, and I accept that a cleaner is not expected to understand such things. But I clean the offices six nights a week and I see what is put in the bins and what is taken out, and twice now I have seen invoices for work that was not done. The scaffolding invoice from Halloran & Sons is for a fortnight when the tower scaffolding stood empty. I checked. Anyone could check. I am not accusing anyone. I only want someone to tell me I am mistaken.*

Noor read it twice. The scaffolding. The restoration fund. She thought of the ledgers in her own care, the tidy columns of the late nineties, and felt the first cold thread of something she would later recognize as dread: her archive might already contain the evidence, filed and numbered and sleeping, and she had walked past it for decades the way one walks past a family face in a crowd.

The next letter was June. Shorter. Angrier.

*Dear Mr. H. You asked me to come to your office and we spoke for twenty minutes and I left more frightened than comforted. You told me that the cathedral protects its own and that I should think about my position, and about my daughter, who is at the cathedral school. I did not misunderstand you. I am not a clever woman but I am not a stupid one either. I will say nothing further. But I want it written down somewhere that I said nothing because I was afraid, not because I was silent in my heart.*

*My daughter.* Noor set the letter down on the dust sheet and pressed both palms flat against her knees until the shaking stopped. The cathedral school. Yes — she remembered St. Aldhelm's Infants, the smell of floor polish and pencil shavings, the walk home past the lychgate. Her mother had been threatened with a nine-year-old girl's schooling, and Noor had never known, had grown up believing her childhood unremarkable, and the unremarkableness of it now seemed like the most elaborate forgery she had ever encountered.

Then October. The handwriting changed — larger, looser, the pen strokes ragged where the hand had hurried or shaken.

*I don't know who to write to anymore so I am writing to myself. The Marsh girl is missing. They are saying she wandered off or fell somewhere and the police are dragging the river. I saw her that week. Wednesday evening, the 13th, I remember because it was the night the heating broke and the radiators clanked all through evensong. She came into the cloisters corridor while I was doing the brass and she was crying, properly crying, and she asked me if the crypt door was ever left unlocked. I told her I didn't know, which was a lie, because I knew perfectly well that Mr. Oakes locked it at six and it was half past. She went off toward the vestry stairs. That is the last I saw of her.*

*I have thought about going to the police. But Mr. H's words are still in my ears about my daughter. And today a man came to the flat asking questions about me, saying he was from the insurance, but the insurance man comes Tuesdays and this was Friday and he had soft shoes. Soft shoes, in October. So I am writing it here instead, and I am putting it away, and God forgive me, I am keeping my mouth shut. If anything happens to me, whoever finds this: ask about the scaffolding. Ask about the invoices. Ask why a dean's office needs a new carpet when the roof over the children is leaking.*

Noor stopped reading.

The attic was very quiet. Rain, bulb, breath. Somewhere below, the refrigerator cycled on with its patient mechanical hum, indifferent as eternity.

*Wednesday evening, the 13th.* Noor's mind, trained on thirty years of cross-referencing, laid the dates side by side with terrible fluency. Elsie Marsh had been reported missing on Thursday the 14th of October, 1999; Noor had catalogued the newspaper clippings herself, had handled the yellowed pages a hundred times. The official record — the record she had helped preserve, had dusted and defended and called *complete* — said Elsie was last seen leaving choir practice Wednesday afternoon. Last seen by no one in particular, near the west door, in the general way of things.

But her mother's letter said Wednesday evening. Cloisters corridor. Crying. Asking about the crypt door.

Either the official record was wrong, or her mother was. And Noor knew her mother's handwriting the way she knew her own pulse. This was no forgery. This was a woman writing to herself in an attic-flat above a chemist because she was too frightened to write to anyone else, recording the last sighting of a missing child and then folding it away in a tea towel for twenty-six years.

*Ask about the scaffolding.* Noor thought of Thomas Reeve, dead in her crypt, the last living witness. The phrase *last living witness* sat differently now. Witnesses, apparently, came in plural, and were retired one at a time.

* * *

She took the bundle downstairs because she could not bear to read the rest in the attic, among the wasp nests and the dead seasons. She spread the remaining letters across her kitchen table under the good lamp, white cotton gloves forgotten — these were not archive documents, she told herself, these were her mother, and her mother had never once been handled with gloves.

There were four more.

One was undated but clearly later — the paper better, the hand calmer, a false calm, the calm of a woman who has decided something. *I have made my peace with it,* it read. *Not with what was done but with what I did, which was nothing. Noor starts at the secondary school in September and the bursar's office has confirmed the fees will be 'looked after.' I know what that means. I know exactly what that means, and I signed the paper anyway, because she is eleven and she loves the old building and I cannot give her much else. Let it be on my soul and not hers. She will never know. I will make sure she never knows.*

Noor read that sentence — *she will never know* — and heard it spoken aloud in her mother's voice, in the particular gentle finality Marion Brightwater used for closing doors. *We won't be discussing that, love. Your dad's not a subject, he's a weather system. Pass the salt.* A lifetime of small sealed rooms, and Noor had called it privacy, had called it dignity, had built her own life of sealed rooms in loving imitation.

Another letter, dated 2004, mentioned a name: *Peter Oakes was kind to me tonight, walked me to the bus stop though it was hardly dark yet. He is frightened too, I think. We did not speak of it. Nobody speaks of it. It is like the weather here, the thing nobody speaks of.*

Oakes. Sacristan Peter Oakes, keyholder number two, whose quarters Ferro's team had photographed yesterday morning looking like a storm had happened indoors. Noor stared at the name until the lamp haloed it. Her mother and the sacristan, walking to a bus stop in 2004, united by a silence so heavy it had its own climate.

The last letter was the shortest, and it broke her.

It was dated — barely legible, the ink feathered as if by damp or tears — to the winter of Noor's mother's final illness. *To whoever finds this. I am tired and the doctors use long words. I have burned most of what I kept, the invoices and the copies, years ago, in the garden, and I would burn these too except my hands are bad now and the matches frighten me. So these few go in the attic and God decides. I never stole anything. I never hurt anyone. I kept quiet to protect my girl and I would do it again and I am ashamed and I am not ashamed. Tell her — no. Don't tell her anything. Let her have the clean version. She always liked the clean version. She files everything so neatly.*

*She files everything so neatly.*

Noor put the letter down and walked to the sink and ran the tap and did not drink, just let the water run over her wrists until they were cold enough to feel like something other than what they actually felt like.

Her mother had known her. That was the unbearable part. Not the fear, not the bribes, not the soft-shoed man in October — those were horrors, but they were historical horrors, belonging to a world of 1999 and its men in offices. What was unbearable was that Marion Brightwater, dying, had predicted her daughter with perfect accuracy: *she files everything so neatly.* Her mother had understood that neatness was Noor's whole religion, that the ledger was her chapel, that order was how she loved the world without having to trust it. And she had counted on that neatness to keep her safe — had trusted that a woman who catalogued everything would somehow never catalogue her own mother.

For three years, Noor realized, she had honored that trust without knowing it existed. Seven boxes in an attic. An unlabeled door. She had been the clean version. She had been the finding aid that pointed nowhere.

* * *

It was past midnight when she cleared the table, laid the letters in sequence, and photographed each one with the same methodical care she gave to incunabula — raking light for the indentations, straight-on for the text, scale ruler beside them because her hands needed a task or they would do something worse than tremble. Then she sat with the October letter one final time and forced herself to do the thing she was actually trained for, the thing grief had been trying to prevent all evening: analysis.

Facts. One: Marion Brightwater observed Elsie Marsh alive and distressed on the evening of 13 October 1999, heading toward the vestry stairs, asking about the crypt. Two: the official record placed the last sighting earlier and elsewhere. Someone had adjusted the timeline — not the police necessarily, but someone whose account the police accepted. Three: her mother believed the disappearance connected to the restoration-fund irregularities, and was silenced by threat — specifically, by threat to Noor. Four: Peter Oakes shared the fear. Five: Thomas Reeve, chorister in 1999, witness to whatever happened on the evening of the 13th, died three weeks ago in a locked crypt dressed in Noor's clothes.

Six — and this was the fact Noor wrote on a card in her own neat hand and propped against the fruit bowl, because seeing it in typeset-like capitals made it real rather than merely unthinkable:

**SOMEONE KNOWS WHAT MARION KNEW.**

Because that was the arithmetic underneath everything, wasn't it? Reeve hadn't been killed and staged at random. The clothes, the mended cuff, the crypt — the frame was built from intimate knowledge of Noor's household and habits. And now she had learned that her mother's silence had been purchased with threats delivered by a man called *Mr. H* through an office that smelled of carpet. The past wasn't reaching toward Noor. The past had been holding her hand her entire life, gently, the way you hold someone still.

She thought of Dean Vellacott's warm voice at their last meeting — *the cathedral protects its own* was surely a sentence he'd never say aloud, but the sentiment lived in him like grain in wood — and she thought of his predecessor, the dean of 1999, whose portrait hung in the cloister gallery, whose name she suddenly could not recall without walking to the archive to check, and whose office, per her mother's letter, had needed a new carpet badly enough to falsify scaffolding invoices.

The rain had stopped. Through the kitchen window the cathedral towers stood black against a bruised sky, floodlit at the base, dark at the top, exactly as they had stood over her entire life — the building she had given everything to, the building that had, it now appeared, been quietly holding her mother's fear in escrow for twenty-six years.

Noor gathered the letters, returned them to the tea towel, and did not retie them with the green twine. Instead she slid them into a document case, and the document case into her bag, because she was done letting her mother's truth sit in attics. Tomorrow she would take the October letter to Detective Ferro. Tomorrow she would pull the 1999 restoration ledgers and find Halloran & Sons and the fortnight of empty scaffolding. Tomorrow she would stand in the cloisters corridor and time-stamp her mother's memory against the floor plan.

Tonight she allowed herself one minute — sixty seconds, timed, because ritual was how she survived — to press her mother's laminated badge flat between her palms and say, to the empty kitchen, in a voice that only shook a little:

"You kept quiet to protect me. I'm going to make noise to protect you."

Then she turned off the lamp, and in the dark her eye went, helplessly, to the card propped against the fruit bowl, and to the question she had not written on any card because she did not yet dare:

Who was *Mr. H*?

She had a horrible suspicion the answer was already filed in her archive, in a hand far more elegant than her mother's — and that she had dusted it, reverently, for thirty years.
