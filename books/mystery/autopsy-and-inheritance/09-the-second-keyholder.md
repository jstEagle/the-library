## 9. The Second Keyholder

I did not sleep. That was the truth of it, though I told the constable at the door that I had slept badly, which is the sort of lie a person tells when the truth sounds like madness. The truth was that I had lain in bed until three in the morning with the lamp on, staring at my own wardrobe as if it were a witness box. My grey cardigan with the mended left cuff. My winter coat with the horn buttons. The dead man in the crypt had worn their twins, stitched by someone who knew me well enough to know where the moth had got at the wool two winters ago.

At six I gave up on sleep and dressed — in clothes I checked twice against my memory of the photographs Ferro had shown me — and walked down through the sleeping close to the cathedral, because it was the only place I could think of where the wrongness might make sense. Archives reward ritual. Ritual rewards the frightened. I let myself in through the cloister door with hands that shook only a little, and I stood in the nave for a long moment while the east windows went from grey to rose above me, and I thought: someone stood here. Someone stood here and planned to dress a corpse in me.

That was when I heard the noise from the sacristy corridor. Not a loud noise. A dragging, and then a thud, and then silence — the particular quality of silence that follows a body settling.

* * *

Peter Oakes had been sacristan of St. Aldhelm's for nineteen years, longer than I had held the archive, and in all that time I had known him as a man knows a fixture: reliable, unremarkable, slightly stooped, smelling faintly of beeswax and brass polish. He kept the vestments, the plate, the candles, the keys. He polished things that would only be dulled again by Sunday. It was, I understood now, a life built entirely around custody — around holding things safe for other people. Which made what I found in his quarters all the more terrible, because someone had come to take, and Peter had stood in their way.

His rooms were off the south ambulatory, up a narrow stair behind the sacristy door — one of those doors everyone in the close could walk past a thousand times without once seeing. The door stood open. That was the first wrongness; Peter locked everything. He locked the candle cupboard, he locked the wine, he locked the vestment presses against moths and against the choirboys' sticky fingers. A man who locks a candle cupboard does not leave his own door open.

Inside, the room had been turned inside out with a thoroughness that frightened me more than violence would have. This was not rage. Rage is random. This was methodical: mattress slit along every seam and the stuffing pulled out in pale ropes; books shaken spine-down and stacked back on the shelf in perfect order, as if the searcher had tidied after themselves; the floorboards pried at the edges and pressed back down; even the brass candlesticks unscrewed from their bases and reassembled. Someone had been looking for something small enough to hide anywhere and important enough to tear a room apart for. And they had not found it, or they had found it and wanted me to think they hadn't.

Peter lay at the foot of the narrow bed, half under a fallen chair, and at first I thought he was dead, and something in my chest went cold and certain — *they are working through the list, and you are on it* — and then I saw the shallow lift of his ribs and heard the wet whistle of his breathing.

I knelt. His temple was swollen and darkening above the ear. His lips were split. There were bruises coming up on his forearms in the shape of fingers — someone had gripped him hard, someone strong, someone patient. His eyes moved beneath closed lids like a man running in a dream.

"Peter." I touched his shoulder. "Peter, it's Noor Brightwater. From the archive."

Nothing. The whistle of breath. Somewhere below us the cathedral clock gathered itself and struck seven, and the sound went through the stone floor into my knees.

I should have called an ambulance immediately. I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the whole point of what I have become since. I knelt there beside him for perhaps ten seconds — ten seconds that I will answer for — and in those ten seconds I looked past him at the room and did what thirty years of training made me do. I read it. I catalogued it. Because some part of me already understood that Peter Oakes unconscious on his floor was not an accident or a burglary, but a document, and documents are my native language.

The searcher had gone through everything except one thing. On the desk, under the window, sat a wooden box — the kind vestment repairs come in, the kind Peter used for buttons and thread. Its lid was open. Inside lay a tangle of ribbon, a spare cassock button, a thimble. Ordinary. Except that the dust inside the box had been disturbed in a rectangle, a clean absence, the ghost-shape of something flat removed — recently, within hours, because the exposed wood was still pale. Either the searcher had taken it, or Peter had moved it before they arrived. Or — and this was the thought that lifted the hair on my arms — Peter had hidden it somewhere else in advance, knowing someone would come, the way an archivist moves a file she knows is about to be audited.

Peter Oakes had been expecting this. Or at least fearing it. For how long?

Then the ten seconds ran out and I did the human thing instead of the archivist's thing. I called for help.

* * *

They took him to St. Werburgh's with a suspected bleed on the brain, and by nine o'clock the close was full of police again, and by ten the whole cathedral community knew, and by eleven everyone had agreed on the story they would tell: poor Mr. Oakes, a fall, a break-in, thieves after the communion silver. I heard the verger saying it to the flower ladies in the north transept with the smooth fluency of a sentence rehearsed. Thieves after the silver. As if thieves slit mattresses and pried floorboards and left the silver cabinet untouched, its lock untried, its contents gleaming.

Ferro found me in the archive. She didn't knock; she'd stopped knocking sometime around our second interview, which I had decided to read as professional courtesy rather than contempt.

"You were first on scene," she said. "Again."

"I heard a noise."

"At six in the morning."

"I couldn't sleep. I came to work. Archivists keep strange hours."

She pulled the chair out with her foot and sat down across from my desk, and I noticed she was carrying a clear evidence bag, and I noticed what was in it, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the building and kept going.

It was a key. Small, old, iron, with a paper tag wired to the bow. The tag said, in faded copperplate: *CRYPT — SUB-DOOR.*

"That was in Oakes's mattress," Ferro said. "Or rather, the void where the mattress stuffing had been pushed aside and packed back around it. Whoever searched the room missed it. Or—" she watched me "—he hid it there himself, recently. The stuffing was disturbed in a way the pathologist's people say doesn't match twenty years of compression."

"The sub-door," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "There isn't a sub-door. There's the crypt, and there's the walled-off section behind the north stair — the old charnel — but it's been sealed since before the war. Bricked. There's no key because there's no door anymore."

"There's a door," Ferro said. "We found it last night, after your ledger comments gave us the layout. Behind the brickwork there's a door, and behind the door there's a passage, and the passage is blocked with rubble about four metres in. But the door itself has a lock, and the lock is old enough that this—" she lifted the bag "—is probably its key. And here's the question I've been sitting with since five a.m., Ms. Brightwater, so I'll put it to you directly. Why does the sacristan of St. Aldhelm's keep a key to a sealed door hidden in his mattress?"

"Because he's the sacristan," I said. "Because keeping things is his entire vocation. Because—"

"Because he's one of three keyholders to the crypt," Ferro finished. "You. Him. And the dean. And two days ago a man was found dead inside that crypt, wearing your clothes, having entered through a lock that wasn't forced. And now keyholder number two is lying in a hospital with a fractured skull and a room torn apart by someone who wasn't after silver." She leaned forward. "You see the pattern. Say you see the pattern."

"I see the pattern."

"So either Oakes is involved and someone is cleaning up loose ends, or Oakes knows something and someone wants to know how much. Either way—" she tapped the evidence bag "—this key is the most interesting object in this case, and I need you to tell me everything you know about the sub-crypt, starting with why an institution seals off part of its own crypt and then keeps a working key to it."

I told her what I knew, which was less than I pretended. The charnel crypt, the older layer beneath the twelfth-century vaulting, closed in the 1930s during structural works and never reopened — officially because of subsidence. It appeared in the earliest registers I held, then dropped out of use. There was no reason to keep a key. Keys to nothing are not kept. Keys to nothing are lost, melted down, forgotten. A key to nothing that survives nineteen years in a sacristan's mattress is a key to something.

"And here's the other thing," Ferro said, standing, putting the bag away inside her coat. "Oakes's phone records. We got them this morning. One number, called regularly, going back years — monthly, like clockwork, always late evening, always short. Under two minutes. We traced it." She paused, and I understood she was deciding how much to give me, and that giving me anything at all meant she had crossed some line of her own. "It's a pay-as-you-go mobile. Unregistered. Bought with cash in 2004."

"Blackmail," I said quietly.

"Or pension contributions," Ferro said. "But yes. My instinct says blackmail. Twenty years of small regular payments to keep a mouth shut — and then Thomas Reeve turns up dead in your crypt, and suddenly the payments stop mattering, because the mouth is shut permanently, and the only problem left is the person who might find out why." Her eyes went to my desk, to the ledgers, to me. "You're not a suspect anymore, Ms. Brightwater. You're a witness. And witnesses to this kind of thing have a life expectancy I don't love. So I'm going to say something I'll deny saying. Watch yourself. And if Oakes wakes up—"

"He'll recant," I said. "Whoever did this will make sure of it."

Ferro looked at me for a moment. "You've been reading case files too long," she said, and left.

* * *

I went to the hospital that afternoon, and they let me sit with him for ten minutes because I lied and said I was family, which was, in a way I didn't yet understand, truer than I knew.

Peter lay small against the white pillows, his head bandaged, his hands — those careful, polishing hands — folded on the blanket as if he were waiting for something to be handed to him. A monitor counted for him. When I leaned close, his eyes opened halfway, unfocused, and found the ceiling, and then, slowly, found me.

"Noor Brightwater," he whispered. His voice was gravel and water. "The archivist."

"Yes. Peter, who did this? Who came to your room?"

Something moved across his face — fear, but an old fear, weathered, a fear he had carried so long it had become furniture. His hand twitched toward mine and stopped.

"They were looking for the record," he breathed. "The record, the record. I told them I never kept one. I told them—"

"What record, Peter? What are you talking about?"

His eyes fixed on me properly for the first time, and what was in them was not confusion. It was recognition — the terrible clarity of a man surfacing briefly from deep water who sees exactly where he is.

"You don't know," he whispered, and something almost like pity crossed the bruised landscape of his face. "You really don't know. Oh, God forgive us all. Ask about the shoes."

"The shoes?"

But the monitor stuttered, and a nurse came in fast, and Peter's eyes rolled back, and the moment was gone — sucked down into the dark water he'd risen from, taking whatever he meant with it. They made me leave. In the corridor I stood under the fluorescent lights with my heart hammering and turned the word over like a coin of unknown currency.

*Shoes.*

I am an archivist. I have spent thirty years learning that memory fails and paper doesn't, that the human mind is a leaky vessel and the record is the ark. Somewhere in the hundreds of thousands of documents I have catalogued — the parish accounts, the restoration fund ledgers, the household receipts, the muniment boxes — somewhere there was a record of shoes. And someone had beaten Peter nearly to death looking for it, and Peter had spent — how long? Years? Decades? — making sure it stayed lost.

I walked back to the close in the failing light, past the cathedral rising black against a bruised sky, and I understood with complete certainty that I was being watched — not by anything I could see, but by the building itself, or by whoever moved through it after dark with keys and patience. Two days ago I had been the woman who found a body. Today I was the woman asking questions. Tomorrow—

Tomorrow I would go home, and I would unlock my own archive of a life — the boxes of my mother's papers I had never fully sorted, grief being its own kind of sealed room — and I would start with the year 1999.

Because I remembered, suddenly, with the sick lurch of a floorboard giving way, that my mother had worked at St. Aldhelm's in 1999. In the office. Handling the accounts.

And I remembered a pair of children's shoes.
