## 13. Peter Wakes

### Noor

The hospital at Meresgate was a fifteen-minute drive from the cathedral close, past the allotments and the shuttered swimming baths, and Noor had made the journey twice in her life: once when her mother broke her wrist falling on the crypt stairs, and once to sit with old Canon Hobb after his stroke. Both times she had noticed how the building smelled of disinfectant laid over something older and sweeter, like incense scrubbed but never gone. Now, walking the corridor toward Ward 4 with Detective Ferro a half-step behind her, she thought the smell had followed her. It was in her coat collar. It was in her hair. Everything since Thomas Reeve's body had been found in her crypt smelled of hospitals and of churches, and there was no longer any clean air between them.

Peter Oakes had been found eleven hours ago, face-down beside his overturned chair in the sacristan's quarters, his room turned inside out with a thoroughness that told Ferro — who had told Noor, because telling Noor things was becoming his strange habit, his way of keeping an eye on her — that whoever searched it had been looking for one thing and not many.

"Not a burglary," Ferro had said in the car. "Burglars take the television. This person took nothing and left everything except, possibly, whatever they came for."

"And you want me to see him why?"

"Because he'll wake up scared, and scared people talk to two kinds of other people: police, and people who aren't police. You're the second kind. Sometimes the second kind gets the truth first."

She had wanted to say that she didn't want the truth first, that the truth kept arriving in her life like water through a roof, finding every seam. But she had said nothing, and now here they were, outside a room with a strip of frosted glass in its door and a nurse inside drawing a curtain with a sound like tearing paper.

"He's awake," the nurse said, coming out. "Confused. He keeps asking about the vestry keys." She looked from Ferro to Noor with the flat professional curiosity of someone who had long ago stopped being surprised by what people did to each other. "Ten minutes. He's got a bleed on the scalp and a concussion, so if he gets agitated, you leave."

Oakes lay propped against pillows looking smaller than Noor had ever seen him, and she had seen him small before — seen him hunched over the brass polishing on Thursday mornings, seen him folding himself into the back pew at evensong as though trying to occupy less space than a man legally required to. His hands were folded on the blanket like something waiting to be collected. There was a plaster above his left ear and a bruise blooming down his temple in the yellow-green of old ivy.

"Mr. Oakes," Noor said softly. "It's Noor Brightwater. From the cathedral."

His eyes came around slowly, like a weathervane in failing wind. For a moment they were empty. Then something moved behind them — recognition, and under the recognition, fear, arriving late but arriving all the same.

"They took them," he whispered.

"Took what, Peter?"

"The pages." His tongue worked. His lips were cracked. "Out of the register. I keep the register, I've always kept it, thirty-one years, and they took the pages out and I heard—" He stopped. His eyes went past Noor's shoulder to Ferro, standing by the window with his arms crossed, deliberately unremarkable, deliberately present.

"You heard what?" Ferro asked, without moving.

"I heard the organ."

Noor felt the word land somewhere below her ribs. "The organ?"

"Practising. Someone was practising. All that time, while my room was—" He swallowed. The monitor beside him ticked its small metronome heart. "Whoever it was, they weren't afraid of being heard. That's what I keep thinking. They played the whole time. Like it was normal. Like it was Sunday."

Ferro glanced at Noor. She understood the glance: *the organ loft*. But she said nothing, because Oakes was speaking again, and his voice had changed — dropped into a lower register, the register men use for the sentences they've rehearsed without knowing they've rehearsed them.

"There was a girl," he said. "In ninety-nine. Elsie Marsh. She used to come down to the crypt sometimes, after practice, to look at the tombs. She liked the crusader one. The one with his legs crossed. I told her once he wasn't really a crusader, that the crossed legs meant he'd been on pilgrimage, and she said she liked the lie better." A ghost of a smile crossed his ruined face and then dissolved. "I liked her. Everyone liked her. That was the trouble, maybe. Everyone liked her, and no one—"

"No one what, Mr. Oakes?" Ferro had come off the wall now, quietly, the way he did everything, as though sudden movement might frighten the words back down Oakes's throat.

"No one watched her properly." His hand came up, trembling, and pressed against his mouth. And then, barely audible through his fingers: "The loft. It was always the loft. He watched from the loft. He used to watch us all from the loft, and we thought—"

The door opened. Not violently. That was the terrible thing, Noor thought later; it opened gently, the way doors open in hospitals all day long, and a tall figure stood silhouetted against the corridor light with a paper-wrapped bundle of flowers in one arm.

Dean Ambrose Vellacott smiled into the room.

"Forgive me," he said. "The ward sister said he'd woken. Peter, dear man. When I heard what happened to you—" He crossed to the bed with the unhurried grace Noor had watched him carry down the nave for years, that grace which seemed to belong to the building more than to the man, and set the flowers on the overbed table where they could not fail to be seen: white lilies, tightly furled, smelling already of funerals. "You gave us such a fright."

And Peter Oakes closed like a book.

Noor watched it happen. Watched the sentence die in his mouth, watched his eyes slide from Vellacott's face to the lilies to the window as though the window were an exit, watched his hands resume their patient folded waiting. Whatever had been rising in him sank again, smoothly, completely, the way a body sinks in deep water when it finally stops fighting.

"I don't remember anything," Oakes said. His voice was level now, emptied. "They told me I fell. Down the cloister steps. I've always said those steps need a rail."

"You fell," Vellacott agreed warmly, "and thank God it was no worse. We shall have that rail up within the week. I'll see to it personally." He touched Oakes's shoulder — a gesture that from anyone else would have been comfort and from Vellacott looked, to Noor, exactly like a seal being pressed into warm wax. Then he turned, and his gaze found Noor, and the warmth in it did not change at all, which was somehow worse. "Miss Brightwater. How good. You two were always close, weren't you, down among your papers. Well — I won't intrude on the detectives' work." A nod to Ferro, courteous, faintly amused, a dean acknowledging a curate. "I'm sure you're doing everything you can."

He left the way he had come, unhurried, and the lilies stayed behind on the table, filling the room.

For a full minute nobody spoke. The monitor ticked. Somewhere down the ward a trolley rattled.

"Mr. Oakes," Ferro said at last, very quietly. "A moment ago you were telling us about the organ loft."

Oakes looked at him with eyes gone perfectly blank, perfectly polite, the blankness of a man who has spent thirty-one years learning what is safe to remember. "Was I? I'm afraid the doctor said there'd be gaps. Confusion. I fell, detective. On the cloister steps." He turned his face to the window. "I'd like to rest now, if you don't mind."

### Ferro

Ferro had interviewed four hundred witnesses in nineteen years, and he had a private taxonomy of the ones who lied. There were liars who overbuilt, adding detail like mortar until the story collapsed under its own weight. There were liars who went vague, misting over like breath on glass. And then there were the rarest kind, the ones that worried him most: liars who recanted cleanly, who handed back their own testimony like a borrowed tool, saying *I never said that, you must have misheard, I fell on the steps.* Those were the ones who had been taught. Not threatened — taught. Somebody had sat them down, kindly or otherwise, and shown them the shape of the story they were permitted to know.

He walked out of Ward 4 with Noor Brightwater beside him and did not speak until they reached the stairwell, because stairwells were the last unbugged rooms in England and he trusted them the way other men trusted churches.

"You saw it," he said.

"I saw it." Her voice was steady but her hands weren't; she had them pushed deep in her coat pockets and he could see the fabric shifting with the tremor underneath. "He was mid-sentence. He said *he watched us all from the loft* — and then the door opened and it was like watching a lock turn from the inside."

"Vellacott didn't threaten him. Didn't say a single wrong word."

"He brought lilies."

"He brought lilies," Ferro agreed. White ones. In a hospital. To the bedside of a man with a head wound who had just started talking about twenty-five years ago. Either the dean of St. Aldhelm's had the emotional instincts of a paving stone, or the message was not for Oakes at all. Or not only for Oakes. He'd seen the way Vellacott had looked at Brightwater — the warmth that didn't reach anywhere, the look of a man checking a pressure gauge.

They descended two flights. At the landing she stopped, and in the sodium light of the stairwell her face looked stripped, younger, the composure of the archive cracked open like a ledger spine.

"Detective. If someone attacked Peter to stop him talking, and then the dean arrived and finished the job without touching him — what does that tell you?"

"It tells me the threat doesn't have to be spoken to be understood. It tells me Oakes has known for a long time what happens to people who remember out loud." He hesitated, then gave her a piece of himself, because she'd earned it and because he needed her thinking clearly rather than defensively. "I pulled the 1999 file this morning. The real one, not the summary. You know what's missing from it?"

"Pages."

He stared at her.

"We excise damaged pages in conservation too," she said. "There's a way it looks. The stubs were cut with a blade, not torn. I've been thinking about it since yesterday." She paused. "Peter keeps the vestry register. Thirty-one years, he said. If Elsie Marsh's file has pages removed, and Peter Oakes starts talking about pages taken from a register—"

"—then somebody's been tidying the paperwork for a very long time," Ferro finished. "With archival precision." He watched her absorb that: the idea that the enemy spoke her own language, handled documents the way she did, respected the same rules of evidence she'd built her life on. It was, he suspected, the loneliest thing she'd felt yet, and he'd watched her stand in a police interview room being told a dead man wore her clothes.

"One more thing," he said. "The attack on Oakes. Time of injury puts it between nine and eleven at night. The close was locked. The sacristan's quarters are inside the inner gate. Whoever did it had access — a key, or a reason to be let in." He let that sit. "Three keyholders to the crypt, you told me. But the inner gate takes a different key again, doesn't it? Who holds that one?"

"Chapter staff. The dean. The succentor. Me." Her jaw tightened. "And the organist has a key to the close itself. Always has. Choir practice runs till ten some nights."

"The organ loft," Ferro said. "That's what he said. *It was always the loft.*"

Above them, faintly, through however many floors of concrete and steel, came a sound neither of them could actually have heard — and both of them heard anyway. A held chord, swelling and dying, swelling and dying, like something breathing under the floor of the world.

Neither of them laughed. Neither of them said *that's impossible, the cathedral is three miles away.* They stood on the landing in the bad light and listened to a sound that wasn't there, and understood that from now on it would be there in every silence, and that this — this was what fear outlasting memory looked like. Not screaming. Not running. Just a man in a hospital bed handing back his own words, and a chord that would not stop.

"I'm going back to the close tonight," Noor said.

"That's not a request I can grant."

"I wasn't requesting." She was already climbing, taking the stairs two at a time, her coat swinging, and Ferro stood a moment longer on the landing looking up after her — the archivist going back to the archive, the witness going back to the scene, the woman whose clothes had dressed a corpse going back to the building that had eaten everyone she loved.

Then he followed her, because someone had to be watching when the organ began to play for real.
