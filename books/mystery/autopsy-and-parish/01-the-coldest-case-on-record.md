## 1. The Coldest Case on Record

There is a particular silence that lives inside a closed case. I've heard it eleven times now — the sound a town makes when the thing it buried finally stops breathing. It's not peace. Peace is quiet and warm. This is something else: a held breath released so slowly that nobody notices it leaving, only that afterward they can stand a little straighter in the grocery line, sleep a little deeper in houses where someone once wept against the window glass.

My name is Odile Pemberton, and for six seasons of *Autopsy and Parish* I have been the person who opens the box, listens to whatever's inside, and then — this is the part my listeners write to me about, the part the critics grudgingly concede — closes it again. Eleven cases. Eleven closures. No retractions, no corrections, no awkward on-air apologies. In an industry built on speculation dressed as investigation, I am the woman who shows her work.

I say this not to boast but to establish the terms of our contract, you and I, because what I'm about to give you is going to test every clause of it.

The Marguerite Voss case is fifty-one years old. It has been worked by three sheriffs, two private investigators, one diocesan commission, and at least one amateur whose name I found scratched into the back of a pew in Saint Adalbert's Church like a man carving his own epitaph before he'd earned it. None of them closed it. The file sits in a records room in a river town I will not name yet — not out of coyness, but because the name of that town is itself a kind of evidence, and I've learned to lay things out in order — gathering dust the way files do, except that dust doesn't get disturbed every few years by hands that can't stop reaching for it.

Marguerite Voss was the organist at Saint Adalbert's. She was thirty-four years old in October 1974, small-boned, nearsighted, with a habit of pushing her glasses up with the heel of her hand while her other hand rested on the keyboard as though the instrument might bolt. She played for two services on Sundays, funerals on request, weddings at a rate she kept deliberately low so that no couple could claim cost as a reason to elope without music. She lived above the old chandlery on Water Street. She kept bees, badly. She wrote letters to her sister in Duluth that were, by all accounts, funny in a dry way that made people feel chosen when she aimed it at them.

On the last Sunday of October 1974, she played the eleven o'clock service. Witnesses agree on this the way witnesses agree on anything: completely, and with small contradictions nobody thought mattered. She played the postlude — Bach, or possibly Buxtehude, depending on which choir member you ask, and I have asked all of them who are still living, and their certainty is inversely proportional to their proximity to the organ loft that morning. She locked the church. The sexton saw her do it. He remembers because she had a way of testing the lock twice, tugging the handle after turning the key, a little superstition he teased her about and she tolerated with the patience of a woman who understood that teasing was how some men said *I see you*.

She never arrived home. Her bicycle was found three days later, upright against the railing of the ferry landing, its basket holding a hymnal and a paper sack of groceries gone soft with rain. The river took the rest of the story and did not give it back.

They dragged the water for nine days. They found a boot, a lawn chair, a child's wagon, and forty years' worth of other towns' sorrows, but no Marguerite. No body, then, and eventually no case — just a missing-persons folder that thickened with clippings and thinned with hope until even the folder seemed embarrassed to exist. The town did what river towns do with grief too large to hold: they made it ornamental. There's a brass plaque on the ferry landing now. There's a scholarship in her name at the high school, awarded annually to a student of musical promise, which is a phrase that has come to mean *a student who plays well enough to make the judges sad*. Every October, the choir sings a memorial anthem, and every October the same elderly soprano cries through the descant, and everyone pretends this is devotion rather than the wound refusing, on schedule, to close.

Fifty-one years. That's not a cold case. That's permafrost.

* * *

I want to tell you why I take the cases I take, because the honest answer is less flattering than the one I used to give at press events, back when I gave press events.

The flattering version is that I believe in justice. The true version is that I believe in finishing. There's a difference, and the difference matters more than I'd like it to. Justice is abstract; it belongs to the victim, the law, the community, God if you keep one. Finishing is personal. Finishing is mine. When I take a case, what I'm really taking is the intolerable fact of its incompleteness — the dangling thread that snags on everything else in the room until you either cut it or follow it, and I have never once in my life managed to cut a thread. My mother would tell you this started young. She'd tell you about the jigsaw puzzle I hid a piece from when I was seven, because a finished puzzle meant the end of the puzzle, and I couldn't bear it — and then she'd tell you about finding me at two in the morning, age seven, sobbing over the completed picture because I'd hidden the piece somewhere even I couldn't find it, and the puzzle was finished anyway, finished wrong, finished with a hole in the sky.

I think about that hole in the sky more than I should. Eleven cases, eleven closures, and every single time I close one I feel both the satisfaction and the grief of the seven-year-old: *it's done, and done means over, and over means I have to go looking again.* People assume a perfect record makes you confident. What it actually makes you is terrified, because perfection is a debt that compounds. Every solved case raises the price of the next failure. Somewhere out there is the case that will break the streak, the one where I follow the thread into the dark and the dark turns out to be a dead end, or worse — a door.

I chose Marguerite Voss the way I choose most of them: reluctantly, obsessively, and against my better judgment.

It started, as these things always do, with mail. A manila envelope, no return address, postmarked from the town — fine, I'll say it now since you'll have it by the end of the episode anyway: Verity Landing, population four thousand and change, sitting on the east bank of the Sable River like a comma in a long sentence. Inside the envelope: a photograph of a brass plaque, a photocopied newspaper clipping from November 1974, and a note written in fountain pen on lined paper, in handwriting so careful it looked typeset:

*You close cases. Nobody has ever closed hers. I don't think anyone can. But I think someone should try.*

No signature. Of course no signature. The unsigned letter is the first instrument in the true-crime orchestra, and I've learned to read them the way a doctor reads a face — for what they withhold. This one withheld well. The handwriting was controlled, educated, older — the penmanship of someone taught by nuns or by fear, which are sometimes the same curriculum. The phrasing was interesting: not *you could solve it* but *nobody has ever closed hers*, as though the case were a debt carried collectively, a mortgage on the whole town's soul that comes due every October when the soprano cries through the descant.

And there was one more thing in the envelope. A strip of paper, maybe two inches long, torn roughly from the bottom of a page. On it, in pencil, in a different hand — looser, quicker, a hand that wrote the way water runs — a string of numbers:

**40 – 17 – 3 – 88**

No explanation. Numbers mean nothing alone; they're the raw ore of coincidence. But I sat with that strip of paper for two nights, and I'll confess something I've never said on air: I pinned it above my desk, and every time I looked up at it, I felt the specific vertigo of recognition without content — the feeling of knowing a thing and not knowing that you know it. Like hearing your name called in a crowd and turning toward a stranger.

It took me a week to understand why the numbers itched. Forty. Hymn number forty. The clipping in the envelope mentioned, in its final paragraph, in that throwaway register newspapers reserve for details they suspect matter: *Miss Voss was known for her unconventional selections; congregants recall she frequently announced incorrect hymn numbers, correcting herself with a laugh.*

She marked every page wrong. Hold that thought. I'm asking you to hold it the way you'd hold a live wire — carefully, and knowing it goes somewhere.

* * *

Before I went to Verity Landing — and I went, because research by telephone is research by rumor, and rumor is the currency of exactly the people I don't trust — I did what I always do. I built the skeleton. Dates, names, distances. And I want to walk you through the skeleton now, because bones are honest in a way flesh isn't, and because when you see the shape of this thing laid bare, you'll understand why I stopped sleeping in October.

October 27, 1974, a Sunday. Marguerite plays the late service. The postlude ends at approximately 12:14 p.m., according to the one witness — the sexton — who wore a watch. She locks the church between 12:20 and 12:30. The ferry landing is a four-minute walk. Her bicycle is found there on Wednesday the 30th, which means it stood in the open air for up to three days, and here is the first anomaly, the first bone that doesn't fit: nobody reported seeing it. A river town. A ferry landing. Foot traffic daily, hourly. Three days, and not one of the four thousand souls of Verity Landing noticed a woman's bicycle standing sentinel at the water's edge?

Unless they did notice. Unless noticing was the problem.

The official timeline says she drowned. Accidental, presumed; the coroner's finding, such as it was, reads like a man signing his lunch receipt. But Marguerite Voss grew up on that river. She swam in it every summer of her childhood, according to her sister, whom I interviewed by phone in 2019, three years before she died — the last Voss, the last keeper of the family's ordinary griefs. *Maggie wouldn't have fallen in,* her sister told me, in a voice worn smooth by fifty years of saying it to people who nodded politely. *Maggie knew that river like the keys of her organ. And Maggie didn't go to the ferry landing on Sundays. She hated the ferry landing on Sundays. Too many people coming back from their mother's house wanting to talk.*

So why was her bicycle there? Why was her hymnal in the basket — the hymnal, singular, the one she used, the one with her penciled annotations, which the police logged and photographed and then, and this is the sentence I underlined three times in the case file, *released to the parish* — why was it in the basket at all? Who rides a bicycle to a ferry landing carrying a hymnal and leaves it? Who drowns with their groceries?

And why — sit with this one — did the parish, upon receiving the hymnal back, quietly retire it? Not discard it. Retire it. It sat in a cabinet in the sacristy for decades, catalogued, insured, untouched, the way churches keep things they can't explain and won't examine. Until, as you'll hear next episode, an eighty-nine-year-old alto put it into my hands with a grip that told me she'd been waiting longer than she'd been alive to pass it on.

* * *

Here is what I knew when I boarded the train north, and I want you to know it too, because I'm asking you to be my witness, and a witness needs the facts before the feelings arrive and rearrange the furniture.

I knew that Marguerite Voss vanished on the last Sunday of October 1974, and that her body was never found. I knew the town grieved her publicly and continuously, in the manner of people maintaining a monument rather than mourning a loss. I knew the hymnal existed, was anomalous, and was in the keeping of a congregation that had chosen preservation over examination. I knew four numbers — 40, 17, 3, 88 — that meant nothing and therefore might mean everything. I knew that someone had mailed me this case without signing their name, which meant someone wanted it opened and wanted deniability, and those two desires coexisting in one breast is the oldest music in the world.

What I did not know — what I could not have known, standing on that platform with my bag and my recorder and my perfect record folded around me like armor — was that the diary was still writing. That the last dated entry falls three weeks after the day she disappeared. That somewhere in Verity Landing, in a house I hadn't yet entered, a dead woman's handwriting was waiting to describe me.

But I'm ahead of myself. Showmanship, my producer calls it, disapprovingly. Fine. Then let me end the way I began, with the contract between us, amended.

Eleven cases, eleven closures. I told you I show my work, and here is the work, laid out on the table like instruments before surgery. I promised you a tidy resolution, because I always promise a tidy resolution, because tidiness is the product and you are the customer and neither of us likes to admit we're in commerce together.

I am breaking that promise now, at the start, so you can't say later that I misled you. The Marguerite Voss case does not end tidy. It ends in a flooded quarry, a courtroom, a shoe that belonged to the wrong woman, and a choice I made with my eyes open that cost me nearly everything I'd built — and I would make it again, which frightens me more than any of the rest of it.

The train pulled into Verity Landing at dusk, the way trains in stories about rivers always do, and the first thing I saw from the window was the water — wide, brown-green, patient, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that has taken what it wants and knows no one will ask for it back. On the far bank, the ferry landing stood empty except for the brass plaque, catching the last light.

A woman was standing beside it. Small-boned. Nearsighted, by the way she leaned toward the plaque. She pushed her glasses up with the heel of her hand.

I blinked, and she was gone — a trick of the light, a trick of expectation, a trick of a town that had spent fifty-one years teaching its ghosts to stand very still. I got off the train with my heart going like a metronome set to allegro, and I told myself what I always tell myself at the start of a case, the lie that keeps the machine running:

*I close cases. This one will be no different.*

The hymnal was already waiting for me. So was everything else.
