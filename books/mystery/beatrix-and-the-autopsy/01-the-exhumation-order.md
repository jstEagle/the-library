## 1. The Exhumation Order

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which Hazel Galloway would later think of as the last ordinary Tuesday of her life.

It came with the morning mail, wedged between a dental insurance reminder and a flyer for a church rummage sale, and it bore the county seal in the upper left corner — that embossed circle of blind justice she had seen perhaps four hundred times in eleven years as a claims investigator. She knew the weight of those envelopes before she opened them. This one was heavier than most. Two staples instead of one. A second page. Court orders were like weather systems: you could read the barometric pressure of the paperwork before the storm itself broke.

She stood at her kitchen counter with coffee going cold beside her and slit it open with a paring knife, because scissors were for people who didn't mind waste.

*IN THE MATTER OF: VOSS, EDMUND T., deceased. PETITION FOR EXHUMATION AND FORENSIC EXAMINATION OF REMAINS.*

Hazel read it twice. The first time was for content. The second time was for everything else.

Edmund Voss. The name sat in her memory the way names did — filed, cross-referenced, permanent. Founder of Voss & Sons Millwork, which had no sons by the end and barely a Voss. Dead five years this coming October, officially by drowning, when his truck was found nose-down in the Miller's Reach with the driver's door open and his hat floating downstream like a small dark thought nobody wanted to finish. The body had never surfaced, which the coroner at the time had attributed to the current, the cold, and the general reluctance of rivers to give back what they took. A closed casket funeral. A headstone in Greenlawn Cemetery, plot 114, row C, under a sugar maple that had been young when Voss bought the plot and was now old enough to drop branches in storms.

And now this. An exhumation order, signed by Judge Albrecht, petitioned by Meridian Mutual Life over a disputed payout on a policy written fourteen months before the drowning.

Hazel set the order down and looked out her kitchen window at the gray light coming up over the rooftops of Halden Falls, population 4,100 and shrinking, a town built around a mill that no longer ran and a river that never stopped. Somewhere out there was a grave that might not hold what everyone believed it held. She felt the first faint pull of it, the way a compass needle feels north before anyone has spoken a direction aloud.

She made fresh coffee. She got her file case from the hall closet. Then she drove to the office, because some documents deserved better than a kitchen counter.

* * *

The Halden Falls branch of Meridian Mutual occupied the ground floor of a brick building that had once been a bank and still smelled faintly of other people's money. Hazel's office was in the back, past the break room, a room with one window facing the alley and shelves enough for the archive she'd spent a decade building: every claim file the branch had ever processed, organized alphabetically by surname, then chronologically, then by policy number. Her colleagues called it the Galloway Library, half affectionate and half the way you'd describe a person who alphabetized their spice rack. Hazel accepted both meanings. Order was not a compulsion for her. It was a courtesy she paid to a memory that never forgot anything and therefore needed somewhere to put it all.

Her photographic memory was not a party trick. She did not perform it. But it meant that when she read a page, she kept the page — the typeface, the coffee ring in the corner, the way the third line of a signature looped or didn't. Eleven years of claims had given her an internal archive of several hundred thousand documents, and she consulted it the way other investigators consulted filing cabinets: patiently, completely, and without ever misplacing anything.

She pulled the Voss file.

It was thick. Policy MV-4471902, term life, face value $250,000, issued to Edmund T. Voss on August 3rd, five years and two months ago. Beneficiary: Beatrix Voss, daughter, sole heir. Premiums paid by automatic draft from the mill's operating account, never missed, never late. And then October 17th — the truck in the river, the empty casket, the death certificate signed by Dr. Emory Lassiter, coroner, and beneath it, the burial transit permit and the mortician's certificate of interment, signed by Dale Pruitt of Pruitt & Sons Funeral Home.

The claim had been paid six weeks after the death. Clean, fast, uncontested. So why the dispute now?

She found the answer on the second-to-last page: a letter from Meridian's regional office, dated three weeks back, flagging the file for review. *Anomalous premium history,* it said. *Policy rewritten within final year of insured's life. Recommend verification of remains prior to closing legacy exposure.* Corporate language, which was to say language designed to say nothing while authorizing everything. Someone upstairs had gotten nervous about a quarter-million-dollar policy on a man who'd rewritten his coverage fourteen months before he drowned, and the cheapest way to stop being nervous was to open the grave and look inside.

Hazel understood the logic. She even sympathized with it. What she did not yet understand — what she wouldn't understand until the light went strange in the late afternoon and she was still at her desk with the file spread out like a patient on a table — was why her hands had gone cold.

Because something in the file was wrong. She'd felt it the way you feel a wrong note in a hymn: not identified, just *off*, a small dissonance the ear catches before the mind can name it. She went back through the pages slowly, letting her memory do what it did.

Page nine. The mortician's certificate. *I hereby certify that I interred the body of Edmund T. Voss in Greenlawn Cemetery...* Signed in blue ink, a confident slanted hand: *Dale Pruitt, Lic. #M-2217.*

She stared at it. And then, quietly, without deciding to, she reached into the archive behind her and pulled a different folder.

The Kessler file. Ruth Kessler, dead four years ago of congestive heart failure, buried in Greenlawn, plot 61, row B. A routine claim, routine paperwork, paid and closed and forgotten by everyone except the woman whose memory had filed it away whole. Hazel turned to the mortician's certificate near the back and laid it beside the Voss document on her desk.

Two certificates. Two deaths, four years apart. Two funerals handled by the same small-town firm.

One signature.

Not similar. Not close. Identical — down to the way the *D* leaned too far right, the way the *Pruitt* ended in a flat horizontal stroke rather than the double-*t* flourish Dale Pruitt used on his business correspondence, which Hazel had also seen, twice, on letters attached to earlier claims. Every human hand has tics it cannot suppress; Hazel had learned to read them the way a tracker reads broken grass. The signature on Ruth Kessler's certificate and the signature on Edmund Voss's certificate shared every tic, every hesitation, every accidental grace note. Same pen pressure. Same ink bleed pattern where the writer had paused mid-word.

Which was unremarkable, if the same man had signed both.

Except it wasn't the same man. Hazel checked the license numbers. On the Kessler certificate: *Lic. #M-2217.* On the Voss certificate: *Lic. #M-2217.* Fine. Consistent. But four years apart, and here was the thing that made her sit very still in her chair with the radiator ticking behind her: Dale Pruitt had retired — or claimed to have retired — eighteen months after Kessler's funeral. His son Harold had taken over the firm. Harold's signature appeared on three more burial certificates in the archive, and Harold wrote like a man signing receipts: fast, cramped, the letters crowded together like passengers on a full bus. The Voss certificate was not Harold's hand. It was the father's — the older, slower, more deliberate hand of a man who supposedly hadn't signed a burial permit in years.

Hazel pulled the third certificate anyway, and the fourth, laying them out in chronological order across her desk like tarot cards. Kessler, four years ago: Dale. Ostrander, three and a half years ago: Harold. Brandt, three years ago: Harold. Voss, five years ago—

Five years ago. She stopped. Recalculated. The Voss funeral predated all of them. Dale's signature on Voss was perfectly normal. Dale's signature on Kessler, two years into his retirement, was not.

She sat back. The dissonance resolved, and what remained was worse than the wrong note had been: silence, and the knowledge that she had heard it at all.

No. Wait. She went back again, because certainty was a discipline and she refused to be sloppy with it. She re-read the dates on the Voss certificate itself. Date of death: October 17th. Date of interment: October 21st. Signature date line: October 20th. All consistent. All fine.

But the paper. She tilted the certificate toward the window light. The Voss certificate was printed on the standard state form, revision year stamped faintly in the margin — and the form revision was dated *two years after* the interment it certified. Forms got revised; offices used up old stock; that alone proved nothing. But combined with the signature — the retired man's hand, identical to a document from a different case, on a form that hadn't existed when Edmund Voss went into the ground —

Hazel reached for her coffee and found it cold again. She drank it anyway.

There were innocent explanations. There almost always were. A clerk filling out paperwork late, grabbing whatever form was at hand. A mortician signing on behalf of his father posthumously — no, that didn't work either, the hand was Dale's own. A reissued certificate, maybe, the original lost in a flood or a fire, recreated years later from records. That happened. That was even plausible. She wrote the possibility down in her neat, upright hand at the top of her legal pad: *Reissued cert? Check w/ county registrar.*

Then she wrote the other possibility underneath it, smaller, as if writing it small could keep it contained:

*Or the paperwork was done long after the burial. By someone who wasn't there.*

* * *

She worked the rest of the day the way she always did, which is to say she appeared to work normally while a second process ran underneath, quiet and tireless, turning the problem over. She answered emails. She returned a call about a fender-bender claim in the next township. At noon she ate a sandwich at her desk and read the exhumation order a third time, and this time she noticed the detail she'd skipped past twice: the petition cited *inability to verify identity of remains* as grounds — not suspicion of fraud, not a tip, not a whistleblower. Just a bland institutional itch. Somebody at Meridian regional had looked at this file and felt the same wrongness Hazel felt, and had expressed it in the only dialect corporations speak: paperwork requesting more paperwork.

Who? She memorized the name on the petition — R. Calloway, special investigations, regional office — and filed it away next to everything else.

At four o'clock, Sheriff's Deputy Ansel Boyd came by to coordinate logistics, because the exhumation required law enforcement presence and Greenlawn Cemetery required a court officer to witness the opening. Boyd was thirty, earnest, with the habit of standing too close to desks. He laid a copy of the court order on her desk, redundant since she already had one, and said, "Seven a.m. Thursday. Coroner wants everybody there before the ground softens up too much in the sun."

"Everybody," Hazel said. "Who's everybody?"

"Me, you, Dr. Lassiter, the cemetery sexton, a rep from the funeral home." He hesitated. "Pruitt's boy, I guess. Harold. Since it's their plot work." He shifted his weight. "Kind of a strange thing, huh? Five years. My granddad always said let the dead stay dug."

"Your grandfather sounds wise."

"He drank." Boyd shrugged. "See you Thursday."

When he was gone, Hazel sat with the file open in front of her and let herself do the thing she rarely permitted: she imagined forward. Thursday morning. The machine digging. The crowd that would gather, because in a town of four thousand an exhumation was theater whether anyone admitted it or not. The lid coming off a coffin that had waited five years under a sugar maple, holding — what? A man who had rewritten his life insurance fourteen months before drowning in a river that gave nothing back? A man whose burial certificate was signed on paper that didn't exist yet?

Or nothing at all.

She looked at the two signatures side by side on her desk, the Kessler and the Voss, twins separated by two years and one impossible form revision. Her memory held them both, would hold them forever, in perfect resolution, the way it held everything: the shape of the ink, the pause in the stroke, the faint tremor in the downstroke of the *u* that belonged to a tired man at the end of a career he may or may not have actually ended.

Eleven years of reading other people's disasters had taught Hazel Galloway one rule above all others: files don't lie, but they can be made to. And a lie, once committed to paper, becomes a physical object — it weighs something, it occupies space, it can be found. Most people hid lies in words. The careful ones hid them in dates and signatures, in the boring margins where nobody looks.

Somebody had looked at this file recently and decided it needed to be opened like a wound. Hazel intended to find out who, and why, and what they expected to find — or hoped nobody would.

She squared the papers, returned them to the folder, and slid the Voss file into her case instead of the archive shelf. It was a small violation of procedure. She told herself it was for efficiency.

Outside, the light was going amber over the alley, and somewhere across town, under the sugar maple in plot 114, row C, Edmund T. Voss had been keeping whatever company the dead keep for five years — and by Thursday morning, Hazel supposed, one of them would finally learn the truth about the other.

She locked the office, and her last thought before sleep that night was not of coffins or signatures but of the beneficiary's name on the policy, typed in the same plain capitals as everything else: BEATRIX VOSS. Sole heir. Sole mourner, if the town gossip could be believed — the daughter who'd come back from the city to run the family foundation, who'd stood at the graveside five years ago in a black coat, alone, while the river downstream kept its counsel.

Thursday, Hazel thought. Two days.

She fell asleep with the file's contents glowing behind her eyes like a photograph she couldn't put down.
