## 2. Dirt and Certainty

The order came down at seven in the morning and the ground came up at nine, and in the two hours between them Hazel Galloway stood at the edge of the Voss plot in Hillcrest Cemetery and memorized the crowd.

She didn't do it on purpose. That was the thing people never understood about her memory — it wasn't a party trick she could switch off, wasn't a camera she aimed and clicked. It was more like a door that wouldn't latch. Everything that passed through stayed. The county clerk's coffee-stained tie knot from three years ago. The exact number of steps between the courthouse steps and the records office (forty-one, if you took the shallow ones on the left). A stranger's face seen once through a bus window in 2011, still filed away somewhere behind her eyes like a pressed flower.

So when the witnesses began arriving — and there were more of them than an insurance dispute should have drawn, which was her first note, the first entry in a ledger she hadn't yet decided to keep — she catalogued them without meaning to, the way other people breathed.

Fourteen people, not counting the gravedigger and his two hired hands, not counting the sheriff's deputy who stood by the fence with his hat in his hands like he'd rather be anywhere else, and not counting herself.

She started at the left and worked right, because disorder made her itch.

A man in a brown overcoat, sixty-ish, heavyset, standing with his weight on his heels like a man at a funeral who had no personal grief to carry but considered attendance a civic duty. Banker's posture. She'd seen him at the chamber of commerce luncheon two winters back; she could not have told you his name under oath, but she could have picked him out of a lineup of four hundred men.

Beside him, a younger man with a clipboard he kept consulting and then not consulting, as though the clipboard were a prop for a role he hadn't rehearsed. Insurance company, almost certainly. Meridian Mutual had sent a letter announcing its representative would attend; the letter had been signed *R. Calloway*, and this man's lanyard, when the wind lifted his coat, read *CALLAHAN*. Close enough to be sloppy or close enough to be deliberate, and she filed both possibilities side by side.

Two women from the historical society, identifiable by their matching enamel pins — the town's founding oak, gold on green. One of them wept quietly into a handkerchief monogrammed with a *P*. The other did not weep but held the first one's elbow with the firm tenderness of long friendship.

A teenage boy on a bicycle, who had clearly come for the spectacle and was about to be deeply disappointed by how slow exhumations actually were. He lasted eleven minutes before pedaling off toward the river.

And then the veiled woman.

Hazel noticed her the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song — not immediately, but completely, once you'd heard it. The woman stood apart from the others, near the yew tree at the cemetery's northeast corner, dressed in black from collar to hem, her face covered by a veil of the kind Hazel had seen exactly twice in her life: once at her grandmother's funeral, when Hazel was nine, and once in a photograph of a state funeral from before either of them was born. Nobody wore veils anymore. Nobody wore them to an insurance proceeding, which is what this technically was, whatever the deputy's hat-in-hands posture wanted to call it.

The woman did not move for forty minutes. She did not consult a watch, check a phone, shift her weight, or speak to anyone. She simply stood beneath the yew with her gloved hands folded, facing the plot, still as furniture.

Hazel memorized her anyway. Height, approximately five foot six. Left-handed — the glove on the left hand showed wear at the thumb seam where a ring would sit, and she'd adjusted the veil twice with the left hand only. Shoes: low-heeled, expensive, resoled rather than replaced, which meant money handled carefully. And beneath the veil, when the wind gusted once and lifted the netting for half a second, a jawline and a mouth that Hazel's memory took and shelved before Hazel herself had finished deciding whether to look.

Half a second. Filed forever.

* * *

The machine they'd brought to dig was too big for the work and everyone knew it, so after ten minutes of noise and torn grass the operator shut it down and the gravedigger finished by hand, the old way, out of what looked like professional embarrassment.

His name was Otis Ferro. Hazel knew this because she'd read the cemetery's maintenance contracts the night before, all eleven pages, along with the original interment permit, the coroner's 2019 report on Edmund Voss's drowning, the funeral home's service record, and the deed history of the Voss plot going back to 1912. She had gone to bed at one in the morning with the documents arranged on her kitchen table in a grid so precise her sister used to call it "the alphabet," and she had woken at five with the grid still open behind her eyes, every signature and stamp and smudge available for inspection without the papers even being in the room.

Otis climbed out of the pit at ten past ten, wiped his face with a red bandana, and looked up at the small crowd with the flat expression of a man about to say something he'd been chewing on for days.

"Ground's wrong," he said.

Deputy Hollis shifted. "Wrong how?"

"Wrong how." Otis spat thoughtfully into the spoil heap. "I dug this grave. Five years ago, October, cold enough that the frost line was coming down and I remember because I had to work fast before it set. I dug it, I set the vault, I backfilled it, I tamped it, and I seeded it in the spring." He pointed the bandana at the walls of the pit, at the neat strata of earth exposed by his own shovel. "That's my cut. You can see the seed layer, see the dark topsoil. But underneath—" He shook his head. "Underneath, somebody's been down here since."

The clipboard man — Callahan, or Calloway, or whoever he actually was — stepped forward. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning the fill's been turned. Once. Years back, judging by how it's settled. Somebody opened this grave and closed it again, and tamped it well enough that nobody walking by would ever know." Otis looked around at the faces above him, and his gaze snagged briefly on Hazel's, and something passed across his own face that she couldn't yet name — recognition, maybe, or the relief of a man finally handing off a weight. "I reported it. Year after the funeral, when I came to reseed and saw the subsidence. Told the funeral home. They told me I was seeing things."

"And?" Hazel said.

Otis looked at her properly then. "And nothing. That's the whole story. Man opens a grave, closes it, and everybody decides the gravedigger's drunk." He shrugged. "Maybe I was. But I know my dirt."

*Somebody opened this grave and closed it again.*

Hazel felt the sentence go into her the way a splinter goes into a finger — small, clean, immediately impossible to ignore. She stood very still and let her mind do what it did, pulling threads she hadn't known were connected:

The mortician's signature on the burial transit permit — *D. Pruitt* — matched, stroke for stroke, curve for curve, the signature on a cremation authorization from a different case file she'd reviewed eight months ago, a case with no connection to Voss whatsoever except that Dale Pruitt had signed both documents, and no man's signature was identical twice unless one of the times wasn't his hand at all.

The coroner's report listing cause of death as drowning, witnessed by no one, in a river with a current strong enough that the body had been found eleven miles downstream — and yet the clothing described in the inventory had been dry-cleaned before burial, per the funeral home's own service record, a courtesy itemized at twelve dollars. Who dry-cleans the clothes of a drowned man before burying him?

And now this. A grave disturbed once, years ago, by someone careful enough to leave no sign anyone but a gravedigger would catch.

She became aware that Otis was still looking at her.

"You're the one from the county," he said. Not a question. "The one that reads everything."

"I read what's relevant," Hazel said, which was a lie so large it deserved its own filing system.

"Mm." Otis went back down into the pit, and the shovels resumed, and the sound of steel biting clay filled the morning like slow applause.

* * *

They struck the vault lid at twenty to eleven.

The crowd compressed toward the graveside — all of it except the veiled woman, who turned and walked.

There was nothing dramatic about it. No hurried step, no backward glance. She simply pivoted on her careful resoled heels and moved off along the yew-lined path toward the gate, unhurried as a woman leaving a church service at the final hymn, and she was through the iron gate and gone before the first bolt came free.

Hazel watched her go and felt something in her chest tighten and settle into a new shape. Because here was the arithmetic that bothered her: the veil meant anonymity, and anonymity meant anticipation. You wore a veil to a thing like this only if you knew, in advance, that you might need not to be recognized. Which meant the woman had known what would be found here — or feared it — before a single shovel of dirt had moved.

Or she had known five years ago. Otis said the grave had been opened once before. Hazel looked at the empty path where the woman had been, and let the two facts lie down next to each other in her mind, and did not yet force them to speak.

"Hold on," she said, and stepped to the edge of the pit.

Nobody stopped her. Deputy Hollis looked like he wanted to, and the Callahan-Calloway man definitely wanted to, but wanting and doing were different animals and Hazel was already crouched at the lip with her notebook out, sketching the vault lid as the bolts came free one by one. Four bolts. Brass, uncorroded, which was itself a small wrongness — five years in wet clay should have bloomed them green. Someone had maintained these bolts, or replaced them.

The lid came up.

* * *

Later — much later, in the report she would write and in the private file she would begin that same night, the file with no official number — Hazel would list what was in the coffin in the order she perceived it, which was not the order the photographs captured:

First, the smell. Not decay. Cedar, from the vault lining, and beneath it something mineral and cold, like a cellar floor.

Second, the color. White. A long white shape where a body should have been, and for one full second her mind refused the obvious answer and reached instead for the grotesque — a shrouded corpse, wrapped tight, some older tradition — and then the shape resolved and the truth arrived all at once, and it was worse than a corpse, somehow, worse because it was tidy.

River stones. Dozens of them, smooth and gray-brown, packed into the outline of a human form inside a folded funeral shroud, arranged with a care that was almost tender. A stone where the head would be. Stones along the arms, the legs. Someone had taken time. Someone had wanted the weight to be honest, at least — a coffin should weigh what a coffin weighs. Whoever did this had thought about the men who would carry it, and spared their backs the lie.

Third — and this was the detail, the one that would keep her up that night and every night after — the fold.

The shroud was hospital linen. She knew it instantly: the weave, the faded laundry stamp at the corner, the particular way it had been tucked. And it had been folded in the manner hospitals fold — edges rolled inward, corners mitered, the whole thing squared off with the crisp geometry of institutional practice. Civilians folded sheets in thirds and shoved them in closets. Nurses and orderlies folded them the way this one was folded, because they'd done it ten thousand times under supervision until the fold lived in their hands.

Edmund Voss had drowned in the Kettle River, alone, unwitnessed. Dead men did not fold their own shrouds. So whoever prepared this coffin had spent real time in hospitals — working in them, not lying in them — and had brought that muscle memory to the task of faking a burial.

Hazel wrote none of this down. She didn't need to. She stood at the graveside while the photographer from the coroner's office worked, while Callahan-Calloway made a phone call twenty feet away and lowered his voice too late to matter, while Deputy Hollis removed his hat entirely now, and while Otis Ferro leaned on his shovel at the bottom of the pit and looked at the stones with an expression she would later recognize as vindication, bitter and complete.

She stood there and she memorized it. All of it. The light angle on the brass bolts. The count of the stones (she counted twice; fifty-three). The laundry stamp's partial serial number. The way the crowd's breathing changed when the truth came clear — a single collective inhale, fourteen people forgetting themselves at once.

And underneath the cataloguing, quiet as groundwater, something else was rising. Not the professional satisfaction of a discrepancy caught. Something colder and more patient. Because a fake burial wasn't a prank and it wasn't simple fraud — fraud was paperwork, and paperwork could be untangled in a conference room. A fake burial required hands. Hands required help. Help required silence, and silence, maintained for five years across a whole town, required either extraordinary loyalty or extraordinary fear.

Someone had stood where she was standing, five years ago, in the dark, and lowered these stones into the ground, and tamped the dirt, and walked away, and said nothing ever since.

Hazel closed her notebook.

"Otis," she said.

He looked up from the pit, bandana in hand.

"That first time. When the ground was disturbed. Was anything reported missing? Any complaint, any inquiry, anything at all?"

"Nope." He wiped his neck. "Like I said. Everybody decided I was seeing things." He paused, and his voice dropped so only she could hear it. "Except one person asked. Year or two back. Woman. Came by the office, real polite, wanted to know about the Voss plot, upkeep schedule, that kind of thing. Then asked, casual as you please, whether the ground had ever settled funny."

Hazel felt the morning tilt. "What did you tell her?"

"Told her the truth. Ground settles everywhere. Didn't mention the rest." Otis shrugged. "Didn't seem like her business. Didn't seem like anybody's business." He studied Hazel's face with frank rural curiosity. "You got a description need writing down?"

"No," Hazel said softly. "I've got it."

Five foot six. Left-handed. Expensive shoes, resoled. A jawline and a mouth held half a second in the wind.

The veiled woman had asked about the soil years ago. The veiled woman had left before the lid came open today. And somewhere between those two facts lay the reason Hazel Galloway would not sleep properly again for months — because she had come to this cemetery to verify a signature and close a file, and instead she was standing over fifty-three river stones, understanding, with the terrible certainty of her own unforgiving memory, that the case everyone had buried five years ago had never been buried at all.

It had only been waiting for someone who remembered everything to come along and dig.
