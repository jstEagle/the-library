## 10. The Company Man

Marcus Webb arrived on a Tuesday, which was itself a small offense. The Voss file had been mine alone for eleven days, and in eleven days I had built something with clean edges — the mortician's double signature, the folded shroud, the river stones, Dale Pruitt bolting from his own confession like a man who'd heard footsteps behind him. It was not finished. But it was *mine*, and I had learned that the things I build in my head are the only possessions I've ever trusted.

He came into the county records office at 9:14 in the morning wearing a charcoal suit that had never seen rain and carrying a leather portfolio embossed with the Meridian Mutual logo. I knew who he was before he introduced himself, because Meridian had faxed ahead — or rather, they had faxed a single line: *Adjuster M. Webb arriving to assist on Voss matter.* No arrival date. No authorization number. Just his name, sitting in my inbox like a stone dropped into still water.

"Hazel Galloway," he said, extending a hand. His grip was calibrated — firm enough to signal confidence, brief enough to signal he wasn't trying to establish anything. "Marcus Webb, special investigations. I'm here to help you close this out."

"Close it out," I repeated.

"These cases have a way of growing moss." He smiled. He had very even teeth and eyes that did not participate in the smile at all. They stayed flat and attentive, the way a camera lens is attentive. "The company would like a resolution."

"The company has a body missing from a coffin," I said. "Resolutions are premature."

Something flickered across his face — not annoyance, exactly. Recalculation. He set the portfolio on my desk and opened it, and I understood immediately that this man had prepared for me the way you prepare for a deposition.

*Assist,* the fax had said. I watched him lay out his materials — copies of my own interim reports, annotated in blue ink, and I felt the particular chill of seeing your private thinking handled by someone else's hands.

---

I want to explain what my memory is like, because it matters to what happened next, and because most people misunderstand it. They think photographic memory means pictures. It doesn't, not really. It means that everything I see is filed — permanently, involuntarily, in full detail — and that I can go back into the file. Most people's memories are summaries. Mine is the original document. When Webb laid out those annotated pages, I didn't just read them. I compared them, instantly and without effort, against the reports I had submitted, word by word, comma by comma.

And there it was. Not in what he'd written. In what he hadn't.

My third interim report, submitted nine days ago, contained a paragraph on the shroud — the hospital fold, the linen protocol, the inference that whoever wrapped those stones had worked in medicine or been trained by someone who had. That paragraph was absent from Webb's copy. So was my note on Pruitt's flight, and my request to subpoena the cash payments. What remained was a version of my work with every dangerous thread snipped out — leaving only the safe, boring skeleton: coffin empty, stones present, fraud suspected, investigation ongoing.

"You've redacted my report," I said.

"I've summarized it." He said it without a flicker. "For distribution upstairs. Some findings generate more noise than value at this stage."

"Which findings?"

"The ones that speculate." He closed the portfolio. "Miss Galloway, you have a reputation. Total recall, they say. A mind like a courthouse vault. That's why they sent me — not because they doubt you, but because a case like this needs someone who can carry the weight of it without bending." He paused, and let the silence do its work. "How are you holding up? Really. These small towns, an exhumation, all the attention. It takes a toll."

There it was. The first question about me. I noted it the way I note everything — automatically, permanently — but I noted it twice, because it was the wrong question. An adjuster sent to assist should ask about the coffin. This one asked about my sleep, my stress, my support system. He asked them gently, sympathetically, and he wrote down my answers in a small notebook, and I understood that I was not being assisted.

I was being assessed.

---

That evening I did what I always do when a fact won't sit still: I went back to the beginning.

Webb said he'd driven in that morning from the regional office in Harlow Bend, four hours east. He mentioned it casually, as explanation for his early-morning arrival and his rumpled collar — though nothing else about him was rumpled. Four hours. Fine. Except.

Except that when he signed the visitor log at the county building, the pen skipped on the W of *Webb*, and beneath the skip I could see the ghost impression of writing already on the page beneath — the carbon copy of yesterday's log, where a guest had signed in at 6:52 p.m. the previous evening. I remember signatures the way other people remember faces. I had glanced at that log yesterday, the way I glance at everything, and the impression under Webb's pen matched the shape of yesterday's entry. Same hand. Same distinctive forward slant on the capital letters, same habit of crossing the b with a long trailing stroke.

Marcus Webb had been in this town yesterday. Possibly longer.

I sat with that for a while in my kitchen, tea going cold beside me, running the timeline. The exhumation order had issued eleven days ago. Court orders don't move fast; this one had taken three weeks to grind through the docket after Meridian's fraud claim triggered it. Which meant Meridian had known about the exhumation for nearly a month. Plenty of time to send a man early. Plenty of time for a man to arrive, settle in, watch, wait — to be standing at the back of the cemetery crowd on the day the coffin rose, one face among forty that I had memorized and catalogued and not yet identified.

I closed my eyes. The cemetery came back whole: the gray sky, the wet grass smell, the sheriff's deputies in their bad suits, the gravedigger Hollis with dirt to the elbows, the veiled woman slipping away before the lid opened. Forty-one faces. I walked through them now, slowly, the way you walk a hallway at night knowing something is different. Face thirty-three. Tall man, mid-forties, charcoal overcoat, standing slightly apart from the cluster of gawkers, near the yew hedge. Even teeth. Eyes that watched the coffin without any grief in them at all — watching it the way a man watches a thing he expected.

It was him. It had always been him. Marcus Webb had stood twenty yards from an open grave five days before his employer officially sent him to town.

---

The next morning I requested Webb's expense file from the regional office, using the bland bureaucratic language that gets clerks to move quickly: *reconciliation of joint investigative costs, per inter-office protocol 12-C.* There is no protocol 12-C. There is no one at the Harlow Bend office who has ever heard of me. But bureaucracy runs on the fear of looking ignorant, and by Thursday afternoon a fax came through with three pages of expense reports.

I spread them on my desk and read them the way I read everything — completely, once, forever.

Hotel receipts. Meal allowances. Mileage logs. And dates.

Webb's expense reports showed him billing Meridian for travel days beginning **six days before** the exhumation order issued. Six days before the court authorized digging up Edmund Voss, Marcus Webb was already charging the company for lodging twelve miles from the cemetery. Before the order existed, before the fraud claim had ripened into legal action, he was here — eating steak dinners and renting a sedan and, presumably, watching.

You don't send an assistant six days early. You send a supervisor early when you expect trouble. You send someone early when you want them positioned before events unfold — or when you want to make sure events unfold *correctly*.

And one more thing, the kind of thing that lives in margins: on the mileage log for the day of the exhumation, Webb claimed 14 miles driven. The round trip from his hotel to the cemetery and back is 11 miles, and I know because I've driven it. Three miles unaccounted. Three miles is a detour. Three miles is the river road, or the old mill property, or the Voss orchard lane. I couldn't prove what the extra three miles were. But I could prove they existed, and I could prove he lied about them by omission, and a man who lies in small ways is practicing for larger ones.

---

I started the second file that night.

Not in the office — never in the office. At home, in a plain manila folder with no label, kept inside the false bottom of a document box I'd bought years ago for tax papers nobody would ever steal. Into it went, handwritten in my own careful script:

*Item one:* Visitor log impression, county building, matching handwriting between prior-evening entry and Webb signature. Dated.

*Item two:* Expense reports, three pages, photocopied at the library machine (cash, no record). Dates preceding exhumation order by six days.

*Item three:* Cemetery crowd, face thirty-three, description and position, correlated to Webb's appearance upon introduction.

*Item four:* Redacted summary of my interim reports — omissions itemized: shroud paragraph, Pruitt flight, cash-payment subpoena request.

*Item five:* Mileage discrepancy, 3 miles, day of exhumation.

Five items. Thin, as files go. But files grow, and I am patient, and my memory does not forget a single thing I feed it.

Here is what frightened me, and I want to be precise about the fear because precision is all I have. It wasn't Webb himself. Men like Webb are instruments; the fear of a hammer misses the point of hammers. What frightened me was the arithmetic underneath him. Someone at Meridian had decided, weeks ago, that the Voss case needed handling. Not investigating — *handling*. They had sent a man before the court order, positioned him at the graveside, given him authority to edit my findings, and instructed him — I would bet the deed to my house — to learn everything about *me*: how tired I was, how reliable, how susceptible, how much pressure I could take and in which direction I would bend.

Institutions do not spend that kind of care on a dead man's empty coffin. They spend it on exposure. Somewhere above Marcus Webb, someone had calculated what the truth about Edmund Voss would cost the company, and had decided the calculation favored burial — and if you're willing to bury a truth, the question that keeps you up at night is: what else are you willing to bury?

Dale Pruitt had known names. Dale Pruitt had bolted from a bar stool two nights ago, terrified of something worse than prison. And now a company man with forged timelines and edited reports was asking gentle questions about my health.

I wrote a sixth item in the folder, and my pen slowed as I wrote it, because writing it made it real:

*Item six:* If Pruitt talks to anyone before he talks again to me, find out who reached him first. Check whether anyone from Meridian visited Harlan County in the last week.

I looked at that sentence for a long time. Then I boxed the folder, slid it into the false bottom, and turned off the lamp.

In the dark, my memory kept working, the way it always does — replaying Webb's handshake, his flat camera-lens eyes, the way he'd said *close this out* as if the case were a wound and he'd brought sutures. And underneath all of it, one detail rising up out of the archive of my own mind, insisting on attention: at the cemetery, face thirty-three had not been watching the coffin.

I was certain of it now, replaying it frame by frame. He had been watching the veiled woman.

And she had left before the lid ever opened — which meant either she feared what was inside it, or she already knew.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would stop chasing the company's paper trail and go back to the woman in the veil. Because whatever Marcus Webb had been sent to bury, I no longer believed it was only documents.

Somebody had paid Dale Pruitt in cash he never counted. Somebody had edited my reports before they reached upstairs. And somebody, six days early, had stood at a grave watching a grieving daughter walk away.

The institution was compromised. Which meant the only file I could trust was the one locked inside my own head —

— and the only person in this town who might understand why was Beatrix Voss.
