## 10. The Second Confession

The envelope was waiting for me on Tuesday morning, wedged into the gap between the storm door and the frame of my apartment like a bill someone didn't want to lose. No stamp. Hand-delivered.

I stood on my own front step in the gray light with my coffee going cold in one hand and that envelope in the other, and I did not open it right away. That's a thing people don't understand about a memory like mine — it isn't just that I remember everything. It's that I remember everything *at once*, and sometimes the remembering needs a moment to settle before you can look at anything new. The first two letters had arrived by mail, weeks apart, both postmarked from the sorting facility out on Route 9. This one had been carried here by a person, at night or very early morning, past Mrs. Delgado's hydrangeas and up my three porch steps, and I hadn't heard a thing.

That bothered me more than the letter did. Or it would have, if I'd let myself think about it too long.

I opened it standing there, because Beatrix Osei had taught me — without meaning to, over cold noodles and a shared bag of fortune cookies three nights earlier — that waiting is its own kind of cowardice, and I'd decided I wasn't going to be a coward about this anymore.

Two pages. Same hand as the first confession. I knew it before I'd finished the first line: the same narrow, upright script, the same peculiar way the writer crossed their t's low and flat, like ruled lines drawn in haste. My memory laid the first letter over this one, ghost against paper, and they matched stroke for stroke.

The letter said:

*You have the ledger. Now you need the hands. The fire at Crane's warehouse was set by Dale Prosser, contractor, Halloway Mutual approved vendor list, 1998–2021. He died in March, two years ago. Heart attack, alone, in his truck behind the VFW hall. Convenient, isn't it, how the dead can't testify. But dead men leave receipts. Check his equipment invoices against the accelerant purchase logs. Check who countersigned his final vendor renewal. You already know whose initials those are.*

*Third installment to follow when you've earned it.*

*— A friend of Edith's*

I read it twice, which is once more than I ever need, and then I stood on my porch and felt the particular chill that comes not from fear but from structure. Because this was structure. This was *method*. First a confession with no name attached. Then a pointer toward the ledger. Now a name — but a dead man's name, a man who couldn't be questioned, couldn't recant, couldn't contradict whatever story the letter-writer wanted told. And a promise of more, metered out like medicine, "when you've earned it."

Someone was feeding us the truth in doses. And whoever doles things out in doses wants something. Either they wanted Roland Pike destroyed — which, after what we'd found in the decoded ledger, I could sympathize with — or they wanted Beatrix and me destroyed *with* him, positioned so neatly in the blast radius that no jury would ever sort the wreckage into guilty and innocent.

Both, maybe. The best traps are generous. They give you exactly what you came for.

* * *

Beatrix was already at the diner when I got there, in the back booth under the window with the cracked vinyl seat she always claimed on principle, a cup of black tea going lukewarm beside her and her father's old claim folder open in front of her like a bible she'd stopped believing in but couldn't put down. She'd started sitting with her back to the wall. I'd noticed that. Three years ago, file 44-C said, she used to sit facing the door.

"You look terrible," she said, which was her way of saying good morning.

"I got mail." I slid into the booth across from her and passed the letter over. "Same hand as the first one. Read the signature line."

She read fast — faster than most people, though nobody reads like I read; reading, for me, is less an activity than a kind of photographing — and when she reached the end her jaw did the thing it did, a small tightening at the hinge, the only tell she'd never managed to train out of herself.

"Dale Prosser," she said. "I knew him. He fixed our roof when I was nine. He let me hold the nail gun." She looked up. "He was at the warehouse that night. I told the police — the second car, remember? I never got a plate, but it was a work truck, white, racks on top. They wrote it down and then they wrote it off. Grief, trauma, whatever word lets you stop listening to a nineteen-year-old girl."

"And now he's dead," I said. "Two years dead. Heart attack in a parking lot."

"You don't believe in coincidences. You've said so four times, and each time you looked smugger about it."

"I'm not feeling smug." I turned my teacup a quarter turn on its saucer, which is what I do instead of pacing. "Beatrix, think about the sequence. Letter one confesses to the fire but names nobody. Letter two points us at the ledger — and the ledger is real, we've held it, we've decoded eleven payments including Pike's signature the week before your father died. So far, everything this person has sent us has checked out. Which means either they're telling the truth, or they're lying with true things, which is worse."

"Which is worse how?"

"Because true things make excellent bait." I leaned forward. "Letter three gives us a dead arsonist. Fine. Maybe Prosser did set the fire — I believe he did, actually, because the accelerant logs match his invoice cycle and I checked that six minutes ago in my head while you were reading. But notice what the letter does. It gives us a man who can't deny it, can't confirm it, can't be cross-examined. If we take this to Ferro, or to a grand jury, the whole case hangs on a corpse and a stack of invoices. And Pike's lawyers will stand up and say: this is a theory built by a fired-up investigator with a grudge and the daughter of the deceased, fed anonymously by someone with an ax to grind. Where's the living witness? Where's the document with Pike's *name*, not his initials?"

"Page seven has his full signature," Beatrix said quietly. "We decoded it together. You recited it to me at two in the morning like a prayer."

"I know what page seven says. I'll always know what page seven says." That came out more raw than I intended, and she had the grace to pretend she hadn't noticed, stirring her tea. "What I'm saying is the letters are leading us somewhere. Dosed, deliberate, patient. And I want to know who's holding the spoon."

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing what I privately thought of as the boring part and Beatrix called, with some justice, "the actual work": pulling every scrap of paper Halloway Mutual had ever generated about Dale Prosser. As an approved vendor he'd been paid through the claims department for twenty-three years, which meant invoices, W-9s, renewal forms, and — crucially — counter-signatures. I remembered all of them. That's not a boast; it's a condition. I can close my eyes and see the 2011 renewal form with coffee ringed in one corner, the 2016 invoice batch with the misaligned staple, the way Prosser signed his middle initial with a loop that dipped below the baseline like a fish hook.

The final vendor renewal was dated January of the year he died. Countersigned in the lower right corner: *R.P.*

Roland Pike. Approving, extending, keeping on the payroll — the man who would later swear to me over sandwiches that he barely knew Prosser beyond "the usual vendor stuff."

"He knew him well enough to sign him on eight weeks before the fire," Beatrix said, tapping the photocopy. "And the letter says check the accelerant purchases. So?"

"So I did. From memory, because the original purchase logs were purged in the records rotation two years ago — which itself is interesting, since nothing else from that fiscal year got purged." I pulled a legal pad toward me and wrote it out, because Beatrix trusted ink more than she trusted my head, even now, even after the night we rebuilt the stolen file page by page until dawn. "Prosser's company bought acetone and pool chlorine through a supply house in Kettletown, quarterly, for six years. Small amounts. Never enough to flag. Except in October of the fire year, the order tripled. And the delivery address on that one order isn't his shop. It's a storage unit on Fenwick Road. Unit 14-C."

Beatrix went very still. "44-C," she said. "My father's file number. And 14-C."

"Could be nothing. Storage places number things however they number them."

"Do you believe that?"

"No," I admitted. "But I believe in being honest about what's coincidence and what's pattern, because if we ever get in front of a prosecutor, Nadia Ferro will eat us alive if we can't tell the difference." I sat back. "Here's what I keep coming back to. Whoever's writing these letters knows things only an insider could know. Vendor lists. Purchase logs. Records rotations. The ledger's existence. Edith Crane's cipher. And they know *us* — they knew where I live, they knew I'd bring this to you, they knew exactly how much truth to give at each step to keep us moving without letting us run ahead."

"Ruth Crane," Beatrix said. It wasn't the first time she'd floated it. Edith's widow, quiet and careful, keeper of the arithmetic primer that had unlocked everything.

"Maybe. Ruth loved Edith, and Edith was murdered for this ledger — I'd bet my pension on that, such as it is. A widow with three years and a grudge fits." I rubbed my eyes. "But Ruth is seventy-one years old, and somebody walked up my porch steps last night without waking me or Mrs. Delgado's dog, and left this. That takes practice. Or help. Or neither, and I'm wrong about everything, which lately feels equally likely."

"There's a third option," Beatrix said, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when she was about to say something she'd been carrying alone. "The option where the letters aren't meant to help us at all. They're meant to build us. Think about it, Quentin. Every letter makes us look guiltier. We're investigating a fire we shouldn't know about, decoding a ledger we can't legally explain having, meeting in secret, and now we're chasing a dead man's paper trail. If Pike finds out — *when* Pike finds out — the story writes itself. The daughter of the insured, obsessed, feeding fabricated evidence to a rogue investigator. We're not the detectives in this version. We're the conspiracy."

The diner noise went on around us — the grill hissing, old Mr. Abernathy complaining about the price of pie — and I sat inside the silence of what she'd said and turned it over, and hated it, because it fit.

"So we stop?" I asked.

"I didn't say that." She reached across the table and took the letter back, smoothing it flat with both hands, the way she smoothed everything — creases, tempers, me. "I'm saying if someone is building a trap out of true things, then the only way to survive it is to get to the truest thing first. Faster than the letters. Faster than Pike. We find the living witness the letter says doesn't exist. Somebody saw Prosser that night besides me. Somebody handled that storage unit. Somebody helped Edith Crane encode eleven bribes and then watched her drive off a road." Her eyes came up to mine, and there it was — the defiance I'd recognized in file 44-C three years ago, banked now instead of burning, but the same fire underneath. "The sender wants us moving along their schedule. So we break the schedule."

* * *

I went home the long way, past the VFW hall, because I'm a creature of habit and also because I wanted to see the lot where Dale Prosser's truck had been found. It was empty except for a dumpster and a cat with opinions. I stood there five minutes doing nothing useful, letting my memory run its nightly audit, and that's when I caught it — the thing that had been itching at me since the porch.

The postmarks. Letters one and two had come by mail, Route 9 facility. I'd noted the dates, the cancellation marks, the little smudged serial numbers, because I note everything whether I want to or not. And the serial numbers on both cancellations were sequential. Not similar — *sequential*. Two letters, mailed weeks apart, processed back to back through the same machine, same shift, same tray.

Which meant they hadn't been mailed weeks apart. They'd been mailed together, held somewhere, released on a schedule. Someone had a drawer with our future in it, rationing it out.

I stood in the empty lot with the cat watching me and felt the paranoia sharpen, finally, into something usable. Fear is a fog; resolve is the same fear with a direction. Whoever "A Friend of Edith's" was, they were methodical, patient, local, and playing a longer game than grief usually allows. They wanted Pike. They might want us. And they had at least one more letter in the drawer.

My phone buzzed. Beatrix: *Ferro called the office today asking for you by name. I didn't pick up. Thought you should know.*

I looked down at the letter in my hand, at the neat upright script promising a fourth installment, and typed back: *Then we move first. Tomorrow. There's someone I need you to meet.*

Because if the letters were going to feed us the truth one dose at a time, there was one person left who might still give it to us whole — the last living soul besides Ruth who'd known both Edith Crane and Dale Prosser, retired now to a bungalow on Larkspur Lane, a former claims clerk named Ada Whitfield who had, according to a personnel memo I could still see word for word, resigned from Halloway Mutual eleven days after Edith's funeral.

Ada had never given a reason. I intended to find out why.
