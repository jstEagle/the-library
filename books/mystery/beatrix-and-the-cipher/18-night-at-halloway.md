## 18. Night at Halloway

### Quentin

There is a particular kind of foolishness that only arrives dressed as necessity. I recognized it at eleven forty on a Tuesday night, standing in the alley behind Halloway Mutual with a camera in my coat pocket and my heart doing something medically inadvisable against my ribs.

"You're sure about the window," Beatrix whispered.

"I'm sure about everything. That's my whole tragedy." I nodded toward the fire exit on the north side, the one with the latch that had featured so prominently in her arrest. "Third floor archive. The latch sticks unless you lift it exactly right — which, incidentally, is why your prints being on it was always nonsense. Anyone who'd actually used that window would have left prints *above* the latch, not below."

"Did you tell the police that?"

"I told Detective Marsh. He wrote it down in a notebook he then appeared to lose." I shrugged. "The town has a way of losing things it doesn't want to find."

Beatrix pulled her collar up against the October cold and looked at me for a long moment — the look she'd perfected over these weeks, the one that said she was weighing me against every other man who'd ever promised her something. I tried to look like a man worth the weight.

"Say it again," she said quietly. "Why we're doing this."

"The ledger's last entry references policy number 88-4417-C. The Crane file number. But Edith died before that suffix existed — Pike added the C-series renumbering *after* the fire, during the 2021 audit. Which means the final page of her ledger was written after her death, or the entry was amended by someone who knew the fire was coming. Either way, the original filing proving when that number changed lives in Pike's safe. Not a copy. The original. Paper doesn't lie about its own dates the way men do."

"And if we're caught?"

I looked at her. She wasn't asking hypothetically. She'd been caught once already; she knew the arithmetic of what a second offense would cost her.

"If we're caught," I said, "you were never here. You keep watch two blocks away, by the pharmacy. If anything goes wrong, you walk home and you know nothing. That was the deal."

"That was *your* deal," she said. "I didn't sign it."

"Beatrix—"

"Quentin." She said my name the way Ruth Crane had said *Edith* — like an argument she intended to win. "You have spent three years memorizing this town because remembering was safer than living. I have spent three years being suspected of everything except the truth. We are not going to stand in an alley dividing up who gets to be brave. We go together or not at all."

Somewhere down the block, the pharmacy's neon sign buzzed and flickered — the V had been dying for years, so the whole town shopped at PHARMAC , which Beatrix once said sounded like a Roman emperor. I thought about that now, absurdly, because terror does that. It hands you jokes at gunpoint.

"Together," I said.

She smiled, and it was the smile from our first meal at the diner, the one that had undone three decades of my careful filing. "Then show me how a man breaks into his own life."

* * *

### Beatrix

The window opened the way he said it would — lift, then pull, then a quarter-turn to the left that no reasonable person would ever guess. Quentin went through first, all elbows and apology, whispering *sorry* to the frame as though the building might hold it against him. Maybe it would. Buildings remember too; he'd taught me that.

Inside, the archive smelled the way it always had when he described it to me: paper, toner, and the faint vanilla of old glue. In the dark it was a cave of shelves, and I understood for the first time why he loved it down here. Nothing in this room had ever lied to him. Every folder sat where it was put. I had spent three years wishing people worked that way.

"Flashlight," he breathed, and I passed him the little penlight I'd stolen — borrowed — from my mother's junk drawer. He cupped his hand around it so the light fell in a coin-sized circle, and he moved through the stacks like a man walking his own hallway at night. Which, I supposed, he was.

"Pike's office is second floor, corner suite," he murmured. "The safe is behind the framed photo of the 1998 adjusters' retreat. He thinks nobody knows. He told me once it was there, actually — back when I was twenty-six and he was teaching me to read claim patterns. He said, 'A man should keep one thing in this building that isn't for sale.' I thought it was wisdom." His voice did something complicated on the last word. "It was a confession. Everything he ever taught me was a confession wearing a cardigan."

We took the stairs. My legs knew they were doing something criminal even if my heart insisted otherwise, and halfway up the second flight I heard it — or thought I heard it — a door somewhere above us, the soft hydraulic sigh of a closer.

Quentin froze. His hand found mine in the dark, and I will admit here, in the privacy of my own head where no prosecutor can reach it: I held on.

"Janitorial closet is on this floor," he whispered. "If someone's here, we wait them out."

We pressed into the alcove beside the elevator, between a water cooler and a motivational poster featuring a mountain climber and the word PERSEVERANCE. I stared at the climber and thought: *buddy, you don't know the half of it.*

Nothing came. No footsteps. After four minutes — Quentin counted them silently against my wrist, tapping — he exhaled.

"Old building," he said. "The doors talk to themselves."

But his hand hadn't let go of mine either, and neither of us mentioned it, and we climbed.

* * *

### Quentin

The combination was Roland Pike's birthday reversed, followed by the year Halloway Mutual was founded. I knew this because Roland had made me memorize it six years ago — "in case I'm ever hospitalized, son, someone should be able to feed the fish" — and because Roland Pike trusted me the way a spider trusts the geometry of its own web. I turned the dial with fingers that refused to stop shaking, and hated them for it, and forgave them, because they were about to do the bravest thing any part of me had ever done.

The safe opened with a sound like a small, polite cough.

Inside: deed boxes, a tin of shortbread (Roland's wife's recipe; he kept them even during the years of the diet), a ledger of legitimate accounts, and beneath all of it, wrapped in a plastic sleeve, a single manila folder with the tab worn soft from handling.

88-4417-C.

I lifted it out. My penlight found the pages, and my memory — that faithful, cursed librarian — began photographing them faster than any camera could. Endorsement rider dated March 4th. Renumbering memo dated March 9th. And the fire was March 11th. Two days. Two days between the moment the coverage was rewritten and the moment the warehouse burned, and the rewrite increased the payout by a factor of four, and the signature at the bottom of both documents belonged to R. A. Pike, Chief Adjuster, whose handwriting I could identify from across a room because he had written *Good work, Quentin* on forty of my early files in that same confident loop.

"Camera," I whispered.

Beatrix handed it over without a word. I photographed each page twice, flash off, bracing the folder against the shelf. The shutter clicked like a beetle in a jar. On the fourth page I stopped.

Because there, clipped to the endorsement, was something I hadn't predicted. A note, handwritten, unsigned, in handwriting I knew almost as well as Roland's.

My own.

Dated six years ago. *Roland — confirming receipt of revised schedule per your instruction. Q.*

He'd kept it. All these years, he'd kept a note in my handwriting sitting inside the proof of his crime, and I understood with a cold, total clarity what that meant. It wasn't sentiment. It was insurance. Roland Pike had insured himself against me the way he insured everything else — quietly, thoroughly, and with a payout calculated in advance. If I ever went to Ferro, this note would say I'd known. That I'd helped. That the archivist of Marrow Creek's secrets had been keeping his own all along.

"Quentin?" Beatrix's hand on my shoulder. "What is it?"

"He's got me in here," I said. My voice came out strange and flat. "One sentence in my handwriting, six years old, filed next to the arson. He built a trapdoor under me and I walked over it every day for six years and thanked him for the building."

"Then we take it."

"We can't. If it disappears he'll know exactly when and exactly who. It stays. Let him think it still protects him." I slid the folder back, closed the safe, spun the dial. "The photographs are enough. Dates don't need originals when I can recite them under oath. He never understood that about memory — he thinks it's storage. It's testimony."

And that was when the lights came on.

Not the overheads — those would have been a mercy, a flood of honest illumination. Just the desk lamp, the small brass one, clicking on with the intimate certainty of a man who had arrived home. And Roland Pike stood in the doorway of his own office in a rain-spotted overcoat, holding a briefcase, looking at us with an expression I had seen him wear a thousand times at claims conferences: patient, disappointed, infinitely kind.

"Well," he said. "Look at this. My two favorite problems, collaborating."

* * *

### Beatrix

Later — in the courtroom, in the retellings, in whatever version of this night survives — people will ask what I felt when I saw him, and I will lie, because the truth is useless. The truth is that I felt *seen*. Three years of being looked through, looked past, looked at with suspicion, and Roland Pike looked at me the way you look at weather you've decided to walk through anyway.

"Mr. Villalobos," Pike said, setting his briefcase down with the care of a man arranging chess pieces. "In my office. At midnight. With her." He shook his head slowly, and God help me, he managed to make it sound fond. "Do you know what I told the board about you? I said, that boy has a vault where his conscience should be. Nothing gets in, nothing gets out. I said it as a compliment."

"It was," Quentin said. His voice was steady. I gripped the back of a chair to keep my own knees honest. "That's the problem, Roland. Vaults don't discriminate. They keep everything — including tonight."

Pike smiled at that, genuinely, and it was worse than anger. "You photographed the folder. Don't bother denying it; you've got shutter-click posture, you've had it since you were twenty-five." He crossed to the window, looked out at the wet street. "Here's what happens now, and I want you to hear it the way I'd say it to family, because you are family, Quentin, that was never a performance. Ferro's grand jury convenes Thursday. If those photographs surface, my attorney produces a certain note in your handwriting. Six years old. Filed neatly. Suddenly this isn't a story about a chief adjuster — it's a story about his protégé, the man with the famous memory, who *confirmed receipt of the revised schedule.* Who knew. Who stayed. Who ate lunch at my table for six years while a dead man's daughter—" he gestured at me, gently, horribly, "—took the suspicion. You wouldn't survive it. Neither would she. Juries don't forgive complicity, even innocent complicity. Especially not innocent complicity; innocence makes them suspicious."

"You rewrote the policy two days before the fire," I said.

Pike turned and looked at me properly for the first time. "Mrs. Osei. Beatrix." He said my name softly, and I realized he'd practiced it. "Your father signed the original amendment request himself. Did Quentin tell you that? Page three. His initials are on it. Grief is a terrible editor — it crops the picture until only the villain remains. Your father needed money. I provided a mechanism. What happened at the warehouse afterward was a contractor's negligence, a tragedy, and I have carried it—"

"You carried it into a safe," I said, "behind a photograph of a fishing trip."

Something moved behind his eyes then — the first honest thing all night. He looked, just for a second, tired. Old. Like a man who had been holding a very heavy briefcase for a very long time.

"Edith Crane also kept copies," he said quietly. "Did Ruth tell you that? There are three sets of the ledger in this world, and I've spent three years finding two of them. I found hers the week after the funeral. I found the primer last spring." He straightened his coat. "Which means somewhere out there, someone still has a set, and keeps mailing pieces of it to an insurance investigator with a photographic memory. I don't suppose either of you knows who."

Neither of us spoke. The rain ticked against the window. And I watched Quentin — watched him stand in the office of the man who raised him professionally, maybe personally, watched him choose, in real time, with his whole face — and what I saw there made me less afraid than I had been since the night of the fire.

"Thursday," Pike said, picking up his briefcase. "Think about Thursday. Think about her, Quentin. You're good at thinking." He paused at the door, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "Lock up when you go. Some habits are worth keeping."

The stairwell door closed behind him.

* * *

### Quentin

Beatrix exhaled like a diver surfacing. "He knew. He knew we'd come. He waited for us."

"No." I was already moving, gathering my thoughts the way I gathered papers, into order. "He *hoped* we'd come. There's a difference, and the difference is that he doesn't have the photographs, doesn't know what we took, and just showed his whole hand because he believed he was holding the winning cards." I laughed — a short, unhinged sound that echoed off the shelves. "He showed me the trapdoor, Beatrix. He *told me* about the note. Do you know what that means? It means he needs me scared more than he needs me silenced. Scared men stay. Dead or arrested men become martyrs with subpoena power."

"So what do we do?"

"We do what he taught me." I took her hand — deliberately this time, in the open, in the building where we'd broken the law together. "We read the claim. Every claim has a flaw, he used to say. Find the flaw and the file closes itself." I looked toward the dark windows, the wet street, the pharmacy sign buzzing its broken alphabet two blocks away. "His flaw is that he thinks memory is a vault. Storage. Something to be robbed."

Downstairs, faint but unmistakable, came the sound none of us had invited: sirens. Two of them. Then three, converging on Halloway Mutual from opposite ends of Marrow Creek, red and blue washing up the walls of the stairwell like tide.

Beatrix's grip tightened on my hand. "Did you call anyone?"

"No."

"Did *he*?"

And that was the question, wasn't it — the one we'd carry down those stairs and into whatever waited outside with its lights spinning. Because Roland Pike had walked out calm. Roland Pike had said *lock up when you go.* And Roland Pike did nothing, in my long experience of him, without a reason filed and cross-referenced.

The sirens grew louder.

"Whichever way this goes," Beatrix said, "we walk out together."

"Together," I said, and reached for the stairs.
