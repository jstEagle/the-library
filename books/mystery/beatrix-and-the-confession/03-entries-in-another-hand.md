## 3. Entries in Another Hand

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a hotel after midnight, and I have come to think of it as the sound of a building listening. The Verlaine at two in the morning, with its radiators ticking and its elevator humming to itself on some private errand, felt less like a workplace and more like an accomplice waiting for me to catch up.

I had taken the diary home against every rule of archival practice and several rules of common decency. I told myself it was for proper lighting. I told myself a lot of things that week.

I sat at my kitchen table with the book flat under a lamp, a magnifying glass I'd borrowed from the lost-and-found box — guests abandon the strangest things; once, a jeweler's loupe, which would have been better — and a pot of coffee going cold beside me like a witness who'd decided not to talk.

The first thing you learn reading handwriting is that handwriting is a confession. People disguise their faces far better than they disguise their letters. A loop tightened here, a stroke lifted there, and suddenly you know whether someone was hurried or calm, drunk or afraid, in love or pretending not to be.

Beatrix Vane's early entries were confident without being showy. A slight rightward lean, as if the words were leaning toward something just out of frame. Her *t*-crosses were decisive slashes. Her margins were clean. She wrote like a woman who had been taught penmanship by someone she resented and had made peace with the result.

*March 4th, 2013. Ambrose came to dinner again. He ordered for me before I could open the menu — the sole, as always, "because you like it." I have not liked it since I was nine years old. I smiled and ate it. There is a version of me he keeps in a glass case, dusted weekly, fed on schedule. Some days I want to break the glass. Most days I am the glass.*

I read that entry three times. Then I turned to the last pages.

*June 17th, 2016. The light here comes off the water and into the room like it's been invited. I planted tomatoes in a box on the sill. They will probably die. Everything I plant dies, except apparently me.*

Dated three years after she walked out of the Verlaine and into the river, according to the police report, which had found her coat on the bank and her shoes on the stones and closed the file with the tidy finality of men who prefer their tragedies pre-packaged.

And yet there she was, complaining about her gardening.

Here is what kept me up until four: the handwriting was wrong. Not obviously wrong. If you weren't looking — if you wanted it to be Beatrix, the way everyone connected to this case seemed to want something — you'd pass right over it. But the lean had straightened. The *t*-crosses were no longer slashes but careful horizontal strokes, laid down like someone setting a table for company. And the *g*s — Beatrix's *g*s had a closed lower loop, a little fist of a letter. These *g*s were open. Relaxed. Almost apologetic.

Someone else's hand. Writing in her voice.

My first thought was forgery, and my second thought was: forged for whom? A diary isn't a document you forge to fool banks or lawyers. Nobody profits from a fake diary. You forge a diary to fool one person only — the dead woman herself, or the people who miss her. It is the most useless crime imaginable, unless the profit is something other than money.

I did what any reasonable person does at four in the morning. I made more coffee and started comparing entries line by line, because if someone was going to haunt a dead woman's diary, they were going to do it in front of me, and I was going to take notes.

* *

By dawn I had the whole book mapped. Entries before September 2013: forty-one. Entries after: sixty-three. The imposter hadn't written occasionally. They had written faithfully — Sundays, mostly, though sometimes Wednesdays, as if real life kept interrupting the fiction.

And the voice. God, the voice.

Whoever held the pen had Beatrix down cold. Not her facts — anyone with access to her room could have scraped up facts. Her *rhythms*. The way she circled a complaint three times before landing on it sideways. The dry deflection that arrived exactly when sentiment got dangerous. In the 2011 entries she wrote:

*I told the concierge my umbrella was stolen so he'd stop looking at me like I was a puzzle he intended to solve. It wasn't stolen. I left it in a taxi on purpose. Some things should belong to no one.*

In a 2015 entry, three years after her death:

*A man at the fish counter asked if I was eating alone again and I said yes, gloriously, and he looked sorry for me, which is the tax you pay for being a woman who says "gloriously" about anything.*

Same music. Same little pivot at the end, the joke that's actually a wound wearing a hat. You cannot fake that from surveillance. You cannot get it from reading someone's mail. That comes from years — years of listening to a person across a desk, or a lobby, or a dinner table where someone else orders the sole.

That was the word that kept surfacing as the sky went grey over the rooftops: *years*. This wasn't a hoax executed in an afternoon. This was a correspondence conducted in one direction, in secret, sustained across half a decade. Someone had received Beatrix Vane's words — her actual words, somewhere, somehow — and had copied them into this book, translated into her hand, dated and filed, keeping her alive on paper the way you keep a plant alive in a dark room. Badly lit. Stubbornly.

Intimacy without identity. I felt like a voyeur at a marriage I couldn't see either spouse of.

* *

Monday I took the diary to a friend. Marguerite Osei spent thirty years in the police forensic lab before retiring to restore paintings, which she claims is the same job with better lighting. She met me at her studio smelling of turpentine and judgment.

"You want a document examination," she said, "on a diary belonging to a woman whose death certificate I remember signing paperwork adjacent to. Sit down before you tell me why."

I sat. I told her why. She put on white gloves the way other people crack their knuckles.

For an hour she said nothing worth repeating, which is to say she said everything in the language of microscopes and raking light. Finally she sat back and pulled the gloves off finger by finger.

"Two hands," she said. "Obviously. Pre-disappearance, post-disappearance. But here's what's interesting."

"I'm listening."

"The second hand isn't disguising itself. A forger thickens strokes, varies slant, does all the amateur theatrics. This writer did none of that. They wrote naturally — and naturally, they write *like her*. Slower, calmer, but structurally identical. Letter formation, spacing habits, even her little habit of pressing harder on the downstroke when she's angry. Look—" she angled the lamp — "this entry about the tomatoes. Whoever wrote this was furious about something and doesn't say a single angry word, but the pressure spikes on every fourth or fifth letter. Beatrix did exactly that in 2012, here, when she mentions her uncle."

"So it's imitation?"

"No." Marguerite said it flatly, the way you'd say *no* to a child asking if the dog went to a farm. "Imitation is performed. This is absorbed. You don't learn someone's pressure patterns by studying their writing. You learn them by watching them write. For years. Sitting close enough to see the pen move." She tapped the page. "This isn't a forger, Odile. This is a student. Or a devotee. Or—"

"Or someone she taught," I said.

Marguerite looked at me over her glasses. "Or that. Who else knew her that well?"

And there it was, the question I'd been circling since the kitchen table. Because the list was short. Ambrose Vane, dead these six years, who had visited weekly and ordered the sole. A lawyer. A maid long gone to Marseille. And a porter — young then, twenty-two maybe, who had wept at the memorial service so openly that the staff still mentioned it eleven years later, half embarrassed, half moved, the way you mention weather that frightened you once.

Tomas.

But knowing a name and understanding a thing are different animals, and I had learned — chapter and verse, in this very hotel — that memory testifies unreliably and grief lies beautifully. I needed the diary itself to tell me more. So I went back to it, back to my kitchen table, back to the lamp, and I read the post-disappearance entries not as evidence anymore but as what they actually were.

Letters. Unsent letters, or received ones transcribed. Read that way, they changed shape entirely.

*April 9th, 2017. You asked once why I never let anyone carry my bags. It's because the year I turned twelve, Uncle Ambrose carried my suitcase to the car for boarding school and unpacked it at the gate to make sure I hadn't taken anything he hadn't approved. He repacked it himself. Neater than mine. I have been checking my own luggage ever since — metaphorically and otherwise. Here, nobody carries anything. It's wonderful.*

*You*, I noticed. Not the third person a diarist uses for an imagined reader. *You asked.* Direct address. Questions posed and answered. An entry from 2018:

*No, I haven't forgiven him. Don't ask me that on a Sunday, of all days. Ask me on a Tuesday, when I've had the sea to myself all morning and I'm feeling generous. Today I am not generous. Today I am a woman who checks her own luggage.*

A conversation. Two people talking across a distance, and one of them keeping the transcript hidden inside a dead woman's diary in a sealed storage room, where no one would ever —

Where no one was *meant* to find it. Which raised the question I set down my pen to consider, cold coffee forgotten: if these entries were meant for no eyes, why keep the diary at all? Why hide it in the Verlaine's storage among trunks and hatboxes, in the one building that connected them both?

Unless it wasn't hidden. Unless it was *kept*. Placed the way you place flowers. The way you place a photograph face-up on a shelf.

I turned to the final entry — June 2019, the true last page, in the second hand, the pressure spiking hard on every downstroke:

*The letters have stopped coming. Three months now. I tell myself the sea ate them, or the post office, or that she's simply tired of Tuesdays. But I know her. When she goes quiet, it's because she's decided something. Please. Whatever you've decided — write one line. One word. Even just your name, the new one, so I know it still fits you.*

The ink there was slightly blurred, as if a drop of water had fallen on it and been blotted in haste.

I closed the book. Somewhere below me, the Verlaine's boiler knocked twice, like a knock at a door, and I found myself thinking about a porter of twenty-two who cried at a memorial for a woman who wasn't dead — crying, perhaps, not for her, but for himself, for the long Tuesdays ahead, for a correspondence already underway that he could tell no living soul about.

Eleven years of letters. And then, two years ago, silence.

I picked up my coat. There was a man in the basement of the Hotel Verlaine who had been answering a ghost for a decade, and I thought it was time somebody asked him how the mail was delivered.
