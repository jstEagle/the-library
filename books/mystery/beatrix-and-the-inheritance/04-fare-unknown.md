## 4. Fare Unknown

Tommy Okafor lived in a third-floor walkup on Delancey Street, above a nail salon that smelled of acetone and ambition even at nine in the morning. Quentin climbed the stairs with two coffees from the cart on the corner—one black, one with enough sugar and cream in it to qualify as dessert—because he'd learned years ago that drivers told you things over coffee they would never tell you over the phone. The phone had a way of making men careful. A cup of warm paper and bad roast made them human.

He knocked. Footsteps, then the chain rattling, then Tommy's face in the gap—round, wary, still carrying the softness of sleep around his eyes.

"Q? It's—" He squinted at his watch. "It's not even ten. I don't start till four."

"I know. Got a minute?"

Tommy looked at the coffees, then at Quentin's face, and whatever he saw there made him unhook the chain without another word.

The apartment was small and clean in the way of a man who had once been married to someone tidier than himself. A couch, a TV mounted too high, a shelf of model ships he built with tweezers and infinite patience—Quentin knew this because everyone at Harborline Cab knew it; Tommy brought photos of the half-finished HMS something-or-other to every holiday potluck and stood beside them like a proud father. There was a photo now of a three-masted schooner with rigging so fine it looked like spider silk.

"Black's yours," Quentin said, handing over the wrong cup first, then switching. "You take it sweet."

"You remembered." Tommy took it anyway, sat heavily on the couch, and gestured at the other cushion. "Okay. You've got the face. The face you get when a fare goes sideways. What happened?"

Quentin sat. He'd rehearsed this on the drive over—not the words, exactly, but the shape of it. You couldn't come at a driver head-on about a fare; they clammed up, assumed they were being written up, started remembering nothing at all. You came at it sideways, the way you'd ease a car into a tight spot.

"Night of the twenty-second," Quentin said. "Two-fourteen a.m. I dispatched you to 44 Larkspur Lane."

Tommy blew across his coffee. "Larkspur. That was—the mausoleum thing, right? The cemetery." He frowned. "Weird one. Why, what's up?"

"That's what I'm trying to find out. Walk me through it. Everything you remember. Don't edit."

"Don't edit," Tommy repeated, and laughed a little, nervously. "Q, it was a month ago. I do forty fares a night."

"Forty fares don't ask you to wait with your engine running at a graveyard."

The laugh died in Tommy's throat. He set the coffee down on a coaster—a man trained by that tidier someone—and rubbed both hands down his thighs.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Yeah. I remember her."

* * *

She had come out of the dark the way fares sometimes did on the dead routes, materializing under the one working streetlight near the cemetery gate as if she'd been standing there for hours waiting for the light to find her. Gray coat, long, belted at the waist. Dark hair pulled back. Young—or young-ish; hard to say, because she hadn't turned her face fully toward him even once, not in forty minutes.

"She flagged me?" Quentin asked.

"Flagged me. Right there at the gate, which is itself weird, because nobody flags a cab on Delacroix Avenue at two in the morning unless their car died or their marriage did. But okay. She gets in the back, says—" Tommy closed his eyes, and Quentin watched him go back there, watched the recollection move behind his face like weather. "Says, 'Just drive along the fence line, slow.' Not an address. Just that. So I'm thinking, all right, lady wants to look at the cemetery at two a.m., Harborline's seen stranger. Hasn't it?" He looked at Quentin for reassurance and didn't wait to receive it. "So I drive. Slow. And she's watching out the window, and I mean *watching*, like she's reading something off the stones. And then she says stop. Right by the Vane mausoleum—that big granite one, looks like a bank that died."

"The big one. Center of the old section."

"Yeah. And she says, 'Wait here. Keep the engine running. I won't be long.'" Tommy picked up his coffee again, mostly to have something to hold. "And Q, I want to tell you I argued. I want to tell you I said, hey, this isn't how we do it, meter keeps running, whatever. But here's the thing." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, voice dropping like they were conspirators. "She tipped before she got out. Folded bills, didn't count them, just—handed them through the gap in the seat. Enough that waiting wasn't a question anymore. You know how it is. You know exactly how it is."

Quentin did know exactly how it was. That was the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone. Every driver in the fleet had a price of patience, and whoever this woman had known it to the dollar.

"How long?" he asked.

"Thirty-five, forty minutes. Long enough that I got out and smoked half a cigarette, which I'm not supposed to do in uniform, which is why I remember it so good—guilt sticks better than memory ever does." He smiled briefly, without humor. "I kept the engine running like she said. Radio low. And I'll be honest with you, Q, halfway through I started doing the math on whether I could leave. Because it's a cemetery, at night, alone, and there's this whole—vibe, you know? The harbor fog coming in off the water, the gates locked, and me sitting there like a getaway driver in a movie where the getaway driver always gets caught."

"What did you do?"

"What I always do. Nothing. I finished my cigarette and I stayed." He said it flatly, and Quentin heard underneath it the same thing he'd been hearing in his own head for days: the small shame of a professional man who had followed procedure straight past the point where procedure stopped making sense.

"And when she came back?"

Tommy was quiet for a moment. Outside, the nail salon's sign buzzed and flickered through the window, painting the room in intermittent pink.

"She had dirt on her hands," he said. "Not a lot. Smudges. Like she'd been—bracing herself against something. On the ground. And she was breathing funny. Not scared-funny. *Working*-funny. Like she'd been lifting something heavy, or digging, or—" He stopped. Shook his head. "No. She wasn't digging. I thought about it after, when it all hit the news, and she wasn't dirty enough for digging. More like she'd been *arranging* something. Careful-like. On her knees."

"Did you see what she carried?"

"No. Hands empty when she got in. Or—" He hesitated, and Quentin let the silence work, because it always worked; people filled silences with truth. "Or held against her body, under the coat. Something flat. Book-sized. Maybe bigger. I didn't see it clear and I'm not going to swear I saw it at all, because I didn't. But she kept her left side angled away from me the whole ride back, like she was protecting a rib."

"Where'd you drop her?"

"Corner of Marsh and Ninth. Nowhere. There's nothing at Marsh and Ninth, Q, it's a shuttered fish cannery and a bus stop nobody uses. She paid cash again—more folded bills—and walked toward the water end of Ninth and that was it. Didn't hurry. Walked like a woman going home from a late shift." Tommy finally looked at him directly. "That's the whole thing. That's everything. Why are you asking? Is she in trouble? Am I?"

"I don't know yet," Quentin said, and it was the truest sentence he'd spoken in a week. "One more question. Her voice. If you heard it again—in person, on a phone, anywhere—would you know it?"

Tommy considered this seriously, the way he considered everything, turning it over like a hull he was checking for leaks.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, I think I would. She had an accent. Not foreign. Local, but—old local. The way old-timers talk, the ones whose families been here since the canneries. Kind of clipped. Like every word costs money." He paused. "Why? Do you know who she is?"

Quentin stood, because if he stayed on that couch much longer his face was going to answer for him. "I've got a list," he said. "It's getting shorter."

* * *

He drove to Marsh and Ninth afterward, though there was no operational reason to. The cannery sat behind chain-link furred with salt rust, its windows boarded, its loading dock facing the gray slap of the harbor like a mouth open mid-sentence. The tide was out, exposing a wide apron of wet sand and old pilings, and Quentin stood at the fence line and tried to see it as she would have seen it at three in the morning: dark, empty, private, close to the water.

Close to the water. He turned that phrase over the way Edith Vane-Cole had turned the exhumation order over in her office, looking for the forgery in it. Everyone in this story kept ending up near water. The mausoleum sat on high ground, but the old Vane plots—the really old ones, the ones from before the family bought respectability in granite—were down in the flood plain, where the century's tides had taken their toll. He'd read that somewhere. In the historical society pamphlet, maybe. In the file he probably shouldn't have photocopied.

His phone buzzed. Dispatch group chat. Marisol Reyes, posting the shift handoff notes, precise as always, timestamps exact, route changes flagged in bold. *Route 19 culvert flooding again, advise cabs avoid after midnight.* A little winking emoji. A colleague covering her bases.

Quentin stared at the message longer than it deserved.

Then he drove back to the depot, pulled the audio archive for the night of the twenty-second, and queued up the call that had come in at 2:09 a.m.—the booking for 44 Larkspur Lane, the one logged under a name that didn't exist. He put on the headset, alone in the dispatch room with the hum of the radio and the blue standby glow of twelve idle screens, and pressed play.

A woman's voice. Clipped. Old-local, the way old-timers talked, every word costing money.

"Harborline Cab? One car, please. Corner of Delacroix and the cemetery gate."

A pause while the system—his system, his logs, his company—assigned the fare. Then, just before the line went dead, so quiet he almost missed it, almost wrote it off as line noise:

*"Tell them it's for the family plot."*

Quentin sat very still in the blue light, the headset humming against his ears, and understood with a cold, settling certainty that he had not stumbled onto this woman's trail at all.

She had booked the cab knowing exactly which dispatcher would take the call.
