## 6. Night Shift Confessions

The break room at Harborline Taxi smelled of burnt coffee and the ozone tang of the space heater that nobody had fixed since Quentin started there six years ago. It was 3:40 a.m., the dead hour, when the radio went quiet between the last bar crowd and the first airport runs, and the whole building seemed to hold its breath.

Quentin sat at the scarred Formica table with his hands wrapped around a paper cup, watching the steam rise and thinking about a name that didn't exist. *Delia Marsh.* He'd said it to himself so many times over the past week that it had begun to feel like a person—a woman made entirely of paperwork, born the moment someone typed her into the fare queue at 2:09 a.m. and killed again when she vanished from every other record in the city.

"You look like a man doing long division in his head."

Marisol Reyes came through the doorway carrying two cups instead of one, which was how she always did it—never for herself alone, never without offering. She set the second cup in front of him before he could protest, then dropped into the chair across the table, tucking one leg beneath her. She wore her dispatch headset pushed up into her dark hair like a crown she'd grown tired of wearing, and there was a smear of white-out on her left wrist that he would think about later, much later, in ways he couldn't yet imagine.

"Long division," he said. "That's one way to put it."

"Try multiplication." She blew across her coffee. "Everything in this town multiplies if you leave it overnight. Rumors. Overtime. My mother's opinions." Her smile was quick and crooked, the kind that made new drivers trust her within a single shift. "You've got the face you get when a number won't come out even."

Quentin turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. Outside the window, the parking lot lay under sodium light, Tommy Okafor's cab parked in its usual spot, dew already forming on the windshield. Tommy had gone home an hour ago, shaking his head, saying he didn't want to talk about the woman in the gray coat anymore, saying it like a man setting down something heavy and hoping nobody picked it back up.

"Marisol," he said. "Can I ask you something professional?"

"You can ask me anything. I reserve the right to lie charmingly."

He almost smiled. That was the thing about Marisol—she made it hard to remember what you'd been worrying about. Six years of night shifts together had built something he'd have called friendship if he were the kind of man who used words like that easily. She knew how he took his coffee. She covered his shifts when his mother had her treatments. Once, during the flood two winters back, they'd spent fourteen hours straight on the board together, rerouting half the fleet around standing water, and somewhere around hour nine she'd started narrating their situation like a nature documentary—"Here we observe the nocturnal dispatcher, defending his territory"—until he laughed so hard he had to mute his mic.

"The fare logs," he said. "The archive system. How far back do edits go?"

Her eyes came up over the rim of her cup. For just a moment—half a second, maybe less—the warmth in them went still, the way water goes still when something passes underneath. Then she blinked and it was gone, and she was Marisol again, amused, easy, leaning back in her chair.

"Edits? Since when do you edit fares? You're the guy who treats the log like scripture. I've seen you re-open a closed ticket at four in the morning because a driver wrote 'corner of Main' instead of 'Main and Pier.'"

"That's different. That's accuracy."

"That's obsession," she said fondly. "But okay. The archive keeps everything. Every entry, every change, timestamped, user-stamped. Why?"

"I'm not asking about changes. I'm asking whether changes can be *hidden*. Whether an entry can be altered without the alteration showing."

She considered him across the table, and he watched her actually think—not deflect, not joke, but turn the question over the way she turned everything over, thoroughly, from all sides. It was one of the things he respected about her. She never gave a lazy answer.

"The interface doesn't have a delete function," she said slowly. "You can void a fare, but the void is logged. You can correct an address, but the correction sits next to the original forever. If you're asking whether somebody could make a fare disappear—" she shook her head, "—not through the front door. Why? What are you looking for?"

"A fare that isn't there."

The heater ticked. Somewhere down the hall, the vending machine hummed its endless hungry note. Marisol set her cup down and laced her fingers on the table, and her voice lost its playfulness by degrees, the way a sky loses light.

"This is about the coffin, isn't it."

It wasn't a question, and Quentin didn't treat it as one. The empty grave of Augustus Vane had been the only story in Harborline for three weeks now—on the radio, in the diner, in the mouths of old men at the marina who claimed their grandfathers had always said the founder wasn't really in that mausoleum. The centennial committee had gone from outrage to damage control to a silence that felt expensive. Detective Brandt had come to the dispatch office twice, and both times Quentin had felt the interview like a hand pressing on a bruise.

"Everyone's talking about the coffin," he said.

"Everyone's *gossiping* about the coffin. You're working it." She said it without accusation, almost with admiration. "I know that look. You had it during the Kowalski thing, when you figured out the missing girl hadn't run away because her fare history showed her going to the bus station twice—buying a ticket, losing her nerve, coming home. Nobody asked you to solve that. You solved it anyway."

"She was seventeen."

"So's the truth, sometimes. Young and scared and hiding in plain sight." She tilted her head. "So tell me. What fare isn't there?"

And here was the moment, though he didn't know it yet—here was the fork in the road, invisible, unlit. He could have told her everything. The 2:14 a.m. call. Delia Marsh, who did not exist. The cab parked on Cemetery Road with its hazards ticking in the fog while a woman in gray stood at the Vane mausoleum door. Tommy's story, told once and then sealed shut behind his embarrassment. He could have laid it all out on the Formica table between the coffee cups, because Marisol was his friend, because six years of night shifts meant something, because who else was there?

Instead he heard himself say: "Routine audit. Brandt wants our records clean in case the exhumation turns into something legal."

Something moved behind Marisol's eyes—relief, or disappointment, or calculation; he couldn't read it then, and afterward he would replay this exact moment hundreds of times trying to decide which it had been.

"Brandt," she said, and clicked her tongue. "That woman interviews like a dentist. 'This might pinch.' Everything pinches with her." She stretched, arms overhead, and when she came back down she was businesslike. "Okay. Audit. Then let me save you some trouble. There are three ways into the log system. The dispatcher terminal—that's us, that's me and you and Dev on days. The driver app—timestamps come from the phone, which means they come from whatever tower the phone likes best, which means they're suggestions, not facts. And the admin backdoor, which is Raj in IT, and Raj does not get out of bed for less than a subpoena."

"Could a driver fake a pickup location?"

"Sure. Could a driver fake a *fare*?" She shook her head. "A fare needs a call. A call needs a voice on the line or a booking in the app. Somebody has to ask to be taken somewhere." She looked at him steadily. "You already know that. So what you're really asking is: who asked?"

Quentin kept his face still. Six years of poker against tired cabbies had taught him that.

"Maybe nobody asked," he said. "Maybe the fare was created from inside."

The words were out before he'd decided to say them, hanging in the burnt-coffee air, and Marisol went very quiet. Not alarmed. Thoughtful. She reached over and took his cup as well as hers, stood, and refilled both from the pot that had been scorching since midnight, and when she sat back down she spoke softly, the way people speak in hospitals.

"If a fare was created from inside," she said, "then it was one of three people. Dev, who falls asleep at his desk by ten p.m. and hasn't learned a new feature since 2019. You. Or me." She smiled, and the smile had an edge he'd never seen on it before, thin and bright as a blade held flat to the light. "And since you're sitting here asking me, I'm guessing you've already ruled yourself out. Which leaves the two of us having a very interesting conversation, Quentin Zhou."

"It's not an accusation, Mari."

"No. It's worse. It's arithmetic." She drank. "So here's my answer, free of charge. If I wanted to hide a fare, I wouldn't delete it. Deleting leaves a hole, and holes are loud. I'd bury it. I'd wrap it inside a real shift, a real route, a hundred ordinary pickups, until it was one gray fish in a gray school. And I'd pick a night when the board was busy enough that no one would ever count the fish." She set the cup down. "Like, say, the night of the harbor festival. Or the night of the storm outage in October. Or—" her eyes found his, mild and terrible, "—the night a bunch of old men dug up a coffin on live local news and every cab in town was running errands for the ceremony."

The heater ticked. Quentin's mouth had gone dry.

"Why would you know how to do that?" he asked quietly.

"Because I trained half the night crew on the archive system, and because I pay attention, and because I've been doing this job eight years and you've been doing it six and neither of us believes the log is just a log." She leaned forward. "Your turn. What did the buried fare say? Where did it go?"

And God help him, he almost told her. The question was so direct, so fair, so exactly the question he'd been carrying around like a stone—he nearly answered it out of sheer exhaustion, out of the animal relief of finally being *asked*. But something in him, some dispatcher instinct older than reasoning, some sense of a route being calculated just outside the frame of the windshield, made him stand instead, making a show of checking his watch.

"Board's quiet," he said. "I should get back before it isn't."

Marisol looked up at him for a long moment. Then she smiled her crooked smile, warm as ever, and gathered the cups.

"Go on, then. Guard your fish." At the doorway she paused, not turning around, her voice casual as weather. "Oh—Quentin. One more thing, since we're auditing. Route Nineteen. The coastal road. It's marked decommissioned in the system, right? Has been for years. But I've seen it come up in the autocomplete when drivers type destinations near Gull Point. Little ghost in the machine." She glanced back over her shoulder, and her expression was perfectly pleasant, perfectly open, and perfectly unreadable. "Funny thing about ghosts. They only haunt the houses that built them."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the corridor toward the dispatch floor, and Quentin stood alone in the break room with two cups of cooling coffee and the sudden, shapeless conviction that he had just been warned—or invited—and could not tell which.

* * *

He didn't go straight back to the board. He stood at the break room window instead, watching the parking lot, watching the sodium light pool around Tommy's cab like something drowned. His reflection hung ghosted against the glass, and behind it, faintly, he could see the dispatch floor through the interior window—Marisol sliding back into her chair, settling her headset on, her pen moving across her shift report in that quick, precise, downward-hooked stroke she used for everything, signatures and notes and times alike, a handwriting like a bird's foot pressed into snow.

*They only haunt the houses that built them.*

He thought about Route 19. He thought about the way she'd answered his question about hidden edits—not with confusion, not with deflection, but with a complete, fluent method for committing the crime. *I'd bury it. I'd wrap it inside a real shift.* Nobody described a murder weapon that lovingly unless they'd held it.

Or unless they were simply good at their job. That was the trap of it, the elegant trap: everything Marisol had said tonight was also exactly what a sharp, experienced dispatcher would say. Every sentence had two readings, innocent and guilty, lying over each other like transparencies on a light table, and no amount of squinting would separate them.

He went back to his station. The board glowed green and amber, the town asleep under its little lights, each one a person wanting to be taken somewhere. At 4:12 a.m. a call came in from the all-night diner, a fare to the naval housing flats, and he dispatched it, and logged it, and watched the entry settle into the archive like a stone into a riverbed, permanent, timestamped, honest.

At 4:31, Marisol laughed at something a driver said on channel two, and the laugh came through his headset warm and familiar, and Quentin hated himself a little for how much he wanted it to still mean what it had always meant.

At 5:03, with the sky outside still black but thinking about gray, he opened the archive and ran a search he should have run a week ago: every fare, any date, flagged to or near the decommissioned segment of Route 19.

The search spun. And spun. And returned.

Forty-one results.

Not three. Not a handful of ghosts. Forty-one entries stretching back twenty-two months, scattered across shifts like seeds, most of them voided within minutes of creation, all of them created from a dispatcher terminal—

—and every single one of them, he saw as he scrolled with his heart climbing into his throat, created during a shift where the terminal ID belonged to the seat next to his.

Marisol's seat.

The screen's glow lit the dark office, and forty-one small green lines waited there in the silence, patient as graves, and Quentin understood that the conversation in the break room had not been a conversation at all.

It had been a countdown.
