## 1. The Lot from the Estate Sale

The rain had been at Wrenlow for three days without conviction, a fine grey drizzle that never quite committed to being weather, and Silas Montrose had spent all three of them in the back room of the parish archive with their sleeves pushed up and the smell of old paper settling into their skin like a second profession.

"Every case can be closed," Silas murmured to the room, which was an old habit and a private one, the kind of thing a person says to keep themselves honest. The room did not answer. The room was made of shelving salvaged from a defunct bakery and it had heard better arguments.

The estate lot had arrived on Tuesday in eleven banana boxes, donated by the solicitors handling the Pellmore estate — old Mrs. Pellmore having died in the spring at ninety-four, leaving behind a house so full of paper that the removal men had reportedly refused to carry half of it until someone signed a form absolving them of responsibility for what might be living in it. The parish archive got first pick, because Mrs. Pellmore had been a churchwarden for forty years and because Wrenlow, like most small parishes, had learned that if you didn't take people's rubbish, they burned it.

Silas sorted by lamplight. That was the method: gloves on, lamp angled low, everything logged. Books first, then papers, then the boxes of unclassifiable objects that every estate produced — a thimble, a tin of buttons, a photograph of a dog whose name would now be lost forever, which struck Silas as a small tragedy every single time.

It was going pleasantly enough. Mrs. Pellmore had owned a great many sermons in pamphlet form and a suspicious number of novels with the covers torn off, which Silas chose not to think about too closely. There were parish newsletters going back to 1971, a christening gown pressed flat between sheets of greaseproof paper, and a ledger of flower-rotas kept in a hand so precise it looked printed.

Then, at the bottom of box seven, wrapped in a tea towel embroidered with wrens, there was a diary.

Not a journal, exactly — Silas was particular about words, being a coroner and therefore professionally acquainted with the difference between what things were called and what they were. This was a proper diary: cheap, marbled boards gone soft at the corners, a brass clasp long since surrendered to rust. The sort sold at the stationer's in Ashford for two pounds fifty, the sort a girl buys because she likes the way the lock clicks.

Silas turned it over. On the inside cover, in blue biro, in handwriting that slanted forward like it was in a hurry:

*Beatrix Hale. If found, give it BACK.*

Silas sat very still for a moment. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above, a beam ticked as the damp worked at it.

"Hale," Silas said aloud, testing it, though of course the name needed no testing. Eleven years in this parish and you learned its wounds the way you learned its lanes. Beatrix Hale, seventeen years old, last seen on the evening of the 14th of September, 2013, walking the lane past the Ashcombe fields toward home. Never seen again. Missing persons file opened, then closed, then reopened briefly in 2016 when a walker reported finding a shoe near the quarry, then closed again when the shoe turned out to belong to a size eight man and the walker to a man who enjoyed attention.

A runaway, the file said. Runaways come home or they don't, and this one hadn't, and Wrenlow had done what Wrenlow always did with its uncomfortable things, which was fold them away gently and speak of them in lowered voices at the Christmas fair, if at all.

Silas put on fresh gloves. It wasn't strictly necessary — the diary was hardly evidence, not after eleven years in a dead woman's house — but some rituals mattered.

*If found, give it BACK.* Back where? Back to whom? A girl who writes *if found* has already imagined not coming back for it herself. Silas noted the thought and set it aside, the way one sets aside a scalpel between incisions.

The early entries were ordinary, and gloriously so. March 2013: *Mrs. T at school says I daydream but she can't prove it.* April: *Dad's new job is making him weird. He keeps saying money will be different soon. Money is never different.* May: *Edmund A. looked at me in church AGAIN. Gross. Except also not gross? Don't write that down. Oh well, wrote it down.*

Silas smiled despite themselves. There was something about a teenager's diary — the way it swung between the cosmic and the trivial, the boy and the homework, the terror of exams and the terror of being perceived. Beatrix Hale came off the page vividly: funny, sharp, impatient with her father, secretly fond of church for reasons she'd have denied under torture. She wanted to be a forensic pathologist, which made Silas laugh out loud in the empty archive, a short startled sound.

"Well," Silas said to her across the years, "you're giving me plenty of practice."

The dates marched on through summer. June, July, August. The handwriting loosened as the weeks warmed, the letters sprawling. She wrote about the river, about a friend named Cass who was "going through a phase of being annoying," about saving up for a train ticket to London because London was where things happened and Wrenlow was where things *didn't*, a word she underlined twice.

Then September.

The entry for the 12th was short. *Something's wrong with Dad's work. He won't say. He and Mr. A. were shouting in the yard and then stopped when I came out and smiled at me, which was worse.*

The 13th: *Blue door again today. I'm not imagining it. I'm NOT.*

And the 14th — the last dated entry of 2013, the last entry of the girl who existed in any official record — read only:

*I know what he did. If anything happens to me, look under—*

The sentence stopped. Not crossed out. Stopped, mid-thought, the pen lifting, the rest of the page blank.

Silas became aware that they had been holding their breath, and let it out slowly, fogging the lamplight. Outside, the drizzle continued its noncommittal business against the window. The archive clock said twenty past four, which meant nothing; time in this room ran on a different currency.

"Looked under," Silas repeated. "Under what?"

There was no answer, of course. But there was something else, and it took Silas another moment to understand why the page felt wrong, why the eye kept snagging on it the way a sleeve snags on a nail.

There were more pages.

Silas turned them with fingers that had suddenly become very careful. The next entry was dated the 21st of September, 2013 — a week after the disappearance. Then October. Then Christmas, marked with a small drawing of a holly sprig. Then 2014 arrived, and 2015, and the handwriting changed — grew smaller, more controlled, the hurried slant straightening into something deliberate — but the voice stayed. The jokes stayed. The same private shorthand, the same spelling of *definitely* with an extra f, the same running feud with Cass, the same Edmund A., who by 2015 had graduated from *gross* to something considerably more complicated.

The final entry was dated the 3rd of November, 2017.

Four years after Beatrix Hale vanished, someone had still been writing in her diary.

Silas set it down on the table, flat, both hands bracketing it, and stared at it for a long while. The sensible explanations presented themselves promptly, hat in hand, like ushers at a wedding. A hoax. A sister, a friend, keeping it alive out of grief. Mrs. Pellmore's own hand — perhaps the girl had left it behind somewhere, and someone had found it and written in it, for reasons sentimental or sinister or merely strange. Diaries got written in. That was what diaries were for.

But the sensible explanations didn't fit right, and Silas had spent twenty years learning to trust that itch at the back of the neck, the one that said *the collar doesn't match the shirt*. Because whoever had written those later entries knew things. Silas hadn't read far yet — had deliberately stopped, the way you stop at a door you hear voices behind — but even in the few lines skimmed, there had been a reference to *the shoe by the quarry*, and the shoe by the quarry had never been in any newspaper. It lived in a closed police file and in the memory of one excitable walker, and nowhere else on God's earth.

Unless it lived here, in box seven, in a dead woman's tea towel.

Silas stood, went to the window, and watched the rain fail to become weather over the churchyard wall. The headstones stood in their rows, patient as ledgers. Somewhere among them, presumably, was the answer to a question Silas hadn't asked yet, and the question was forming itself now, cold and clear, the way questions do when they've been waiting a long time:

*Where is Beatrix Hale?*

Not *what happened to her* — that was the question the file asked, the question the parish had folded away. Where. Present tense. Because a diary doesn't grieve for four years. A diary doesn't keep appointments with Christmas. Whatever had happened on the 14th of September, 2013, somebody had carried on afterward, writing in a missing girl's voice, in a missing girl's book, and had then — apparently — given it to old Mrs. Pellmore, or hidden it where Mrs. Pellmore would find it, or simply let it drift into the vast sediment of a hoarded house.

*If found, give it BACK.*

Back to whom? To a girl who couldn't be found? Or —

Silas returned to the table and sat down, and did the thing that Coroner Vane had taught them thirty years ago, in this same parish, in a room that smelled of pipe smoke instead of damp: start a record before your memory starts editing itself. A clean sheet. Date, time, source, item description. *One diary, marbled boards, inscribed B. Hale. Contents extend past date of subject's disappearance by approx. four years. Provenance: Pellmore estate lot, box 7 of 11.*

Vane would have had opinions about this. Vane, who could close a case with a signature and a sigh. Vane, whose own files Silas had inherited along with the office, the lamp, and the habit of talking to empty rooms. Vane, who had once told Silas, over a lunch of cheese sandwiches in the churchyard, that the secret to the work was knowing which cases wanted closing and which ones only wanted *looking* closed.

Silas had believed, for twenty years, in the first kind. Every case can be closed. It was practically a motto. It was stitched somewhere into the lining of the profession.

But the pen hovered over the log sheet, and for the first time in a long career built on the comfortable arithmetic of open-and-shut, Silas Montrose felt the arithmetic wobble. Because this case had been shut for eleven years, shut politely, shut with cassocks and casseroles and the gentle collective agreement of a parish that preferred its mysteries pre-solved. And here it was, open again, sitting on a bakery shelf under a forty-watt bulb, breathing.

*Look under—*

Look under what, Beatrix?

Silas finished the log entry, closed the diary, and did not put it back in box seven. Instead they wrapped it in acid-free tissue, tucked it into their satchel — technically irregular, definitely necessary — and switched off the lamp.

At the door, they paused. The churchyard lay dark beyond the window, the headstones invisible now, which somehow made them more present, a congregation waiting in the pews of the night.

"I'll find where," Silas told the dark, quietly. "Whoever you are. Whatever you are. I'll find where."

The dark, being a professional, declined to comment. But as Silas locked the archive door and stepped out into the persistent, undecided rain, they noticed — and told themselves they were imagining it, and knew perfectly well they weren't — that the drizzle had finally made up its mind, and was falling harder now, straight and cold, like something beginning in earnest.

Tomorrow, Silas decided, walking home with the satchel held close, tomorrow they would walk the churchyard at dawn and check, name by name, stone by stone, whether Beatrix Hale had ever been given so much as a marker. And tomorrow they would have a word with Rector Fenwick, who had been in post in 2013, and who had a face that had always reminded Silas of a locked drawer.

The diary rode against Silas's hip the whole way home, light as a bird and heavy as a verdict.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep, Silas got up at two in the morning and read the final entry properly, slowly, twice.

It was dated the 3rd of November, 2017, and it was five lines long, and it was written in Beatrix Hale's hand, in Beatrix Hale's voice, four years after the whole world agreed she had stopped existing.

*He thinks I've forgotten. He checks the blue door but he doesn't check me. I'm putting this where only a reader would look. If you're reading this, then I got it right, and he got it wrong, and you should tell the coroner. Tell Montrose. He closes everything. Make him close this one too.*

Silas read it a third time, and the lamp hummed, and the rain kept on, and somewhere in the dark of Wrenlow a door that should have been painted any other colour waited to be found.

"Right," Silas said softly, to the diary, to the girl, to the night. "Consider yourself opened."
