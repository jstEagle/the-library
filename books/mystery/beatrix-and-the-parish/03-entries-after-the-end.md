## 3. Entries After the End

Silas Montrose had a rule about reading other people's diaries, and the rule was this: don't, unless the person is dead, and even then, knock first.

They knocked. Metaphorically. They stood in the archive room of the parish records office with the diary flat on the green baize table, both hands resting on its cover, and said, quietly, to the room at large, "Beatrix Hale. I'm coming in."

The radiator ticked. The rain did what Wrenlow rain always did, which was to fall in a manner best described as apologetic. Silas opened the diary to the first page and began to read.

The early entries were exactly what a person expects from a nineteen-year-old girl in a parish where nothing happens: complaints about her job at the bakery, complaints about her mother, complaints about the weather — a through-line, Silas noted with sympathy, that ran unbroken from 2011 to the present day. Beatrix Hale had hated the fog. She'd hated the way it came off the marsh and swallowed the church tower by four in the afternoon. She'd hated that her whole life fit inside it.

*Everyone here is a comma,* she wrote in March of 2013. *Nobody finishes a sentence. You're born, you work at the bakery or the estate or the church, and then you're just a dash in the parish register.*

Silas smiled at that, then stopped smiling, because they knew how her sentence had ended. With a dash. March 2013 was four months before she vanished.

* * *

The methodical reader would have gone straight through, page by page, in order. Silas was a methodical reader — it was practically the whole of their professional personality, a fact their late predecessor, old Coroner Vane, had once described as "Montrose, you alphabetize your grief" — but even a methodical reader, three hours and two cups of cold tea in, starts to notice when a book stops behaving like a book.

The trouble began with the dates.

The diary was a small thing, cloth-bound, the kind sold in the stationery shop in Millbrook for three pounds fifty. It was an annual diary — one year to a volume, the days pre-printed, the owner meant to fill them and move on. Beatrix had filled hers faithfully through the spring of 2013. And then, on the 14th of June — four months before the disappearance — the entries stopped.

Except they didn't stop. They resumed.

Silas flipped back and forth between the pages, checking, rechecking, the way one checks a lock one has already locked. The entries resumed in a different ink — the early ones in blue ballpoint, the later ones in a black fountain pen — and in a subtly different hand. Not a different hand, exactly. The same hand, aged. The same forward slant, the same peculiar way of crossing a *t* with a single impatient stroke, but slower now, more deliberate, as if the writer had grown careful. Or frightened.

The first resumed entry was dated the 3rd of September, 2016.

Three years after Beatrix Hale walked out of Wrenlow and was never seen again.

* * *

Silas did what any sensible coroner would do at half past nine on a rainy Tuesday night: they made a third cup of tea, they got the case file out of the cabinet, and they laid the two documents side by side on the baize like a surgeon laying out instruments.

The case file was thin. That was the first dishonesty in it, and Silas had known it was thin for eleven years, back when they'd been the junior officer fetching files for Vane and the case had come across the desk like a bird with a broken wing. *Hale, Beatrix Anne, b. 1992. Last seen 14 October 2013, Church Lane, approx. 9 p.m. Presumed runaway.* Presumed runaway, stamped and signed, and the file had closed like a door.

Now Silas read the diary's later entries against the file, and the file began to lie.

The 3rd of September, 2016 entry read, in that older, carefuler hand:

*The blue door again today. I stood in the lane and looked at it for a long time and nobody saw me, which is the one mercy this parish grants. He thinks I've stopped watching. Let him think it.*

Silas read it four times. The words *blue door* sat on the page with the terrible calm of a thing that has been waiting a long time to be read.

There was no blue door in the case file. There was no blue door anywhere in the parish record, the missing-persons circular, the newspaper clipping — the *Wrenlow Advertiser* had run forty words under the headline LOCAL GIRL MISSING, which was, Silas had always felt, roughly the length of an obituary for a hamster — and no blue door in any statement taken in 2013. Silas had read every statement. There were eleven of them. One was from the rector, one from the postmistress, and nine were variations on *didn't see anything, terrible thing, lovely girl, quiet though.*

But here was the thing that turned Silas's tea cold in the cup: Silas *knew* about a blue door. Not from the file. Not from the statements. From the parish's other paper trail — the one nobody reads because it's boring, which is precisely why it tells the truth. The planning consents. In 2009, an application had been filed to paint a cellar door at a cottage on Marsh Lane — the old Toll House, derelict since the nineties — and the consent had been granted with a condition attached, because Wrenlow's planning committee would attach a condition to the Second Coming. The condition concerned the color. The applicant had wanted to paint the cellar door a particular blue, and the committee, in its wisdom, had required the shade be approved against the conservation guidelines, and the shade had been approved, and the whole affair had generated four pages of correspondence.

Four pages about a cellar door on a derelict cottage, filed and forgotten. And a vanished girl writing, three years after her own disappearance, about *the blue door again today.*

*He thinks I've stopped watching. Let him think it.*

Silas sat very still. The radiator ticked. Outside, the fog was coming up off the marsh the way Beatrix had hated, swallowing the tower.

There were, Silas reflected, two possible explanations, and one of them was that Silas Montrose had finally, after forty-odd years, gone quietly mad, which would at least be tidy. The other explanation was that someone had continued Beatrix Hale's diary after Beatrix Hale disappeared — had written in her hand, in her voice, with her private grievances and her private jokes — and had written about a door whose existence was recorded nowhere but four pages of planning consent.

And there was only one reason a person writes in a dead girl's diary.

* * *

Silas worked through the night, and if the night had a shape, it was the shape of a net being drawn slowly through water.

The later entries were sparse — a few lines a month, sometimes less, the diary kept the way one keeps a wound clean. But they were dense with things. Initials. Dates. A private shorthand that Beatrix had apparently used all along, because the early entries used it too, and Silas now went back and decoded it against the early entries' plain text, the way one decodes any cipher: find where the code and the plain agree, and work outward.

*E.A.* appeared eleven times. Edmund Ashcombe. The founding family's son. The last person seen with her, per statement number four, which had been taken by Vane himself and ran to three sentences. *E.A. was kind to me once. That was my mistake. Kindness from that house is a loan, and they collect.*

A date appeared beside one of the *E.A.* references: *14/10.* The day she vanished. And beside it, in the 2016 hand: *Four years. He lit a candle for me at St. Brannoc's. I watched him do it. The wax ran down his fingers and he didn't wipe it away. I think he liked it.*

Silas put the diary down and walked to the window and stood there for a while, looking at nothing, because there was a limit to what a person should read before bed, and Silas had blown past it around suppertime.

There were other things. A reference to *M.P.* — Miriam Pell, the Ashcombes' secretary, though Silas didn't know that yet; at this hour of the night, *M.P.* was just two letters with a shadow behind them. A reference to *the register,* capitalized, in a way that made Silas want to pull the parish baptismal register off the shelf at once and did. And once, chillingly, a reference to *V.,* with a date — *V. came to the cottage. He took the envelope. He didn't ask what was in it. They never do, if the envelope's heavy enough.*

V. Silas knew exactly one V. in the professional history of Wrenlow parish, and V. had been Silas's teacher, and V. had been dead six years, and Silas was not ready, at one in the morning, to consider what an envelope and a heavy weight and a dead man's initial were doing in a dead girl's diary.

They made a note instead. That was the Montrose Method, such as it was: when a thing is too big to think, write it down small. Silas had learned it from Vane, which, in light of the entry about the envelope, suddenly felt like learning to swim from a crocodile.

* * *

By three in the morning, Silas had assembled the later entries into a rough chronology, and the chronology told a story, and the story was this: whoever held the pen after September 2016 believed Beatrix Hale was alive. Not alive in the sentimental sense — alive in the operational sense. Watching. Counting. Marking anniversaries. The entries read like surveillance notes written from inside a ghost: *I saw her mother at the shop. She still buys the bread Beatrix liked. Nobody tells her.* *The blue door has been repainted. Fresh. Someone is keeping it.* *He goes to the churchyard on the 14th of every month. He thinks nobody counts.*

*He thinks nobody counts,* Silas thought. *I count. It's the entire job.*

And then the entry that Silas would return to, over the following days, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The last entry in the book, undated, in the careful hand, pressed so hard into the paper that the words showed through on the reverse like a scar:

*If someone is reading this, then I got it out. Check what I checked. The door, the register, the 14th. I wrote it where only a reader would look, because a reader is the one thing he can't buy.*

Silas read it aloud, once, in the empty archive room, because some sentences need to hear themselves.

*A reader is the one thing he can't buy.*

And Silas — who was tired, and whose tea was cold, and who had spent eleven years believing that Wrenlow's great sin was forgetfulness rather than anything with teeth — understood two things with perfect, dreadful clarity.

The first was that the diary was real. Not the later hand — the later hand was a mystery, and possibly a crime, and Silas would be pulling that thread until it unraveled or until it pulled back. But the *voice* was real. The complaints about the fog, the joke about commas, the grief for her mother — no forger alive could have invented a girl that particular. The early entries were Beatrix Hale, whole and alive and irritated by the weather. And the later entries were someone who knew her well enough to wear her — or someone who *was* her, which raised questions Silas's training had not equipped them to ask.

The second thing was worse. Because the diary matched the file — matched it in the wrong places. The 14th of October, the time of the sighting on Church Lane, the fact that her mother still bought the bread: all of it was true, and almost none of it was *in* the file, because the file had been built thin on purpose. Silas had spent a decade trusting that file the way one trusts a load-bearing wall. Now they stood in the archive room holding a paper wall in their hands and could feel, for the first time, the hollowness behind it.

The case file wasn't incomplete. It was *edited.* And the editing had been done by someone with access, and patience, and eleven years of quiet.

Silas closed the diary. Knocked, metaphorically, on the cover — *coming out now, Beatrix, mind the step* — and sat in the dark of the archive room for a long moment, listening to the radiator tick, feeling the particular loneliness of the person who has just learned that the map is a forgery and is standing, at three in the morning, somewhere in the forgery's blank places.

Then they did the only reasonable thing. They got out a fresh sheet of paper, wrote at the top of it, in their own careful hand, *Questions the file cannot answer,* and beneath it wrote:

1. Who wrote the entries after the end?
2. Where is the blue door, and who has been keeping it?
3. What did V. carry out of a cottage, in an envelope, and what was he paid?
4. Who edited the file?
5. Why does a dead girl count the 14th of every month?

Five questions. Silas looked at them and felt the old professional calm settle over the dread like a lid on a pot. Every case could be closed. That was the belief. That had always been the belief. You followed the questions down until they stopped being questions.

Silas went home as the fog was lifting, slept four hours, and woke at dawn with question two already pulling at them like a tide. There was a cottage on Marsh Lane. There was a cellar door painted a particular approved shade of blue. And there was one way to find out what a door had been keeping for eleven years, and it involved wellington boots, a torch, and a conversation with the rector that Silas was very much not looking forward to.

They put on the wellington boots first. Priorities.

But at the office door, they stopped. On the mat, in an envelope with no stamp and no name — the first post of the morning, delivered by hand in the night, while Silas slept four hours in a house the fog could find whenever it liked — was a single sheet of paper.

Silas picked it up. Read it. Read it again.

It was written in black fountain pen, in a slow, careful, forward-slanting hand.

And it quoted the diary — the last entry, the one pressed through the paper like a scar — word for word.
