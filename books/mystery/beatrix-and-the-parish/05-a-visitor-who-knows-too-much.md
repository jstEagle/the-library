## 5. A Visitor Who Knows Too Much

The note arrived on a Tuesday, which Silas would later consider fitting, because Tuesdays were the day Wrenlow's postman, Dennis Frobisher, delivered everything that nobody wanted — tax assessments, dental reminders, and now, apparently, threats.

It came in a plain white envelope, hand-addressed in a small, precise script that slanted slightly to the right, like handwriting leaning away from something. No stamp. No return address. Which meant it hadn't come through the post at all; it had been carried up the lane and pushed through the slot of Silas's office door by someone with legs and nerve.

Silas found it at half past seven in the morning, having arrived early to finish cataloguing the estate lot before the parish secretary, Mrs. Tilling, came in and rearranged everything according to a system she described as "intuitive" and which Silas described, privately, as "weather."

The envelope was heavy paper. Good stock. The kind sold at the stationer's in Millbrook, twelve miles off, because Wrenlow had no stationer's of its own — a fact the village considered charming and Silas considered inconvenient.

They stood for a moment in the grey light of the office, coat still on, kettle not yet on, holding the envelope the way one holds a wasp that has landed politely on one's sleeve.

"Well," Silas said aloud, to the empty room, because talking to oneself was one of the few perks of being coroner in a parish of two hundred souls. "Let's see what you are."

* * *

Inside was a single sheet, folded once. The same small, precise hand. And across the top of the page, in letters so neat they might have been typeset, were words Silas knew intimately:

*He watches the blue door on Thursdays. He thinks I don't know. He counts his money on Sundays after the second hymn, when everyone's head is bowed.*

Silas sat down slowly.

Those lines were from the diary. Page forty-one, if memory served — and memory served perfectly, because Silas had read page forty-one four times, had copied it out longhand into their own notebook, had lain awake on Thursday night staring at the ceiling while the phrase *counts his money on Sundays after the second hymn* turned over and over like a coin spinning on a table.

But here was the thing. Here was the thing that made the tea go cold in Silas's cup before it was ever poured.

Page forty-one had never left the office safe.

Silas had shown Mrs. Tilling nothing. Had shown the rector nothing beyond the existence of the diary itself, and even that reluctantly, extracted from him over sherry like a tooth. The pages quoted in the note — the blue door, the Thursdays, the hymn — were among the entries Silas had flagged as matching details never made public: facts about Beatrix Hale's disappearance that existed nowhere but in that diary and, presumably, in the mind of whoever had written them.

And now they existed in a third place. On a stranger's desk. In an envelope without a stamp.

Beneath the quotation, the writer had added three lines of their own:

*You have something that does not belong to you. Return it to the archive, uncatalogued, and forget you opened it. Wrenlow remembers what it chooses to remember. Be wise enough to choose nothing.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. A signature is an invitation to reply, and this was emphatically not an invitation to anything except obedience.

Silas read it twice more, then did what any sensible person would do: put on the kettle, made tea, and drank it standing at the window watching the fog peel itself off the churchyard wall, thinking very hard and feeling very many things at once.

* * *

The first feeling, Silas admitted, was violation. There was no dressing that one up. Someone had been inside the office — or inside the safe, which was worse, because the safe required knowing the combination, and only three people alive knew it: Silas, Mrs. Tilling, and old Coroner Vane, who was eleven years dead and whose knowledge of combinations could reasonably be assumed to have expired with him.

Unless, said a small cold voice in the back of Silas's skull, Vane had told someone. Unless Vane, who had buried his mistakes rather than report them, had also passed along his keys.

Silas checked the safe anyway. Nothing disturbed. The diary lay where it always lay, wrapped in acid-free tissue, patient as a sleeping cat. The office showed no signs of entry — no scuffed sill, no displaced dust on the window ledge, no complaint from Mrs. Tilling about anyone having moved her stapler, which she treated as a tripwire.

Which meant either the visitor had been extraordinarily careful, or —

Or the writer had never needed to enter the office at all. Because the writer already knew what was in the diary. Knew it the way one knows one's own handwriting.

Silas set down the cup. That was the second feeling arriving now, elbowing past the first: not fear exactly, but its close cousin, the thrill. The specific electric thrill of the hunter who realizes, mid-stalk, that the tracks in front of them are fresh — and that some of them are pointing backward.

Someone wanted the diary gone. Someone knew precisely what was in it. And someone, most interestingly of all, had chosen to warn rather than simply take. If the visitor could reach the safe, they could have reached the diary. They could have burned it in the grate and been done, and Silas would have spent the rest of their career believing it lost in the estate lot, one more casualty of other people's tidiness.

Instead: a note. Polite. Almost courteous. *Be wise enough to choose nothing.*

"You're telling me," Silas said to the fog, "that this can be negotiated."

And that meant it couldn't be ignored. Threats come in two varieties: those meant to frighten, and those meant to be obeyed. This one was meant to be obeyed, which meant the writer believed Silas was the sort of person who could be reasoned with — or bought, or cowed, or managed. It meant the writer had thought about Silas specifically, had weighed them, had decided they were a variable that could be adjusted.

Nobody enjoys being a variable. But a coroner learns to take a certain professional comfort in being underestimated. It is, after all, the natural condition of the trade. People talk freely around coroners the way they talk freely around furniture.

"Right," Silas said, and got out the evidence bags.

* * *

By nine, the note was photographed, bagged, and logged in a new file that Silas labeled, after some deliberation, HALE / ASSOCIATED CORRESPONDENCE — a title bland enough to bore a burglar and precise enough to satisfy posterity. By half nine, Silas had walked the length of the lane outside the office looking for footprints in the soft ground by the gate, and found the ground raked. Actually raked — fine gravel dragged smooth with what looked suspiciously like the tine-marks of a garden cultivator, the kind sold at the garden centre in Millbrook, the kind kept in sheds all over Wrenlow.

Somebody tidy. Somebody thorough. Somebody who swept cellars and raked lanes and folded their threats in good paper.

"Somebody," Silas murmured, "who irons their bedsheets."

Mrs. Tilling arrived at ten, bringing with her the smell of toast and her usual air of a woman prepared to be disappointed by the morning. She was sixty-eight, built like a well-stocked pantry, and had run the parish office through four rectors and one minor scandal involving the flower rota, which she still referred to as "the unpleasantness."

"Post?" she asked, eyeing the empty envelope on Silas's desk.

"No stamp," Silas said. "Hand-delivered."

"Hmph." She peered at it. "That's Dennis's lazy cousin's doing, I expect. Or one of the Hollis children playing at postmen." She sniffed. "Anything interesting?"

"A survey," Silas said smoothly, "about churchyard maintenance."

"Then bin it." She deposited a stack of invoices on the corner of the desk with the tenderness of a woman laying down bricks. "Oh — Mrs. Ashcombe's lot. The archive donation. Her man rang yesterday asking whether we'd finished cataloguing. I said we were proceeding carefully, as is proper."

Silas's pen stopped moving.

"Her man," Silas repeated. "The Ashcombes' man rang here?"

"Yesterday afternoon. Very polite fellow. Wanted to know when the estate effects would be formally received into the archive." Mrs. Tilling shrugged her enormous shrug. "I told him these things take time. He said, 'Indeed. Some things take longer than others.' Then he hung up. Estate people," she added, with the contempt of a lifelong parishioner for anyone paid to be pleasant. "All velvet voices and no substance."

"When exactly yesterday?"

"Half three? Quarter to four? I was watering the geraniums."

At quarter to four the previous afternoon, Silas had been at the churchyard with the rector, walking the rows, confirming for the third time that no stone bore the name Hale. Which meant that while Silas had been out counting graves, someone had been in — or nearly in — the office, asking questions about the very lot that contained the diary.

Coincidence, said the polite voice of routine. Of course it's coincidence. Estates get settled, archivists get curious, telephones get used.

And yet.

"Mrs. Tilling," Silas said lightly, "when you left last night — did you lock the safe?"

She gave Silas a look normally reserved for parishioners who asked whether the church had a roof. "I lock the safe every night. I've locked that safe since Coroner Vane's day. Why?"

"No reason. Habit of the trade. We like our locks locked and our tea hot."

"And your mysteries thick," she said, gathering her cardigan around her. "You've been odd since that book lot came in. Odder than usual, I mean, and I say that fondly." At the door she paused. "Whoever delivered your survey walked up the lane and back, then. Dennis would have said if he'd seen them, and he says everything, so he saw nothing, so they came early or late or quiet." She nodded once, satisfied with her own logic, and went to terrorize the photocopier.

Silas looked down at the evidence bag. Through the plastic, the neat rightward-slanting script seemed almost apologetic. *Return it to the archive, uncatalogued.*

Uncatalogued. As it had been for eleven years. Whoever wrote this note knew how the diary had lived before Silas found it — known, or guessed, that it had sat in some drawer, some box, some limbo of things nobody wished to name. They weren't just asking for silence. They were asking for restoration. Put it back where it was. Unread. Unspoken. Unbelonged.

Wrenlow remembers what it chooses to remember.

"That," Silas told the note quietly, "is the single worst sentence anyone has ever sent me, and I once received a Christmas card from Coroner Vane."

* * *

The afternoon was spent doing what Silas thought of as the unglamorous arithmetic of suspicion. Not deduction — television had ruined deduction for everyone — but arithmetic. Who knew the diary existed? The rector. Mrs. Tilling, vaguely. The estate solicitor who'd arranged the donation. Whoever had packed the lot. And whoever had written the diary's later entries, assuming those entries had a second author, which the note's verbatim quotation now made very difficult to doubt.

Against that list, set the contents of the note: the blue door. The Thursdays. The hymn and the money. Details never public. Details, in fact, that Silas had verified against nothing yet at all — they might be fantasy, might be code, might be the ravings of a frightened girl. But the note's writer treated them as fact worth killing to protect, which was, in its grim way, a kind of confirmation.

So: the writer knew the diary's secrets because they had written them, or read them in another hand, or — the thought arrived cold and complete — lived them.

Silas took out the diary itself and laid it open under the desk lamp, and for the hundredth time studied the shift in the writing. The early entries were round and hurried, a girl's hand, ink blotted where she'd pressed too hard. The later entries — the ones dated years past her disappearance — were smaller. Controlled. Precise. Slanting slightly to the right.

Silas held the anonymous note up beside the page.

The slant matched. The pressure matched. The peculiar way the letter *k* kicked its leg out like a startled hen — matched.

For a long moment Silas didn't move. The fog pressed against the window. Somewhere down the lane a dog announced a bicycle with the full gravity of a town crier.

The same hand. The note and the diary's false entries, written by the same person — the person who had spent years putting words in a dead girl's mouth, and who had now, in a fit of anxiety or arrogance, put those same words onto Silas's desk.

"They've given me a sample," Silas said softly. "They've handed me their handwriting, signed with her name."

It was either a mistake — panic making people careless — or it was confidence. Confidence of the sort that rakes gravel and sweeps cellars and buys good paper. Confidence that said: *I am showing you my hand because there is nothing you can do with it.*

Silas put both documents away, locked the safe, spun the dial, and stood a moment with their palm flat against the cold steel, thinking about old Vane, who had taught them that records were the parish's conscience and then demonstrated, in his final years, that consciences could be edited.

Not this time. Not this record-keeper.

Outside, the fog was thickening toward evening, folding the churchyard into itself stone by stone. Silas switched off the lamp, and in the sudden dark the last line of the note seemed to hang in the air, patient as the fog itself:

*Be wise enough to choose nothing.*

"Wise," Silas murmured, pulling on their coat, "has never actually been my strong suit."

Tomorrow, Silas decided, locking the door behind them, it was time to pay a call on the family whose fortune had grown in the exact season a girl stopped being seen. Time to look the founding family of Wrenlow in their endowed, upholstered faces and ask, pleasantly, about land transfers and estate sales and why their man had been ringing the parish office asking after a box of books.

If the Keeper wanted to know what Silas chose, let them watch.

Silas intended to give them quite a show.
