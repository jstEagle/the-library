## 7. Blue Door

The phrase appeared eleven times in the diary, and Silas had counted every one of them the way a man counts the teeth of a key.

*He took me past the blue door again.* — March 2014.

*I left something at the blue door. He won't look there. Nobody looks at the blue door.* — June 2015.

*If you are reading this, go to the blue door before he does.*

That last one was written in a hand that shook so badly the letters leaned against each other like drunks holding one another upright. Silas had read it four times that morning, coffee going cold beside the case file, and each time the sentence rearranged itself in their mind until it sounded less like an instruction and more like a hand reaching out of the page.

The trouble was that Wrenlow contained, by Silas's rough estimate, forty-one doors that could plausibly be called blue. The parish had a long affection for the color — the shutters of the bakery were blue, the vicarage gate was blue, half the boats on the mill pond wore paint that had once been blue and had since retired into a sort of tired teal. Silas spent two days with an Ordnance Survey map, a property register borrowed from the magistrate's office (the magistrate himself being unfailingly courteous in the way of men who have nothing to fear, which Silas had begun to find more unsettling than hostility), and a list of structures abandoned or unoccupied since before 2013.

That narrowed it to six. Then three. Then one.

The cottage sat at the end of a lane that the map called Hollow Lane and that everyone in Wrenlow called, when they called it anything at all, "the back way to nowhere." It had been a gamekeeper's cottage once, then a widow's, then nobody's. The roof had partially surrendered; moss had annexed the north wall; the garden was a riot of elderberry and nettle doing what nature does when no one is watching, which is everything.

Silas stood at the gate for a long moment, hands in coat pockets, breath fogging.

"Right," they said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud kept the fear at a manageable size. "Let's see what careful hands have been up to."

* * *

*12th of April, 2014*

*He thinks I don't notice things. That is his whole mistake, I think. Everyone in this parish has decided I am the sort of girl who doesn't notice things — too dreamy, always with her nose in a book, always humming. Edmund used to say it like it was a compliment. "You're not really here, Beatrix, are you?" And I'd smile because smiling is free and costs nothing later.*

*But I notice. I noticed that the ledger in the church office has a page torn out, and I know which month it was, because I helped Mrs. Pell file the correspondence that spring and the letters jump from the 9th to the 14th as if five days never happened. Five days. The same five days the land papers were signed. I am not clever the way people mean clever, but I can count.*

*Today he walked me out past the blue door again. He likes to walk there. He talks about his mother and the estate and how everything will be his someday, and he holds my arm a little too tight when we pass the door, like he's afraid I'll open it. Why would I open it? It's just a cellar under a dead woman's house.*

*Except now I think about the door at night. Isn't that funny. All my life I ignored it and now I can't stop seeing it behind my eyes, blue as a bruise.*

Silas read that entry standing in the nettles, having made the mistake of opening the diary outdoors where the light was honest. The word *bruise* sat in the stomach like a swallowed stone.

They circled the cottage slowly. The front door hung open on a hinge that had given up sometime during the previous decade; inside, the rooms were bare in the way rooms get bare — floorboards gone grey, wallpaper hanging in tongues, a bird's nest in the fireplace built from wire and wool. Nothing here. This was the part of the work Silas genuinely loved, if love was the right word for it: the slow reading of a building, the way a house confesses through its wear patterns. Which stairs got used. Which window stuck. Where the damp came in and where someone had tried to stop it.

Someone had tried to stop it here. Recently.

Under the sink in the scullery, a rag. Not rotted. Damp-wrung and folded, the way a particular kind of person folds things — corners aligned, edges squared. Silas knew that folding. Old Coroner Vane had folded his handkerchiefs exactly that way, and had once told a teenage Silas, with tremendous seriousness, that a man's discipline showed first in his linen and last in his ledgers. Silas had laughed then. They did not laugh now.

The floor of the scullery had been swept. Not cleaned — swept. A broom had been dragged across flagstones that no one had any earthly reason to sweep, and the drag marks were crisp-edged, undusted-over. Days old. Perhaps a week.

"Hello," Silas said softly, to the cottage. "Who's been tidying you?"

And then, because the diary had trained them to it, they looked down.

* * *

*3rd of September, 2014*

*I have started leaving things at the blue door. Small things. Things only I would know to leave, so that if anyone ever finds them, they'll know it was me and not some tramp. A button from my green cardigan. A hair ribbon. Today I left my left shoe from the pair I wore the day I ran — I kept the right one hidden in the church, in the place under the pew where the wood is loose. If someone finds one shoe here and one shoe there, they'll know I passed between the two places alive. That's the message. That's the whole message: I WAS ALIVE AND I PASSED HERE.*

*Miriam saw me near the lane today. She looked at me for a long time and didn't smile, and I thought: she knows. And then I thought: good. Let her know. Let somebody in this parish carry it besides me.*

Silas found the shoe at half past four in the afternoon, in the cellar, behind the blue door.

Getting to the cellar required a torch, a crowbar borrowed from the boot of the car, and a private negotiation with the self about whether this was a job for the police. It was, of course. Everything from here forward was a job for the police. But Silas had learned — was learning, day by day, in a parish that moved like syrup around anything uncomfortable — that calling in the constabulary meant waiting, and waiting meant telephone calls upward, and telephone calls upward meant the Ashcombe name arriving somewhere in the conversation like weather. So Silas pried the door themselves and told the voice of caution, which sounded suspiciously like Vane, to wait outside.

The door was blue. Not faded-accidentally blue. Painted. The paint was newer than the door, newer than the cottage, laid on thick and even over a frame that had been scraped and primed first. Someone had restored this door within the last few years. Someone had wanted it blue.

Inside, stone steps went down into cold air that smelled of earth and lime and, faintly, of disinfectant. That last smell stopped Silas on the third step with one hand flat against the wall. Disinfectant. In a derelict cellar. In a cottage that had been empty since before Beatrix Hale vanished.

The torch beam found the walls first.

Scratch marks. Low on the stone, at the height of a kneeling person, or a person sitting with their back to the wall, or — Silas made themselves stand still and breathe — a child-sized person reaching up. Some of the marks were words worn nearly smooth: *help*, or the ghost of *help*, and a string of tally strokes, dozens of them, grouped in fives the way prisoners count. Some were older, softened by lime bloom. One group was not soft at all. It was sharp-cut and recent-looking, and it said:

*B.H. — TELL SILAS M.*

Silas read it twice. Their own name, cut into a cellar wall in a cottage they had never visited, in handwriting they had spent three weeks living inside of. The torch trembled slightly, and Silas let it, because there was no one down there to see, and because pretending otherwise would have been its own kind of lie.

Then the beam swung, and found the shoe.

It stood neatly against the far wall. Upright. Placed, not dropped — placed with its toe pointing toward the steps, toward the door, toward the world above, like a small signpost. A child's shoe, leather, the buckle style that had gone out of fashion years before 2013 but that a girl who shopped at jumble sales and charity lots might well have worn. Inside the heel, in ink gone brown with age, someone had written a name in a schoolgirl's careful capitals:

*B. HALE.*

Silas crouched beside it for a long time without touching it. The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above, water dripped off the broken roof with the patience of a metronome, counting out seconds that eleven years had already spent.

"There you are," Silas whispered. It came out wrong — too tender, too much like greeting an old friend — and they cleared their throat and tried again, professionally. "There you are."

Neither version was adequate. Some things aren't.

* * *

*19th of November, 2015*

*I saw him bury something tonight. I shouldn't have been out there. I know I shouldn't. But I couldn't sleep in that room anymore — the walls know too much, I've told them too much — and I walked, and walking always takes me where it shouldn't.*

*It was behind the blue door cottage, in the strip of ground where the elder grows. He had a spade and a lantern shuttered down to almost nothing, and he worked fast, like a man who has done it before. I stood among the trees with my heart going like a bird in a box and I thought: that's it. That's the thing I've been missing. That's why he keeps coming back here. Not for me. For whatever's in the ground.*

*He straightened up at the end and he looked around — the way you do, the way I do when I've left something at the door — and I swear on my mother's grave he smiled. Standing alone in the dark over a hole in the earth, and he smiled like a man looking at a finished piece of work.*

*I'm writing this fast because I don't know how many nights I have left where I feel safe enough to write at all. Here is what I know, all of it, in order: the torn ledger page. The land transfer signed the week after I "ran away," for a price no sane buyer pays. The five days that don't exist. The blue door. The shoe. The smiling man with the spade.*

*If I disappear properly — if they never find me — then whoever reads this, go to the ground behind the blue door. Dig where the elder grows thickest. And say my name out loud when you do it. Names are how the dead stay real. He understands that better than anyone. It's why he's winning.*

Silas climbed the cellar steps on legs that felt borrowed, stood in the grey ruin of the scullery, and wrote in their notebook with a hand that only mostly obeyed: *Shoe — B.H. — cellar. Scratches incl. recent, w/ my name. Floor swept, rag folded, disinfectant residue. Site active. Site MAINTAINED.*

Maintained. That was the word that would not leave. Ghosts did not sweep floors. Grief did not repaint doors. Whatever had happened in this cottage had happened long ago, but someone tended it — visited it, cleaned it, kept the blue door blue — the way a gardener keeps a bed weeded, or the way a man keeps a grave.

Or keeps a larder.

Silas put the thought down immediately, the way you set down something hot, but it stayed burned into the palm all the same.

Outside, the light was failing. The elder thicket behind the cottage stood black against the sky, dense and tangled, exactly where a girl's diary said it would be thickest. Silas walked to the edge of it and stopped, hands in pockets, and did not take another step, because the next step was a shovel's step, and a shovel required witnesses, and warrants, and a magistrate's signature — and the magistrate was an Ashcombe cousin, and the diary had already taught Silas what this parish did with inconvenient pages.

From the lane behind them came a sound. Footsteps? No — lighter than that. The click of a gate. Silas turned.

At the mouth of Hollow Lane, perhaps fifty yards off, a figure stood motionless beside a parked car Silas did not recognize. Too far to make out a face in the dusk. Close enough to make out that the figure was not leaving, and had clearly been there long enough to watch Silas go in, and had done nothing to hide it.

The figure raised one hand — not a wave. Something smaller. An acknowledgment. *I see you seeing me.*

Then it got back in the car, unhurried, and drove away toward Wrenlow, taillights shrinking down the darkening lane like two red eyes closing.

Silas stood a moment longer in the cold, the notebook heavy in their pocket, the diary heavier in the other.

"Well," they said to the empty lane, and their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Somebody's been reading over my shoulder."

They got in their own car and did not start the engine right away. Because there was one more thing — small, easily missed, the kind of detail Vane would have called *linen*. On the passenger seat, where no such object had been that morning, lay a single pressed violet, fresh, its stem trimmed square and clean.

Folded-neat. Corners aligned.

The kind of flower, Silas thought, pulling out of Hollow Lane with the headlights off until the lane bent out of sight of the cottage, that a certain kind of person leaves at a door.
