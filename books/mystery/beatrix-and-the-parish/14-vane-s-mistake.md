## 14. Vane's Mistake

The sealed files lived in the basement of the parish records office, which was less an office than a stone throat the building had swallowed sometime in the last century. To reach it you went down two flights of stairs that smelled of cold iron and old paper, past a door with a padlock that had rusted into a kind of decorative suggestion, and through a corridor where the lightbulbs had been replaced so rarely they seemed to burn out of politeness rather than electricity.

Silas Montrose had a key to all of it. That was the thing about being coroner in a parish like Wrenlow: you inherited not just the title but the whole sedimentary history of everyone who'd held it before you. The job came with a ring of keys and a weight of other people's decisions, and nobody ever told you which was heavier until you'd carried both for a while.

The seal on Coroner Ambrose Vane's files was official — a wax stamp over the box flap, *SEALED BY ORDER OF THE MAGISTRACY*, dated March 2017, four days after Vane's death. Sealed files were supposed to require a magistrate's writ to open. But the wax had cracked years ago along one edge, and Silas had learned long ago that in Wrenlow, procedure was often just a lock on a door everyone already knew how to open.

Still. Silas stood there a moment, thumb resting on the cracked wax, and felt the particular gravity of what they were about to do.

"You're hesitating," Silas said aloud, to no one, because talking to oneself in basements was one of the occupational hazards and better to own it than fight it. "You've broken into a cottage cellar, interviewed a hostile rector twice, and eaten Mrs. Tallow's kidney pie without complaint. This is a cardboard box."

The box did not answer, which Silas found more dignified than most of Wrenlow's responses.

They broke the wax the rest of the way.

* * *

Inside: eleven files, ordered by year, 2009 through 2016. Vane had been coroner for twenty-two years before that, but these were the ones he'd chosen to seal, which meant these were the ones that mattered. Silas carried them up to the one window in the records office that admitted actual daylight, set them on the table, and began at the beginning.

Vane's handwriting was a revelation in itself. Silas had known the man only in their first year, when Silas was twenty-six and eager and Vane was seventy and tired, and everything Silas knew about record-keeping had come from watching those broad, careful hands rule lines across a ledger page. Vane wrote like a man laying bricks — each letter load-bearing, each entry built to stand a hundred years. His early files confirmed it. Dates cross-referenced. Witness statements initialed. Marginal notes in a small, dry hand that occasionally betrayed a sense of humor (*deceased's dog refused to leave the body; dog has been rehomed with the widow; the widow reports the dog is insufferable about it*).

Reading him again was like hearing a dead teacher's voice. Silas sat with that for a while longer than strictly necessary.

Then Silas reached the file dated September 2013, and the handwriting changed.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. A man who wrote like bricklaying didn't suddenly scrawl; he simply laid his bricks slightly crooked, slightly faster, slightly less certain of the ground beneath them. The September 2013 file documented the initial missing-person inquiry into Beatrix Hale, aged nineteen, last seen the evening of the 14th near the Ashcombe estate lane.

And the timeline was wrong.

Silas knew it was wrong the way you know a floorboard is rotten — not by looking, but by the give under your foot. Because Silas had spent three weeks now living inside Beatrix's diary, and the diary said she had gone to the blue door on the night of the 14th, and the parish ledger — the one Silas had photographed weeks ago — recorded Edmund Ashcombe returning from the coast on the morning of the *15th*. Every account of that final evening placed Beatrix walking toward the estate after dark. But Vane's file stated, in his careful bricklaying hand:

*Last confirmed sighting of subject: 11th September, Wrenlow high street, approx. 4 p.m.*

Three days early. Three days shaved off the front of her disappearance like a man trimming a photograph so the inconvenient person falls out of frame. And with the timeline moved back, the night of the 14th became unremarkable — no inquiry, no canvass of the estate lane, no questions asked of anyone who might have been abroad in the fog that evening.

In the margin, in that same careful hand, someone had written: *Runaway presumed. No further action.*

But it wasn't Vane's marginal note style. Silas had read hundreds of his margins by now. Vane annotated like a fussy schoolmaster — *verify*, *see file 88-C*, *ask the ferryman, he notices things*. This note sat flat and final, a door closed rather than a question opened. It was a different hand imitating his, or — more likely, Silas thought, turning the page toward the window light — it was Vane's hand writing words someone else had chosen for him.

At the bottom of the page, almost invisible unless you tilted the paper, was a faint indentation. Someone had written on the sheet above this one and pressed hard enough to ghost through. Silas fetched the desk lamp, angled it low across the paper, and read the shadow of a sentence that had been erased from the file above:

*Payment received in full. Matter closed.*

Silas sat back. The chair creaked. Somewhere upstairs, the records clerk dropped something and swore with impressive fluency for a man of sixty.

"Bought," Silas said quietly. "You were bought."

It shouldn't have hurt. Silas had known since the blue-door cottage that the rot ran deep, had watched Miriam Pell twist her hands around a confession she'd carried for eight years, had heard the rector say *I was told to log her as a runaway and I did* in a voice like a man describing his own funeral. But knowing institutions fail in the abstract is different from finding your teacher's fingerprints on the failure. Vane had taught Silas that every name deserved its line in the ledger. And then, in September 2013, he had taken money to strike a line through a nineteen-year-old girl.

Or — Silas turned the thought over carefully, the way you turn over a coin to check which face is up — or he had taken the money and hated himself for it, and spent the next four years trying to buy his way back out.

* * *

The proof was in the later files, and it took Silas until well past noon to find it, because Vane had been cleverer than he'd been honest.

December 2013: a file on the Hale case, reopened briefly at the insistence of a "concerned party" — unnamed, though the concerned party's concern had apparently evaporated within a fortnight. In it, Vane noted that the initial timeline required "correction" and then, remarkably, did not correct it. Instead he appended a single sheet of loose notes, unfiled, tucked behind the report proper where only someone reading every page would find them.

*Subject seen 14th by D. Ellery near the mill bridge. Ellery unreliable (drink) but consistent. Timeline as filed cannot be correct. Recommend reinquiry.*

Someone had written *NO* across the recommendation in red ink — heavy, impatient, a magistrate's pen if Silas had ever seen one. Below it, in pencil, in Vane's hand, smaller than any of his bricklaying letters:

*I know what I was paid to do. I am asking what I am permitted to do.*

Silas read that line three times. Then they got up, walked to the window, and looked out at the churchyard for a while, because some sentences need distance before they'll let you keep working.

*I know what I was paid to do. I am asking what I am permitted to do.* That was Vane entire — the dry humor, the meticulousness, and underneath both, the flaw. Not corruption as appetite. Corruption as surrender. A good man who had decided somewhere around age seventy that the system he served was bigger than his conscience, and who had made his peace with that arithmetic, and who had then discovered, too late, that the arithmetic didn't balance.

The remaining files told the rest in a kind of slow-motion collapse. Through 2014 and 2015, Vane's marginalia grew stranger — queries to himself, lists of dates that matched nothing in the files they accompanied, once a full page of the alphabet written out in careful capitals for no discernible reason except perhaps to prove to himself his hands still worked. In June 2016, a file noting that Vane had requested access to the sealed land-transfer records of the Ashcombe estate. Request denied. Requested again in August. Denied. In October 2016, a single-sheet memo, unsent, still folded in thirds:

*To the Magistrate: I intend to reopen the Hale matter in full at the January session. I am an old man and my mistakes are heavy and I would rather carry them publicly than privately. — A.V.*

He never sent it. Or he sent it and it changed nothing. Either way, the January session came and went, and the next document in the box was the death certificate.

Ambrose Vane, died 9th February 2017, aged seventy-four. Cause of death: cardiac event, witnessed by housekeeper. Found at his kitchen table. And here Silas paused, because the housekeeper's statement — brief, formulaic — contained one detail that snagged like a nail in wool: *he was at his papers when it happened, and the papers were gone when I came back from calling the doctor.*

Gone. An old man dies at his kitchen table mid-correspondence, and before the doctor arrives, the papers vanish. The housekeeper hadn't thought anything of it — people tidy, people panic, families collect things. But Silas had spent enough weeks now inside the machinery of Wrenlow's silence to recognize the sound of it operating. Somewhere between the kitchen and the graveside, someone had collected Ambrose Vane's last work the way you'd collect evidence against yourself.

Silas looked down at the box, at eleven years of a man's slow unraveling, and felt something that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite fear and occupied the unfortunate territory between.

Because here was the shape of it: Vane had taken the money in 2013, and regretted it by December, and fought it in his small cowardly ways for three years, and announced his intention to come clean, and was dead within four months with his papers scattered to the wind. And Silas Montrose, current coroner of Wrenlow, holder of the same keys, keeper of the same ledgers, was now sitting in a basement holding the exact thread Vane had died pulling — and had recently received an anonymous note quoting a diary page no one was supposed to have seen, warning them off in terms that had been polite the way a knife is polite.

"I see my possible ending," Silas told the empty room. "Thank you. Very illuminating. Do you do tarot as well?"

The room declined to comment.

* * *

There was one more thing. There is always one more thing; Silas had learned that from Vane too, though Vane had phrased it differently — *the file ends when the truth ends, lad, and truth never does*.

Beneath the death certificate, pressed flat against the bottom of the box, was an envelope. Unsealed. Unlabeled. Inside was a photograph, old-stock, slightly foxed at the corners: the churchyard at dusk, shot from the lych-gate. Ordinary enough — headstones, yew tree, the church's squat tower — except that in the foreground, half-turned toward the camera as though interrupted mid-stride, stood a figure Silas recognized instantly, because Silas had spent the last month staring at county council portraits trying to put faces to names.

Magistrate Harrow Ashcombe. Younger by fifteen years, thicker through the shoulders, standing in the churchyard at dusk with an expression the camera had caught at exactly the wrong moment — or exactly the right one. He was not grieving. He was not visiting. He was looking directly into the lens with the flat, patient attention of a man watching something he owns.

On the back, in Vane's hand, one word:

*Why?*

Not *who*. Not *where*. *Why* — the question of a man who had stopped asking whether and started asking motive, and had gotten far enough to photograph the answer and not far enough to understand it.

Silas turned the photograph over twice, as though the second reading might improve it. It didn't. What it did was confirm the architecture of the whole rotten house: Vane had suspected the magistrate. Had maybe even approached him — the unsent memo was addressed to the magistrate, after all, and perhaps it had been sent after all, and perhaps the reply had been a visit, and a conversation, and a kitchen table, and a cardiac event that arrived with suspicious punctuality.

Or perhaps Silas was constructing ghosts out of foxed photographs. That was the trouble with this case; every fact came pre-shadowed. You couldn't hold a single document from 2013 to 2017 without it seeming to implicate everyone, and exhaustion made paranoia feel like insight.

Silas pocketed the photograph anyway. Evidence was evidence, even when it whispered.

* * *

By late afternoon the light through the records-office window had gone amber, and Silas had filled six pages of their own notebook — the good one, the one kept at home, because after the office search two weeks ago Silas trusted the parish filing system approximately as far as it could be thrown, and the parish filing system was mostly lead-lined boxes.

The summary, when Silas wrote it out plainly, looked like this:

1. Vane falsified the initial timeline in September 2013, under payment, under pressure.
2. He regretted it immediately and fought it feebly for three years.
3. He threatened full reopening in writing.
4. He died with his final papers removed from the scene.
5. The magistrate's refusal to exhume now looked less like procedure and more like maintenance.

And underneath all of it, the item Silas wrote last and underlined twice:

6. He tried to do it alone. That is why it killed him.

That was the lesson, wasn't it. Not *don't pull the thread* — the thread was pulled, had been pulled since a donated book lot landed on Silas's sorting table, and there was no unpulling it. The lesson was that Vane had pulled alone, in the dark, with nothing but his conscience and his bricklaying hand, and the machine had simply waited for his heart to do its work.

Silas closed the notebook. Outside, the fog was coming in off the marsh the way it did most evenings, folding itself around the churchyard wall, softening every edge until the headstones looked like teeth in a closing mouth. Somewhere out there, whoever had searched Silas's office was going about their evening — supper, perhaps, a fire, the comfortable routines of a life built on a grave with no name.

Well. Two could keep comfortable routines.

Silas gathered the files, returned them to the box, and left the broken wax exactly as it lay. Let anyone who checked see that it had been opened. Let them wonder what had been found. Fear traveled faster than facts in Wrenlow, and Silas intended to start spending it.

At the top of the stairs, the records clerk looked up from his crossword. "Find what you were after?"

"No," Silas said pleasantly. "I found something much worse."

The clerk nodded, satisfied, and returned to seven-across.

Walking home through the fog, photograph in coat pocket, Silas rehearsed the next move the way Vane had taught them to rehearse everything: twice, slowly, out loud. The sealed files gave suspicion, not proof. Proof needed a place, and the place was the ground behind the blue-door cottage — the unmarked plot Miriam Pell had described with her hands shaking. The magistrate would never grant the exhumation. Fine. Then the exhumation would have to be arranged so that refusing it would cost him more than allowing it.

But that was tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight there was only the fog, and the photograph of a young magistrate watching the camera with patient eyes, and one word in a dead man's hand — *why?* — and the growing, unwelcome certainty that Silas already knew the answer, and that the answer was sitting in a warm house somewhere in this parish, waiting to hear whether the new coroner was going to be sensible.

The fog thickened. Silas walked faster.

Behind them, at the edge of hearing, a churchyard gate swung shut — though no wind stirred, and no footsteps followed, and the parish of Wrenlow settled into another night of keeping what it kept.
