## 2. What the Tide Returned

The sea gives back what it takes, but never in the condition it took it. That's the first thing you learn working a hotel on the coast — guests arrive salted and rumpled, luggage dented, marriages slightly waterlogged. The Meridian has stood above the tidal flats for ninety years, and in that time I've developed a professional respect for the ocean's habit of returning things late, damaged, and with an entirely new story attached.

The diary came back on a Tuesday.

I know because Tuesdays are when Detective Marsh does his paperwork at the corner table of the lobby bar, and because Ilse had taken to sitting two tables away from him every morning, drinking coffee she never finished, watching him the way gulls watch unattended chips. She'd been at the Meridian eleven days by then. Long enough for the staff to stop calling her "the guest in 309" and start calling her "the sister." Long enough for me to have run out of polite ways to tell her I had nothing.

Then Marsh got a phone call, closed his notebook, and said the words every waiting family both prays for and dreads: *They found something.*

* * *

I wasn't supposed to be there when they brought it in. Technically, I'm never supposed to be anywhere — a concierge's jurisdiction ends at the property line, and even then only in matters of dinner reservations and lost earrings. But Marsh had long ago stopped pretending he didn't use me. A hotel is a small city with one street, and I am its postmaster, its census taker, and its priest. If Beatrix Voss — no relation, though everyone enjoyed the coincidence — had been last seen near the Meridian, then the Meridian was where the answers would be filed.

So I stood in the doorway of the little conference room off the service corridor and watched them lay the diary out on the table like evidence from a much larger crime.

It was smaller than I expected. Leather, once green, now the color of old bruises. The tide had done its work — the cover swollen soft as bread, pages fused in places into a single gray brick. Someone had spent hours drying it, separating leaves with tweezers or patience, and the result lay open under a desk lamp looking less like a book than like something rescued from drowning. Which, I suppose, was exactly what it was.

"Found it wedged in the rocks past the point," Marsh said, not looking up. He knew I'd come; he'd left the door open six inches, which for Marsh was an engraved invitation. "Fisherman spotted it at low water."

"Low water," I said. "Where she went missing."

"Where she *probably* went missing. Nobody saw anything. The flats are a mile of nothing at low tide and a mile of nothing at high tide. Slippery rocks, a rising tide, a woman who liked to poke around tide pools alone at dusk." He shrugged with the weariness of a man who had explained this to a sister who refused to hear it. "Accidental drowning. It happens here twice a year. The sea doesn't need a motive, August."

"No," I agreed. "It just needs an opportunity."

He looked up at that. Marsh is not a stupid man; he is merely a tired one, and there is a difference that matters more the longer you do this work. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look you get before you cost me a weekend."

"I don't know what you mean. I came down to offer condolences to Miss Brandt and perhaps arrange for the return of her sister's effects."

"Her sister's effects are a wet notebook and a boot."

"One boot?"

"Both boots. One on each foot, if you must know, which apparently you must." He turned back to the table. "Case is closed, August. Drowning. Misadventure. I'm sorry for the sister, genuinely, but sorry doesn't reopen files."

I should have left it there. I want that on the record, wherever this record ends up: I should have left it there. Eleven days of a grieving woman's coffee going cold had already taught me that some cases are grief wearing a trench coat, and grief is not my business. Comfort is. Comfort I can provide. What I cannot provide is a dead woman returned to life, and no amount of concierge training equips you to hand someone back their hope intact.

But then Marsh, in a moment of either kindness or carelessness, slid the diary across the table toward me.

"Family gets it back after processing," he said. "You can tell her it's coming. Might help her let go."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about objects: they testify. Not in words — in condition, in placement, in the small dishonesties of their arrangement. A room tells you whether its occupant slept or paced. A suitcase tells you whether its owner packed in haste or in sorrow. And a diary, I would learn over the next sleepless night, tells you everything, including the things its writer believed were secrets.

Ilse received it the way people receive ashes. Both hands. No sound. She sat in the lobby armchair by the window — her window now, the staff had learned not to sit there — and held the ruined thing against her chest while the rain did its usual performance against the glass.

"Thank you," she said, and it was the first time in eleven days her voice hadn't sounded like a question.

"You'll want privacy," I said.

"Yes." Then, as I turned: "August."

"Miss Brandt."

"She wrote in it every day. Since we were girls. She said a day unwritten was a day unaccounted for." Her thumb moved along the swollen spine. "She'd hate this. Being unread."

I told her the pages might be legible in places, that the police photographer had documented everything, that time and care could sometimes raise the drowned dead of ink. All true. None of it was why I stayed standing there while she opened the cover with the delicacy of someone defusing something.

None of it was why, when she began to turn the fused gray pages with a letter opener borrowed from the front desk, I found myself reading upside down across her shoulder like the world's worst-mannered valet.

* * *

Beatrix Voss wrote a tidy hand. That was my first observation, and it mattered more than I yet understood. Researchers' hands usually are tidy — a person who labels specimens doesn't scrawl. Her entries were compact, ruled straight without lines, dates in the margin like a proper logbook. *Tide 14:40, wind SW, pool survey grid C.* A woman accounting for her days, just as Ilse had said.

The early pages were almost entirely science. Anemones. A dispute with a colleague about sampling methodology rendered with more venom than I'd have credited the word "quadrat" capable of carrying. Small human weather: a missed call home, an apology drafted and abandoned. I felt the peculiar shame of the eavesdropper and kept reading anyway. Curiosity, as I may have mentioned, is my occupational disease.

The police had photographed through the date of her disappearance — the fourteenth. The final entry they'd catalogued was dated the thirteenth, mundane as milk: a note about a storm coming, about wanting to check the pools before it hit. Case closed. Woman walks the flats ahead of a storm, tide comes in early, sea keeps its appointment. Even I had nodded along, and I nod along to very little.

But the diary in Ilse's hands contained more pages than the photographs had shown. Fused ones, stuck together, that the drying process had only partially separated — pages the technicians had dismissed as blank, or as damage.

They were not blank.

The page Ilse coaxed open that evening bore a date. I read it three times before my mind consented to accept it.

The twenty-third.

Nine days after Beatrix Voss walked onto the flats and, according to every official record in this county, drowned.

* * *

I did not say anything. That is the part I keep returning to, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone. I stood in that lobby watching Ilse read her sister's handwriting dated nine days after her sister's death, and I said nothing, because what would I have said? *Madam, your sister appears to have kept writing after she died?* There is no protocol for that. There is no card in the concierge's drawer for it.

Ilse read for a long time. Her face did what faces do — confusion first, then the flicker of hope that is crueler than grief, then something harder settling over both. When she finally looked up, her eyes were dry and terrible.

"These aren't right," she said.

"In what sense?"

"The dates. She—" Ilse pressed her lips together. "The police said the last entry was the thirteenth. These go on. Nine more days." She looked down at the page again. "And it doesn't read like her. Look — she always started with the tide. Always. *Tide, wind, conditions.* It's how she thought. This starts mid-sentence. Like she was afraid of being caught starting."

I looked. She was right, and I hated how quickly I saw it, because seeing it meant believing it, and believing it meant the case was not closed, whatever Detective Marsh's stamp said. The entry was cramped, angled up the margin, written in bursts — ink pooling where the pen had paused too long, the way it does when a hand is shaking or a writer is listening hard for footsteps.

"And the handwriting," Ilse whispered. "That's her letters. But it's not her hand. It's like — like someone doing an impression of her. Like she was writing as herself instead of writing as herself."

I have spent thirty years reading people, and I will tell you the truth: I have rarely seen anything so precisely wrong as that handwriting. Every letter correct. Every stroke strained. It was Beatrix Voss's script performed by Beatrix Voss under duress — a signature forged by its own owner, which is a trick I did not previously know the living could manage.

"Why would she fake her own hand?" Ilse asked. "If she was alive, why hide it? Why not write HELP ME in letters a mile high?"

Because, I thought, whoever she was hiding from could read. Because a diary that washes ashore is a message meant to survive, and messages meant to survive must be legible only to the right reader. Because a woman clever enough to document tide pools was clever enough to know that a plea gets destroyed and a puzzle gets kept.

But I said none of this. Instead I heard myself say, in my best front-desk voice, "Grief plays strange tricks on the eye, Miss Brandt. Stress, poor light, water damage — the samples are unreliable."

Which was a lie. The first lie I told in this affair, and I tell it against myself now so you understand: I lied not to protect the case, but to protect her. Because I saw what hope was doing to her face, and I knew that if I said aloud what I was thinking — *your sister was alive nine days after the sea supposedly took her, and she was afraid* — then Ilse Brandt would stop sleeping entirely, and I would own that.

Also — and this is the confession beneath the confession — I wanted to look first. I wanted to be sure before I set fire to a closed case and burned down Detective Marsh's weekend and whatever peace this hotel had left. Curiosity, yes. But also caution, my older and wiser companion, whispering: *check your facts before you check your loyalties.*

* * *

I took the diary's photograph file home that night — Marsh owed me, and I called the debt in without apology. I sat in my rooms above the boathouse with the images spread across my table like tarot cards, and I read the ninth-day entries until two in the morning.

The degraded hand continued. Dates marched on: the twenty-third, the twenty-fourth, the twenty-fifth. The entries grew shorter, tighter, pressed into margins and between lines as though paper itself had become scarce — as though she couldn't risk more pages, or couldn't risk more *time*. One entry was a single line. Another was mostly scratched out, the ink dragged into black rectangles, and beneath the worst of them, faintly, in the indentations the pen had left in the page beneath:

*— knows the building better than I do —*

Not *the shore*. Not *the town*. The building.

I sat back. Rain worked at the windows. Somewhere below, the tide was turning, filling the flats, covering the rocks where a fisherman had found a dead woman's diary wedged tight — wedged, I now understood, not thrown. Wedged the way you wedge something when you want the sea to hold it for you until it's safe to collect. Or until you can't collect it yourself.

A woman had died on those flats. Of that I remained nearly certain — the boots, the search, the silence. But the diary said she had been alive afterward, writing in fear, in code, in a hand performing itself. And the diary had washed ashore pointing, in its ruined way, toward the one place no one had thought to search for a missing researcher:

Back toward the hotel.

Toward the Meridian. With its hundred rooms and its service corridors and its staff who noticed everything and reported nothing.

I poured myself the drink I'd been refusing all week and made the call I'd been avoiding. Ilse answered on the first ring, as though she'd been holding the telephone since evening.

"Miss Brandt," I said. "About those later entries. I think your sister was telling us where she was."

A pause. Then, carefully, in a voice stripped of everything except intent:

"Then tell me what she said."
