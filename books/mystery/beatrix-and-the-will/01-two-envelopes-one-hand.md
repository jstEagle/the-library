## 1. Two Envelopes, One Hand

The first envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which is the day the universe traditionally chooses to ruin me, and I want that noted for the record.

I'd been running *The Long Silence* for three seasons by then — cold cases, mostly British, mostly old enough that everyone who could be hurt by my digging was either dead or had learned to live with the wound. My producer, Dev, liked to say I had a gift for finding the one person in a village who still remembered everything. What he meant, kindly, was that I had a gift for making people talk when they'd spent decades being paid not to.

So when my assistant — a rotating cast of freelancers who lasted about four months before fleeing to jobs with health insurance — dropped two envelopes on my desk with the postmark smudged to illegibility, I almost didn't look up from my notes on the Harrowgate drowning.

"Fan mail," she said. "One of them's weird."

"They're all weird," I said. "That's the demographic."

But she lingered, which she never did, so I opened them both there at the desk, in the grey light of a London morning that couldn't commit to rain.

The first envelope was cheap, the kind you buy in packs of fifty, and inside was a single sheet of lined paper torn from a spiral notebook. The handwriting was old-fashioned, careful, the letters of someone taught penmanship by a nun or a governess or simply by fear. It said:

*I killed Beatrix Vane on the fourth of October, 1974. She is under the boathouse floor. I have kept this forty-nine years because I was afraid. I am not afraid anymore, because I am dying anyway. Do what you like with it.*

It was signed, simply: **B**.

I sat with it for a long moment. This is the part nobody tells you about true crime — the part where your body reacts before your ethics catch up. There's a chemical little thrill, a sugar hit, when something real lands on your desk. Forty-nine years of cases and I can tell you the exact temperature of that feeling. And then, half a second behind it, comes the shame, because somewhere out there a family is going to have to hear this read aloud, and the thrill doesn't care.

I picked up the second envelope. Same paper. Same handwriting — and I mean identical, down to the way the *k* kicked out and the *4* stood open-topped like a sail. Not similar. Identical, the way only one hand can be.

The second letter said:

*You will receive a confession. It is false in every particular except the name. Beatrix Vane was not murdered; she was rescued. The rest is in the ledger. Burn nothing. Trust no one who profits from her staying dead.*

No signature at all.

Dev found me twenty minutes later still holding them, one in each hand, like a woman deciding which fuse to light.

"Well?" he said.

"Either we've got a murderer with excellent penmanship and a flair for the dramatic," I said, "or we've got someone who wants us to think exactly that."

"You're hoping it's the second one."

Of course I was. A confession ends a story. A riddle starts one. I'm forty-one years old and I have built an entire career on the difference, and if you want to know what kind of person that makes me, I've asked myself the same question at roughly three a.m. every night for a decade.

"Beatrix Vane," Dev said, pulling up the file. "Heiress. Vaned — sorry — vanished October 1974, age twenty-three. Father reported her missing from the estate. Body never found. Case went cold by Christmas." He looked up. "There's basically nothing. One newspaper archive scan and a parish record."

"That's not nothing," I said. "That's a vacuum. Vacuums don't happen naturally. Somebody hoovered."

He gave me the look he gives me when I'm about to do the thing, the look that is equal parts loyalty and pre-emptive grief. "Elodie. Mid-season. We have three episodes banked and a sponsor call Friday."

"I know."

"We are not pivoting the entire season on two anonymous letters."

"I know."

"Which means you already have."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Vane House before any of this: nothing, honestly, beyond the shape every disappeared heiress leaves in the public imagination. Rich girl, big house, bad weather, no body. The kind of case that breeds amateur sleuths like damp breeds mould. In 1974 it had made the nationals for eleven days — *HEIRESS VANES FROM VANE HOUSE*, a headline some subeditor must have wept over with joy — and then the Falklands happened, then everything else happened, and Beatrix became a paragraph in books about unsolved crimes, filed between a canal murder and a man who walked into the sea.

Her father, Edmund Vane, had died in 1988, leaving the estate to a foundation that left it, eventually, to whatever Vane remained. There were always Vanes. Old money is like ivy; you can burn it back but the roots hold the wall together.

And here is what I did with what I knew: I did the thing I always do, which is to say I put it on the air, because secrets that arrive in the post are addressed to everyone whether the sender knows it or not.

We recorded the episode in one take, which almost never happens. Dev sat behind the glass with his arms crossed like a disapproving vicar while I held the two letters up to the microphone and read them aloud, slowly, letting the silence after *under the boathouse floor* do its work.

Then I said: "Two letters. One hand. A confession and a correction, arriving together, like a lock and its key. Either someone out there killed Beatrix Vane and has decided, forty-nine years on, to unburden themselves — or someone out there knows exactly how she disappeared and has chosen me, of all people, to start pulling the thread." I let a beat pass. "I don't believe in coincidences that arrive in matching envelopes. So over the next few weeks, we're going to find out which story is true. If you know anything about Vane House, the ledger, or the five servants whose names keep appearing beside payments that shouldn't exist — yes, I've seen them too — write to me. Carefully."

That last bit wasn't in the script. Dev noticed. Dev always notices.

Afterwards he came into the booth and said, "Five servants? You didn't mention five servants to me."

"Because until ten minutes ago I hadn't mentioned them to myself." I tapped the second letter. "'Trust no one who profits from her staying dead.' That's not a madman's sentence, Dev. Madmen don't edit. That's a warning from someone who knows where the bodies are buried — metaphorically, since apparently there isn't one."

"And the confession?"

"The confession is bait. Beautifully made bait. Whoever wrote it wants attention pointed at Vane House — wants it pointed hard and fast, before someone else gets there first." I looked at the open-topped fours, the careful governess loops. "This person is dying, Dev. You can smell it in the ink. Dying people don't waste time on games unless the game matters more than the time they've got left."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "You're going regardless."

"I'm going tomorrow."

* * *

I want to be honest about the night between, because honesty is the only currency I trade in that doesn't devalue.

I lay awake doing the arithmetic I always do. Weighing the living against the dead. Somewhere in Yorkshire, presumably, a descendant of Edmund Vane was sleeping soundly on money and legend, and tomorrow I would start taking a crowbar to both. Was that fair? Was it mine to do? Every season I ask it, and every season the answer arrives the same way: not as permission, but as compulsion. The story picks you. That's the dirty secret of this job. You don't choose the case; the case chooses you, and then you spend months telling yourself it was your idea.

At two a.m. I got up and read the letters again under the kitchen light. And that's when I saw it — small, easy to miss, the kind of thing you only catch when exhaustion strips away everything but pattern.

The confession was dated. Fourth of October, 1974 — the date of the killing, written into the text itself. But the second letter, the correction, bore a different date in the same hand: *the ninth of October, 1974.*

Written five days apart. By the same hand. Forty-nine years ago.

Not recently copied. Not forged last week by someone studying samples. These letters had been written in 1974 — written within days of Beatrix Vane's disappearance — sealed, and held, all this time, by someone who had finally worked up the nerve, or run out of it.

Someone had waited half a century to send me a lie and its key.

I stood in my kitchen at two in the morning with my heart going like a trapped bird, and I understood two things with total clarity. First: I was going to Yorkshire. Second: whoever wrote these letters hadn't sent them to tell me a story.

They'd sent them to make sure someone finished it.

The episode aired Thursday. By Friday morning there were four hundred emails in the inbox, most of them noise, two of them threats, and one — subject line blank, sender a defunct address, postmark York — containing a single scanned page from a household ledger, dated March 1975.

A wages entry. For a gardener who had, according to every record that existed, died the previous winter.

Beneath it, in that same careful hand, someone had added a note, decades old and fresher than any headline:

*Now you see why it had to be you.*
