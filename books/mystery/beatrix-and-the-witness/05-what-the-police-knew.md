## 5. What the Police Knew

The Vane file lived in a grey box on the third floor of Ashcombe Constabulary Headquarters, in the cold-case annex that smelled of damp carpet and toner, and Detective Inspector Ruth Adeyemi had come to think of it as a patient she could not cure.

She had inherited it eighteen months ago, along with a desk whose previous occupant had left a coffee ring shaped like a crescent moon and a drawer full of takeaway menus for restaurants that no longer existed. The transfer had been framed as a promotion—*consolidation of historical enquiries, Adeyemi, you're the right sort of thorough*—but Ruth had been in the job long enough to translate: nobody else wanted it, and she was the kind of officer who could be trusted to bury a file without actually burying it.

Beatrix Vane. Thirty-one. Archivist, county records office. Last seen on the fourteenth of October, two years ago, leaving the Rose and Crown on Fenwick Lane at 9:40 in the evening, alone, in a green coat, carrying a canvas book bag. Her flat was undisturbed. Her car was where she'd left it. No body, no blood, no ransom, no pattern of debt or despair. A woman who had simply stepped out of the world between the pub and the corner of Marlow Street, a distance of four hundred metres, most of it under working streetlamps.

The original investigating officer, a DCI named Hollis who had retired to Spain before the case went cold, had written in his final summary, in the flat prose of a man closing a door: *No evidence of third-party involvement identified. Enquiry to remain open, pending further information.*

Pending further information. The four saddest words in policing.

Ruth had read the file four times in her first month. Then twice more at six-month intervals, the way a doctor re-examines a scan looking for the thing everyone missed. She had walked the route from the Rose and Crown to Marlow Street at the same hour, in comparable weather, and stood in the pool of light beneath the lamp at the corner where the CCTV had been down for scheduled maintenance on the night in question—because of course it had been; in her experience the cameras were only ever working for crimes nobody cared about.

She had found nothing. That in itself was a finding. Cases like this one were never nothing; they were *something* wearing the costume of nothing, and the costume was always tailored by someone patient.

It was on the fifth read-through, in February, that she noticed the request log.

Every cold-case file had one: a plain card slipped inside the front cover where any officer who pulled the box was meant to sign, date, and note the reason. It was a bureaucratic courtesy honoured mostly in the breach. Ruth had signed it herself five times. Before her signatures, the card was nearly empty—Hollis twice, a DC named Prewitt once, and then, scattered through the eighteen months after the file went cold, a constellation of names she had never heard of and could not place.

*P. Lassiter — records audit.*
*J. Fenmore — liaison enquiry, re: press interest.*
*A. Okonjo — background check, unrelated matter.*
*M. Teale — FOI support.*
*D. Carrow — audit (2nd).*

Seven names in two years. That was not unusual in itself; missing-person files got pulled by journalists' researchers, by true-crime podcasters, by the occasional obsessive relative of some other missing person hoping the cases rhymed. But something about them bothered Ruth the way a wrong note bothers a musician. She sat with the card for a long time that February afternoon, turning it under the desk lamp, and finally put her finger on it.

The handwriting.

Six of the seven signatures were in different hands—she'd give them that, the forger had range. But the *pressure* was the same. She held the card up to the light and looked at it the way she'd been trained to look at documents in a forgery seminar a decade ago: not at the shapes of the letters but at the indentations, the ghost-grooves pressed into the card beneath each signature. Every one of them had been written by someone pressing hard, with a heavy pen, in a room with a hard surface beneath the card. Every one of them had been written *fast*—the tails of the letters flung, not drawn. And every one of them, when she measured the spacing, sat at exactly the same height on the line. People's signatures wandered. These didn't. These had been placed.

Someone had sat down, once, probably in a single afternoon, and signed seven names in a row.

* * *

She did what she was paid to do, which was to say she did it quietly and on her own time.

The request log was one thing, but files didn't leave the building on a signature alone; physical copies required a requisition through records management, and records management kept its own system. Ruth spent a Thursday evening—she had learned long ago that the only way to get clean data out of that department was to go when nobody was defending the gate—pulling the requisition history on the Vane file. Seven physical copy requests matched the seven names on the card. All approved. All signed out and signed back in, on time, no discrepancies.

Except.

She ran the names through the staff directory. P. Lassiter had left the force in March, three years ago. J. Fenmore had never existed in the directory at all. A. Okonjo was real—there were two, in fact, a custody sergeant in the north of the county and a finance officer—but neither had any connection to the cold-case annex, and the finance officer, when Ruth checked the dates, had been on maternity leave during the week of the request bearing her name. M. Teale had retired in 2019. D. Carrow did not exist.

Five names. Five ghosts. Someone had walked into her building, or sent someone, and borrowed the faces of the departed and the nonexistent to read a dead woman's file.

Ruth made herself a cup of coffee she didn't drink and sat in the annex after everyone had gone home, with the box open in front of her, and asked the question that mattered: *why this file?*

There were forty-one boxes in the cold-case annex. Missing persons, suspicious deaths, the sediment of two decades. If someone wanted to study police procedure, any of them would do. If someone wanted to study *this kind* of case—a woman vanishing between a pub and a street corner—there were three others that rhymed. But the requisition history on those files was clean. Only Vane. Only Beatrix Vane, seven times, under five false names, in a two-year window that began almost exactly when the case went cold.

Not curiosity, then. Not research in the general sense. *Specificity.* Someone had needed to know exactly what the police knew about Beatrix Vane—what they'd found, what they'd missed, which stones had been turned and which had been left—and had needed to know it repeatedly, returning to the file the way a writer returns to a manuscript, checking, revising, confirming.

Ruth wrote the seven names on the inside cover of her own notebook, in her own hand, and dated it. She told no one. Not because she was breaking procedure—technically she was initiating an integrity check, which any inspector was entitled to do—but because she had learned, in eleven years of service, that the moment you say a thing out loud in a police building, the thing stops being yours and starts being a rumour with a badge. And there was something about this that she wanted to keep close, the way you keep a hand closed around a coin until you're sure of its weight.

She started a private list. It lived in a green notebook with no official markings, kept in her home desk drawer behind a tax file no one would ever steal. The seven names went in first. Then the dates. Then the requisition numbers. Then, on a fresh page, a single question she underlined twice:

*Who benefits from knowing what the police don't know?*

* * *

March brought the first answer, or the shape of one.

She widened the net. If someone had ghosted their way into the Vane file, they might have ghosted into others—anything adjacent. Ruth pulled the requisition histories on every file that touched the Vane enquiry: the scene logs, the house-to-house records, the CCTV maintenance records, the statements. Most were clean. But the statement bundle—the ninety-one witness statements taken in the fortnight after Beatrix disappeared—showed two additional requests under names Ruth did not recognise, and when she checked, one of the names belonged to a retired property clerk and the other to a serving officer who had, on the date of his supposed request, been hospitalised with a ruptured appendix.

She sat back in her chair in the empty annex and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from *competence*. Whoever this was knew the building. Knew which names would pass a cursory check. Knew that requisitions were approved by a clerk who signed ninety forms a day without reading them. Knew that the request card in the file itself was never cross-referenced against the requisition system, because no one had ever thought it worth cross-referencing. This was not an outsider's crime. This was someone who had stood in these corridors, or stood in corridors exactly like them, until the bureaucracy had become as legible as a bedtime story.

A former officer? A civilian staffer? A records clerk?

The phrase *records clerk* arrived in her mind unbidden and stayed there, itching.

She went back to the file itself and read it a sixth time, but differently now—not as an investigator reading a case, but as a burglar reading a house. What would someone want from these pages? The witness statements, certainly: to know who had been asked what, and who might have been missed. The scene photographs: to know exactly what the police had seen. The timeline: Hollis had built a minute-by-minute reconstruction of Beatrix's last documented evening, and it was the best thing in the file, meticulous and ultimately useless, ending at 9:52 p.m. with a shape on a broken camera that might have been a green coat and might have been shadow.

And the personal effects inventory. Ruth lingered on that page. One canvas book bag. One purse, containing £23.40 and a library card. One set of keys. One fountain pen, black, engraved *B.V.* One paperback, water-damaged, recovered from a storm drain on Marlow Street three days after the disappearance: *The Hollow Man*, by an author Ruth had never heard of, with a receipt from a bookshop called Marrow & Page tucked inside at page 117.

She noted the bookshop's name without knowing why. It sat in her notebook like a seed.

* * *

In April, she did something she had been putting off, because doing it would make the thing real: she requested the personnel and access records for everyone—officer, civilian, contractor—who had held keys to the cold-case annex in the relevant two-year window.

The list came back on a Friday: forty-three names. Ruth took it home, spread it across her kitchen table beside the green notebook, and began the slow, unglamorous work of elimination. Officers seconded elsewhere during the window. Officers retired. Officers she could account for by simple geography—people who had never, as far as she could establish, shown the faintest interest in the Vane case.

She got the list down to eleven, then to six, then to three, and there she stalled, because the remaining three were all plausible and all unremarkable: a civilian archivist named Prentiss who catalogued returned files; a DC named Sorrell who had done a three-month rotation in the annex and was now in traffic; and a records management clerk named T. Merrick, who had processed copy requests for the annex for nine years and whose signature appeared, in his own hand, approving five of the seven ghost requisitions.

Ruth looked at that name for a long time.

It proved nothing. Merrick approved hundreds of requests a year; that was his job. A man could sit at the gate of a building for nine years and never once walk through the wrong door. But she remembered something now, dredged up from the first month of the case, from the witness statements—a name in the margins of the house-to-house log, a records clerk from the county office who had come forward voluntarily in the first week to say he'd seen Beatrix Vane at the records office the afternoon before she vanished, that she'd seemed agitated, that she'd left late. A helpful man. A small, precise statement, the kind that adds a brushstroke and changes nothing.

T. Merrick.

The same name, or close enough. Ruth pulled the statement itself and read it twice. It was beautifully done, she thought. Modest. Unmemorable. It placed itself inside the enquiry without asking for attention, the way a forged painting places itself in a credible frame. And it contained one detail that had never been corroborated and never needed to be: that Beatrix Vane had been carrying a leather diary that afternoon, brown, with a broken clasp.

No diary had ever been found. It was in the file as an unresolved personal effect. *One diary, brown leather, clasp damaged—unrecovered.*

Ruth closed the statement and sat in her kitchen with the radiators ticking and the streetlight coming orange through the curtains, and she understood that she was no longer doing an integrity check. She was doing what she had privately promised herself she would never do with a cold case: she was building a theory out of almost nothing, and the almost-nothing was beginning to cohere, and cohering theories are the most dangerous objects in policing because they want to be true.

She wrote in the green notebook:

*Merrick, T. Records clerk (county office). Approved 5 false requisitions. Gave statement wk 1 re: Vane's diary. Detail uncorroborated. Why mention the diary? Who volunteers an uncorroborated detail unless they need it to exist?*

She underlined the last sentence and then, because the hour was late and her tea had gone cold and the thought had grown teeth, she wrote the sentence that had been forming all evening and that she had been afraid of:

*Or unless they need the police to believe it existed—so that when it turns up, it will be believed.*

She did not sleep well. At half past three she got up and stood at the window looking down at her street, at the parked cars and the wet pavement and the ordinary dark, and she thought about Beatrix Vane's mother, whom Ruth had called once, early on, and who had answered on the first ring, as if she had been sitting by the phone for two years waiting for it to be about her daughter.

*We're pursuing several lines of enquiry,* Ruth had said, and heard the woman breathe, and hated herself for the sentence.

In the morning she came in early, before the annex staff, and signed the request card in the Vane file for the sixth time—*R. Adeyemi, review*—and beneath it, in the space where the reason went, she wrote something she would never write in an official document: *Someone is reading over our shoulders.*

Then she locked the box, logged the key, and went to her desk, where the morning's tray held the overnight reports from the division: a burglary on Cordwell Rise, a domestic on Tanner Street, a road traffic collision on the bypass, and, flagged for her attention because it was a death in unexplained circumstances, a body pulled from the canal at Mill Lock in the early hours—a local man, mid-fifties, no identification yet, found by a jogger.

Accidental drowning, the responding sergeant had written. *No suspicious circumstances apparent.*

Ruth read it twice. She thought of her green notebook, and the seven ghosts, and a brown diary with a broken clasp that no one had ever found, and she reached for her coat, because Mill Lock was a twenty-minute drive and she had learned never to trust the word *apparent*.

The canal, she would think later, was where the case stopped being a file and started being a hunt.
