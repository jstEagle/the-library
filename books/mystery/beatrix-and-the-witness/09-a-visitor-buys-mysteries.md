## 9. A Visitor Buys Mysteries

The bell above the door of Marrow & Page had a voice like a spoon dropped in an empty cup—thin, apologetic, easy to ignore. Oscar heard it at eleven minutes past four on a Thursday afternoon and did not look up from the ledger. Most customers entered the way weather did: they drifted in out of the rain, dripped on the mat, murmured at the shelves, and left without buying anything or disturbing the dust. She had built her life around not being disturbed.

"Good afternoon," said a voice that was not thin at all. It was soft, but soft the way felt is soft—a deliberate quality, applied rather than inherent. "What a lovely shop."

Oscar looked up.

He stood just inside the door with his umbrella furled and dripping politely into his own cupped hand so as not to wet her floor. A man of perhaps fifty-five, grey at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, wearing a cardigan the colour of weak tea over a collared shirt buttoned to the throat. His face was unremarkable in the specific way that made her cataloguing mind reach for adjectives and find none waiting. He was the kind of man you would fail to pick out of a queue, out of a photograph, out of a memory.

She knew him anyway.

Not his face—she was almost certain she'd never seen his face—but his name. It sat in the Vane file like a splinter: *T. Merrick, records clerk, county archives office.* The man who had signed off on the transfer of Beatrix Vane's personnel file two weeks before she vanished. The man whose signature appeared, small and neat, on three separate document requests in the months leading up to the disappearance, each one routine, each one unremarkable, each one, when Oscar laid them side by side at two in the morning with cold tea going stale beside her, forming a pattern as clear as footprints in snow.

He was standing in her shop. He was smiling at her shelves.

"We're mostly secondhand," she said, and her voice came out level, shopkeeper-flat, though somewhere beneath her sternum a wire had begun to hum. "Mystery's in the back left. History along the far wall. Poetry is wherever it hasn't been knocked over."

"I'll find my own way, if I may." He set his umbrella in the stand with a small ceremony, as if he were checking a coat at a dinner party. "I do enjoy discovering things myself. Being *led* to a book feels like being told the end of a joke."

"Then I'll leave you to it." She returned to the ledger. She did not return to the ledger. Her eyes tracked him over its ruled lines while her pen made small, meaningless ticks against columns that needed no ticking.

He moved through the shop the way water moves through cloth—slowly, thoroughly, without urgency. He read spines. He tilted his head at the window display. When he reached the back-left corner where the mysteries lived, he did not browse; he *audited*. He pulled a Sayers and checked the printing. He opened a Christie to the frontispiece and seemed to weigh it in his hand. And then he began, without any apparent system, to set books aside. Three of them. A stack, squared with a precision that made something in Oscar's hindbrain sit up and bare its teeth.

*The Nine Tailors.* *Crooked Hinge.* *Death of a Bookseller.*

She had shelved that last one herself only last week. It had come in a box of donations from a house clearance in Ashcombe—the same box, she realized now with a slow, cold turning in her gut, as the diary. The same box. Of all the hundreds of books in this shop, he had found the three that mattered, and one of them had been sitting in her stock for exactly six days.

Coincidence, said the part of her that still believed in it, the part that had once been young.

*Nothing about this man is coincidence,* said the part that had spent thirty years learning otherwise.

* * *

He brought the stack to the counter and laid the books down one at a time, squaring their edges, aligning them with the counter's edge as though the transaction were a small ritual requiring correct form.

"You have a remarkable collection," he said. "I don't suppose you know how many volumes?"

"Somewhere north of nine thousand. I stopped counting when counting stopped being useful."

"And yet everything is where it should be." He touched the spine of *The Nine Tailors*, straightening it by a millimetre it didn't need. "Most secondhand shops are chaos pretending to be charm. This is order pretending to be modesty. There's a quiet intelligence here." He looked up, and his eyes were pale grey, patient, attentive in a way that felt less like interest than like *reading*. "I mean that as a compliment. Intelligence that doesn't announce itself. That simply... attends."

"Thank you," Oscar said. "That'll be fourteen pounds fifty."

He laughed—a soft, pleased sound, as if she'd said something cleverer than she had. He produced a wallet, old leather gone dark at the corners, and counted out the money in notes smoothed flat. Then, as she wrote the receipt, he said:

"Do you read them yourself? The mysteries?"

"When I have time."

"What's your theory, then?" He asked it lightly, conversationally, a man making talk in a queue. "About why people read them. I've always thought it isn't really about the crime at all. It's about the *witness*. The person who sees what everyone else looks past. Poirot, Wimsey—all of them, they're not really detectives. They're readers. They read a room the way you'd read a page, and the murderer is the author, and the whole game—" he smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't— "the whole game is finding the reader who reads you correctly."

Oscar tore the receipt free and held it out. Her hand did not shake. Thirty years of practice held it steady, and she was grateful to every one of those years.

"That's one theory," she said.

"It's mine." He took the receipt, folded it once, precisely, and slid it into his breast pocket. Then he paused, and something shifted in his face—not a change, exactly, more a focusing, the way a lens settles. "I wonder if I might impose on you. A card. For the shop. Do you keep them?"

"We do." She reached under the counter for the little tin of cards and the pen on a bead chain, and her mind was moving fast and cold behind her pleasant face. *Don't give him your name spelled right. Don't give him anything he can file.* But refusing a card would be its own signal, and signals were what she could not afford. He was watching for flinches. She could feel him watching for flinches the way one feels a draft.

She handed him a blank card and the pen.

He wrote unhurriedly, tongue of concentration nowhere visible, posture perfect. Then he turned the card around and slid it across the counter to her, and there it was, in a small, neat, clerk's hand—the handwriting she had studied under a magnifying lamp at two in the morning, comparing it against transfer forms and request slips until the letters swam:

*T. Merrick.*

No address. No phone number. Just the name, offered like a calling card from another century.

"For your records," he said.

Something in the phrasing landed wrong, deliberately wrong, a key turned a half-notch past its stop. *For your records.* As if he knew she kept records. As if he knew what kind.

"I'm afraid we don't hold a mailing list," she said. "We're not that organized."

"Oh, I think you're very organized, Mrs.—"

"Coldharbour. It's on the sign."

"Coldharbour," he repeated, tasting it. "A good name. Sheltered. Defensible." He gathered his books into a canvas bag—he had brought his own bag, of course he had, a man who planned even his errands—and paused with his hand resting flat on the counter, fingers together, formal as a witness taking an oath. "I'll be honest with you, since honesty between readers seems fitting. I've been meaning to visit for some time. I drive past most weeks. There's something about a bookshop that survives when everything around it closes—that takes donations, that keeps other people's stories alive—that strikes me as a kind of quiet heroism."

"The rent does most of the surviving."

He smiled again, that reaching-the-eyes smile, and shook his head slowly, as if gently correcting a student who had nearly grasped the lesson. "Modesty, again. Order pretending to be modesty." He lifted the bag. "Thank you for these. I'll take good care of them. I always take good care of the things I'm given."

The bell gave its thin spoon-drop sound as he left, and the door closed on the grey afternoon, and Oscar stood behind her counter with his card in her hand and did not move for a long time.

* * *

She locked the door at five. She turned the sign, drew the blind halfway, and carried the card to the back room, where she kept the things that constituted her real life: the ledger of marginalia, the clippings, the three folders she thought of privately as the accidents, and the fourth folder, thicker, tabbed BEATRIX VANE in her own block capitals.

She set Merrick's card beside the open file and compared.

The signature on the 2019 transfer authorization: *T. Merrick*, the T crossed with a single economical stroke, the final k ending in a tiny upward flick, like a bird lifting off.

The card: identical. Not similar. Identical—the flick, the stroke, the slight forward lean of a hand written by someone who had signed his name ten thousand times and never once thought about it. People could disguise a signature when they thought about it. Nobody disguised the way they ended a letter k.

She sat down on the stool that groaned under her and pressed both palms flat against the desk, as if steadying a deck in rough water.

Think. Think like the woman you used to be.

He had come to the shop. He had bought three detective novels—one of which had arrived in the same donation box as Beatrix Vane's diary, a fact he could only know if he knew where the diary had come from, which meant either he had handled the estate sale himself or he had followed the box from Ashcombe House to her counter. He had spoken about witnesses and authors and readers reading correctly, in a shop owned by a retired detective, using language that was either a coincidence of temperament or a message wrapped in velvet. He had given her his name. His true name, verifiably his name, the name attached to the file.

Why give her the name?

Because he wanted her to know. That was the answer, and it arrived with the certainty of a key turning. A man who wanted to hide would have paid cash and mumbled a false name and left no card. This man had performed his own identification. He had walked into her shop carrying his name like a lantern and held it up so she couldn't miss it.

Either he was innocent and simply fond of detective novels, in which case the world was kinder than she believed—

—or he was telling her that he knew she had the diary. That he knew she had read it. That he knew what she was, the anonymous solver, the margin-writer, the ghost who had solved three murders from behind a counter, and that he had been watching her do it, perhaps for years, the way a writer watches a reader he especially respects. *The only reader who reads me correctly.* He had said it about fictional murderers. He had not said it about himself. He hadn't needed to.

And underneath all of it, one question, cold and rising like water in a cellar: *if he knows she has the diary, then the diary is not evidence she found. It is bait that was placed.*

Entries continuing past the date of a disappearance. Descriptions of rooms Beatrix Vane had never been photographed in. One entry describing a bookshop—*a narrow shop, rain on the glass, a woman behind the counter who watches everything and says nothing.* Oscar had told herself it was coincidence, or grief-fantasy, or forgery by some sentimental hand. She had told herself a great many comfortable things.

Now the forger, if forger he was, was standing at her counter buying Christies and complimenting her order.

Her hands were cold. She noticed it distantly, the way one notices weather through a window. Ice down the spine, yes—but the smile stayed on her face even alone in the back room, because muscles trained for decades do not relax merely because the audience has gone. She caught her reflection in the darkened front glass: a grey-haired woman behind a counter, pleasant, unremarkable, a shopkeeper closing up on a Thursday.

*Order pretending to be modesty,* he'd said. He had seen her. Not the shopkeeper. The thing underneath.

She opened the Vane file and read the clerk's details again. Thomas Merrick. Twenty-two years in the county records office. Responsible for archiving coroner's reports, death certificates, police transfer documents. The man through whose hands passed, eventually, everything the county chose to remember about its dead. A man who had once written, in a performance review she'd pulled from a public staffing record, a sentence she had underlined twice: *Maintains the integrity of the historical record above all personal considerations.*

Above all personal considerations. She had read it as bureaucratic boilerplate. Now it sounded like a creed. Now it sounded like the kind of sentence a certain sort of man writes about himself and believes is humility.

And the paper hats. Three deaths, three accidents, three scenes where something small and folded and childish had been left—found in a hedge after the fall from the quarry path, tucked in a pocket after the drowning, wedged beneath the brake pedal nobody had questioned. She had never published the connection. She had never told anyone. The knowledge lived in her ledger and nowhere else on earth.

Unless it lived in the files too. Unless someone else had seen what she'd seen, years ago, and had watched to see whether anyone would ever say it aloud—and had learned that no one would, that the woman who saw it would fold it away in silence like a note slipped into a returned library book.

The bell rang.

Oscar's head came up. The shop was locked; she had locked it; she had drawn the blind. But the bell rang, thin and apologetic, and footsteps crossed the floorboards toward the back room—unhurried, thorough, water moving through cloth—and she was on her feet with the ledger shoved shut and her heart going like a trapped bird when the knock came at the door, three soft raps, and a voice through the wood, mild and patient:

"Mrs. Coldharbour? Sorry to trouble you—it's Ruth Adeyemi. County CID. We spoke on the phone last month about the Ashcombe House donation. Might I have a word about Beatrix Vane?"

Oscar stood in the middle of her back room, Merrick's card in one fist and the Vane file open on the desk behind her, and understood that the day had just split into two halves: everything before this knock, and everything after.

She put the card in her pocket, closed the file, and went to unlock the door with a shopkeeper's pleasant, empty smile already fixed in place.

"Inspector," she said. "Of course. Come in out of the rain."
