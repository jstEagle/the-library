## 18. An Uneasy Alliance

Oscar heard the bell over the door at eleven minutes past four on a Thursday, and she knew before she looked up that it was trouble, because trouble always came in out of the rain with its shoulders set like it had rehearsed the entrance.

Detective Inspector Ruth Adeyemi stood in the doorway of Marrow & Page, shaking water from a coat that cost more than the shop's monthly rent, and looked around the premises with the expression of a woman cataloguing exits. Oscar had seen that expression on witnesses, on suspects, on her own face in dark windows. It was the look of someone who did not intend to be surprised twice.

"We're closed," Oscar said, though the sign said OPEN and the lights were on and she was, quite visibly, shelving a stack of paperbacks.

"Then you won't mind if I don't buy anything." Adeyemi crossed the floor, her shoes marking wet crescents on the boards. She stopped at the counter, took in the till, the ledger, the small brass bell, and finally Oscar herself, with the patient thoroughness of a woman reading a document she already suspected was forged. "Oscar Coldharbour."

"Bookseller."

"Retired DCI. Twenty-two years, Met and county both. Resigned rather than face a disciplinary tribunal in—" she consulted a notebook she did not need to consult, "—March, eleven years ago. Took over this shop six months later. Sole proprietor. No staff. No family listed. No social media. No dentist, which frankly I find more suspicious than the rest of it."

"You've been busy."

"I've been thorough. There's a difference, and I'd have thought you of all people would appreciate it." Adeyemi set her notebook on the counter, squared it against the edge, and left it there like a piece on a board. "I'm going to ask you some questions, Ms. Coldharbour. Before I do, you should know I'm not recording. There's no one else in the shop. There's no one in the car, because I didn't drive. I walked, in the rain, so that no one would ask why a DI was visiting a failing bookshop in Marlow Street on a Thursday afternoon."

Oscar set down the paperback she was holding. It was a Le Carré, spine cracked, and she had been about to shelve it under Espionage where it had lived, in various editions, for the better part of a decade. "And why is a DI visiting my shop, Inspector?"

"Because three men died in this county in six years, and the coroner called all three accidents, and someone who thinks very much the way you think knew they weren't." Adeyemi's voice was level, almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. "And because Beatrix Vane has been missing for two years and three months, and yesterday I found a diary entry in her handwriting describing a room she could not have known, and the entry was written four months after she vanished. And because every road I walk on this case ends at this counter."

The rain filled the silence, drumming on the skylight above the poetry section, the one that leaked and that Oscar had never fixed because the sound of it made the shop feel inhabited.

"You think I wrote it," Oscar said.

"No. I think you *read* it. I think you read it the way you read everything—completely, hungrily, and without telling a living soul what you found." Adeyemi leaned forward, both palms flat on the counter, and for the first time the professional mask slipped enough to show the face underneath: tired, furious, and afraid. "Three annotations. In three different books, in three different libraries, left inside three closed cases. A folded-hat detail in the Hollis drowning that was never released to the press. A reference to the angle of the stair rail in the Prentice fall. A chemical note on the Fennimore inquest that the pathologist himself got wrong in his own report. Whoever wrote those margins solved three deaths that this county's entire apparatus failed to solve, and then—" she straightened, "—went back to selling secondhand novels and said nothing. For years. While a man with a signature kept working."

"You've been reading my margins."

"I've been reading everything. That's the job." Adeyemi's eyes didn't leave hers. "Obstruction is a funny charge, Ms. Coldharbour. It doesn't require you to have committed a crime. It only requires that a crime occurred and that you, knowing of it, did nothing. I could write that warrant tonight. I could be back here tomorrow with two constables and a box for your ledgers, and I could take every book in this shop apart page by page until I found whatever else you've been hiding in them."

"But you haven't."

"No."

"Why not?"

Adeyemi looked at the shop then—really looked, the way she hadn't on entering. The sagging shelves. The hand-lettered section signs, one of which read MYSTERIES, SOLVED AND OTHERWISE. The armchair by the window with its worn left arm, the one regulars fought over. The rain on the skylight.

"Because I've spent four months being the only person in that building who thinks Beatrix Vane is alive," she said, "and I know exactly what it costs to be right in a room full of people who've decided you're wrong. And because—" she paused, and Oscar watched her decide to say it, watched the words cost her something, "—because whoever is doing this considers you an audience. He's been performing for you. And I need to understand the audience before I can catch the performer."

The bell over the door gave a small sympathetic clink as a draught moved through the shop. Neither woman looked at it.

"You said no recording," Oscar said at last.

"I said no recording."

"Then sit down, Inspector. The chair by the window is the good one. I'll make tea, and I'll tell you about a diary, and you can decide afterward whether you're arresting me or asking for my help." She was already moving toward the back room, and she paused at the curtain, not turning around. "But understand something first. I didn't stay silent because I didn't care. I stayed silent because the last time I spoke up, the man I spoke about walked, and two witnesses recanted, and a detective constable named Oscar Coldharbour learned that the truth is a currency and she was broke. So if we do this—whatever this is—I'm not doing it out of redemption. I'm doing it because a woman is alive somewhere and writing to me in a dead woman's diary, and I am done being an audience."

Behind her, the silence stretched. Then Adeyemi said, quietly: "The chair by the window. Right. I'll try not to get it wet."

Oscar pushed through the curtain before her face could give anything away, and put the kettle on, and let her hands shake where no one could see.

* * *

The tea was strong and slightly oversteeped and Adeyemi drank it without comment, which Oscar took as a point in her favor. They had arranged themselves across the counter from each other like negotiators, the ledger between them now serving as a border, and for an hour they had traded what each knew—or rather, Adeyemi had traded and Oscar had rationed, doling out facts in small denominations and watching the DI's face for the flicker that would tell her how close to the truth each one landed.

Adeyemi, for her part, was harder to read than most. But not impossible.

"You pulled my personnel file," Oscar said. It wasn't a question.

"Sealed records require a superintendent's signature. I got one." Adeyemi set down her cup. "You refused to testify against DCI Edwin Marsh. Four counts of evidence tampering, one count of perjury, and a pattern of case dismissals that benefited—" she checked the file in her memory, not the paper, because she had read it enough times to carry it, "—a development consortium with interests in three of his districts. You were the arresting officer. You were the one who found the tampered chain of custody. And on the morning of the tribunal, you withdrew your statement."

"The witnesses recanted."

"Both of them. Within a week of each other. One moved to Spain." Adeyemi's gaze was steady. "I've read the tribunal transcript, Ms. Coldharbour. Your withdrawal statement is four sentences long. I've read a great many withdrawal statements and they're usually longer, because people justify. You didn't justify. You just—stopped. Like someone closing a book."

Oscar looked at the rain on the skylight. "Is there a question in there?"

"The question is whether you stopped, or whether you were stopped." Adeyemi turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a precise, deliberate motion. "Because here's what interests me. Marsh's file clerk in those years—the junior who handled the physical evidence transfers, the one whose signature is on every single document you flagged—was a man named Thomas Merrick."

The name landed in the shop like a coin dropped down a well. Oscar kept her face still, but her heartbeat had changed, and she suspected Adeyemi heard it anyway.

"Go on," she said.

"Merrick transferred to county records two years before Beatrix Vane disappeared. He processed her records request—the one she filed three weeks before she vanished, the one about the amended death certificates. He signed the donation manifest for the Ashcombe House estate sale." Adeyemi leaned back. "He's been in the periphery of this case like a watermark. Visible only when you hold the page up to the light. And eleven days ago he walked into this shop and bought three detective novels and signed a card, and you recognized the name, and you said nothing. To me. To anyone."

"You've been in my shop."

"I've been watching your shop. There's a difference." Adeyemi's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "The man who runs the tobacconist across the street is my sergeant's cousin. He notices things. It's a family trait."

"Then you know he came back. Tuesday. He asked after a book on forgery."

"I know." The not-quite-smile faded. "Which is why I'm here today and not tomorrow. Ms. Coldharbour—Oscar." She used the first name deliberately, and they both felt the floor of the conversation shift. "I'm going to lay out a proposal that could end my career, and you're going to listen, and then we're both going to pretend this tea was about books."

Oscar waited.

"Officially, I found the diary in the estate donation, I followed procedure, and the investigation proceeds through proper channels with proper warrants. You were never involved. Unofficially—" Adeyemi pulled a folded sheet from her coat, not the notebook, a separate paper, and smoothed it on the counter between them, "—there is a second file. Mine. Off the system. It contains the diary analysis, the Merrick connections, the request logs under false names, and everything you're about to tell me. It contains no names of informants, because it has no informants. It has a consultant. Unpaid, unlisted, unofficial, who reviews documents I bring to her and offers, in her professional capacity as a retired officer, her analytical impressions. Which I am free to disregard. Which I will, publicly and loudly, if it ever becomes necessary."

"And if it becomes necessary," Oscar said slowly, "I burn."

"If it becomes necessary, you were a bookseller who once sold a detective a paperback." Adeyemi met her eyes. "I won't pretend that's nothing. I know what it is. I'm asking you to trust me with your exposure the way I'm asking you to help me with a case that's eating me alive. That's the trade. Thin ice, both of us, crossing together."

Oscar looked at the paper. It was a single page, typed, headed with a case number that did not exist in any system: a ghost file, a shadow docket. At the bottom was a line for a signature and the printed word CONSULTANT, and beneath it, in Adeyemi's small square handwriting, the words *no obligation, no lies between us*.

"You could be Marsh," Oscar said. "You could be exactly what he was. A superior officer who decides what the truth is worth and pays for it in other people."

"I could." Adeyemi didn't flinch, and Oscar gave her credit for it. "But Marsh never sat across from anyone and offered them the knife. He told them what to say and made the cost of refusing worse than the cost of lying. I'm telling you the cost up front, and I'm handing you the refusal. Walk me out of this shop right now and I'll write my warrant and we'll do this the ugly way, and maybe we'll still save her, and maybe we won't, and either way you'll have lost nothing you hadn't already lost."

The rain thickened against the skylight. Somewhere down the street a delivery van changed gears. Oscar became aware, with a strange clarity, that she had not made a decision in eleven years—only observations, only marginalia, only the small safe violence of ink in other people's books. That every silence had been a door she'd locked behind herself, and that she had told herself the locking was a kind of peace.

She picked up the pen Adeyemi was holding out.

"Conditions," she said.

"Of course."

"One. I see everything you see. No filtered summaries, no 'need to know.' If you get a document, I get it the same day." 

"Agreed."

"Two. When we find her—when, Inspector—I'm in the room. Not at the arrest, if that's your line. In the room after. She's been writing to me for weeks. She deserves a face that isn't a warrant."

Adeyemi was quiet for a moment. "Agreed," she said again, and something in her voice suggested she'd have agreed to more.

"Three." Oscar set the pen down without signing. "You tell me what the file says about why he does it. Not the what—the why. Because I've read his purchases and his visits and his diary corrections, and I have a theory, and I need to know if you've reached the same one, because if we've both reached it independently then it's probably right, and if it's right—" her voice did not change but something underneath it did, "—then he isn't finished, and Beatrix Vane is not his last chapter."

Adeyemi pulled a photograph from the ghost file and laid it on the counter between them: a coroner's evidence shot, a small paper hat, folded from a page of ledger paper, resting on the edge of a canal lock.

"He corrects people," Oscar said quietly. "People who found his errors. People who made him fallible in a system where he's spent thirty years being the invisible hand on the record. He doesn't kill out of rage. He kills out of *editorial principle*. And the hat—" she touched the edge of the photograph, not quite touching the hat, "—the hat is his colophon. The mark of the press. He's not hiding the murders, Adeyemi. He's *publishing* them. To whoever reads carefully enough."

For a long moment the DI said nothing. Then she turned the photograph over, and on the back, in her own handwriting, was a single line she had evidently carried for days without anyone to say it to:

*He thinks the case files are books and he's the only one reading them properly.*

"No," Adeyemi said. "I don't think we reached it independently. I think we reached it because it's true, and because you and I are the only two people in this county who read properly." She slid the pen across the counter again. "Sign, consultant. And then tell me about the acrostic in the diary, because I know there is one, and I know you found it, and I've been staring at the word CELLAR for three days trying to decide if it's a message from the victim or an invitation from the killer—and you're the only person alive who might know the difference."

Oscar signed. The pen scratched, small and enormous, on the ghost file's ghost line. Outside, the rain kept on, and the bell over the door hung silent, and somewhere across town a man who believed himself an author was waiting, with great patience, for his favorite reader to turn the page.

"Before I tell you about the acrostic," Oscar said, capping the pen, "you should know one more thing. The date in the entries. The one the acrostic points to."

"Ten days from now. I did the arithmetic."

"So did he." Oscar looked up, and for the first time since the bell had rung, she let the bookseller fall away and the detective stand in her place. "He didn't choose that date because it's convenient, Inspector. He chose it because it's the anniversary of the day I withdrew my statement. He's not just inviting me to the cellar." She pushed the ghost file back across the counter. "He's inviting me to the ending. And he's written it so that either I come—and watch—or I don't come, and Beatrix Vane becomes the last correction in his collected works. He's left us exactly one good move, and it's the one that costs us everything."

Adeyemi stood, folding the file into her coat, and at the door she paused with her hand on the bell's cord, not ringing it, just holding it still.

"Thin ice," she said.

"Together," Oscar said. "And quickly."

The bell gave its small bright sound as the door closed, and Oscar stood alone in her shop with the rain on the skylight and ten days on the clock, and began, at last, to plan a story of her own.
