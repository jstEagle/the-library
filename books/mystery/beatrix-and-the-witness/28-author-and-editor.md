## 28. Author and Editor

The water reached the second chair first.

Oscar noticed it the way she noticed everything in her shop—by sound before sight. The drip had changed its rhythm in the last ten minutes, quickened, gone from a metronome to a pulse. Somewhere above them or behind them, a main had let go, and the old registry annex was drinking from it through every crack in sixty years of concrete.

Merrick hadn't noticed. Or he had, and didn't care. He sat across the little table with his spine straight and his hands flat on either side of the diary, a man at a recital he had rehearsed all his life.

"Page one," he said, and opened the cover with two fingers, delicately, the way you'd open a reliquary.

"Tobias," Oscar said. "Before you begin."

His eyes came up. In the lantern light they were pale and patient and utterly without warmth, but there was something underneath the patience—something that leaned forward. She'd seen that lean in forty years of interviews. It was hunger. Not for blood; for attention. For *witness*.

"You're going to read me Beatrix Vane's death," she said. "I'd like to know whose sentence I'm dying inside."

A flicker crossed his face—pleasure, quickly disciplined. "You've always asked the right questions," he said. "That's why I chose you."

"You didn't choose me." She kept her voice level, conversational, the voice she used on customers who wanted to argue about Chandler versus Hammett. "You auditioned for me. Three times. The paper hats. The marginalia you left where I'd find them. You wanted an editor, Tobias, and you never once asked if your work was ready."

The lean deepened. Good. Water crept past the legs of the table, cold against her ankles, soaking into her trousers. She did not look down. Looking down was losing.

"Read," Merrick said, and began.

---

He read well. That was the horror of it. He read the way a man reads scripture he believes—not performed, not theatrical, but *inhabited*. And the words were Beatrix's. Her cadences, her little archivist's tics, the way she dated things twice because certificates had taught her that dates were liars. Oscar had spent weeks inside that diary. She knew the voice like she knew the creak of her own stairs.

*"On the fourth of March I will stop being afraid, because fear is only the body's rough draft of an ending, and tonight the ending is final."*

"No," Oscar said.

Merrick stopped. The word hung between them, small and enormous.

"I'm sorry?" he said, and the politeness in it had edges.

"That line. 'Fear is only the body's rough draft of an ending.'" She shook her head slowly, a shopkeeper declining a trade-in. "It's a beautiful sentence. It's also not hers."

"It is entirely hers. I have studied her for two years. Every entry, every—"

"Every entry you *have*." Oscar leaned back, letting the chair creak, letting herself take up space. "That's the flaw, Tobias. You've studied the archive. But archives are what people wrote when someone else might read it. Beatrix wrote two diaries. Everyone does. There's the one for the shelf and the one for the drawer, and you only ever got the shelf copy. So when you forge her, you forge her *public* voice—and her public voice would never say anything that pretty about fear. She distrusted prettiness. She told me so. Page forty-one. 'Euphemism is how institutions spell cover-up.'"

She watched him go still. Not angry stillness. Scholarly stillness—the stillness of a man who has been handed a correction and cannot yet decide whether it wounds him or delights him.

"She said that to you?"

"In this room, as a matter of fact. Well—" Oscar glanced around the flooded chamber, the brick arches sweating, the table set for two like a dinner party at the bottom of a well. "Not this precisely."

The water made its argument beneath their feet. It had found the low channel now, running toward the old coal chute in a thin black ribbon, and the ribbon was widening. Somewhere overhead, pipes groaned like a ship changing its mind.

"Continue," Merrick said, and turned the page.

---

He read the forged entry of her drowning. He had written it, Oscar realized, in the second person at first and then corrected himself into the first person, and the seams showed to anyone who knew how to look—a sentence that began in terror and ended in acceptance, crossing over mid-paragraph like a swimmer changing strokes. He had given Beatrix Vane a graceful death. He had given her last thoughts a structure: image, reflection, resolution. Beginning, middle, end.

Fiction. All of it fiction. Real deaths were ragged. Oscar had stood beside enough of them to know that nobody's final thought arrives with a resolution. You die mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-*spelling*, sometimes. The neatness was the tell. The neatness was always the tell.

"You've done it again," she said when he paused to turn the page.

"Do what?"

"You've ended her." She tapped the page with one finger, wet to the knuckle now. "Look at the paragraph breaks. Terror, reflection, peace. Three movements. That's a sonnet, Tobias. That's a hymn. Nobody drowns in three movements. Beatrix would have drowned in fragments—she'd have been furious about the date, or thinking about an uncatalogued box, or wondering whether she'd locked the records office. You gave her an arc because you can't help yourself. You're not a murderer who writes. You're a writer who murders, and writers lie. It's the whole profession."

Something moved behind his face then—something that wasn't hunger anymore. It took her a moment to name it, and when she did, her stomach went cold even as the water climbed her shins.

Doubt.

"You're saying it isn't good," he said quietly.

"I'm saying it isn't *true*." She held his gaze. "And you told me once—I believe it was the day you bought three detective novels and flattered my stock—that truth was the only standard. Your words. 'A forgery that convinces is more honest than a fact nobody checks.' Well. I'm checking, Tobias. I'm the only honest reader you ever had, remember? So here is my honest review."

The lantern guttered. Water lapped at the table's edge, rocking the diary a half-inch, and his hand shot out and steadied it—fast, protective, almost tender.

"Your victim doesn't sound alive," Oscar said. "She sounds edited. And your killer—" she nodded at the manuscript, at the shadow-self threaded through every entry, the correcting hand, the patient voice that tidied Beatrix's sentences the way he'd tidied four death certificates—"your killer is the most boring character in the book. Do you know why? Because he's never wrong. A character who's never wrong has no story. No friction. He just... files things." She let the pause stretch. "Even Beatrix saw it. Even under everything, even terrified, she left you an error in the text. Because she understood something you don't."

"What error?" His voice had thinned.

"The misdated certificate. Entry of the ninth of January—you transcribed it faithfully, which means she wrote it, which means she beat you. She put a mistake into your masterpiece, in your own hand, and you never caught it. Your co-author was editing you the entire time." Oscar smiled, and it cost her everything to make it warm. "You didn't write her ending, Tobias. She's been writing yours."

---

He stood up so fast the chair went over backward into the water.

For three seconds—one, two, three—Oscar was certain she had miscalculated, that vanity curdled faster than she could pour it, that the next thing would be the knife she could see now, finally, riding his hip in a leather sheath like a librarian's pen. The water was at her knees. The black ribbon had become a current, tugging at her legs, running somewhere toward the chute, toward the stairs, toward air.

But Merrick didn't draw. He stood with his fists flat on the table and breathed, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd built for him: if she was lying, he could prove it by finishing the reading; if she was telling the truth, then Beatrix Vane had out-authored him, and the only way to answer that was to finish the book *his* way, now, tonight, and make the ending true by force.

Either way, he needed her to listen. Either way, he needed the witness.

"Sit down," he said.

"I am sitting."

"*Listen*, then." He righted the chair, sat, drew the diary close, and his composure reassembled itself piece by piece, though it fit worse now, like a coat taken in too hastily. "You think the error undoes me. It doesn't. Errors are the point. Errors are how I find the pages worth keeping." He turned to the final entry—the real final entry, the one he'd written for tomorrow, for after. "You want truth? Here is the truest thing I've ever written. Listen to how she forgives me."

Oscar listened. And while he read—while his voice rose gentle and terrible over the black water, giving Beatrix Vane a death in three neat movements—she counted.

The drip had become a rush. The current at her knees had opinions now, pushing, insistent, flowing toward the coal chute in the corner where the brickwork was newest, where someone had recently disturbed eighty years of grime. She'd noticed it on the way down. She was almost sure she'd seen the hinge glint.

She kept her eyes on Merrick. She kept her face full of reluctant admiration, the expression of a critic being won over despite herself, and she fed him another correction whenever his pace slowed—*that adverb, cut it; she'd never use "suddenly"—* and each correction bought her thirty seconds, a minute, while the annex drank and drank.

Water at her thighs. The lantern hissed.

Merrick lifted his head from the page, and for the first time since she'd known him, Tobias Merrick looked uncertain of his own ending.

Then, from the darkness above the coal chute, came the scrape of a filed hinge giving way.
