## 33. Testimony

The interview room at Ashcombe Row had been a storage cupboard until someone dragged in a table and two chairs and called it a room. The walls were the color of weak tea. A recorder sat between us, its red light steady as a small patient heart.

Adeyemi set down two paper cups of tea that smelled of tannin and exhaustion. It was four hours since they'd carried Merrick up the annex stairs, his wrists cuffed behind him, water still streaming from his trouser cuffs and pooling on the concrete like he'd brought the cellar with him. It was three hours since a paramedic had wrapped a blanket around Beatrix Vane's shoulders and she had refused, quietly, to let go of Oscar's hand until they reached the ambulance.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Adeyemi said. "You've earned the right to sleep first."

"No." Oscar's voice came out scraped, rusted from disuse. She cleared her throat. "If I sleep, I'll wake up and find a way to make it smaller. I'm very good at making things smaller. I did it for fifteen years."

Adeyemi studied her for a long moment. Then she reached across and pressed RECORD.

"State your name for the record."

"Oscar Marrow Coldharbour. Formerly Detective Chief Inspector, Ashcombe Constabulary. Currently proprietor of Marrow & Page, Booksellers, Wren Lane." She paused. "Formerly many other things."

"And you understand this statement is being taken under caution—"

"I understand it's being taken *finally*," Oscar said. "Which is not the same thing, but close enough. Ask your questions, Inspector. Or better—don't. Let me start at the beginning, because if I wait for your questions I'll answer only what's asked, and answering only what's asked is how I got here."

The red light burned. Adeyemi leaned back, arms folded, and gave her the room.

* * *

Oscar began with the scandal, because everything else grew out of it like rot out of a wound.

"Superintendent Edgar Lyle," she said. "Fifteen years ago. He ran the Major Crime desk, and he ran it the way a man runs a private business—which is to say, for himself. Case outcomes were negotiable. Convictions were commodities. I found out because I found a body that didn't fit the story he'd already sold to the press. A young woman, drowned, ruled suicide. Except she couldn't swim, and there was pondweed under her fingernails, and fingernails are not where you put them when you're drowning yourself."

She turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the tea shiver.

"I built the case properly. Chain of evidence, witnesses, the lot. And then Lyle called me into his office and explained, with great kindness, what would happen to my career, to the careers of everyone who'd worked under me, if I testified. He didn't threaten me directly. He was better than that. He showed me the cost of speaking and let me do the arithmetic myself."

"What did you choose?"

"I chose silence." The word came out flat as a coin on a counter. "I took early retirement. I bought a bookshop with money I'd saved for a house I never bought. And I told myself a beautiful story about it—that I was tired, that I wanted quiet, that books were honest in a way people weren't. All true. None of it the truth. The truth is I traded one woman's justice for my own comfort, and I've been paying interest on that debt ever since, in a currency nobody audits."

The recorder's light held steady. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, soft as a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

Then the three accidents.

"The first was Daniel Okafor, eleven years ago. Fell from the footbridge at Tanner's Reach. Ruled accidental—wet rail, dark night. But Daniel had a fear of heights so severe he used to take the long way to work rather than cross that bridge in daylight. I knew because he told me, once, over a book about canal engineering he never finished reading. I wrote the solution in pencil in the margins of a returned library copy of *Rivers of the District* and posted it back to the library, addressed to no one. Someone must have read it. Nothing happened. I told myself nothing happening was the same as nothing being wrong."

"The second?"

"Marta Ilves. Hit by a car on the ring road, three years later. The driver was never found. I found the driver—or the shape of him—in the pattern of her last week. Again: marginalia, again: silence. The third was Thomas Renwick, eighteen months ago, the canal drowning." Her hands had gone still on the table now, flat, as if pressing something down. "Each time I solved it a little better and said a little less. Each time I congratulated myself on my restraint. Restraint." She laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "I called cowardice by a gentleman's name for fifteen years."

"And the folded paper hats?"

Oscar looked up sharply. "You know about those."

"I read your margins, Ms. Coldharbour. All of them. It took me three nights, but I read them." Adeyemi's voice was careful, professional—and beneath it, something warmer, or at least less cold. "I'm asking what you know."

"They were in all three scenes. Small, folded from newsprint, tucked somewhere the first responders wouldn't look—a drain grate, a wheel well, a culvert lip. I thought they were a mourner's token. Grief does strange, tidy things. It wasn't until Renwick that I understood they were signatures. And it wasn't until last night, in a flooded cellar, with a man holding a knife, that I finally understood what they meant." She exhaled. "Merrick folded them. He told me, while the water rose—he was proud of them. He said a correction isn't complete until it's marked. He killed those three men because each of them, in some small archival way, had made an error he'd made first. Okafor had misfiled a survey that Merrick had indexed wrong. Ilves had flagged a certificate he'd amended. Renwick—" her voice caught, just once, and she steadied it, "—Renwick had corrected a date. That's all. A date. He killed a man over a date, and then he folded a little hat out of the newspaper reporting the death, and he went home satisfied that the record was clean."

The room was very quiet. The red light burned on.

* * *

It took six hours.

Six hours, two more cups of tea gone cold, one break when Oscar's voice failed entirely and she sat with her forehead nearly touching the table, breathing, while Adeyemi pretended to check her notes and gave her ninety seconds of unwatched privacy. Six hours through the diary, the marginalia, the hollowed book, the acrostic spelling CELLAR, the misdated certificate that had saved Beatrix Vane's life—the error in the text, the flaw in the forgery, the small deliberate crack through which a living woman had signaled a living mind.

At several points Adeyemi stopped her. Not to redirect—to confirm. Names, dates, chain of custody, the location of every piece of evidence Oscar had touched before the police had. Adeyemi wrote it all down in her precise, unhurried hand, and twice she said, "That will complicate things for you," and both times Oscar said, "Good."

Near the end, Adeyemi closed her notebook.

"One more thing. Off the record—I'm turning it off for this." She reached over and clicked the recorder. The red light died. "Why now? You had fifteen years of practice at silence. You were good at it. What changed?"

Oscar considered the question honestly, which was more than she'd given most questions in six hours.

"A woman wrote her own name in a book and hid it where only a reader would find it," she said. "And then she stayed alive for two years inside a man's sentence, waiting for someone to read closely enough to notice the grammar was wrong. She did the thing I never did. She spoke, in the only way available to her, and she kept speaking until it worked." She looked at her own hands—ink-stained, older than she felt, steady now in a way they hadn't been in years. "I spent fifteen years telling myself that silence was survival. Beatrix Vane spent two years proving that witness is. I'd like to spend whatever I have left on the right side of that lesson."

Adeyemi nodded slowly. Then she turned the recorder back on.

"For the record," she said. "Do you wish to add anything further?"

"Yes." Oscar straightened in the chair, and something in her spine seemed to settle, vertebra by vertebra, like a book finding its place on a shelf. "For fifteen years I chose silence over duty, and my silence had a body count I am only beginning to count. I offer this statement freely, completely, and under my own name. And I will repeat it in any court, before any jury, as many times as it needs repeating. I am done being a margin note in other people's stories."

She paused.

"That's all. For now."

Adeyemi stopped the recording. 11:47 p.m. Six hours and nine minutes.

* * *

Outside, the rain had stopped. Oscar stood on the steps of Ashcombe Row and breathed air that tasted of wet stone and, faintly, impossibly, of morning. Across the car park, an ambulance idled with its lights off; Beatrix Vane had refused the hospital twice and been overruled twice, but she had made them open a window, and her face appeared at it now—pale, exhausted, unmistakably alive—and she raised one hand, not a wave exactly, more a salute between soldiers.

Oscar raised hers back.

Her phone buzzed. A message from Adeyemi, sent thirty seconds ago, though the woman was still inside:

**Three files reopened at 6 a.m. Families notified by noon. You'll be called to give evidence. Formal summons, probably Monday.**

And then, a second message, arriving as Oscar watched:

**One more thing. When we pulled Merrick's effects, there was a package in his cell belongings. Addressed to you. It's sealed. Do you want it opened, or delivered?**

Oscar stared at the screen for a long moment. Rain dripped from the awning above her, counting seconds.

Somewhere in an evidence locker, in a man's careful handwriting, her name waited on an envelope—written, no doubt, in that same neat, self-satisfied script that had amended certificates and forged diaries and folded little paper hats over the eyes of the dead. He had written to her from the beginning. He would go on writing. Even caged, he considered himself an author, and she, apparently, his readership.

She typed her reply with a thumb that did not shake.

**Open it. Read it first. Then decide whether I ever need to see what's inside.**

She pocketed the phone and walked down Wren Lane toward her shop, past shuttered windows greying with dawn, and did not look back at the police station, or the ambulance, or the fifteen silent years standing behind her like furniture in a room she had finally, at last, moved out of.

Behind her, unbeknownst to her, in the evidence room at Ashcombe Row, DI Ruth Adeyemi slit the envelope open—and went very still at the first line.
