## 3. Three Accidents, One Pattern

The motel room in Harrow Creek had two lamps and Ada had broken one within an hour of arriving, so she worked by the light of a single bulb and the blue glow of her laptop, the way she'd worked every night for eleven years. Evidence spread across the bedspread like a card game she was losing: photocopies, printouts, the diary's scanned pages glowing on her screen with their impossible dates.

She hadn't meant to open the old case files tonight. She'd meant to sleep. But sleep had been unreliable since the box arrived, since the entry dated *June 14, 2000* — three years after Marisol Reyes walked out of Bellwether Lane and into nothing — and now her hands moved on their own, pulling up the folder she kept labeled, with the kind of gallows humor that passes for professionalism in true crime, **CLOSED**.

Three cases. Three accidents. Three times she'd poked at something everyone else called finished, and three times she'd been right.

She laid them out like tarot cards.

*Daniel Okafor, 2009.* Fell from the water tower outside Millbrook, forty miles east of Harrow Creek. Ruled accidental. Except Daniel was afraid of heights — his mother told Ada that in the first interview, weeping, holding a photo of him white-knuckled on a playground ladder at age nine — and except that Daniel had spent the last six months of his life working as a landscaper for a private estate. The estate's deed traced back through two shell companies to Bellwether Holdings.

*Carla Jimenez, 2014.* Drove off Route 9 on a clear October night, no skid marks, blood alcohol zero. The coroner wrote *possible medical event*. Carla had been a bookkeeper until six weeks before her death, when she'd been hired — suddenly, generously, out of nowhere — by a Harrow Creek accounting firm whose largest client was Bellwether Holdings. Her sister told Ada Carla had been "weirdly quiet" those last weeks. Keeping her head down. Counting things she maybe shouldn't have been counting.

*Tomas Reyes, 2018.* No relation to Marisol that anyone could prove, though both families came from the same valley in Oaxaca, and in towns like this, *no relation* often just meant *relation nobody would admit to*. Tomas was a maintenance man at the Bellwether Community Center. He fell down a stairwell that had passed inspection twice that year. His widow took a settlement and left the state and never answered another letter from Ada Vasquez.

Ada sat back on the creaking mattress and looked at the pattern she'd been too busy, too scattered, too *alone* to see before.

Every victim had touched Bellwether Lane. And every death had come within ten days of June 21st.

The summer solstice. The longest day of the year.

The date of the Bellwether anniversary dinner.

Her skin went cold in the stuffy room. She pulled up the dates again, checked them against the town's own promotional materials — the Harrow Creek historical society website, all sepia photos and proud prose — and there it was, printed in cheerful serif font: *Join us each June as the Bellwether family celebrates Founders' Night, honoring 150 years of service to our community.*

Founders' Night. The dinner. The one Edmund Bellwether III still hosted, year after year, the invitation list rumored to be shorter than it used to be.

Three deaths. Three dinners. Three people who had gotten close enough to the family to see something.

Ada thought about the word she'd been using casually for years in her scripts — *pattern* — and felt it change weight in her mouth. A pattern wasn't a story anymore. It was a shape, and shapes had edges, and she was standing inside this one.

She got up, poured the motel's terrible coffee, and made herself say it out loud, because saying things out loud was how she tested them.

"The dinners aren't celebrations." Her voice sounded strange in the empty room. "They're rituals. Rituals of erasure."

Outside, a dog barked once and stopped, as if corrected.

* * *

**June 20, 1997**

Marisol Reyes pressed her cheek against the cool window of the servants' corridor and watched the cars arrive.

They came up the long drive like beetles in the dusk, headlights sweeping the hedges, and she counted them the way she counted everything — a habit from her grandmother, who said numbers were the only thing nobody could take from you. Nine cars. Ten. The Whitfields' green sedan. The police chief's personal truck, which was funny, because why did the chief need an invitation? Wasn't he always invited?

"Marisol." Her aunt Rosa's voice, low and urgent, from the kitchen doorway. "You're on glasses tonight, not windows. Come."

Inside, the ballroom of the Bellwether house smelled like lilies and candle wax and something underneath both, something older, like a cellar. Marisol had worked three Founders' Nights already, carrying trays since she was fourteen, and she knew the choreography: the family at the head table, the toast at nine o'clock sharp, Edmund Bellwether Jr. standing beside his father with that smile that never reached his eyes. Young Edmund was the good one, the town said. The one who'd fought with old Edmund about the mill closing, about the layoffs, about the pension fund that had gone thin as winter soup.

Tonight he looked pale. Tonight, when Marisol refilled his water glass, he glanced up at her — really looked, the way nobody in this house ever looked at the help — and said, very quietly, "Do you know what time it is?"

"Almost nine, sir."

He nodded slowly, like she'd confirmed something dreadful. "Stay near the doors tonight," he said. "If anything happens, stay near the doors."

Then old Edmund rang the bell — an actual bell, a ship's bell mounted on a stand, rung once for every decade the family had owned the valley — and the room went silent, and Marisol slipped back along the wall toward the service door, heart tapping quick and small.

She didn't stay near the doors. That was the thing she would carry for the rest of her life, folded up small and hidden deep: she had been told, and she had not listened, because Rosa called her from the kitchen for one more tray, and Marisol was seventeen and obedient in all the wrong directions.

At nine-oh-four, crossing the back hall with a tray of champagne, she heard the argument through the study door. Two voices. One was young Edmund's, cracking with fury: *"I'm telling them tomorrow. All of them. The fund, the mill, everything — I don't care what it costs us."*

And then the other voice, low and even and terribly calm, and Marisol never forgot it, though for years she would pretend she had: *"Son. Sit down."*

A sound followed. Not loud. A sound like heavy furniture being moved wrong.

Marisol stood frozen in the hallway, tray trembling, candles guttering in the draft from somewhere, and the study door opened six inches, and old Edmund Bellwether looked out at her — looked directly at her, his face composed, his hands clean — and smiled the family smile.

"You," he said pleasantly. "What's your name?"

The truth rose in her throat like floodwater. *Marisol Reyes, seventeen, I saw—*

"Elena," she heard herself say. The first lie of her new life, told before she even understood she would need it.

"Elena," old Edmund repeated, tasting it. "Well, Elena. I believe there are glasses that need polishing."

She turned and walked toward the kitchen, one step, two, three, not running, never running, and behind her she heard the soft click of the study door, and the bell began to ring again — decades ringing out into the summer dark, one for every year the family had owned everything, including, as of four minutes ago, the truth.

* * *

Ada woke at dawn with her laptop open on her chest and the ghost of a dream she couldn't hold — a girl's voice, a name being asked, a lie being born. She sat up, rubbed her face, and told herself it was just the research talking. Just the brain doing its overnight filing.

But her hands were shaking slightly when she reached for the coffee, and she noticed she'd written something in the notebook sometime in the night, in handwriting looser than her own waking hand:

*Ask who served at the dinners. Ask who was in the house.*

She stared at it. Then she flipped back through her notes from yesterday — the interviews, the rehearsed innocence of every resident on Bellwether Lane — and found what she'd written after speaking with the archivist's neighbor, a chatty woman named Dolores: *Staff for gala comes from same families, generation to generation. Reyeses used to do the catering. "Before the trouble."*

Before the trouble. Everyone in Harrow Creek had a *before the trouble*, and nobody would say what the trouble was.

Ada opened her recording software and started a new file. Her voice, when she spoke into the microphone, was steadier than she felt — that was the trick of it, the only trick she knew, you recorded yourself becoming brave.

"For years I've covered cases where someone died and everyone agreed to call it an accident," she said. "I used to think the interesting question was *how*. Now I'm starting to think the interesting question is *when*. Because in Harrow Creek, the calendar isn't just a calendar. It's a schedule."

She paused, listening to the silence of the motel, the hum of the ice machine down the hall.

"June twenty-first. Founders' Night. One hundred and fifty years of the Bellwether anniversary dinner. And I'm beginning to understand that every one of those dinners has a guest list, and a menu, and — if you know where to look — a body count."

She stopped the recording. Saved it. Then she sat very still, because the next thought arrived with the weight of something dropped from a great height:

If the dinners were rituals of erasure, then they required witnesses to erase. And if Marisol Reyes had been in that house in June of 1997 —

Ada grabbed her jacket, her recorder, her keys. There was a retired schoolteacher on her list today, a woman who'd taught half the town, and Ada was done asking gentle questions. She was going to ask about the staff. About the catering. About who stood in the back halls of the Bellwether house with trays in their hands and eyes in their heads.

Somewhere across town, she suspected, a man named Edmund Bellwether III was reviewing his guest list for this year's dinner, and somewhere on it — newly added, in fresh ink — was a name he'd learned only recently.

Hers.

Ada stepped out into the gray morning light of Harrow Creek and locked the door behind her, and for the first time since arriving, she checked the parking lot twice.
