## 5. Entries After the End

The motel room in Harrow Creek had one lamp that buzzed like a trapped insect and a desk chair with a wobble that kept time better than any metronome. Ada Vasquez sat hunched over Marisol Reyes's diary for the fourth night running, a pot of gas-station coffee going cold at her elbow, and let the handwriting tell her what it wanted to tell her.

She'd started with content. That was the podcaster's instinct—mine the words, find the story. But by the third night she'd stopped reading the entries as text and started reading them as *object*, because something about them had been bothering her the way a splinter bothers you only when your hand passes over it: not pain, exactly, just wrongness.

The ink was wrong.

The 1997 entries—the ones written before March 14th, before Marisol walked out of the Bellwether anniversary dinner and out of the world—were written in a blue ballpoint that faded, feathered, aged. Ada had held those pages under the motel's harsh light until her eyes watered. The blue had gone gray-green at the edges, the way old ink does, sunk into paper that had yellowed around it like a bruise healing.

The post-disappearance entries were different. The final entry—dated June 2nd, *2000*, three years after a girl who was never found had supposedly stopped existing—was crisp. Black ink, uniform pressure, sitting on top of the paper rather than in it. Ada wasn't a forensic document examiner. But she'd spent two seasons of her podcast learning just enough of everything to be dangerous, and she knew what she was looking at. She photographed the page against the window light, zoomed until the pixels broke apart, and felt the cold spread through her chest with something adjacent to joy.

Someone had written this recently. Someone had taken a twenty-three-year-old diary and added to it, carefully, in a hand that mimicked Marisol's slanted cursive almost perfectly.

*Almost.*

Ada laid photocopies of every entry across the bedspread in chronological order, a paper river flowing from 1994 to 2000. And once she stopped looking at what the words said and started looking at how they moved across the page, the river split into two currents.

Marisol's early entries leaned hard to the right, letters crowded, ascenders tall and impatient—a girl writing fast because life was happening faster than her pen. The later entries were slower. Controlled. The same vocabulary, the same pet phrases—*Mama says*, *the mill whistle*, *Dios mío* dropped in where English failed her—but the rhythm underneath was different. It was the difference between a voice and an impression of a voice. Between a person and someone doing their best, year after year, to keep a dead girl talking.

Ada sat back in the wobbling chair and pressed both hands over her mouth.

Because that was the thing she couldn't stop thinking about now that she'd seen it. Whoever wrote these entries hadn't done it once. They'd done it over *years*. Dated them forward, spaced them out, matched the ink to the era, matched the hand to the girl. This wasn't a forgery committed in an afternoon. This was devotion with a deadline. This was someone keeping a case alive on a drip feed, one impossible entry at a time, knowing—or hoping—that someday, someone like her would come along and notice that the dead don't keep diaries, and start asking why.

*Why would someone do that?*

Ada looked at the wall above the desk, where she'd pinned her index cards in a chain of string and thumbtacks that would have embarrassed a real detective and made perfect sense to anyone who'd ever loved a mystery. MARISOL REYES, 17, last seen March 14, 1997, Bellwether Lane. And beneath it, newly written, in her own blunt capitals:

**WHO LOVED HER ENOUGH TO LIE FOR SEVEN YEARS?**

* * *

**June 2nd, 2000**

They say if I write in her voice long enough I will forget my own. Maybe that's true. Some nights I finish an entry and have to say my own name out loud just to remember which sister I am.

But nobody reads these pages unless they're already asking questions, and so I write for the asker. Whoever you are, someday, holding this book: she didn't run away. Marisol did not run away. Say it however you need to say it to make people listen. Put it in whatever voice will carry.

I am seventeen years younger than my sister and I have spent three years being her ghost. The Bellwethers think the story closed when the search parties stopped. Let them think it. A door that everyone believes is locked is the easiest door in the world to open.

If you are reading this, then the door is open. Come through.

—L.

* * *

Lucia Reyes had been thirteen years old in the spring of 1997, and Ada found her the way you find most ghosts—not through records but through residue. A mention in a 1998 newspaper feature about the vanished girl's family ("the youngest Reyes child, Lucia, 14, keeps her sister's memory alive at Holy Trinity"). A yearbook photo from Harrow Creek High, class of 2001: a thin girl with Marisol's same dark eyes and none of Marisol's ease, standing slightly outside the frame of her own photograph, as if she'd learned early that edges were safer than centers.

And then nothing. No college records, no marriage license, no obituary. Lucia Reyes evaporated from Harrow Creek in 2004, seven years after her sister, and no one Ada called seemed able to say when or why.

"She went off to live with an aunt," said the woman at the county clerk's office, in the same smooth tone everyone in this town used, the tone of a sentence rehearsed in front of a mirror. "Out west somewhere."

"Which aunt?"

A pause exactly one beat too long. "I couldn't tell you, hon."

Everyone here had rehearsed their innocence. But Lucia hadn't rehearsed anything. Lucia had left a trail—and she'd left it in the only place she could be sure no Bellwether would ever look: inside a dead girl's diary, written in a dead girl's hand.

Because that was the other thing Ada had figured out, sitting cross-legged on the motel bed with the photocopies fanned around her like tarot cards. The post-disappearance entries weren't just forged. They were *coded*. Not with any cipher you'd find in a puzzle book—with intimacy. Lucia had used her sister's private language, the shorthand of sisters, the references only the two of them would understand, and buried the real story in plain sight beneath a surface story any casual reader would accept.

The surface entries told a simple tale: Marisol, alive somewhere, writing home in secret. *I can't say where I am. I'm safe. Don't look for me.* A runaway's letter stretched across years—the kind of thing a tired police department would read once and use to close a file.

But read it the way a sister would read it—read it the way Lucia had *written* it—and another story surfaced like a body rising in a pond.

* * *

**November 9th, 1999**

*Mama says the mill whistle sounds different in winter. Shorter. Like even the mill gets tired of holding its breath.*

I know what you'll hear when you read this, Marisol, if there's any heaven and you're in it: I know you'll hear me telling the truth sideways, the way we used to pass notes in church, folded so small they fit inside a hymnal. So listen sideways.

You saw what happened at the dinner. You told me before they took you away—you came to my window at four in the morning with your coat over your pajamas and your face white as the moon, and you said, *Lulu, I saw Mr. Bellwether kill his son,* and I laughed because I was thirteen and stupid, and you didn't laugh. You never laughed. You said, *They're going to say he had a bad heart. Everyone will say it. But I was in the hall and I saw his hands.*

Two days later you were gone and the whole town said runaway, and Mama cried for a month, and Papa stopped speaking entirely, and the Bellwether family sent flowers to our house with a card signed in beautiful fountain-pen script that said *Our deepest sympathies during this difficult time* — sympathies, Marisol, for a family whose daughter ran away, as if they were mourning us. As if they knew exactly what they'd done to us.

They put you somewhere. I don't know where. Ruthie from the clinic disappeared the same week and I heard Papa whisper to Mama that the Bellwethers "take care of their problems," and I understood that he meant you were still a problem, still breathing, and that they had solved you the way they solve everything — quietly, generously, with money and distance and new names.

So I write these entries. Every few months, when it's safe, when Papa is asleep. I make you alive on paper because the police need you alive on paper to keep looking, and if they ever stop looking, they win. I dated the first one wrong on purpose — I wrote it too neat, too calm, and I've gotten better since. Practice. Isn't that the awful joke? I've had practice being you.

Mr. Bellwether killed his son at the anniversary dinner and called it a heart attack, and the doctor who signed the paper drives a car the Bellwethers bought him, and the deputy who took the call got promoted within the year, and you SAW IT, and they couldn't kill you twice so they buried you alive instead.

I'm going to find where they put you. I swear it on Mama's rosary. And when I do, I'm going to write one last entry in this book in MY OWN handwriting, with MY OWN name, and it's going to be the truest thing anyone ever wrote in Harrow Creek.

Wait for me. Wherever you are, whoever they made you — wait for me.

—L.

* * *

Ada read the November 1999 entry eleven times.

The first times, she read it as evidence. The Bellwethers. The dinner. The son—Edmund Jr., dead at forty-one in 1997, heart attack, per every record the town kept, and Ada had already pulled those records, had already noticed that the attending physician, a Dr. Alan Pruitt, had retired suddenly in 1998 and moved to Scottsdale, and that the deputy who'd handled the original missing-persons call, a Dale Merrick, had been promoted to sheriff within eighteen months of a case he'd essentially closed in a weekend.

Then she read it as a human document, and that was worse. That was the version that made her take off her glasses and press the heels of her hands into her eyes until she saw stars. A thirteen-year-old girl, alone in a town that had swallowed her sister whole, spending her adolescence impersonating the dead to keep a investigation breathing. Keeping a candle lit in a room where everyone else had agreed the dark was fine.

*The diary was never evidence,* Ada thought. *It was a scream. Seven years of screaming into a book, in a stolen voice, because no one would listen to her own.*

And then the thought arrived that she'd been circling all night, the one that made her stomach drop through the motel floor and into the dark hillside below:

The entries stopped in 2000. The last one was dated June 2nd, 2000. But Lucia didn't disappear until 2004. Four years. Four years of silence in a diary that had been her lifeline, her weapon, her vigil.

Unless the silence meant she'd found something. Unless the silence meant she'd gotten close enough that writing became dangerous. Unless the girl who had spent seven years pretending to be a ghost had finally attracted the attention of the people who made ghosts.

Ada grabbed her phone and pulled up the file she'd started on Lucia Reyes: the yearbook photo, the newspaper clipping, the aunt-out-west line that no one could source. Then she opened a fresh search tab, fingers moving fast, and typed the query she should have typed days ago.

*LUCIA REYES HARROW CREEK MISSING 2004*

The results loaded. There were only three, and the first two were digitized microfiche from the *Harrow Creek Gazette*—tiny, blurry scans she enlarged until her screen filled with gray type.

**REYES SISTER LEAVES TOWN, FAMILY DECLINES COMMENT**, August 2004.

And beneath it, from October of the same year, a follow-up piece barely two paragraphs long, the kind of article written so no one would read it:

*Lucia Reyes, 21, formerly of Harrow Creek, has not been seen since late August, according to relatives. Police state there is no indication of foul play and note that Ms. Reyes, as an adult, is free to travel. A spokesperson for the Bellwether Charitable Trust declined comment.*

*A spokesperson for the Bellwether Charitable Trust declined comment.*

Ada stared at that sentence until the buzzing lamp seemed to dim around it. Twenty-one years old. Vanished four years after her last diary entry. And the town's ruling family—the family whose name was on the library, the clinic, the mill itself—had been asked for comment on a missing waitress's sister, and had *declined*, and the Gazette had printed the decline like it was weather.

They'd erased Marisol. Then, when the little sister kept scratching at the seal, they'd erased her too.

Ada stood up so fast the chair went over backward and clattered against the radiator. She paced the narrow room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, her breath loud in her ears. Her instinct—the well-worn instinct of two seasons of true crime—was to pack up, to protect the files, to get out of a town that had now eaten two Reyes women and showed every sign of having room for a third.

But underneath the fear, steady as a pilot light, was the other thing. The thing that had brought her here. Somewhere out beyond the edge of everything Ada could prove, there might be a woman in her seventies living under a name that wasn't hers, who had watched a man die at a dinner party and been buried alive for it in politeness and paperwork. And somewhere there might be answers about a second woman who had screamed for seven years into a borrowed voice and then gone silent.

Both of them might be alive. Both of them might be reachable. And tomorrow—she checked the calendar on her phone and her throat tightened—tomorrow was Sunday, and two towns over, in a place called Millbrook, there was a retired nurse named Ruth whom the 1997 records listed as employed at Harrow Creek Clinic until March of that year, after which her employment history simply *stopped*.

Ruthie from the clinic. *Ruthie from the clinic disappeared the same week.*

Ada swept the photocopies into their folder, pocketed the diary, and killed the buzzing lamp. In the dark, the index card on the wall caught the parking-lot light through the curtains, and her own block capitals seemed to glow:

**WHO LOVED HER ENOUGH TO LIE FOR SEVEN YEARS?**

Tomorrow she would drive out to Millbrook and knock on Ruth's door. Tomorrow she would ask a frightened old woman to tear open a wound twenty-six years old.

She just didn't know yet that before she ever reached Millbrook, someone would already be waiting for her on the ridge road—and that her rental car's brakes were about to become the least reliable thing in Harrow Creek besides its conscience.
