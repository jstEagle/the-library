## 5. What Bram Kept

The verger's quarters sat above the vestry, a narrow room the width of a confessional and twice as long, reached by a stone stair worn into a shallow smile by two centuries of climbing feet. Vesper had been up here perhaps six times in eleven years—once to deliver a parcel of new cassocks, once to retrieve a misfiled key ring, three times for tea. Bram had kept his door open to her the way he kept everything open: without ceremony, without suspicion, as if openness were simply what a man did with his life.

Now she stood at the threshold with Wren's card in her pocket and the detective's warning in her ears—*stay off it*—and understood that she had already decided to disobey him, and had probably decided the moment he said it.

The room smelled of him still. Beeswax and boot polish, the ghost of strong tea gone cold in its pot, and beneath it all the faint mineral breath of the cathedral itself, which seeped into everything on this side of the close like damp into plaster. His bed was made with hospital corners; his one chair sat squared to the desk; his black shoes waited side by side beneath the washstand, polished to the dull gleam of pewter. A man who ordered his small space so carefully. A man who had died face-down in the flooded crypt with his torch still burning, its light going out only when the water found it.

She stood there longer than she meant to. Grief, she had learned, was best handled the way she handled fragile documents—with dry hands, at arm's length, one page at a time. But grief did not obey archival protocol. It sat down uninvited in Bram's squared-off chair and looked at her with his patient eyes.

"You knew," she said aloud, to the empty room. "You knew something."

* * *

She began where an archivist begins: not with the obvious places but with the wrong ones. The desk drawer held nothing but what it should—candles, a spare pair of spectacles, a tin of mints gone soft. The wardrobe held cassocks and, folded into the pocket of the oldest one, a bus timetable from 2019, which told her nothing except that Bram had once considered leaving and had never done it. She noted the date anyway. Archivists note everything; it is both gift and affliction.

It was the Bible that betrayed him—or rather, the shelf that held it. The books were arranged by height, which was unlike Bram, who filed everything by logic. She pulled them out one by one and found why: behind them, set into the wall in a recess that had probably once housed a saint's statue before the Reformation took its head off, was a biscuit tin.

A biscuit tin. Of course. He was a verger; he thought in practical containers. She lifted the lid with the same care she'd give a folio from 1400.

Inside: a notebook, cheap and spiral-bound, the kind sold in the stationer's on the corner for two pounds fifty. A torch battery. And a photograph she did not recognize—a young man in choir dress, laughing, caught mid-turn, his surplice slightly askew. On the back, in faded ballpoint: *Danny, Easter 1987.*

Vesper sat down on Bram's bed because her legs suggested it, and opened the notebook.

The first pages were mundane—verger's notes, service timings, a reminder about the boiler. Then, halfway through, the handwriting changed. It tightened. It became the handwriting of a man writing things he did not want to be true.

*Aldhelm's crypt floods. Always has. So does the record office, apparently.*

She turned the page.

*I know what she does. I've known since the Hollis business.*

Vesper's breath went thin.

The Hollis case. Three years ago. An elderly bell-ringer found dead at the bottom of the tower stair, ruled a fall, the inquest taking forty minutes. Vesper had spent four nights with the maintenance logs and the duty rosters and had established, quietly, privately, in a file no one would ever read, that the stair rail had been loosened—and that only one person had access to the scaffold clamp needed to do it. She had never told anyone. Not the police. Not the dean. Not even, she had thought, God, though she supposed she'd told Him in her own way, every night, in the language of ledgers.

*I know what she does,* Bram had written. *I've known since the Hollis business. She thinks nobody sees her. Everybody sees her; they just don't say. The Dean sees her most of all. Watches her work like a gardener watching a cutting take root.*

Her hand had gone cold around the notebook. She read on.

*Three of them now. Hollis. Mrs. Pargeter. The young curate, Whatley. All accidents. All filed away. She solved them all without telling a soul, and I don't know whether to be proud of her or frightened for her, and I've decided to be both.*

Mrs. Pargeter—the organist's widow, dead of an insulin miscalibration two years back. Whatley—the curate who fell from the triforium gallery eighteen months ago, whose death Vester had unraveled through a single anomalous entry in the cleaning rotation. Three cases. Her three cases, laid out in Bram's blunt schoolboy hand like specimens pinned to a board.

He had known. For years, he had known, and he had never said a word—not to warn her off, not to turn her in, not even to ask her how she did it. He had simply watched over her the way he watched over the building, silently, thoroughly, with a torch and a ladder and no expectation of thanks.

Betrayal rose in her first, hot and irrational—he had spied on her, he had catalogued her secret like an accession number—and then it collapsed under its own weight, because this was Bram, and Bram did not spy. Bram guarded. He had found her secret and built a wall around it and stood on the wall, and she had walked past him every day for years without knowing the wall was there.

Tenderness came then, and it hurt worse than the betrayal would have.

* * *

The later pages changed again. The entries grew shorter, harder, pressed into the paper as though the pen were being driven by something other than ink.

*Went to see the diocesan records. There's a fourth. Older. Sealed file, 1989. A boy called Daniel Ferrier—organ scholar. Drowned in the lower crypt when the tide came in early. Ruled misadventure. But the tide doesn't come in early. Tides keep appointments better than bishops do.*

Vesper's mouth went dry. She looked at the photograph in the tin—*Danny, Easter 1987*—and felt the room tilt very slightly, the way the cathedral floor seemed to tilt sometimes in old engravings, perspective drawn by someone who loved the building more than geometry.

Bram had known Danny Ferrier. Had kept his photograph in a biscuit tin behind his Bible for thirty-odd years.

*Asked the archive for the file. Refused—sealed by order of the coroner's office, signatory redacted. Asked the Dean about Danny. He went very quiet and very kind, which is what he does instead of answering. Said the boy's death was a tragedy that made the cathedral stronger, which is a sentence that should never be said about anything.*

*Someone loosened the flood gate. Same trick as the tower stair. Same trick as the insulin. Whoever it is has been doing this a long time, and they're still here, and they wear a cassock every day.*

*I think I know who. I don't want to know who. Tomorrow I'll check the tide tables against the maintenance ledger and if I'm right I'll go to her and we'll decide together what to do. She'll know what to do. She always knows what to do.*

The last entry was dated the day before Bram died.

*Ledger confirms it. Going down tonight to photograph the gate mechanism before the spring tide destroys it. If anything happens to me, tell Vesper. She'll understand. Tell her it was never her secret alone.*

Vesper closed the notebook and held it against her chest and let herself, for exactly one minute, be a woman crying in a dead man's room above a dead man's church.

Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, because discipline was armor and armor did not weep, and she looked at what remained.

The photograph. The battery. And, at the bottom of the tin, a single torn scrap of paper, water-stained, that must have been in his pocket when the crypt took him. On it, in his hand, half-legible:

*...gate mechanism — initials scratched on housing — C.H.—*

C.H.

She sat very still. Outside, the cathedral bells began the quarter-chime, and the sound came up through the floor and through her bones, and she thought: *the Dean's name is Corliss Hale.* She thought: *thousands of people have those initials.* She thought: *I will not leap. I am an archivist. I follow evidence, not echoes.*

She thought all of these things in perfect order, like a well-run filing system.

And underneath the filing system, in the dark sub-basement of herself where she kept the things she refused to accession, something whispered that she had known since the word *kind*, since the Dean's voice on the phone asking his oddly gentle questions about her archive work, since the day Bram died and Corliss Hale had put his hand on her shoulder and said, *We'll get through this together, my dear. Whatever this turns out to be.*

Whatever this turns out to be. As if he already knew what it would be.

She repacked the tin exactly as she had found it, except for the notebook, which she wrapped in her scarf and carried out against her ribs like something rescued from a fire. At the door she paused and looked back at the neat room, the squared chair, the waiting shoes.

"Thank you," she said to Bram, to the room, to whatever part of him lingered in the beeswax smell. "And I'm sorry. Both. In that order."

On the stair going down, the notebook pressed warm against her side, and she was already composing the next step the way she composed everything: tide tables, maintenance ledgers, the flooded lower crypt, and the arithmetic of water. Because the letter writer had pointed her toward the tide, and Bram had pointed her toward the tide, and two roads converging on the same drowned place meant the place mattered.

Spring low tide was in four days. And somewhere below the nave, behind a flood gate with initials scratched into its housing, the evidence was waiting for the water to give it up.
