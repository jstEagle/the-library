## 8. Low Water

The tide table said 4:47 a.m., and Detective Inspector Wren had learned, in twenty-two years of policing a city built on mud and prayer, that tide tables did not lie. People lied. Clocks lied. Alibis lied with the fluency of choirboys. But the sea kept its accounts with an honesty that bordered on the cruel, and at 4:47 on the morning after the spring equinox, it would pull back from St. Aldhelm's lower crypt and show him whatever it had been holding all winter.

He stood at the top of the external stair in the dark, breath fogging, a torch in one hand and a warrant folded in his inside pocket like a guilty letter. The cathedral rose above him against a sky the color of wet slate, its spire a needle stitching cloud to earth. Somewhere up there, behind the locked doors of the archive, Vesper Ishikawa was presumably awake too. She had struck him as the sort of woman who slept the way cats did—one ear always open for the sound of something being filed incorrectly.

"Detective Inspector."

The night verger—Bram Oduya's replacement, a nervous young man named Pellow who kept wringing his hands as though trying to return them to their owner—held the door at the bottom of the tower.

"You're sure about this, sir? The steps down to the sump are... they're not really steps anymore. More of a suggestion."

"That's why I brought these." Wren lifted one foot to display a pair of thigh-high waders, rubberized and ancient, borrowed from the harbor master's store. "And this." He patted the coil of rope over his shoulder. "And a very strong opinion that I'd rather not fall into standing water alone."

Pellow almost smiled. It looked painful, like a man testing a bruise. "Mr. Oduya used to go down there, you know. After every big tide. He said the crypt breathed."

"Breathe," Wren repeated.

"In and out. With the water. He said if you listened long enough you could hear it thinking."

Wren had liked Bram Oduya, in the limited way he liked most witnesses: thoroughly, briefly, and from a safe distance. The verger had been found three weeks ago at the base of the bell tower stairs, neck broken, a mop bucket overturned beside him in a spreading pool that the coroner had called *consistent with a slip*. Consistent. The word did a lot of quiet work in that report. Wren had read enough reports to know when consistency was doing someone's thinking for them.

And then there was the letter. Postmarked the day *after* the funeral, claiming responsibility, addressed not to the police but to the archivist. To Vesper Ishikawa by name, in handwriting so careful it might have been engraved. Someone out there wasn't confessing. Someone was corresponding.

"Right," Wren said. "If I'm not up in two hours, send someone who's better at this than I am."

*

The stair down into the lower crypt was cut into the cliff-side of the old Norman foundation, and it descended through forty years of damp into a darkness that smelled of stone, salt, and something older and sweeter underneath—the smell of a church that had been quietly rotting since before anyone alive was born. His torch beam slid across walls furred with green-black mold, across the ribs of a vaulted ceiling dripping in slow metronome, across the water itself.

At low water, the sump was exposed: a shallow basin maybe four meters square at the lowest point of the crypt floor, where the drainage of centuries collected before finding its way out through a culvert toward the harbor. In winter, with the springs running high, it filled to the waist. Tonight, with the equinox pulling the harbor dry, it held perhaps thirty centimeters of black water over a floor of silt and fallen masonry.

Wren clicked on his headlamp, stepped down into the basin, and felt the cold close around his legs like a verdict.

The water was warmer than the air. That was the first thing. The second thing was the silence—not true silence, but the layered hush of a place holding its breath: drips counting somewhere in the dark, the far-off groan of the culvert taking its slow meal, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse under a wrist, the sound of the sea working at the foundations. The crypt breathing. Bram had been right. It was unsettling how right the dead man kept being.

He worked methodically, because method was all he had ever trusted. Torch left, torch right, silt probe—a telescopic rod with a blunt steel tip, the kind dredger crews used—pushed into the ooze in overlapping sweeps. The sump was perhaps knee-deep in soft sediment above the original flagstones, and things lived in it: bottles, a bicycle bell, a doll's porcelain arm that made him jerk the probe back like a man bitten. He catalogued each find aloud, voice flat in the dark, because speaking kept the animal part of his brain quiet.

Forty minutes in, the probe struck something that was neither stone nor bottle nor bone.

It gave differently. A dense, yielding resistance, wrapped in something fibrous. Wren crouched, plunged both arms into the black water to the elbow, and worked the object free of the silt with the patience of a surgeon delivering something breach.

A satchel. Leather, once brown, now the color of tea left standing. Buckles fused with corrosion but not destroyed. He turned it over in the torchlight, heart doing something unprofessional in his chest, and saw stitched into the flap, in thread gone pale with age and immersion:

*B. O.*

Bram Oduya's bag. The bag that had vanished from the vestry the night he died. The bag the first responding officers had noted as missing and then, in the way of such notes, forgotten.

Hope arrived in Wren's chest like a fist unclenching. Because Bram Oduya had been a careful man—a verger's carefulness, the care of someone whose whole job was making sure nothing was lost, nothing left burning, nothing left unlocked—and a careful man who suspected something would write it down. Would keep a notebook. And a notebook, in a satchel, sunk in a sump only accessible at spring low tide, was evidence with a pulse.

He carried it up the stair cradled against his chest like an infant, set it on a tarpaulin spread over the flagstones of the mid-level landing, and photographed it from six angles before touching another buckle. Procedure. Procedure was a kind of prayer; you performed it correctly and sometimes the universe answered.

The buckles fought him. He used a penknife's tip, swore once with feeling, and got the flap open.

Inside, wrapped in a plastic evidence bag that Bram himself had evidently sourced—he'd been thorough, God rest him—was a notebook. A5, hardbound, marbled cover swollen with damp but intact. Wren eased it out, opened it to the first page, and read:

*If anything happens to me, this is what I know.*

His breath went out of him slowly. The handwriting was Bram's: round, unhurried, the script of a man who wrote sermons' worth of notices on a board every week and never smudged them.

*V. thinks she is invisible. She is not. I have watched her work for five years. Three deaths the police called accidents were not accidents, and she knows it, and she has never told anyone, because she thinks the truth is safer in the dark. She is wrong. The truth in the dark is food.*

Wren sat down slowly on a fallen block of masonry, heedless of the water soaking through his waders, and read on.

Page after page. Bram had reconstructed her methods—the cross-referencing of burial registers against coroner's dates, the pattern of parish visits before each death, the small administrative fingerprints that killers leave because they cannot help signing their work. He had done it without her knowledge, from the outside, the way Wren himself had half-suspected and never proved. Two watchers, watching the same woman, neither telling the other.

*There is a fourth name,* the notebook said, near the end. *Older than hers. Older than me. I have the file reference. I will tell her when I am sure. If I am sure too late, then whoever reads this should look where the ink has gone through.*

Then the pages stopped.

Not ended—stopped. Torn out. Wren thumbed forward and found the stubs: eight, nine, ten pages ripped cleanly along the binding gutter, torn by someone who knew how notebooks came apart and wanted no ragged edges. The final surviving page carried a partial impression, ghosted into the sheet beneath, of handwriting pressed hard enough to leave its shadow—but the words themselves were gone, carried off in someone's pocket.

Someone had reached the sump first.

The thought landed with physical weight. Wren looked at the satchel again—at the corrosion on the buckles, at the silt line dried along the leather—and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. The bag had been down here since the night Bram died. Three weeks. But the notebook's wrapping had been opened and resealed, hastily, the tape misaligned. The torn pages were recent. The paper at the tear-points was still white, still raw.

Within the last few days. Perhaps within the last day or two. Which meant the killer knew about the sump, knew about the tide tables, knew about spring lows—and had come down here in the dark, alone, with a knife or a steady hand, and taken exactly what mattered and left exactly what didn't.

Left, in fact, enough to be found. Enough to be *found by Vesper Ishikawa*, if the letters were any guide—or by the police, if she handed them over. Either way, a message. *I was here first. I am always here first.*

Wren closed the notebook with exaggerated gentleness, as though it might bruise. Above him, through forty feet of stone, he could hear the cathedral beginning its day—a door, a cough, the first tentative notes of someone practicing on the organ, the sound falling down through the building like light through water.

He thought about Vesper Ishikawa receiving a letter postmarked the day after her friend's funeral, and about how she must have felt reading it, and about the fact that she had not come to him with everything. He was sure of that now. People who withheld came to interviews with their hands folded just so, and she had folded hers like a woman keeping a ledger balanced.

*She is wrong,* Bram had written. *The truth in the dark is food.*

And something, down here in the breathing dark, had been feeding on it for a very long time.

Wren stood, sealed the notebook into an evidence bag of his own, and climbed toward the gray morning light—already composing, in his head, the conversation he did not want to have: the one where he told the archivist that her friend had known her secret all along, and asked her, finally, to stop being invisible.

Because the torn pages weren't just a loss. They were a countdown. Whoever took them would soon learn whether they'd missed something—and the next letter, Wren was certain, was already in the post.
