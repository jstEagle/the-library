## 10. Confession, Second Letter

The morning after the tide table, Vesper Ishikawa woke with salt in her dreams and the taste of iron at the back of her throat. She had slept badly—three hours, perhaps four—in the narrow flat above the vestry where she had lived for eleven years, her window facing not the city but the cathedral's north wall, so that even in sleep she was oriented by stone. She had dreamed of water climbing stairs. She had dreamed of Bram's voice reading aloud from a book she could not see, patient as a lesson, while the ink ran off the pages like rain down glass.

She dressed in the dark out of habit. Wool stockings, the grey dress with the buttons she never had to look at, her hair pinned back with the same tortoiseshell combs she had worn since her first week at St. Aldhelm's, when Dean Hale had told her that an archivist's appearance should be as unremarkable as her finding aids—that people should remember what she showed them and never her. *Invisibility is a discipline, Miss Ishikawa,* he had said, and she had taken it for kindness then, and took it for kindness still, though the word had begun to sit differently on her tongue these last few days, the way a familiar coat sits differently when you suspect someone has been through its pockets.

The letter was waiting for her.

Not at the chapter house post tray, where all official correspondence arrived, sorted by the sacristan's careful hand. Not slipped under her door. It was inside the archive itself—inside the reading room, weighted down on her own desk beneath the brass paperweight shaped like a mitre that Hale had given her the Christmas of her third year, the one she used to hold down documents too fragile for clips.

She stood in the doorway of the reading room for a long moment before she let herself cross the threshold.

The room smelled as it always did: vellum and dust and the faint camphor of the conservation cupboard, the ghost of beeswax from the floor she polished herself every Thursday. Morning light came through the east lancet in two long blades that lay across the reading tables like drawn swords. Everything was exactly where it should be. The document trays aligned. The foam wedges supporting the open folios at their prescribed angles. The humidity gauge reading fifty-four percent, written in her own hand on the little card by the door at seven o'clock yesterday evening, when she had locked up, checked the lock twice, and hung the key ring on the hook inside the safe in the vestry corridor—a safe with a combination known to three living people, and one of them was dead.

Three people. She counted them again, standing there in the cold light, because counting was what she did when the world misfiled itself. Herself. Dean Hale. And Bram Oduya, who had carried his key on the same ring as his verger's keys for nine years, who had died eleven days ago, whose funeral she had attended eight days ago, and whose key—the police had confirmed this to Wren, and Wren had told her with the particular flatness he reserved for facts he didn't like—had been found in the pocket of the cassock they'd dressed him in for burial.

So the key was in the ground. The safe was intact; she had checked it before dawn, walking the corridor in her stockinged feet like a thief in her own house. The lock on the archive door showed no scratching, no forcing. The alarm log—she kept her own parallel log, because she trusted no system she did not personally witness—showed nothing between her departure at 7:14 yesterday evening and her arrival at 5:52 this morning.

And yet.

The envelope lay on her desk under the mitre paperweight, square and heavy, cream laid paper, addressed in a hand she now knew almost intimately: the small, upright, unhurried script of the first confession letter, the one postmarked the day after Bram's funeral for a death that had occurred days before it. No stamp this time. No postmark. It had not come through any mail. It had been *placed*.

Vesper did not touch it at once. She made herself do the work first, because the work was the armor and the armor was holding, barely. She photographed the envelope in situ from four angles with the little camera she used for condition reports. She noted the position of the paperweight relative to the desk edge—eleven centimeters, matching the placement of the first letter's arrival, which meant the sender had set it down deliberately, had wanted her to know it was deliberate. She checked the reading room door's hinges for oil, the floor for scuffing in the dust along the aisle between the stacks. Nothing. Whoever had come had come with the same discipline she practiced, had left the room cleaner than most visitors ever managed, had walked where she walked.

Then she opened it.

Inside was a single sheet, folded once, and she recognized the paper before she read a word: the same cream laid stock, the same weight, from the same box that sat in the stationery cupboard of the deanery office. She had catalogued that box herself. Ream forty-seven of the 2019 consignment, watermarked with a fleur-de-lis that caught the light at a certain angle. She tilted the page. The fleur-de-lis shone.

*Dear Miss Ishikawa,* the letter began, in that small upright hand.

*You will forgive me for not posting this one. I thought, after our last exchange, that you would prefer the personal touch. You are, after all, a great believer in provenance.*

*You keep your notes in the green ledger, third shelf of the case cabinet, behind the 1970s faculty correspondence. You write in pencil because you say pencil forgives, though I notice you have begun using ink for the entries that matter most. On the fourth of March, at 11:40 in the evening, you wrote: "Bram asked again about the organist. He has found something in the music library. He would not say what. His hands were shaking. I have never seen Bram afraid."*

*On the eleventh of March, 12:15 a.m.: "Wren suspects me. He said 'you find things, Miss Ishikawa, that trained officers miss.' I laughed and he did not."*

*On the nineteenth of March, 9:05 p.m.: "I am beginning to think the accidents are not accidents. I am beginning to think they never were. God forgive me for seeing it and saying nothing for five years."*

*I could go on. There are sixty-one entries. I have read them all.*

*You will want to know how. You will check the safe, the locks, the alarm log, and you will find everything in order, because order is what I promised you years ago, isn't it? Order, and protection, and time to do your quiet work. You trusted the walls, Vesper. But I built the walls.*

*The next spring tide is in six days. The sea keeps better records than either of us. Come alone, or bring your detective—it changes nothing. What the water holds, it held before any of us learned to read.*

*Yours in the truth we both serve,*
*A Reader*

Vesper read it three times. Her hands did not shake until the third reading, and then they shook so badly she had to set the page down on the foam wedge and press her palms flat against the cold oak of the reading table, breathing the way she had taught herself to breathe during the panic attacks of her twenties—in for four, hold for seven, out for eight—while the two blades of morning light lay across the backs of her hands like something judging her.

He had quoted her notes. Verbatim. Word for word, including the timestamps, including the private abbreviations, including the sentence about laughing at Wren that she had written knowing no living soul would ever read it, written in the shorthand of a woman talking to herself in the dark because the dark was the only confessor she could afford.

Her notes were not filed in any system. They were not digitized, not indexed, not entered into the catalogue. The green ledger lived behind the faculty correspondence on the third shelf of the case cabinet, and the case cabinet stood in the inner stack, and the inner stack was behind the reading room, and the reading room was behind two locked doors and a keypad whose code she had changed after Bram's death—or believed she had changed—and the whole architecture of her secrecy was a thing she had constructed over eleven years with the paranoid loving care of a woman building a ship inside a bottle.

And he had walked through it as if it were a corridor.

*I built the walls.*

She thought of Hale's hands on the ladder in the crypt last month, steadying it while she climbed to the high shelf. She thought of him asking, over tea in the deanery, seemingly idle questions: *Do you still keep that old ledger? I've often wondered what an archivist writes when no one's watching.* She had laughed and deflected, because deflecting was her native language. She thought of the way he had appeared in the archive doorway at odd hours these past weeks—not often, not suspiciously often, just often enough, each visit carrying some plausible errand: a request for the consecration rolls, a question about the memorial plaques, once merely to stand in the reading room and look at the light and say, *You keep this place beautifully, Vesper. Better than I deserve.*

Better than I deserve.

She pressed her hands harder against the table. The wood was cold enough to hurt, and she let it hurt, because pain was data and data was preferable to the other thing rising in her chest, which was not grief and not fear but something older and more animal: the vertigo of a woman discovering that the room she thought was hers has been someone else's all along.

Because there was another possibility. She made herself consider it, made herself run the alternative hypothesis the way she would run it on any document of doubtful provenance. The letters could be forged. Someone could have copied the ledger—photographed it, transcribed it—without entering the archive. Bram could have read her notes; Bram had access; Bram was dead, but Bram might have shared what he knew before he died. The killer could have tortured or tricked or simply asked, and Bram, gentle Bram, who had protected her secrets for nine years, might have given up her ledger the way he seemed to have given up everything else—to protect her, to draw the fire toward himself.

But the letter anticipated that reasoning. Of course it did. *I built the walls.* The killer was not content to be feared; he wanted to be understood, wanted her to arrive at him by the correct scholarly route, citation by citation. He was writing her a monograph with himself as the thesis.

And there was the detail that closed the case shut in her mind like a clasped cover: the entry of the fourth of March. *His hands were shaking.* She had written those words at 11:40 at night, alone, and she had never spoken them aloud to anyone, because speaking them would have meant admitting that Bram was frightened, and admitting that would have meant acting, and acting would have meant exposure. That sentence existed nowhere but in pencil on page thirty-one of the green ledger, in her own cramped hand, in a room that three keys could open.

One key was buried. One was hers. 

She looked up at the east window, where the morning light had climbed high enough to gild the lower panes, turning the Victorian saints into figures of amber and fire. Somewhere above her, the tower clock began to strike seven, and the sound came down through the stone the way it had come down through the stone every morning of her eleven years here, and for the first time it sounded to her less like a bell than like a count. Seven days until the spring tide. Six, now. The letter had said six days, and letters from killers, unlike parish notices, did not round up.

She gathered the evidence with hands that had steadied themselves by sheer administrative will: photographs of the envelope, the sheet in its mylar sleeve, the fleur-de-lis watermark documented against the stationery consignment record, the timestamps logged. If Wren was going to accuse her of obstruction again—if he was going to stand in the cloister with his tired eyes and tell her that withholding evidence was a chargeable offence—then she would hand him this, all of it, and watch his face when he read the words *I built the walls*, and see whether he heard what she heard.

Because that was the sentence she kept returning to, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth. Not the threats. Not the tide. *Order, and protection, and time to do your quiet work. You trusted the walls, Vesper. But I built the walls.*

It was a boast. It was also, she realized with a slow cold settling, a confession of something else entirely—something the writer might not have intended to give away. Because the walls of her sanctuary had not been built by any single person. The archive's security, the safes, the protocols, the keypad codes—those had accreted over decades, patched by registrars and chapter clerks long dead. No one person had built them.

But one person had built *her*. Had hired the shy, unemployable young woman with the strange gift and the thin references. Had given her the keys, the rooms, the title, the legitimacy. Had taught her that invisibility was a discipline, that noticing was a vocation, that the truth was a thing you served quietly and without applause.

Dean Corliss Hale had built her, brick by brick, the way you build anything you intend to live inside.

She stood in her beautiful violated sanctuary, among her ordered trays and her aligned folios and her fifty-four percent humidity, and she understood that the second letter was not a threat at all. It was an invitation home. The killer was not hiding from her.

He was waiting for her to finish reading.

* * *

She found Wren at the police incident trailer in the close, drinking vending-machine coffee with the resigned efficiency of a man who had stopped tasting it years ago. He listened without interrupting while she laid out the photographs, the sleeve, the watermark comparison, and she watched him move from professional skepticism to something more guarded and more alert, the way a man shifts his weight when the ground begins to speak.

"Verbatim," he said at last, tapping the photograph of the March fourth entry. "Timestamps and all."

"Word for word."

"And your ledger's accessible to—"

"Three keys. Mine, the dean's, and Bram's. Bram's is buried with him."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Outside the trailer window, the cathedral rose against a sky the colour of wet slate, its pinnacles dissolving into cloud. "You know what this letter says to me?" he said finally. "It says the killer wants you at that spring tide. Alone, if possible. It's not a taunt, Miss Ishikawa. It's bait."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway," she said. "But not alone, and not blind. Detective Inspector—" she paused, choosing the words with the care she chose archival descriptions, "—there's one more thing you should read. The last line. *Yours in the truth we both serve.*"

Wren frowned. "A signature?"

"A quotation," Vesper said, and felt the words leave her like something being unshelved after years in the dark. "It's what Dean Hale says at every commissioning of a new volunteer. Every single one, for twenty years. *We serve the truth together.* I wrote it in the front of my very first notebook, the day he gave me my keys."

The coffee cup stopped halfway to Wren's mouth.

"Six days," Vester said quietly, correcting herself without blinking. "Six days until the tide. And I think I know where the rest of Bram's notebook is—and I think I know who's been reading over my shoulder for eleven years."

Wren set the cup down. "Tell me," he said, "everything. From the beginning. And Miss Ishikawa—" his eyes were steady and tired and, for the first time, entirely without suspicion, "—this time, don't leave anything out."
