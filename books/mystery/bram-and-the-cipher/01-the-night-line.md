## 1. The Night Line

The city told the truth at 3 a.m., or at least it stopped bothering to lie.

Quentin Eastwick knew this the way other people knew weather—by feel, by accumulation, by ten years of listening through a headset to everything the daylight hours were too proud to admit. At 3 a.m. the calls came in stripped of performance. Nobody called Meridian Cabs at 3 a.m. because they wanted to be seen arriving somewhere. They called because they had no other options left, and options were Quentin's whole business: where to send them, who could take them, how fast, how gently.

"Meridian dispatch," they said, for the four-hundredth time that week, and somewhere in the city a stranger exhaled like they'd been holding their breath since midnight.

The headset was a beige plastic band older than some of the drivers, and it fit Quentin's skull the way a collar fits a dog—not cruelly, but permanently. Behind it, Quentin was nobody and everybody. A voice with no face. The woman on Halsted who cried every Tuesday at 2:40 and never said why. The man from the hospital who always asked for the same driver, Rennick, and always tipped in exact change counted out of an envelope. The kid outside the county building at 4 a.m., once a month, going home from something he wouldn't name. Quentin catalogued them all in a mental ledger that no one had ever asked to see. Ten years of small tragedies, filed under fares.

"Can you send someone to 1140 Verrone Street?" a woman asked now, her voice flat as unseasoned broth. "The gray door."

"Car's eight minutes out. You'll see the roof light."

"I'll be inside until then." Click.

Quentin entered it into the log without thinking, which was the problem, because thinking caught up a half-second later and tapped them on the shoulder. *Verrone Street.* They pulled up the address on the board. 1140. Gray door. The number sat there being ordinary, and still the back of Quentin's neck did something it almost never did on shift, which was pay attention.

They'd been there before. Not the address itself—they'd never dispatched to 1140 Verrone—but the street. As a child. In a car that smelled of cigarettes and pine air freshener shaped like a little tree, riding shotgun while their mother worked the night line and narrated the city like a nature documentary. *That's where the baker gets up, baby. That's where the river keeps its secrets.* Her hands on the wheel at ten and two, always, even running red lights.

Quentin shook it off. Memory was a bad dispatcher; it sent cars to addresses that didn't exist anymore.

* * *

The depot at night was a room the size of a generous closet, lit by one fluorescent panel that buzzed in B-flat and had buzzed in B-flat since before Quentin was hired, possibly since before Quentin was born. Two monitors, a coffee maker that produced a liquid legally distinct from coffee, a corkboard of driver photos gone amber with age. Quentin had long ago stopped noticing any of it, the way you stop noticing your own pulse.

The night crew drifted through in ones and twos between runs. Rennick came in at 3:20 smelling of menthol and the river, dropped his trip sheet on the counter, and grunted—the full extent of his conversational range before dawn. Marsh Kettle, who drove the oldest cab in the fleet and refused all offers of a newer one, leaned in the doorway eating sunflower seeds and spitting the shells into a paper cup with sniper accuracy.

"You look like a woman who's about to ask me for a favor," Marsh said.

"I look like everyone at three-thirty."

"That too." He cracked another seed. "Slow night?"

"Slow-ish. One weird call." Quentin hesitated, then heard themselves say it anyway: "Verrone Street. Near the canal."

Marsh's cup paused halfway to his mouth. It was nothing—a hitch, a hiccup of attention—and then it was over, and he spat a shell into the cup and shrugged. "Canal people keep odd hours. Water doesn't sleep, I guess."

"Guess not."

But Quentin filed it. They filed everything; it was the occupational disease of people who listen for a living. Marsh Kettle had driven nights since before Quentin could drive anything, and in ten years Quentin had never once heard him say the word *canal*.

* * *

At 4:15 the calls thinned to nothing, and Quentin did what they always did in the dead stretch: read back through the log. Other dispatchers skimmed. Quentin reread, hunting patterns the way their mother used to hunt fares—knowing which hotel emptied at which hour, which bar closed honest and which one lied about its closing time. The log was the city's diary, and most nights it was boring, and boring was fine. Boring meant everyone got home.

Tonight's entries scrolled past like usual. The Tuesday crier, logged and delivered. A fare from St. Anthony's ER to a residential address, passenger "nonverbal, wheelchair, brother paid cash." A group of nurses off shift, loud and laughing, the best kind of call. And then:

*3:02 — 1140 Verrone St. Female, adult. Dest.: n/a (changed en route). Driver: O. Reyes.*

Odile Reyes. Quentin sat up slightly, which for a dispatcher constituted standing ovation territory. Odile drove the 11-to-7 like a metronome and had done so for longer than Quentin's employment, longer than Quentin's adulthood. She was the closest thing the night shift had to management, though she held no title she'd admit to. She remembered everyone's kids' names. She covered shifts nobody else would touch. When Quentin had the flu two winters ago, Odile had shown up at their apartment with soup and a thermos and stood in the doorway exactly long enough to establish that refusing would be rude, then left.

And she'd taken the Verrone call, and the destination had changed en route, and the entry just—stopped. No final address logged. No completion time. Quentin scrolled down. The next entry was 3:19, a pickup on Delmore, seventeen minutes later. Seventeen minutes is nothing. Seventeen minutes is a red light and a slow customer and a wrong turn. Seventeen minutes is also enough time to drive to the canal district and back if you don't stop.

Quentin keyed the radio before deciding whether to. "Unit 12, you around?"

A pause with static in it, the particular quality of pause that means *I'm deciding how to answer*. Then Odile's voice, warm as ever, maybe warmer: "Right here, Q. What do you need?"

"Just closing out the Verrone run. Log shows no drop point."

Another pause. Shorter this time, but denser. "Passenger changed her mind. Got out near the viaduct, paid cash, walked off. Happens."

"It does," Quentin agreed. "You okay? You went quiet after."

A laugh, easy, real-sounding. "I went quiet because it's four in the morning and the radio's dead, sweetheart. Not every silence is a mystery." A beat. "Get some coffee. You sound like you're listening for ghosts."

"Occupational habit."

"There it is." Warm again, warm as a hand on the shoulder. "Go home when Rennick relieves the board. You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one your mother used to get."

The words landed before Quentin could decide whether they should. Odile had known their mother—everyone old at Meridian claimed to have known her, the way people claim to have known a neighborhood before it changed—but she rarely said so, and never that casually, never at 4 a.m. down an open channel.

"Copy," Quentin said, because there was nothing else to copy, and clicked off.

* * *

Shift end came at five with gray light leaking under the depot door like something spilled. Quentin signed out, stepped into the cold, and stood on the curb doing the thing they did every morning: failing to remember what waiting for them felt like. There was an apartment across town with a couch that had learned the shape of their body and a mailbox that learned it faster. There was a plant they kept alive out of spite. There was silence, high-quality silence, the kind you could build a life inside if you weren't careful.

Ten years behind the headset. Quentin knew four hundred strangers' griefs by heart and couldn't name a single person who'd know theirs. That wasn't self-pity; it was inventory. Quentin took stock the way they took stock of everything—calmly, completely, alone.

The bus came. Quentin rode it home watching the canal district slide past the window, dark water lying flat and secretive between its concrete shoulders, and thought, without meaning to: *she changed her destination and nobody wrote down where she went.*

People did that. People changed destinations constantly. It meant nothing.

It meant nothing, probably.

* * *

Sleep came late and thin. Quentin surfaced at noon to the apartment's particular brand of quiet—refrigerator hum, neighbor's television through the wall, the city's daytime lies already in full broadcast—and made coffee and tried not to think about Verrone Street, and failed, and gave in the way you give in to scratching a mosquito bite, knowing it only makes it worse.

They looked it up. That was all. Public records, property listings, the digital sediment every address leaves. 1140 Verrone Street: a narrow brick row house, assessed value modest, owner listed as a holding company, which meant nothing or everything. Street-view imagery showed the gray door, paint peeling at the bottom rail. Quentin zoomed in on the door as if it might confess something. Doors don't confess. Quentin knew that better than anyone; they spent their nights asking doors' worth of people questions and receiving, in return, mostly weather reports.

Still. Their mother had driven these streets for eleven years, and in eleven years she must have taken a hundred fares down Verrone, and Quentin had ridden along for some fraction of them, asleep against the window more often than not. Half-recognizing a street was not evidence of anything. Half-recognizing a street was the normal condition of having grown up in the back of a cab in a city that rearranged itself every decade.

Quentin closed the laptop. Made lunch. Did not think about the seventeen minutes.

At dusk they went back in early, which they never did, and took the board from Priya with a joke that landed approximately nowhere, and settled into the collar of the headset like a animal settling into a familiar cage.

* * *

The letters were waiting when Quentin got home the next morning.

This requires explanation, because nothing waited for Quentin. Mail arrived in the form of coupons, a utility bill, occasionally a postcard from a driver who'd moved to Arizona and missed complaining about the cold. The mailbox had the nutritional profile of cardboard. So when Quentin opened the little metal door and found two envelopes—real envelopes, heavy cream paper, addressed by typewriter—they stood on the front walk for a moment in the flat morning light, holding them, feeling the weight of paper that had been chosen rather than grabbed.

No stamp on either. Hand-delivered, then. Someone had walked up this walk, past the spiteful plant's window, and slipped them through the slot, and walked away, and Quentin had been here the whole time, sleeping ten feet away, hearing nothing.

Inside the first envelope: a single page, typed.

*I am writing to confess to the death of Daniel Ferro, drowned in the Merrow Canal on the night of October 14th, 1994. I was present. I have carried it thirty years. The details are mine to give and I will give them, but not on paper, and not all at once.*

No signature. No salutation. Just the type, slightly uneven, the ink heavier on certain letters the way old machines press harder where the keys have worn.

Quentin read it twice on the sidewalk, then a third time inside with the door locked, which was ridiculous, but the body does what the body does. Daniel Ferro. The name rang a bell down a very long corridor—something from the morgue of local history, a councilman's aide, an accident, water. Thirty years ago. Quentin had been eleven.

They opened the second envelope with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be.

One line.

*The rest is coming. You were there too.*

Same typewriter. Same worn pressure on the same letters. Quentin held the page up to the window light as if backlighting might reveal intent, and outside the morning went on telling its daytime lies, traffic and sprinklers and a dog losing an argument with a squirrel, and none of it knew, none of it cared, that somewhere a piece of paper had just reached thirty years through the dark and closed its hand around the back of someone's neck.

*You were there too.*

Quentin had been nowhere. That was the whole biography—nowhere, no one, a voice behind beige plastic. They had built the nowhere themselves, plank by plank, and lived in it comfortably, the way you can live in anything if you never turn the lights on.

But their mother had vanished in the autumn of 1994. And their mother had driven nights. And their mother's streets had included, apparently, a gray door on Verrone Street, and a canal that kept its secrets, and a company called Meridian whose badge Quentin suddenly needed very badly to go find in a shoebox at the top of a closet they hadn't opened in years.

The kettle clicked off. Quentin didn't make the tea. They stood in the kitchen holding two pieces of paper like a hand of cards dealt by someone who knew the game, and for the first time in ten years of listening to other people's midnights, Quentin Eastwick felt the city turn around and start listening back.
