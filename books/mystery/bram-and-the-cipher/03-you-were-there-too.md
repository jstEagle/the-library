## 3. You Were There Too

Sleep and I have never been on good terms, but that morning we weren't even speaking.

I got home at seven-forty, hung the headset on its hook by the door like a surgeon hanging up a scalpel, and stood in the middle of my apartment holding two envelopes the way you'd hold a live grenade with the pin already out. The sun came through the blinds in stripes and made the paper glow, which felt obscene. Confessions shouldn't be backlit. They should come to you in the dark, where they belong.

I put them on the kitchen table. I made coffee. I did not look at them while the water boiled, which took a certain discipline, because they were sitting there doing what letters do—waiting, patient as landmines.

*The rest is coming. You were there too.*

There's a particular kind of vertigo you get when someone reaches back through thirty years of silence and puts a hand on your shoulder. I know this now. At eight-fifteen that morning, all I knew was that my coffee tasted like tin and my hands wouldn't stay still.

Here's the thing about me: I don't have a past. Not in the poetic, wounded way people mean when they say it at parties. I mean it practically. My mother left—or vanished, or was taken, the word changed depending on how old I was when I told the story—in October of 1994, when I was eleven. Before that it had been her and me in a third-floor walkup on Delancey, her working nights, me sleeping through her comings and goings the way children sleep through weather. After that it was foster homes, group homes, a state-issued duffel bag that smelled like other kids' lives, and then, at eighteen, a city bus ticket and a hiring bonus from Meridian Cabs for anyone willing to work the night dispatch desk, which nobody was.

Ten years behind that headset. A decade of listening to strangers tell me where they'd been and where they needed to go, cataloguing the city's small tragedies one fare at a time—the woman who called every Friday at 2 a.m. to ride past the same house without stopping, the old man who asked drivers to take him to the hospital where his wife died and then just sat in the cab in the lot for twenty minutes. I knew everything about this city's nights and nothing about my own days. That was the trade. I'd signed it willingly.

And now some stranger with a typewriter was telling me I'd been *there*. Wherever *there* was. In 1994, when I was eleven years old and my entire world was a walkup apartment, a cereal bowl with a chip in it shaped like a crescent moon, and my mother's coat smelling of cold air and cigarette smoke she always swore she'd quit.

I sat down across from the letters like they were a witness I was about to depose.

"Okay," I said out loud, to no one, which is a habit of mine and probably explains a lot about my social life. "Let's do this properly."

The confession letter first. I'd read it once at the depot, standing under the fluorescent hum, and once more on the bus home, hunched over it like a kid hiding homework. Now, in daylight, it read differently. Typewritten, single-spaced, the keys slightly uneven—one *e* that always printed high, like it was trying to escape the line. No signature. No return address. Just the facts laid out flat and terrible: Daniel Ferro, November 1994, the canal off Pier Road, an accident that wasn't. The writer didn't ask forgiveness. Didn't explain. It ended with a sentence I'd been hearing in my head all shift: *I am dying anyway, and the truth should not die of the same disease.*

Then the second letter. Shorter. Same typewriter—I checked, the way you check, tilting both pages under the lamp until your eyes ache. Same high-flying *e*, same tired *r*. But different hands, somehow. Different pressure. The confession had been typed by someone who meant every word and hated typing them. This one had been typed by someone who wanted something.

*You were there too.*

Eleven-year-olds are not usually present at drownings. Eleven-year-olds are usually asleep, or watching television, or being eleven. Unless—

Unless their mother worked nights. Unless their mother's nights involved places like canals and men like councilmen's aides. Unless the child slept through her comings and goings the way children sleep through weather, and the weather, all along, had been something else entirely.

My coffee went cold. I let it. Some mornings you earn the right to waste a cup of coffee.

* * *

Memory is a dispatcher too. It takes calls all day long, most of them wrong numbers, and sends cars to addresses that don't exist anymore. When I tried to summon 1994, what came back was mostly static: the smell of the stairwell, rain on the fire escape, the blue light of the TV making ghosts on the ceiling. My mother's voice saying *stay put, Q, I'll be back before you're up.* She called me Q. Nobody else ever has. It's a strange thing to be someone's only witness and not know what you witnessed.

But there were fragments. Fragments with edges.

A man's voice on the phone one night, angry in a low way, the anger of someone who doesn't need to raise it. My mother covering the receiver with her hand and telling me to go brush my teeth even though it was three in the afternoon. Her coming home once with canal mud dried on her shoes—brown-gray, gritty—and laughing when I asked about it, saying she'd taken a shortcut. My mother did not take shortcuts. My mother believed shortcuts were how women ended up in newspaper articles.

And one more. This one surfaced slowly, the way bodies surface, and I want to be honest that I might be building it retroactively, fitting memory to evidence the way you fit a story to a headline. But I remember sitting on the top stair—it was late, past my bedtime by hours—and hearing her say to someone, maybe to herself, maybe into the phone: *I heard it. All of it.*

I heard it. All of it.

I was thirty-one years old, sitting at my kitchen table with two letters and a cold cup of coffee, and those six words went through me like a fare spike through a quiet Tuesday.

Because here's what I hadn't said yet, even to myself, even in the privacy of my own skull: if my mother *heard* something in 1994—if she was somewhere she shouldn't have been, listening to something she should never have heard—then she didn't leave me. Or rather, leaving me wasn't the point of her going. People who run away take their coats. People who are taken don't get to choose. And people who are running *from* something leave everything behind, because everything is weight, and weight is death when you're moving fast in the dark.

She'd left her things. All of them. The state had boxed them up and given them to me at eighteen along with the duffel bag and the bus ticket, and I had carried that box through four apartments without opening it more than twice. Once to make sure it was mine. Once, drunk, at twenty-four, when I'd pulled out a photograph and looked at her face for exactly as long as I could stand—which was four seconds—and put it away again.

The box lived on the top shelf of my closet, inside a shoebox, inside a garbage bag, inside a layer of denial approximately ten years thick.

I got the chair. I got the box.

* * *

Dust comes off old cardboard in a way that feels personal, like the box is exhaling after holding its breath since 1994. I set it on the kitchen table next to the letters, and the tableau was so grimly perfect I almost laughed—a shoebox flanked by two confessions, lit by striped sunlight, presided over by a man in yesterday's shirt talking to himself.

Inside: her winter gloves, fingers curled like they'd just been set down. A hairbrush with hair still in it, brown going gray at the temples, and I had to sit with that a moment, the gray, because I'd never thought of her aging. Dead mothers and vanished mothers don't age. They stay fixed at the last frame, and the frame I had was a woman of thirty-four laughing about muddy shoes.

Beneath the gloves: photographs. Her and me at the lake, me maybe five, wearing a life vest two sizes too big, her squinting into the sun with her arm around me like a seatbelt. Her and a woman I didn't recognize outside a diner, both mid-laugh, caught in the amber of somebody else's camera. Her alone, younger, in front of a building I didn't recognize either—brick, wide windows, a sign too blurry to read.

And beneath the photographs, wrapped in a dish towel gone stiff with time: the small metal things. A watch that didn't run. A ring she never wore. A key to an apartment I no longer lived in. And—

A badge.

Not a police badge. A laminated card in a plastic sleeve, cracked along one edge, the photo faded to sepia tones. MERIDIAN CABS, it said. OPERATOR. Below the logo, in blocky nineties font:

**M. EASTWICK — ID 0417**

Marion Eastwick. My mother. Employee number 417 at Meridian Cabs.

I want to tell you I gasped, or dropped it, or had some cinematic reaction worthy of the moment. What actually happened was smaller and worse. I read it. I read it again. And then I set it down very carefully on the table, aligned parallel to the letters, because some part of my brain that had spent ten years organizing other people's emergencies decided that if everything was neat, nothing was actually happening.

Meridian Cabs. Meridian Cabs. The firm whose calls I had routed for a decade. The firm whose drivers I sent into the night, night after night, whose radio codes I knew better than my own birthday. The firm that had hired me at eighteen with a signing bonus and never once—not in the interview, not in the paperwork, not in ten years of payroll—mentioned that my mother had worn their badge before I was born.

Or had they known? Had some personnel file with her name on it been sitting in a cabinet in the fleet office while I answered phones forty feet away? Had Odile Reyes—no. Stop. I'm getting ahead of myself. At that point I didn't know Odile Reyes existed. At that point the cast of this thing was still just me, a dead aide named Ferro, a typewriter with an escaping *e*, and a woman in a faded photograph who had apparently driven a cab for the same company that would later hire her orphaned son without either of us knowing we were family.

That's the joke, isn't it. That's the punchline the universe has been sitting on for thirty years. I became a night dispatcher because it was the only job that fit the shape of my damage—awake when the world sleeps, useful to strangers, invisible. And the whole time, I was walking my mother's route. Literally. Her route book, her badge, her nights. I'd inherited her shift without knowing there had been a shift to inherit.

*You were there too.*

Maybe the letter-writer meant it differently than I first took it. Maybe it wasn't an accusation. Maybe it was arithmetic: your mother was there; you are her; therefore. Blood as alibi. Inheritance as indictment.

Or maybe—this occurred to me around noon, standing at the window with the badge in my palm, watching the day-shift cabs slide through the wet street below my window like fish in an aquarium I used to own—maybe whoever wrote that letter knew exactly what it would do to me. Knew I'd open the box. Knew I'd find the badge. Knew that a man with no past, handed one suddenly, would have no choice but to follow it down.

You can't unlearn you're somebody's son. Even if finding out costs you the comfortable fiction that you're nobody's anything.

* * *

I called in sick for the first time in six years. The relief dispatcher, a kid named Petey who thought night shift was a punishment assignment, took the board without questions. I listened to the depot chatter on the scanner for an hour out of pure reflex, hearing the city breathe, and then I turned it off, which felt like holding my breath.

By evening I had laid everything out on the table like evidence, though I had no case yet, only artifacts: two letters, one badge, one photograph of my mother squinting into a sun that had set thirty years ago. The high-flying *e*. The employee number. The date on the back of the photo, in her handwriting, slanted and quick: *First week. Oct '94.*

October 1994. The autumn she vanished. The first week of her last month of being my mother's name attached to a living woman.

Her first week at Meridian. Which meant she hadn't worked there long. Which meant whatever she saw or heard or drove into happened fast—weeks, maybe days, between the badge and the empty apartment and the state-issued duffel bag with my whole childhood folded inside it.

I sat there until the streetlights came on, and then I did the thing I do, the only thing I know how to do: I started working the problem like a call. Who was Daniel Ferro? Where was he drowned? Who found him? What did the papers say? Because if my mother's badge connected her to Meridian, and Meridian connected her to the canal, then the canal was the exchange, the place where all these fares intersected, and I'd spent enough years behind a headset to know one thing for certain:

Every call has an origin point. You just have to find who picked up first.

I put the badge in my jacket pocket, where it rode against my ribs all night like a second heartbeat, and I went to find out what the newspapers remembered about a drowning the city had decided to forget.

It wasn't until I was on the stairs, three flights down into the dark, that I realized I'd left the letters face-up on the table—and that the second one, the promise, had been right all along.

The rest was coming. Whether I wanted it or not.
