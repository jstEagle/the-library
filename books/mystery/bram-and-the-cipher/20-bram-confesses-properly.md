## 20. Bram Confesses Properly

The hospice at night smelled like lavender and institutional lemon, a combination that suggested someone had tried very hard to make dying feel like a spa weekend and given up around the second floor. The corridor lights were dimmed to a color that could only be described as apologetic. A nurse I'd come to know by her sneakers rather than her name—she moved like weather—nodded me through without asking why a dispatcher was visiting a dying cab driver at eleven on a Tuesday.

Bram was awake when I got there. He was always awake when I got there, which I suspected was less coincidence than craft. He'd arranged himself in the chair by the window with the particular dignity of a man who refused to be seen horizontal unless absolutely necessary. His oxygen line curved behind his ear like a piece of wire he'd been meaning to fix for years.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You should see the other guy."

"I did. He's me." He patted the bed beside him—an invitation to the mattress, not the chair, which meant this was going to be one of those conversations. "Sit down, Eastwick. My legs are going and my mouth is finally catching up. That's a bad combination for everyone but you."

I sat. The mattress dipped under me and the whole bed tilted toward the window, toward the city, which glittered out there like it didn't know what it had done.

"Odile came to see me," Bram said.

I kept my face still. It's a dispatcher skill—you learn to give away nothing over an open line, because every pause is data and every breath is testimony. "Did she."

"Don't do the voice." He said it mildly, but there was iron under it. "The neutral voice. The one that says *copy that* to everything from a drunk calling about his wife to a kid calling about his mother. I drove this city for thirty-one years. I know what dispatchers sound like when they're hiding something, because half of them learned it from me."

"Then you know what I'm going to ask anyway."

"Ask it properly." He turned his head, and in the dim light his eyes were wet-bright and absolutely steady. "You've been circling it for three weeks. You've brought me coffee twice, which you hate doing because the machine here tastes like pennies. You've asked me about the matchbook four times and pretended each time was casual. Ask it like a man who wants the answer."

So I did. "Tell me what happened at the canal."

He closed his eyes. For a moment I thought he'd gone to sleep sitting up, which wouldn't have been the first time, and I felt the specific disappointment of a door closing just as your hand touches the knob.

Then he started talking.

* * *

"It was October," he said. "The eleventh. Cold enough that we were all running the heaters and hot enough inside the cabs that you'd sweat through your shirt between fares. Ferro had been riding with us for months—Daniel Ferro, the aide, though nobody called him an aide then. He called himself a fixer, which is what ambitious men call themselves before they're either councilmen or convicts."

I knew the shape of this already. I'd read the clippings until the newsprint wore soft as cloth. But knowing the outline of a thing and hearing it spoken are different animals entirely, the way a map of the ocean is different from drowning.

"He worked for Wrenn even back then. Aldous Wrenn, zoning committee, rising star, the kind of man who has his photograph taken shaking hands with children. And Ferro was his errand boy, except Ferro had figured out that the errands were worth more than the wages. Meridian money was moving through a shell company—a garage contract, a radio lease, little cuts off the top of everything—and Wrenn thought nobody could trace it because nobody cared about taxi companies. We were furniture to men like him. Furniture with meters."

"And Ferro found out," I said.

"Ferro *was* the mechanism. That's what nobody understood. He wasn't discovering the theft—he was performing it. Moving the paper, signing the names, keeping the ledger clean. And somewhere that autumn he decided he wanted to be more than a pen. He started keeping his own copy of everything. Insurance, he called it." Bram's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he knew was thin. "Wrenn found out. Of course he found out. Men like Wrenn always find out, because they assume betrayal the way normal men assume weather."

"Where do you five come in?"

"We were working the night shift. Odile, Marsh, me, and two others." He paused on the fifth name the way you pause at a stair edge in the dark. "We picked up a fare near the canal depot at eleven-something. Short run, cash, no conversation. And coming back empty—we used to cut through the maintenance road along the water, saved six minutes—Marsh saw the cars. Two of them, parked nose to nose under the old freight lamp. One was Wrenn's. We knew the car. Half the drivers knew the car; he never tipped."

He stopped. The oxygen machine breathed for him, a small mechanical tide.

"Bram," I said.

"I'm getting there. Give an old man room to be a coward for another minute." He opened his eyes. "We stopped. That's the part people never believe—that we just *stopped*. But it was Odile's night to check the depot lockbox, and the road was blocked, and honestly? Curiosity. Five tired men who spent their lives watching the city's windows and wondering. We got out. We stood in the dark like idiots, and we heard them fighting."

"What were they saying?"

"Loud parts and quiet parts. The loud parts were money. *Where is it, where did you put it, you signed it.* The quiet parts were worse. Quiet means a fight has stopped being about the thing and started being about the person." His hand moved on the blanket, restless, arranging nothing. "And then Ferro said something—I've spent thirty years trying not to remember exactly what—and Wrenn grabbed him. Shook him. And Daniel Ferro went backward."

"Backward into the water."

"Backward onto the edge first. There was a low wall, a loading lip, and he caught it wrong, and then—" Bram made a small motion with his open hand, a falling gesture, almost gentle. "The canal doesn't make noise when it takes someone. That's the thing nobody tells you. All those movies with the big splash. It doesn't. It just closes. Like a book."

The room hummed. Somewhere down the hall a cart rattled and went quiet.

"And Odile?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd known since the route book, since the symbol, since Marsh's rehearsed denials. Knowing a thing in advance doesn't spare you the landing.

"She ran forward," Bram said. "Before any of us could move. She got to the edge and she reached down—God knows what she thought she was doing, pulling him out maybe, or grabbing him—and he caught her wrist. Caught it hard, from the water, with both hands, and he was screaming, and she panicked." His voice cracked on the word, not dramatically, just structurally, like a floorboard giving. "She hit him. Once. With the flashlight she'd been carrying since her shift started. And he let go of her wrist, and he let go of everything else too."

"Pity and horror holding hands" — that's what I'd have called it if anyone had asked me to describe the next sixty seconds, though nobody asked and I wouldn't have answered. Because here was the truth arriving in installments: a man dead by accident stacked on accident, panic stacked on panic, and five witnesses standing in the cold deciding, in real time, what kind of world they lived in.

"Wrenn was still there," I said.

"That's the poison in it. Yes. He was standing ten feet away with his hands shaking, and he looked at us—five cab drivers in yellow jackets—and he said, very quietly, *nobody saw anything.* And Odile was on her knees with blood on her sleeve, and Marsh was already saying *we weren't here, we were on Fairmont,* and I—" Bram exhaled, long and slow, the way he probably used to before picking up a fare he didn't like. "I said nothing. Which is its own sentence, if you're ever inclined to write it down."

"The alibi was hers."

"The alibi was hers. The story was hers. *One story, five mouths.* We built it around Odile because she was the one who'd go to prison, and because—" he smiled, and it was the saddest use of a smile I've ever witnessed up close, "—because she was twenty-six and the best of us, and the city had already taken everything else from her. Her brother, her license once, her apartment twice. We weren't going to let it take her hands too. So we lied. All five of us, sworn on a matchbook like children playing at oaths, and the lie held for thirty years because nobody was looking. Accidental drowning. Drunk aide, dark canal, case closed. The city believed what it wanted to believe about men like Ferro."

"And Wrenn?"

"Wrenn got clean. That's the joke of it. He lost his ledger-keeper and gained a free lesson, and he spent the rest of his career being so careful, so *boring*, that they made him a councilman. And once a year, sometimes, he'd send a card to the depot. No words. Just his signature. Just to remind us he remembered us remembering." Bram's eyes found mine. "Your mother wasn't part of that night, if that's what you're carrying. She came after. She was the one who listened too well."

* * *

I don't know how long we sat there after that. The oxygen machine kept its tide. Out the window, a cab crossed the far end of the lot with its dome light on, some stranger's last ride home, and I watched it the way you watch anything moving when you can't move yourself.

"My mother," I said finally. "You said *protecting her*—last week, you said that. In here. You said the alibi was to protect her."

"I said protecting *her*," Bram agreed. "Her being Odile."

"But that's not all you meant."

He was quiet long enough that I counted the machine's breaths. Four. Five. Six.

"There were two things buried that night," he said at last. "A body in the water and a truth in five mouths. And then a few weeks later there was a third thing, because your mother came asking questions—not police questions, cab-driver questions, *route* questions—and she found the seam in our story before we even knew it had one. She'd been driving that stretch for two years. She knew the maintenance road was flooded that night, closed for repairs, which meant our Fairmont alibi had a hole in it shaped like a canal." He shook his head slowly. "She never threatened us. That's what kills me, Eastwick. She came to Odile quiet, woman to woman, and she said: I know, and I haven't told anyone, and I won't—unless you tell me he suffered. That was her condition. Not money. Not silence bought. She wanted to know if the man in the water had suffered."

"And?"

"And Odile told her the truth, and your mother kept her word, and then she went to Wrenn instead." Bram's laugh was a dry rustle, no humor in it anywhere. "Because she was smarter than all of us combined. She figured out that the danger wasn't five tired drivers—it was the man who'd said *nobody saw anything* in a voice like a signature on a contract. She went to him and told him she knew about the money. Thought she could trade. Silence for safety, information for leverage. She was running copies of everything by then. Ledger pages. And Wrenn—" 

He stopped.

"Bram."

"Wrenn smiled at her," he said, "and thanked her for her discretion, and within a month she was gone. And I have spent thirty years telling myself that men like Wrenn don't kill women over paperwork, that she ran, that she chose to disappear, because the alternative was admitting that the oath we took—the one story, five mouths—had a sixth mouth, and we let it close without ever saying her name out loud." He turned to me fully then, and took my hand, and his grip was still strong, still a driver's grip, calibrated to a meter and a door handle. "Until you walked into my room with her chin and her stubbornness and asked me about a matchbook."

The machine breathed. I breathed. Somewhere below us the city went on making its small tragedies, and I sat in the middle of the largest one I'd ever touched, holding the hand of a man who had helped bury my mother's name inside a lie.

"Why now?" I asked. "Why the letters? Why me?"

"Because the water's been giving things back all year," he said. "They dredged the east channel in March. Construction crews. And because I'm dying on a schedule now, and a confession that dies with me isn't a confession—it's just a story I told a nurse." His grip tightened. "But listen to me, and listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to be tired for approximately forever. What I wrote in that letter is true. Every word of the night, every word of the lie. But the letter that promised you *the rest*—"

He coughed, deep and wet, and waved off my reaching for the call button.

"—the rest was never mine to promise," he finished. "I confessed to what I did. I didn't promise you anything else. I don't make promises anymore. Promises are how five young idiots ended up carrying a body for thirty years."

I sat very still. Outside, the far-off cab had vanished, and the lot was just a lot again, ordinary and lit.

"Read your letters again," Bram said, settling back into his pillows, his eyes already going heavy. "Both of them. Side by side. And then come tell me who else knows you've been visiting me—because somebody does, Eastwick, and if they know that, they know everything, and I'd very much like to meet the man who thinks he can finish my sentence for me."

His eyes closed. Within a minute his breathing changed, the long slow rhythm of real sleep or its convincing imitation, and I was left alone in the lavender-and-lemon dark with a dead man's truth in my lap and a question I hadn't earned yet rattling around in my chest:

If Bram hadn't written the second letter—

who had?
