## 23. What Odile Wants

She called me at four in the afternoon, which for Odile Reyes was the equivalent of showing up at my door in a bathrobe, weeping. Odile did not weep. Odile did not do afternoons. Odile ran Meridian's fleet the way harbor pilots run freighters—calmly, from a position of terrifying authority, with a radio voice that could part traffic.

"Eastwick. My office. Tonight. After the shift change."

Not a question. I said, "Is this about the extra shifts you keep offering me?"

"It's about a lot of things," she said, and hung up before I could decide whether that was a threat or an invitation. With Odile it had always been both. That was the whole trick of her, I'd come to understand. She made every door look like an exit.

I spent the hours before the meeting doing what I always did with dread: I organized it. I made coffee I didn't drink. I reread the letters, both of them, side by side on my kitchen table under the lamp like evidence at a trial—which, I supposed, they were, though whose trial was still an open question. The confession in Bram's crumbling hand. *I saw what happened to Daniel Ferro on the night of October 14th, 1994, and I have said nothing for thirty years, and the saying-nothing has become its own kind of crime.* And beneath it, the second letter, the promise: *The rest is coming. You were there too.*

I still hadn't figured out the second one. I was starting to suspect nobody had figured it out, that it was a letter written by the dark itself, by the general conspiracy of the past to keep arriving uninvited.

* * *

Odile's office sat above the depot, a glass box overlooking the yard where the night cabs came home to roost at dawn, dented and lightless and patient. She'd decorated it in the style of a woman who expected to be audited: nothing personal, nothing expensive, nothing true. A photo of the harbor. A dying plant she clearly watered out of obligation rather than hope. Two chairs in front of the desk, and she gestured me into the one closest to the window, so that the whole city lay behind me and she could watch my face while pretending to watch the skyline.

"You look tired," she said.

"You look like someone about to ruin my evening."

"I am." She folded her hands. Her nails were painted the color of old taxi vinyl, a detail I found unbearably specific. "I'm going to tell you something I've told exactly four people in my life, and two of them are dead, and one of them is dying, and the fourth is you, and I'd very much like it if you'd let me finish before you do whatever it is you're going to do with your face."

"Odile—"

"Let me finish." And there it was, under the fleet-manager calm: something raw, moving. Like a fish under ice. You saw it once and then the surface healed over.

So I let her finish.

"I struck Daniel Ferro," she said. "October 14th, 1994. I hit him with a flashlight, and he fell, and he went into the canal, and he did not come up. And Bram, and Marsh, and Aldous Wrenn, and a man named Teddy Sorrel who's been dead eleven years, helped me bury it. Not him." She looked at me steadily, and I'll give her this—she never once looked at the skyline. "We buried the truth. The man the water took care of on its own."

The room got very quiet in the way rooms do when a sound has just ended that you'd been hearing so long you'd stopped noticing. I realized I'd been hearing 1994 for weeks now—Bram's voice, Marsh's rehearsed denials, my mother's route book with its little repeated symbol—and Odile had just switched it off.

"You're confessing," I said. "To me. A dispatcher."

"I'm confessing to the child of Mireille Eastwick," she said, "who was the sixth person on that canal path that night, and who paid for our lie with her whole life. So yes. You'll do."

My mother's name in her mouth did something to the air. I made myself stay in the chair. "Bram said you didn't mean for him to die."

"Nobody means anything at that age. We were twenty-four, twenty-five. Wrenn was screaming at Ferro about the ledger, Ferro was threatening to take it to the council, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'd driven Ferro home twice that month. He was scared of Wrenn and he was right to be. I told him to keep his head down. And then he didn't, and Wrenn cornered him by the water, and I came around the cab and I swung before I knew my arm was moving. He hit the piling. That's all. One second of panic and thirty years of everybody paying for it."

"Including my mother."

"Including Mireille." Something moved through her face then that I recognized, because I'd been wearing it for a month: grief that had been rearranged so many times it had stopped being grief and become architecture. "She heard it. The argument, the threat—Wrenn told Ferro he'd end up in the canal before the week was out, and she was sitting in cab six with her door open, ten yards away, and she had a cassette deck running because she recorded everything, she was that kind of careful, God rest her." Odile's voice caught, just once, and she smoothed it flat like a wrinkle in a bedsheet. "She ran. I don't blame her. Wrenn had money and friends and she had a kid and a route book. But she ran, and we let the city say she'd abandoned you, and we let Ferro's death be an accident, and we let Bram carry it alone because he was the only one of us who couldn't stomach the lie quietly. He had to carry it loudly, in his own head, for thirty years."

"That's why the alibi," I said. "Five drivers, one story. It was never about where you all were. It was about where *you* were."

"It was about keeping me out of a cell and Wrenn out of the papers and Mireille out of a shallow grave," Odile said. "All three at once. You do what you can with one matchbook." She almost smiled. It was the saddest almost-smile I've ever been in the room with. "You think I don't know what it cost? I know to the penny. I've been doing the arithmetic since I was twenty-four years old."

* * *

She got up then, and went to the window, and stood looking out at the yard with her back to me, and I understood that this was not a woman composing herself. This was a woman letting me look at the back of her while she said the hard part, because she knew her face would sell it too well.

"Bram's got weeks," she said. "You know it. I know it. The hospice knows it. When he goes, the confession becomes what it already is—a dying man's letter that no court will touch, because there's no body to exhume that isn't already bones, no witness but a dead driver and a woman who's been gone thirty years, and a councilman who will bury it under six inches of lawyers before the ink dries. Or—" she turned around, and her face was doing the thing she'd warned me about, "—or it dies with him. Clean. One story, five mouths, and then no mouths."

"And Ferro?" I said. "Does he stay an accident?"

"Ferro stays drowned," she said, "which is the only thing anyone can do for him now. The truth doesn't raise him. I've checked. I've done the math every way there is. The truth gets Wrenn maybe—*maybe*—a manslaughter charge that'll be pleaded down before his golf buddies finish being shocked. It gets me a cell. It gets Marsh, who's got a bad heart and a shop he loves, a cell too. It gets your mother's name dragged through a courtroom and called everything from accomplice to coward by people who never knew her. And it gets you—" she came back toward the desk, and her voice went gentle, which was worse than any threat, "—it gets you nothing. Quentin. It gets you nothing but the satisfaction of being right, which is the most expensive thing there is. I know. I've been buying it for thirty years."

I should have hated her. I want to be honest about this, since honesty is apparently the whole theme of my life now: I didn't. Sitting there in her glass office with the night fleet ticking and cooling in the yard below, I felt the terrible gravity of her. She was the first person in my entire life to look at me—really look, the way nobody had since my mother walked out—and say, in effect: *you are one of us, and here is what that costs.*

"You said on the phone you wanted a favor," I said.

"I want you to stop." She sat down across from me, forearms on the desk, close enough that I could see the gray coming in at her temples like frost on a windshield. "Stop digging. Stop visiting Bram with your notebook. Stop letting Tuck pull sealed files, because the boy's going to get himself arrested and he's too sweet for jail. Let the letters be what they are—an old man making his peace—and let him make it. And then come work for me."

"Work for you."

"Fleet operations. Days, if you want them, though I think you're a nights person and so am I. Real salary. A desk with your name on it—actual letters, I'll have them cut this week. Quentin Eastwick, Operations." She said my name like she'd been practicing it. Maybe she had. "You're good, Eastwick. Don't make that face, I've listened to you run the night line for a year. You know the city by its voices. You know which callers are lying and which ones are scared and which ones just want somebody to say their address back to them so they know they exist. That's not a dispatcher. That's an operations chief. That's family."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"Family," I repeated.

"I know what you had," she said. "I know what you didn't. A mother who vanished and a string of foster addresses and ten years of nights behind a headset because the dark doesn't ask you questions. I know, because I've read your file—your employment file, before you ask, though Wrenn's people have read the other kind." She let that sit. "You've spent your whole life being the voice nobody sees. I'm offering you the opposite. A door with your name on it. A crew that covers for you. People who will lie for you, if it ever comes to that—God knows we've had the practice."

And here is the part I keep turning over, the part that makes me distrust my own memory of that night: for about four seconds, I wanted it. Not the cover-up—I want to be clear that some cold little clerk in the back office of my head was already filing the confession under *not acceptable*. But the rest of it. The desk. The name. The crew. The image of myself at forty, at fifty, at Odile's age, standing in a glass office over a yard full of cars, belonging so thoroughly to a place that its secrets were my secrets and its people were my people. I have spent eleven years listening to the city's last calls, cataloguing its small tragedies, invisible behind a headset, and in four seconds Odile Reyes made me feel more seen than a decade of the night line ever had.

That's the thing about temptation nobody warns you: it doesn't come dressed as sin. It comes dressed as home.

"I can't," I said.

"You can," she said. "It would be easy. That's what I'm telling you. It would be the easiest thing you've ever done. Bram dies in three weeks with a clean record and a clear conscience, or as clear as a man like Bram gets. Marsh keeps his shop. I keep my pension. Wrenn keeps his seat, and believe me, the city survives him—it's survived worse, it survives everything. And you get a life, Quentin. An actual one, with people in it."

"Built on a drowned man," I said.

"Built on a mistake," she said. "Made by a frightened girl who's been paying for it since before you could drive. You want to punish somebody? Punish Wrenn. He started it, he profited from it, he's the one who had your mother's route book copied and her name whispered around until 'ran off' became 'ran off on her kid.' Go after him. But leave the rest of us out of the wreckage, because the rest of us were twenty-four and terrified and we have been dying by inches ever since, and I am *tired*, Eastwick. I am so tired of being the strongest person in every room, because the strongest person in the room is the one who has to keep holding the lie up, and it weighs—" her voice finally, finally cracked, just a hairline fracture, "—it weighs what you'd think. It weighs a man in a canal."

I stood up. My legs did it without consulting me.

"Odile," I said, "why are you telling me this? Really. If you want me to stop digging, confessing to me is a strange way to go about it. Now I'm not guessing. Now I *know*."

She looked at me for a long moment. And then she did something I didn't expect: she laughed. Short, tired, real.

"Because Bram's going to tell you anyway," she said. "He's been circling it for weeks. He wrote you a confession, for God's sake—the man mailed you his soul in an envelope, and you think he's going to take my name to his grave? He's just waiting for me to say it first, because he's a gentleman, God help me, the only gentleman I ever knew." She shook her head. "I told you the truth because you were owed it. Your mother was owed it. And because—" she paused, and for a second she looked every one of her sixty-odd years, and then some of the years she'd borrowed from the rest of us, "—because if you're going to destroy us, I wanted you to know what you're destroying. Not monsters. A girl with a flashlight, and four men who loved her enough to lie, and a mother who ran to keep you safe, and thirty years of people holding each other up in the dark. That's what you'll be burning, Quentin. Make sure you mean it."

"I mean it," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to. "But not the way you think. I'm not burning anyone. I'm just not going to stand in the dark holding the lie up either. Not for a desk. Not for a name on a door. Not for—" and here my voice did its own cracking, because the next part was true and I hated that it was true, "—not for family. I'm not going to buy belonging with someone else's silence. I've seen what that costs. I'm looking at it."

Odile studied me the way she probably studied drivers who'd just told her they were fine to finish a double. Then she nodded, once, like a judge.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Then hear my last offer, and it's the real one, and it's not a bribe: when this goes wherever it's going—when you do whatever you're going to do—come to me first. Before the police, before the press, before Tuck and his scanner and whatever dead-man's-switch nonsense you're cooking up with that boy. Give me one day's warning. One day to tell Marsh myself, and to put my affairs in order, and to walk into whatever's coming standing up. That's all I ask. Not silence. Just dignity. Can you give a dying crew that much?"

I thought about it. I thought about Marsh in his garage, rehearsing his denials like scales. I thought about Bram in the hospice, wry and unafraid, waiting for the water to give things back. I thought about my mother's route book, and the symbol inked inside it, and the fact that somewhere out there, someone was still moving pieces I couldn't see.

"One day," I said. "If you've told me everything. If there's no second letter, no second secret, no Odile behind the Odile."

Something flickered across her face then—fast, gone before I could name it. Guilt, maybe. Or calculation. Or just the exhaustion of a woman who'd been managing a thirty-year fire and had just been asked to certify the building clean.

"There's no Odile behind the Odile," she said.

I believed her about eighty percent, which, for a woman who'd just confessed to manslaughter, was probably the best I was going to get.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the canal, because apparently I'm the kind of person who does that now. The water was doing its nighttime trick, holding the streetlights upside down and pretending to be sky. Somewhere under all that black glass was a piling, and a night in 1994, and a young woman with a flashlight who'd swung before she knew her arm was moving.

I didn't forgive her. I want that on the record too. But I understood her, and understanding is its own kind of sentence.

My phone buzzed as I came up out of the underpass. Tuck. I almost didn't answer—I was full up on revelations for one night—but Tuck doesn't call late unless it matters, and Tuck's judgment of *matters* had been getting better by the week.

"Eastwick." His voice was doing the thing it did when he was trying to be calm, which is to say it was pitched about a fourth too high. "You need to come see something. I ran the second letter—the promise one. Through the typewriter forensics stuff, the ribbon analysis, the whole nerd catalog."

"And?"

"And Bram didn't write it." A breath, crackling down the line. "Different machine. Different pressure on the keys entirely. Quentin—the second letter's a fake. Somebody's been feeding you Bram's confession like it's bait."

I stood there at the edge of the canal, the water holding its stolen light, and did the arithmetic Odile had taught me: one confession, one promise, two different hands. Somebody wanted me digging. Somebody had wanted it from the very first envelope.

"Tuck," I said. "Pull the postmark again. And this time, check the return address against the fleet records."

There was a pause on the line, the particular pause of a young man realizing he's about to have a very bad, very good night.

"Why the fleet records?"

"Because," I said, "I want to know if the person who mailed me my own past has been driving for Meridian the whole time."
