## 34. Bram's Last Broadcast

The hospice had a smell Quentin had learned to stop noticing: lavender trying its best over antiseptic, over something older and sweeter that no amount of lavender would ever win against. The nurses knew them by now. One of them, a broad-shouldered woman named Petra who moved through the corridors like a barge through fog, had stopped asking whether Quentin was family. She just nodded them down the hall to Room 9, and once she'd said, "He's having a good morning," in the tone other people used to report weather, which in a place like this counted as a miracle worth announcing.

Room 9's window faced east, which meant it caught the morning light Bram mostly slept through and woke into at odd hours like a man surfacing from deep water to check the time. He was awake when Quentin came in, propped up against his pillows with the TV muted on some shopping channel, a woman holding up a necklace as if it were evidence.

"You look terrible," Bram said.

"You should see the other guy."

"I have seen him." Bram's eyes were yellow now, the jaundice doing what the doctors kept calling *progressing*, and his hands had thinned until the veins stood out like map routes. But his voice still had the gravel-and-honey quality of a man who'd spent thirty years talking people out of their worst moods at four in the morning. "You've been sleeping in your car again."

"I've been sleeping in my car *efficiently*."

"Mm." He turned off the television with a deliberateness that told Quentin this wasn't going to be an ordinary visit. "Sit down. We're doing it today."

Quentin sat. The plastic chair by the bed had molded itself over months of visitors into a shape that fit no one comfortably, which felt appropriate. "Doing what?"

"The broadcast." Bram said it the way he'd say *the fare* or *the shift* — a job with a name, something you clocked into. "I've been putting it off because I wanted my head clear, and then I realized that ship sailed around 1994, so we're going with what's left."

"Bram—"

"Don't." He held up one finger, and even diminished, the gesture had authority. It was the finger of a man who had ended a thousand arguments from the front seat of a cab. "I know what you're going to say. You're going to say I should wait, rest, let the lawyers handle it, let Odile's statement do the heavy lifting. And I'm going to tell you what I told every rookie who ever rode with me: the city doesn't wait. The city takes. If you've got something to give it, you give it while you're still holding it."

The recorder sat on the bedside table where it had been sitting for three days, a small digital thing Tuck had provided along with two backup devices and a laminated instruction card, because Tuck believed in redundancy the way other men believed in saints. Next to it: a glass of water, untouched. A notebook with Bram's handwriting getting progressively less certain toward the bottom of each page.

"Marsh is out of the hospital?" Bram asked.

"Tuesday. Concussion, cracked rib. He's already complaining about the food."

"Good. Complaining means he's planning." Bram looked at the window, at the light coming in thin and gray over the parking structure across the street. "Odile?"

"She signed everything. She's meeting the prosecutor Friday."

"And Wrenn?"

Quentin hesitated. "Quiet. Too quiet. Tuck says the PI's office went dark — phones unanswered, lease apparently up. Either they're running or they're regrouping."

"They're regrouping." Bram said it without drama, the way he'd say *it'll rain*. "Men like Aldous don't run. Running admits there's something to run from. They tidy. They burn." His eyes came back to Quentin, and there was something in them that made Quentin's chest tighten — not fear exactly, but the particular gravity of a man arranging his last affairs. "Which is why today. Everything on record. Everyone named. Including me."

"Including you," Quentin repeated.

"Especially me." He reached for the water, drank half of it with visible effort, set it down. "You can't hand someone else's guilt to the world and keep yours in your pocket, kid. That's how men like Wrenn get made. They start by deciding whose truth gets told and whose gets managed, and thirty years later they're councilmen with a PI on retainer and a garage scheduled for a fire."

"You know about the garage?"

"I know about everything. Dying men hear more than priests." He almost smiled. "Tuck's scanner chatter. Marsh's cousin works dispatch at the fire department. Odile has eyes. We were always a crew, Quentin. That part never died. Only the honesty did."

He picked up the notebook, and his hands shook, and he steadied them against the blanket with the patience of long practice.

"Turn it on."

*

Quentin pressed record at 10:47 in the morning, and Bram Voss began to talk.

He started with his name, his date of birth, his license number — recited flat and complete, a dispatcher's cadence, every fact logged before the story. Then he took a breath that seemed to come up from somewhere below the mattress, and he told it.

All of it.

Daniel Ferro, thirty-one years old, aide to Councilman Aldous Wrenn, who had been skimming city transportation contracts through Meridian Cabs for six years and had finally decided his bookkeeper knew too much. The meeting near the canal on the night of October 14th, 1994, that Ferro thought was a negotiation and Wrenn intended as a warning. The five drivers who'd been drinking at the Anchor after shift — Bram, Odile, Marsh, Aldous Wrenn himself, back when he still bought rounds and called himself one of them — walking the towpath because the night was warm and nobody wanted to go home.

"We heard them before we saw them," Bram said, and his voice didn't waver, but his hands had gone still in a way that looked worse than shaking. "Wrenn had Ferro by the collar against the rail. Ferro was saying — he kept saying, *Aldous, Aldous, we can fix it* — like fixing it was still on the table. Like the man hadn't already decided. And then Odile stepped in. She always stepped in. Thirty years I've known her and she has never once watched a man put hands on another man without stepping in."

He described it plainly. Odile shoving between them. Wrenn swinging wild. Ferro losing his footing on the wet stones. The sound — Bram made no attempt to dramatize it, just said *he hit the water wrong* — and the four seconds, maybe five, when everyone stood there believing he'd come up, because people come up, people always come up.

"He didn't come up."

Bram talked about the decision afterward with the same flat clarity. Not a conspiracy in the end — something sadder. Five frightened people standing in a circle agreeing, out of panic and love and the animal arithmetic of survival, that they had all been somewhere else. One story, five mouths. The matchbook passed around. The oath taken young and kept too long.

"Wrenn wrote the alibi," he said. "That's the thing nobody understands. We weren't protecting ourselves first. He gave us the story, times and places, and we took it because taking it felt like the only door out of that night. And then he spent thirty years making sure the door stayed shut. Promotions. Favors. Quiet money to Marsh's shop when the tax trouble came. A job for Odile when Meridian restructured. He bought us with kindness, which is worse than buying us with threats, because kindness makes you complicit in your own purchase."

Then he said the name Quentin had been waiting for, and the room seemed to tilt.

"Marisol Eastwick. Your mother." Bram turned his head on the pillow and looked directly into the recorder, and somehow through it, at Quentin. "She drove nights for Meridian starting in '92. Best route memory I ever saw — she could reconstruct a fare's whole evening from what they left on the seat. She picked up Ferro twice in September of '94. He talked. Bookkeepers always talk to cabbies; we're furniture with ears. She heard enough to be dangerous, and she was smart enough to know it, so she started recording. Her own insurance. She told me once — she said, *Bram, if anything happens to me, the tape happens to him.*"

His voice finally cracked, just once, on the next part.

"She was at the canal that night. Not by accident. She'd figured out the meeting was happening and she went to warn Ferro, and she got there in time to see the end of it — the shove, the water, all of us standing there like statues. She ran. She took her copies and she ran, and I have spent thirty years hoping she got far enough, and three weeks knowing she didn't get far enough from *me*, because her kid walked into my hospice room with my own handwriting in their pocket."

He stopped. The machines made their small sounds. Somewhere down the hall a cart rattled past.

"I'm not asking forgiveness," Bram said. "Not from Ferro's family, not from Marisol, not from God, who I suspect stopped taking my calls around the same time everybody else did. I'm not asking forgiveness from the kid sitting in that chair either, though God knows I'd take it. I'm telling it because a lie kept warm for thirty years is still a lie, and the only thing colder than the truth is the grave, and I'll be in the grave soon enough. Let the truth do the traveling."

He named them all again, slowly, deliberately: himself, Odile Reyes, Marsh Kettle, Aldous Wrenn. And then, in a voice gone gentle:

"And Marisol Eastwick, who owed this city nothing and paid it everything, and who I will testify protected no one that night except a stranger who couldn't be saved. Whatever Wrenn's people say about her, whatever paper trail he's spent decades polishing — she was a witness. She was the witness. And she was brave, and I loved her like a sister, and I am sorry in every cell I have left."

He reached over and Quentin's hand moved before their mind did, and stopped the recording at 11:52. Sixty-five minutes. The file weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

*

For a while neither of them spoke. The light had shifted; the shopping channel woman was frozen mid-gesture on the dark screen. Bram lay back against the pillows looking, for the first time since Quentin had known him, genuinely tired — not ill-tired, not dying-tired, but the honest exhaustion of a man who has carried a trunk up a long staircase and finally set it down.

"Well," he said. "That's the broadcast."

"That's the broadcast."

"Tuck's got copies?"

"Three. Plus the dead-man's switch goes live tomorrow noon unless I check in."

"Paranoid little gremlin. I'd have liked him in the old days." Bram's eyes drifted to the window. "You know what's funny? All those years I told myself we kept the secret to protect Odile. And part of that's true — she swung first, she panicked, she's paid for it every day since in ways none of us saw. But underneath it, underneath everything, we kept the secret because keeping it meant we were still a crew. The lie was the clubhouse. Take away the lie and maybe there's no crew anymore." He looked at Quentin. "And then you show up. And it turns out the crew survives the truth after all. Took a dispatcher to figure that out. We were too busy driving."

Quentin found their throat had gone tight. They'd learned, over these weeks, to expect grief in specific shapes — the shoebox, the route book, the cassette labeled *M.E.* — but this was a different species entirely, and it ambushed them anyway.

"Bram. What you said on there. About her being brave."

"She was."

"You didn't have to put that on the record. The lawyers could've — it complicates things, her being at the canal, it gives Wrenn's people a thread to pull—"

"Kid." He said it softly, and the softness was worse than the finger. "I put a dead man's name on the record and a confession to obstruction and I burned thirty years of loyalty in sixty-five minutes. You think I flinched at defending your mother?" He shifted, wincing, resettling. "Wrenn's whole play — the letters, the second letter, the photograph, all of it — was to make you believe you were investigating your own blood. That if you dug far enough you'd find her guilty, and you'd stop digging, or worse, you'd dig and hate yourself forever. That's what men like him do. They don't hide the truth. They poison it first, so anyone who drinks it poisons themselves."

"I know."

"Do you?" He studied them with eyes that missed nothing, that had missed nothing across ten thousand fares and thirty years of watching the city lie to itself from behind a windshield. "Because I watch you flinch every time her name comes up. I watch you brace. Like you're still waiting for the part where it turns out she abandoned you on purpose. Where you were the reason she ran."

Quentin said nothing. The machines said their small sounds.

"She didn't run from you," Bram said. "She ran *for* you. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and someday you're going to stand at her grave and say it out loud, and it's going to hurt worse than anything, and then it's going to heal something you didn't know was broken. I've seen it happen. Water gives things back. That's the whole business of water."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true." He held out his hand — the thin one, the map-routes one — and Quentin took it, careful of the IV line, careful of everything. His grip was still stronger than it had any right to be. "Now listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and I don't have the stamina for encores."

"Bram—"

"*You were there too.*" He said it slowly, weighing each word like coins. "That's what I wrote on the letter, and I've watched you carry it around for weeks like it was an accusation. Like it meant you were guilty of something. Guilty of existing near all this. Guilty by blood." His thumb pressed once against Quentin's knuckles. "Kid, I'm a taxi driver. I write like one. *You were there too* means you were on the route. It means the story runs through you same as it ran through her, same as it ran through five idiots on a towpath in 1994. It means when the truth finally comes home, it comes to your address."

The room was very quiet.

"It means you're ours," Bram said. "You were ours before you ever walked in here. Marisol's kid, night-shift blood, headset voice, the whole lineage. We've been waiting thirty years for somebody to pick up the call." His mouth pulled into something that was almost his old grin, worn thin at the edges. "*Act like it.*"

Quentin didn't cry. Or rather — they cried the way they did everything, invisibly, efficiently, without letting it interrupt the shift, and if Bram noticed, and he noticed everything, he had the grace of a lifetime of night work and said nothing at all.

*

They stayed through the afternoon. Bram slept in fits and surfaced between them with fragments — asked twice whether the recorder was still running (it wasn't), once whether Odile had signed ("Friday," Quentin reminded him, and he nodded like a foreman confirming a delivery), and once, drifting somewhere in the shallows between decades, he looked straight through Quentin and said, "Mari, you can't park there, love, you'll get a ticket," and Quentin's heart broke along a seam that had been breaking for weeks and was, somehow, the better for it.

It was nearly dusk when Petra appeared in the doorway with a tray of pills and the unspoken authority of her profession, and Quentin gathered their coat and the recorder and the weight of sixty-five minutes that would be everywhere by tomorrow noon.

At the door, Bram's voice caught them.

"Quentin."

They turned. The evening light came through the east-facing window sideways and thin, gilding the edge of the bed, the edge of the machines, the edge of the old man propped among them like a lighthouse being decommissioned.

"When it breaks — and it'll break fast now, faster than Tuck thinks — Wrenn won't go at the tapes first. Tapes are copies; copies are cheap. He'll go at the *source*. The places. The people." His eyes were clear and level. "The old garage. Locker 44's already empty, but the building remembers everything, and men like him need buildings to forget. Watch the water, kid. And watch the fire."

"I will."

"And Quentin." A pause. The machines breathed. "Tell Marsh his cousin's a good egg. Tell Odile—" He stopped, and for a moment the wryness failed him entirely, and what was underneath was just an old man looking at the last light of a long shift. "Tell Odile the oath's paid. Tell her she's free."

Quentin nodded, because speech had temporarily left the building.

"And tell yourself," Bram said, settling back into his pillows, closing his eyes, already halfway to wherever he went, "that you were there too. Means you're ours." The ghost of the grin. "Act like it."

*

Quentin made it three blocks before the phone buzzed.

Tuck. Which wasn't unusual — Tuck texted like a teletype machine with anxiety, constant low-level pings, scanner fragments, updates nobody asked for. But this message was different. This message was four words, no punctuation, sent twice.

*garage. smoke. GET HERE.*

Quentin stood on the corner of Ninth and Halloway with the dusk coming down and the recorder heavy in their bag, and looked south toward the port district, where the old Meridian garage sat dark and condemned and full of everything a certain councilman needed forgotten.

They were running before the second message arrived.
