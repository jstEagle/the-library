## 5. What the Founding Families Paid For

There is a particular smell to a man's courage failing. I'd spent thirty years cataloguing it in interview rooms — the sour shift of sweat, the sudden interest in a window, the way a voice climbs half an octave and pretends it hasn't. Seven years out of the trade, I'd hoped I'd forgotten it. I hadn't. On the morning I set out to call on the three most respectable men in Hollowmere, I smelled it before I'd even knocked on a single door, drifting off the town like fog off the marsh.

The ledger — Marian's ledger, or the thing wearing Marian's handwriting — had given me names. Not directly; it wasn't built that way. It gave me initials, dates, and sums, and it gave me a routing: payments, quarterly, flowing into an account held under the name *M. Ishikawa, Trust*. My wife's name. My wife's estate. The bank that administered it was Callow & Fenn, and its manager was a man named Aldous Fenn, whose initials appeared in the ledger more often than anyone's except, God forgive me, my own wife's.

I started with him because banks keep hours and guilt keeps longer.

* * *

Callow & Fenn occupied a Georgian front on Wetherell Square that had been handsome when I was a boy and had settled into merely expensive. Aldous Fenn received me in an office that smelled of beeswax and fear. He was seventy-one, pink as a ham, and when I laid the ledger on his desk his hand went to his collar the way a man checks for a noose.

"Emrys," he said. "What a — I haven't seen you since Marian's — since the funeral."

"No," I said. "You sent flowers and a card that said *deepest sympathies* and then, I now notice, a quarterly transfer of four hundred pounds into her trust account. Very sympathetic. Very deep."

The colour left him by degrees, like a tide going out. "I don't know what you think—"

"December 1998," I said. "Ferrow Mill. A boy called Thomas Voss went into the water and didn't come out. You were seventeen. So were Dr. Prewitt and Mr. Latch of the parish church. And Daniel Voss, who was twelve and adored his brother, and who spent the rest of his life in this town being paid to forget it."

"Emrys." His voice had dropped to the register men use in chapels and confessionals. "You can't — those are old wounds. There was an inquest. Accident."

"There was an inquest," I agreed, "and then there were payments, and the payments are the interesting part. An accident doesn't require an annuity, Aldous. Guilt does. Or fear. Which is it?"

He looked at the ledger, then at the window, then at his own hands, which had begun to tremble in a way I suspected was not entirely theatrical. "You should ask Marian," he said, and it was the cruelest thing anyone had said to me in years, possibly including the coroner.

"Marian is dead."

"Yes," he said softly. "She was the only reason any of us slept."

* * *

Dr. Prewitt still practised out of the same surgery on Latchford Lane where he'd stitched my head when I was nine and a gate had opinions about my skull. He was smaller than I remembered, the way all the formidable men of my childhood had shrunk, and he received me with the weary courtesy of a man who has been expecting the police for twenty-six years.

"You've seen the ledger," he said, before I'd sat down.

"You knew about it?"

"We knew there was a book. We never saw it. Marian kept it. She said—" He stopped, took off his spectacles, polished them with a motion so automatic it was clearly a ritual, and put them back on. "She said a debt you can't see is a debt you never pay. So she made us see it. Every quarter. She'd send the entry. Initials, date, sum. Like a schoolmistress marking homework."

My wife. Marian, who alphabetized the spice rack and the poetry shelves and, apparently, the sins of Hollowmere. Marian, who had sat across from me at dinner for thirty-one years while I complained about cold cases, and had never once said, *Emrys, dear, the coldest one is in your soup.*

"Why her?" I asked. "Why your wife, Emrys? Why not a solicitor, a bank vault, a locked box?"

"Because a solicitor talks," Prewitt said. "A bank vault gets opened by court order. But a dead woman's recipe book—" he smiled, and it was the saddest smile I'd seen since the funeral — "who opens a dead woman's recipe book? We paid her to be the lock, Emrys. And she agreed because she said someone in this town ought to know what the money was for, and it couldn't be any of us. We'd only spend it."

"And what was the money for?"

He told me. Not all of it — he told me the shape of it, which was worse. Four children at the mill on an October evening in 1998, a rope swing, a dare, Thomas Voss going into the water where the old sluice pulled. Three of them on the bank. Daniel in the water, too small, too late. And the three on the bank making a pact before the boy was even cold — not spoken aloud, Prewitt insisted, as if that mattered, as if silence needed to be notarized — that no one had been there. That Thomas had come to the mill alone, as boys do.

"And the payments?"

"Started the year Daniel's mother died. He'd started asking questions by then. He'd never stopped, really — he was the only one of us who hadn't agreed to anything, being twelve, but he'd lived inside our agreement his whole life. When he started writing letters, we started paying. Marian administered it. Half to Daniel, to — God help us — to keep him comfortable enough not to leave. Half into a fund, in case." He met my eyes. "In case anyone ever came asking. It was hush money in both directions, Emrys. We paid Daniel to stay, and we paid into a reserve in case staying wasn't enough."

"And was it enough?"

Prewitt looked at me for a long moment. "Daniel left seven years ago," he said. "The same week you retired. You might ask yourself why those two events felt, to some of us, like the same event."

* * *

I saved the churchwarden for last because I am, whatever else has decayed, still a professional. Mr. Ambrose Latch had been a boy at the mill too, and he was now the keeper of St. Brannoc's, a man so upholstered in respectability that he seemed to creak when he walked. I found him arranging lilies for the harvest festival, which struck me as the correct flower for a man whose whole life was an arrangement of things over rot.

"Emrys Ishikawa," he said warmly. "Come about the memorial fund? We're doing the mill garden in spring—"

"Ambrose." I set the ledger on the vestry table between the lilies. "Let's not insult each other. It's a long drive from my bookshop and I'm not as patient as I was."

The warmth left him like a snuffed candle. He was the youngest of the three — sixty-eight, silver-haired, handsome in the way of portrait photographs — and he was also, I noted with the old cold pleasure of the trade, the only one whose fear had hardened into something with edges.

"Where did you get that?"

"Off a dead man," I said. "A man wearing a coat I sold three days before he was fished out of the marsh ditch. You'll have heard. Sergeant Hale is very thorough, and the marsh road is not as private as people hope."

"Then you know we're all in danger of a madman's—"

"I know," I said, "that the ledger's last complete entry is a payment scheduled for this week. A large one. Initialled A.L. I know that the man in the grey coat came here asking about 1998, and that within a week he was dead, and that the fund you've been feeding for a quarter century has suddenly, urgently, come due. I'm not a policeman anymore, Ambrose, so I'll say what a policeman couldn't: I think you're frightened. And I've found that frightened men with ledgers do arithmetic, and frightened men without them do worse."

He was quiet for a long time. Somewhere above us a rook complained about the roof. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone soft and terrible.

"Do you know what it's like," he said, "to be the last keeper of a thing everyone wants buried? Prewitt has his surgery and his gin. Fenn has his bank and his Florida. But I stand in that pulpit every Sunday, Emrys, and I look out at the Voss name on the war memorial, and I hold the whole weight of it alone, because I'm the one who said the words first. *Nobody was there.* I was seventeen and I said it, and Thomas Voss has been under the water ever since, and I have been under it with him."

"The payment this week," I said. "Who's it for?"

"Nobody." He said it too quickly.

"Ambrose."

"Nobody," he said again, and began, with great deliberation, to trim a lily stem. "The fund closes. That's all. Everything ends, Emrys. Even debts. Especially debts."

I left him to his lilies and his lies, and I stood in the churchyard in the cold doing what I had spent seven years refusing to do, which was think like the man I used to be. Three old men, three versions of the same terror, and every one of them lying about something small enough to hide a truth behind. Prewitt had told me the fund was half for Daniel. Fenn had flinched at Daniel's name. Latch was closing the fund the very week a man came to town asking about Daniel's family.

And through all of it, one thread I could not stop pulling: *Marian knew.* Marian had kept the lock, kept the book, kept the town's rot in her own careful hand, in a book disguised as the recipes she'd cooked me on cold Sundays — the rabbit stew, the honey cake — every page a lie I had eaten and praised.

I walked home through the gathering dusk, and the fog came up off the marsh the way it always did, patient as a creditor.

The bookshop was lit when I arrived. Wren had left the lamp on for me, the way she always did, and there was a plate under a cloth on the counter — honey cake, still warm, in Marian's recipe, from Marian's card, written in that same looping hand.

I stood in the doorway a long time before I went in, because for the first time in seven years I looked at that warm little light and did not see comfort.

I saw a lock. And I began, with a sickness I couldn't name yet, to wonder what my household had been built to keep shut.
