## 9. Love Against Duty

There is a particular kind of waiting that happens in a police station, and I have decided it is worse than any other kind. Waiting at a bus stop, you can leave. Waiting for a kettle to boil, you can make tea. But waiting in a room with a scarred table and a window painted over with frosted glass — waiting to find out whether the man you love like a father is about to hand you to the gallows of public opinion — that is a waiting with teeth.

They had given me tea anyway. Sergeant Hale was nothing if not procedural. The cup sat in front of me, untouched, going the colour of old pennies. Across the table, Emrys Ishikawa sat with his hands folded like a man at a funeral who has already made his peace with the deceased.

I kept waiting for him to look at me. He didn't. That was the worst part. Seven years of living above his bookshop, seven years of him reading my silences the way other people read headlines, and now he wouldn't so much as glance in my direction.

He knows, I told myself. Of course he knows. He's known since the night I came home smelling of marsh water with a borrowed key in my pocket and a lie on my tongue. He's known, and he's been deciding what to do about it, and this room — this table, this tea, this frost-glazed window — is the verdict being typeset.

"Miss Voss," Hale said, not unkindly. He had a notebook open. Everything about Hale was open: his notebook, his face, his terrible tie. "We've been through most of it. Your fingerprints on the grey coat. Your statement about the visitor — which we both know was—" he paused, searching for the gentlest word, "—incomplete."

"Incomplete," I said. "That's one word for it."

"You moved evidence, Miss Voss. You interfered with a scene. You lied to an officer of the law." He said it all in the tone of a man reciting a shopping list, which somehow made it worse. "Now, Mr. Ishikawa here has asked for a few minutes before we proceed, and I've granted them, but I want you to understand where you stand."

I understood exactly where I stood. I stood at the edge of everything, looking down.

Three weeks ago, a man had come to Hollowmere wearing his brother's face and carrying his brother's fury. Three weeks ago, I had begged him to leave, and when he wouldn't, I had done the only thing my panicked heart could think of: I'd dressed him in clothes from the stockroom like a paper doll, grey coat and all, and pointed him toward the night road. And then someone else had followed him out into the dark, and Daniel Voss's brother had ended up dead on the marsh road wearing my fingerprints on his collar.

I had spent every day since waiting for the knock. Not Hale's knock. Emrys's. The quiet one, the disappointed one, the one where he sets down his cup and says *Wren, sit down* in the voice he used for mispriced first editions.

The door opened. A constable looked in, murmured something to Hale, and left. Hale gathered his notebook, gave me a look I couldn't parse — pity? apology? indigestion? — and stepped out into the corridor.

And then we were alone, Emrys and I, in a room built for confessions.

* * *

"Wren."

There it was. My name in his mouth, two syllables, worn smooth as a river stone from seven years of use. *Wren, mind the till. Wren, you've put Milton under poetry again. Wren, come down, the fog's come in off the marsh and I don't like you walking it alone.*

"I'm sorry." It came out before I could dress it up. "I'm sorry about the coat, and the key, and the lies, and — I didn't kill him. I need you to know that first, before anything else, whatever happens after. I didn't touch him. I put him on the road alive and angry and breathing, and I swear to God I would give anything—"

"I know."

Two words. He said them the way he shelved books — carefully, in their proper place.

"You know?" My voice cracked somewhere in the middle. "How could you possibly—"

"Because I watched you carry a spider outside rather than kill it. Because you cried for a week over a hedgehog. Because in seven years I have never once seen you do a cruel thing, and murder is only cruelty with consequences attached." He finally looked at me then, and his eyes were tired in a way I hadn't seen since Marian died. "You lied to protect someone. I've been doing the same thing for longer than you've been alive."

"That doesn't make sense," I whispered.

"No," he agreed. "It rarely does."

Hale came back in. Behind him came the smell of rain and corridor coffee, and behind that, I realized, came the shape of the moment I'd been dreading. This was it. Emrys was going to tell Hale everything — the visitor, the stockroom, the ledger, all of it — and I would sit here and let him, because what right did I have to stop him? I had lied to a policeman. I had moved a dead man's effects. I had, in the small hours of too many nights, wondered if some part of me had known someone would follow that man into the dark, and let him go anyway.

"Mr. Ishikawa," Hale began, settling back into his chair, "before we continue with Miss Voss, there are matters regarding your own statements that—"

"Yes," Emrys said. "Let's do mine first."

I blinked. "Yours?"

Emrys folded his hands again. When he spoke, his voice had the flat, careful calm of a man walking across ice he has already tested.

"Sergeant Hale. Seven years ago, a man named Daniel Voss disappeared from Hollowmere. Everyone believed he abandoned his daughter and ran from debts. I believed it. I repeated it. I let a frightened eleven-year-old girl walk into my bookshop and I never once asked her the questions a detective should have asked, because asking them would have meant admitting that the town I loved — that *I* loved — was capable of burying a boy at Ferrow Mill and paying for silence ever since."

My breath stopped somewhere in my chest.

"Marian knew," he went on. "My wife knew. She kept the accounts. Payments routed through her estate, quarterly, for years — hush money from four families to keep a drowning quiet. I found the ledger three weeks ago and I chose, for a while, to move slowly. To be careful. And my slowness cost time that a murderer used, and nearly cost this girl her freedom. So before you write down another word about Wren Voss's lies, Sergeant, you'll write down mine. I obstructed justice by omission for seven years. I protected the guilty because they were my neighbours, my customers, my friends. Every entry in that ledger passed through a house I shared. That's where you should start."

The room went very quiet. Even the radiator seemed to hold its breath.

Hale stared at him. Then, slowly, he turned his notebook to a fresh page. "Mr. Ishikawa, you understand that aiding and abetting—"

"I understand perfectly. I've read the statutes. I helped write half of them before I retired." A ghost of the old dry smile. "Write it down, Sergeant. All of it. If Wren Voss is to answer for three weeks of fear-driven foolishness, then I will answer for seven years of comfortable cowardice, and we will see whose account weighs more at the end of the day."

"But she *lied*—"

"She lied the way a child lies when the house is on fire — badly, desperately, and to buy seconds. I lied the way adults lie: efficiently, and for profit of a kind. Comfort. Reputation. The luxury of not knowing." He turned to me at last, fully, and what was in his face undid me completely. It wasn't disappointment. It wasn't even forgiveness. It was something older and quieter — the look of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. "Wren. Say your piece. All of it. The truth is the only currency this town hasn't debased yet, and I intend to spend every coin I have."

So I did. I told Hale about the man in the grey coat, about the proof he carried, about how he'd sworn his brother hadn't run — that Daniel had left to gather evidence against the pact and never came back. I told him about stealing the clothes, about the night road, about hearing footsteps behind the visitor that I'd assumed were his own echo. I told him about moving the ledger page, about the key, about every small sin in order, alphabetized, because that was how Emrys had taught me to keep a clear head.

Hale wrote it all down. His pen scratched like a mouse in a wall.

When I finished, he looked up, and something in his face had shifted — not softness exactly, but a recalibration. "For the record," he said, "the churchwarden has confessed. Mr. Ishikawa's recording saw to that. Which means, Miss Voss, that your statements corroborate rather than contradict. There may still be charges. Obstruction isn't nothing."

"I know," I said.

"But," Hale continued, and here he closed his notebook, which felt significant, "a magistrate tends to weigh a terrified twenty-year-old differently when the alternative narrative involves a respected churchwarden strangling a man to protect four families' secrets. I'll make my report accordingly. And Mr. Ishikawa—" he hesitated, and for a moment the terrible tie seemed almost sympathetic, "—what you've just given me will have consequences. You understand that."

"I'm counting on it," Emrys said mildly. "Consequences are how a town proves it's still alive."

* * *

They let us go near midnight. Rain had started again, the fine sideways kind that Hollowmere specialized in, and we walked home through it without umbrellas because neither of us thought of it, or because both of us did and neither wanted to break the strange new silence between us.

Halfway down the marsh road — the same road, though it looked different now, washed and ordinary — I finally said it.

"Why?"

Emrys walked a few more steps before answering. The lamps made halos in the wet air.

"Because duty isn't punishment, Wren. I spent seven years thinking it was — that doing the right thing meant making someone bleed for the wrong. Marian's ledger taught me otherwise. Duty is just telling the truth in the right order, at the right time, no matter whose name comes up. Including mine." He glanced at me, fog-grey eyes catching the lamplight. "Especially mine."

"And what happens to you now?"

"What happens to any book that tells the truth," he said. "It gets challenged. Banned, maybe. Read anyway." 

Ahead of us, the bookshop windows glowed warm against the dark, and through the glass I could see the shelf where Marian's photograph kept watch over the cookery section — over the recipe book that was no recipe book at all, its last cipher cracked, its final payment exposed, its secrets dragged blinking into the light.

But as we reached the door, Emrys stopped with his hand on the latch, and I saw his expression change — that particular narrowing, the detective waking after seven years asleep.

"Wren," he said quietly. "The ledger listed five payments. Four families signed the pact." He pushed the door open onto the dark shop, and the smell of paper and rain and something faintly wrong. "Someone has been inside."
