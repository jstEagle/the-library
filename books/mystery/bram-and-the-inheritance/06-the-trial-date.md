## 6. The Trial Date

Detective Imre Kade had a theory about conference rooms: the nicer the furniture, the worse the news. The one on the fourth floor of the county courthouse had leather chairs that sighed when you sat in them, a view of the parking garage, and a carafe of water nobody ever poured. It was where good investigations went to be flattened into convenient shapes.

District Attorney Marisol Quist was already there when Kade arrived, standing at the window with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a door that kept trying to open on its own.

"Sit down, Imre."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would." Quist turned. She was fifty-one, sharp-eyed, and had the particular exhaustion of someone who'd spent the morning on the phone with people richer than God. "Do you know who owns the Bramwell Grand?"

"An LLC," Kade said. "Three layers deep. I looked."

"Hollis Fenwick. He's the man behind all three layers, and this morning he was behind me." Quist poured herself water after all, which told Kade everything. Marisol Quist never drank the courthouse water; she said it tasted like depositions. "He wants this closed. Quickly. Quietly. A concierge in custody reads as *solved*. A concierge awaiting trial reads as *handled*."

"And if he's innocent?"

"Then his lawyer will have a very interesting year." Quist set the glass down. "We're charging Ulyana Eastwick with the murder of Gerald Ostrander. Trial date is six weeks out. I'm telling you because you'll hear it from a reporter by lunch otherwise, and I'd rather you heard it from me while I still have some credibility left to spend."

Kade felt something cold settle behind her ribs. Not surprise — she'd watched this particular train coming for days, lights flashing, horn blaring — but a kind of professional grief. "The evidence is circumstantial."

"The suit is his."

"The suit could've been taken from his closet. By anyone with access to the hotel."

"The victim's appointment book has Eastwick's name in it."

"Which I flagged as possibly planted, or at minimum unexplained. Ostrander was an insurance investigator, Marisol. He made appointments for a living. Half the town could be in that book."

Quist rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Imre. Sit down. Please."

Kade sat. The chair sighed. Even the furniture was tired of her.

"I believe you have doubts," Quist said, more gently now. "I've read your reports. They're careful. Almost too careful — you write like a woman building a wall one brick at a time so nobody notices there's no house behind it. But here's my problem. The prosecutor's office doesn't run on doubts. It runs on probability, pressure, and the calendar. And right now all three are pointing at the Bramwell Grand's front desk."

"What if I find something?"

"Then bring it to me before the trial, not during. I hate surprises more than I hate losing." She paused at the door. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much this week — I hope you're right. Fenwick's phone calls had the flavor of a man protecting something bigger than a reputation."

She left. Kade stayed in the sighing chair a moment longer, watching dust drift through the light, and thought about how institutions were like hotels: both were designed to make you feel that everything was under control, right up until the moment you found the body.

* * *

Back at the precinct, Kade did the thing she was best at, the thing that had made three different supervisors call her "a dog with a bone" and one of them mean it as a compliment: she went back to the beginning.

The service stairwell of the Bramwell Grand, third floor landing. Crime scene photos spread across her desk like a deck of terrible cards. Gerald Ostrander, sixty-eight years old, retired from Meridian Mutual Insurance, lying at the base of the flight with his neck at an angle necks don't go. Dressed in a charcoal suit that Kade had personally watched Ulyana Eastwick confirm, with the flat calm of a man reading his own obituary, was his spare — kept in the staff locker room, second row, third hook.

Kade had walked that stairwell four times now. Officially twice, unofficially twice more, once at night when the hotel was busy pretending she didn't exist.

Here was what bothered her. Not the suit. Suits could be stolen. Not the appointment book — books could be planted. What bothered her was the *geometry*.

She pinned the security camera map to her corkboard. The Bramwell Grand had eleven cameras. A sensible layout, installed in 2011 after a jewelry heist in the ballroom that the owner had never reported to police but had definitely reported to his insurance company. Kade had stood in the corridor outside the stairwell door and checked the sightlines herself.

Camera seven covered the main corridor. Camera nine covered the service entrance to the kitchen. And between them — precisely between them, in a pocket of dead air roughly forty seconds wide if you walked at an unhurried pace — sat the stairwell door.

Forty seconds. Kade had timed it. You had to *know* the gap existed. It wasn't marked on any guest-facing document. It wasn't in the employee handbook — she'd checked, mostly out of spite. It lived in the institutional memory of people who had worked the building long enough to stop seeing corridors and start seeing routes.

Ulyana Eastwick had worked there twenty-two years. He would know the blind spot the way he knew his own pulse.

But here was the thing. So would anyone else who'd worked there long enough. A chambermaid. A bellhop. A night manager.

Kade pulled the personnel files she'd requested two weeks ago and had received yesterday, in a folder so thin it felt apologetic. Turnover at the Bramwell Grand was low — another point in its favor, another brick in its wall. But fifteen years ago, the night manager had resigned. No forwarding address. No exit interview notes beyond a single line in the HR system: *resigned, personal reasons.*

Dara Voss. Hired 1999, age twenty-four. Resigned 2010.

Kade stared at the grainy employee photo. A young woman with dark hair pulled back, standing slightly apart from the group in what was clearly a staff anniversary picture. Everyone else smiled at the camera. Voss smiled at something just past it, as if she'd already seen the joke coming.

Kade wrote the name on an index card and pinned it below the camera map. Then she drew a line from Voss to the stairwell, and another line from the stairwell to the words *forty seconds*, and sat back.

It proved nothing. She knew that. A resignation fifteen years ago was not a murder last month. People left jobs; people even left towns. But instinct — the thing Quist's calendar had no column for — whispered that the geometry of this crime had been drawn by someone who had learned the building's secrets from the inside. And there were exactly two categories of people who fit: the man in custody, and everyone he might have taught.

* * *

At six p.m., with the precinct emptying around her and someone's microwave burrito perfuming the bullpen, Kade did the other thing she was best at: she ignored the case she was assigned and opened the one she wasn't.

The 2005 file came up from archives in a box that smelled like a basement's regrets. *Eastwick, incident report, October 2005.* She'd skimmed it before — a slip on the service stairs, a chambermaid named Petra Ilves, twenty-three years old, dead of head trauma, ruled accidental. Open and shut. The kind of file that went into boxes and stayed there.

But Kade had learned something about open-and-shut files in twenty years of police work: they were usually shut by somebody with strong opinions about staying shut.

She read it slowly this time.

The responding officer's notes were thin. Witness statements: three, all staff, all brief, all using suspiciously similar phrasing — *she was carrying linens, she must have slipped.* The same phrase, nearly word for word, in three separate statements, as though someone had handed out a script along with the grief counseling. One statement was signed by the night manager on duty.

Dara Voss.

Kade set the page down and looked at it for a long time. On her corkboard, forty feet of cork away, the index card with Voss's name caught the desk lamp's light.

Two cases, fifteen years apart, both touching the same stairwell. Both touched by the same night manager. And in between them, a retired insurance investigator who had spent his final weeks asking quiet questions about a death that everyone in 2005 had agreed, with remarkable coordination, not to examine too closely.

Gerald Ostrander hadn't been investigating Ulyana Eastwick. Kade was almost sure of it now. He'd been investigating 2005 — and the concierge's name had been in his appointment book because the concierge was a witness, not a killer. Possibly the only living witness who knew what really happened on those stairs.

Which meant whoever killed Ostrander hadn't been silencing him about a suit.

They'd been silencing him about a fall.

Kade took out a fresh index card. At the top she wrote: *Petra Ilves, 10/14/05.* Below it: *Who benefits?* Below that, after a moment, she added: *And why does the owner of this hotel want the case closed in six weeks?*

She pinned it to the board, in the center, where she'd see it every time she looked up.

Outside, the first snow of the season was starting, fat and unhurried, settling over the courthouse, the precinct, and the Bramwell Grand with its eleven cameras and its forty-second gaps. Somewhere in that building, a man sat in a cell he technically wasn't in yet, alphabetizing his own defense, waiting for a trial that Kade was increasingly certain was built on a foundation of someone else's sins.

She reached for the phone, then stopped. There was one person who knew what happened in 2005 — and he was currently the state's prime suspect, which meant anything he said was worth exactly as much as a jury believed him.

Unless she found corroboration first.

Kade put on her coat, tucked the 2005 file under her arm, and headed for the archives basement, where the real records lived — payroll logs, shift schedules, maintenance requests. If Dara Voss had been on duty the night Petra Ilves died, Kade wanted the paper trail. And if Voss had vanished fifteen years ago without a forwarding address...

Well. Nobody vanished completely. Not anymore. Not with databases, and tax records, and the stubborn human habit of needing a paycheck.

She punched the elevator button. Somewhere across town, she'd have bet her badge, a woman who once ran the Bramwell Grand at night was living under a name that wasn't hers, hoping the past stayed filed away.

Kade intended to start pulling files.
