## 4. The People Who Keep the Lights On

The diner on Delancey opened at five, which meant it was really a night establishment that had given up and turned the lights on. Gideon had been coming here after shift for six years, long enough that the waitress, Dot, stopped asking what he wanted and just brought it: eggs scrambled soft, rye toast, coffee that could strip paint and probably had. She'd started writing *Gideon* on the ticket instead of a table number around year two. He'd never told her how much that meant. Some things you kept in the ledger you didn't show anyone.

They took the corner booth the way they always did—Ray sliding in first, big shoulders filling the window seat, Priya folding herself in beside him with her backpack still on like a kid who might bolt, Sal at the end nursing a ginger ale at seven in the morning because his stomach had been "acting philosophical" since Tuesday.

"Philosophical," Ray repeated, shaking his head. "Your stomach doesn't have opinions, Sal."

"Mine does. It's a stoic. It accepts what it cannot change and then it gurgles."

Priya laughed into her coffee, and Gideon felt the old familiar warmth of it, the booth like a lifeboat, the six of them—counting Dot as a maybe—adrift together in the hours the rest of the city pretended didn't exist. Night people. The ones who kept the lights on so the day people could sleep believing the world was still running.

Ray was telling a story about a fare, a bride whose wedding car died on the overpass and who'd arrived at her own ceremony in a cab with a pine air freshener swinging from the mirror and a bumper sticker that said HONK IF YOU LOVE SOMETHING. "She tipped me forty bucks and cried on my shoulder. Her mascara on my shirt, Gideon. I looked like a raccoon had died on me."

"You charged her a cleaning fee?"

"I gave her my number in case she needed a ride to the reception. Married people always need rides."

Priya was shaking her head, smiling. "You're a soft touch, Ray."

"I'm a professional," Ray said with dignity, and stole a piece of Gideon's toast, which was also professional, apparently, because he'd been doing it for years and Gideon had never once objected.

This was the thing Gideon could never explain to day people: that a family could be built out of shift work and shared coffee, out of the particular trust of people who talked to each other through radio static at three a.m. He'd solved three deaths from this booth, in a way—from this booth he'd watched the police close files that should have stayed open, and he'd gone home and opened them himself, quietly, in the margins of his life. None of them knew. He was the quiet one, the invisible one, the dispatcher whose voice lived in their ears but whose face they'd struggle to describe.

He'd thought that was a kind of safety. Sitting there with toast crumbs on his sleeve, he was starting to think it was a kind of hiding.

* * *

Ray Okonkwo had been driving nights for nineteen years, and he could read a fare before they got the door open. The drunk who'd cry. The couple about to break up, riding in silence like the cab was a hearse for the relationship. The kid running from something, eyes in the mirror the whole way.

Gideon, he'd never figured out. Gideon was a closed book, and Ray had stopped trying to read him around year three and just started liking him, which was easier. The man remembered everything—every fare, every street, every driver's kid's birthday—and said almost nothing about himself. Ray figured everybody kept a ledger. Gideon's just happened to be internal.

So when the waitress dropped the coffees and Gideon said, casual as weather, "You all remember Vera Ostrander? The auditor who was in a few weeks back," Ray felt something tighten behind his ribs, some instinct from nineteen years of night streets.

"Sure," Ray said. "Quiet girl. Nice manners. Left a good tip."

"You drive her home once, didn't you? The night she audited the fare records?"

"Maybe. I drive a lot of people." He reached for the syrup. It was true. It was also a door closing, and he heard it click.

Priya was studying the menu she'd had memorized for a year. Sal had developed a sudden fascination with the salt shaker.

Ray didn't like the feeling. He liked these people. He'd have taken a bullet for any of them, or at least a flat tire on the expressway at four a.m., which was its own kind of martyrdom. But there was a question in Gideon's voice that wasn't really a question, and everyone at the table had just decided, in the same silent instant, not to hear it.

He changed the subject. Told another story. Watched Gideon watch all of them, patient as a fare meter, and thought: *what are you counting?*

* * *

Gideon let it go. That was the technique—let it go, and watch what comes back.

Priya recovered first, the way young people do, filling silence with talk about her dispatch certification, about how she'd finally nailed the priority-call triage drill. "Marisol says I could sit a full board solo by spring."

"She's right," Gideon said, and meant it. Priya was good. Priya listened the way he did—not to reply, but to hear what wasn't said. It was why he'd noticed, three weeks ago, that she'd gone quiet when the auditor's name came up in the break room. He'd filed it. He was filing it now, watching her hands wrap around her mug like it was something that could be held onto.

"Sal," Gideon said, turning, easy, friendly. "You worked on 44 that week, right? The unit Vera took her breaks in. You had it on the lift?"

"Did I?" Sal's face did something complicated. "That week's a blur, Gid. You know how it is. Grease and overtime." He grinned, and the grin was a shade too wide, a shade too fast. "Why the interest? She's been gone near a month. Sad business, but the police—"

"The police closed it," Gideon said. "Runaway. Credit cards unused, apartment tidy, no note." He let that sit. "Tidy apartments bother me."

"Maybe she was just a tidy person," Sal said.

"Maybe," Gideon agreed, and smiled, and inside himself he marked the moment the way he marked everything: *deflection, subject change, alibi offered unprompted.* Three for three. Ray, Priya, Sal. His whole family, each one closing a door when Vera Ostrander's name walked into the room.

He didn't believe any of them were guilty. That was the terrible part. He knew these people down to their coffee orders. But he knew something else too, from three cases the police wouldn't touch: guilty people aren't the only ones who lie. Scared people lie. Loyal people lie. People who saw something they shouldn't have, or were asked to do something small and wrong for a reason that felt big and right—those people lie best of all, because they're not lying to protect themselves.

They're lying to protect someone at this table.

* * *

Priya walked out with Gideon into the gray beginning of morning, the sky the color of dishwater, the diner sign buzzing off. Ray had gone to his cab; Sal had left first, abruptly, mid-sentence about a transmission, which wasn't like him.

"You asked about Vera," Priya said, on the sidewalk, not looking at him. "At breakfast."

"Did I?"

"You're doing the thing where you ask and it's not asking." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were sharp and scared at once. "Gideon. Why do you care about her? The police said—"

"The police say a lot of things." He kept his voice gentle. He liked this kid. He didn't want to frighten her, and he was about to. "Priya. Did Vera ever talk to you? Ask you anything? About the fare system, the logs, how dispatch records trips?"

The pause was half a second too long. He watched her decide something.

"She asked me once how long we keep trip records," Priya said. "I told her. That's all. That's all there is." And she hurried to her bus stop like the sidewalk was on fire.

Gideon stood in the dishwater light and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. *How long do you keep trip records.* Not a question a runaway asks. A question someone building a map asks.

He thought of the diary in his desk drawer at the depot, its post-disappearance entries in that cramped, frightened hand. *They count the cars that never move.* He thought of Marisol catching him at the terminal, her warning wrapped in worry. He thought of Sal's too-wide grin, Ray's closing door, Priya's half-second pause.

Four people. Four small lies. And somewhere underneath all of them, the truth, sitting in the dark like a cab with its lights off, waiting for a fare.

He started walking toward the depot, and behind him the diner sign buzzed on for the day crew, handing over the lights, and Gideon understood with sudden clarity that whatever he found in the fare records, he would not be finding it alone much longer—and that the next person to ask him a question that wasn't really a question might be someone he loved, asking whether he was the one who couldn't be trusted.
