## 8. The Trap We Build Together

The police station sat four blocks from the depot, and Gideon had walked past it maybe a thousand times without once going in. He stood across the street now, coffee going cold in his hand, and tried to imagine the conversation.

*Officer, I have a diary written by a missing woman, entries dated after her disappearance, and a theory about money laundering built out of taxi fares. Also my mechanic might have done it. Or possibly my boss. Or the owner. One of us.*

They'd laugh him into a holding cell for wasting time. Worse—they'd ask questions he couldn't answer without burning everyone he loved. Vera's name would go into a system. The diary would go into an evidence locker with a paper trail a mile long, and whoever had ransacked his office would know exactly where it sat before the sun came up.

He turned around and walked back to the depot instead.

Priya was at the dispatch desk, feet tucked under her on the chair, working through the night's trip logs with the fierce concentration of someone who still believed paperwork mattered. Twenty-three years old, three months into the job, still said *copy that* like she meant it. He'd trained her himself. He knew every tell she had.

"Priya," he said. "I need to show you something. And then I need you to decide if you want to be part of it."

She looked up. Whatever was in his face made her set down her pen.

* * *

He told her everything. The diary, the phantom routes, the cipher of fares, Sal's debts and too-clean alibi, the dead drop behind the laundromat, the office torn apart while he'd been tailing it. He laid the photocopies—the only copies now—across the dispatch desk like a losing hand of cards.

"The police can't help yet," he said. "Not because they're bad cops. Because we don't have anything a prosecutor could hold. A stolen diary isn't proof of who stole it. A dead drop isn't proof of who used it. Right now all I've got is a shape where a person should be."

"So we make them leave fingerprints on the shape," Priya said.

Gideon blinked. "That's exactly what I was going to say."

"You were going to say it slower." She pulled her chair closer to the desk. "Okay. What do we plant?"

"Vera hid the real accounts inside fake trip logs. Whoever took the diary wants to read the cipher—and they can't, not fully, because the last page is keyed to something only a dispatcher would recognize." He tapped the terminal. "So we give them a page that looks like the key. Forged fare records, route numbers that map to a location. Make it point somewhere specific enough that they have to go look."

"Where?"

"Storage unit. Harbor Self-Storage, unit 114. I rented it this morning under a name I haven't used since I was nineteen." He almost smiled. "There's nothing inside but a cheap camera and a note that says *I know who you are.*"

"Gideon." Priya said his name carefully, the way you'd test ice before stepping on it. "If they take the bait, they'll know it's a trap the second they open that door."

"No. They'll think Vera left a second copy. That's what I want them to believe—that the ledger exists twice, that destroying one doesn't save them. Panic makes people move fast. Fast people make mistakes."

Priya studied him for a long moment. "You've done this before. The other three cases."

"I noticed things," he said. "This is the first time I'm building the thing I notice."

"And if it's Sal? If it's actually Sal?" Her voice dropped. "Sal fixed my car when I couldn't afford it. He didn't charge me. He just said *you're night crew now, we take care of each other.*"

Gideon looked at the photocopies spread across the desk—at Vera's careful handwriting, her terror compressed into numbers—and felt the weight of what Priya was really asking. *What happens to us if it's one of us?*

"Then we were wrong about what family means," he said. "But it doesn't stop being true either way."

* * *

They planted the forged page at two in the morning, folded into the dispatch logbook—the one place a thief who'd already searched the office would check first, because thieves return to scenes of success. Gideon did the folding. Priya logged a dummy fare at 2:14 so the terminal showed the office occupied, giving them cover to sit in the dark of the break room with the door cracked open and wait.

Waiting, it turned out, was its own kind of confession. In the dark, Priya whispered questions Gideon didn't have answers to. *How long has Marisol known? Do you think Ray knows? What if it's nobody and Vera just ran away?* He let her ask. Asking was how you kept the fear moving instead of letting it pool.

At 3:41, the outer door opened.

Footsteps crossed the office floor—not hurrying, not sneaking. Unhurried steps meant keys, meant belonging, meant someone whose presence in the depot at 3:41 required no explanation. Gideon's stomach dropped through six floors of concrete, because he knew those footsteps the way you know a song from the first bar.

Ray Okonkwo stopped at the dispatch desk. Through the crack in the break room door, Gideon watched him stand over the logbook for a long moment—a big man silhouetted against the terminal glow, motionless, as if praying or counting. Then he opened the logbook, took the folded page, and put it in his jacket pocket.

He didn't even look around. That was the worst part. He walked out the way he'd walked in, certain no one was watching, and the door sighed shut behind him.

Priya's hand found Gideon's sleeve in the dark. "No," she breathed. "No, that's not—it's Ray. It can't be Ray."

Gideon couldn't speak. Something in his chest had come loose and was swinging, breaking things on the way past. Ray, who drove the 11-to-7 because his daughter's school started at eight. Ray, who kept a photo of his kids taped inside his visor and turned it face-down on the bad nights so it wouldn't distract him. Ray, who had taught Gideon, back in his first year, that the night shift wasn't a job you survived but a family you kept.

*Family.* The word turned over and showed its underside.

"He's being paid," Gideon said finally. "Or he's being pushed."

"You saw the dead drop. You saw Sal near it. How does Ray fit?"

"Ray doesn't fit anywhere. That's what scares me." Gideon stood, joints protesting. "Ray is the most predictable man in this building. Same routes, same diner order, same jokes. A man like that doesn't steal unless something's got a gun to his life."

"Then we follow him."

"No." Gideon was already pulling on his coat. "We follow the page. If he delivers it, we find out who's holding the gun. But Priya—" He stopped at the break room door and made himself turn around. "Whatever we see tonight, whatever it turns out to be, you stay in the car. Promise me."

"You promised me I could be part of it."

"Part of it. Not all of it." He held her eyes until she looked away. "I've spent my whole life invisible, Priya. Turns out there's exactly one good use for that."

* * *

Ray's cab left the lot at 4:02, running dark—no meter flag, no dispatch entry. Gideon followed in his own ancient sedan with the headlights off until the streetlights did the work, keeping three cars back through the empty downtown, out along the harbor road where the sodium lamps thinned and the water showed black between the pilings.

Ray didn't go to the storage units. He went to the marina.

He parked in the gravel lot by the fuel dock, sat for five minutes with his dome light off, then got out and walked down the pier to a white-hulled boat riding low at slip nine. Gideon left his car behind the bait shop and came along the shore side, close enough to hear the water slap the hulls.

A man was waiting on the boat's aft deck. Even in the dark, even at forty yards, Gideon knew the silhouette—the broad shoulders, the way he stood like the world owed him rent. Dale Voss.

Voss took the page from Ray's hand. Read it under a small flashlight. And then he laughed—short, satisfied, carrying flat across the water—and clapped Ray on the shoulder the way you'd pat a dog that fetched well.

Gideon pressed himself against a piling and felt the night reorganize itself around him. Not Sal. Not Marisol. Voss had never needed to search the office, because he'd had a key all along—a driver's key, a trusted one. *Threat to his family,* Gideon thought, and pictured the photo taped inside Ray's visor, and felt sick at how easily loyalty became a leash.

Ray said something Gideon couldn't catch. Voss answered, and the tone alone told Gideon everything: casual, bored, absolute. An employer reminding an employee of the terms.

Then Voss paused. Turned his head slightly, toward the shore, toward the darkness between the bait shop and the pier.

Gideon didn't breathe.

Ten seconds. Fifteen. A gull complained somewhere up the coast. Voss turned back, said one more thing to Ray—shorter now, sharper—and went below deck. Ray stood alone on the pier for a moment, shoulders sagging like a man setting down something heavier than paper, and walked back to his cab.

Gideon gave him ten minutes' head start, then sat in his car behind the bait shop with his hands shaking on the wheel, doing arithmetic he hated.

Voss had the page. Which meant Voss would go to unit 114. Which meant Voss would find the camera, the note, the trap—and understand that someone in his depot had built it.

Which meant that sometime in the next few hours, Dale Voss was going to start hunting the hunter.

And Gideon realized, with a calm that frightened him more than fear would have, that he hadn't planted the only message tonight. Somewhere in that forged page, in the route numbers he'd chosen, in the pattern only a dispatcher could read—he'd hidden a second cipher of his own.

One that named Bay Nine.
