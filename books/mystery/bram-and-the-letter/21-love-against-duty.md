## 21. Love Against Duty

The weeks after a scandal are strange animals. Everyone expects them to be dramatic — headlines, hearings, the satisfying clank of handcuffs — and then instead you get forms. Forms in triplicate. Forms asking you to describe, in your own words, the events of the night of the fourteenth of March, as though language were adequate to the task of explaining why the Dean of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral had tried to drown an archivist in his own crypt.

I filled out the forms. I am, whatever else may be said of me, good at forms.

What I was not good at — what I discovered I was catastrophically bad at — was the question that arrived on cream-colored paper from the Crown Prosecution Service, typed by someone with a firm hand and no imagination: *Please provide a complete account of all evidence in your possession relating to the above matter.*

All evidence. In my possession.

I sat at my desk in the archive with the letter spread out before me — the one from the lead tube in the north wall, the one naming three heirs dispossessed by a rewritten will — and I understood, finally, what a trap made entirely of truth looks like. Because the letter was not the only thing in my possession. There was also Maren's confession, given to me in the flood-lit quiet of the conservation room: how she'd been hired, anonymously, generously paid, to razor a page from the 1989 burial register. How she hadn't done it. How she'd hidden the page instead, inside the binding of a water-damaged psalter nobody would ever open, because she couldn't bring herself to cut history, even when cutting it might have saved her.

If I gave the prosecution everything, they would ask where the missing page was. They would ask who else knew. And Maren's name would travel from my mouth to a courtroom to a newspaper, and she would become *the conservator hired to falsify records* — guilty of intent, if not of act, in the eyes of everyone who had never spent a sleepless night holding a scalpel over vellum and choosing not to use it.

Harrow had killed five people to protect his inheritance. The system that would judge him was the same system that would grind Maren fine for a crime she'd refused to commit.

Duty, as I understood it professionally, was very clear. Duty said: the archive is whole or it is nothing. Duty said: you do not curate truth. Duty said, in the voice of every archivist who ever lived: *the record is sacred.*

The record, I thought, looking at the psalter on its foam cradle, does not love anybody back.

* * *

Okonkwo came to see me on a Tuesday, which felt deliberate; Tuesdays are the most bureaucratic day of the week and he knew I'd be in a mood to match him.

He sat across from my desk, took off his glasses, cleaned them with the hem of his tie, and put them back on. This was his version of clearing his throat. I'd learned to read him the way I read marginalia — the meaning lives in what's struck through.

"They've scheduled Harrow's committal hearing," he said. "Three weeks. You'll be called."

"I assumed."

"You'll be asked about the evidence chain." He set a folder on my desk but didn't open it, which meant it contained something he wasn't supposed to show me. "The letter from the foundation stones. The will Edith Vane kept in the belfry. Your testimony about the crypt."

"And?"

"And there's a gap." He looked at me with those patient, terrible eyes. "The 1989 burial register has a razored page. Forensics confirmed it months ago. Whoever removed it did so with archival tools, in this building, within the last two years. If the defense finds that before we do—"

"We already know who removed it," I said. "Nobody removed it. That's rather the point."

"Ines." He never used my first name unless he was frightened for me. "Maren Okafor was approached by person or persons unknown and offered money to destroy a record. She accepted the commission. Whatever she did afterward, the approach happened, the payment cleared into her account, and there is a paper trail. Harrow's solicitors will find it. And when they do, they will use her to muddy everything — your testimony, Edith's, mine. A compromised witness attached to the case is worth more to them than ten alibis."

"She wasn't compromised. She refused."

"The record won't say refused. The record will say *hired.*" He leaned forward. "You're the archivist. Tell me what the record says."

I wanted to throw my tea at him. Instead I said, quietly, "The record says what we make it say. That's the whole dirty secret of my profession, Sergeant. Every archive is a series of mercies. Somebody decided what to keep."

"Somebody decided what to keep," he agreed. "And somebody decided what to burn. And I've spent eleven years learning the difference between the two, and I'm telling you now, as a man who owes you his life in a flooded crypt: don't make yourself the difference. Make the truth the difference. All of it."

He left the folder unopened on my desk and went away, and I sat with the dust motes drifting through the archive light like slow snow, and I thought about mercy, which is just another word for editing.

* * *

Maren knew. Of course she knew. She'd been living inside the sentence *they will find out* for two years, wearing it like a coat that fit worse every season.

She came to the archive that evening without being asked, carrying two paper cups of terrible coffee and the particular silence of a person who has decided not to pretend anymore. She set the cups down among the document trays and stood with her back to the window, so the last light caught the gray coming in at her temples.

"Okonkwo talked to you," she said.

"He talks to everyone. It's his superpower."

"Ines." My name again, but from her it landed differently — not a warning, a hand held out. "Don't do anything stupid on my account."

"That's a remarkably specific request for someone who doesn't know what I'm considering."

"I know exactly what you're considering. You've been looking at me for three days like I'm a fragile manuscript and you're deciding whether to bind me or bury me." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it; I watched her hate it. "I can take care of myself. I've been taking care of myself since before you ever noticed me. If testifying means telling them everything — the approach, the money, the page — then tell them. Don't you dare build your testimony around protecting me. I won't be loved as a redaction."

"Wouldn't dream of it," I said. "Redactions are ugly. I'd have to design the layout."

"Ines."

"Maren." I got up and crossed the room, because some conversations are too far apart to have across desks. "Here is what I believe, and you can argue with me, but you'll lose, because I've had more practice. What you were asked to do was a crime. What you did was refuse it, hide the evidence of the refusal, and then spend two years helping restore the very registers you were paid to gut. The law doesn't have a box for that. The law has boxes for *hired* and boxes for *not hired*, and it will put you in the wrong one, and the wrong box will follow you for the rest of your career. Every register you touch afterward will be touched by a woman who once took money to destroy one. That's not paranoia. That's how institutions work. I work in one."

"So your plan is to lie?"

"My plan," I said, "is to tell the truth in an order of my own choosing. It's the only power an archivist actually has."

She laughed — a wet, broken laugh, but a laugh. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, and I'm horrified by it."

"Wait until you hear my proposal for the hearing."

* * *

The committal hearing was held in a room that smelled of radiators and old carpet, with Harrow brought up between two officers wearing a gray suit that fit him beautifully, because of course it did; the man had built his entire life out of clothes that fit beautifully. He looked rested. He looked, God help us, dignified. He found me in the gallery with his eyes and inclined his head slightly, the way one acknowledges a colleague across a chapter meeting, and I understood that he still believed — would always believe — that we were playing the same game, he and I, two custodians of institutional memory, merely on opposite sides of a technicality.

I took the oath on a Bible I suspected neither of us believed in, and I told the truth.

I told it in order. The drowned man in my coat. The scratches on the crypt lock. The letter in the foundation stones, naming Thomas Aldery, Ruth Calloway, and Edmund Vane — heirs erased by a will rewritten in 1989 by Gerald Harrow, Dean's father, forger, murderer. The exhumation and the sandbags. Edith Vane, alive after thirty-five years in the belfry, holding the original will in oilcloth like a relic. The second body, dressed in my clothes. The crypt. The water rising. Silas ringing the alarm peal like the whole tower was screaming.

Harrow's solicitor rose twice to object to my narrative style — *"the witness is giving a story, not answering questions"* — and both times the magistrate let me continue, because sometimes the truth is allowed to have a plot.

Then the solicitor stood a third time, and I felt Maren go still beside me in the gallery, and I knew what was coming before the man opened his mouth.

"My client instructs that the burial register for 1989 was tampered with. A page removed. We have forensic confirmation of the removal and evidence of a payment made to a contract conservator for precisely this service. We ask whether the witness is aware—"

"I'm aware," I said.

Of course I was aware. I had been aware for three weeks, drafting and redrafting, and here is what I had settled on, the sentence I had built my testimony around like a keystone:

"The page was not removed. I can speak to that with authority, because the page exists, intact, and has been examined by Detective Sergeant Okonkwo, whose report I understand is before this court. Someone was hired to remove it. That person declined to do so and preserved the page instead. I'd ask the court to note the distinction, since my profession depends on it."

The solicitor blinked. "And the person hired? Does the witness know their identity?"

"I know," I said, "that the person walked into this cathedral with a commission to destroy a record, and walked out having saved it. In thirty years of archival work, I can count on one hand the number of people who've passed that particular test under real pressure. Most of us never get tested. She got tested and passed, and the only reason we're discussing it is that Dean Harrow needed a fog machine." I turned to the magistrate. "The defense wants to introduce doubt. Fine. But let's be precise about where the doubt belongs. The only person in this affair who successfully altered the historical record is sitting in the dock."

It was not the whole truth. It was the truth with a mercy folded into it, the way you fold a letter — and I knew, as I said it, that I would carry the fold forever. Archivists are supposed to be flat. Transparent. Every crease visible. I had just creased myself, deliberately, in front of a court, to protect a woman who had begged me not to.

Maren's hand found mine under the gallery rail and gripped hard enough to hurt, and I thought: *duty is what you owe the dead, and love is what you owe the living, and today, for once, I could pay both.*

Harrow watched me the entire time with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Not rage. Something worse — recognition. As though I'd finally shown him what I was made of, and he approved.

* * *

Afterward, Okonkwo walked me out through the courthouse steps into rain that couldn't decide whether to commit.

"You lied by omission," he said, falling into step.

"I testified accurately to every fact within the scope of the questions asked."

"You're going to be a nightmare in heaven."

"I'm not planning to qualify."

He stopped at the bottom of the steps. Rain beaded on his glasses. "For the record — and I want you to hear this from me, not from a subpoena — the page in the psalter. I logged it as recovered material, provenance unclear. Provenance unclear happens. Archives are full of it." He almost smiled. "You taught me that. Everything in a record is a series of mercies. Just—" he tapped the railing once, "—don't make a habit of being the mercy. It corrodes."

"Sergeant Okonkwo. Was that a moral lesson or a favor?"

"It was both. That's the job." He put up his collar. "Go home, Miss Ravensworth. You look like a document left out in the sun."

* * *

Spring was doing its tentative, unconvincing best outside the cathedral when I finally filed the case closed.

Not the official case — that belonged to the courts now, to barristers and verdicts and the long machinery of appeal. I meant my private ledger, the one I'd kept for six years in a drawer disguised as a conservation inventory, the one with three accidents catalogued before I'd known they were chapters of a single book. I wrote the date. I wrote *Ravensworth vs. the Drowned Man, closed.* Underneath, after a moment, I added a line I had never added before:

*One entry withheld. By choice. Knowingly. On purpose.*

An archivist's confession, in her own hand, in her own archive. If some future researcher ever found it, they'd know exactly what I'd done and exactly why, and they could judge me with the benefit of context, which is more than any of us usually gets.

Maren was waiting for me at the west door, where the evening light comes through the glass sideways and turns everyone golden whether they deserve it or not. She had the psalter under her arm — the real one, the one with the page sewn back where it belonged, restored so cleanly that only the two of us would ever know the scar was there.

"Edith wants tea on Thursday," she said. "She says if she's going to be a living legend she intends to be catered accordingly. And Silas has offered to ring a peal for the reopening. A proper one. He says the bells remember, and it's time they were allowed to say so out loud."

"The bells remember," I said. "Let's hope they keep better records than I do."

We walked out together under the door, past the stone saints who had watched everything and said nothing for eight hundred years, and behind us the crypt waited with its new door — unlocked, lit, open to whoever came asking. I had spent my life believing the record was sacred. I still believed it. I had simply learned that the record is made of people, and people are made of choices, and somewhere between the two, mercy lives — folded small, pressed flat, filed away where only the patient will ever find it.

In my ledger, the ink was drying on the last line. I locked the drawer.

Some archives, I've decided, are allowed one sealed stone.
