## 3. What Ruthie Kept

The call log didn't lie. That was the one thing in my life that never lied.

I pulled it at six a.m., before the day shift came in, before anyone could ask me why I was reading a dead man's name off our own board. Danny Voss. October 14th, 11:52 p.m. Pickup requested from the Greyhound depot on Callahan, destination entered as *home*. The dispatcher who took it was a kid named Okafor, three months on the job. He told me the caller sounded tired. Said the man on the line gave an address on Fenwick Street and then, before hanging up, said something strange.

"What kind of strange?" I asked.

Okafor shrugged. "He said, *tell August I'm coming in from the cold.* Then he laughed. Like it was a joke."

I'm coming in from the cold. Danny Voss had spent thirty years out there, and he'd called our company — *my* company — to bring him in. He never made it to the pickup. The cab Okafor sent found an empty curb and a fare who'd stopped existing somewhere between the depot and Fenwick Street.

Which meant Danny's last living act, or one of them, was reaching for me. And I'd been asleep three blocks away, dreaming about nothing.

I sat in my car in the depot lot and let that settle like a stone in my chest. Then I drove to Fenwick Street, because there was only one person in this world who still lived on it.

* * *

Ruthie's house sat at the dead end where Fenwick gives up and becomes gravel. Same aluminum awning, same wind chime made of flattened spoons that she'd hung when we were kids. I hadn't stood on her porch since the trial. Thirty years, and the spoons still turned in the wind with that thin, broken-bell sound.

She opened the door before I knocked twice. She'd been crying, or she hadn't slept, or both. Her hair was white now and cut short, and she had Danny's journal in her hands.

I hadn't told anyone I had copies of those pages. Reyes had them. The journal was evidence. So the fact that Ruthie was holding one meant Danny had sent her one too — or she'd been the one keeping it safe all along.

"You knew," I said.

"I knew he was coming back." She stepped aside to let me in. The house smelled like coffee and lavender and something older, something like the inside of a locked room. "I've known for two months. He called me from wherever he'd been living. Boise. He said he couldn't do it over the phone. He said he had to say it to our faces."

"And you didn't tell me."

"August." She said my name the way you'd set down something fragile. "You were the one who got out. You had a life. A record nobody could touch. I wasn't going to be the one who dug all that up again."

I sat on her couch because my legs decided for me. "He was going to recant. Wasn't he."

She nodded. She set the journal on the coffee table between us like it was a bomb she'd been defusing for thirty years.

"He practiced it," she said. "Out loud. In my kitchen, two weeks ago. I drove him everywhere that week — he didn't rent a car, he didn't trust them, he said. He'd sit right there—" she pointed at the chair I was sitting in, and I swear the cushion went cold under me "—and he'd rehearse. *I, Daniel Voss, do hereby recant my testimony given on March 3rd, 1994.* He'd say the whole thing, start to finish, and then he'd start over. He wanted it perfect. He wanted to look at you when he said it."

"Me."

"You especially." Her hands were folded tight in her lap, knuckles like river stones. "He said you were the one he owed. Your alibi, August. The one you gave him. He said he'd carried it thirty years like a stone in his shoe and he was done."

The alibi. Mine and Danny's, braided together the night Gerald Marsh's store burned and old man Marsh went into the ground. The night the state needed a witness and Danny gave them one, and the night I gave Danny something to hold the state off — a story about the two of us at the all-night diner, splitting fries, splitting the truth down the middle. My foster father went to prison on Danny's word. Our house came apart like wet paper. Marisol aged ten years in a summer. Teddy went a different direction entirely. And Ruthie went quiet, the way she'd always been quiet, so quiet we all forgot she was a person who saw things.

"Tell me about the night he died," I said.

She looked at the window. The spoon chime turned and turned.

"He called me at eleven. He was at the depot, he said, and he was scared. He said someone had been leaving him notes. *We know what you're planning.* That kind of thing. He asked me to come get him instead of a cab, because he didn't want his name on any more paper than it already was. But I'd had a glass of wine, August, I swear to God it was one glass, and I told him I'd call him a cab and meet him at my house. I called your company. I gave the address."

"You called in the pickup."

"I called in the pickup." Her voice cracked on it. "And then I sat here and waited, and he never came, and at two in the morning a detective called me and I knew before she said his name. I knew."

"Ruthie." I leaned forward. "You told Reyes you were home all night."

"I told Reyes what I had to tell her." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Because when I got in my car — I went out looking for him, around one, I couldn't sit still — I passed the depot road. And I saw a car I recognized parked in the dark with its lights off. Near the tracks. Where they found him."

My heart was doing something slow and terrible. "Whose car, Ruthie."

"Teddy's." She said it like she was spitting out a bone. "The green Impala. He's had it since '96. I'd know it anywhere — he used to rev it at four in the morning outside our old house and Marisol would throw a shoe at his window."

* * *

Teddy lived in a trailer court out past the quarry, in a unit with a plastic flamingo in the yard and a dog that barked at me like it knew my sins. He opened the door in a tank top, older and thicker than the boy who'd taught me to drive in a Kmart parking lot, but with the same crooked grin that always made you forgive him a half-second before you remembered why you shouldn't.

"August Vane." He spread his arms. "Come to accuse me of something? Everybody else has."

"Your car was at the depot the night Danny died."

The grin died. He didn't act surprised, and that was its own kind of answer. He stepped out onto the gravel, pulled the door shut behind him, and for a long moment we just looked at each other across thirty years.

"Ruthie saw it," I said. "Green Impala, parked dark, by the tracks. That's where they found him, Teddy."

"Yeah, I know where they found him." He rubbed his jaw. "And yeah, my car was there. Earlier that evening. Six, seven o'clock. I went to talk to him myself."

"Talk."

"He called *me*, August. Two months ago. Told me the same thing he told Ruthie, I figure — that he was coming back to fix it. And I panicked. I'll own that. I drove out there to beg him to let it lie. Thirty years, man. I just got my life back. I got a parole officer who almost likes me. I told him, you recant, they reopen everything, they look at everybody, they look at *you*." He jabbed a finger at my chest, not quite touching. "At the girl who gave him the alibi. You think that story holds up after thirty years? You think they won't come for you?"

"I've been asking myself that since they found him in my jacket."

"Yeah, and that's a whole other thing I want to hear about." He went back inside and came out with a shoebox, of all things, and opened it on the hood of my car. Inside was a stack of receipts, a parking stub, and a photograph. "Six-forty p.m., I'm at the quarry weigh station buying diesel for my boss. Seven-fifteen, I'm at the diner on Route 9 — you can see my ugly face on their camera, ask them. Eight o'clock I'm home, and my neighbor across the road, Mrs. Delgado, was on her porch smoking till ten, she'll swear to it. I went to see Danny at six. He was alive, he was scared, and I told him to go home to Boise and forget the whole thing. He said no. I left. That's it. That's the whole ugly truth, and every minute of it is papered."

I looked at the receipts. Dated October 14th. Real ones, or good enough.

"Then your car wasn't there at midnight," I said slowly. "Which means Ruthie lied."

"Or Ruthie saw what she wanted to see." Teddy closed the shoebox. "Or somebody wanted Ruthie to see it." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "August. Think. Who knew Ruthie drives out that way at night when she can't sleep? Who knew Danny was scared enough to ask for her? Who knew all of it — every piece of it — because we told her everything, our whole lives, because that's what family does?"

The wind came up off the quarry and the dog started barking again, and I stood there on the gravel with the noose of it drawing tight around my throat, because there was only one name left in the circle. Only one person who'd held us all together after the trial. Only one person who'd kept feeding us, checking on us, calling us, for thirty years, the way you keep a fire banked so it never quite goes out.

"Marisol," I said.

Teddy didn't answer. He didn't have to. He just looked past me, down the long road toward town, where the hospital stood white and windowless against the dark, and I understood that he'd gotten there before I had. Maybe days ago. Maybe he'd been sitting with it the way I'd been sitting with the journal pages, unable to make his hands do what his head knew.

"She drove Ruthie's route home every night that week," Teddy said quietly. "Ruthie told me. Said Marisol kept showing up, said she was worried about her, said she'd follow her home from her shift to make sure she was safe."

Safe. The word went through me like a nail.

I got in my car and pointed it at the hospital, at the night shift, at the woman who had signed my foster paperwork with a flourish and cried at my graduation and never once, in thirty years, let any of us out of her sight.

The family myth didn't collapse all at once. It collapsed the way old houses do — one quiet crack at a time, and then all at once, while you're standing in the kitchen.
