## 1. The Register Book

The rain came to Casper Parish the way bad news did—sideways, without apology, and determined to get into every crack you'd foolishly left open. By Tuesday of the third week in October, the gutters of the Casper Hotel had given up entirely, and water sheeted down the leaded windows of the lobby like the building was crying about something it wouldn't name.

Astrid Galloway had worked the front desk long enough to know that rain changed the guests. Sunny days brought tourists who wanted directions to the lighthouse and complained about the Wi-Fi. Rain brought people running from something. They arrived damp and apologetic, they paid cash, and they asked strange questions at odd hours. She'd learned to read them the way Mrs. Voss, the hotel's ancient owner, read weather off her bad knee: not precisely, but well enough to know when to bring an umbrella.

So when the man came through the revolving door at quarter past four on Wednesday—dripping, hollow-eyed, clutching a leather satchel against his chest like a rescued infant—Astrid already had a room key half-cut in her imagination.

"Evening," he said, though it was midafternoon, and the lobby lamps were burning against the gloom. "I—yes. Hello. I'd like a room."

"Of course." She slid the register book across the desk. The Casper still kept one, pen and all, because Mrs. Voss maintained that computers were "a passing enthusiasm" and also because Astrid privately loved the book—the way names accumulated there like sediment, decade upon decade, the whole parish's comings and goings pressed flat between ruled lines. "How many nights?"

"I'm not sure yet." He said it quickly, as if he'd rehearsed the wrong answer. "A week. Let's say a week."

"A week it is. Name?"

He hesitated over the pen. Not the hesitation of a man forgetting his own name—the hesitation of a man deciding which one to give. Astrid watched his throat move.

"Corwin," he said finally. "Just Corwin."

"Just Corwin," she repeated pleasantly, writing *C. Corwin* in her best concierge's hand, which was a small art form all its own: legible enough for the tax man, vague enough for everyone else. "Room 12, second floor, water side. You'll hear the gulls. Consider them free entertainment."

He almost smiled. It was a decent face under the exhaustion—fortyish, maybe younger worn down to look it, with the kind of paleness that came from years spent indoors worrying. His clothes were good but rumpled, the rumple of someone who'd slept in them on a train rather than someone who couldn't afford better.

"And your occupation?" she asked, because the register asked, and because she was curious.

"Nothing interesting."

"Those are always my favorite answers." She pushed the key across the desk. "Breakfast is until ten. The radiator in your room has opinions—if it clanks, hit it once on the left side and apologize to it. It responds to respect."

That earned a real smile, brief and startled, like a bird landing somewhere unexpected. Then it was gone, and he was looking past her shoulder at the lobby wall—at the framed photographs of the parish through the decades, the regattas and harvest fairs and the anniversary portraits—and something in his expression closed like a shop pulling down its shutters.

"Is there—" He stopped. Started again. "The parish dinner. I heard there's an anniversary dinner coming up. At the end of the month."

"The Founders' Dinner," Astrid said. "Two hundred years since old Ezekiel Casper decided this swamp was worth arguing with. Every chair in the parish hall will be full and every speech will be twenty minutes too long. Are you thinking of attending? It's open to visitors, if you don't mind being looked at. We're a small parish. New faces are an event."

"Yes," he said. "No. I mean—I wanted to ask about it. Whether it would be appropriate."

*Appropriate.* An odd word for a chicken dinner. Astrid tilted her head. "It's a public event. Everyone's welcome. Even the butcher, and he's been feuding with the council since 2019."

His hands were on the desk now, resting beside the register book, and she saw it then—saw the fine tremor running through them, the way his fingers curled inward as though trying to hold something down. When he'd signed the register, the ink had shivered. She'd assumed cold. Wet men were cold men. But he wasn't shivering anywhere else, and the lobby was warm enough that the windows had begun to fog.

"It's just," he said, "an important anniversary. For my family."

"Ah." She let the syllable sit there, unhurried. In her experience, the most useful thing a concierge could do was leave silence where other people rushed in with questions. Silence was a door; people walked through it on their own.

"My aunt lived here. Years ago." He was looking at the register book now, at the ledger itself, as if eleven years might be visible in the paper grain. "Marianne Quill."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere upstairs a door thumped; the pipes groaned their evening complaint. Astrid kept her face in its professional arrangement—pleasant, attentive, faintly amused by life—and behind it, quietly, a bell began to ring.

She knew the name. Of course she knew the name. There were only so many parishes in the world where a woman could walk into the sea and stay walked-into, and Marianne Quill's drowning was the parish's most polished piece of furniture: mentioned gently, dusted annually, never sat upon. Eleven years ago this very autumn. Astrid had been new at the desk then, barely six months in, and she remembered the week of it—the candles in the churchyard, Aldous Quill's black coat, the way everyone spoke in lowered voices that somehow sounded rehearsed.

"She drowned," Astrid said carefully. "Before my time here. Well—nearly before. I'm sorry. Was she family to you?"

"My aunt." He swallowed. "Yes. My aunt."

There it was again, that fractional hitch, the small mechanical failure between sentence and truth. Astrid had spent eleven years learning the difference between grief and guilt and fear, and Corwin's voice had a little of all three, blended like paint colors nobody would admit to mixing.

"We keep a copy of the parish records in the back office," she offered. "If you're doing family history. Lots of guests do, this time of year. Roots and all that."

"No," he said, too fast. "No, thank you. I have—"

He stopped. His hand went to the satchel, adjusted it on the counter, and in the motion the flap fell open an inch. Astrid saw leather—dark green, cracked with age—and the edge of a page, and handwriting. A woman's handwriting, slanted and urgent, the letters leaning east like windblown grass.

He caught the flap and closed it, and his hand stayed on it afterward, pressing it flat, the tremor worse now.

"I have what I need," he finished.

"Room 12," Astrid said, as though nothing whatsoever had happened. "Second floor. Towels are in the wardrobe, extra blankets in the cedar chest, and if you hear knocking in the walls after midnight, it's only the boiler. The boiler is dramatic. We've all learned to live with it."

This time the smile came easier, and for a moment he looked less like a man carrying something heavy and more like a tired traveler glad to be out of the rain. "Thank you, Miss..."

"Galloway. Astrid Galloway."

"Corwin," he said again, and then, wincing at himself, "I know I already said."

"You did. It's a memorable name. You'll want to say it twice."

He took the key and crossed the lobby, his footsteps ringing on the old tiles, and paused at the stairwell. "Miss Galloway? The dinner. Do you think—" He turned halfway, and the lamplight caught his face, and what she saw there wasn't curiosity or nostalgia. It was dread wearing hope's clothing, badly. "Do you think anyone would mind? If I came? Someone who didn't—someone connected to. To the Quills."

Astrid considered him across the length of the lobby, the rain hammering the glass behind her like applause from a hostile audience.

"I think," she said, "that this parish minds far less than it pretends to, and pretends far more than it knows. Come to the dinner, Mr. Corwin. Wear something dry. And if anyone gives you trouble, tell them the hotel sent you—we're neutral territory. Like Switzerland, but damper."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound—and climbed the stairs, and the satchel swung at his side, and Astrid watched it go with the particular attention she gave to all unsolved things.

* * *

At seven, she carried up fresh towels herself—not because Room 12 needed them, but because Mrs. Voss had gone up to bed at six with her knee predicting "a storm with teeth," and Astrid liked knowing her hotel after dark, liked the creak of the third stair and the smell of beeswax and wet wool that October pressed into everything.

She knocked. No answer. She knocked again, and heard, faintly, the sound of pages turning—quick, restless, the reading of someone searching rather than enjoying.

"Mr. Corwin? Towels."

"Just—leave them, please. Thank you." His voice came thin through the door, strained at the edges.

"Goodnight, then."

She set the towels on the chair outside the door, and as she straightened, she saw it: the gap beneath the door, lamplight spilling into the corridor, and across the light, a shadow. A hand, holding a page up to the lamp. And on the page, upside down and reversed, a fragment of that same slanted handwriting, and beneath it—she couldn't help herself, she was a woman who read labels in other people's pantries—a date.

Eleven years was a long time to remember a stranger's handwriting. But Astrid had filed Marianne Quill away the way the parish filed her: neatly, permanently, under *finished*. And the date on that page, glimpsed for half a second through a door's dark seam, was from this year.

Astrid stood in the corridor a moment longer than towels required, listening to the rain and the turning pages and the distant, theatrical clank of the boiler announcing itself to Room 14.

Then she went downstairs, took out the register book, and looked at the entry she'd written that afternoon. *C. Corwin*, in her careful hand. Just Corwin. No surname, no address, no next of kin.

In eleven years of keeping the Casper's books, she had learned that the register never lied—but it also never told the whole truth. It simply waited, patient as the tide, for the rest of the story to check in.

She blew out the desk lamp and stood a while in the dark lobby, watching the rain write its own long entries on the window glass.
