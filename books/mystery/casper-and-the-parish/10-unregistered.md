## 10. Unregistered

The first frost came on a Tuesday, and with it the smell of Mrs. Voss burning leaves behind the hotel, which was technically against parish ordinance and had been since 1987. Astrid watched the smoke curl past the kitchen window while she waited for the kettle, and thought that some laws were less like rules and more like suggestions made by people who had never been cold.

The investigation into Aldous Quill had entered its sixth week. It moved the way investigations in small parishes always move—glacially, then all at once. First there had been a detective from the county who took tea in the lobby and asked careful questions about sealed rooms. Then two more detectives. Then a woman with a briefcase who asked to see the linen closet in the oldest wing and stood inside it for a long moment with her hand flat against the back panel, as if listening for a heartbeat through the wood.

Aldous had resigned as council chairman on a Thursday. The announcement in the *Parish Gazette* was four lines long and used the word "stepping back," which Astrid found almost admirably dishonest. Stepping back implied you had been standing forward. Aldous Quill had spent eleven years standing on top of a story and calling it a grave.

She had not seen him since the dinner. She did not expect to, though she sometimes imagined it—the lobby, his polished shoes, whatever he might say to her now that the whole parish knew what she'd read aloud over the clink of dessert spoons. In her imaginings he said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than anything he could have said, and so she stopped imagining it and started thinking instead about boiler maintenance, which was a comfort in its way. Boilers were honest. They broke for understandable reasons.

* * *

Corwin came back on a Wednesday in November, carrying a suitcase this time instead of a secret.

He stood at the desk in a coat too thin for the weather, and Astrid looked at him—at the face that was Marianne's face rearranged by a father's jaw—and felt the strange doubled thing she'd been feeling for weeks now: anger and tenderness occupying the same room, refusing to leave.

"You lied to me," she said.

"I know."

"You let me think you were a nephew. You let me build everything on that."

"I know." He set the suitcase down. "I told myself it was practical. That if I said *son*, people would start asking why I'd never come before, why I didn't know her handwriting, why I needed a stranger to read my own mother's diary to me." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "But that wasn't the reason. The reason is I was ashamed. Eleven years I thought she chose to leave me. And when I started to suspect she hadn't—that maybe she'd been driven out—I couldn't stand for anyone to see how much I wanted her. Wanting that much looks pathetic on a grown man."

"It looks human," Astrid said. "There's a difference. It's subtle. Most people miss it."

"Is she—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know where she is?"

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. She thought about the envelope that had arrived ten days ago, postmarked from nowhere in particular, containing no letter, only a photograph of a grey sea and, written on the back in a hand Astrid now knew better than her own: *Tell him the hairbrush was never about vanity.*

"She's safe," Astrid said. "That's what I can tell you. What I can also tell you is that she left something with me. An address. With instructions that it go to you only if you came back here honest."

His breath went out of him like air from a punctured tire.

"So," Astrid said, sliding a piece of hotel stationery across the desk along with a pen, "let's try the truth one more time. Start with your name. Your actual name."

"Corwin Quill," he said. Then, quieter, like a man setting down something heavy he'd carried so long his arms had forgotten their shape: "Thomas Corwin Quill. She named me after her grandfather. I changed the order of it when I was nineteen because I couldn't stand seeing his name every day without knowing why she gave it to me."

Astrid wrote it in the register book, in her careful concierge's hand, under the date. *Room 4,* she said. *The radiator knocks but only between two and three in the morning, and if you complain about it, Mrs. Voss will tell you the building is talking in its sleep and charge you extra for the entertainment.*

He smiled—a real one, wobbly at the edges—and picked up his suitcase, and Astrid watched him walk toward the stairs, and thought: *there goes a man going home to an address he hasn't read yet.* There were worse things to witness from behind a front desk. There were very few better ones.

* * *

She burned the pages on a Sunday night, in the same dented tin pail Mrs. Voss used for leaves, behind the hotel where the ordinance couldn't see.

Not the whole diary. That had taken her weeks to decide, and she had decided it the way she decided most things now—not by asking what was safe, but by asking what was true. The truth was that the diary belonged to Corwin. It was his inheritance, his mother's voice across eleven years of silence, and he deserved all of it except the parts that existed only as ammunition. So Astrid sat on the back step with the diary open on her knees and went through it page by page, and kept everything that was Marianne—the tides, the recipes, the furious jokes, the entries dated after her own death that described gulls and loneliness and the specific courage required to buy bread while being legally drowned—and she tore out only three pages.

Three pages describing, in a young wife's level handwriting, exactly what Aldous Quill had done behind a closed door in the years before the water. Pages that would have destroyed him in a courtroom. Pages that would also have made his son read them, and made the whole parish pass them around like sweetmeats, and turned Marianne's escape into everyone's entertainment forever.

The investigation didn't need them. The detectives had found the old police reports; they had found the sealed room; they had found, Astrid suspected, other women willing to talk now that the parish's polite silence had cracked open like ice on a pond in March. Justice, it turned out, could limp along well enough on evidence alone. It didn't need Astrid to feed it a woman's worst nights.

She struck the match anyway with a steady hand, which surprised her. She had expected her hands to shake. They had shaken the night of the dinner, holding that single line above her head like a lantern while two hundred faces went white as the tablecloths. But that was the last time. Since then her hands had been remarkably calm, as if they too had finally been given honest work.

The pages caught, curled, went orange, went black. Ash lifted on the cold air and drifted toward the water tower, and Astrid thought of Marianne somewhere on a coast with salt wind and a new name, and hoped the ash got nowhere near her, and knew that was silly, and let herself be silly about it.

"You're going to hell for littering," said Mrs. Voss, appearing beside her with two cups of tea, because Mrs. Voss appeared everywhere like weather.

"Probably," Astrid said, taking a cup.

"Some guests pay to be unregistered," Mrs. Voss said, settling onto the step with a groan of ancient joints. "You understand that now."

"I understand it."

"And yet you registered the boy under his true name and sent him up the stairs like a parcel marked *fragile*." The old woman sipped her tea. "Eleven years I kept that room. Do you know how many times I asked myself whether I was doing right?"

"No. How many?"

"Every single day," Mrs. Voss said. "And every single day I answered yes, because the alternative was handing her back to him, and I am many terrible things but I am not that." She looked into the pail, where the last of the pages had gone to grey flakes. "You'll do differently than me, I expect. You'll ask the questions out loud. Loudly. In front of the canapés."

"Somebody has to," Astrid said. "The canapés were getting smug."

Mrs. Voss laughed—a dry rustling sound, like the leaves she wasn't supposed to burn—and for a while they just sat there, two women and a tin pail and a hotel full of sleeping guests, watching the smoke go up into the dark where nobody official could see it.

* * *

On the first of December, a new guest checked in.

She was perhaps sixty, with a canvas bag and the sunburned look of someone recently arrived from a coast, and she stood at the desk while Astrid fetched the register book, and said, "One night. Maybe two. I'm passing through."

"Business or pleasure?" Astrid asked, because the question was ritual, and rituals are how hotels tell time.

"Oh," the woman said, and something crossed her face—quick, private, gone again, like a fish under ice. "Neither. Reckoning, mostly. Finished now."

Astrid looked up. The woman looked back. Neither of them said anything for the length of one clock tick, and then the woman smiled faintly and touched the edge of the register book, the way you touch a doorframe of a house you once lived in.

"Room 9 is free," Astrid heard herself say.

Something flickered in the woman's eyes—alarm, then amusement, then something softer that Astrid decided not to name, because naming it would have meant admitting what she already knew, and some knowledge is best left unregistered.

"The room with the good window?" the woman asked lightly.

"All our rooms have good windows," Astrid said. "It's a policy."

The woman signed. Astrid watched the name go down—*M. Cole*, in a hand that had gotten steadier over eleven years of practice—and slid the key across the desk, and understood, standing there in the lamplight of the lobby she had tended through rain and secrets and one unforgettable dinner, that she was not invisible anymore. Perhaps she never had been. Perhaps invisibility had only ever been the story she told herself so she wouldn't have to be seen choosing.

"Breakfast is at eight," she said. "The coffee is terrible. The toast, miraculously, is excellent. It's one of the mysteries of this establishment."

"I've heard this place has a few of those," M. Cole said, and carried her canvas bag up the stairs.

Astrid closed the register book and left her hand on its cover a moment, feeling the weight of all those names—all the registered and unregistered lives that had passed through these walls, the hidden and the hiding, the ones who paid to disappear and the ones who paid simply to belong somewhere for a night. Outside, the frost was coming down again, silvering the window that faced the water tower. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked like the building talking in its sleep.

The phone rang.

Astrid picked it up on the second ring, the way she always did—prompt, professional, present—and said, "Casper Hotel, good evening. This is Astrid speaking. How may I help you?"

And from the far end of the line, a voice she did not know said, "Miss Galloway? My name is Detective Harrow—we met briefly at the anniversary dinner. I'm sorry to call so late. There's something you should know about the Quill investigation." A pause, long enough for the boiler to knock twice. "It seems Aldous Quill has retained a lawyer. And the lawyer is asking specifically about you."
