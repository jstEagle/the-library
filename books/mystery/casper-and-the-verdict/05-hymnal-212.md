## 5. Hymnal 212

The church archive smelled the way all church archives smell: dust, old paper, and the faint ghost of incense that had soaked into the plaster sometime during the Eisenhower administration and never left. Noor Kavanagh stood in the doorway of the annex room behind St. Brigid's and let the smell settle over her like a coat she'd forgotten she owned.

"Coroner," said Father Ambrose, hurrying in behind her with a ring of keys and the expression of a man who had already decided to regret this. "I want it on the record that I am cooperating *voluntarily*."

"Father, you called me."

"I called you because the organist called me, and the organist called me because a man in a gray coat was seen photographing the annex door with his telephone, and I am eighty-one years old and I have learned that when strange men photograph doors, one telephones the person in town who is paid to be suspicious." He said all of this in one breath, which Noor suspected was a skill honed over decades of parish committee meetings. "Also I made tea. It's in the vestry. It's been there since Tuesday, so I'd approach it with faith rather than expectation."

"Thank you, Father."

"Don't thank me. Just don't tell the diocese I let you touch anything." He pressed a key into her hand, warm from his pocket. "Third door on the left. The light switch sticks. Flick it twice and insult it gently — it responds to kindness."

* * *

The archive was three rooms of shelving that had begun life as a walk-in safe for the old mill office and been donated to the parish in 1974, which meant the church had inherited not only the shelves but the mill's habit of keeping everything and explaining nothing. Baptismal registers. Marriage records. Minutes of the ladies' auxiliary going back to a time when the minutes were recorded in pencil because ink was for the rich. Boxes labeled with a confidence that did not survive inspection: MISC., PAPERS, THINGS FROM THE BASEMENT.

Noor worked the way she worked a body — systematically, respectfully, and with the growing certainty that something in here had been arranged for her benefit.

The crossword had told her to come. *Seven down, four letters: "What the faithful keep."* The answer was VOW, and the number beside the clue had been 212, printed in ink slightly fresher than the rest of the puzzle. The newspaper was three weeks old, delivered to her office among a bundle of back issues nobody remembered ordering. Someone was communicating in the way that people communicate when they believe their mail is being read: sideways, patiently, in the margins of ordinary life.

She had spent two evenings with the second letter before she saw it. *The archive keeps what the town forgets. Look for what they sang while they buried the truth.* And beneath that, the number again, 212, written so small she'd needed her reading glasses and then, indignantly, a magnifying glass.

Hymnal 212. Shelf or box or page — she'd find out.

The light flickered on after two flicks and one gentle insult. She began with the hymnals themselves, a wall of them, maroon covers, spines cracked, each stamped with a number in gold leaf. Hymnals 1 through 40. 41 through 88. She counted as she moved down the shelf, and the counting slowed, and stopped.

Between hymnal 211 and hymnal 213, there was a gap the width of a hymnal, and in the gap, a hymnal.

It looked like all the others. Maroon, cracked, gold-stamped. But the stamp did not say a hymnal number. It said, in the same gold leaf, the same font, *Hymnal 212*, and when Noor lifted it from the shelf, it weighed wrong. Hymnals weigh like hymnals — light, hollow, full of breath and song. This one weighed like a brick that had been to church.

She carried it to the reading table by the window, where the afternoon came in at an angle that made everything look like evidence, and she opened it.

The first page was a hymnal page. *Abide With Me*, verses one through four, the paper gone the color of weak tea. She turned the page. *Abide With Me* again — no. Not again. The same page, but printed over, a second text laid beneath the first in a fine, close hand, the way smugglers print documents over innocuous ones. She tilted the page toward the window and the light did the rest: beneath *Fast falls the eventide*, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood, ran columns. Dates. Names. Amounts.

A ledger. Sixty years of it, printed page by page beneath the hymns, the way a confession hides inside a prayer.

Noor sat down without quite deciding to. Her hands were steady — they were always steady; it was one of the things people said about her, *steady as a scalpel* — but her breath had gone shallow, and she noticed it the way she'd notice a murmur in a patient's chest. Something underneath, off-rhythm, worth watching.

She began to read.

* * *

The entries started in 1964. The handwriting of the early pages was small, precise, a doctor's hand — she knew a doctor's hand when she saw one; she'd been trained in one, had spent seven years watching Aldous Pike write case notes in exactly that unhurried, upright script, each letter standing at attention. Her chest did something complicated at the recognition, and she set it aside, the way she set aside everything that wasn't the body in front of her.

The entries were simple. Brutally simple. A date, a name, a sum, a purpose.

*14 March 1965 — R. Okafor — $400 — silence, mill fire, family of the Delgado girl.*

Noor read that one twice. Okafor. She thought of Sam, who'd grown up three counties over and claimed no family here, and made a small note in her mind to ask him about it, gently, or possibly never.

*2 June 1966 — Sheriff T. Brann — $250 quarterly — discretion, re: mill fire findings.*

*September 1968 — Widow Prentiss — $50/month — re: husband's questions. Discontinue if questions discontinue.*

The mill fire. 1965. Four men dead in the Verrell mill, the fire ruled accidental, the mill closed within the year, the insurance paid, the family's name polished rather than stained. Noor had grown up on the official version the way everyone in this town had: tragedy, bad wiring, the town's great sorrow. She had autopsied two of the era's survivors in her time and never once thought to look sideways at the file. Why would she? The town had already decided. The town was very good at deciding.

She turned pages. The hymns marched on overhead — *Just As I Am*, *The Old Rugged Cross*, *In the Garden* — and beneath them the ledger marched too, a second congregation, paying and being paid. Bribes to inspectors. Pensions to widows who agreed to grieve quietly. Payments marked *gratitude* that were clearly anything but. A whole economy of silence, running beneath the town's surface for sixty years like groundwater, invisible until you dug, and then everywhere.

And then, on a page beneath *Blessed Assurance*, she found the black crosses.

* * *

There were three of them.

Not ink-black — she could tell even in the fading light that they'd been drawn in something else, something that had gone down into the paper and stayed. A cross beside each of three names, drawn with a heavy hand, the kind of mark a person makes when they have stopped writing and started sentencing.

The first cross stood beside *Daniel Reyes, fisherman — 11 May 2019 — final payment made — account closed.*

Noor heard the church clock strike the half hour and did not hear it. Daniel Reyes. She had stood over Daniel Reyes on a slab in her own basement three years ago, a man pulled from the harbor with water in his lungs and a bruise on his temple that the ruling had called *consistent with impact against the harbor pilings*. She had written *consistent with* in her report and hated every word of it. *Consistent with* was the phrase she used when the truth was standing in the room wearing its coat and hat, waiting to be acknowledged, and everyone else had decided to discuss the weather. The police had discussed the weather. The harbor master had discussed the weather. She had filed her doubts in a drawer that was getting full.

Daniel Reyes had received payments. Final payment made. Account closed. And then a cross.

The second cross: *Walter Brandt, roofer — 3 August 2021 — balance settled in full — account closed.*

Walter Brandt had fallen from the Kestrel Building on a windless Tuesday, a man with twenty-six years on roofs and no history of dizziness, and the ruling had been *misstep*. Noor had asked for the toxicology twice and been overruled once and ignored once, and she remembered standing in the parking lot afterward watching Walter's daughter — a teenager then, all elbows and fury — and thinking: *this family will spend the rest of their lives believing a lie, and the lie has a mortgage.*

Balance settled in full. Account closed. A cross.

The third: *Edith Verrell — 14 February this year — see separate instruction — account closed.*

Edith Verrell. Noor's hands finally, briefly, were not steady. Edith Verrell, whose funeral she had attended two weeks ago, whose death she had ruled *cardiac event, natural causes* while noticing, and noting, and being ignored about, the bruising on the upper arm consistent with a hard grip, a fall prevented or a fall caused, impossible to say, *consistent with*.

Edith Verrell was a Verrell. The ledger was the Verrells'. And someone had crossed out Edith Verrell in her own family's book of debts.

Noor sat back. The window light had gone amber and long. Somewhere in the annex a pipe ticked as the building cooled, and she listened to it and let the shape of the thing assemble itself in her mind, because this was the part of her work she was actually good at — not the cutting, not the chemistry, but the moment when the pattern came up out of the fog like a coastline.

Three deaths. Three deaths she had suspected and could not prove. Three accounts, opened, maintained, and *closed* — and closed not with a payment but with a cross. The cross was not a debt. The cross was what happened when the debt was no longer worth servicing. When the person became cheaper dead than paid.

And the letter — the impossible letter, postmarked the day after Edith's funeral, naming all three — had known. Had named them in the same order. Had promised a fourth.

*Before the anniversary dinner.*

Noor looked down at the ledger and understood, with the calm that always arrived at the center of her worst days, that she was not reading a confession. Confessions were written by the guilty to unburden themselves. This was written in a doctor's hand, maintained across sixty years, updated within the last month — Edith's entry was in fresher ink than the 1960s pages, the same hand, older and shakier but the same — and it had been left on a numbered shelf in a numbered church for a woman who could read a crossword puzzle's second meaning.

Someone had wanted her to find this.

Someone had wanted her to find this *now*, three weeks after Edith Verrell went into the ground, with the anniversary dinner invitations already on their way to every family the ledger had ever bought.

* * *

She photographed every page. Her phone's camera clicked softly in the empty annex, and the sound had a liturgical quality she chose not to examine — the small repeated noise of a person taking a vow.

On the last legible page, beneath *Nearer, My God, to Thee*, the entries thinned. The final one was dated six weeks ago, in the shaky late version of that familiar upright hand:

*E.V. settled. The book must not outlive the dinner. If it does —*

The sentence stopped. Not faded. Stopped, mid-clause, as though the writer had been interrupted, or had lost the nerve, or had understood that the end of the sentence was the end of something larger.

Noor read it four times. *The book must not outlive the dinner.* Not the family. Not the estate. The *dinner*. A date on a calendar, an event with a seating chart and a toast, was the deadline for the destruction of sixty years of record — and the letter had promised a fourth death before that same dinner.

She closed the hymnal and held it a moment against her sternum, the way you'd hold a cold child, and felt the weight of it — the brick-weight, the truth-weight — and thought about the man in the gray coat photographing the annex door, and about the organist who had a habit of noticing things, and about how many hours it would take for the wrong person to learn that the right person had been here.

The light switch, on her way out, required two flicks and an insult. She gave it both.

Father Ambrose was waiting in the vestry with the tea, which had achieved a state Noor could only describe as geological. He looked at her face and then at the hymnal under her arm, and his own face went through several expressions in quick succession, like a man flipping through a book of sermons to find the right one.

"You found it," he said. Not a question. And then, quieter, with the weariness of a man who had perhaps always known there was something in that annex worth being afraid of: "I was a curate here in '65, you know. The year of the fire. I rang the bell for those four men." He looked at his own hands. "I always wondered why the town rang so few bells after that. As if the grief had been... *handled*."

"Father—"

"I know." He raised a hand. "I'm cooperating voluntarily. But Coroner —" and here his voice dropped into something older than the church, something from before the incense and the hymnals, "— whoever wrote that book wrote it in the hand of a healer. I buried enough of this town to know a doctor's writing when I see it. And there's only one doctor this town ever trusted enough to hold its secrets."

Noor's arms, holding the hymnal, had gone very still.

"Aldous Pike," she said. "I know."

She knew the handwriting the way you know a parent's voice on the phone. She had learned forensic medicine under it. She had loved it. And three years of unprovable deaths had just been signed, in her mentor's patient, upright script, *account closed* — with a fourth entry still open, and a dinner six days away.

Outside the annex window, down the hill, a gray coat crossed the churchyard and did not look up.

Noor tucked the hymnal inside her coat, against her heart, where it beat like a second pulse, and went out through the vestry, past the geological tea, into the evening — moving, for the first time in her careful life, like a woman being followed, because she was.
