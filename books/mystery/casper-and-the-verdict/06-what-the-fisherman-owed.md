## 6. What the Fisherman Owed

The morning the second letter came to Noor Kavanagh's office, Marta Reyes was in her kitchen teaching her granddaughter how to gut a fish without apologizing to it.

"You don't say sorry," Marta told Sofia, guiding the small knife along the belly. "You say thank you. The fish doesn't care about sorry. Sorry is for people."

"Abuela, that's morbid."

"That's Tuesday." Marta wiped her hands on the towel that had been wiping hands since before Sofia was born. Outside the window, the harbor did what it always did — glittered like it had no secrets, gulls wheeling and complaining the way gulls do, as if they'd been personally wronged by the tide.

Sofia was twelve, all elbows and opinions, and she had her grandfather's stubborn chin. That chin was going to be a problem someday, Marta thought. It already was.

"Tell me about Abuelo," Sofia said suddenly, the way children do — dropping a stone into still water and then watching, innocent, for the ripples.

Marta kept her eyes on the fish. "He smelled like salt and diesel fuel and he snored like a man fighting the sea even in his sleep."

"No, I mean—" Sofia set down the knife. "How he died. Tía says it was an accident. But Marisol's mom says—"

"Marisol's mom says a lot of things," Marta said, and heard her own voice come out flat as a filleting board. She softened it. "It was an accident, mija. The water was cold, his boots were heavy, and the sea doesn't negotiate."

Sofia looked at her with those dark eyes that missed nothing, because twelve-year-olds are the best detectives in any town and nobody pays them for it. Then she shrugged and went back to the fish, and Marta stood at the sink and let the water run over her hands for longer than the dishes required.

* * *

Tomás Reyes had been afraid for exactly six weeks before he drowned.

Marta could date it precisely, because fear has a calendar when you live with it. It began on the fourteenth of March, a Thursday, when Tomás came home from the Verrell house with an envelope in his jacket pocket and a look on his face she had never seen before — not on him, not in thirty-one years of marriage, not through two boat fires, one bankruptcy, or the year their son Rafael broke his back in three places.

Tomás Reyes was not a man who frightened easily. He had once argued with a Coast Guard officer about the weather. He had named his boat after their wedding anniversary and then refused, for twenty years, to repaint the peeling letters because "the boat earned those scars same as we did." He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with hands like cured rope and a laugh that carried across the whole harbor.

But on the fourteenth of March he came home quiet, put the envelope in the flour tin where they kept the emergency money, and didn't eat his dinner.

"You're sick," Marta said. Not asking.

"I'm fine."

"You left the flan."

That was how she knew. Tomás would have walked into a burning building for her flan. He looked at the plate like it belonged to someone else, someone whose life he used to have.

"Marta." He said her name the way men say things they've been rehearsing on a boat alone. "If anything happens to me—"

"Nothing is happening to you."

"If anything happens," he said again, patient, terrible, "you take the kids and you go to your sister's in Santa Rosa. You don't ask questions. You don't talk to anyone from up the hill. You take the envelope out of the tin and you go."

"What envelope?"

He almost smiled. Almost. "You know what envelope."

She did know. She had known since Tuesday, when she counted the flour-tin money out of habit and found eleven hundred dollars more than there should have been, in crisp bills that didn't smell like fish or sweat or their life together. Money that smelled like nothing. Money that smelled like a bank.

"Tomás." She sat down across from him. The kitchen was small; their knees nearly touched under the table, the way they had for three decades. "What did you do?"

"Nothing." And then, because he had never once lied to her successfully: "I saw something. A long time ago. I thought it was over. It's not over."

"Saw something where?"

"Marta." He reached across and took her hand, and his palm was shaking, this man who'd hauled nets in forty-knot winds. "I need you to promise me you'll be dumb. Can you do that? For once in your life, can you be gloriously, magnificently dumb? Don't read anything. Don't remember anything. If anyone asks, you knew I was worried about money, about the boat, about my heart. My heart." He tapped his own chest. "Fifty-eight years old, bad cholesterol, family history. That's the story. It's even mostly true."

"It's not true at all."

"It's mostly true," he said, which was the most honest thing he said that night, and she would think about that sentence for years afterward — how a lie can be built out of true pieces, mortared with love, and stand up to almost anything except the truth itself.

* * *

In the six weeks that followed, Marta learned what fear looks like on a fisherman, and it looked like small things.

It looked like Tomás taking the long way home from the dock so he wouldn't pass the church. It looked like him burning papers in the woodstove — actual papers, which he'd never done, being a man who kept receipts from 1987 because "you never know." It looked like him standing at the window after dark, watching the road up to the Verrell estate, watching it the way you watch a dog you don't trust around the baby.

Once she woke at three in the morning and he wasn't beside her. She found him in the kitchen, dressed, sitting in the dark with his boots on.

"The boat?" she asked.

"The boat," he agreed, and didn't move, and after a while she went back to bed, and neither of them ever mentioned it, because marriage is full of rooms you agree not to enter.

He took Rafael aside too — she caught the tail end of it, the two of them in the boathouse, Tomás with his hand on their son's shoulder saying something low and urgent. Rafael came out gray-faced and wouldn't tell her anything either. *Gloriously dumb*, the whole household, performing ignorance like a community theater production with one rehearsal.

And then, on the last night — she knew it was the last night, later, though at the time it was just a Tuesday — Tomás came home early and happy. Actually happy. He'd made a decision, he said. He was going to fix it. He'd called someone, or been called, and there was going to be a meeting, and after the meeting everything would be clean.

"Clean how?"

"Just clean." He ate two helpings of the flan. He laughed at Rafael's terrible jokes. He danced her around the kitchen to the radio, badly, stepping on her feet on purpose because he knew it made her swat him. "Whatever happens," he said into her hair, "remember I fixed more than I broke. Say it back."

"You fixed more than you broke."

"That's my girl." He held her at arm's length and looked at her face like a man memorizing a chart. "Forty-one years I've been looking at this face. Best view in the harbor."

"You've been married to me thirty-one."

"I looked for ten years first," he said. "You were worth the approach."

They found his boat adrift two days later, half a mile off the point, engine off, his boots on the deck. The coroner's report said accidental drowning. Cold water, heavy boots, heart probably gave out — fifty-eight years old, bad cholesterol, family history.

Mostly true.

* * *

Deputy Sam Okafor came to the house on a Wednesday in April, a year and some months after the funeral, and Marta saw him coming from the kitchen window because everyone in this town sees everything from kitchen windows; it's practically the municipal charter.

He was a good boy, Sam Okafor. Big, careful, polite in a way that wasn't weakness. He took his hat off on the porch, which put him ahead of half the town already. He had brought nothing in his hands, which meant he'd deliberately left the notebook in the car, which meant he wanted her to think this was a social call, which meant it wasn't.

"Mrs. Reyes. Ma'am. I won't take much of your time."

"You'll take coffee though. Sit. Sofia, mija, go do homework where you can't hear anything."

Sofia went, radiating the outrage of the surveilled.

Sam sat with his big hands around the mug like it might bolt. He asked about the family. He asked about the boat — sold now, and wasn't that a shame, she'd gotten a fair price? He asked gentle questions with soft edges, and Marta answered them the way she'd promised Tomás she would: gloriously, magnificently dumb.

"Heart trouble ran in his family," she said. "His father went the same way. On the water. It's almost traditional."

"That must have been hard, knowing and watching him go out anyway."

"A fisherman's wife doesn't watch, Deputy. Watching implies you could stop it." She refilled his cup. "You want the lemon cookies or the ones with the anise? The anise ones are better but they stick in your teeth."

"The anise ones," he said gravely, as if accepting a sacrament.

He was good. He really was good. He waited until the third cookie before he said, lightly, folding it into the conversation like a card into a deck: "We've been reviewing some old files. Housekeeping, mostly. Did Tomás ever do work for the Verrell family? Odd jobs, deliveries, that kind of thing?"

"Everyone in this town has done something for the Verrells," Marta said. "Half our streets are named after them. You want to know if he painted a fence up there sometime? Probably. He painted every fence in this zip code."

"Nothing more involved than that?"

"Like what?"

"Like consulting. Like being somewhere he wouldn't normally be. Like receiving payment for services that aren't easy to describe."

There it was, under the polite voice: the hook, thin and bright. Marta felt the old fear rise in her chest, the six-weeks fear, and she looked at this earnest young man with his hat on his knee and understood something with terrible clarity: whatever Sam Okafor was pulling at, it was attached to something bigger than Tomás, and things bigger than Tomás had already taken enough from her.

She thought about Rafael, who had finally stopped flinching at the sound of the Verrell name. She thought about Sofia, who asked questions now instead of just absorbing them, and who would keep asking until someone answered, and answers were dangerous currency in this town. She thought about the flour tin, and the eleven hundred dollars that had appeared in it, and the lawyer who had come to the funeral — not a family friend, a *lawyer* — who had pressed an envelope into her hand at the reception and said, "Mr. Verrell wishes to express his condolences," and inside was enough to pay off the boat loan twice over.

Silence, delivered in crisp bills. She had spent it anyway. That was the part she couldn't explain to anyone, the part that lived in her like a swallowed hook: she had spent it. On Rafael's physical therapy. On Sofia's school shoes. On groceries and the roof and the good coffee Tomás liked. Every cup of coffee in this house was bought with her silence, and she drank it every morning, and it tasted like nothing, like a bank.

"Payment," she said now, to Sam Okafor, and smiled the smile she'd practiced in the bathroom mirror until it stopped feeling like a wound. "Deputy, Tomás owed the Verrell account if anything. That family's store carried us through the bankruptcy year. He paid them back slow for a decade. If money moved between our house and that hill, it moved uphill, believe me."

Sam looked at her for a long moment. He had kind eyes, and kind eyes are the worst, because they make lying feel like kicking a dog.

"Okay," he said finally. "Thank you, Mrs. Reyes. The anise cookies are excellent."

"They stick in your teeth," she said. "Everything good does."

He got up, put his hat on, paused at the door. "One more thing, ma'am, and then I'm gone. Off the record, no notebook, just a man talking." He turned. "Did Tomás seem afraid? At the end?"

And here was the trap, laid gently as a snare under leaves: say yes and she confirmed there was something to fear; say no and she lied about the truest thing she knew.

"He seemed tired," Marta said. "Fifty-eight years old, working alone, bad cholesterol. They all seem tired at the end."

Sam nodded slowly, like a man filing something away in a drawer he'd open later. "My condolences again, ma'am. Thank you for the coffee."

She watched him walk down the path, get in his truck, sit there for a full minute not starting the engine. Then he drove off toward town, and Marta stood at the window and realized her hands were shaking, and pressed them flat against the cool glass until they stopped.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. She got up past midnight and went to the closet, to the shoebox on the top shelf behind the winter coats, where she kept the things that weren't evidence of anything, just objects, just grief with weight.

Tomás's watch, stopped at 9:47. His spare glasses. The photograph from their anniversary, the one where he's laughing at something off-camera. And underneath, folded into eighths, a page she had torn from the back of his logbook the week after the funeral — torn, because keeping the whole book felt like keeping a confession, and tearing the page felt like mercy, and mercy was the only thing she had to offer him now.

It was a list. Numbers, mostly, in his cramped handwriting. Dates going back years. And beside each date, a name or initials, and beside some of the names, a single letter she had never been able to interpret: a *P*.

She had shown it to no one. Gloriously dumb. She had almost burned it a dozen times and a dozen times hadn't, because it was the last thing his hand had touched with intent, and because some stubborn fisherman's-wife corner of her soul believed that one day, someone would come asking the right question, and she would need to decide — finally, at last, with interest compounded — whether she was the woman who stayed bought.

Down the hall, Sofia turned over in her sleep. The harbor glittered outside like it had no secrets.

Marta put the page back in the box, closed the lid, and sat in the dark holding the watch, listening to the house breathe, waiting for morning the way you wait out weather — because you have to, because the boats still need tending, because the dead don't need you and the living do.

She did not know — could not know — that across town, in a church archive that smelled of dust and hymnals, a ledger was waiting with Tomás's name in it, and beside his name, in handwriting she would recognize anywhere because it had signed her husband's death certificate, a single black cross.

The tide was turning. It always does. The only question, the eternal question of this coast, is what it brings in with it.
