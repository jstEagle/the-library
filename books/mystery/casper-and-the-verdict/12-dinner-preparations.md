## 12. Dinner Preparations

Cyrus Verrell had given exactly one hundred and eleven toasts in his life — he'd counted, because counting was the sort of thing a man did when he could no longer sleep past four in the morning — and he had never once rehearsed any of them.

He was rehearsing this one.

The dining room of Verrell House had been built to intimidate, back when intimidation was considered good manners. The table seated twenty-four without a leaf added; the chandelier alone weighed more than the fisherman's boat that had gone down off Gull Point three years ago, though Cyrus tried very hard not to think about boats, or roofs, or cars that burned too cleanly on empty roads. He stood at the head of the table with a glass of water — water, because his doctor had opinions and because a man did not practice a toast drunk, he *performed* it drunk — and cleared his throat at the empty chairs.

"Sixty years," he said. "Sixty years ago, my grandfather Elias Verrell stood where I stand tonight—"

He stopped. Frowned. Moved two steps down the table so that he was standing where Elias had actually stood, which was not at the head but at the third chair on the left, because Elias had believed the head of the table belonged to whoever cooked, and in 1965 that had been a woman named Ruthie Calloway who made a ham that people still lied about remembering.

"Sixty years ago," Cyrus tried again, "my grandfather stood in this room and promised this town that the mill would be the beating heart of Harrow's Bend for a hundred years."

That part was true. What came next was the problem.

The speech sat on the sideboard in his own handwriting, twelve drafts deep, and every draft had the same wound in it: the paragraph about the fire. You could not toast sixty years of the Verrell legacy without mentioning the fire, because the fire *was* the anniversary. March 14th, 1965. Four men had gone into the mill on a Friday evening shift and none of them had come out, and the town had lit candles, and Elias Verrell had stood in this room and wept — genuinely wept, by all accounts, though accounts were kept by people whose mortgages the Verrells held — and then the mill had been declared an electrical fault, and then it had been demolished, and then it had been forgotten, which was the most expensive service the family had ever purchased.

Cyrus picked up the glass again. His hand was steadier than it had any right to be.

"To the four," he said quietly, to nobody. "To Tomás Ibarra. To Wendell Cho. To Albert Frey. To—" and here he faltered, because the fourth name would not come, the way it never came, the way it had been slipping from him for years like a coin through a hole in a coat pocket, and he hated himself for it, hated that he had to walk to the sideboard and check his own handwriting to remember the name of a man who had died for the family's good name.

"Peter Osei," he read aloud. "To Peter Osei."

There. Said in an empty room. That counted for something, surely. God kept books too, his mother used to say, though she'd said it about card games.

"You're doing the sad part standing up."

Adrian was in the doorway. Cyrus hadn't heard him arrive, which was normal; Adrian moved through houses the way cats move through fences, silently and with proprietary interest. He was forty-one and dressed as if the dinner were already underway, dark suit, no tie, the first two buttons undone in a style that passed for relaxed in the city and looked like contempt here. He held a cup of coffee he almost certainly wasn't drinking. Adrian didn't drink things; he held them, the way other men held grudges.

"The sad part is the whole point," Cyrus said. "You can't have the toast without the sad part. It's like — a funeral without a body. People notice."

"People noticed anyway." Adrian came into the room and walked its length slowly, trailing one finger along the tablecloth, checking the drape the way a customs officer checks a seam. "Mrs. Halloran put the Reyes woman at table two. Between Aunt Colleen and the bishop."

"Deliberately. Marta Reyes lost her husband. The bishop has spent forty years learning how to sit with grief without flinching. It's called seating chart compassion, Adrian, it's a real discipline."

"It's a liability. She's a coroner's widow-in-law now. Kavanagh's been out to see her twice this month."

"Then Mrs. Halloran should seat her next to someone charming instead. Who's free?"

"Nobody's free. That's rather the theme of the evening, isn't it? Everyone we invited is either grieving, suspicious, or both." Adrian set his untouched coffee down on the table with a small precise click. "Tessa Brandt confirmed yesterday. Her plus-one declined."

"Tessa Brandt is welcome at my table until the sun burns out. Her father worked that roof for us."

"Her father fell off that roof for us."

The words landed in the room and stayed there, the way certain words do, filling the space like smoke. Cyrus looked at his son. Adrian looked back with the pleasant, patient expression he wore the way other men wore wedding rings — constantly, and as proof of belonging to something.

"That's not what happened," Cyrus said.

"No," Adrian agreed. "Of course not. The ladder was faulty. The county said so. The county says lots of things." He smiled. "I only meant she'll be emotional. Emotionals make speeches. Speeches are your department."

Cyrus turned back to his notes, mostly so he wouldn't have to keep looking at that smile. Sixty years. In sixty years the family had survived a fire, a flood, two bankruptcies, one elopement, one scandal involving a racehorse and a state senator, and the slow attrition of everyone who remembered why the Verrells were rich. And now, in the autumn of the anniversary, with the invitations sent and the ham ordered and the good china out of the cabinet for the first time since his wife was alive to scold him about it — now the letters had started.

He hadn't told Adrian about the letters. That was the trouble with sons: you spent their childhood teaching them to finish what you started, and then one day you realized they had learned the lesson too well, and you couldn't remember whether you'd ever taught them where to stop.

* * *

By noon the house smelled of beeswax and lilies, and Mrs. Halloran ran the ground floor like a naval operation, and Cyrus escaped to the study to do the thing he actually dreaded, which was the phone calls.

The list sat by the telephone in his own hand. Eleven families. Eleven names of people whose connection to the Verrells was, officially, gratitude — scholarships, pensions, the memorial fund, the annual Christmas basket that Mrs. Halloran assembled with a generosity Cyrus paid for and never took credit for, because credit was the one thing the family had always understood you must never take.

He dialed the first number. "Marta? Cyrus Verrell. Yes — yes, it's been too long. Listen, I wanted to say personally how glad we are you're coming Saturday. Daniel would have—" He paused. He had known her husband's name once. He was almost certain he had. "—he'd have raised a glass to this house, I think. Anyway. There'll be a place for you at our table as long as there's a table. Give my love to the children."

He hung up and wrote a small checkmark beside her name and felt, distinctly, like a man signing something.

The second call went better, or worse; with Tessa Brandt it was hard to tell. She was brisk and kind and asked after his health in a tone that suggested she already knew the answer, and at the end she said, "Mr. Verrell, can I ask you something honest?" and he said, "It's the only kind I can afford," and she laughed, and then she said:

"Why now? Sixty years. Why does a family invite the whole town to look at it now?"

And Cyrus, holding the telephone in the study where his grandfather's portrait watched him with painted patience, said the true thing, because he was old and tired and it slipped out before the training could catch it:

"Because somebody's decided the looking starts whether we invite it or not."

There was a silence on the line long enough to hold a funeral in. Then Tessa Brandt said, gently, "Saturday, then," and rang off, and Cyrus sat for a while listening to the dial tone, which was the closest thing to honesty the house contained.

* * *

In the afternoon he did the rounds.

This was ritual. Every dinner, before the guests, Cyrus walked the rooms himself — not because he distrusted Mrs. Halloran, whom he trusted beyond his own children, but because a house told you things when you walked it alone. The dining room first: candles counted, crystal polished, the long table gleaming. Then the hall, where the portraits hung in their appointed order — Elias at the far end, stern as a tax notice, and Cyrus's father beneath him wearing the expression of a man perpetually waiting for Elias to finish talking.

He stopped in front of Elias. He always stopped. It was a form of prayer, probably, though he'd have denied it under oath.

"What would you have me say Saturday?" he asked the painting.

*Say nothing,* said the painted eyes, which had been saying nothing for ninety years. *Say nothing and pay everyone.*

"And if paying stops working?"

The portrait declined to speculate. Portraits always did. It was living people who answered questions like that, usually at the worst possible moment.

He found Adrian in the library, standing at the window that overlooked the drive, watching the caterer's van come up through the elms. He had the posture of a man keeping watch, which was absurd — nothing was coming up that drive but flowers and folding tables — and yet Cyrus felt the old cold walk up his spine, the one that visited whenever he caught his son being still.

"They'll want the good room for the bar," Adrian said, without turning. "I told them no. Guests wander less when the exits are boring."

"Wander toward what?"

"Nothing. Everything." Adrian turned. In the afternoon light his face was all planes and patience. "The archive annex at St. Brigid's had a smell of smoke around it this morning, did you hear? Nothing caught. Just a smell. Father Ambrose is telling people it was the boiler."

"I heard." Cyrus had heard. He had also heard, from the same source, that the coroner had been seen leaving the churchyard at seven in the morning carrying a book wrapped in her coat like a foundling. "Fires and this family have history."

"History's the wrong word. History is what other people have. We have *records*." Adrian crossed to the desk, straightened a pen that was already straight. "Dad. A question. Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically is my favorite kind. Go on."

"If someone — hypothetically — had spent years making sure the family's story stayed clean. Paid the right people. Closed the right doors. Kept the inheritance worth inheriting." He looked up, and his eyes were his mother's eyes, warm brown and utterly unreadable. "Would you thank them? At the dinner. Publicly. Or would you let them stand in the corner and take the blame quietly, the way this family has always arranged things?"

The clock on the mantel ticked through four full seconds. Cyrus thought: *my son is asking me to bless him.* He thought: *or he's asking me to warn him.* With Adrian the two had always traveled together, like the front and back of a coin.

"I'd tell them," Cyrus said slowly, "that the inheritance isn't the money. It never was. The inheritance is the debt. Sixty years of it. And the only thing dirtier than owing a debt is pretending you don't."

Something flickered across Adrian's face — disappointment, maybe, or relief, or the specific expression of a man recalculating. "That's a lovely sentiment," he said. "You should put it in the toast."

"It's cut from the toast. Twelve drafts. Every version."

"Then say it Saturday anyway. Off the page." Adrian picked up his cold coffee, considered it, and set it back down untasted. "Off the page is where everything happens in this family. You taught me that."

He left the way he'd arrived, without sound, and Cyrus stood alone in the library with his grandfather's records and his son's hypotheticals and the growing, gravitational certainty that Saturday night, somewhere between the soup and the toast, the bill for sixty years was going to arrive at his table — and that he would know it had come, not by anything anyone said, but by the sight of his son raising a glass with that same pleasant, patient smile.

Across town, in a coroner's office with bad lighting and good intentions, Noor Kavanagh was at that very hour bending over a hymnal with a magnifying lamp, decoding the ledger's final pages — and finding, in ink fresher than anything else in that sixty-year-old book, a fourth name marked with a black cross.

It was not Cyrus Verrell's.
