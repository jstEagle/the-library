## 13. A Fourth Name

The hymnal had begun to feel like a houseguest.

It sat on Noor Kavanagh's kitchen table between the salt shaker and a cold cup of coffee she'd made at six and forgotten by seven, its green cloth cover gone soft as felt from sixty years of hands. She had covered her table with a white bedsheet — the good kind of paranoia, she told herself, the kind that came with clean surfaces and a paper trail of one's own — and laid out her tools like a surgeon: magnifying glass, cotton gloves, three pencils sharpened to needles, and a legal pad on which she'd drawn, in careful columns, the mapping that was slowly driving her mad.

Hymnal page 212. That was where it started, the way everything in Harrow's Bend seemed to start somewhere ordinary and rot outward.

* * *

The system, once you saw it, was almost beautiful. Almost.

Whoever built the ledger — and Noor now knew whose hand had built it, which was its own small wound — had used the hymnal itself as the key. The book's printed pages were numbered 1 through 640. The archive copy had been rebound with blank leaves interleaved throughout, and each blank leaf corresponded to a hymn number. Hymn 212, "Just As I Am," sat at the center of the first cluster of entries: the earliest debts, the smallest sins. A roofer paid off in 1968. A constable's Christmas bonus that never appeared in any town budget. The handwriting marched across those pages in a neat, upright script that Noor had spent twenty years trying to imitate in her own case notes and never quite managed.

Aldous Pike's handwriting. Her mentor's. The man who taught her that a body tells the truth even when everyone in the room is lying.

She turned another leaf with gloved fingers and let herself have one dry, unamused laugh.

"Just As I Am," she said to the empty kitchen. "He did have a sense of humor. God help me, he did."

The later entries changed hands. Pike's script gave way sometime in the late seventies to a rounder, more impatient fist — Cyrus Verrell's, if the signature blocks meant anything — and then to a third hand, smaller, colder, all downstrokes. Noor had a guess about that third hand now. She'd seen it on the anniversary invitations, on the estate's correspondence, on a charity pledge card in the library's glass case. Adrian Verrell wrote like a man pressing his pen hard enough to leave a mark on whatever was underneath.

Three generations of accountants for the same sin. Sixty years of entries, and every entry was a person. That was the thing no ledger could disguise, no matter how tidy the columns: amortization schedules for grief. Payments to silence a widow. Payments to un-notice a fire. Payments, in one chilling recurring line item, marked only as *maintenance*, quarterly, unbroken, for longer than Noor had been alive.

She worked through the morning. The coffee went cold twice; she microwaved it once out of stubbornness and then abandoned the pretense. Outside her window, October was doing its best work — the maples along Coroner's Lane going copper, a neighbor's dog conducting his daily negotiations with a squirrel — and Noor found herself resenting the world a little for continuing so cheerfully while she sat cataloguing the price of human beings.

By noon she had reached the middle years. The eighties. The nineties. The drowned fisherman was there — Tomás Reyes, 1994, an entry she'd known was coming and still wasn't ready for. *R. — settled. Boat loss ruled weather. Widow compensated via parish fund.* Ruled weather. She had ruled it suspicious, and Sheriff Danvers had ruled her difficult, and here was the whole argument settled in nine words and a dollar figure, written before she'd ever opened the file.

The roofer was there too. Dale Brandt, 2001. *Scaffold inspection signed off. T.B. annuity arranged.* Tessa Brandt's anonymous checks, arriving like guilt with a return address withheld.

And the burned car — Marcus Ellery, 2011 — with a line beside it that made Noor set down her pencil and press her palms flat against the table until they stopped wanting to shake.

*Third cross confirmed. M.E. cooperative.*

M.E. She had spent eleven years telling herself that Edith Verrell's death was the first time the family had ever noticed her existence. Eleven years of being the inconvenient coroner in a town that preferred its dead pre-explained, and it turned out she'd been a line item all along. Cooperative. The word sat on the page like a compliment from someone holding a knife.

"No," she said aloud, because the kitchen deserved to know where she stood. "I was not cooperative. I filed minority reports. I made Danvers's life hell." She paused. "Quietly. I made his life quiet hell."

Which was, she admitted to the hymnal, possibly exactly what cooperative meant in this particular accounting.

* * *

She took a break at two because her eyes were crossing and because Sam Okafor had texted her four times, escalating in tone from *any progress?* to *Noor.* to *NOOR* to a photograph of his desk, on which sat a sandwich he claimed was getting cold in solidarity with her. She sent back a thumbs-up, then, feeling that was insufficiently warm for a man willing to weaponize lunch, added: *Found the fisherman. Found the roofer. Found me, sort of. Still working.*

His reply came fast: *Found YOU?*

*Working,* she repeated, and put the phone face-down, because Sam Okafor was a good deputy and a better friend, and if she told him what she suspected was coming, he would do something dutiful about it, and duty was currently the most dangerous substance in Harrow's Bend.

The afternoon light shifted gold. The neighborhood squirrel won, finally, spectacularly, and Noor heard the dog's outrage through the window and thought, absurdly, of Pike teaching her to suture — *slow hands, Noor, the truth doesn't reward hurry* — and kept turning leaves.

The final cluster of entries lived around hymn 494, "Abide With Me," which Noor decided was either the deepest cynicism or the last shred of a conscience, and she genuinely could not tell which. These pages were the third hand entirely. Adrian's. The ink darker, the entries shorter, the euphemisms thinner, as though whoever held the pen had stopped bothering to dress the transactions up.

Edith Verrell. Dated eleven months ago. Beside it, the black cross — the same cross that marked Tomás Reyes, Dale Brandt, Marcus Ellery. Three crosses. Three deaths Noor had called accidents and been overruled on. Three families invited to an anniversary dinner, seated among the heirs like exhibits at their own trial.

She copied each entry onto her legal pad, date, amount, cross, and then, because thoroughness was the only prayer she knew, she checked them again. Then a third time, because the alternative was admitting what she'd glimpsed at the bottom of the second-to-last leaf, and her hand wanted one more pass of plausible deniability before it committed.

There was a fourth cross.

Fresh ink — not the faded brown-black of the older entries but a blue-black that still had a faint sheen under the magnifier, recent, weeks old at most. It sat beside an entry dated five days after Edith's funeral. Five days after the burial. One day before the postmark on the first letter.

Noor read the entry the way she'd once read autopsy findings on people she loved: slowly, twice, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into mercy.

*N.K. — aware of three. Ledger access probable. Anniversary scheduled. Cross pending.*

Cross pending.

Pending.

Her kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside, the victorious squirrel was presumably enjoying his winnings. And Noor Kavanagh sat at her own table, in her own house, on her own street, looking at her own initials in a murderer's ledger, marked the way you'd mark a task not yet finished.

The strange thing — the thing she would remember afterward, when people asked her what it was like — was that the fear arrived second. First came a kind of terrible clarity, cold and clean as a scalpel, the same clarity she felt standing over a table when the cause of death finally announced itself. *Of course,* said some clinical corner of her mind. *Of course it's you. You're the loose end. You've always been the loose end; you just didn't know you were attached to anything.*

Then the fear came, all at once, the way water comes through a ceiling — not where you expect it, everywhere at once, soaking into things you didn't know were exposed. Her hands. She watched her own hands shake and thought distantly that Pike would have told her to grip the edge of the table until they stopped, and she did, and they mostly did.

They weren't a confession. She'd been reading them wrong for two weeks — the letters, the crossword clue, the trail of breadcrumbs leading her gently, patiently, toward the church archive. She'd assumed a killer boasting, or a killer taunting, or some dying accomplice clearing a conscience. But killers didn't send maps to their own evidence. Killers burned annexes; they didn't annotate them. Whoever mailed those letters — postmarked the day after a funeral, written who knew how long ago — hadn't been confessing.

They'd been warning her. Handing her the thread and trusting her to pull, because she was the only person in Harrow's Bend who pulled.

And the warning had a deadline. *Anniversary scheduled.* Saturday night. Tomorrow night. Sixty years of Verrells raising glasses in that dining room, and tomorrow the fourth cross was due to stop being pending.

Gratitude and terror, Noor discovered, are nearly the same sensation in the body — the same quickened pulse, the same tightness in the throat — differing only in what they point toward. Someone had risked everything, maybe already paid everything, to get this book into her hands. Someone had looked at the fourth name in that ledger and decided it was unacceptable. She sat with that for a long moment, letting it warm her the way you'd cup a match in wind.

Then she picked up her pencil, because gratitude without work is just sentiment, and sentiment doesn't save anybody.

"Okay," she told the hymnal, and meant it as a promise to whoever's hand had guided hers here. "You got me the message. Now I'm going to earn it."

* * *

She worked until the windows went dark and she had to turn on the kitchen light, which made her jump at her own shadow and then laugh at herself, a little hysterically, alone at a table with a dead woman's prayer book and her own death warrant. Gentle humor was going to be how she survived tonight; she could feel it assembling itself, gallows-adjacent, ready.

The last leaf before the back board held the newest entries, and one of them stopped her pencil mid-stroke.

Not an entry, exactly. A fragment. Three lines in the small, cold, downstroking hand, but different — hurried, the pen pressed through the paper, as if written in anger or fear or both:

*Grandfather kept the fire. Father kept the fire. I keep the fire.*
*A ledger is only a debt if someone can read it.*
*After Saturday, no one reads it.*

No one reads it. Which meant the book itself was scheduled — and everything in it, every widow's payment, every maintenance fee, every cross — was due to end up ash, the way the mill had ended up ash, the way the archive annex had very nearly ended up ash two nights ago before the organist's phone call got her there in time.

And it meant something else, something that made the cold walk back up her spine and settle in familiarly, like a colleague taking the seat beside her. If Adrian planned to destroy the ledger after Saturday, then the letters — written years ago, held, mailed on schedule — weren't part of his plan at all. They were a flaw in it. A splinter. Someone inside the house had slipped the truth out the door and trusted a coroner to come collect it.

Someone inside the house who knew the mailing schedule. Who knew the postmark trick. Who knew, down to the day, when the fourth cross was coming due.

Noor looked at the fragment again, at the fury in the pen-strokes, and felt the shape of the answer move closer without resolving — a figure at the edge of the porch light, present, patient, not yet named.

But there was one more thing. Beneath the fragment, half-hidden in the gutter of the binding where a careless eye would never fall, was a final notation in yet another hand — older, shaky, the letters leaning like tired men. She angled the lamp. Magnified. Held her breath.

It was Pike's writing. His late writing, the tremor she remembered from his last year, from the note he'd left on her desk the week before he died: *Keep your files straight, Noor. Straighter than mine.*

This note said:

*If you are reading this, then I failed you quietly and someone else refused to. Check my journals. Third shelf. What I agreed to, and why — and what I made them promise about you.*

Noor sat back. The refrigerator hummed. Tomorrow night, a toast would be raised in a house that had been laundering a murder for sixty years, and her name was on the menu.

But first — tonight, before the light, before the fear could talk her out of it — she was going to find out what her mentor had traded away, and what he'd made them swear.

She reached for her coat.
