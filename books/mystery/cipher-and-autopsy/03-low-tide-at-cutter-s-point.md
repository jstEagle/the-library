## 3. Low Tide at Cutter's Point

The coordinates arrived the way everything from my client did: typed, unsigned, and timed to make me feel like I was being managed. They came at 4:17 in the morning, which told me two things. One, my client didn't sleep well. Two, they wanted me on those flats before anyone else could be.

*48.9127° N, 124.6603° W. Low tide 6:42 AM. Come alone. Wear boots you don't like.*

I'd spent a decade in true crime, long enough to know that people who send anonymous instructions fall into two categories: the guilty trying to steer you away from something, and the terrified trying to steer you toward it. The trick was telling which was which before one of them steered you into the ocean.

The tide table said 6:42. The envelope of cash said this was worth getting up for. My boots — a pair I'd already mourned after the Ferndale mudflat incident — said nothing, because boots can't talk, though after three years of interviewing Marrow Bay's founding families, I'd stopped ruling things out.

* * *

Cutter's Point at dawn is the kind of beautiful that makes you angry. The fog was doing its slow theatrical burn-off over the water, gulls wheeling around the sea stacks like they were paid by the hour, and the whole coastline wearing that bruised lavender light that photographers would kill for and locals never once look at. Somewhere under all that postcard scenery, a girl had walked out onto these flats thirty years ago and simply ceased to exist.

Ada Quill, seventeen, last seen October 14th, 1994, heading down toward the water below Cutter's Point with a flashlight and, according to her sister, an argument she'd been rehearsing all week. The tide came in that night the way tides do — indifferent, thorough — and when it went out again, there was no Ada. No body, no shoe, no note. Just a town full of people who all suddenly remembered they'd been looking at fog.

I parked at the pullout above the beach, where the gravel shoulder still held the ghost-ruts of a hundred search parties' trucks, and looked down at what the ebb had uncovered: a vast apron of wet sand and shale, tide pools glinting like scattered coins, kelp laid out in dark ropes. The flats ran maybe four hundred yards to the waterline, and beyond them the Point itself jutted out like a jaw.

Nobody else was there. That should have felt peaceful. Instead it felt staged.

"Okay," I said to the gulls, because talking to yourself is either a sign of madness or a professional habit, and I prefer the second framing. "Show me."

The walk down was treacherous in the way my client had presumably intended — slick rock, ankle-deep channels, barnacles waiting like tiny lawyers for me to slip so they could file suit. My unloved boots earned their contempt. Halfway down I stopped checking my footing and started checking my surroundings, because that's the other thing a decade in this work teaches you: beautiful empty places are where bodies go, and the person who knows exactly where to send you is rarely thinking about your safety.

The coordinates put me at a shelf of flat shale near the base of the Point, sheltered from the road by a fold in the bluff. From up top, you couldn't see this spot. From the water, you'd have to be close. It was, I noted with the particular chill that passes for excitement in my line of work, an excellent place to hide something.

And someone had recently tried.

The tire tracks were fresh — fresh as in this morning, or possibly late last night, the edges still sharp, the sand inside them not yet slumped. They came down the old maintenance track that wound behind the bluff, a rutted dirt road the county had officially abandoned sometime in the nineties but which clearly hadn't gotten the memo. Two sets of tracks, actually. A truck coming down heavy, and leaving heavier. Deep treads, aggressive pattern, the kind of tires you put on a vehicle you intend to take places vehicles aren't supposed to go.

I crouched beside them and did the math I always do: width, wheelbase, direction. Coming down between midnight and three, if the dampness of the sand meant anything, and going back up the same way. Someone had driven down here in the dark, to a spot visible from nowhere, on the one night the tide would drop low enough to expose it.

People say coincidence like it's a comfort. In my experience, coincidence is just a pattern you haven't finished counting yet.

* * *

I followed the tracks to where they ended in a churned semicircle — a turn-around, deliberate, the driver backing up to whatever they'd come for. And there, in the lee of the shale shelf, the sand had been disturbed. Not dug, exactly. *Opened.* A rectangle maybe four feet by three, scraped back and then pushed roughly into place again, the way you'd cover something you expected to come back for rather than something you wanted gone forever.

My heart was doing that thing it does, the slow heavy knock, the one I've learned to trust more than any polygraph. I've stood over graves before. I've stood over places that used to be graves. Your body knows the difference before your brain catches up, some animal arithmetic about disturbed earth that no podcast has ever successfully explained.

I didn't have a shovel. What I had was a folding camp trowel, a pair of nitrile gloves, and a phone camera, and I used all three in the order that would keep me most defensible later: photographs first, wide and close, with a lens cap for scale. Then gloves. Then the trowel, working at the edge of the disturbance, lifting the wet sand in careful plates.

Six inches down, the trowel knocked against something that wasn't stone.

Tarp. Heavy-duty, blue-silver, the kind you buy at a hardware store by the roll. I cleared enough of it to understand the shape underneath — folded, wrapped around something roughly the size of a carry-on suitcase, bound with what looked like ratchet straps. It was buried shallow, shallow enough that the recent storms could have worked it loose, shallow enough that whoever put it here planned to retrieve it soon.

Here's the moment where I'm supposed to tell you I exercised caution. Where the responsible journalist steps back, calls the sheriff, documents from a distance, lets the professionals handle it.

I got both hands on the edge of the tarp and pulled.

It was heavier than it looked and lighter than I feared, which in the taxonomy of buried objects is its own kind of relief. I dragged it two feet clear of the hole, and through the folds I could feel the shape of it: rigid corners, metal edges, a handle. Not a body. A container.

I peeled back one corner of the tarp.

A toolbox. Old steel, rust blooming along the seams like orange lichen, the kind with the cantilevered trays that open into little steel staircases. A padlock hung from the hasp, corroded green, seized solid. Someone had wrapped this toolbox in plastic sheeting under the tarp — I could see the translucent film beneath the canvas — which meant they'd cared about keeping the contents dry. Which meant the contents mattered. Which meant someone, very recently, in the dark, had driven down to the flats where Ada Quill was last seen alive and buried a locked toolbox that they intended to come back for.

Or — and this was the thought that walked up my spine on cold little feet — someone had come back for it, found the tide wrong or the timing bad or their nerve failed, and reburied it. Planning to return. Maybe tonight. Maybe at the next good low tide.

Maybe at the equinox, when the water pulls back farther than any other night of the year.

I sat back on my heels in the wet sand and let that settle. Three years I'd been picking at this case — the identical alibis, the fog story, the four families who spoke about October 1994 like they were reciting from the same hymnal. Three years of being told, politely and then less politely, that Ada Quill was a tragedy, not a mystery, and that Marrow Bay had grieved enough. And now, within seventy-two hours of receiving an envelope full of cash and a sentence about a murder that hadn't happened yet, I was kneeling over freshly turned earth at the exact spot where the whole thing began.

My client knew these flats. Knew the tide tables. Knew — I was increasingly sure — that this spot mattered, had sent me here not to find a body but to witness that the past wasn't resting. That somebody was actively managing it. You don't bury a toolbox at the scene of a thirty-year-old disappearance unless the disappearance isn't finished with you.

The gulls had gone quiet. I hadn't noticed until then, the way you don't notice silence until it's total. Out past the waterline, the fog was burning off the horizon in long banners, and the tide, patient as a creditor, had begun its slow turn. The flats I'd walked in on were already softening at the edges, channels filling, the sea reclaiming its reading room.

I photographed everything — the tracks, the hole, the toolbox from six angles, the padlock, the tire treads with my lens cap beside them for scale. Then, because I am a professional and not an idiot, I took samples: a scraping of the rust, a snippet of the strap webbing, a jar of sand from the fill. Chain of custody begins at the moment you decide to care, and I decided to care somewhere around the second inch of sand.

Then I did the harder thing. I rebury it? Leave it? Take it?

Taking it was tempting. Taking it was also how evidence gets thrown out and witnesses get discredited and podcasters end up as footnotes in someone else's trial. But leaving it meant leaving it for whoever made those tracks, and every instinct I had said they were coming back. The straps, the plastic, the shallow grave of it — this was storage, not disposal. Storage implies retrieval. Retrieval implies a schedule.

I compromised the way compromise always works: badly, but survivably. I reburied the toolbox exactly as I'd found it, tamped the sand back into its rough rectangle, scattered a handful of dried kelp over the disturbance to break up the geometry, and marked the location three ways — GPS pin, physical landmarks sketched in my notebook, and a mental photograph I trusted more than either. If someone came back for it, I wanted to know. If they came back while I was watching, I wanted to know *who.*

Then I climbed, boots screaming, up to the pullout, and sat in my car with the engine off and watched the maintenance track through binoculars like the world's coldest, dampest voyeur.

* * *

Two hours. That's how long I gave it, coffee going cold in the cupholder, fog burning off into a bright indifferent morning, tourists beginning to trickle toward the lighthouse trail above. At 10:15, a truck came down the coast road.

Not onto the maintenance track — just along the highway, slowing. A gray F-250, county plates, the kind of truck that's everywhere in coastal towns and therefore invisible. It slowed at the pullout. Slowed enough that I slid down in my seat and became one with the upholstery. It sat there for maybe forty seconds. Long enough to look. Short enough to deny.

Then it rolled on, and I caught what I caught: a bumper sticker half-peeled from sun-bleach — MARROW BAY MARINA, EST. 1981 — and, when the driver's arm rested on the door, a wristwatch. Gold. Old-fashioned. The kind of watch a man inherits rather than buys.

I wrote it all down, because writing things down is how I pray.

A marina sticker. In a town where the marina belonged to the Marsh family, where the mayor's son ran the docks, where Elias Marsh — whom I hadn't met yet, whose name I only knew from clippings and a harbor license — was the third generation to hold the keys to the water. I wasn't ready to draw lines between dots yet. Lines need more than one dot, and I've hanged men in my head on thinner evidence than a bumper sticker, only to have them walk free in the daylight of better facts.

But the watch stayed with me. Gold, old-fashioned, inherited. There was a photograph in my archive — the dedication of the seawall, 1996, four founding families standing in a row with shovels like they were planting something instead of covering it. Aldous Marsh, front and center, and on his wrist, catching the sun: a gold watch, old even then.

Coincidence, I told myself. Coastal towns run on inherited watches the way they run on inherited grudges.

The tide was coming in now, fast and businesslike, erasing the flats, erasing the tracks, erasing the rectangle of turned sand until the whole shelf of shale was underwater and the sea looked like it had never kept a secret in its life. That's the thing about Marrow Bay I'd never been able to shake in three years of coverage: the town doesn't hide its history. It doesn't have to. It just waits for the water to do it.

I drove back toward town with wet socks and a head full of arithmetic. Fresh tracks plus buried toolbox plus equinox tide plus a client who knew exactly where to point me equaled a question I could no longer pretend was hypothetical:

If someone was storing secrets at the place where Ada Quill disappeared — secrets worth driving down to in the dark, worth wrapping in plastic, worth guarding from a highway pullout —

then what was in the box?

And more urgently, since my client had promised me a murder on a schedule: was the box the reason, or the warning?

I needed to know who else had been asking questions about Cutter's Point lately. And in a town of four thousand people, there was exactly one institution that tracked comings and goings on the water, one family that had eyes on every boat, every dock, every tide chart posted at the harbor mouth.

The marina office opened at noon. According to the website, it was run by a man named Elias Marsh, who — the *Marrow Bay Beacon* had noted, in a puff piece I'd read eleven times — "never forgot a face, especially not the faces of the kids who grew up on these docks."

Kids like Ada Quill, who'd grown up on those docks too.

I pointed the car toward the harbor, telling myself I was following a lead, knowing full well I was also walking straight into the arms of the family whose name was starting to surface like something the tide had given up.

Some cases you investigate. This one, I was beginning to suspect, was investigating me right back.
