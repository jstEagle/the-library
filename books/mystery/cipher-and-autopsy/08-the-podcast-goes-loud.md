## 8. The Podcast Goes Loud

There's a particular kind of silence that follows the moment you hit *publish* on something you know is going to hurt people. It's not the silence of an empty room. It's the silence of a held breath — yours, and everyone else's, all at once.

I sat in my rented studio above the bait shop on Harbor Street, finger hovering over the mouse, listening to my own heartbeat do its impression of a metronome. On the screen, the episode title glowed back at me: **"Cipher & Autopsy, Episode 47: The Fog That Never Was (Part One)."**

I hadn't used the weather records yet. Not fully. That was Chapter Thirteen of my private outline, the one I kept in a composition notebook with a rubber band around it because I'd grown paranoid enough to write things down on paper like it was 1994. But I had enough. I had the tire tracks at Cutter's Point photographed from four angles. I had the tarp, or rather the rectangle of disturbed sand where a tarp had been buried and recently dug up again, the tide doing its patient forensic work. And I had the phrase — the phrase all four patriarchs had given me, word for identical word, across three decades and four separate interviews: *the fog rolled in around nine.*

Four men who barely spoke to each other anymore, according to everyone in town, describing the same night with the same sentence, down to the same pause before "nine." You don't get that from memory. You get that from rehearsal.

So I teased it. I didn't name names — not yet, not without the weather service records to make the lie physically impossible instead of just suspiciously symmetrical. But I laid out the four quotes side by side, let listeners hear the identical cadence, and ended the episode with a line I'd rewritten eleven times:

*"Someone taught these men what to say about the night Ada Quill disappeared. Next week, we're going to talk about what the sky says about their story."*

Then I clicked publish, took a long drink of cold coffee, and waited for Marrow Bay to start screaming.

* * *

It started screaming within the hour, which was faster than I expected and told me something about how many people still listened with their fingers hovering over the dial, so to speak.

The first call came into the show's voicemail line at 4:12 p.m. A woman's voice, shaking: *"You should be ashamed. That girl's family has suffered enough."* Click.

The second, at 4:31, was a man who identified himself only as "a concerned citizen" and informed me that outsiders who came to town stirring up old grief tended to have accidents on our roads. *"Curves out past Cutter's Point,"* he said helpfully. *"Real dangerous in the dark."* Click.

By six there were eleven messages. By seven, Doreen from the chowder house — my sole ally among the local business community, mostly because I ate there four nights a week and tipped like a woman with a guilty conscience — called my cell directly.

"You're famous," she said. "Or infamous. Half the room walked out when your episode came on over the kitchen radio."

"The other half?"

"Ate their chowder and didn't say a word. Which around here is louder than walking out." A pause, the sound of her covering the receiver, then, lower: "Hollis Marsh was in earlier. Mayor Marsh. He asked me where your studio was."

I felt that land somewhere under my ribs. "What did you tell him?"

"I told him I didn't know, which is technically true, since you've never told anybody and I've never asked. But Cyrus—" Doreen's voice dropped further, into the register she probably used for telling sailors a storm was coming. "He already knew. He said 'the place over the bait shop' like he was reading it off a list."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I did next, because it wasn't brave. It was the opposite of brave. I went home to my rental on Larkspur Lane, checked that the door was locked twice, and lay on top of the covers fully dressed, staring at the ceiling, cataloguing every creak the old house made until about midnight, when exhaustion won the argument.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about this work: the fear doesn't arrive all at once like a wave. It arrives in installments. First it's a phone message. Then it's a mayor asking after your address. Then it's you, lying awake at 1 a.m., realizing you've memorized the exact pitch of your own door hinge so you'll know if it opens when it shouldn't.

Somewhere in that gray hour before sleep finally took me, I thought about Elias Marsh. I thought about his hands on the wheel of his boat, the way he'd said *my father was fishing with two witnesses* and how his eyes had done a small, quick thing — a flinch, a flicker, gone before I could swear to it. I thought about how much I wanted him to be clean, and how wanting something has never once made it true in my professional experience, and how I was apparently willing to keep interviewing him anyway, coffee cup in hand, heart doing things hearts have no business doing during an investigation.

Then I slept, badly, and woke to sunlight and gulls and the ordinary machinery of a Tuesday, and I almost convinced myself the whole thing was manageable.

Almost.

* * *

The break-in happened while I was at the chowder house eating lunch, which I later understood was the point. Whoever did it watched me leave. Watched me walk the six blocks to Doreen's, order the same bowl I always ordered, sit in the same seat by the window. They gave me an alibi, of sorts. Considerate, in the way that a knife between the shoulder blades is considerate because at least it's efficient.

I came back at 2:40 p.m. and knew before I touched the knob.

The lock was fine. That was the first wrong note — the lock was *fine*, unscratched, working smoothly, which meant they'd picked it or had a key or were good enough that it didn't matter. The second wrong note was the smell: someone else's cologne lingering faintly in the closed room, something cheap and sharp, drugstore aftershave with ambitions.

The third wrong note was my tapes.

I record everything on two formats because I'm a child of analog paranoia living in a digital world: everything lives on a hard drive, and the important interviews also live on cassette, in labeled boxes, alphabetized. Ada Quill. The founding families. Ferro the boatman, whose interview I'd recorded three weeks ago in his smoke-blue kitchen while he talked in circles around whatever he actually knew.

Every box had been opened. Every cassette pulled from its sleeve and set back in slightly wrong — a half-inch out of alignment, labels facing different directions. My neat little alphabetized archive looked like it had been through a washing machine and politely reassembled by someone with enormous patience and contempt.

I stood in the doorway for a long time. Long enough that the gulls outside changed shifts.

Here's what was missing: nothing. My laptop sat exactly where I'd left it. The hard drive, untouched. Cash in the desk drawer, untouched. Even the cheap microphone, worth maybe two hundred dollars to a pawn shop, sat in its foam cradle like a sleeping cat.

Nothing stolen. Everything examined.

That's not a burglary. A burglary takes things. This was a *reading*. Someone had gone through every tape — or pretended to, or wanted me to think they had — and left the room arranged just enough wrong that I would know, immediately and completely, that they'd been there. It was a sentence written in my own handwriting: *We can reach you. We can touch your work. We chose not to take anything this time.*

A message, not a theft.

I did what any reasonable person would do. I called the police. Specifically, I called Deputy Ansel Roy, who was the only member of the Marrow Bay PD who had ever returned my calls, and even he did it grudgingly, like I was a bill collector.

Ansel arrived twenty minutes later, a big slow man with a face like a basset hound who'd been disappointed by humanity early and often. He walked the room, wrote some things down, and crouched by the tape boxes with an expression I recognized as the local equivalent of a shrug.

"No forced entry," he said.

"Nope."

"Nothing taken."

"Nope."

"You got enemies, Ms. Villalobos?"

"I've got a podcast, Deputy. Same thing."

He almost smiled. Almost. He wrote something else down. "You know how it looks from where I sit? Looks like somebody wants you rattled. Question is whether rattling you is the whole point or step one."

"That's the question I was going to ask you."

"Yeah, well." He stood, knees popping like green firewood. "I'll file it. Don't expect miracles. Half this department eats Thanksgiving dinner at tables owned by the families you're poking at."

At least he was honest. In thirty years of true crime work I've learned that honesty from a cop is rarer and more valuable than competence.

After he left, I did the thing I'd been avoiding: I went through the tapes myself, one by one, checking each against my logbook. It took two hours. Every cassette accounted for. Every recording intact, as far as I could tell by spot-checking.

Except one.

Ferro's tape — the boatman, the one who'd talked in circles, the one who'd said, near the end, in a voice gone suddenly quiet: *"You want to know what happened to that girl, ask the tide. Tide keeps what the town gives it."* Except that tape, when I played it back, had a gap. Ninety seconds of blank hiss right in the middle, spliced so cleanly I wouldn't have caught it if I weren't looking. Someone had edited my tape. Physically cut and spliced the cassette, removed ninety seconds, and put it back in the box.

I sat very still and tried to remember exactly what Ferro had said in those ninety seconds. I remembered the shape of it but not the words — something about a boat, something about being paid to forget a schedule, something about a night in April when he'd taken money to keep his engine quiet. The specifics were gone, filed away in my memory under the general heading of *old man rambling*, because at the time I hadn't known it mattered.

They'd known it mattered. They'd listened to every minute of every tape, found the one fragment that scared them, and surgically removed it. And they'd left the rest, because taking everything would be theft, and theft gets investigated, and a message is only a message if the recipient survives to read it.

I should have been terrified. Part of me was — the part that had lain awake cataloguing door hinges. But underneath the fear, rising up through it like heat through floorboards, was something else. Something hotter and more useful.

Because here's what they didn't understand. I'm a true crime podcaster. I have spent three years making a show about a missing girl in a town that decided, collectively, to stop looking. I have been ignored, stonewalled, patronized, and now burgled with editorial precision. And every single one of those things has taught me the same lesson, the lesson that keeps this whole rotten profession alive:

When they come for the tapes, it's because the tapes matter.

They had just confirmed, with their own careful hands, that Ferro knew something worth cutting out. They'd told me where the live wire was buried. All I had to do was go back to the source and dig it up again — except this time, I'd do it properly. Two recorders. A witness. A transcript signed the same day. And a copy that lived somewhere no one with drugstore cologne and a lockpick could reach.

Fear is a fine emotion. It keeps you alive. But defiance pays better, in this line of work, and I have never once balanced a budget on staying alive alone.

* * *

I spent the evening turning my little studio into a fortress of redundancy. Backup drives wrapped in plastic and hidden in three locations I won't describe here, one of them involving Doreen's freezer and a bag of frozen peas that will now forever be off-limits. A duplicate of the Ferro tape log, handwritten, mailed to my attorney in Portland with instructions to hold it unopened. And a note to myself, taped above the desk, in block letters:

**THEY EDITED THE TAPE. THE TAPE MATTERS. FIND OUT WHY.**

Then I poured the last of the coffee and allowed myself five minutes of self-pity, which is my daily allowance, and spent four of them thinking about Elias.

I could call him. I could tell him about the break-in, watch his face — well, hear his voice, which is nearly as good — and measure the flinch. If his father sent someone to my studio, Elias either knew or didn't, and either answer would tell me something. That was the investigator's math, clean and cold.

But there was another column in the ledger, the one I kept trying to pretend didn't exist, where the math wasn't clean at all. Where calling Elias Marsh about a threat was also just... calling Elias Marsh. Hearing his voice. Giving him a reason to worry about me, and letting myself enjoy being worried about.

I didn't call. Not yet. Instead I picked up the phone and dialed the number Ferro had scrawled on the back of a tide chart three weeks ago, and when the old man answered on the fourth ring, voice like gravel settling in a bucket, I said:

"Ferro. It's Cyrus Villalobos. We need to talk again. And this time, I'm bringing a witness."

There was a long pause on the line. Long enough for me to hear the sea behind him, or a radio tuned to the marine band, or both.

"Figured you'd call," he said finally. "Figured it the minute I saw the truck parked outside your building yesterday afternoon. Gray truck, county plates, fella smoking cigarettes with the window cracked." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Been sitting there since Tuesday, Miss Villalobos. You want to know why your tapes got rifled, might start with asking who's been watching the watcher."

I looked out my window at the street below, empty in the lamplight, and felt the last warm coal of feeling safe go out.

"Ferro," I said slowly. "Tell me about that gray truck."
