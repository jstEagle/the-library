## 11. Guarding Ferro

The lie I told Silas Ferro was a small one, and it fit in a coffee cup.

"Free boat inspection," I said, sliding the cup across his galley table. "Insurance company promotion. They're doing them for boats over forty years old. Yours qualifies."

Ferro looked at me over the rim of his reading glasses with the expression of a man who had spent six decades being lied to by people who thought he was stupid, and who had learned to find it entertaining. His skiff, the *Marta Lee*, was fifty-one years old and held together partly by paint and partly by spite.

"Insurance companies don't do promotions," he said. "They do reductions."

"Then call it a reduction."

He set down his glasses. Outside, through the salt-hazed window of his houseboat, the marina water did its slow gray breathing. It was four days until the equinox tide, and every hour since my client's letter had named him — *the old boatman has begun talking; they will use the spring tide* — had felt like sand running through a cracked hourglass.

"Miss Villalobos." He pronounced it carefully, like a word in a foreign language he'd decided to learn anyway. "You think I'm in danger."

"I think you're a man who's started telling stories."

"I've always told stories. That's what old men are for." He turned the coffee cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a fussy, deliberate gesture. "But yes. Lately I've been telling one particular story to anyone who'll stand still for it. About the night Ada Quill went in the water."

"And that makes you interesting to the wrong people."

"It makes me *interesting*," he agreed. "Interesting is dangerous at my age. Interesting gets you invited places. Interesting gets your brakes looked at." He smiled, and there was something under the smile that wasn't smiling at all. "So. This free inspection. Where does it happen?"

"There's a motel up past the headlands. The Driftwood. Quiet this time of year. The insurance adjuster comes Thursday."

"The insurance adjuster," Ferro repeated.

"A friend of mine. He's very thorough about hulls."

Ferro laughed then, a dry rattle like a winch taking strain, and I understood that he knew exactly what I was doing and was letting me do it, because a man who lives alone on a boat for sixty years develops a taste for other people's conspiracies. He'd been waiting, maybe, for someone to come lie to him kindly.

"All right," he said. "I'll be inspected. But I'm bringing the *Marta Lee*'s logbooks, because if I'm going to sit in a motel room pretending to wait for an adjuster, I'm going to spend the time being useful. You want to know about the night of September ninth, 1994? I kept records, girl. Boatmen keep records. Tides, weather, who chartered what, who paid cash, who came back late with mud on their boots." He tapped his temple. "And in here, the parts that don't go in books."

My heart did something unprofessional. "Why didn't anyone ever ask you?"

"Somebody asked me once. Thirty years ago. A man with a nice coat and a district attorney's business card, and afterward I decided Marrow Bay preferred its stories the way it prefers its fish — gutted, cleaned, and sold without the head." He stood, joints announcing themselves like a ship changing tack. "Pack a bag, Miss Villalobos. Let's get me inspected."

* * *

The Driftwood Motel sat above the headland road like something the fog had forgotten to take back: twelve rooms, a rusted sign shaped like a seagull that no longer resembled anything, an ice machine that hummed all night like it was confessing. I paid for Room 6 in cash under the name of the insurance scheme — Calloway Adjustments, which was real enough, a favor from a contact in Portland who owed me for a story I never ran — and installed Ferro with a bag of groceries, a burner phone with my number as the only entry, and strict instructions.

"No charters. No dock gossip. No walks after dark. If anyone knocks, you're deaf and expecting an adjuster."

"I've been deaf for years when it suits me," Ferro said, setting his logbooks on the dresser like scripture. "Go do whatever it is podcasters do. Skulk, I imagine."

Skulking, as it happened, was exactly the plan.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing: if someone intended to use the equinox tide to make Ferro disappear, they would need to know where he was. Moving him was half the protection. The other half was finding out who came looking — because whoever came looking would lead me back up the chain, and the chain, everything in my bones insisted, ended at the mayor's office.

So I did the thing I'd sworn off doing, which was asking Elias Marsh for a favor.

I told myself it was operational. The marina was the town's throat; everything that moved on the water passed through Elias's office, his fuel dock, his ledger of moorage fees and charter bookings. If a stranger had come to Marrow Bay recently — a man with a camera, a man with a boat, a man whose hands were wrong — Elias would know. And if I asked anyone else, the question itself would travel faster than the answer.

I found him at the fuel dock at dusk, coiling a hose with the absent competence of a man who'd been doing chores on that dock since he could walk. The light was doing that thing the Pacific Northwest does in early spring, turning the water to hammered pewter and gilding the edges of everything, including, inconveniently, him.

"You look like a woman with a question she doesn't want to ask," he said, without turning around.

"How can you tell?"

"You've got your reporter face on. It's the same as your regular face but with the joy removed." He shut off the hose and faced me. "Ask."

I'd rehearsed a version built on the podcast — *researching charter traffic around the disappearance dates, historical and current* — but standing there with the smell of diesel and low tide between us, I heard myself say something closer to true.

"If someone wanted to hurt an old man in this town quietly, how would they do it?"

Elias went still. Not dramatically — Elias didn't do dramatic; his stillnesses were structural, like a building settling — but I felt the temperature of him change.

"What kind of old man?"

"Boatman. Talks too much. Remembers things."

"That describes half the docks north of San Francisco." But his eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere behind me, doing math I couldn't see. "Cyrus. What have you got?"

"Nothing I can hand you yet. That's not evasion, it's—" I exhaled. "It's the only kind of safe I know how to be right now. I need names. Anyone new hanging around the marina in the last month. Anyone asking about Ferro, or about tides, or about the Quill case. Anyone your father's office sent down here for any reason."

That last one landed. I watched it land. There was a flicker in his face — quick, quickly buried — that I filed away with all the other flickers, the flinch when he defended his father's alibi, the pause before he said *my dad would never*, the way he said *never* slightly too many times.

"My father's office doesn't send people to my marina," he said. "My father's office barely acknowledges my marina exists unless there's a ribbon involved."

"Then this should be easy. A list of strangers. That's all."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something — whether to trust me, whether to protect me, whether those were different things. When he spoke again his voice was quieter.

"There's a guy. Been around three weeks, maybe. Rents a slip on D-dock, says he's a sport fisherman out of Coos Bay, but his boat's got no wear on it. Brand-new anchor line, factory shine on the cleats. Nobody fishes a boat that clean." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Name on the registration is Dale Kessler. I ran it through a buddy at the harbor patrol. The registration's real. The address is a PO box. And the photo on the license looks like it was taken by someone who hated him."

"Why didn't you mention him before?"

"Because until thirty seconds ago he was just a sad man with a clean boat." Elias's jaw worked. "Cyrus, if this goes where I think it goes—"

"It doesn't go anywhere yet. That's the point of asking instead of announcing." I put my hand on his forearm, and felt him steady under it, and hated how much I needed the steadying myself. "D-dock. Which slip?"

"Twelve. But Cyrus—" he caught my wrist as I turned, gently, the way you catch something you're afraid of breaking, "—whatever you're going to do tonight, don't do it alone, and don't do it close. Look from far away. Promise me."

"I promise to be excellent at looking from far away."

"That's not a promise."

"It's the best one I've got that I intend to keep."

He let go of my wrist slowly, like releasing a knot he might need later. I walked away along the dock with the boards knocking softly under my feet, and behind me I heard him coil the hose again, harder than necessary, the sound of a man winding himself tight around a thing he couldn't say.

* * *

I took position at eleven p.m. in the gravel lot of the shuttered cannery, two hundred yards and one chain-link fence from D-dock, with a thermos of terrible coffee, my camera with the long lens, and the particular patience that three years of dead-end interviews had burned into me like scar tissue. Stakeouts, I'd learned, are mostly a war between your eyes and your imagination, and the imagination cheats.

The marina at night was a different animal than the marina by day. By day it was ropes and gulls and tourists buying bait they'd never use. At night it breathed. Halyards ticked against masts like slow clocks. Water slapped hulls in irregular applause. Somewhere a sealion complained about the terms of its existence.

Slip D-12 showed a single dim light aboard the clean boat. No movement. I settled in.

At 11:40, a car came down the marina access road without headlights.

That's a thing you don't forget how to notice. Headlights-off driving is either a drunk, a teenager, or a professional, and the car — a gray sedan, county plates, the kind of vehicle that is deliberately invisible because invisibility is its whole job — parked with the smooth economy of a man who has parked in the dark many times. It stopped in the visitor lot, nose pointed toward the exit, engine left running.

I raised the camera.

The driver sat for a full minute. Then the dome light bloomed once as he got out — and the lens gave me what the darkness withheld: a heavyset man in a canvas jacket, mid-fifties, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone on familiar errands. He crossed to D-dock, and here was the detail that made my mouth go dry: he didn't hesitate at the gate code. He keyed it in from memory. First try.

A stranger of three weeks wouldn't know the gate code cold. A sport fisherman out of Coos Bay wouldn't stroll D-dock like a man walking his own driveway.

He stopped at Slip 12. Not the clean boat's slip — the slip two down, where a beat-up twenty-foot skiff lay under a tarp. Ferro's backup boat. The *Marta Lee*'s little sister, the one Ferro used for crab pots when the big boat was feeling temperamental. The man crouched beside it, and I saw the small bloom of a screen light against his face — a phone, held up, photographing. He photographed the skiff. He photographed the tarp's tie-downs. Then he straightened, turned, and photographed the empty slip where the *Marta Lee* herself should have been.

And stayed there. Looking at the empty slip. For a long time.

I understood, watching him, with a clarity that arrived cold and complete: he was confirming. The target had moved. The primary vessel gone, the houseboat dark, the old man vanished into the town's anonymous interior — and this man's job tonight was to document the absence and report it, so that someone above him could decide whether the plan bent or broke.

He took one more photo — of the marina office, dark, Elias's truck parked outside it — and then walked back to the sedan with the same unhurried stride, and I got my shot: the dome light again, a half-second of full illumination, and the camera drank it.

When I got home and blew the image up on my laptop, the face resolved out of the grain like a body surfacing in murky water. Heavy jaw, thinning hair, a face you'd pass in a hardware store and forget by the parking lot. I ran it through nothing official — I had nothing official — but I had spent three years building a map of Marrow Bay's faces, and faces travel in packs here. I'd seen this one before. In the background of a 2019 chamber-of-commerce photo. Standing at the edge of a groundbreaking ceremony. Always at the edge, always in frame, always near the center of gravity.

Near Mayor Richard Marsh.

The name came to me by morning, from a harbormaster's retirement party guest list I'd pulled months ago and never had reason to care about: **Roy Pell**. Facilities liaison, mayor's office. The man whose actual job, according to the city website, was "coordination of municipal waterfront operations."

Coordination of municipal waterfront operations. Photographing a missing man's boats at midnight, gate code memorized, engine running.

I sat with the printout in the blue hour before dawn, the motel ice machine's confession humming through the wall, and let the resolve settle into me the way cold settles into a diver's bones — not suddenly, but totally, and all the way down. They had moved on Ferro already. The letter's countdown wasn't metaphorical; it was a schedule, and I was now officially ahead of it by one motel room and one photograph.

Ferro knocked on my door at seven with two cups of gas-station coffee and his logbooks under his arm, bright-eyed as a boy playing hooky.

"Well?" he said. "Did the adjuster come?"

"Nobody came," I said. "But somebody's looking. And last night, Roy Pell — the mayor's facilities man — photographed your boats and reported you missing from the marina."

Ferro took this in, sipping his coffee. Then he nodded slowly, unsurprised, and set his cup down on the logbooks like a paperweight on history.

"Then we'd best hurry with these," he said. "Because a man who's been photographed is a man on a clock. And I've got thirty years of September nights in here that certain people would pay a great deal to see stay in a motel room forever."

He opened the first logbook to a page flagged with a yellowed strip of tape — September 9, 1994 — and my phone buzzed against the table.

Unknown number. One line of text:

**You moved him. Clever. Now ask your boyfriend what his father pays Pell to coordinate.**

I looked at the message for a long time. Then at the old man waiting patiently with his open book. Then out the window, toward the marina, where the fog was burning off the water in long silver sheets, revealing, as fog always does, only what was already there.
