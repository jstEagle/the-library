## 12. Elias Confronted

The ledger page had been sitting in my glove compartment for two days, riding around Marrow Bay in the passenger seat of my rental like a coiled snake I kept forgetting to feed. I'd take it out, look at it, put it back. Take it out. Put it back. A very disciplined investigative method, I know. Somewhere a journalism professor is weeping into his tenure.

It was one photocopied page from the mayor's discretionary fund — the kind of document that exists in the gray zone between public record and buried evidence, the kind you get from a clerk who looks over both shoulders and then pretends he never saw you. Dated March, three weeks after Ferro started "talking" — Ferro's word, delivered with the relish of a man who'd spent thirty years being nobody and had recently discovered the intoxicating flavor of being somebody. The page listed a payment. Four hundred dollars a week, ongoing, to one D. Pruitt, described as "marina security consulting."

D. Pruitt. Dale Pruitt. The man I'd watched photograph Ferro's motel door with the patience of a man photographing a door he intended to walk through eventually.

Four hundred a week doesn't buy much security consulting. It buys exactly one thing: eyes. And eyes, in my experience, are the down payment on hands.

I'd been carrying the page around because I couldn't decide what to do with it, and I couldn't decide what to do with it because of the specific problem of Elias Marsh, and I couldn't avoid the specific problem of Elias Marsh because he kept bringing me coffee like a man who had never once considered that coffee could be a weapon.

* * *

He was under a boat when I found him, which felt symbolically apt. The *Merrilee Ann*, a thirty-two-foot sloop hauled out for spring scraping, and Elias beneath her on a creeper, just his legs sticking out into the gray morning like the punchline to a joke nobody had told yet. The marina smelled the way it always smelled — diesel and brine and old rope — and the gulls were conducting their usual parliament over the bait shed, and for one stupid, hanging moment I stood there and let myself enjoy the ordinary fact of him. The rolled sleeves. The smear of bottom paint on one forearm. The way he whistled something tuneless and content while he worked, as though the world were a place where contentment was a reasonable position to hold.

Then I remembered the photograph of Ferro's door. The one taken from the dune grass by a man on his father's payroll.

"Your starboard leg is dirty," I said.

He slid out, blinking up at me, and his whole face did the thing it always did when it found me — a brightening, quick and unguarded, the face of a man arriving somewhere he'd been looking forward to. I hated that face. I hated how reliably it worked on me. Three weeks ago I'd have told you I was constitutionally immune to men who whistled while they worked; I'd have said my type was "fictional" and left it at that. Then a man had handed me a coffee and remembered how I took it — black, "like my sense of humor" — and here we were.

"Cyrus." He sat up, wiping his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "You're up early. Or up late. With you it's honestly hard to say."

"I keep journalist hours. Which means I keep no hours and blame the profession." I crouched down to his level, which was a mistake, because it put us close, and closeness with Elias Marsh was a drug with a very short half-life and a very long tail of regret. "I need to ask you something, and I'd like you to answer it before you charm me, because you're good at the charming and we both know it."

His smile flickered — not out, just dimmed, like a bulb sensing weather. "That sounds ominous."

"It is." I took out the ledger page. I'd rehearsed this in the truck, various versions — the soft approach, the hard approach, the approach where I simply slid it across and said nothing, letting the document do the talking, which is the approach I always claim is my favorite and never actually use because silence is a muscle I haven't built. I went with the slide. It landed on the creeper beside his knee, face up, in the flat gray light.

He looked at it.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only thing I have left when everything else goes sideways. Elias Marsh did not snatch the page. He did not laugh it off. He did not do the thing guilty men do, which is reach for the paper like it's burning them, trying to control it, turn it over, pocket it. He looked at it the way you look at a diagnosis. Still. Reading it twice. And the color went out of his face in a single tide-like withdrawal, all at once, top to bottom, until he was the color of the fog that never rolled in on the night Ada Quill disappeared.

"Dale Pruitt," he said. His voice came out level. Too level. The voice of a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"Four hundred a week," I said. "Marina security consulting. Your father's discretionary fund. Dale Pruitt, who spent Tuesday evening photographing the motel room door of a seventy-eight-year-old man who has recently become talkative."

"I know who Dale Pruitt is."

"I know you know who Dale Pruitt is. He's on your dock twice a week. He eats lunch at the Harbor Light with your harbor master. He's not a ghost, Elias. He's an employee. Yours or your father's, and the ledger says your father's, and the ledger is the least of it, because what the ledger *means* is that the mayor of this town is paying a man to watch the last living witness to the worst thing that ever happened here, and I have to believe there's an explanation, and I have to believe it isn't the obvious one, and I'm running out of runway on both counts."

I hadn't meant to say all of that. I'd meant to be surgical. But it had been building for days — the photograph, the ledger, Ferro's bruised knuckles and defiant grin, the client's letters with their childlike handwriting and their terrible arithmetic — and it came out of me in one long exhale, and once it was out I couldn't take it back, and I didn't especially want to.

Elias stood up. He did it slowly, the way big decisions get made in the body before they get made in the head. He looked at the ledger page again. Then he looked at me, and what was in his face was not guilt. I want to be fair to him here, even now, even after everything. It was not guilt. It was something worse to watch, actually, because guilt is simple and this was not. It was the look of a man standing at a fork in a road where both paths go through his own chest.

"Where did you get this?"

"A clerk with a conscience and a bad cough. That's not the question that matters."

"It matters to me." And there it was — a flash of something hard under the softness, the Marsh jaw setting the way I'd seen it set once before, the night I'd asked him about his father's fishing trip alibi and he'd defended Tom Marsh with a fluency that had felt, even then, slightly over-rehearsed. "You went through my father's accounts."

"I went through *public* accounts that your father keeps *private*, which is its own little sermon." I stood too, so we were eye to eye, or eye to chin, because Elias was built like the boats he fixed, all beam and ballast. "I didn't come here to fight about methodology. I came here because you are the only person in this town who has ever told me anything true, and I need one more true thing. What is your father doing with Dale Pruitt?"

The gulls went up off the bait shed all at once, a white ragged sheet of them, screaming about something, and in the noise Elias didn't answer, and in the silence after the noise he didn't answer either, and I understood with a cold professional clarity that the silence was the answer, or at least a wing of it.

"Cyrus." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "I need forty-eight hours."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me like a bark, ugly and surprised. "Forty-eight hours. You're asking for forty-eight hours."

"Two days. That's all I'm asking."

"Two days." I held up the ledger page between two fingers, a little white flag that wasn't one. "Elias, the spring equinox is in four days. The lowest tide this coast has seen in thirty years. My client — the one who hired me, the one who knew things about 1994 that only an insider could know — says a murder is scheduled. Scheduled, Elias, like a dentist appointment, like a dedication ceremony. And your father has just announced a marina dedication at Cutter's Point timed to the exact hour of that tide. And the man he's paying four hundred a week is parked outside the door of the one witness who can put a Marsh family boat in Cutter's Cove the night Ada Quill went into the water. And you want forty-eight hours."

"Yes."

"To do what?"

He didn't answer. He looked at the boat above us, the *Merrilee Ann* with her belly stripped to bare fiberglass, and then at the water beyond the fuel dock, flat and pewter-colored and keeping its mouth shut the way Marrow Bay water always had, and I watched him weigh something, and I watched him decide against it, and the deciding happened right there on his face where I could see it, which was somehow more insulting than if he'd lied.

"I can't tell you that yet."

"Yet." I folded the ledger page along its original crease, slowly, giving him every second to stop me, to say *wait, here's the truth, here it is, you don't have to fold anything.* "That's a very interesting word, yet. It implies a schedule. It implies you know something with a timeline attached. It implies that in two days the thing you can't tell me will either be tellable or moot."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Take everything I say and turn it into evidence." His voice cracked on *evidence*, just slightly, just enough, and for one naked second he looked young — nineteen, maybe, the age his father had been in 1994, the age at which the Marsh family apparently began learning its family trade. "I'm not the enemy here, Cyrus. Whatever you think is happening, I am not the enemy."

"I don't think you're the enemy." And I didn't. That was the whole catastrophe of it. If I'd thought he was the enemy I could have walked away clean, taken the ledger to the DA, taken Ferro to a motel two states over, done the job the way the job was supposed to be done. But I didn't think he was the enemy. I thought he was a good man standing in front of a bad thing, and I thought the bad thing had his name on it somewhere, in the way family names get written on things — in ink you don't choose and can't wash out. "I think you might be standing between me and one, though, and I think you know which one, and I think forty-eight hours is either you fixing it or you burying it deeper, and I have no way of knowing which, and that — that is the problem, Elias. Not the ledger. The not-knowing. I can live with a lot of things. Not-knowing is the one thing my whole life is built to refuse."

The marina was waking up around us. A crab boat grumbled out past the breakwater, trailing its smell. The harbormaster's radio crackled something about a mooring dispute. Ordinary life, doing its ordinary pantomime, while the two of us stood under a gutted boat and conducted the autopsy of whatever we'd been building.

"Two days," he said again, quieter. "Then I'll tell you everything. I swear it."

"You understand that's what people say in the last act," I said. "In my experience, 'give me two days' is what men say right before the thing happens that the entire podcast was about."

"Then don't give me two days." He spread his hands. It was, I think, the most honest gesture he'd ever made at me — an open offer, no cards in them, or at least no cards he was willing to show. "Go to the sheriff. Go to the DA. Publish the ledger page tonight if you want. I won't stop you. I'm asking, not demanding. I'm asking because I think — I *believe* — that two days from now you'll understand why I couldn't do it today, and I believe you'll be glad I didn't."

Nobody has ever been glad anyone waited. I want that on the record. In thirty years of true crime and true liars, no one has ever come back from an asked-for delay with anything but a worse truth.

But here is the other thing I want on the record, the thing I'm less proud of: he said *I won't stop you*, and his eyes didn't move, and his voice didn't shake, and some soft, stupid, tide-addled part of me looked at that stillness and read it as innocence. Read it as *he has nothing to hide*. When every professional instinct I own — every instinct that found the fog story, that caught the identical alibis, that heard the rehearsed phrase in four different mouths — was standing in the back of my skull with its arms crossed, saying: *the calm ones are always the ones holding something. The calm ones learned it young.*

"Forty-eight hours," I said. "From now. Not from sunset, not from whenever it's convenient. From now. And Elias — if Ferro so much as stubs a toe in the next two days, the clock doesn't just stop. It reverses. Everything you've ever told me gets re-read in the worst possible light, and I will burn this town down to its pilings to get to why. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you." And he did something then that I'm still turning over, weeks later, in the small hours, in the way you turn over a shell and find the animal gone. He reached out and touched my shoulder — just my shoulder, just a second, just a weight and a warmth through my jacket — and said, "Thank you for asking me first. Before anyone else. It matters that you asked me first."

It matters that you asked me first.

I drove out of the marina with the ledger page back in the glove compartment and my hands at ten and two and my heart doing something I refused to name. I got a mile up the coast road before I had to pull over, because my eyes had gone unreliable, and I sat in the rental with the engine ticking and the sea doing its gray breathing beyond the guardrail, and I made myself say it out loud to the windshield, because saying things out loud is how I keep from drowning in them:

"You let him go. You had the page, you had the photograph, you had Ferro, and you let him walk back under that boat, and you did it because he touched your shoulder and said your name like it was something worth keeping."

The windshield said nothing. Windshields are terrible confidants.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I do instead of feeling things: work. I cross-indexed Dale Pruitt's shifts against the marina log — he'd signed on for "night security" the very week Ferro first mentioned Cutter's Cove to me, which meant the watching had begun within days of the talking. I photographed the ledger page from four angles. I drafted — and did not send — an email to the district attorney with three attachments and a subject line I rewrote six times, finally settling on *Possible imminent harm — please advise*, which is the most cowardly subject line ever composed by a woman who bills herself as fearless.

And at 6:40 in the evening, with the light going bronze over the harbor, I drove past Ferro's motel out of pure habit — the stakeout habit, the one I'd told myself I'd retired — and saw the sedan.

It was parked across the highway in the gravel pull-off by the bait freezer, engine off, windows down despite the chill. Dale Pruitt's sedan, or one indistinguishable from it. And in the driver's seat, in the last of the light, a man with a camera.

Not photographing the door this time. Photographing the window. Ferro's window, where the lamp burned, where the old man sat — I could see his silhouette through the curtain, moving, alive, pouring something from a bottle into a glass — and the camera's lens tracked him the way a lens tracks something it is learning. The way you study a route. A schedule. A habit.

Pruitt wasn't watching Ferro anymore. Watching is what you do when the question is open. Pruitt was *recording* him. Watching is research; recording is rehearsal.

I sat in my rental on the shoulder of the highway with my heart going like a bilge pump and did the math I'd been refusing to do all day. Forty-eight hours. Elias's clock. And across the highway, a man on the mayor's payroll, logging the last witness's every movement with the patience of a tide.

The two clocks were not the same clock. I'd let myself believe they were. I'd let a shoulder-touch and a sincere pair of eyes convince me that Elias's two days and Ferro's safety ran on the same current, and now, watching the camera lens glint in the dying light, I understood — with the flat, sick certainty that always arrives about forty-eight hours too late — that one of those clocks was built to run out just after the other one did.

I picked up my phone. I pulled up Elias's number. My thumb hovered.

Then Ferro's lamp went out, and the silhouette folded down out of sight, and across the highway, very quietly, the sedan's engine started.
