## 6. A Client Who Lies Beautifully

The call came at 4:17 in the morning, which Cyrus Eastwick would later understand was the first honest thing about Edmund Voss.

No one dies honestly at a civilized hour. The dead keep the hours of the tide, of the body, of the things that come undone in the dark. The voice on the phone belonged to a Dr. Ferrow, who identified himself as the attending physician at Greyharbour Cottage Hospital, and who said, with the flat kindness of a man who delivers this sentence nightly, that Mr. Voss had passed in his sleep, quietly, that his papers had named her, and would she come.

She sat for a long time on the edge of her bed with the phone still warm against her ear, watching the streetlight through the gap in her curtains make its thin gold blade across the floor. Passed in his sleep. Quietly. She had spent three weeks in the company of Edmund Voss's frailty — the rattling breath, the blue-veined hands, the way he gripped her wrist when he spoke of Maren as though the name were a rope thrown from a drowning ship — and she had believed it. She had *pitied* it. And pity, she knew now, was the most expensive thing a lawyer could spend, because it was the one currency clients could counterfeit flawlessly.

She dressed in the dark. She did not turn on lights. This was habit, not superstition: a woman who had erased herself learned to move through her own life the way a burglar moves through someone else's house, touching nothing, leaving no prints, wanting nothing to remember her by.

* * *

Greyharbour in the grey hour before dawn looked like a town holding its breath underwater. Fog lay along the harbor road in long white ropes. The fishing boats knocked against the quay with a sound like patient knocking at a door no one intended to answer. The cottage hospital was a squat granite building at the town's edge, its windows lit the color of weak tea, and inside it smelled of disinfectant laid over something older and saltier, the smell of a place where the sea had been getting in for a hundred years and the staff had stopped fighting it.

Dr. Ferrow was younger than his voice. He had the careful hands of a man who had learned early not to trust what he was handed.

"Ms. Eastwick." He did not offer to shake. "He was lucid yesterday evening. Asked for paper. Wrote for nearly an hour, sealed the envelope himself, gave it to the night nurse with instructions. Then he took his medication and went to sleep." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "I want to be clear with you. Nothing about his death was unexpected. Nothing about it was irregular. But I've been a doctor in this town for eleven years, and I'll say this once, off the record: that man was the most composed dying person I have ever attended. He wasn't afraid. He was *finished*."

"Where is the envelope?"

He led her down a corridor the color of old bone to a small room where Edmund Voss lay under a sheet, his hands folded on his chest by someone else's arrangement. On the bedside table, squared precisely to the edge, sat a heavy cream envelope with her name on it in a hand she recognized — the same careful, old-fashioned script that had filled the pages of the will she'd spent three weeks trying to authenticate.

She took it. Her fingers were steady. She was proud of that, in the way one is proud of small virtues in large disasters.

"Was anyone else informed?" she asked. "Family?"

Something moved behind Ferrow's eyes — not quite reluctance. Wariness. "There's a son. Alistair. He lives up the coast, past the headland. He and his father weren't close." He said it the way the harbor clerk had said *Maren died twice* — a sentence worn smooth from handling, a stone everyone in this town had picked up and put down so many times it had lost its edges. "I'd expect him to arrive today."

Cyrus thanked him and left without looking at the bed. She had a professional's discipline about the dead — you do not stare at what you may someday be — but in the corridor she realized she was sorry she hadn't looked, and the sorry surprised her. She had liked Edmund Voss. That was the thing she kept circling, the splinter she couldn't dig out. In three weeks of meetings, in the dim parlor of his house above the harbor with its photographs of a woman whose face he could barely look at, he had been the first client in years who had seemed to want nothing from her but the truth. Everyone else wanted a version of it. He had wanted the thing itself, whatever it cost.

She believed that for exactly as long as it took her to get back to her rented room, boil water for tea she wouldn't drink, and open the envelope.

* * *

The document inside was not long. It was written in that same deliberate script, dated the previous evening, and it began, in language she recognized as her own — phrases she had used in their meetings, returned to her like her reflection in dark water:

*To Cyrus Eastwick, executor, and to no one else, because no one else will do what must be done.*

*I am dying, and I find I am not brave enough to say this aloud, and not clever enough to write it plainly and trust it to survive me. So I have built you a machine, Ms. Eastwick. You have been turning its gears for three weeks. I apologize for the deception. I do not apologize for the necessity.*

*You were hired to prove that my wife's murder never occurred. You have discovered instead that it occurred exactly as the record says — that Maren Voss drowned in the cove below Greyharbour on the fourth of November, and that I put her there.*

The tea went cold in its cup. Cyrus read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves. The room did not soften.

*I will not insult you with a confession of remorse. Remorse is what men like me feel for ourselves. I will give you only the mechanism, because you are a lawyer and mechanisms are your scripture.*

*The will I showed you — the one dated three days after her death — is genuine. Maren made it. She made it the week before she died, when she still believed she could leave me, and she hid it where she thought I'd never look, which was the one place I always look: among my own papers. It names her wishes. It names, in cipher — because she knew me, and knew I'd find it, and wanted to make me work for it — the name of the person she feared would kill her. I have solved her cipher. I am the answer. I have always been the answer.*

*The town helped me. They did not know they were helping me — they thought they were helping her, God forgive them, they built her a beautiful false death with a false body and false grief, and I simply stepped inside it. A murder hidden inside a faked death is the safest murder in the world. Everyone swears she's dead. Everyone is telling the truth.*

*You will find the cipher key in the lining of the hatbox in her old room, third shelf, under the winter gloves. She never could resist hiding things in plain sight. Neither, it turns out, could I.*

*Execute her will. Expose me. I have left you the means, the motive, and the map. It is the only inheritance I have that isn't poisoned.*

*One more thing. The body in the cove was not Maren. You may already suspect this. Find out whose it was. That answer is the one I could not give you, because I do not know it — and because the person who does know it is still alive, and still dangerous, and I am no longer in any position to protect you.*

*You were right not to trust me. Trust the dead woman instead. She was the only honest Voss.*

*— E.V.*

Cyrus set the pages down on the table with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might detonate.

Then she laughed. It came out of her like something breaking — one short, ugly sound in the empty room, and she pressed her hand over her mouth as if she could push it back.

The tenderness. The trembling hands. The way he'd said *Maren* as though the name cut him. Three weeks she had sat in that parlor cataloguing his grief like evidence of his innocence, and it had been theater — not the theater of an innocent man, but the theater of a *director*, a man staging his own posthumous trial and casting her, without her consent, in the role of prosecutor. He had hired her the way a suicide hires a witness. He had paid triple because confession is the one service worth triple rates. Every question he'd asked her had been a gear in the machine. Every document he'd produced had been baited. She had thought she was investigating a mystery. She had been *performing* one, on cue, in a play whose script was written before she ever arrived.

And underneath the fury — because it was fury, hot and humiliating and entirely new — something colder was assembling itself, piece by piece, with the terrible efficiency of her own trained mind.

He had known. He had known what she was from the beginning — not Eastwick, not the estate lawyer with the immaculate paper trail. He had chosen *her*. Out of every lawyer on the coast, out of every stranger he might have hired, he had chosen a woman with an erased past to execute a will built on a faked death. That was not chance. Edmund Voss had looked at Cyrus Eastwick and seen, with a dying man's terrible clarity, exactly what she was: another person the tide had given a new name. He had hired her because she was the only kind of person who would understand that a faked death could be an act of mercy — and the only kind of person ruthless enough to unmake it when mercy turned out to have a body count.

*Find out whose it was.* The body the tide had given up. The fixer, she had thought — the man who sold women their disappearances. Murdered to close accounts. And Edmund's last line, the one she kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: *the person who does know it is still alive, and still dangerous, and I am no longer in any position to protect you.*

Maren was alive. Cyrus had suspected it in the sea cave, had felt it like a hand closing around her wrist when she'd found the coat folded with care, the train ticket dated after the death, the note bearing a nickname no one living should have known. Now it was confirmed by the dead man's own hand — and with it, the rest of the arithmetic. If Maren was alive, then Maren had a reason to stay dead. And if someone had murdered the fixer to keep her dead, then that someone was still working, still hunting, and had just lost the one man who stood between them and the only living witness to the whole architecture of lies.

Cyrus stood at the window. The fog was lifting off the harbor, and down the coast, past the headland, she could see a thin thread of smoke rising from somewhere among the trees — ordinary, domestic, a chimney or a bonfire. She watched it for a long time.

Alistair Voss would arrive today. The son who was not close to his father. The son whose inheritance now hung on a will that named his father a murderer and demanded its own unmaking. Cyrus thought of the harbor clerk's worn-smooth sentences, the town's word-for-word grief, the records that contradicted themselves like witnesses coached too well — and she thought of paper, and how paper was the only thing in Greyharbour that told the truth, and what a man might do if the truth were kept in filing cabinets.

She packed her bag. She put the hatbox key — the cipher key, *under the winter gloves* — at the top of her list, and Maren Voss, alive and hunted, at the top of that.

Then she went downstairs, past the landlady's dozing cat, out into the brightening cold, and stopped.

Across the street, at the mouth of the lane that led up toward the old records office, stood a man she had never seen before, watching her rented window. He was not pretending otherwise. When their eyes met, he touched the brim of his hat — courteous, almost gentle — and turned, and walked away up the lane, toward the building where every false record in Greyharbour slept in its drawer.

The smell of smoke, when it came an hour later, would be the least surprising thing about that morning.
