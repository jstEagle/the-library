## 9. Corbin Ashe

The thing about parking garages is that they're designed by people who hate you.

I don't mean that as a metaphor. I mean the actual architects. Somebody sat at a drafting table and drew concrete pillars every forty feet so you can never see more than half a lot at once, then specified lighting fixtures that hum at a frequency just below annoyance and just above dread, and then — this is my favorite part — put the stairwell at the far end of the structure, past the blind corner, so that anyone walking to their car has to do it alone, in bad light, with their footsteps announcing them like a town crier.

I'd parked in the Halcyon Tower garage maybe six times since my visit to the Trust two weeks ago. I knew the layout. I knew which pillar cast the deepest shadow on level three. I told myself that was professional diligence, the same way I tell myself the third cup of coffee is hydration.

It was 11:40 on a Tuesday night, and I was walking to my car after an evening spent cross-referencing initials against obituaries, which is exactly as cheerful as it sounds, when a man stepped out from behind pillar C-14 and said, "Ms. Lockridge."

I didn't scream. Screaming is for people who haven't spent nine years listening to other people's worst moments through a headset. What I did was catalog: male, mid-forties, gray suit that cost more than my annual rent, hands visible and empty, posture relaxed in the way of someone who has never needed to be afraid in a parking garage because garages are afraid of *him*. And behind him, half in shadow near the ramp, a second shape that didn't move and didn't need to.

"Corbin Ashe," I said. "You're shorter than your reputation."

"You've heard a reputation?" He smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the kind that gets measured in millimeters before it's deployed. "I'd love to know what it says."

"That you fix things." I kept walking toward my car, slow enough to seem unhurried, fast enough to keep the distance I wanted. "Leaky roofs. Noisy neighbors. People who ask questions."

"And yet here you are, asking them anyway." He fell into step beside me, matching my pace without seeming to try, which is its own kind of message. "I admire that. Genuinely. The Trust admires it. Mr. Voss reads the clippings, you know. He keeps a file on everyone who writes about us. Yours is thin. That's not an accusation. It's an observation about how much room there is for it to stay thin."

My car was four spaces away. I made myself look at it instead of him. "If this is a recruitment pitch, I should warn you I already have a job. It pays terribly and the hours are worse, but the benefits include not going to prison."

"This isn't a pitch." He stopped, and I stopped too, because stopping was the move that said *I'm not afraid*, and I needed him to believe it even if the math in my chest said otherwise. He reached into his jacket — my pulse did something unprofessional — and came out with an envelope. Cream-colored. Heavy stock. The kind of envelope that holds checks or subpoenas, depending on which side of the ledger you're standing on. "This is a courtesy."

"I don't open courtesies in parking garages."

"Then let me describe it." He held it up between two fingers like a sommelier presenting a label. "It's a consulting fee. Retainer, technically. For services rendered and, more relevantly, services *not* rendered. There's a figure inside that would make your building manager stop leaving notes on your door. There's also a phone number. You call it if you ever find yourself holding paper that doesn't belong to you. Old paper. Burned paper. Paper belonging to dead women."

The garage hummed its low fluorescent hum. Somewhere above us a car door slammed, and we both waited out the echo like men waiting out artillery.

"Mara Lockridge," I said. "Say her name if you're going to stand here selling her."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt; I want to be clear about that. Recognition. The flicker of a man confirming a file against a face. "Mara," he repeated, politely, the way you'd repeat the name of a wine you had no intention of ordering. "Seven years ago. A tragedy. The fire investigation was thorough."

"The fire investigation was bought."

His smile didn't waver, but it changed temperature. "You've been reading old newsprint. Newsprint is a terrible source, Ms. Lockridge. It's written by tired people on deadline, and it stays wrong forever." He took a step closer, and I caught his cologne — cedar and something colder underneath, like money stored in a vault. "Here's what I actually came to say, and then I'll let you get home. The Trust has no interest in you. None. You're a dispatcher. You answer phones at night. Whatever hobby you've developed — the clippings, the questions, the little visits under pretexts — hobbies end. People get bored. People get sensible. We'd simply like to accelerate the sensible part, and pay you for the privilege."

"And if I'm not bored?"

"Then you're curious," he said, "and curiosity is a debt. Everyone who carries it eventually settles. The only variable is the currency." He set the envelope on the hood of my car, weighted it with one finger, and looked at me with something that in a kinder man might have been sympathy. "Take the envelope. Or leave it. But understand what leaving it means. It means you've been noticed. Not by me — I'm the friendly part. By the part that isn't friendly. There's a list, Ms. Lockridge. There's always a list. The question you should be asking yourself tonight, in your apartment, with your locks checked, is whether your name is on it in pencil or in ink."

"Which is it?"

He tilted his head, considering, and for one unguarded half-second he looked almost young, almost sorry. "That depends entirely on what you do with the next seventy-two hours."

* * *

He walked away. Of course he did. Men like Ashe don't chase; they issue invitations and let you walk into them at your own speed. I watched him pass the second man by the ramp — a nod passed between them, small as a decimal point — and then they were gone down the spiral, and I was alone with a cream envelope sitting on my hood like a bird that had landed and decided to stay.

I didn't touch it for a full minute. I stood there doing dispatcher breathing — four counts in, four counts hold, four counts out, the rhythm that keeps your voice level while someone tells you their child isn't breathing — and I made myself think it through like a call, because calls have structure and structure is where I live.

One: they knew my name, my car, my schedule, and my recent movements. That meant surveillance, which means time, which means I'd been watched since at least the Trust visit, probably longer. Since the harbor woman, maybe. Since the coat.

Two: the envelope was a test wearing a bribe's clothing. If I took it, I was purchasable, and purchasable people can be priced, and priced people can be owned. If I refused loudly, I was a problem, and problems go on lists. The only winning move was the one he'd left me — take it or leave it, both losing — unless there was a third option he hadn't priced.

Three: *seventy-two hours.* He'd given me a clock. Men like Ashe don't give clocks out of generosity. They give them because the deadline serves them, because something happens in seventy-two hours that they want me either bought or frightened for. Which meant whatever was moving was moving soon, and I had a window, narrow and lit, to move faster.

I picked up the envelope. Not because I was taking the deal — because evidence shouldn't be left on cars in garages, and because I wanted to see the figure. Vanity, maybe. Or reconnaissance. You learn things from numbers. The number inside was eleven thousand dollars, drawn on an account at Meridian Private Bank, and beneath it, typed on a slip with no letterhead, a single line:

*For discretion regarding matters concluded prior to your involvement.*

Prior to my involvement. As if I'd wandered late into a movie. As if Mara hadn't been my sister first, before she was their bookkeeper, before she was their problem, before she was ash and a closed box in my closet. *Prior.* They'd been erasing her for seven years and now they were erasing the timeline itself, tidying the record so thoroughly that even the crime was being reorganized around my absence from it.

I laughed. I couldn't help it — one short, ugly sound that bounced off the concrete and came back embarrassed. Eleven thousand dollars. For Mara they'd paid, what, settlements? Hush funds? The notebook's initials had run alongside figures in the tens of thousands, hundreds once. My sister's silence had been worth a house. Mine was worth a used sedan and a year of rent. Even in murder-adjacent accounting, I was a rounding error.

Good, I thought, turning the check over like a card sharp. Underestimate me. It's the only advantage I'll ever get.

* * *

I drove home the long way, doubled back twice, watched my mirrors out of habit I'd learned from three years of reading police scanners. Nobody followed me, or nobody competent, and I'd decided those were the same thing until proven otherwise.

My apartment looked the way it always looked: small, clean, deliberately anonymous. No photographs on the walls except Mara, and hers faced away from the window. No mail with my name visible through the slot. Nine years of dispatch work teaches you that visibility is a choice, and I had chosen invisibility the way other people choose religion — completely, daily, without thinking about it until someone made me.

I stood in my kitchen and laid the check on the counter and stared at it, and what I felt wasn't fear, or not only fear. It was the specific vertigo of a pattern completing. Three accidents solved from a distance, anonymously, safely — a hit-and-run in Delmore Park that wasn't random, a fall from a marina catwalk that wasn't drunk, a gas leak that wasn't faulty wiring. I'd sent my anonymous letters, seen the small tectonic shifts in the news coverage, and gone back to my headset feeling like a ghost with good aim. Untouched. Unlisted.

But the harbor woman had worn my sister's clothes out of my own closet, and now a man in a beautiful suit had said *list* to my face in a parking garage, and the truth rearranged itself with an almost audible click: I had never been invisible. Invisibility was a service they provided. They let me be unseen because unseen was harmless, and the moment I stopped being harmless, the curtain came down and there I was — lit, named, priced at eleven thousand dollars, standing on a list I'd been on since the night I pulled the threads of a seven-year-old fire.

They hadn't found me. They'd *allowed* me, and the allowance had expired.

I thought about calling Osei. I had her card in a drawer, and I'd been circling it for two weeks like a moth deciding whether glass counts as sky. She suspected me — I'd lied to her twice, smoothly, and she'd noted it with eyes that filed everything — and walking into that interview room again meant giving up the last of my anonymity for a cop whose captain, if the scanner chatter was anything to go by, wanted the harbor case cold and buried.

Seventy-two hours, Ashe said. Something was coming. And I kept coming back to the retired auditor, Walter Brandt — the last living name in Mara's notebook with a legible address, the one lead I hadn't burned through yet. I'd drafted him a letter three days ago. Careful, coded, nothing actionable. It was sitting in my bag, stamped, unsent, because some instinct had told me to wait.

Ashe had just told me why. Seventy-two hours. If they were cleaning house, Brandt was the dustiest surface in the room.

I took the letter out. Held it over the trash. Thought about the list, and ink, and pencils, and the difference between being safe and being spared — because those had turned out to be different words, hadn't they? Mara had been spared nothing, and she'd been careful too.

I mailed the letter. Then I called the number on the cream stationery — not to accept, but to leave a message on whatever line a fixer keeps for purchased silences, and I kept my dispatcher voice, flat and pleasant and impossible to read:

"Tell Mr. Ashe the retainer's declined. Tell him the matter he described isn't concluded. And tell him—" I paused, and heard my own heartbeat, steady as a shift clock. "Tell him I know what happened to the arson report from the Halcyon fire, and so does a retired auditor who kept copies."

It was a bluff. Mostly. Brandt had told me, in our one brief phone call, that he remembered *the inspector's arithmetic not adding up* — but copies, plural, retained? That was hope dressed as leverage.

The message light blinked off somewhere across the city, and I understood, standing in my kitchen with the receiver still warm, that I had just signed something. Not the check. The other document. The one with names on it, in ink.

Seventy-two hours. I intended to spend sixty-nine of them making sure that if my name stayed on that list, it stayed there next to a story too loud to bury quietly.

The phone rang at 3:12 a.m., an hour before my shift ended, and the caller ID read a number I didn't recognize — but the prefix was Halcyon Hills, and Walter Brandt's address was in Halcyon Hills, and my stomach dropped through nine floors of concrete before I even answered.
