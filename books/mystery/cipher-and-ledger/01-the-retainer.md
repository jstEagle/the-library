## 1. The Retainer

The rain had been coming down on Vesterbro for three days straight, and by Tuesday afternoon it had worked its way into the bookshop itself — a slow, patient drip from the skylight that landed, with what Lena Eriksen considered deliberate malice, on the one shelf of first editions she'd never managed to sell.

She moved the copper pot under the drip. The pot had been a plant's home once; the plant had died in 2019 and Lena had kept the pot because throwing things away felt like admitting the shop was shrinking, which it was, visibly, measurably, in the way that her accountant — no, not Marta, the other one, the boring one who sent invoices — kept reminding her.

"Page & Tide," said the sign above the door, in letters that had been gold in 2008. People assumed it was nautical. It was actually a pun on her own name — *Eriksen*, daughter of Erik, and Erik had been a fisherman before he was anything else — but explaining puns to customers was like explaining retirement to herself: technically possible, emotionally useless.

Lena was behind the counter pretending to read when the bell over the door rang at ten past four, and a woman came in shaking rain off an umbrella the color of a bruise.

She was perhaps fifty, dressed the way people dress when they want to look unremarkable and have paid good money for it: grey coat, grey scarf, sensible shoes. Her hair was pinned back with the severity of a woman who had once been told she looked soft and had taken corrective action. She stood just inside the door and did not browse, which Lena noted the way an old dog notes a stranger's gait.

"Closed?" the woman asked.

"Open," Lena said. "Barely."

"I'm looking for a book."

"We have those." Lena set down the novel she hadn't been reading. It was a western. She'd read forty pages and could not have named a single character. "What kind?"

The woman crossed the shop with the unhurried precision of someone who had rehearsed this walk, and stopped at the counter. Up close there was something wrong with her composure — not cracks, exactly, but the faint tremor of a surface holding weight it wasn't built for. A teacup filled exactly to the brim.

"You're Lena Eriksen," the woman said. Not a question. "You were with the police. Homicide. Twenty-two years."

"Twenty-three. And then I saw the light, which turned out to be a skylight that leaks."

"I need to hire you."

"Madam, I sell books. Badly, but I sell them. If you want a detective, there are firms downtown with actual offices and young men in nice suits who still remember how the databases work."

"They'd take the case," the woman said. "That's the problem."

Lena looked at her properly then. Rain on the shoulders of the grey coat. Hands clasped in front of her like a schoolgirl about to recite. Eyes that went, briefly, to the window — checking the street — before returning with the discipline of a metronome.

"All right," Lena said slowly. "You've got my attention, which is more than the skylight gets. Sit down. Coffee's terrible but it's hot."

* * *

The coffee was worse than advertised, and the woman — who gave her name as Marta Lindqvist, accountant, no firm named — wrapped both hands around the cup without drinking, as though the cup itself were the assignment.

"I want you to prevent a murder," she said.

Lena waited. In twenty-three years she had learned that the silence after a sentence like that belonged to the other person, and if you filled it, they'd let you.

"The murder hasn't happened yet," Marta added, unnecessarily.

"So I gathered. Preventing murders is generally my favorite kind of case. Who's the victim?"

"My employer."

"And the murderer?"

"I don't know yet."

Lena set down her own cup. "Mrs. Lindqvist—"

"Marta."

"Marta. You've come to a retired detective in a failing bookshop and asked her to stop a murder that hasn't happened, committed by someone unidentified, against your employer, whom you won't name. Do you understand how this sounds?"

"Like a waste of time," Marta said. There was a flicker of something almost human under the metronome — frustration, maybe, or fear wearing frustration's coat. "I know how it sounds. I've spent three weeks telling myself not to come here. I came anyway."

"Why here? Why me?"

"Because you closed the Kolding case. Because you don't talk to newspapers. And because—" Marta paused, and for a moment the teacup brimmed over; her hands shook, just slightly, around the cup. "Because everyone else would go through channels. Channels can be reached. You can't be reached. You're nobody now."

"That," Lena said, "is the nicest insult I've received all year."

"It wasn't meant as one."

"No," Lena agreed. "That's what made it nice."

Marta finally drank, grimaced at the coffee as if it confirmed something she'd suspected about the world, and set the cup down. Then she opened her bag — a leather satchel, older than her coat, scuffed at the corners — and took out an envelope. She placed it on the counter between them and slid it across with two fingers, the way people slide things across tables in films, except that her hand was steady and her eyes were not.

Lena didn't touch it. "How much?"

"Enough."

"That's not a number."

"Thirty thousand kroner. Now. Thirty more at the end of the month. And thirty more when it's done."

Lena did the arithmetic without meaning to. Ninety thousand. The shop's rent was four months late in spirit if not yet in fact; the boiler made a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something unpleasant; Theo, her assistant — a word she used loosely, since his primary duties appeared to be reading her stock and eating her biscuits — cost her nothing but cost her pride every time she caught herself feeding him.

Ninety thousand was not a fortune. Ninety thousand was a reprieve.

"What's the job, exactly?" she asked, keeping her voice flat, because she had already decided yes and hated how fast the decision had arrived, like a dog that comes when anyone whistles.

"My employer is Halvard Brandt," Marta said. "Shipping. You'll have heard the name."

Everyone had heard the name. Brandt Line containers sat stacked outside the harbor like a child's blocks arranged by a giant with a grudge. Halvard Brandt's face had been on the business pages for thirty years — silver-haired, square-jawed, the smile of a man who has never once been surprised by good news.

"On the thirtieth of next month," Marta continued, "there will be a memorial. His brother drowned thirty years ago. Same date. Same place — the family estate, the dock below the boathouse. Every year there's a gathering. This year—" She stopped.

"This year you believe someone means to kill him."

"I believe someone has been preparing to kill him. There's a difference. Preparation is quiet. Murder is loud. I've been listening to the quiet for months, Ms. Eriksen, and I am very good at listening. Numbers whisper, if you know how to hear them. Money has been moving in ways money shouldn't move. Small amounts. Careful amounts. The kind of amounts a person moves when they're buying something they can't put on an invoice."

"Like what?"

"Like rope. Like a boat repair that was never made. Like a second phone." Marta's voice stayed level, but her thumb pressed white against the side of the cup. "I can't prove any of it. That's why I need you. I need someone to stand between him and the date, and find out what I'm hearing before it stops being quiet."

Lena leaned back in her chair, which complained, as everything in the shop eventually did.

"Has he been told? Brandt?"

"No."

"He might want to know someone's planning to kill him."

"He wouldn't believe me. And if he believed me, he'd handle it himself, and his way of handling things makes bodies." She said it simply, without drama, the way you report weather. "He's not a good man, Ms. Eriksen. I've done his books for eleven years. Good men's books don't look like his."

"Then why save him?"

Marta looked at her then — really looked, the metronome slipping, and underneath it Lena saw something raw and old, grief with dust on it, grief that had been stored in an attic for decades.

"Because it isn't about him," Marta said quietly. "It's about the date. And the brother. And what happened on that dock thirty years ago that everyone agreed to forget." She stood, abruptly, as though the chair had become unbearable. "Take the envelope or don't. But if you don't, I'll find someone else, and they'll go through channels, and channels can be reached."

Lena looked at the envelope. Thick. The kind of thick that solves boilers.

She thought about saying no. She thought about it seriously, the way you think about exercise. She was sixty-one years old; she had retired because the job had taken a husband, most of her friendships, and a portion of her soul she preferred not to itemize; she had opened a bookshop because books ask nothing of you but attention, and even that you can ration. She had spent five years proving to herself that she could be a person who sold novels and argued with the boiler and did not, under any circumstances, get involved.

And beneath all of that, in the cellar where she kept the things she didn't look at, was the other truth: that she had not felt awake in five years. That the drip from the skylight and the unsold first editions and the westerns she couldn't finish were not peace. They were anesthesia. And somewhere under the anesthesia, some stubborn, unforgivable part of her had been waiting for a grey-coated woman to walk in out of the rain and say *prevent a murder* so that the part of her brain that had lain down like an old dog could get up again, tail wagging, ashamed of itself.

Prideful and broke, she thought. What a combination. It explained half the world's bad decisions.

"I'll take the case," she said. "On conditions. One: you tell me everything, and I mean everything — the moment you decide I can't handle a piece of information is the moment I fire my only client. Two: nobody dies. Not him, not whoever's planning it, not you. Three—" she tapped the envelope, "—this buys my discretion, not my obedience. If I find something you don't want found, that's between you and your conscience."

For the first time, Marta Lindqvist smiled. It was a small smile, quickly folded away, but it changed her face entirely — took ten years off and added a decade of sorrow back on, somehow, at the same time.

"Agreed," she said.

She wrote a number on the back of a receipt from the shop — Lena noticed it was for *Death in the Afternoon*, Hemingway, purchased eleven days ago, and filed that away without knowing why — and left it beside the envelope. A phone number. Nothing else.

"When do I meet him?" Lena asked. "Brandt."

"You don't. Not yet. He knows I've hired a consultant for the memorial. He thinks it's security." Marta paused at the door, hand on the frame, umbrella dripping. "Ms. Eriksen. One more thing. The brother who drowned — Anders. Whatever you find about him, however it looks — remember that the water keeps its secrets, but paper doesn't. Start with paper."

Then the bell rang, and she was gone into the rain, and the shop was quiet again, except for the drip, which landed neatly in the copper pot like a small round of applause.

Lena sat for a long moment. Then she picked up the envelope, weighed it in her hand — ninety thousand kroner's worth of trouble, give or take — and called out toward the back room, where a floorboard creaked with the studied innocence of a nineteen-year-old who had definitely been eavesdropping.

"Theo. Put the kettle on. And fetch me the box of old case notes from under the stairs."

A pause. Then, muffled: "We getting robbed?"

"Worse," Lena said, already reaching for the phone directory, feeling something in her chest turn over like an engine in cold weather — reluctant, rusty, and, God help her, starting. "We're getting curious."
