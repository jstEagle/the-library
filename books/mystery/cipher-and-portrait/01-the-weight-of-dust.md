## 1. The Weight of Dust

Isandro Crane woke at 5:47 a.m., as he did every morning, three minutes before the alarm he had set for 5:50 — a habit he considered less punctuality than courtesy to his own nervous system, which preferred to arrive at events slightly ahead of schedule.

The cathedral bell rang six while he shaved. He counted the strokes out of habit and was mildly disappointed when there were six, because a miscount would have given him something interesting to think about over breakfast. Breakfast was toast, tea, and the obituaries, which he read not out of morbid curiosity but professional interest: every death in St. Aldhelm's parish eventually produced paperwork, and paperwork, if you were lucky and patient, came to him.

He dressed in the dark, which required no skill after fifteen years. Charcoal trousers, the grey cardigan with the elbow that had been darned so many times it now resembled a small grey tumor, and last — always last, like a collar, like a vow — the chain.

The key hung from it. A single iron key, older than the town's paving stones, black with age except where his thumb had polished a bright crescent into the bow. It opened one door: the crypt door beneath the cathedral's north transept, the door through which no one passed without Isandro Crane knowing about it, because no one else had a key, and because Isandro Crane kept a log.

He kissed the key once, briefly, the way some men touch a saint's medal, then tucked it under his shirt where it lay against his sternum all day, cold at first and then warm, like a small sleeping animal made of obligation.

* * *

The archive occupied what had once been the cathedral's charnel house, which Isandro felt was fitting, since both institutions existed to keep the past from wandering off. Three rooms above the vestry, connected by a corridor that smelled of beeswax and paper and, faintly but permanently, of the lavender sachets Isandro replaced on the first of every month whether they needed replacing or not. (They never needed replacing. He replaced them anyway. Order, he had learned, is mostly the performance of order until the performance becomes true.)

His morning ritual took forty minutes and admitted no variation:

First, the windows. Opened two inches each, top sash only, to let the room breathe without letting in weather. The east window stuck; it had stuck since 2011, and Isandro had developed an entire relationship with it involving a specific angle of hip and shoulder that he performed now, wincing, muttering, "Yes, yes, I know."

Second, the temperature log. Sixteen degrees. He wrote it down. He had written it down every working morning for fifteen years, producing a continuous meteorological record of three rooms that no climatologist would ever want. He found this thought comforting rather than sad, in the way that he found most things comforting rather than sad, because the alternative framing rarely occurred to him until late at night.

Third, the ledger check. Not the baptismal register — that lived downstairs in the strong room, and we will come to it, though Isandro didn't know yet how much he would come to it — but his own accession ledger, the great green book in which every item in the collection was recorded by number, provenance, and condition. He ran his finger down yesterday's entries. Item 4,417: a funeral banner, silk, water-stained, donated by the Okafor-Lund family. Item 4,418: seventeen photographs of the 1978 flood, transferred from the newspaper office. Item 4,419: one tin of shortbread, anonymous donor, consumed at staff meeting.

There was no staff. There was Isandro. He had eaten the shortbread himself, standing at the window, and logged it honestly, because an archive that lies about shortbread will lie about anything.

"Good morning, Ambrose," he called down the stairwell, hearing the shuffle-and-tap of the sexton's broom on the flagstones below.

"Morning, Mr. Crane." Ambrose Pell — no relation, or rather, everyone insisted, no *known* relation, though the surname sat in the parish rolls going back to Josiah Pell himself, and Isandro had privately wondered about it for years the way he wondered about everything, quietly and without conclusion. Ambrose was eighty-one and had swept the cathedral floor since before Isandro was born, and their friendship consisted almost entirely of these morning calls and an unspoken agreement that neither of them would ever retire, because retirement was just death with better lighting.

"Anything interesting?" Ambrose asked, meaning: any gossip among the dead.

"Bishop Thorne's visitation records, 1889," Isandro said. "He complained about the choir."

"Don't blame him. I've heard the choir."

"He also complained that the verger was drunk during evensong."

"Thorne wasn't wrong there either." A pause, the broom whispering. "You eating lunch today, Mr. Crane?"

"I have the Hollis bequest to catalogue."

"That's what you said Tuesday."

"It was true Tuesday."

Ambrose's sigh drifted up the stairwell, carrying sixty years of watching thin men forget meals. "I'll bring you a roll."

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to. That's what makes it kind." And the broom moved on, and Isandro smiled at his ledger, and the morning proceeded exactly as mornings should.

* * *

The Hollis bequest turned out to be four crates of Victorian ephemera: playbills, ration cards, a child's copybook with a terrifying drawing of the vicar, and — Isandro's heart actually lifted at this, a small warm hydraulic event behind his ribs — a complete run of the parish magazine, 1874 to 1902, missing only two issues.

Missing issues were the closest thing Isandro had to a love affair. He spent the better part of the morning cross-referencing the gaps against the diocesan record office's holdings, composing in his head the letter he would write requesting duplicates, revising the salutation four times (*Dear Dr. Fenwick* too familiar; *Dear Sir* too cold; *Dear Dr. Fenwick, I hope this finds you well* — well, obviously it hoped that, why else write). This was loneliness, though Isandro would have denied the word with genuine puzzlement. He was not lonely. He was *in correspondence.*

At eleven, Maren Vasse-Holt telephoned.

"The portrait committee wants to move the unveiling to the fourteenth," she said, without hello. Maren did not believe in hellos; she believed conversations should begin where they meant to go. "Which gives me nine days instead of twelve to finish cleaning Edmund Vasse, and I wanted to warn you that I'll need the original consignment papers. The ones from 1923."

"Consignment papers," Isandro repeated, already reaching for the index. "Portrait, oil, founder, Vasse, Edmund — here. Deposited by your grandfather, 1923, condition noted as 'surface grime, varnish yellowing, frame sound.' Signed receipt in the second drawer of the flat file."

"You're reading that to me right now, aren't you."

"I'm telling you where it is."

"You're reading it to me. You have it in your hand." A pause, and then, in a different voice — lighter, almost shy, the voice she used perhaps twice a year — "Has anyone ever told you that talking to you is like consulting a very polite oracle?"

"Frequently," said Isandro, who had never been told any such thing. "The papers will be ready by Thursday. How is His Foundership looking?"

"Yellow. Underneath the yellow, possibly interesting. Underneath the *possibly interesting*, I'll let you know." She rang off, and Isandro sat for a moment with the receiver still in his hand, experiencing the peculiar afterglow of a conversation that had gone well, which for Isandro meant: had gone at all.

* * *

Lunch was the roll, delivered up the stairs by a hand attached to Ambrose, along with the observation that "the centennial lot were measuring the crypt steps again."

Isandro looked up from his tea. "Measuring them?"

"With a tape. Little fellow with a clipboard. Said they needed the dimensions for the — what did he call it — the *proceedings*. For the reburial." Ambrose's broom paused. "That's still happening, then. Digging him up."

"The centennial committee voted in March. Exhumation, ceremony, reinterment in a new coffin. It's all quite public, Ambrose. There were minutes."

"There are always minutes," said Ambrose, in the tone of a man who had outlived enough committees to distrust anything that produced them. "Doesn't mean anyone reads them." He tapped his broom handle twice on the stone, which was either a farewell or a superstition, and shuffled off toward the nave.

Isandro finished his roll and did not think about the exhumation. He was very good at not thinking about things; it was the shadow discipline that accompanied his talent for thinking about everything else. He catalogued the copybook. He shelved the playbills. He updated the environmental log — sixteen degrees, holding steady, the world obedient — and at half past two he went down to the strong room to fetch the baptismal register for a routine condition check, because routine was the architecture of his happiness and he inspected its pillars daily.

The strong room was below the vestry, one flight down from the archive and one landing across from the crypt door. He passed the iron door as he always did, and as he always did, he touched the key through his shirt — a gesture so automatic that a visitor once asked if he was checking his pulse. In a sense, he was.

The register lay in its acid-free box on the second shelf. Isandro carried it to the reading table, put on white cotton gloves, and opened it with the tenderness other men reserved for infants and first editions.

*Baptisms, Parish of St. Aldhelm, 1861–1931.* The ink browns and blacks of four generations of curates. He checked the binding, the sewing stations, the board corners. All sound. All correct. He began turning pages toward the 1900s, because the turn-of-century quires showed the most use and therefore warranted the most attention, and he noted — idly, professionally, with no alarm whatsoever, not yet — that the margins around the 1909 entries were unusually dense with small penciled numerals, tiny figures in a cramped hand, clustered like birds on a wire.

Old ledgers accumulated marginalia the way old coats accumulated lint. Someone had probably been tallying something. Collections, perhaps. Candle accounts.

He photographed nothing. He suspected nothing. He closed the box, returned it to the shelf, climbed the stairs, touched the key, and went back up to his sixteen degrees and his green ledger and his life, which had exactly the shape he had built for it, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall.

* * *

The letter arrived with the four o'clock post, mixed in with a diocesan circular and a flyer for a roof-appeal concert.

It was on cream stock, embossed with the centennial committee's seal — the Vasse coat of arms, a ship above three wheat sheaves — and signed by the chairman, Councilman Aldous Merrick, whose handwriting was as handsome and confident as the man himself. Isandro had known Aldous since boyhood; the councilman had been the one, years ago, who'd fought the budget committee to keep the archivist position funded, who'd said in open session, "A town that loses its records loses its reflection," a sentence Isandro had written on a card and pinned above his desk, where it still hung.

*Dear Mr. Crane,* the letter began.

*The Centennial Committee confirms that the exhumation of Edmund Vasse, founder of our town, is scheduled for Friday next, the ninth, at ten o'clock in the morning, at the parish cemetery, pursuant to the faculty granted by the diocese. The remains will be received at the cathedral following the ceremony and prepared for reinterment in the new memorial vault.*

*As keeper of the crypt, you are requested to attend the reception of the remains and to witness the sealing of the new coffin. Your long stewardship of the founder's resting place is, of course, known and valued by us all.*

*With respect and esteem,*
*Aldous Merrick, Chairman*

Isandro read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way one rereads a sentence that has done something grammatically impossible.

There was nothing strange in it. That was the thing he would remember afterward, in the bad weeks that followed, when he would take the letter out and read it again hunting for the warning he must have missed: there was *nothing strange in it.* Courteous, precise, warm even. *Known and valued by us all.*

And yet he stood in the middle of his archive — his ordered, breathing, lavender-scented kingdom — with the paper trembling very slightly in his hand, feeling the way a man feels when the floor of a familiar room makes a sound it has never made before. Not a crack. Just a creak. Rooms creak. Old buildings settle.

But Isandro Crane had spent fifteen years learning the difference between a building settling and a building failing, and somewhere beneath the sternum, underneath the warm weight of the iron key, something in him went quiet and attentive, the way the whole house goes quiet when you hear, upstairs, a door close that should not have been open.

He filed the letter — Centennial Committee, Correspondence Received, 2010, folder seven — because filing was what he did with the world, and the world could be borne if it was filed.

Then he stood at the east window, the one that stuck, and watched the light go gold over the cemetery across the green, where on Friday morning half the town would gather to watch them open the grave of Edmund Vasse, founder, benefactor, the man whose portrait hung in the nave and whose name was on the school and the mill race and the oldest street sign, the man who had lain in consecrated ground for a hundred years.

*One hundred years,* Isandro thought. *What could possibly be left to disturb?*

He did not have an answer. But he noticed — and this too he would remember, this small cold fact arriving ahead of all the others — that his hand had gone, without his permission, to the key at his chest, and was holding it, hard, as if it had asked him a question.

Below, faint through the floor, he heard Ambrose's broom stop sweeping.
