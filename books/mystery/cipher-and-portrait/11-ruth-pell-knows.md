## 11. Ruth Pell Knows

The thing nobody tells you about dying people is how much they talk. Not to you, necessarily — mostly past you, into the middle distance, as though the air itself had been appointed their confessor. Ruth Pell had learned this over eleven years of night nursing, and she had learned it especially well in the four months since Ada Merrick had come home from the hospital for the last time, installed in the big front bedroom of the Merrick house on Wexler Street like a queen taking up residence in a besieged castle.

"More laudanum," Ada said, which was her joke. She'd been saying it since 1974. It meant: turn me.

Ruth turned her, fluffed the pillow, checked the morphine pump out of habit she didn't need. "You're a terrible addict, Mrs. Merrick. You keep refusing your doses."

"I refuse nothing but sentimentality." Ada's eyes were still sharp, even now, even at ninety-six, even with the whole machinery of her winding down. She fixed Ruth with the look that had made three generations of Merricks sit up straighter at dinner. "You have ink on your ear."

Ruth touched her ear. There was, indeed, ink on her ear. She'd been doing the crossword in the kitchen at four a.m., as she did every shift, and apparently she'd scratched her head while puzzling over seven across. *Ephemeral.* Which felt apt.

"Crossword," she said.

"Mm." Ada closed her eyes. Then, drifting: "They never found him, you know. The boy in the river."

Ruth's hands went still on the blanket.

"They said they found him," Ada went on, in the flat, conversational tone of someone commenting on weather or hydrangeas. "But they found what they were looking for. That's not the same thing. Never is."

"Mrs. Merrick—"

"Hush. I'm talking to my mother." And she was gone again, back behind her eyes, murmuring something about January and envelopes and *don't let them count it in front of the girl*, which meant nothing to Ruth, or should have meant nothing, except that Ruth Pell had grown up in a house where January was spoken of the way other families spoke of funerals.

She finished the crossword at dawn. Seven across was *ephemeral*. Eight down was *legacy*.

* * *

Ruth Pell was thirty-four years old and the last of her name, which was a sentence she could say lightly now, the way you can say anything after enough repetitions. The Pells of Harrow's Bend had once been a founding family — third on the list, after Vasse and Holt, before Merrick and Okafor-Lund — and then, according to every history book in the county library, they had simply *ended*, a line extinguished sometime around 1910, leaving behind only a street name (Pell Row, which everyone assumed was named for a tree) and a plot in the cemetery with a stone so worn you had to take its word for it.

What the history books did not record was that the line hadn't ended. It had just gotten very, very quiet.

Her grandmother had explained it to her when Ruth was nine, sitting her down at the kitchen table with the seriousness usually reserved for talks about death or money, which — her grandmother would have said — was exactly what this was.

"Every January," Grandma Odell said, "an envelope comes. No stamp. No return address. Hand-delivered, always before sunrise on the second, always in the same hand. You will not open it in front of anyone. You will not tell anyone it came. You will put it in the tin, and when there are enough envelopes in the tin, you will do what your mother did and her mother did and her mother before her."

"What's in them?"

"Money. And a note." Her grandmother's mouth did something complicated. "The notes stopped being legible around 1950. Before that, they said the same thing every year. One sentence."

Ruth waited. Her grandmother looked out the window at the gray January light, and when she finally spoke, it was in a voice Ruth had never heard her use before or since — a voice with all the blood drained out of it.

"*For Josiah, who we wronged, and for you, who we keep.*"

"And what do we do with the money?"

"We keep it until we can't keep it anymore, and then we give it away. Quietly. To people who need it more than we need to be comfortable." Her grandmother took Ruth's hands across the table. Her skin was papery and warm. "You understand, child, that this is not a gift. Gifts are free. This is rent. We pay rent on a grave that isn't ours, in a town that isn't ours, for a crime we didn't commit. And one day you'll want to stop paying. Everyone wants to stop paying. Your great-aunt Cora wanted to stop paying, and she wrote a letter to the newspaper, and—" Her grandmother stopped. Started again, quieter. "Cora moved to Duluth very suddenly in 1961, and the letters followed her there, and she understood, and she came home, and she never mentioned it again. Do you understand?"

Nine-year-old Ruth had not understood, not really. But she had nodded, because her grandmother's hands were shaking, and because some part of her — the part that would grow up to be a night nurse, the part that could read a chart and know the truth of a person before the doctors did — already understood that fear passed down through generations doesn't fade. It just changes hands.

Thirty-four years later, Ruth had a biscuit tin full of envelopes in the bottom of her closet, under the winter sweaters. Twenty-five of them. One per year since her grandmother died. She gave the money away each spring — quietly, to the food bank, to the school lunch fund, once memorably to a family whose house burned — and kept the notes, because somebody had to keep the notes, because if nobody kept them, then the whole century of keeping had been for nothing.

And every January second, she woke before sunrise without an alarm, lay in the dark listening for footsteps on the porch, and hated herself a little for how carefully she listened.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Ada said that evening.

Ruth blinked. "What face?"

"The face you make when you're thinking about the tin." Ada was propped up against her pillows, and the sunset through the west window turned her white hair to brass. She'd refused the hospital bed in favor of her own four-poster, refused the hospice nurse in favor of Ruth, refused everything except, eventually, the morphine, and even that grudgingly, like a woman accepting terms from an enemy. "Don't look scandalized. I'm ninety-six, not senile. There's a difference, whatever my son says."

"Your son," Ruth said carefully, "is a councilman."

"My son," said Ada, "is Aldous." She said it the way other people said *my migraines* or *my bunions*: with weary ownership. "He visits on Sundays and tells me the town is fine. He's been telling me the town is fine since October, and he's begun repeating himself, and Aldous has never repeated himself in his life unless he was frightened. So." She turned her head on the pillow with effort. "Tell me what the archivist has been finding."

Ruth kept her face very still. "I don't know what you mean."

"Child." Ada sighed. "I have been dying for four months, which leaves a great deal of time for listening. My son takes phone calls in the driveway because he thinks I can't hear through the window. The Vasse girl has been asking questions at the historical society. And last week Sheriff Marsh requested the parish records, and Father Anselm called *Aldous* about it, which he should not have done, and Aldous called someone else, and that someone else's voice I did not recognize, and I have known every voice in this town for ninety years." Her eyes, sunken and bright, held Ruth's. "So. What has he found?"

Ruth thought about lying. She was good at it — eleven years of telling patients cheerful untruths about their prognoses had honed the skill — but lying to Ada Merrick felt like lying to a lighthouse. Besides, Ruth had come here tonight with something in her pocket, and pockets get heavy.

"He found the ledger," she said. "The baptismal register. The dates are wrong — baptisms recorded before births, going back to 1909. He thinks it's a cipher."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a car went by, and somewhere a dog offered its opinion of the car.

"Well," Ada said at last. "Well. A hundred and fifteen years, and it's a *ledger man* who finds it. Not a sheriff, not a priest, not one of us. An archivist." Something moved across her face that might have been relief, or might have been its opposite. "Does he know about the payments?"

"I don't think so. Not yet."

"But he'll get there. Ledger men always get there; that's the whole trouble with them." Ada's hand crept out from under the blanket, and Ruth took it. The grip was still surprisingly strong. "Listen to me, Ruth Pell. Listen, because I may not be lucid tomorrow, and tomorrow I intend to say things I've been saving, and I need to know you'll have heard today's things first."

"I'm listening."

"The boy in the river was real. His name was Josiah Pell, and he was forty-one years old, and he was murdered by Edmund Vasse in March of 1909, and his body was put in a coffin that was never buried, and Edmund Vasse's body was put in a coffin that was never filled, and this town has been standing on top of those two lies ever since like a table standing on two uneven legs." The words came out level, rehearsed, as though Ada had been saying them to herself in the dark for decades. "My grandfather helped carry the coffin. He told my father. My father told me. We are custodians, all of us, whether we wish to be or not."

"Second coffin," Ruth said. The words arrived in her mouth from somewhere — from Ada's murmuring, maybe, weeks ago, words she'd filed away without knowing why. "You said 'the second coffin.' Twice."

"Did I." Ada smiled, and it was a terrible smile, fond and broken at once. "Then I'm further along than I thought. Yes. The second coffin. Ask your archivist to look beneath the flagstones, and God help him when he does, because the seal won't be intact. It hasn't been intact since—" She stopped. Her breath caught, and for a moment Ruth reached for the pump, and Ada waved her off with two fingers. "Since autumn. Since Ambrose died."

"Ambrose Fell? The sexton?"

"The sexton knew too much and said too little, which is the safest combination in this town, and someone decided otherwise." Ada's eyes closed. Opened again, fierce. "Ruth. The envelope. This January. Did it come?"

Ruth's stomach dropped. "It's November."

"I know what month it is. Did it *come*?"

"No. Of course not. It comes in January. It has always come in January."

"Has it." Ada turned her face toward the window, where the last light was going copper. "Mine came early this year. October the ninth. Three days after Ambrose fell down stairs he had climbed ten thousand times." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "The note inside wasn't the old sentence, Ruth. It was new handwriting. And it said: '*Last payment. Keep the covenant. A.M.*'"

The room was very quiet. Downstairs, a clock ticked with the patience of furniture.

"That's your initials," Ruth said stupidly.

"It is also," said Ada, "the initials of the first Aldous Merrick, who carried the lantern the night they moved the coffin. It is a signature pretending to be a tradition. Whoever wrote that note wanted me to think the covenant was ending on its own terms, gently, the way it began." Her hand tightened on Ruth's. "Covenants don't end gently. They end the way Ambrose ended. And my son has been in my driveway making phone calls, and the Vasse girl is asking questions, and a ledger man is reading numbers, and you are the last Pell, child, which makes you the most dangerous person in this town, because you are the only one with nothing left to protect except the truth."

Ruth sat with that. The morphine pump clicked softly, metronomic, counting down something.

"Why are you telling me?" she asked finally. "You're a Merrick. The covenant protected your family. It built your family."

Ada laughed — a dry, rusted sound, genuine despite everything. "Protected us? Child, the covenant *imprisoned* us. I have spent ninety-six years being polite to people I despised and grateful to a dead man I never met, and I have watched my son grow up learning to smile the way wardens smile, and I am dying, and I find that I would like, just once, before the end, to say a true thing out loud in front of a witness." She drew a breath that rattled. "Also, I liked your grandmother. Odell Pell made the best gooseberry jam in three counties and never once pretended otherwise. If the covenant had to exist, it should at least have had better jam in it."

Despite herself — despite the cold walking up her spine — Ruth laughed too.

* * *

Isandro Crane came to the care home the next afternoon, which was not where Ruth had expected to find him, and not looking the way she'd expected either. She'd seen him around town for years — the archivist, the key man, the fellow with the chain and the careful hands — and he had always seemed to her like a man built entirely of order, a filing cabinet that had learned to walk. Today he looked like a cabinet that had been through a flood. There was a bandage above his ear she hadn't seen before, and his coat was missing a button, and he stood in the lobby of Wrenwood Care holding his hat like a man who'd forgotten hats had brims.

"Ms. Pell." He said her surname the way he probably said *folio* and *provenance*: with respect, and a certain wariness. "Sheriff Marsh suggested — that is, she thought you might —"

"She told you to talk to me." Ruth had spoken to Delia Marsh two nights ago, off the record, in the care home parking lot, and had said more than she'd planned to, because Ada's words had loosened something in her that a hundred Januaries had kept shut. "Delia doesn't waste words. If she sent you, she thinks I'm ready."

"Are you?"

Ruth looked at him. He looked back. He had tired, honest eyes, the eyes of a man who had spent fifteen years alone with paper and had recently discovered the paper was armed.

"Not even slightly," she said. "Come on. There's a residents' lounge with a fire. The good cookies are in the tin on the mantel, and Mrs. Okafor-Lund's grandson works Tuesdays, so no one will bother us. If we're going to burn down a hundred and fifteen years of silence, we may as well be comfortable."

The fire was lit and the lounge empty. Ruth got them tea in the good cups — the ones without chips — and sat him down by the window, and then, because there was no gentle way into it, she simply opened her bag and set the biscuit tin on the table between them.

Isandro looked at the tin. Looked at her. "Cookies?"

"Envelopes." She lifted the lid. Twenty-five of them, yellowed and browned and soft-edged with age, packed in rows like specimens. "One for every year since my grandmother died. And before that, a hundred more in my grandmother's attic, and before that, her mother's, and hers. Every January second, before dawn, no stamp, same hand. Money inside. And a note."

She handed him the oldest one in the tin. He opened it with the delicacy of a man handling a first edition, and read aloud, barely breathing the words:

"*For Josiah, who we wronged, and for you, who we keep.*"

"That's the old sentence," Ruth said. "Grandma said it was on every note until about 1950. After that, just initials. Different initials, rotating. V.H. E.M. M.O.L. Always the same five sets, cycling through the decades." She watched Isandro's face do what scholar faces do — the quick flicker, the recalculation. "You recognize them."

"Vasse-Holt," he said slowly. "Edmund Merrick. Merrick-Okafor-Lund." He looked up. "The custodians. It's a rotation. The office passes between the families."

"One envelope a year, for a hundred and fifteen years. Rent on a grave." Ruth wrapped her hands around her teacup. "Here's the part I need you to understand, Mr. Crane, because everyone always gets it wrong, and I'm tired of being wrong at. My family didn't kill Josiah Pell. My family *was* Josiah Pell. We're his descendants — his daughter's line, the daughter nobody counted because she was married off to a farmer in 1908 and changed her name. The payments weren't buying our silence about a murder we committed. They were buying our silence about a murder committed *against* us. Hush money for grief. We've spent a century mourning a man in secret while the town that erased him put his murderer's statue in the square."

Isandro had gone very still. In him, Ruth was learning, stillness meant the machinery was running fast.

"The ledger," he said. "The first entry. The recipient line. I couldn't read it — the cipher degraded. But I got the sum, and I got the annotation." He closed his eyes briefly. "*E.V., being alive.* I thought it meant Vasse was alive, receiving. But if the payments flowed outward — if the first payment went to *you* —"

"Then the ledger isn't a record of bribes paid," Ruth said. "It's a record of penance distributed. And the first line means Edmund Vasse was alive in 1909, writing checks to the family of the man he killed." She set down her cup. "There's more. Ada Merrick told me things last night that I think she's been rehearsing since before I was born. About a second coffin under the crypt flagstones. About Ambrose. And about a note she received in October — early, wrong month, new handwriting — signed *A.M.*, saying the payments were finished."

"Finished." Isandro's voice was flat. "Or *ended*."

"That's what I said. She said covenants don't end gently." Ruth reached into the tin one more time and brought out the newest envelope — October's, which Ada had pressed into her hands that morning with fingers like birds' claws, saying *you keep it, you're the keeper now, God forgive me for the delay*. "Read it yourself."

He read it. She watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"This is Merrick's hand," he said. "Councilman Merrick. I've catalogued enough of his correspondence to know it — he writes to the archive every quarter about the centennial fund." He looked up at her, and there was something raw in his face now, something that had been there since the empty coffin, since the torn log pages, since the broom laid across the shelf like a warning. "Ruth. When did Ada receive this?"

"October ninth."

"Ambrose died October sixth." He set the envelope down on the table with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off. "Three days. The payments stopped three days after the sexton fell down the stairs." He was quiet a moment, and then he said, in a different voice, smaller: "He mentored me. Merrick. When I took the archive post, he — he found funding for the climate system. He remembered my birthday. I thought he was the best man in this town."

"I know," Ruth said gently. "Ada knows. That's rather the point of what she's doing. She's not confessing to save herself; she's confessing to break her son's alibi before he builds it any higher." She leaned forward. "Mr. Crane — Isandro. There's one more thing, and then you should go, because I've been a nurse long enough to know when a conversation has become a countdown. Ada wants to give a statement. Full testimony, on the record, before she loses the thread. She's lucid in the mornings, between eight and ten, and she says — her words — '*bring the sheriff, bring the recorder, and bring that poor ledger man, and lock the door behind you, because what I have to say names names.*'" Ruth paused. "She also said to tell you one more thing. She said: '*Tell him to check the flagstones tonight, before Merrick remembers they exist.*'"

Outside the lounge window, the November dusk was coming down early, blue and cold. Somewhere in the building a call bell rang, and a cart rattled, and the ordinary world went on with its ordinary noises.

Isandro stood. He picked up his hat, and then he picked up the envelope instead, and looked at Ruth with an expression she recognized from mirrors — the look of a person deciding, once and finally, which side of a door they were going to stand on.

"Tonight," he said. "Before midnight. Will you come with me? If there's a second coffin under that crypt, then the last Pell should be the one to open it."

Ruth looked at the tin, at twenty-five envelopes and a century of rent, and heard her grandmother's voice saying *we keep, we keep, we keep*.

"Yes," she said. "But Isandro — if we open that floor, we'd better be prepared for whoever else has been opening it. Because Ada's right about one thing." She slid the October envelope back into the tin and closed the lid with a small, final click. "That seal was cut recently. And whoever cut it will not be happy to find us standing in their work."
