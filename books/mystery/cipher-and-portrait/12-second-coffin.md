## 12. Second Coffin

Isandro Crane had spent fifteen years learning every sound the crypt made, and none of them had ever included a heartbeat that wasn't his own.

He stood at the top of the seven steps at half past eleven on a Tuesday night, holding a lantern in one hand and Ada Merrick's fragment in the other — three words scrawled on the back of a 1962 funeral card in a hand gone loose and wandering: *under the third stone, the second coffin waits.*

Ruth Pell had pressed it into his palm that afternoon like contraband. "She said it twice," Ruth had told him, her nurse's voice steady and her eyes anything but. "Once to me, once to the wall. I don't think she knew the difference anymore."

Now the words sat in his hand and refused to mean anything sensible. The crypt floor was flagstone, laid in 1911 when the Vasse memorial wing was consecrated. Forty-one stones in the main aisle. He had counted them more times than he would ever admit aloud, because counting was what he did instead of sleeping.

"Third stone," Maren said behind him, her voice bouncing off the vaulted ceiling. She came down the steps carrying her own lantern and a canvas tool roll she'd borrowed from her studio. "You're sure we shouldn't wait for daylight? Or for, say, the sheriff?"

"The sheriff thinks I moved a body," Isandro said. "I'd rather not give her a second one to blame me for."

"Fair."

They stood together in the aisle, two people who had each spent their lives alone among old things and were only now discovering how loud another person's breathing could be.

The third stone from the entrance was unremarkable — same gray limestone as its neighbors, worn concave by a century of mourners' feet. Isandro knelt beside it and ran his fingers along the edges, feeling for what he could not have named until he felt it: a seam too clean. Lichen grew up to one edge of the stone and stopped, as if the stone had been lifted and reset within living memory.

"Maren. Your knife."

"You said this wasn't going to involve knives."

"It involves prying. The knife is incidental."

She handed it over with the expression of a woman recalculating an alliance. He worked the blade into the seam, and the stone came up easier than any stone laid in 1911 had any right to — a whisper of grit, a breath of cold air from below, air that smelled not of grave-dust but of *recently disturbed earth*, which is a smell Isandro hoped never to become familiar with and recognized immediately anyway.

Below the stone: not earth. A lip of poured concrete, and set into it an iron ring, and hinged to the ring a flat slab with a lead seal stamped across its closure.

The seal showed a ship and a sheaf of wheat. The Vasse arms.

"Isandro," Maren said quietly. "That's my family's seal. That's the seal from my great-grandfather's will."

"I know."

"No, you don't understand. I've *held* that will. There's a wax impression of exactly this in the county records. This is a formal seal. This isn't someone hiding something — this is someone *burying* something, officially, with ceremony." Her voice did something complicated in the middle of the sentence. "This is a family act."

Isandro looked at the seal for a long moment. Then he took out his phone, photographed it from four angles, and noted the time in the small waterproof notebook he carried everywhere, because a man who kept logs of everything eventually learned that the log was the only witness that never lied.

Except, he thought, remembering the torn pages, when someone taught it to.

"We should document before we open," he said. "Whatever's down there, once we lift this, we're part of its history. I'd rather be the part that wrote things down."

Maren crouched beside him, lantern held low, and for a moment neither of them moved. The crypt ticked and settled around them the way old buildings do, patient as a sleeping animal.

"You're afraid," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm an archivist. Fear is just data arriving early." He fitted his fingers under the slab's edge. "On three."

* * *

Maren Vasse-Holt had grown up on stories of Edmund Vasse the way other children grew up on bedtime prayers: nightly, reverently, and without much thought about what the words actually meant. Great-grandfather the Founder. Great-grandfather whose portrait hung in the cathedral narthex, whose name was on the school and the mill race and the good china. She had restored that portrait with her own hands, had lain her palms flat against his painted cheekbone while solvent lifted a century of varnish, and somewhere in those long quiet hours she had begun to feel she knew him — the set of his jaw, the ambition in the eyes, the small human vanity of the slightly-too-thick hair.

She had not expected to meet him tonight at the bottom of a hole.

The slab came up on hinges, counterweighted, engineered — someone had built this to be opened more than once — and below it a brick-lined shaft descended into a chamber barely large enough for two. Isandro went first because he was the kind of man who went first into holes, which Maren was beginning to suspect was either courage or a complete absence of imagination, possibly both.

The chamber held two things.

The first was a coffin. Not the grand lead-lined monument that had been exhumed to public horror three weeks ago, but a plain pine box, unadorned, its lid screwed down with the haste or humility of a poor man's burial. A brass plate, green with age, read simply: *J.P.*

"Oh," Maren said, and then couldn't say anything else, because her body had understood before her mind caught up. Josiah Pell. The partner. The name from the ledger cipher, the name from her grandfather's diary — *our covenant* — the name that had been paid for, annually, in secret sums, for a hundred and fifteen years.

"He's been here the whole time," she whispered. "Under the founder's memorial. Under the *pews*. Every Sunday of my childhood I sat ten feet above him."

Beside the coffin, arranged on a low shelf cut into the brick, lay a collection of objects that made no sense together and perfect sense together all at once: a pocket watch stopped at 4:17; a signet ring; a folded frock coat wrapped in oilcloth; a leather portfolio swollen with damp; and on top of the portfolio, a letter sealed with the same ship-and-wheat stamp, addressed in a fine copperplate hand:

*To the Custodian, whosoever holds the office. To be read only upon discovery.*

"Custodian," Isandro repeated. The word came out of him carefully, like something that might go off.

"That's the word from Grandfather's diary," Maren said. "*Our covenant.* I thought it meant the business partnership. The foundry shares, the land deal." She reached for the letter and stopped, her fingers an inch from the paper. "Isandro. If this is what I think it is, reading it doesn't just tell us a secret. It makes us part of keeping it. Or part of ending it. There's no version where we walk away the same."

He didn't answer right away. In the lantern light his face did something she'd never seen it do in six years of cautious professional acquaintance: it looked young. Frightened and young and stubborn all at once, a boy who had organized his entire life into shelves so nothing could ever be taken from him again, standing in front of a box that proved everything could be.

"We photograph everything first," he said finally. "Then we read it. And then—" He paused. "And then I think we've already decided, Maren. We climbed down here, didn't we?"

She almost smiled. Almost. "We did."

She lifted the letter free.

And that was when Isandro's lantern, sweeping back across the chamber floor on its way up, caught what it had missed on the way down.

"Stop," he said.

"What?"

"Look at the seal. The slab seal." He climbed halfway out and crouched over the iron ring, holding the light close. The lead seal — the one they had photographed, the one stamped with the Vasse arms — was not whole. It had been cut, cleanly, with a sharp blade, and then pressed closed again. Lead remembers. The scar of the cut ran through the wheat sheaf like a hairline fracture.

And along the fresh edge of the cut, the metal was bright.

Not tarnished. Not green. Bright, the way lead looks for days, maybe weeks, after it's been opened.

"Someone's been down here," Isandro said. His voice had gone very quiet, the voice he used in the archive when he found a book shelved wrong — except now the misfiled thing was a century-old conspiracy. "Recently. Weeks, not years. They cut the seal, went down, and sealed it again."

Maren felt the chamber get smaller around her. "The confession letter. If they read it—"

"Then they know what we know." Isandro was already climbing out, moving fast, checking the surrounding flagstones with the lamp held low. And there — a scuff in the dust along the aisle, a heel mark pointing toward the stairs, and on the seventh step, catching the light like a small dull star: a button. Brass, military-shank, snapped off at the thread.

He picked it up and turned it over. Engraved on the face, tiny and elegant: an *M* inside a wreath.

Maren stared at it, and the warmth drained out of the crypt, all of it, every degree.

"My father wore buttons like that," she heard herself say. "On his good coat. Aldous gave them to him — matching sets, he always joked about it. *Civic uniform,* he called it."

Above them, far off through the cathedral's stone bones, a door closed. Softly. The way a careful person closes a door.

Isandro put the button in his breast pocket, next to his heart, next to the key on its chain, and blew out neither lantern.

"Out," he whispered. "Slowly. Don't run."

They climbed toward the dark of the stairwell, toward the sound of nothing at all — which was somehow worse than footsteps — and Maren understood, with terrible clarity, that the letter in her hands was no longer a discovery.

It was a countdown.
