## 1. The Drowned Man Wears My Coat

The herons knew before I did.

I was halfway across the millpond footbridge when I saw them lift off in a single gray sheet, the way they do when something disturbs the water that isn't water. Thirty years of living in Harrow's Bend teaches you the difference between a fish breaking surface and something else rising—something slow, something patient, something that has been down there long enough to stop being in a hurry.

I stopped at the rail. The mist hadn't burned off yet, and the pond lay flat as poured glass, except for one place near the old sluice gate where the current still remembered how to move. That's where the coat was.

That's where *my* coat was.

The charcoal wool, the one with the torn silk lining I'd been meaning to fix for six years. It floated face-down with its arms spread wide, like a man who'd finally stopped apologizing. And beneath it, just visible through the tea-colored water, the pale suggestion of shoulders, of a head tilted at an angle no living neck allows.

I want to tell you my first thought was professional. I want to tell you I reached for my phone and called it in with steady hands and the flat cadence I use for grieving families. Some of that is even true. But the first thing—the very first thing, before training, before duty, before the coroner in me woke up and put on her gloves—was this:

*I know those clothes.*

Because I did. Not just the coat. The oxblood scarf knotted at the collar in the particular half-hearted way I knot scarves when I'm pretending I don't care how I look. The dark jeans. Even from the bridge, even through thirty feet of October water, I knew them the way you know your own handwriting.

And pinned at the lapel, catching the early light like a small insolent star—

My mother's brooch. The enameled wren. The one that had been in my jewelry box, under the false bottom, since the day I buried her.

* * *

They let me work the scene, which surprised me, and then didn't. Sheriff's deputies stood at the perimeter with their hats in their hands and their eyes everywhere but on me, which is its own kind of statement. Detective Reyes—new, three weeks new, still carrying the city in his posture—stood closest, watching me not because he doubted me but because he was cataloguing me. I could feel it like weather.

"Coroner Villalobos." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something that might be loaded. "You called it in?"

"I was walking. I saw the herons." I crouched at the waterline beside the body, now laid out on the tarp the deputies had stretched on the mud. "Time of recovery, we'll call it six-forty. Rigor will tell us more."

"You want to tell me about the clothes?"

Here is what nobody tells you about being the only person in a town who knows things: eventually, the knowing becomes the most dangerous thing about you. For eleven years I had been Harrow's Bend's coroner precisely because I was nobody—a transplant, an orphan, a woman who kept to the edge of every photograph. Invisibility was my craft. I had built it plank by plank, the way my mother built our little house on Cane Run: quietly, out of sight of the road, so no one would come asking questions.

And now a dead man was wearing my invisibility like a costume.

"The coat is mine," I said. "So are the jeans, the scarf, and the boots, if they're still on him." I turned back the collar with two fingers, careful, gloved, and there it was: the wren brooch, enamel chipped at the wing exactly where my mother dropped it on the kitchen floor in 1991 and cried for an hour over a two-dollar pin because it had been her mother's before her. "This is also mine."

Reyes went very still. Deputies don't go still; they go loud or they go away. Only detectives and predators go still.

"Yours," he repeated.

"In the sense that they were stolen from my closet. Which you'll find was entered sometime in the last—" I checked the body's bloat, the marbling along the jaw, the slippage I could see even without opening him up. "—week. Possibly less. Water accelerates decomposition; he may have gone in four days ago, maybe five."

"That's quite precise for a man you've known for ten minutes."

"It's quite imprecise," I said. "Ask me again after the autopsy."

One of the deputies—Dell Harrow's nephew, of course, everyone in this town is someone's nephew—shifted his weight and said, too casually, "Ma'am, you mind if we ask where you were Thursday night?"

And there it was. Not even eight minutes into the scene, and the question arrived, right on schedule, wearing its polite little hat.

I looked up at him. I made my face into the thing I'd spent eleven years perfecting: pleasant, competent, slightly tired, entirely unremarkable. A woman who files paperwork and signs certificates and has never once been interesting.

"I was home," I said. "Alone. As I am most nights. You're welcome to look at my electric meter data if you don't believe me."

"We might do that," Reyes said quietly.

"You should," I told him. "You should also note that whoever dressed this man went into my house, opened my closet, opened my jewelry box—which was locked—and chose items that fit him within two inches of my own frame. That's not opportunity, Detective. That's rehearsal."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a man realizing the town he'd been sent to was not the town in the brochure.

"You think this is a message," he said.

I looked down at the drowned man's face. He was Black, mid-thirties, handsome in the way of men who were once boys everybody loved. There was pond silt in the creases of his eyelids. His hands were soft—no rope burns, no dock work, no labor. A returning son, maybe. Someone's boy come home after a long time away, to a town that swallows people whole and calls it hospitality.

"I think," I said, "that somebody wanted me to see this body and recognize myself in it. And I think they succeeded."

* * *

The autopsy suite smells the same whether the county funds it or not: formalin, bleach, and underneath it all, faintly, the river. I worked alone, because I always work alone, while outside the windows the town went about its morning as if the millpond hadn't given anything back.

I'll give you the findings the way I gave them to myself, in order, because order is the only prayer I know.

Cause of death: asphyxia consistent with drowning—but. There is always a but, and the but is where the truth lives. The water in his lungs was wrong. Millpond water is tea-colored, tannic, thick with decayed leaf matter and iron bacteria. What I lavaged from his bronchi was clear, cold, faintly mineral. Lake water. Or a deep quarry. Something that wasn't the pond he was floating in.

He died somewhere else. Somebody drove him here—or carried him here—and put him in my clothes and slid him into the water like a letter into an envelope, addressed to me.

Time of death: roughly ninety-six hours prior, give or take. Which meant Thursday night. Which meant the night I was home alone with no witness but a refrigerator hum and a dog next door who doesn't like me.

Petechiae in the eyes. Defensive bruising on both forearms—he'd fought, briefly, against someone strong. And on the second finger of his right hand, a pale band of skin where a ring had been removed recently. Recently enough that the groove hadn't faded.

I wrote it all down in my careful hand, the hand Ed Marsh taught me to write in, block letters, no ambiguity, because—as Ed used to say, tapping my knuckles with his fountain pen—*the page is the only witness that can't be bought, Tamsin. Everything else in this town has a price.*

Ed. I hadn't thought of him in three weeks, and now twice in one morning. Retired these five years, living up on Prospect Hill in the house with the copper beech, beloved by everyone, godfather to half the volunteer fire department. The man who signed my mother's death certificate in 1994. *Accidental. Smoke inhalation.* The man who took in the orphaned daughter afterward, taught her anatomy on borrowed textbooks, walked her down exactly zero aisles but filled every other role a father fills, and never once—not once in thirty years—would answer a single question about the fire.

I sat with Daniel Okafor—I'd found the name in his back pocket, a North Carolina license, expired last spring, and the name hit some shelf inside me and rattled around without settling anywhere I could reach—and I thought about Ed's fountain pen, and I thought about the wren brooch, and I felt the cold come up through the steel table into my palms.

Whoever did this knew about the brooch. Knew it was hidden. Knew my house well enough to walk through it in the dark and leave everything else untouched. My books, my mother's dishes, the cash in the coffee can above the stove—all of it, still there. They took only what they needed to make a corpse into a sentence.

And the sentence read: *I know you, Tamsin Villalobos. I have been inside your life. Nothing you buried is buried deep enough.*

* * *

Reyes came by at four, holding two coffees and offering me one like a peace treaty between nations that haven't formally declared war.

"ME's office confirmed your fluid analysis," he said. "Wrong water. You were right."

"I'm usually right. It's my least popular quality."

He almost smiled. Then didn't. "Daniel Okafor. Born here in '89. Family moved away after—" He paused, and I watched him decide how much to say in front of me. "After some trouble. He came back two weeks ago. Rented a room at the Pinecrest Motel under a different name. Nobody at the motel will say they saw him after Tuesday."

"Nobody ever sees anything in this town, Detective. It's our chief export."

"His rental car is still in the motel lot. His phone's missing. And here's the part I figured you'd want to hear, since you seem to collect the parts nobody wants to hear." He set a photograph on my counter: the Pinecrest guest register, photographed in situ. Room 6, paid in cash. And in the emergency contact line, in Daniel Okafor's own handwriting, a local number.

I recognized the prefix before I recognized the rest. Prospect Hill.

Ed Marsh's number.

The coffee went cold in my hand. Outside, the light was doing that thing October light does in river towns, going gold and then going gone, and somewhere down the street a church bell rang six o'clock, and I stood in my own autopsy suite feeling the floor of my invisible life tilt beneath me like a deck in rough water.

A dead man wearing my clothes. Dying in water that wasn't his. Carrying the phone number of the only man I have ever called family.

"He was coming to see me," I said, mostly to myself. "Or he already had."

"You want to tell me why a stranger lists the retired coroner as his emergency contact?" Reyes asked.

"No," I said honestly. "But I can tell you where I'm going tonight."

I locked the brooch in my evidence drawer instead of taking it home. Then I got in my truck and drove toward Prospect Hill, toward the copper beech, toward the man who taught me that the page is the only witness that can't be bought—and for the first time in eleven years, I wondered what else Ed Marsh had taught me that I'd been too grateful to learn.
