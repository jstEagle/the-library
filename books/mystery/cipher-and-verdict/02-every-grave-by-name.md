## 2. Every Grave by Name

The dead are honest in a way the living never manage to be.

That was the first thing Ed Marsh ever taught me, back when I was nineteen and green as pond scum, holding a clipboard in his basement morgue while he peeled back a sheet with the tenderness of a man uncovering a sleeping child. *The body will tell you everything, Tamsin,* he said. *Your job is not to make it talk. Your job is to shut up long enough to listen.*

I had been listening to this one for six hours now, and what he told me was a lie.

Not him—the town. The town was lying about him, and the town lied fluently, in unison, the way it always did, like a choir that had rehearsed for generations.

His name was Daniel Okafor. I knew that before I opened his mouth and matched his dental records, before I ran his prints through AFIS and watched the screen bloom with a 1998 juvenile file sealed so fast it might never have existed. I knew it because I'd seen his face before—not on him, but in the millpond's reflection of him, and then again in the way Deputy Quist's jaw tightened when she looked at the body, a tightening so small you'd miss it if you hadn't spent thirty years learning to read this town the way other people read weather.

Daniel Okafor. Born here. Left at nine years old—shipped off to an aunt in Columbus after the fire, after the funeral nobody held for anyone, after the whole ugly business got filed away under *accidental* by Edward Marsh, coroner, pillar of the community, the man who taught me every grave has a name if you're willing to say it out loud.

Nobody in Harrow's Bend admitted expecting him. That was the strange part. A man comes home after three decades and not one person—not the diner owner, not the motel clerk, not the woman at the gas station who gossips like a switchboard—admits to having seen him alive. I'd made eleven calls by noon. Eleven polite, professional, deliberately casual calls. Eleven versions of the same blank wall.

*Okafor? Don't know any Okafors.*

The town didn't forget its Black families; it erased them, gently, the way water erases a footprint. And then it drowned one of them and dressed him in my clothes, and suddenly everyone remembered exactly where the millpond was.

* * *

The autopsy room smelled of formalin and cold steel and, underneath it, the faint mineral tang the river leaves in a body no matter how carefully you wash it. I worked alone. I always worked alone—that was the arrangement, the quiet architecture of my life: one set of hands, one signature, no witnesses. Invisibility wasn't something that happened to me. It was something I built, board by board, like a dock over deep water.

I stood over Daniel Okafor and I listened.

Drowning victims tell you where they drowned. Freshwater in the lungs means ponds, rivers, lakes—the water crosses the alveolar membrane, hemolyzes the blood, leaves the lungs heavy, waterlogged, sometimes ballooned. Saltwater pulls the opposite direction. And dry lungs—dry lungs mean the heart stopped before the water got in, or the larynx clamped shut in spasm, or somebody was already dead when they went into the drink.

Daniel Okafor's lungs were wrong.

There was water in them—I aspirated a sample myself, cloudy and faintly green, consistent with the millpond's particular cocktail of algae and tannin. But the volume was insufficient. The edema was patchy, localized to the lower lobes. His blood showed dilution, but not enough. Not the drowning of a man who struggled and sank and breathed the pond into himself in great desperate gulps.

This was the picture of a man who had inhaled some water—enough to leave traces, enough to fool a lazy eye—and nothing more. Post-mortem immersion. He'd gone into the millpond already dying or already dead, and the pond had been asked to sign a confession he never wrote.

I sat down on my stool and looked at my own hands, because looking at his face was suddenly too much.

Somebody wanted this ruled accidental. Somebody wanted it ruled accidental badly enough to dress him in my mother's cardigan, my jeans, my brooch—to build a scene that pointed at me like a weathervane. And the first step of any frame is the cause of death. Drowning in the millpond, coroner's jurisdiction, open-and-shut, tragic accident, maybe negligence on the part of the local coroner whose clothing the victim wore. The story wrote itself. All it needed was my signature on the bottom line.

Ed would have signed it. Ed, I thought, would have looked at those lungs and seen exactly what I saw, and then he would have signed it anyway, because Ed believed—no, Ed *taught* me—that the truth was a tool, and tools exist to be used by whoever holds them.

I picked up the phone and called the sheriff's office.

* * *

Detective Reyes arrived twenty minutes later, which told me two things: she'd been close by, and she'd been waiting for my call. She came into the autopsy suite the way new detectives always do—too fast, too upright, coat still buttoned against a cold she hadn't acclimated to yet—but then she stopped three feet from the table and just looked, and I revised my estimate upward. She didn't flinch at the Y-incision. She didn't perform grief or toughness. She looked at the body the way I look at bodies, which is to say she listened.

"Coroner Villalobos." She offered her hand, then seemed to register the gloves I wore and converted the gesture into an awkward salute. "Reyes. Detective Reyes. Transferred up from—" She stopped herself. "You know what, you don't care where I transferred from."

"Correct."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Approval, maybe, from a woman too new to have learned to hide it.

"Walk me through it," she said.

So I did. I walked her through the lividity fixed anteriorly with blanching over the pressure points, consistent with prone positioning post-mortem—which meant he'd lain face-down somewhere before the water, or on something. I walked her through the petechiae in the conjunctiva, sparse, inconsistent with agonal drowning. I walked her through the lungs, and I watched her face while I did it, because this was the moment where a detective either followed you or started composing her report in her head.

She followed. Her eyes actually widened—a small, involuntary widening, quickly mastered, but real.

"He didn't drown," she said.

"He inhaled some water. Whether that was ante-mortem or introduced afterward, I can't yet say. But the pulmonary findings are incompatible with death by drowning. If I ruled this an accidental drowning, I would be committing fraud."

I said it flatly, professionally, and only after the words were out did I hear what else they carried. *If I ruled.* As though ruling otherwise were a choice I'd ever had. As though there weren't three files locked in my memory—three deaths, three signatures I'd talked myself into, three graves I'd named only to myself in the dark. My hands were steady on the table's edge. I made sure of it.

"You sound like you've had this argument before," Reyes said.

"I sound like a coroner describing physiology."

"Mm." She pulled out a small notebook—paper, actual paper, another point in her favor—and clicked a pen. "Cause of death pending?"

"Pending tox, pending histology, pending the scene reconstruction. I'll want the pond dragged. I'll want his movements for the last seventy-two hours, and I'll want them from someone who admits having seen him, which—" I snapped off my gloves, "—based on my morning, may require divine intervention."

"Nobody's seen him?"

"Nobody's *admitted* seeing him. There's a difference, and in this town the difference is the whole story."

She wrote that down. Word for word, I noticed. *In this town the difference is the whole story.* Most cops paraphrase. Paraphrasing lets them keep their distance. She was writing down exactly what I said, which meant she was building a file, which meant she was building it about the town, or about me, or both.

"How long have you been coroner here?" she asked, conversational, pen still moving.

"Eleven years."

"And before that?"

"Deputy coroner. Under Ed Marsh. Twenty-two years total in that office."

"That's a long time to spend listening to dead people."

"It's the only conversation worth having," I said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back.

* * *

We stood over the body together for a while after that, and I let her ask her questions, and I answered all of them true except the ones I couldn't afford to answer at all. Where was he found—east bank, shallows, near the old sluice gate. When—estimated interval, eighteen to thirty hours, refined once tox came back. What was he wearing—

And there it was. The question I'd been circling all day like a bird over open water.

"The clothing," Reyes said. "Any significance? The ME's intake photos show a cardigan, brooch at the collar. Looks almost deliberate. Like someone dressed him."

"Someone did dress him," I said. "People don't drown in coordinated outfits. The buttons are fastened correctly. The brooch is pinned level. Whoever put those clothes on him took their time."

"Recognize them?"

And here was the fork in the road. Here was the moment where the life I'd built—the dock, the invisibility, the careful silence—either held or didn't.

I could lie. I was good at it; I'd had practice, the kind of practice that keeps you awake at 3 a.m. cataloguing your own sins like specimens. I could say the clothes looked secondhand, generic, nothing I could place. Sheriff Quist would back me—Quist always backed the office, the office being the thing she loved more than any person in it. Ed would back me from his porch with his coffee and his kind, terrible eyes. The town would close around the lie like water over a stone, and in six weeks Daniel Okafor would be a closed file and I would still be invisible, and safe, and damned.

Or I could say the truth, or a piece of it, and watch what moved.

"They belong to me," I said.

Reyes's pen stopped.

"The cardigan, the blouse, the jeans. They're mine. They were taken from my house sometime in the last—recently. The brooch—" My voice did something I hated, a hairline fracture, and I pressed through it. "The brooch belonged to my mother. Ruth Villalobos. She died in 1994. I've kept it in a drawer for thirty years. Someone has been inside my home."

Silence, except for the hum of the refrigeration unit and the small sounds of Reyes writing, writing, writing.

"When were you last in that drawer?" she asked.

"I don't know. Weeks. Maybe longer. You don't check on things you believe are safe."

"That's not an accusation."

"It's a diagnosis," I said. "Of me."

She looked at me then—really looked, the way she'd looked at the body, listening instead of watching—and whatever she saw made her close the notebook.

"Here's what I know about you, Coroner," she said. "I've been in this town four days. In four days, three separate people have told me, unprompted, that you're the best thing that ever happened to that office. Two of them said it like a warning. And this morning the sheriff's dispatcher tried to steer me away from interviewing you until 'things settle down.' So I'm going to ask you one question, and you can give me any answer you like, and I'll write it down word for word either way." She met my eyes. "Do you know who Daniel Okafor was?"

The refrigeration unit hummed. Somewhere above us, in the world of the living, a truck ground past on Route 9.

"Yes," I said.

"Who was he?"

"A boy who left this town in 1994 and came back to collect something. And everybody in Harrow's Bend knows what, and nobody will say it out loud, and one of them killed him for it and put my mother's brooch on his chest to make sure that when the shouting started, it would be about me."

I hadn't planned to say all that. It came out the way blood comes out—under pressure, following channels cut long ago.

Reyes opened her notebook again.

"Why your mother's brooch?" she asked. Quiet. Careful. The question of someone who has already understood that the brooch isn't jewelry, it's a sentence, and sentences have authors.

"Because whoever wrote this knows my history," I said. "Knows it better than I've ever said it out loud. Knows about my mother. Knows about the fire." I stopped. Too far. I'd said *fire*, and the word hung in the cold air between us like breath in winter.

"What fire?" Reyes asked.

And I gave her the smile I use at budget hearings, the one that closes doors politely.

"There've been a lot of fires in thirty years, Detective. Start with the pond. Drag it tonight. Whatever he carried into that water, I want it on my table by morning."

* * *

She left. I stayed.

That was the other thing Ed taught me: never leave a body before you've thanked it. Sounds like superstition coming from a woman who doesn't believe in anything she can't stain on a slide, but it isn't superstition. It's discipline. It forces you to stand there one more minute, past the point where the work is done and the temptation to walk away begins, and look at what's in front of you until it stops being a case and becomes a person again.

Daniel Okafor. Nine years old in 1994, the year my mother burned. Nine years old, shipped away, erased. Fifty-one now, or near it—gray threading the temples, calluses on the hands that spoke of labor, a faded tattoo on the left forearm I'd photographed and enlarged: a small flame inside a circle, ink gone blurry with age. Inked young. Inked by someone who remembered fire the way I remembered fire.

He came back for something. People come back for money, for answers, for apology. Money can be paid. Answers can be given. Apology costs nothing but pride.

But inheritance—inheritance is different. Inheritance is a claim the town itself has to honor, in public, on paper. And if what he'd inherited was the truth, then everyone who'd spent thirty years keeping that truth buried had woken up last week to find a shovel standing in their yard.

I covered his face with the sheet, and I said his name out loud, once, the way you'd call across a room to someone who'd finally come home.

Then I turned off the light, and I climbed the stairs into the ordinary gray afternoon, and I thought about the drawer where my mother's brooch had slept for thirty years, and about how many times I'd walked past it without looking, and about how a person can guard a secret so long she forgets she's also guarding the door.

Somebody had been inside my house. Somebody had known about the fire before I'd ever spoken of it. And tomorrow, when the pond gave up whatever Daniel had carried into it, one of us was going to stop pretending we didn't know why he'd come home.

I intended for it to be me.

I intended it with everything I had, and I was already afraid it wouldn't be enough—because the last time this town buried a fire, it used my mentor's signature to do it.

And mine was getting very good at looking just like his.
