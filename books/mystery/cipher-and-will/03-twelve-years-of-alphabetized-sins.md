## 3. Twelve Years of Alphabetized Sins

The drawer stayed locked for two days. Dominic told themselves it was procedure—a document of questionable provenance belonged in secure storage pending verification, and that was all, and there was nothing strange about a lawyer being careful with a will signed by a dead man.

They were lying to themselves, and they knew it, and the knowing sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

On the third morning Dominic unlocked the drawer, took out the Voss will, and read it again at the window while the town below woke up without them. *The truth, itemized.* Five words in a notarized block of legalese, dated three days after Edmund Voss's funeral. Someone had forged a dead man's signature with enough skill to pass a casual glance and enough contempt to leave the date intact—no, not contempt. Precision. The date was the message. Whoever drew up this document wanted it known exactly how impossible it was.

Dominic had spent twelve years making things possible and impossible on paper. They knew the craft when they saw it.

They put the will back, locked the drawer, and did what they always did when a thought got too large to hold: they went to work filing.

* * *

Marrow Creek had taken Dominic in the way small towns take in strays—cautiously at first, then completely, then as if it had never been any other way.

That was eleven years ago now, though the story began a year earlier, in a glass tower in the city, in a conference room where Dominic Joubert had learned the difference between winning a case and surviving one. They'd been thirty-four, a rising name at a firm whose clients included people whose names appeared in financial sections rather than police blotters. The scandal, when it came, was almost quaint: a client's documents, shredded improperly, fragments recovered by a journalist who'd been patient for two years. Dominic hadn't shredded anything themselves. They had merely known about the shredding, and arranged the schedule around it, and said nothing—which in the eyes of the bar association was nearly the same crime, and in the eyes of the city was exactly the same crime.

The disbarment proceedings collapsed on a technicality. The career didn't. Dominic resigned before they could be pushed, packed two suitcases and a box of law books, and drove until the money ran low enough to make the next decision obvious. Marrow Creek had posted an opening for a part-time probate clerk with legal experience. The pay was poor. The requirements were modest. Nobody asked why a person with Dominic's credentials wanted the job, because nobody in Marrow Creek believed anyone would lie about wanting to be there.

For the first year, Dominic was invisible in the way they'd intended to be. They processed estates. They filed deeds. They learned which of the town's two coffee shops tolerated silence and which one required conversation, and chose accordingly.

Then came the Aldous Wrenn matter, though it wasn't called that at the time. It was called a favor. Wrenn, the banker, needed a quiet amendment to a trust—nothing improper, he insisted, just discretion, just speed, just a man who understood that some paperwork was better handled without an audience. Dominic handled it. And in the weeks after, other requests arrived, each one small, each one deniable, each one accompanied by the unspoken understanding that discretion given is discretion owed.

That was how the system built itself, brick by brick, without anyone ever proposing it. A councilman needed a property dispute to resolve itself in a particular direction; Dominic found the precedent that made it so. A doctor needed an incident report to read a certain way; Dominic knew which clerk to talk to and which form to use. The mayor needed a land sale to move through probate without scrutiny; Dominic drafted the instruments so cleanly that no examiner would think to look twice.

In exchange, the town looked away from Dominic's past. In exchange, the town gave Dominic something rarer than money: indispensability. Every secret in Marrow Creek passed through the office above the hardware store, and every person whose secret it was understood that Dominic Joubert kept everything—and kept everything *quietly*.

Twelve years of alphabetized sins. That was the phrase Ilsa Reyes would eventually use, though she hadn't yet. Dominic had thought of it first, alone, at three in the morning, staring at the cabinets that lined the office walls like confessionals. Fourteen linear feet of hanging folders. Deeds and trusts and settlements and sealed agreements, each one accurate, each one lawful on its face, each one a small act of burial.

Dominic had told themselves for years that this was neutral work. That a keeper of records was not a keeper of guilt. That the town would have found its mechanisms of concealment with or without them, and at least Dominic's versions were orderly, reversible, *findable*. That last word mattered more than they admitted. Everything Dominic buried, they buried shallow, with a map. Somewhere in the back of their mind lived the belief that one day the digging might need to happen, and that when it did, Dominic's careful indexing would serve justice rather than obstruct it.

It was a comfortable fiction. It had survived twelve years of practice.

Until someone started pulling files like threads.

* * *

The realization came gradually, then all at once—the way rot does.

It started with small wrongnesses. A settlement agreement from 2016 that Dominic pulled for routine reference and found one page renumbered, subtly, the way you'd notice a book reshelved by someone who almost knew your system. A deed abstract with a marginal note in pencil that Dominic had no memory of making—*check the water dates*, written in handwriting close enough to their own to be unsettling, but not theirs. The letterforms were too patient. Dominic wrote fast, aggressively, slanting uphill. This hand had taken its time.

Then there was the copy machine log. Dominic kept the office copier on a metered account, an old habit from the firm, and reviewed the usage quarterly the way they reviewed everything. The quarter just closed showed forty-three copies made at 11:40 p.m. on a Tuesday. Dominic had not been in the office that night. The building's back door lock showed no forced entry, because the lock was old and the key hung on a hook inside the courthouse basement, where half the town's institutions kept duplicates of each other's keys under arrangements predating everyone currently alive.

Forty-three pages. Dominic counted backward through memory and arrived at the number with a cold, arithmetic certainty: forty-three pages was very nearly the exact length of the Voss estate file plus the groundwater correspondence that had been folded into it years ago and never separated. The file Callum Bright had died carrying, photocopied and stuffed into his pockets like a man fleeing a burning library.

Someone had come into Dominic's office, used Dominic's machine, taken Dominic's files, and left them otherwise undisturbed. Not stolen. *Copied.* You steal from an enemy. You copy from a map.

And that was the sentence that broke something open in Dominic's chest: *a map.*

Because that's what the office was. That's what it had always been. Twelve years of favors and filings and discreet amendments, and every single one of them documented—because Dominic could not help documenting, because documentation was the only integrity they'd permitted themselves, the one thread of honesty running through the whole fabric of complicity. If it was written down, some part of Dominic reasoned, it could someday be undone.

But a record that can undo a conspiracy can also prosecute one. And Dominic had handed the map to whoever wanted it most. The forged will. The body in borrowed clothes. The photocopied pages in a dead drifter's pockets. None of it required breaking into anything. All of it required knowing where to look—and the where was alphabetical.

Dominic stood in the middle of the office that evening, coat still on, lights off, and let the full geometry of it arrive. Every folder was a confession someone else had signed. Every cross-reference was a route. The killer—or the forger, or whatever you called a person who weaponized a probate file—hadn't needed to investigate Marrow Creek at all. They had needed only to read it, and Dominic had spent twelve years writing the reading list.

*I built this,* Dominic thought, and the thought had no self-pity in it, which somehow made it worse. *I built this, and I filed it, and I labeled it.*

There was a version of the next twenty-four hours in which Dominic simply destroyed things. They thought about it honestly, standing there in the dark. Shred the sensitive files. Burn the correspondence. Twelve years of evidence reduced to ash and a cleaner town, or at least a quieter one. It would have been easy. The shredder was in the corner, humming its standby hum, patient as everything else in the room.

But even as the fantasy played out, Dominic knew its shape, because they'd lived its shape once already. In the city, the destruction of documents had been the sin—not the underlying deal, not the client's greed, but the shredding, the erasure, the refusal to let the record speak. Dominic had lost everything to other people's shredders. They would not become one.

Besides—and here was the thought that finally sent them home, coat still buttoned against the October wind—besides, the files had already been copied. Burning them now wouldn't protect anyone. It would only destroy the one complete account of what had been done, the one artifact that could someday prove the town's guilt or, if it came to it, Dominic's own.

You don't burn the map when you're standing on it.

* * *

That night Dominic slept badly, which was becoming a genre.

Around two, they got up and made tea they didn't drink and stood at the kitchen window looking down at the courthouse, dark and squat against the sky. Callum Bright had died on those steps wearing Dominic's overcoat. Dominic kept returning to that detail, turning it over. Not the murder—murder was terrible but legible, a category with procedures. The coat. The scarf. The clothes lifted from a closet Dominic had stopped thinking of as having a door anyone else could open.

Whoever it was had been *inside* the house. Not literally—Dominic checked, twice, the locks and the dust and the faint disturbance patterns on closet hangers—but inside the life. They knew which coat was missing before Dominic did. They knew Dominic well enough to dress a corpse in him, to make the body a message addressed specifically, personally, to the one person in Marrow Creek who would understand the postage.

Which meant this wasn't random. Which meant Dominic wasn't collateral. Which meant that somewhere in those fourteen linear feet of folders was a page with Dominic's own name on it, or near it, or underneath it—and someone was reading.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down the hill, a dog barked twice and quit. Dominic thought about Edmund Voss, dead ten years, signing his name three days after his funeral. Thought about the phrase that had been sitting in them since the drawer: *the truth, itemized.*

Itemized. Like an invoice. Like a filing system.

Whoever had drawn up that will hadn't just known Dominic's world. They spoke its native language.

Dominic rinsed the untouched tea down the sink, went back to bed, and lay listening to the house settle—beam by beam, the small honest sounds of a structure holding its own weight. Tomorrow, they decided, they would pull the Voss file themselves, every page, and read it the way an enemy would. Tomorrow they would start mapping their own map, finding the threads before the thread-puller did.

Tomorrow. But sleep, when it finally came, brought no relief—only the image of the courthouse steps in morning light, and a stranger in Dominic's coat, pockets full of pages, waiting for someone to come identify the body.

When Dominic arrived at the office the next day, the drawer was open.

The will was gone. In its place, squared neatly in the center of the empty drawer, lay a single sheet of typing paper, and typed across the top of it in clean, patient letters:

**YOU KEPT THE FILE. NOW READ IT.**
