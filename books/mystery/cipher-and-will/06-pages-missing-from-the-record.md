## 6. Pages Missing From the Record

Ilsa Reyes had learned to read a room by its paperwork. Give her a man's filing cabinet and she'd tell you his marriage, his debts, and which of his neighbors he hated. So when Captain Boyd told her to close the Bright case as a robbery gone wrong, she did the next best thing to disobeying him.

She went to the archives.

The Marrow Creek courthouse kept its records in the basement, in a room that smelled of cold concrete and old toner, where the fluorescent lights buzzed at a frequency that made her back teeth ache. The clerk on duty—Ruth Ann Delacroix, gray cardigan, reading glasses on a chain—let her in with the mild courtesy of a woman who had nothing to hide and knew exactly how much of it there was.

"Probate's on the east wall," Ruth Ann said. "Land records west. Vital statistics are boxed up from '09 onward, we never got the funding to digitize." She smiled. "Anything particular?"

"Just getting oriented," Ilsa said. "A drifter dies on our steps wearing stolen clothes full of photocopied case files. I'd like to know what else lives down here."

"Take your time." Ruth Ann set a key ring on the desk between them, an offering or a test. "It's all alphabetized."

It was. That was the first thing Ilsa noticed, and it bothered her more than mess would have. Twelve years of filings, every box labeled in the same careful hand, every folder squared to the edge of its shelf. A room this tidy wasn't organized. It was curated.

She started with Callum Bright—or rather, with what should have been Callum Bright's paper trail. The photocopied pages in his pockets had come from somewhere, and the copies were good ones: clean text, no skew lines, no coffee rings. Machine copies made recently. She pulled the originals they referenced.

Three files were missing outright. Their boxes still held the numbered dividers, the little plastic tabs marking where each folder belonged, but the folders themselves were gone—lifted so cleanly you'd only notice if you counted. Ilsa counted. Then she pulled the surrounding years and counted those too.

Fourteen folders missing across a decade. Not random ones. Water permits. A pharmacy inspection log. An environmental survey commissioned in the spring of Edmund Voss's last year alive, referenced in three other documents but filed nowhere itself.

And then she found the alterations, and the alterations were worse than the thefts.

* * *

The first one she caught by accident. A deed transfer dated March 14, 2013, referencing a survey completed March 2. But the survey's own cover sheet said March 9—and the ink of the *9* sat a half-tone darker than the rest of the line, the way type looks when it's been printed twice, once over a correction. Someone had shifted the date. Three days. Enough time for a sale to close before a contamination finding could attach to the land.

She sat down on the rolling stool and stopped being casual about any of it.

For six hours she worked backward through the boxes, and the pattern emerged the way a photograph develops—slowly, then all at once. Dates nudged. Names struck through in ways that looked like clerical error but never were; a struck name always reappeared two pages later under a different capacity, so the record stayed legally functional while the connection dissolved. Signatures witnessed by men who, cross-referenced against other filings, had been dead or out of state on the dates claimed.

This wasn't vandalism. This wasn't a hack job. Every alteration required knowing exactly how much you could change before a document stopped holding up—which clauses carried the weight, which dates anchored the others, which names could be struck without collapsing the instrument. It required legal precision. It required someone who understood that a record isn't paper. It's a story the law agrees to believe.

Ilsa had worked county corruption cases before, in her previous life two counties over. Those guys used bulldozers. They burned things, shredded things, left gaps you could drive a truck through. Whoever did this had left no gaps at all. The record read smooth as glass from end to end. You'd only see the seams if you already suspected there were seams—if you came looking for them because a dead man's pockets were full of photocopies.

Every altered document had one thing in common.

Not a signature—the signatures varied, deliberately. Not a date. A handling mark. The internal routing stamp, the little blue initials-and-date code the clerk's office applied when a filing passed through intake. Twelve years of documents, fourteen folders missing, twenty-some alterations, and every single touched page bore the same intake code.

She wrote it down and stared at it.

J-1147-D.

"That's just the processor assignment," Ruth Ann said later, when Ilsa asked, too casually, what the codes meant. "Each filer gets assigned to a deputy clerk. J-1147 is—" Ruth Ann adjusted her glasses. "That would be Dominic Joubert. Our probate attorney. Lovely person. Very thorough."

Very thorough. Ilsa drove home with the windows down despite the cold, letting the air scrape her face, thinking about thoroughness. Because here was the problem, and she turned it over all night like a stone with something underneath: the alterations weren't sloppy, and they weren't secret. Anyone auditing deeply enough would find them. And the one person who handled every file—who knew the routing system, who knew which documents anchored which, who had the legal training to make a lie structurally sound—was also the one person whose name was stamped on every lie.

Either Dominic Joubert had spent twelve years quietly rewriting Marrow Creek's history.

Or someone wanted Ilsa to think so.

* * *

She didn't sleep much. In the morning she pulled Dominic's bar registration, employment history, the works. Clean. A city firm, twelve years ago, a resignation with no scandal attached that she could find—which itself was interesting, because nobody leaves a city firm for a dying farm town out of ambition. People run toward small towns or away from something. She couldn't yet see what Dominic was running from, and the not-knowing itched.

At four o'clock she called the law office and asked if Mr. Joubert might have fifteen minutes.

Dominic met her at the door of the office above the hardware store, and Ilsa watched the composure settle over their face like a coat being buttoned. She'd seen it the day of the body on the steps—that terrible calm, the answers arriving slightly ahead of the questions. Most people, confronted with a stranger's corpse wearing their own coat, got rattled. Dominic had gotten precise.

"Deputy Reyes." Dominic stepped back to let her in. The office was spare and immaculate: law books in perfect rows, a single orchid on the windowsill, not one loose paper anywhere except a legal pad turned discreetly blank-side-up. "Is this about the Bright case? I've told you everything I know about the coat."

"It's not about the coat." Ilsa didn't sit until invited, and wasn't invited, so she stood. "It's about the archives."

Something moved behind Dominic's eyes—not fear exactly. Recalculation. "The archives."

"I spent yesterday in the basement. Fourteen folders missing. Twenty-three documents altered—dates shifted, names struck, in ways that hold up legally unless you know what to look for. I know what to look for." She let that sit. "Every alteration carries the same intake stamp. J-1147-D."

The silence lasted three seconds. Ilsa counted them, the way she counted everything.

"You're saying I altered those documents," Dominic said.

"I'm saying your stamp is on them."

"And you're telling me this in my office instead of arresting me." Dominic folded their arms—not defensive, Ilsa decided. Thinking. "Why?"

"Because if you did it, you're better than anyone I've ever chased, and people that good don't get caught by an intake stamp. And if you didn't do it"—she watched their face carefully—"then somebody with access to this office has been signing your name to twelve years of lies, and they wanted me to find it."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had changed—lower, stripped of the professional polish. "You audited the whole archive in one day."

"Took me six hours. The rest of the day I spent proving I wasn't crazy."

"The date shifts." Dominic uncrossed their arms. "Which direction?"

"Forward, mostly. Sales closing before findings could attach. Why does the direction matter to you?"

"Because if I'd done it, I'd have shifted them backward." Dominic crossed to the window, looking out at the street below, at the ordinary traffic of an ordinary town. "Backward makes a defect pre-existing. Forward makes it discovered after the fact. Any lawyer covering their own tracks picks the version that protects the client. These protect—" They stopped.

"Protect who?" Ilsa said.

Dominic turned around, and what Ilsa saw in their face wasn't guilt. It was the specific horror of a person doing arithmetic they'd been avoiding for years and finally getting the sum. "Protect the town," Dominic said quietly. "Every alteration protects the town. Or the people who decided they were the town."

"So you're saying you didn't do it."

"I'm saying I need to check my own files before I answer that question, and I need you to not be standing here when I do." Dominic met her eyes. "Off the record. If you write this down now, whoever did this will know within a day. Your captain wants the Bright case closed. Your sergeant—" a fractional pause, "—Pruitt, is it? He's related to the Pruitts out past the orchard road."

Ilsa felt the floor tilt a degree. She hadn't mentioned Pruitt's family connections to anyone in Marrow Creek. She'd barely let herself think it. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've lived here twelve years, Deputy, and I keep records." Dominic's mouth curved, without humor. "You're starting to understand my problem. Everything I know, I know because I filed it. Which means everything I know is suspect—including what I know about you."

The afternoon light had gone long and amber across the office floor. Downstairs, the hardware store was closing; she could hear the grate rattling down. Two strangers in a quiet room, each holding a knife neither wanted to use.

"Here's what I can offer," Ilsa said. "Seventy-two hours. You audit your own files, I audit mine, and we compare notes off the record. No reports. No captains. If either of us finds the other lying, all bets are off and I take everything to the state police myself."

"And why would you trust me?"

"I wouldn't. That's the point." Ilsa pulled out her notebook, tore out the top page—blank—and handed it over. "Write down a number. Any number. Tomorrow at nine, I'll tell you my number, and if yours matches, we'll both know neither of us talked to anyone in between. It's not trust. It's a tripwire."

Dominic took the pen. Wrote. Folded the page once and set it under the orchid pot without showing her. "Nine o'clock," they said. "There's a diner on Route 9. Neutral ground. And Deputy—" For the first time since she'd known them, Dominic Joubert looked tired. Not composed. Tired, in the bone-deep way of a person who has been carrying something alone for a very long time. "Whoever did this knows my system better than I do. Do you understand what that means? I built that filing system. I built it so carefully that it became a map of every sin this town ever committed. And someone is walking that map like they drew it."

"Maybe they did draw it," Ilsa said. "Maybe they learned it from the inside."

"Then God help us," Dominic said, "because the inside is me."

They shook hands at the door—briefly, formally, two professionals sealing a contract neither fully believed the other would honor. On the stairs Ilsa heard the lock click behind her, precise as everything else about the probate attorney of Marrow Creek.

In her car she sat for a moment before starting the engine. Somewhere in that tidy office, under a potted orchid, a number waited in a fold of paper. Somewhere in the basement of the courthouse, twenty-three lies sat in their boxes, stamped and shelved and patient. And somewhere in town, a person who understood records the way surgeons understand anatomy was deciding what to alter next.

Her phone buzzed. Dispatch. A report of a disturbance at the Voss orchard—trespasser, possibly. She turned the key, and thought, pulling onto Route 9: *the orchard*. Where Edmund Voss had died. Where the fire had been.

Where, she had a feeling, the map led next.
