## 1. The Wrong Clothes

The gulls found him before anyone else did.

Kestrel Marchetti heard them from the end of the pier, that particular ragged chorus they made over something dead, and they were still screaming when the harbor patrol boat backed in and the crowd on the dock went quiet in the way crowds do — a held breath, a collective leaning forward. Kestrel didn't lean. Kestrel stood very still, coffee cooling in one gloved hand, and watched the tarp come off the gurney with the detached attention of a professional who had seen too many bodies to flinch and too many mistakes to look away.

The dead man was bloated gray-green, harbor-swollen, his face softened past recognition. But the clothes—

The clothes were wrong.

Not wrong the way drowned men's clothes are wrong, waterlogged and clinging. Wrong the way a sentence is wrong when one word has been swapped for its twin. Kestrel's eyes went to the jacket first — a charcoal wool overcoat, single-breasted, with a missing button on the third hole and a repair at the left elbow where the lining had once torn and been stitched back by hand, badly, in black thread. Then the scarf: deep teal, frayed at the tail end where it had caught in a car door three winters ago, a pale thread of original dye still visible in the unravel.

Kestrel knew that jacket. Kestrel knew that scarf.

They had hung in the closet of Kestrel's apartment on Delmore Street, third hook from the left, between a rain shell and a coat Kestrel never wore because it had belonged to their mother.

The coffee cup slipped. It didn't fall — Kestrel caught it, an automatic correction, the body obeying while the mind reeled — but the small motion was enough. A uniformed officer at the edge of the tape turned, followed Kestrel's stare to the gurney, and then to the jacket, and then back, and Kestrel watched the arithmetic happen behind the man's eyes. Watched the word *witness* dissolve into the word *suspect* like sugar into cold tea.

"Ma'am. Sir." The officer approached, hand resting easy on his belt. "You want to step over here and tell me what you're looking at?"

"My clothes," Kestrel said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "That man is wearing my clothes."

* * *

The interview room at the Ninth Precinct smelled of burnt coffee and industrial lemon, a smell Kestrel would remember forever, the way they remembered everything — every room catalogued and filed in the relentless archive behind their eyes, nothing discarded, nothing dimmed. The detective across the table was young, with a wedding band and a notebook he didn't open, and he asked the questions detectives ask when they've already decided the answer.

"Where were you between eleven p.m. and three a.m.?"

"Asleep. Alone. I live by myself."

"Convenient."

"Accurate," Kestrel said. "There's a difference, though I understand it may be a subtle one in this room."

His jaw tightened. "You reported a break-in last month. Nothing taken."

"Nothing I could prove was taken. Things were moved. My closet was rearranged. I mentioned it because I have a — an unusual memory, and the disorder bothered me. The responding officer suggested I buy a better lock." Kestrel leaned forward. "Detective, I'm going to tell you what your medical examiner will tell you in twelve hours, and I'd like you to consider how I know it."

"Go ahead."

"Your victim is between fifty-five and sixty. He has a surgical pin in his left ankle — old injury, tibia fracture, plated. He's missing the tip of his right ring finger, lost to a boat winch, I'd guess, given the scar tissue pattern. And he has a tattoo on his inner left wrist that the water hasn't touched: an anchor with a broken chain."

The detective had stopped pretending not to write.

"That's Elias Voss," Kestrel said. "Retired last year. Twenty-six years in this department. He was the detective who closed my mother's hit-and-run when I was nineteen. He told me, sitting in a room very much like this one, that the driver was never found and the file was going inactive. He shook my hand. I remember the grip. I remember everything."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere down the hall a phone rang four times and stopped.

"You're telling me you identified him from a tattoo."

"I'm telling you I identified him from a tattoo, a surgical pin, an amputation, and the way he held his shoulders when he walked, which I last saw fifteen years ago and have not forgotten since. The clothes are a separate matter. The clothes are a *message*." Kestrel kept their hands flat on the table, deliberate, visible. "Someone took my coat and my scarf out of a locked apartment and put them on a dead cop and dropped him in the harbor where I'd be likely to see him pulled out. That's not evidence. That's theater. And theater has an audience in mind. The audience was me."

The detective studied Kestrel for a long moment. "You watch a lot of crime shows, Marchetti?"

"I investigate them," Kestrel said. "Insurance fraud, mostly. Three deaths in the last four years that your department called accidents and I called murder, with documentation. Ask your captain. Ask Captain Rourke, if he's speaking to you this morning. He's had opinions about me before."

Something flickered across the young detective's face at the name — quick, involuntary, quickly buried. Kestrel filed it.

* * *

They released Kestrel by late afternoon, no charges, no apology, a card with a phone number and a warning not to leave town. Kestrel walked home through streets going blue with early dark, and the whole way, the memory worked.

It always worked like this — unbidden, involuntary, the archive opening itself. The apartment on Delmore Street, the night of the supposed break-in, six weeks ago. Kestrel's photographic recall laid the room out in perfect detail: the door locked, the chain on, no forced entry. The closet door open two inches when Kestrel was certain it had been closed. The coat hangers — wire, mismatched, Kestrel kept them because Marisol said a person should own at least one ugly thing — the hangers rotated a quarter turn from their habitual angles. The third hook from the left, empty. The faint smell of cigarettes, unlit, carried in on fabric, on *someone's* fabric, someone who smoked and didn't, someone who had stood in the closet long enough to choose carefully. Not to rob. To *select*.

And beneath the cigarette ghost, something else. Diesel. Harbor diesel.

Kestrel stopped under a streetlight and let the reconstruction finish itself, frame by frame, the way it always did, the terrible gift their mother used to call a camera that never learned to blink. Whoever had taken the clothes had come by water or by boat. Had come from the harbor. Had known the lock was old, the building quiet, the tenant's habits — Kestrel's habits — down to the hour.

Someone who knew Kestrel. Someone who wanted the police to look at Kestrel and see a killer. Or someone who wanted Kestrel to look at the body and see a *warning*.

Both, probably. The best messages worked twice.

Kestrel let themselves into the apartment, checked the chain, checked the closet. The third hook from the left was still empty. It would stay empty. It felt, absurdly, like a grave.

They stood a long time in the dark of the kitchen without turning on the light, and the memory that came then was the oldest one in the archive, the one that never needed reconstructing because it had never once faded: rain, a crosswalk, a green light, their mother's yellow coat bright as a struck match against the wet asphalt, and the sound — not the impact, Kestrel had been too far for the impact, but the sound after, the brakes that never braked, the engine opening up instead, *accelerating*, a choice made in real time by someone with a foot on a pedal. Fifteen years ago. Closed as an accident. Closed by Elias Voss.

Voss, who had drowned in Kestrel's coat.

Kestrel's hands had gone cold. They noticed it the way they noticed everything — precisely, completely, without the ability to stop.

"You knew," they said aloud to the empty room, to the dead man, to the fifteen-year-old file with its inactive stamp. "You didn't just close it. You knew something. And someone made sure you stopped knowing it in the worst way there is."

The dread that had sat in Kestrel's chest since the pier was changing shape, cooling, contracting, hardening the way wax hardens into a seal. This was no longer a case. This was no longer even a frame. This was a conversation, opened across fifteen years and one gray harbor, by someone who believed Kestrel Marchetti remembered too much.

They were right. Kestrel remembered everything.

Kestrel took down the ugly wire hangers, all of them, and laid them on the kitchen table in a row, and began — methodically, precisely, the camera behind their eyes clicking frame after frame — to list everyone who had ever seen the inside of that closet.

The list was short. It was about to get shorter.

And at the top of it, in memory as vivid as the night itself: the young detective's face this afternoon, when Kestrel had said *Captain Rourke* — the flicker, the flinch, the fear.

Kestrel reached for their coat. Not the charcoal one. The rain shell.

First stop: the storage unit Voss had kept, according to a retired colleague, on Fenwick Street. Second stop: whoever Voss had been afraid of.

The harbor gulls were still screaming somewhere behind them, all the way down the stairs.
