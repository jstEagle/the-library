## 1. Two Envelopes, One Morning

The key stuck in the lock the way it always did, and Orsen Oyelaran jiggled it the way they always did, and for one ordinary moment the world was nothing but a cold October morning, a stubborn door, and the smell of yesterday's dust burning off the radiator inside Pellucid Books.

Then the door gave, the bell above it rang its cracked little note, and Orsen saw the envelopes on the mat.

Two of them. Cream-colored, heavy stock, laid side by side with a precision that felt less like mail and more like an arrangement. No stamps. No postmark. Hand-delivered sometime between when Orsen had locked up at eleven last night — late inventory, the quarterly kind that ate whole evenings — and now, six-forty in the morning, when the street outside was still the color of weak tea.

Orsen stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the cold come in around their ankles, and did not pick them up.

Twenty-six years of police work teaches you things that never quite leave. One of them is that your body often knows before your mind does. Orsen's body knew. There was a tightness under the sternum, a small animal alertness, the same feeling they used to get walking into a room where something had happened — not because of what was visible, but because of what the room was doing to the air.

The shop did this every morning, of course. It was just a shop. Narrow and deep, shelves leaning into each other like old friends at a bar, the mystery section in the back where Orsen had always said it belonged — you had to work for it, walk past everything else first. A kettle in the back room. A cat named Verdict who appeared only when he felt like being symbolic.

But this morning the air was doing something.

Orsen crouched, picked up both envelopes in one hand, and turned them over.

The first was addressed in a hand so familiar it made Orsen's mouth go dry: *O. Oyelaran, Proprietor.* The R was sharp, almost angry; the y dropped below the line like something falling off a ledge. Orsen would have known that handwriting anywhere. They'd spent eleven years reading it across a car's front seat, on case notes, on the backs of receipts in diners at two in the morning.

It belonged to Detective Rell Hargrove. Retired now, like Orsen. Living out past the quarry road, growing tomatoes he complained about and drank whiskey he didn't.

The second envelope was addressed too, but in a different hand entirely — small, careful, upright, the letters standing like people waiting politely in a queue. No name on the return corner. Nothing.

Orsen locked the door behind them, flipped the sign even though the sign said CLOSED until nine anyway, and carried the envelopes to the back room as if they were live ordnance.

* * *

The kettle went on mostly out of habit. Orsen didn't remember deciding to make tea.

They sat at the scarred table by the window — the one with the good light, the one where they did the accounts badly and read the new arrivals well — and set the two envelopes down between the sugar tin and a stack of unpriced paperbacks.

Drink your tea first, said the sensible voice, the voice of routine, the voice that had kept the shop alive through three bad winters. Open them after. Everything is worse before tea.

Orsen drank the tea. Their hands were steady. That was the strange part. Twenty years retired and the hands still knew how to be steady, which meant some part of them had already decided what these were before the rest of them caught up.

The first envelope opened along the seam with a sound like a small tear in fabric.

Inside, four pages, written on both sides, in that furious, falling R-hand. Dated — no date, actually, which was itself a choice. Signed at the bottom, full name, in ink pressed hard enough to emboss the page.

*I, Rell Hargrove, confess to the murder of Ada Voss.*

Orsen read the line twice. The words didn't rearrange themselves. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs, in the flat, Verdict shifted his weight across a floorboard with a single creak.

Then Orsen did what twenty-six years had trained them to do, the thing they could no more stop doing than stop blinking: they slowed down, and they read it properly.

The confession ran four pages. It was detailed in a way that made the skin crawl — not the fevered scrawl of a crank, not the looping self-mythology of the confessor-cranks who used to write to the precinct every few months claiming every unsolved case in the county. This was procedural. This was someone who knew how an investigation moved, knew the sequence of a night, knew what evidence existed and where it had been found.

Ada Voss. Found at the bottom of her own staircase on the fourteenth of November, twenty years ago this month. Sixty-one years old, widowed, beloved in the way that quiet women who run charity drives and correct your grammar are beloved. Death ruled accidental. Staircase, slippers, a glass of sherry, a wet leaf tracked in from the garden. Case closed in nine days. Orsen remembered the number because Hargrove had joked about it — *nine days, we should get a plaque* — and Orsen had laughed, and then hadn't slept well for a week, and had never once said why.

Because there had been a thing. A small thing. A lead Orsen had been told to drop, in words that hadn't sounded like orders at the time but had aged into them. That was a matter for later chapters of the night, the ones Orsen didn't reread.

The confession knew things.

It knew Ada had been found face-down, left arm folded beneath her, right hand open. It knew the sherry glass had been on the hall table rather than the stair landing, though she'd told the neighbor on the phone that evening that she was taking her sherry up. It knew the timing of the rain that night, that the wet-leaf explanation required leaves that the wind direction made nearly impossible. It knew that the housekeeper, Mrs. Ansel, had been given the week off — a detail that had never appeared in any newspaper, because Mrs. Ansel had never spoken to a newspaper, because nobody had ever asked her anything at all.

And it knew the staircase had thirteen steps, and that Ada Voss had lived in that house thirty-four years, and that a woman does not fall down a staircase she has climbed ten thousand times.

Orsen set the pages down. Picked them up again. Read the final paragraph.

*I have carried this for twenty years because I was told I could carry it safely, and I believed it, and belief is the luxury of the protected. I am old now and the protection has expired. What I did, I did, and what was done afterward was done by others, and none of us gets to choose which of those things weighs more. I am sorry. Not to the town. To her. And to the girl.*

*— Rell Hargrove*

*To the girl.* Maren. Maren Voss, who had been nineteen when her mother died, and twenty when she walked into the reservoir with her coat pockets full of stones, and whose death had been ruled what it obviously was while her mother's death had been ruled what it obviously wasn't, and the symmetry of those two rulings had sat in Orsen's chest for twenty years like a swallowed hook.

Orsen looked at the signature a long time.

Rell Hargrove's hand. Rell Hargrove's name. Rell Hargrove's knowledge — because whoever wrote this knew the case from the inside, knew it the way only investigators knew it, and Rell had been lead detective, and Orsen had been the other half of that lead.

Except.

Except that Orsen had spent eleven years watching that hand write, and something was wrong with it, and it took another cup of tea and most of the second page before Orsen could name what.

The hand was right and the hand was wrong. The R's were perfect — too perfect, performed, the way a person draws a letter when they're thinking about the letter instead of the sentence. Real handwriting is lazy. This wasn't lazy anywhere. Every character was being presented, like exhibits. And the pressure — uniform, relentless, the pen bearing down identically on every stroke, page after page. People don't write like that. People vary. People tire. People's hands wander when they're writing about the worst thing they ever did.

This was a copyist. Someone writing slowly, deliberately, imitating a hand they knew extremely well.

Someone forging Rell Hargrove.

Orsen put the pages face-down on the table, which was absurd, there was no one to hide them from, and pressed both palms against their eyes until the dark bloomed orange.

* * *

The second envelope waited.

Orsen made themselves finish the first pot of tea, wash the cup, dry it, put it away — the full liturgy of delay — before picking it up. Inside was a single card, the expensive kind sold at the stationer's near the church, blank on the back. The front held five words in that small, upright, patient hand:

*The rest is coming. Wait.*

No signature. No salutation. Just the five words, centered, like a title page.

Orsen read it until the letters stopped meaning anything, then read it again and let them mean it.

Wait.

Not a threat, exactly. Threats want a reaction. This wanted patience. This was someone with a schedule. Someone who had written — or assembled — a four-page confession in a dead-perfect imitation of a living man's hand, delivered it by night to a bookshop, and then, in a different voice entirely, told the recipient to sit still for whatever came next.

Orsen got up and checked the front door. Locked. Checked the back. Locked. Stood in the middle of the shop listening to the building do its morning sounds — pipes, radiator, the awning rope knocking the window frame in the wind.

Whoever came through that door last night had known the shop. Had known the mat lay flat and visible from the street through the gap in the curtain Orsen never quite drew. Had known Orsen opened early, alone, that the first hour belonged to no one else. This wasn't a stranger's crime. This was addressed mail in every sense.

Back in the kitchen, Orsen took out a sandwich bag from the drawer — the old habit, preserve the document, chain of custody, though custody of what, and before whom — and slid the card inside. Then the confession pages, separately. Then sat back down and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding.

Twenty years ago this month, Ada Voss died. Nine days later, the case closed. A year after that, Maren drowned. The town had spent two decades turning the first death into a cautionary tale about staircases and the second into a sad footnote, and this autumn the anniversary committee — Hale's committee, Dree Hale presiding, Hale who had been the first officer on the scene and had somehow become, by the alchemy of small-town politics, the face of remembrance — had spent six weeks planning a memorial dinner with printed programs and a caterer from two towns over.

In nine days, the town would raise a glass to Ada Voss.

And this morning, someone had put a murder confession on Orsen's doormat.

* * *

At eight-forty-five, Orsen unlocked the door and flipped the sign to OPEN and spent the next hour performing normalcy for a total of four customers: the schoolteacher buying a paperback romance with a cover she hid inside a dictionary, the twins from the bakery arguing over a chess book, old Mr. Fenn who came in every Tuesday to touch the spines without buying anything and whom Orsen suspected simply missed talking to people.

Orsen talked to all of them. Orsen's voice worked. Inside, behind the counter, in the bag beneath the register where the till receipts used to go, the two documents sat like coals.

At noon, Orsen turned the sign, hung BACK IN AN HOUR, and walked the twelve minutes to the telephone box outside the chemist's, because the landline in the flat had died in August and Orsen had never quite gotten around to resurrecting it, and because part of them — a large part, if they were honest — wanted to make this call standing up, in public, where it would be harder to say the soft thing.

Hargrove answered on the fourth ring.

"Quarry house."

"Rell. It's Orsen."

A pause with weather in it. "Orsen Oyelaran. The bookselling life agrees with you, I hope. You sound like a woman reading a meter."

"I need to ask you something and I need you to answer it straight."

"That's my favorite kind of question." But the cheer had thinned. Rell Hargrove had been a good detective once, underneath the jokes, and good detectives hear the shape of a call before its content.

"Did you send me a letter?"

Silence. Long enough that Orsen checked the connection.

"What kind of letter," Rell said finally, and his voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice he'd used on doorsteps when he didn't know yet whether the person opening the door was a witness or a suspect.

"A confession, Rell. Four pages. In your handwriting. Signed with your name. It says you killed Ada Voss."

The silence this time was different. Orsen had heard a lot of silences in eleven years of partnership — the silence of a man thinking, the silence of a man deciding what to tell you, the silence of a man relieved. This was none of those. This was the silence of a man standing very still because something is moving nearby in the grass.

"I didn't write that," Rell said.

"You're sure."

"I'm sure I know what I wrote. Orsen—" and now the fear came through, plain, undecorated, and it was the wrong fear, Orsen realized. It wasn't the fear of a guilty man caught. It was the fear of a man who has just learned he's been named. "When did it come?"

"This morning. On the mat. No stamp."

Another silence, shorter, tighter. "Mine came Tuesday."

Orsen's hand tightened on the receiver. "Yours came."

"A letter. Unsigned. Told me to be ready to account for myself." A breath, ragged at the edges. "I thought it was a prank. Some kid dug up the old case files, having a laugh at the retirees. I burned it, God help me, I burned it, because I didn't want it in the house where June could find it. And now you're telling me there's a confession going around with my name signed to it like a warrant."

"Rell." Orsen watched the chemist's window across the street, at their own reflection ghosted in the glass, at the slow traffic of a town getting on with its lunch. "The handwriting is yours. Near enough that I believed it for a full page, and I watched you write for eleven years."

"Then somebody practiced," Rell said. "Somebody practiced a long time."

"And they know the case. Inside. Things that never left the file."

The wind pushed at the phone box. Somewhere down the street a delivery van beeped its way backward.

"There were four people who knew that file the way this letter knows it," Rell said quietly. "You. Me. Hale. And the coroner's clerk, and he's dead. So either you've taken up forgery in your old age, Orsen, or—"

"Or someone taught it to them," Orsen said. "Page by page. Over years."

Rell didn't answer that. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing, and it was the oldest thing he'd ever said to Orsen, older than the case, older than the partnership.

"Orsen. Whoever wrote mine — they knew things about me too. Small things. Things I never told anyone." A pause. "Things I barely told myself."

Orsen stood in the phone box with the receiver cold against their ear and thought about the second envelope. *The rest is coming. Wait.*

"Come to the shop," Orsen said. "Tonight. After close. Bring whatever's left of your nerve."

"I'll bring the whiskey," Rell said. "You bring the questions."

Orsen hung up and stayed in the box a moment longer, watching the town move, ordinary and warm and unknowing, nine days from its toast.

Behind the glass of the chemist's window, in the reflection, the street went on being innocent. And Orsen understood, with the calm certainty that had once made them good at this work and now made the mornings difficult, that the person who had left those envelopes on the mat had not finished.

That *the rest* was already written. That it was sitting somewhere in this town, in that small, patient, upright hand, sealed and sorted and waiting for its mornings.

Waiting for Orsen to wait.
