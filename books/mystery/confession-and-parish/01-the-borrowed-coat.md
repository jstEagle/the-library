## 1. The Borrowed Coat

The rain had been falling since noon with the patience of something that intended to keep its word, and by half past five it had worked its way under the door of Eastwick's Books and was pooling in the groove where the threshold had swelled. Freya Eastwick stood behind the counter with a mug of tea gone cold in her hands and watched the water creep toward the poetry section, which was, like everything else in the shop, losing ground.

She had stopped counting the losses in the spring. Now she counted only the days, and even that had begun to feel like an indulgence. The shop had been her husband's idea, twenty-three years ago — *a place for you to land, Frey, when you finally put the badge down* — and it had landed her precisely nowhere. The shelves were full, the till was not, and the October light through the warped front window turned every surface the colour of old bone.

The bell over the door gave its cracked half-chime. She looked up expecting Mrs. Tredinnick, who came every Thursday for the large-print westerns and stayed forty minutes to complain about the price of them.

It was not Mrs. Tredinnick. It was a boy of maybe nineteen, soaked through, holding a bicycle helmet under one arm like a bowl he was afraid to spill.

"You're Freya Eastwick," he said. Not a question. He was breathing hard, as though he had ridden fast, or as though the thing he had come to say had chased him the whole way.

"I'm the only one here by that name."

"There's a — " He stopped. Swallowed. Rain ran off his fringe and down the bridge of his nose. "There's a body. Out on the marsh road. By the second stile. They said — the woman in the shop on Fore Street said you used to be police. She said to fetch you."

Freya set down the mug. The tea had been cold long enough that the china made no sound against the counter, or perhaps she simply didn't hear it over the sudden loudness of her own pulse.

"Has anyone called it in?" she asked. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "An ambulance? The station?"

"The signal's bad out there. I tried. I rode in to phone from the box on Fore Street, and they said an officer would come, and the woman said — " He shifted his weight. "She said you'd want to know it was a woman. The body."

The shop was very quiet around them. The radiator ticked. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice and went still.

"Wait here," Freya said. "Lock the door behind me."

She took the torch from under the counter — the big rubber-sleeved one, kept for the winter power cuts — and her coat, and she did not allow herself to think about why her hands were already steady, why the old machinery was turning over in her chest as though it had never been left out in the weather at all.

Outside, the rain met her like a wall. She pulled her collar up and walked, because the boy's bicycle was a girl's machine with a bent basket and she would have been faster on foot than waiting for him to pedal her out. The marsh road ran west from the village, a raised causeway of old stone between the black water and the blacker sky, and she had walked it a thousand times in the years since she'd stopped walking it professionally. The reeds hissed. The ditches had swallowed their banks. By the time she reached the second stile her shoes were ruined and the torch beam was a trembling cone of grey.

She saw the shape from thirty yards and knew, before the light found the first detail, that she would know it.

It lay on its side in the lee of the drystone wall, where the ground dipped toward the saltings. A woman, or the suggestion of one. The rain had flattened everything — the grass, the reeds, the fabric — into a single wet grammar. And the fabric was grey.

Freya stopped walking.

The coat was grey. A long grey coat, belted, with the collar turned up against the weather, and at the throat a scarf knotted in the loose, careless way she herself had never managed to learn — the way her sister had tried to teach her at their mother's funeral, the way she'd worn it every cold morning since because it was the only knot her fingers remembered. The torch shook. She made it stop.

She knew that coat. She had hung it on the back of the shop door on the last cold day of September, and when she'd gone to fetch it a week later it had not been there, and neither had the scarf, and she had told herself what one tells oneself about small losses in a small town: *misplaced, borrowed, sold by mistake in the charity box.* She had even, with a flicker of irritation she was now ashamed of, suspected the Saturday volunteer.

The light moved down the figure. Hands folded at the breast. Dark hair spread on the drowned grass. One shoe off, lying a yard away, its sole upturned like a small black mouth.

"Hello," Freya said, to the body, because it was what you did — the first thing they had taught her twenty-five years ago in a draughty classroom in Bodmin: *speak to them, they were people, speak to them first.* Her voice cracked on the second syllable and she let the silence take its place.

She crouched, not touching, the way the ground itself seemed to demand, and looked at the face.

It was not a young face. It was a tired, pleasant, ordinary face, fifty or so, the kind of face you saw behind counters and across committee tables, and it was wrong in the way that dead faces are wrong — emptied, the rain pooling in the hollows of the eyes. Freya did not know her. That was the first thing. That was almost a relief, and then it was worse, because the relief meant she had been braced for a face she knew.

The coat was hers. She would swear to it on anything — the missing button at the left cuff, the one she'd meant to sew back for two years. The scarf was hers, the wine-dark wool her sister had posted from Leeds. Someone had taken them from her shop, or from her home, and put them on this dead stranger, and laid her out on the marsh road where anyone walking in from the village would find her, where *Freya Eastwick*, who walked this road every evening the rain allowed, would find her.

The cold went through her then, all at once, as though the wet had finally reached the skin. Not the cold of the evening. A colder thing, older, with a familiar shape.

She had seen this before. Not this coat, not this woman, not this road. But the *arrangement* of it — the folded hands, the single shoe removed and set apart, the body placed with its back to the water as if in deference to it — she had seen this grammar before, in another October, in another parish, when she had been young and certain and twenty minutes too late to matter.

*No,* she told herself. *You're seeing patterns in the rain. Twenty years of murder novels will do that to a person.*

But her hands did not believe her. Her hands had gone still and competent, the way they used to, and they were already cataloguing: no visible blood, no disturbance of the ground beyond the one set of footprints leading in from the road — large boots, deep tread, half-filled with rain already — no handbag, no ring on the left hand but the pale band of skin where one had been, recently. The shoe removed *after* death, or the ground would show a struggle. The hands folded *after* death, because dead hands do not fold themselves.

The boy came clattering up the causeway behind her, pushing his bicycle, and stopped ten feet away with his mouth open.

"Don't come closer," Freya said, without turning. "And don't touch anything."

"Is she — "

"Yes."

He made a small sound and turned his back, and Freya was grateful to him for it.

She looked at the dead woman a moment longer. The rain ran down the grey sleeve, her sleeve, and dripped from fingers that had never been hers. *You are a message,* Freya thought, and the thought arrived whole and terrible, the way convictions arrive, the way they had arrived all those years ago in interview rooms when a suspect's story finally broke along its fault lines. *You are a message, and you are addressed to me.*

She stood. Her knees complained — she was sixty-one and the ground had been wet — and she took the boy's arm and walked him back up the causeway a way, out of earshot of the body, though she could not have said why it mattered. Decency, perhaps. Or the older instinct: *contain the scene, contain the witness, contain yourself.*

"What's your name?"

"Tom. Tom Pascoe."

"Tom. I need you to go back to Fore Street and stay with the phone box until an officer arrives. Tell them Freya Eastwick found the body at" — she checked her watch, its face fogged — "at 6:14. Tell them exactly that. Can you do that?"

He nodded, too fast.

"And Tom — did you touch her? Before you came for me?"

"No. I saw the shoe and I — I didn't like it. I knew."

*You knew,* Freya thought. *Yes. Everyone knows that knowledge when it comes. It's the only inheritance the job ever gave me.*

She watched him go, the bicycle lights wobbling away into the dark, and then she stood alone on the marsh road with the rain coming down and the dead woman in her coat at her back, and she let herself think the thing she had been refusing to think since the torch beam found the grey wool.

The coat had gone missing more than a week ago. Which meant someone had been in her shop, or her rooms above it, taking their time, choosing. Choosing the coat. Choosing the scarf. Choosing, perhaps, her. And choosing this woman — this stranger with the ring's pale shadow on her finger — to wear them into the ground.

A car came along the causeway, headlights carving the rain, and Freya turned to face it with her heart going hard and slow, like something being carried up from a cellar.

* * *

Detective Inspector Sable Okafor arrived forty minutes later, which for a wet Thursday out here was practically a flying response, and Freya understood that the boy had told them exactly whose shop he'd run to. The unmarked car stopped with its lights still on. Okafor got out under an umbrella, took one look at Freya standing at the cordon tape she had improvised from the twine and two bamboo canes she kept in her bag — *old habits, Frey, honestly* — and closed her eyes for a moment as if praying for patience she did not expect to receive.

"Ma'am," she said.

"Don't ma'am me, Sable. You've three inches and twenty years on me."

"And you've got twine around a crime scene." Okafor came under the tape — under her own tape, Freya corrected herself, *this is not your tape* — and stood beside her, looking down at the body, and Freya watched the younger woman's face do what her own had done twenty minutes ago: the professional blankness arriving a half-second after the human flinch. "The doctor's twenty minutes out. SOCO behind him. The road's closed at both ends."

"Good."

"You found her at 6:14, according to a very wet young man who is currently being given tea by my sergeant and who will not stop saying your name."

"He was helpful."

"He was a witness, Freya, and he rode straight to the one retired DCI in the county with a — " Okafor stopped. She chose the next word with visible care. "With a history."

"With a history," Freya agreed. The rain drummed on the umbrella Okafor was holding over both of them now, an intimacy neither of them had offered. "Sable. Look at the coat."

"I'm looking at the coat."

"Look at it properly."

Okafor looked. Freya watched her look — the beam of the scene lamp they'd rigged, the water beading on the grey wool, the turned-up collar, the knot at the throat — and she watched the moment arrive in the other woman's face, the moment where recognition curdled. Okafor had been a constable in her twenties when they'd worked the Vane case together. She had stood in that parish house in 2004 and seen a woman laid out with her hands folded and one shoe set apart, and she had been young enough to believe the confession that followed had meant something clean.

"That's your coat," Okafor said quietly.

"It's my coat."

"And the scarf."

"Is my scarf. They went missing from the shop the week before last. I reported it to no one, because it was a coat." Freya heard her own voice go flat and precise and hated it a little, the way you hate a scar that shows. "Sable, I need to say this before the doctor gets here and before anyone else arrives to hear it, because once it's said in front of a room full of officers it becomes a different thing. Someone has dressed this woman in my clothes and laid her out the way Elspeth Vane was laid out. Hands folded. Shoe off. Back to the water. I have spent twenty years trying not to see that scene every time I close my eyes, and I am telling you as a witness and not as a colleague: this was done for me to find."

Okafor was silent. The rain filled the silence. Down the causeway, the second vehicle's lights swung and steadied.

"You understand," Okafor said at last, "how that sounds."

"I understand exactly how that sounds. It sounds like the woman who found the body knew too much about it. I'd think the same, if I were you. I did think the same, for about four seconds, standing right there." She nodded at the spot where she'd first stopped walking. "And then I remembered that I've been waiting twenty years for someone to stage a body in my coat on the marsh road, and that the waiting is the point. Whoever did this didn't want me to be a suspect. They wanted me to be an audience."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment. Under the umbrella, in the scene light, her face was very still, and Freya saw in it the young constable she had trained and the inspector she had become and the wall that had gone up between them somewhere in the years since — the wall Freya herself had built, stone by stone, with her silences.

"The vicar's coming," Okafor said finally. "Someone phoned the rectory. He's identifying her if he can."

"Then I'll know who she is in ten minutes, and you'll have your first thread."

"Freya."

"Yes."

"Go home. Please. Write down where you were all day, every hour, and go home, and don't come back to this road until I say so." Okafor's voice was gentle in a way that was worse than sharpness. "You know I have to ask you those hours. You know why."

"Because the coat's mine. Because the staging matches a case I worked. Because I found the body." Freya smiled, and felt the smile go wrong on her face. "Because I'm the obvious one. I've been the obvious one before, Sable. I know how it fits."

The headlights of the vicar's ancient Volvo came wobbling along the causeway, and Freya stepped back from the tape, back into the rain, out from under the umbrella, and the cold took her again, all the way through.

The vicar was a man she half knew, a soft-spoken Cornishman named Tregose who had come to the parish four years ago and who still said *good evening* to her as though the evening could be relied upon. He got out of the car with an umbrella he forgot to open, and Okafor met him, and Freya stood at the edge of the lamplight and watched him walk to the body with his head bowed as though he already knew what courtesy required of him.

He looked down at the dead woman for a long time. The rain fell on his bare head. When he straightened, he was holding on to the drystone wall.

"That's Gerald," he said. "That's Gerald Pym. Our parish clerk."

The name went through Freya like a splinter working toward the bone. *Pym.* She knew it, and it took her a moment, standing in the rain with her dead coat on a dead stranger, to find where she knew it from — not the man's face, which she had seen a hundred times at jumble sales and parish meetings without ever once seeing it, but the name itself, inked in a file, on a certificate, at the foot of a page.

Gerald Pym, notary. Gerald Pym, who had witnessed and stamped and sealed.

Gerald Pym, who had notarized the confession of Marcus Vane, twenty years and three weeks ago, in a back room of the parish house while a young detective named Freya Eastwick stood in the doorway believing every word of it.

The rain came down. The marsh hissed. And Freya Eastwick stood very still in the dark and understood, with the whole of her cold skin, that the message had been delivered, and that she had just begun — only just begun — to read it.
