## 2. Twenty Years of Quiet

The rain had settled into that grey, patient drizzle that the marsh seemed to manufacture out of its own breath, and Freya Eastwick stood at the edge of the police tape watching a young constable decide whether she was a witness, a mourner, or a problem.

He was very young — twenty-three, perhaps twenty-four — with rain beading on the brim of his hat and the particular stiffness of someone who had learned procedure but not yet weather. He kept glancing at her the way people glance at a dog of uncertain temperament.

"Ma'am, I'm going to have to ask you to step back behind the tape."

"I am behind the tape."

"Further back, ma'am."

She didn't move. The body lay forty feet down the road, on the verge where the tarmac gave up and became gravel and then became marsh, and even from here she could see the grey coat, the dark scarf wound once around the throat with the ends trailing like something spilled. Two SOCOs crouched over it under a portable canopy, their white suits ghostly against the black wet ground. A photographer's flash strobed silently, again and again, as though the road were being taught to remember itself.

"You'll want to wait for the DI," Freya said. "Sable Okafor. She's coming from Ashcombe; it's forty minutes if the ford road is open."

The constable blinked. "How do you—"

"Because I called it in, and because when there's a body on the marsh road in October, it's always Okafor." She said it mildly, and watched him decide not to ask how she knew. Twenty years ago he would not have had to ask. Twenty years ago her name would have been enough to part the tape like a curtain.

Now she was a woman in a wet cardigan standing in the rain outside a cordon, and the coat on the dead man was hers.

That was the thought she kept setting down and picking back up, the way one worries a loose tooth. *Her* coat. The grey wool one with the horn buttons, gone from the shop's back room sometime in September — she'd assumed mislaid, assumed donated by accident, assumed any of the small domestic failures a woman of fifty-eight permits herself rather than suspect the truth. And the scarf, the charcoal one with the frayed fringe that she'd worn through three winters and half intended to throw away. Both of them wrapped now around a stranger's corpse on the marsh road, arranged with a care that was worse than violence. Arranged like a sentence, punctuated correctly.

Not theft. Not coincidence. *Message.*

Freya had spent thirty years reading scenes the way other people read letters, and this one was addressed to her in her own handwriting.

Headlights swung up the road through the drizzle, and a dark Audi pulled onto the gravel with the precise economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times. Sable Okafor got out before the engine finished settling — tall, broad-shouldered, hair cropped close and going silver at the temples now, which was a fact Freya found herself unreasonably moved by. Time had been at work on all of them.

Okafor saw her immediately. Of course she did. Something crossed her face — not surprise, exactly. Recognition with a weight attached.

"Mrs. Eastwick." Not *Freya*. Not *ma'am*. The surname, formal as a summons, though her voice was quieter than the formality suggested. "You should be at home."

"The body's wearing my coat, Inspector. Home is where I keep the rest of my wardrobe, and I'd rather know what else of mine is about to turn up on the marsh."

A muscle worked in Okafor's jaw. Around them, the scene had gone subtly attentive — the young constable, the SOCOs, all of them listening without appearing to. They knew the name too. Of course they did. There were probably officers here tonight who had studied the Vane case at Hendon, who knew exactly whose coat that was and what it meant that it was here.

Okafor stepped closer, lowering her voice beneath the rain. "Twenty-two years," she said. "I worked under you for two of them, and in twenty since, you have never once come to one of my scenes. Not when it was your neighbour. Not when it was the Hartley boy. So don't stand there and tell me this is about a coat."

"It is about a coat."

"It's about *you*." Okafor's eyes went past her shoulder, down the road, to the grey shape under the canopy, and Freya watched her colleague — her officer, once, her protégé in everything but name — do the arithmetic every detective does when a familiar object turns up on a stranger: *who benefits, who suffers, who is positioned.* When the arithmetic finished, Okafor's face closed like a door.

"I can't have you inside the tape," she said. "You understand that. You more than anyone understands that."

"I understand it perfectly."

"But—" and here Okafor exhaled through her nose, a sound of surrender already resented, "—I can't stop you standing on a public road in the rain, either. And I would rather you were where I could see you than where I couldn't." She held up a finger before Freya could speak. "This is not an invitation. This is containment. Do you hear the difference?"

"I hear it," Freya said. "It's the same difference it always was."

Something flickered then, briefly, in Okafor's eyes — the old warmth, or the old wound, hard to say which — and then she turned and ducked under the tape, and Freya was alone with the rain and the road and the slow strobe of the camera, watching her own coat rise and fall on a dead man's chest in the wind off the water.

* * *

They brought the tent eventually, because the tide table said the marsh would take the road's edge within three hours and no one wanted the sea for a witness. Freya stayed where she was. She had learned, long ago, that the most valuable position at any scene was the one nobody thought to move you from.

Through the gap in the canvas she caught fragments. The pathologist's gloved hands lifting the scarf away. The murmur — *ligature, post-mortem interval, no wallet, no phone* — each phrase landing on her like stones dropped into still water. No wallet. No phone. A man walking the marsh road at night stripped of everything identifying except the clothes on his back, and the clothes on his back belonged to her.

At some point Okafor came out and stood beside her, not looking at her, both of them watching the tent.

"Pym," Okafor said. "Gerald Pym. Parish clerk. Sixty-one. Lived alone above the old bakery on Fen Street."

The name went through Freya like a fishhook finding flesh. She kept her face still, but Okafor felt it anyway — twenty years of reading her had left its own record.

"You knew him."

"Everyone knows Gerald Pym. He's been the parish clerk since Moses." She made herself say it lightly. "He took the minutes. He filed things. He was the sort of man who remembered which charity the flower fund belonged to in 1998."

"Mm." Okafor turned her head slightly. Rain ran off the brim of her hat between them. "His sister's been called. There'll be a formal ID in the morning. Cause of death looks like strangulation — ligature, consistent with the scarf, but we'll wait for the post-mortem before I say that twice." She paused. "There's something else you should hear from me before you hear it from the village, because the village will have it by breakfast."

Freya waited.

"He was found facing the church," Okafor said. "Laid out. Not dumped — laid. Arms at his sides, feet together, coat buttoned to the top. The first officer on scene thought at first he was asleep."

The marsh wind came across the flats and Freya felt it go through her, under the cardigan, under the skin, all the way into the place where 2004 still lived like a splinter the body had never managed to push out. Because she knew that arrangement. She had stood over Elspeth Vane in the parish house twenty-two years ago and seen a body composed with the same terrible tidiness — hands folded, face turned toward the window, dressed with a care that no killer in haste could manage. She had written it up, in her younger voice, as *ritualistic presentation, significance unknown*, and the phrase had followed her out of the job like a dog.

"You're thinking what I'm thinking," Okafor said quietly.

"I'm trying very hard not to."

"Well, stop trying. Say it, so I know we're looking at the same thing."

Freya looked down the road at the tent, at the shape beneath it wearing her grey coat buttoned to the throat, laid out with liturgical neatness on the verge like an offering.

"It's the Vane staging," she said. "Or close enough that whoever did it either studied the file or was there."

Okafor was silent for a long moment. The rain ticked on the tent canvas. Somewhere out on the flats a curlew cried, that rising, falling note like a question asked of nothing.

"And Marcus Vane," Okafor said at last, carefully, "has been in Full Sutton for twenty-two years."

"Yes."

"So either he's found himself a very long arm, or—"

"Or someone is telling me the case isn't finished." Freya heard her own voice go flat and cold, the working voice, the one she hadn't used in two decades, and hated how easily it came back. Like a bicycle. Like sin.

Okafor looked at her fully then, and whatever she saw made her shake her head slowly. "No. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to pretend I never said it. If this is a message, then the person sending it wants *you*. Wants you frightened, or wants you involved, or wants you standing exactly where you're standing right now — wet, exposed, and looking guilty as hell to anyone who doesn't know you." Her jaw tightened. "And I've known you twenty-two years, Mrs. Eastwick, so I know you're going to ignore every word of that. But I'm asking. As the girl whose paperwork you corrected in green ink until three in the morning. Stay out of it. Let me carry this one."

The green ink. Freya almost smiled, and the smile hurt her face like frost. Sable Okafor at twenty-six, ferocious and meticulous, sleeping at her desk during the Vane trial because she couldn't bear to miss a day of it. Believing — they had all believed — that justice was a machine that worked if you fed it honestly.

"We fed it honestly," Freya said aloud, before she meant to.

"What?"

"Nothing." She turned up her collar. "Thank you for letting me stay, Inspector. I'll be at home. Reachable."

"That," said Okafor grimly, "is what worries me."

* * *

The walk back along the lane took twelve minutes and cost her more than the evening had. By the time Freya reached the dark windows of Eastwick's Books — *EST. 1987* in gold leaf flaking off the glass, the window display of autumn novels gone soft-edged with damp — the village lights of Thurlmere had come on one by one behind her, and she could feel the story moving through them faster than the rain. *A body on the marsh road. Wearing her coat. Wearing Eastwick's coat.*

By morning it would be everywhere. By morning she would be either the target or the suspect, and the village had never been good at telling those apart.

She let herself in the front, put on the kettle out of pure habit, and stood in the kitchen not drinking the tea while the events of the night rearranged themselves into questions.

Gerald Pym. She turned the name over. She had known him forty years — he'd been a boy sweeping the vestry when she was a girl sitting in the third pew with her mother. A dry, careful, invisible man. The sort of man who knew where everything was filed and told no one. The sort of man who, if you needed a document witnessed, a signature certified, a confession—

The kettle clicked off. Freya set her cup down very slowly on the counter, because her hand had developed an opinion of its own.

*A confession.*

Twenty-two years ago, in the back room of the parish office, a broken man named Marcus Vane had signed his name to the killing of his wife, and Freya Eastwick had sat across from him and believed she was witnessing justice. Someone had notarized that confession. Someone with a stamp and a ledger and a clerk's incurious competence. She had never thought about who. It had never once, in twenty-two years, occurred to her to wonder whose stamp sat beneath Marcus Vane's signature.

And now that man was dead on the marsh road, dressed in her clothes, laid out facing St. Brannoc's with his arms at his sides like a man prepared for burial.

Coincidence was a word Freya had spent a career learning to distrust, and it fit this like the coat fit Pym: closely enough to pass, wrong in every seam.

She carried her tea upstairs to the flat, meaning to sleep, and instead stood a long while at the window looking down into the yard behind the shop, at the back door with its frosted pane, at the narrow strip of gravel between the door and the wall where the bins stood.

Three weeks ago — she dated it now, precisely, the way she used to date things — she had noticed the door sticking. Not broken. Sticking. The latch catching a half-second longer than it should, and she had oiled it and thought nothing further, because the shop was failing and her hip ached and there had been no reason, then, to look closer.

Tonight there was a reason. Tonight her coat was forty feet of wet tarmac away from a murdered man's face.

Freya set the tea down untouched and went downstairs, past the dark shelves, into the back room where the spare rail stood half-empty and the coat hangers swung faintly in some draught she couldn't locate. She tried the back door. The latch caught, hesitated, gave — that same half-second hitch, oil or no oil.

Someone had been in her house. Someone had stood in this room, among her coats and scarves, choosing — and had left the door to look exactly as it always looked, and had walked out into the ordinary Tuesday world carrying pieces of her life away like relics.

She stood in the dark with her hand on the cold latch, and felt the thing she had buried in 2004 turn over in its grave and open one eye.

Not fear. That was the terrible discovery of it. Underneath the violation, underneath the dread, something older and hungrier had woken and was already asking the only question that mattered:

*What did Gerald Pym know?*
