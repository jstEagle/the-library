## 14. What Freya Buried

The rain had stopped by the time Freya reached the sea wall, but the wind hadn't. It came off the marsh in long grey pushes, flattening the reeds, carrying the smell of cold mud and old salt — the smell of the road where Gerald Pym had died wearing her coat. She stood at the rail with her hands jammed in her pockets and watched the tide come in over the flats, patient as a debt collector.

Okafor found her there at half past seven. Freya heard the car door before she saw the headlights swing off the lane; Sable always parked with her nose toward the water, an old habit from stakeouts they'd shared when Freya still wore a warrant card and Sable still called her ma'am without irony.

"You shouldn't be out here alone," Okafor said, coming to stand beside her. She didn't look at Freya. She looked at the marsh, the way coppers look at things they don't trust yet.

"Neither should you. You've been up forty hours."

"I sleep in the car now." A pause. "Maren's awake, by the way. Talking. Slowly. She remembers a smell — beeswax and pipe smoke."

Freya's stomach turned over, slow and deliberate, like something rolling in its grave. Beeswax and pipe smoke. She kept her face still. "That's not much."

"It's more than we had this morning." Okafor finally turned her head. In the dark her eyes were unreadable, which was itself a message. "You know who smells like that."

Freya said nothing. The tide slid forward another few feet, swallowing a sandbar that had stood all day.

"You asked me to meet you," Okafor said. "Off the record. I want to know why, because right now everything about you is on the record, Freya. Your coat on a dead man. Your window display on Maren. Your fingerprints on Pym's archive request — yes, we pulled the county records too. And your history with the Vane case, which reads like motive if a lawyer squints hard enough." Her voice stayed level, but underneath it Freya could hear the strain of a woman holding two loyalties apart like live wires. "So talk. Because I can't protect you from what the evidence says. Nobody can."

"That's why I asked you here." Freya turned from the rail. "Because it's time someone heard what I buried."

* * *

She didn't tell it standing up. She told it in the car, parked behind the closed fishmonger's where no one would pass, with the heater ticking and the windows fogging until the world outside was only a smear of streetlight. It felt right, telling it in a car. Confessions lived in cars. Twenty years ago she'd sat in a car very like this one, outside the parish house at Wetherell St. Cuthbert's, listening to a man cry through an open window, and she had written down none of what she heard.

"Start anywhere," Okafor said. "Start wherever it's hardest."

Freya almost laughed. "It's all hard. That's the thing nobody tells you about a lie you helped build. It doesn't have one weak point. It's strong everywhere, like bone."

She looked down at her hands. They were older hands than the ones that had held the file in 2004, spotted now, the knuckles thickened, but they remembered the weight of it. *Operation Ledger. The Vane Inquiry.* Forty pounds of paper in a buff box with a red stripe.

"In March of 2004 I was thirty-four years old," she began. "Six years in CID, first big case as acting detective inspector. Elspeth Vane was found strangled in the parish house on the fourth of March. Marcus was arrested within seventy-two hours. He confessed on the ninth." She recited the dates the way other people recited psalms — automatically, and with something that wasn't quite faith. "Crane ran the inquiry. He'd been my mentor since I was a probationer. He taught me how to read a room, how to hold silence, how to make a man feel that telling the truth was the only kind thing left anyone could do for him. He was the best interviewer I ever saw. Better than me. I used to watch him work and think, *that's what I want to be when I'm good.*"

"And?"

"And on the eighth of March, the night before the confession, I noticed three things." Freya held up fingers, an old habit from briefings, absurd in the dark of the car. "One. Marcus Vane's hands. Strangulation leaves marks on the attacker too — fingernail crescents, defensive bruising. Elspeth had fought; the pathologist was clear about that. Marcus had no marks at all. Not one. Crane explained it: he'd strangled her from behind while she slept, she'd never woken properly. Except the lividity and the disturbance in the room said she'd fought, and fought hard, and gotten partway up. You don't fight like that against a man who walks away unmarked."

Okafor had gone very still. "Two?"

"Two. The timeline. Marcus claimed — in his own account, before he confessed — that he'd been at the boatyard until eleven. There was a witness who half-remembered his van. Crane leaned on that witness until the memory firmed up. But the van's logbook put him home by ten-fifteen, and Elspeth's neighbour heard the parish house gate at ten-thirty, and heard *two* sets of footsteps going in. Two. Marcus's statement mentioned one visitor. When I raised it, Crane said the neighbour was elderly and unreliable. He was right that she was elderly. He was wrong that she was unreliable. I checked her later — she'd been a schoolteacher for thirty years. Sharp as a tack."

The heater ticked. Somewhere out on the marsh a curlew cried, one long falling note, like a question nobody answered.

"Three," Freya said, and her voice dropped, because this was the one that had lived under her ribs for twenty years like a swallowed stone. "The confession itself. I transcribed part of it by hand — we did that then, for accuracy. And in the middle of it, Marcus describes coming through the parish house kitchen. He says he took off his boots because Elspeth hated mud on the flags. It's a tender detail. It sounds true. But the boots he was wearing that night were seized at arrest, and there was no mud on them, because it hadn't rained that week. He described mud that didn't exist. Do you understand? He was describing a night that wasn't his. Someone had fed him the shape of it, and he'd filled it in with whatever kindnesses he could remember, trying to be *helpful*, trying to give us what we wanted, because he thought — God help him — he thought cooperating was the way home to his daughter."

Okafor exhaled slowly through her nose. "And you took these three things to Crane."

"I took them to Crane." Freya closed her eyes. The car interior glowed red through her lids. "I laid it out exactly as I've laid it out for you. The hands. The footsteps. The mud. I remember standing in his office with my notebook open like a schoolgirl presenting homework. And Aldous Crane listened to every word, nodded gravely, thanked me for my thoroughness — he actually said *thoroughness*, he praised me — and then he closed my notebook with one hand, gently, the way you'd close a child's eyes at a funeral, and he said: 'Freya. This man confessed. The parish needs this to be finished. His wife is dead and his daughter is watching us decide whether her father is a monster. Sometimes the job isn't finding the truth. Sometimes the job is choosing which truth a community can survive.'"

"He said that," Okafor repeated flatly.

"Word for word. I've carried it word for word for twenty years. And then he said the part that worked, the part I'm ashamed of. He said, 'You're going to be brilliant, and brilliant officers don't die on hills like this one.' And he smiled at me. And I—" Her breath caught. She made herself go on. "I went back to my desk and I wrote my report confirming the confession was voluntary, coherent, and consistent with the evidence. Consistent. I wrote the word *consistent* knowing about the mud."

The fog on the windscreen had begun to run in slow rivulets, the world outside dissolving. Freya watched the drops instead of Okafor. It was easier.

"People will say I was naive," she said. "I wasn't. Naive officers ask questions and get answers. I knew what I was looking at. I knew Crane was building something. What I told myself was that it wasn't *my* case, that the confession was Marcus's responsibility, that a man doesn't confess to murder he didn't commit unless there's something wrong with *him*. I told myself the jury would see the cracks even if I didn't point at them. I told myself a hundred tidy little stories, and every single one of them had the same purpose: to let me keep my career, my reputation, my place at Crane's right hand. Because he'd promised me promotion. And because — this is the worst of it, Sable, worse than the ambition — because I loved him. Not romantically. The way you love a father who chose you. He picked me out of a room full of probationers and said, *this one has ears.* Twenty years I've known what I traded for that, and twenty years I've told myself the trade was done, sealed, past saving. Marcus got fourteen years. He served every one of them, and then he died in a cell in April, and the trade stayed sealed."

"But it isn't sealed now," Okafor said quietly. "Pym opened it."

"Pym opened it." Freya laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "A parish clerk with a stamp and a conscience. He notarized that confession, Sable. He sat in the room. And twenty years later he pulls the file from county archives, and he starts asking questions, and within weeks he's dead on the marsh road wearing my coat — dressed up as Elspeth Vane, do you see it? The staging isn't a taunt aimed at me. It's a *rehearsal*. Whoever killed Pym is showing me they know exactly how the original was done. Down to the details that never made the papers. Details only someone inside that inquiry would know."

Okafor was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — flatter, more careful, the voice of a officer stepping onto ice and testing each stride. "You're saying the person who staged Pym was in the room in 2004."

"I'm saying the person who staged Pym knows things only four people alive could know. Me. The pathologist, who's dead. Marcus, who's dead. And Aldous Crane."

The name hung in the car between them, and Freya watched Okafor's face do what faces do when a load-bearing wall shifts: nothing visible, everything structural.

"You understand what you've just handed me," Okafor said at last. "If you'd raised those anomalies in 2004 and been silenced, fine — that's a grievance, that's history. But you kept silent for twenty years, and now the case has reopened around you, and the first person you accuse is the man who promoted you. Any defence barrister in the county will say you invented all of it tonight to redirect suspicion. Your word against a decorated retired inspector's."

"I know."

"So I need more than your word. I need the anomalies documented somewhere dated before tonight. I need the 2004 materials — originals, not your memories of them. And I need you to understand that if you're lying to me, I will not be able to save you twice."

"I'm not lying." Freya turned to look at her directly for the first time since the story began. "I lied for twenty years by omission. I'm done paying that interest. Ask me anything. Check everything. That's the only apology I've got that's worth anything — verification."

Okafor studied her for what felt like a full minute. Then she nodded once, a small motion, a door opening rather than a decision announced. "The 2004 interview recordings should still exist. Magnetic masters, county archive annex, pre-digital retention schedule. If there's a second voice coaching Vane—"

"There is," Freya said. "I've already requested access. I'll hear it tomorrow morning, nine o'clock, supervised viewing, signed log." She hesitated, and then gave Okafor the last piece, the one she'd been holding back like a card played late. "There's one more thing. Something I found today, going back through the estate paperwork Pym requested. Elspeth's fortune didn't pass to Marcus. It went into a trust — controlled by parish trustees, paying out to St. Brannoc's restoration fund. Four trustees named in the 2004 instrument. Three are identifiable. Julian Vane. The diocesan registrar. And Elspeth's father's old solicitor, deceased. The fourth trustee is listed only by initials and appointment date."

Okafor's eyes narrowed. "What initials?"

"A.C." Freya said. "Appointed 1989, by Elspeth's father personally. Still active on the trust as of last year's filings."

Outside, the tide had finished its work; the marsh was sea now, seamless and indifferent. Somewhere across the black water, in a warm house smelling of beeswax and pipe smoke, a man was presumably sleeping soundly, the way men sleep who have chosen which truth a community can survive.

"Tomorrow at nine," Freya said, "I'm going to hear his voice on a tape from 2004, teaching a frightened man to describe mud that never fell. And then, Sable — then I'm going to find out what a trustee gets paid."
