## 16. Nell Vanishes

The phone rang six times before Freya gave up. She stood in the back room of the bookshop with the receiver still warm against her ear and the dial tone humming like a wasp trapped in glass, and she told herself what she had been telling herself since yesterday morning: Nell Vane was seventy-one years old, stubborn as a gate hinge, and perfectly capable of letting a phone ring itself hoarse.

She dialed again anyway.

Seven times. Eight. On the ninth, the line didn't ring at all — it simply clicked open onto a silence that had texture to it, the dead-air silence of a handset lifted from its cradle and left dangling, or of a line that had been cut at the pole. Freya listened to that nothing for a long moment, her thumb pressed white against the phone's plastic ridge, and then she set the receiver down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might go off.

*Don't*, said the sensible voice in her head, the voice that had kept her alive through twenty years of quiet. *You are a bookseller. You are sixty-three years old. There is a detective inspector whose number you have, and there is a procedure—*

But the sensible voice had been losing ground all week, drowned out by the other one, the younger one, the one that used to walk into rooms where bad things had happened and feel — shamefully, unforgivably — awake.

She took her coat from the hook. Not the grey one; that coat was in an evidence bag somewhere, tagged and photographed, the ghost of herself worn by a dead man on a marsh road. She took the brown tweed, and she drove out along the estuary road toward Nell's cottage with the wipers beating their tired rhythm and the fog coming off the water in slow grey tongues.

* * *

Nell's cottage sat at the end of a lane that turned to mud after the second bend, a low stone house with a lean-to greenhouse and a garden gone beautifully wild, the kind of garden that announced its owner had better things to do than tame it. Freya had visited twice now. Both times there had been smoke from the chimney, a radio going inside, and Nell herself in the doorway before the knock landed, arms folded, eyes sharp as a gull's, already deciding whether she'd let you in.

Today there was no smoke. The radio was silent. The door stood closed but not locked — it swung inward at Freya's touch with a soft complaint of hinges, and the smell that came out to meet her was cold tea and wet wool and something else underneath, faint but unmistakable: fear has no scent, exactly, but a house where someone has left in a hurry smells like a held breath finally released.

"Nell?" Freya called it into the dimness, though she already knew. "It's Freya Eastwick."

Nothing answered her but the tick of a clock in the front room, running faithfully on with nobody to hear it.

She went through the cottage the way she'd once gone through a hundred rooms like it, hands in her pockets, touching nothing, reading everything. The kettle sat on the hob, full and untouched. A mug of tea on the kitchen table had gone cold beside a half-eaten slice of bread, the butter knife laid across the plate at an angle — set down mid-meal, not finished and cleared. In the front room, Nell's reading glasses lay folded on the arm of her chair atop a library book, bookmark jutting out two-thirds through. Nobody abandoned a book two-thirds through unless they meant to come straight back.

And nobody left through the back door without their coat. The pegs by the scullery held Nell's oilskins, her cardigans, the heavy wool coat she wore eleven months of the year. All of them still hanging. On the floor beneath them, one shoe — a single brown lace-up, tipped on its side.

Freya crouched over it for a long time. Her breath made small ghosts in the unheated air.

There were other things, subtler. A drawer in the dresser pulled out further than its neighbors, its contents — string, batteries, rubber bands, the archaeology of a careful woman's junk drawer — disturbed, rifled. The little desk by the window where Nell kept her papers: the drawer there hung half-open too, and when Freya eased it fully out with a pencil through the handle, she found it near empty. No letters. No bank book. No photographs. A woman who kept every scrap of paper for forty years, and her desk was stripped clean.

Or someone had stripped it for her.

Freya stood in the middle of the cold front room and let the shape of it assemble itself, piece by piece, the way a negative develops in its bath. Nell had telephoned her three days ago, breathless, triumphant, whispering as if the walls had ears — *I've got his letter, Freya. Marcus wrote me before the end. He named who came to Elspeth that night. I haven't shown anyone. I wanted you to see it first.* And then, when Freya had driven out, Nell had produced the letter with trembling ceremony — four pages in a prison clerk's typewritten hand, or rather three pages and a ruin, because the fourth page had been torn across, raggedly, the paragraph that mattered amputated at the fold. *He names him,* Nell had said, pressing the torn edge flat against the table as if she could make the missing words grow back. *He names him right here, and someone's been at this letter, Freya. Someone got to it before I did.*

Someone had gotten to it. Someone who knew what page four said, even without reading it — because you don't need to read a sentence to know that tearing it out is safer than leaving it whole.

And now Nell's desk was stripped, her tea abandoned, one shoe on the floor, and her telephone either dangling or dead.

Freya walked slowly around the outside of the cottage, her boots sinking in the wet grass, and that was where she found the thing that stopped her heart for one full beat. The gravel of the turning circle at the lane's end held tire prints, recent, softened at their edges by last night's drizzle but not erased by it — wide tires, heavy car, the tread pattern crisp and expensive. And beside the gate, half-hidden in the hedge shadow, a cigarette end. Crushed neatly underfoot, filter intact, not weathered. Crane had smoked a pipe for thirty years and given it up for cigarettes when the doctors frightened him, and he had always, always crushed them out with that same precise downward twist, tidy even in vice, tidy even in murder.

He had stood at her gate. He had waited, perhaps, watching the lit windows. Or he hadn't needed to wait at all.

Freya got back in her car and sat with her hands on the wheel and made herself think it through to the end, because the end was the only place the truth lived. Marcus's letter had named the man who visited Elspeth Vane on the night she died. The torn page had gone missing before Nell ever showed it to Freya — which meant whoever tore it knew the letter existed, knew where it lived, and had access. And now the letter's keeper had vanished from a locked-and-unlocked house between one cup of tea and the next, leaving her glasses on her chair and her coat on its peg, and Aldous Crane's cigarette lay crushed by her gate like a signature.

Nell had told him. That was the arithmetic Freya couldn't escape. Somewhere between showing Freya the mutilated letter and this morning, Nell had done the brave, foolish, loyal thing she had probably been doing all her life for her brother: she had confronted him. Or written to him. Or telephoned him and said, in that flinty Vane voice, *I know what you did.* And Crane — patient, charming, twenty years practiced at making problems disappear — had collected her the way he'd collect a file from an archive.

The only living witness to the truth was in the killer's hands.

Freya started the engine. Her hands were shaking so badly she had to grip the wheel and wait, counting breaths, until they steadied enough to drive.

* * *

Okafor picked up on the second ring, and Freya talked fast and low, pacing the narrow length of the bookshop's back room while rain lashed the window above the sink. She gave her everything: the cold tea, the single shoe, the stripped drawers, the tire treads, the cigarette. When she finished, there was a silence on the line long enough that she checked whether the call had dropped.

"You touched the drawers," Okafor said at last. It wasn't quite an accusation. It was worse — it was weariness.

"I opened one. With a pencil. The rest I looked at."

"Freya." A breath. "If she's been taken, that cottage is a crime scene, and you've walked your size-seven boots through it."

"I know what I've done. Do you know what's happened? Because from where I'm standing, a witness is missing and your lot is still measuring footprints on a marsh road."

Another silence, and Freya could hear Okafor deciding something, the way you hear a lock turn. "I can't treat it as a crime yet," Okafor said. "A seventy-year-old woman missing less than a day, no body, no forced entry, family history of going off to stay with cousins without telling anyone — I'll get it flagged, I'll put a circulation on her, but if I throw resources at it and she walks in tomorrow wanting her tea, we both look like fools and you lose whatever standing you've got left with this force. Which is approximately none."

"She didn't take her coat, Sable. It's October. She didn't take her glasses or her bloody book."

"I hear you." A pause, and when Okafor spoke again her voice had dropped into the register she used for things she shouldn't be saying. "Off the record, then. Tell me about the car."

"Wide tires. Heavy. Expensive tread — I'd say a large saloon, German, kept well."

"Crane drives a black Mercedes S-Class. It's in the traffic camera log twice on the coast road yesterday evening, heading east out of the parish at 6:40 and returning west at 9:15." A beat. "That's also in the log, incidentally, for the night Gerald Pym died. I haven't told my superintendent that part yet, because when I do, I'll need to be very sure of what I'm telling him."

Freya closed her eyes. "You're saying he has an alibi-shaped problem and you're sitting on it."

"I'm saying I'm a careful officer." The dryness in Okafor's voice could have preserved fruit. "Freya — listen to me. If Crane took Nell Vane, he took her because she was about to give that letter to someone official. Which means the next name on his list is the person she'd give it to. Think about who that is."

The line hummed. Rain drummed. Freya stood very still in her own back room and felt the thought arrive like cold water finding the crack in a wall.

"There isn't another name," she said quietly. "There's only me."

"Then don't be alone, don't be predictable, and for God's sake don't go looking for her yourself. If she surfaces — if he makes contact, if you find so much as a note — you call me first. Before you act. Promise me that much."

Freya promised. She was almost surprised to discover she meant it.

* * *

She did not go home. Home was predictable; home was a ground-floor flat with a door Crane had already forced once, weeks ago, when he'd wanted a grey coat and a scarf. Instead she drove to the Anchor & Hope on the quay, took a room over the pub under her own name because hiding would only flatter him, and sat by the window with a whisky she didn't drink, watching the rain stitch silver holes in the black harbor water.

It was there, in the small hours, that the memory surfaced — the one she'd been refusing all week, rising now with the inevitability of a body coming up when the river is done with it.

Three days ago, in Nell's kitchen, holding the torn letter. Nell had watched her read it, and when Freya reached the ragged edge of page three and looked up, Nell had been crying — not the dramatic grief of a funeral but the exhausted crying of a woman who has been carrying something alone for a very long time. And she had said, wiping her face with the heel of her hand: *"I rang him, you know. After Marcus died. I rang him and asked him straight out — did you go to Elspeth that night? And he laughed at me, Freya. He was kind about it, the way he is, he called me 'my dear' and said grief makes us invent monsters. But he rang back an hour later. He never rings back. And he asked me — very gently — whether Marcus had left any papers. Any letters. Whether I'd found anything among his effects."*

Freya had heard it then and filed it, the way she'd once filed a hundred wrong details in a hundred tidy reports. Now, in the dark over the harbor, she took it out and understood it. Crane hadn't learned about the letter afterward. He had known it might exist before Nell did — had gone looking for it within the hour of hearing her ask her question, because a guilty man hears an accusation and immediately calculates what evidence it implies. He had found the letter, or found where it lived, and torn out the page that named him. And Nell — loyal, flinty, grieving Nell — had spent three days deciding what to do about it, and had decided, in the end, to tell him what she thought of him.

To his face, probably. At his door, or on the telephone, in that voice that had made grown policemen shift in their chairs.

*Oh, Nell,* Freya thought, and the whisky sat untouched, and the rain came down, and she understood with terrible clarity that she was not searching for a missing person anymore. She was racing a man who had killed twice to protect a lie and had just discovered that the lie had a witness left alive — a witness he couldn't afford to kill carelessly, not yet, not until he knew what she'd already passed on and to whom. Which meant Nell was alive. For now, Nell was alive precisely because Crane needed to know how much damage she'd done.

Which meant there might be a bargain to be interrupted. A place to be found. A window measured in hours.

Freya took out her notebook — the cheap spiral one, not the old leather one, that had gone into evidence with everything else — and wrote down what she knew of Crane's geography. The house above the creek at Trelowarth, all glass and cedar, bought with money no inspector's pension explained. The sailing club where he kept his boat. The restoration works at St. Brannoc's, where he chaired the fund with the serene authority of a churchwarden. And beneath St. Brannoc's, the crypt — closed this week, scaffolded and floodlit for the restoration crew, the Vane vault somewhere in its oldest corner, waiting.

Elspeth's private papers had reportedly been interred with her. Everyone said so; no one had ever looked.

Crane would be moving Nell somewhere he controlled. Somewhere private, defensible, his. And there was one such place in this parish that belonged to him more completely than his own house — a place where the tide came in twice a day through channels only he understood, where the restoration had given him keys and schedules and a legitimate reason to be seen coming and going at any hour, and where, if a second grave proved necessary, the ground had already been consecrated.

Freya looked at the clock behind the bar. Half past two. The tide tables were pinned by the pub door, as they'd been pinned in every pub on this coast for two hundred years, and she crossed the room and read them with her heart going like a hammer.

High water at St. Brannoc's creek: 7:40 tomorrow morning.

She had five hours to find out whether Nell Vane was lying in a flooded crypt beneath a church — and if she was, Freya would have to go in after her alone, because there was no one left in this story she could trust to follow her down except the one person she'd sworn to call first.

She took out her phone. Okafor's number glowed on the screen.

Freya stared at it for a long moment, thumb hovering — and then, outside, at the edge of the harbor lights, a black saloon slid slowly past the pub windows, unhurried, its headlights doused, moving through the rain like a shark through dark water.
