## 7. The House on Wren Lane

The house on Wren Lane was the kind of house that pretended it had nothing to hide.

Ansel had learned long ago that the most dangerous buildings were the ones that performed innocence — white clapboard, blue shutters, a garden gone slightly shaggy with neglect, as if grief itself had been watering it. Judge Eleanor Voss's home sat at the end of a cul-de-sac like a retired schoolteacher: respectable, quiet, and full of secrets it intended to take to the grave.

Which, Ansel thought, standing on the sidewalk with her hands in her coat pockets, it very nearly had.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money," Theo said beside her. He'd insisted on coming, which had surprised her until she understood why. He was twenty-three and carried himself like a man who had already survived several lifetimes; he also could not bear to let her walk into anything alone. "Also, we've been standing here for four minutes. That's how you get reported to neighborhood watch."

"We're not doing anything wrong yet."

"Yet," Theo repeated, savoring it. "That's my favorite word of yours."

The estate was sealed for probate — Ansel had confirmed this twice, once through the county records office and once through a florist who still delivered weekly arrangements to the porch, because apparently even in death, Judge Voss received lilies she couldn't smell. A yellow notice from the probate court was taped to the front door, curling at one corner, warning that entry by unauthorized persons constituted trespass.

Ansel read it the way she read everything: not for what it said, but for what it revealed. The tape was fresh. The tape meant someone official came by regularly. And the porch light — she tilted her head — the porch light was on a timer set for evening, which meant someone was paying the electric bill, which meant someone was *tending* this house.

Houses under probate were abandoned houses. This one was being kept alive.

"All right," she said. "Here's the plan."

"There's a plan?" Theo brightened. "I assumed we were going to stand here until the house confessed."

"The plan is that I'm going to knock on the back door, and I'm going to be honest."

Theo blinked. "Honest."

"Honesty is the best disguise," Ansel said. "Nobody believes an honest person is up to something. It's the liars who get searched."

"That is deeply cynical and I love it."

* * *

The back door belonged to a kitchen that smelled faintly of lemon oil and old roses. Ansel knocked three times, arranged her face into its most disarming configuration — apologetic, competent, harmless — and waited.

Footsteps. Slow ones, deliberate, the tread of someone who had decided years ago never to hurry for anyone.

The door opened on a small woman in her sixties with iron-gray hair pinned back so severely it looked structural, wearing a cardigan the color of oatmeal and an expression that suggested Ansel had interrupted something important, possibly a funeral, possibly a murder.

"Yes?"

"Mrs. Adeyemi?" Ansel said. "My name is Ansel Lockridge. I'm the concierge at the Grand Meridian Hotel."

Something flickered across the woman's face — quick, quickly buried. Recognition? Wariness? Ansel catalogued it and moved on.

"I knew Judge Voss," Ansel continued. "Not well. She stayed with us twice last month. I didn't know who she was at the time — she used another name — but I remember her. And since she died, I've been trying to understand something, and the answer might be in this house."

Mrs. Adeyemi's eyes narrowed to the width of a coin slot. "The house is sealed. There's a notice."

"I read the notice. It's very clear. That's why I knocked instead of letting my friend pick the lock." She tipped her head toward Theo, who gave a small wave and then, catching Mrs. Adeyemi's expression, un-waved.

"And what," Mrs. Adeyemi said, "is it you're trying to understand?"

And there it was — the question Ansel had rehearsed eleven versions of on the drive over. The truth, she had decided, was the only version that would work on this woman, because Mrs. Adeyemi had the look of someone who had spent decades reading judges and would smell a fabrication the way other people smelled smoke.

"A letter," Ansel said. "Someone sent me a letter claiming the judge's death wasn't natural causes. It knew things that were never made public. I don't know if it's true. But I keep thinking about her staying at our hotel under a false name, a month before she drowned in her own bathtub, and I can't make myself stop asking questions. I'm sorry. I know I have no right to be here."

Silence. The garden ticked in the breeze. Somewhere down the street a dog offered one professional bark and thought better of it.

Mrs. Adeyemi studied her for a long moment, and Ansel had the distinct sensation of being measured against some internal standard — weighed, perhaps, against every reporter, every relative, every cop who had come to this door wanting something.

"You said she used another name," Mrs. Adeyemi said at last.

"She did. I won't say what it was. If she chose it, she chose it for a reason, and I don't think reasons stop mattering when people die."

The housekeeper's mouth did something complicated — not quite a smile, more like a door opening a crack. "She called me the week before," Mrs. Adeyemi said. "Told me she'd been somewhere she hadn't been in thirty years, and nobody had known her. Said it was the freest she'd felt since law school." Her voice caught, just barely, and she smoothed it flat with visible effort. "You may come in. Ten minutes. You do not touch anything, you do not open drawers, and you—" this to Theo, "—do not put your feet on anything, including floors you think are clean."

"Ma'am, I'll hover," Theo promised solemnly.

* * *

Inside, the house was a museum of a life that had ended mid-sentence.

Ansel had been in enough homes of the newly dead to know the two kinds: the ones stripped bare by relatives within the week, and the ones left exactly as they were, like stage sets waiting for an actor who wouldn't return. This was the second kind. A teacup sat on the side table in the parlor, half-full, a skin forming on the cold tea. Reading glasses rested on top of a newspaper folded to the crossword — unfinished, seven squares blank. A cardigan hung over the arm of the reading chair, sleeves drooping, patient as a dog.

"She liked to do the crossword in pen," Mrs. Adeyemi said, following their gaze. "Never pencil. She said changing your mind should cost you something."

Ansel felt something tighten in her chest. In the hotel, the judge — under whatever name she'd traveled — had taken her coffee black and done the lobby crossword in pen too, and Ansel had noticed it, filed it away, thought nothing of it. Now it landed differently. Now it was a person.

"The study," Ansel said quietly. "May I?"

Mrs. Adeyemi hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Ansel had built a career — an unlabeled, unpaid, entirely self-appointed career — on small hesitations. People lied with their words and told the truth with their pauses, and Mrs. Adeyemi's pause before answering was the longest one yet.

"The study is as she left it," the housekeeper said finally. "I dust, but I don't move things."

The study was at the back of the house, windows facing the overgrown garden, bookshelves floor to ceiling, a desk with a green lamp. And against the far wall, beneath a sheet of white cotton, something tall and rectangular.

An easel.

Ansel stopped in the doorway. Of everything in the room — the law books, the framed diploma, the photographs of a younger Eleanor Voss shaking hands with governors — it was the covered shape that pulled at her, the way a missing umbrella pulls at a concierge who has spent her life noticing what isn't where it should be.

"Was she painting?" Ansel asked.

"No." Mrs. Adeyemi had come to stand behind her, and her voice had dropped into something careful. "Someone else was. A painter. She came to the house, oh — many times over the years. Then again, at the end. The last month. His Honor— " she corrected herself, an old habit dying hard, "—the judge cleared her afternoons for it. Cleared her *afternoons*. Eleanor Voss did not clear afternoons. Not for surgery. Not for her daughter's memorial."

Her daughter's memorial. Ansel filed that away with a gentle hand, the way you pocket something fragile.

"Who was the painter?"

"I don't know her name. She was quiet. Kept her supplies in a case. They'd talk while she worked, low voices, and I made tea and I didn't listen, because in this house you learned what not to hear." Mrs. Adeyemi's jaw tightened. "After the judge died, the painter came once more. Stood in this doorway. Didn't come in. Cried, actually — stood there and cried, and then left, and hasn't come back."

Ansel crossed the room slowly. Up close, the sheet was good cotton, tucked with care — tucked by the judge herself, perhaps, or by the painter on her last visit. Beneath it, the shape resolved: stretcher bars, canvas, the ghostly suggestion of a seated figure.

"May I?"

"No," Mrs. Adeyemi said.

Ansel withdrew her hand.

They stood there a moment, the three of them — Ansel, Theo, the housekeeper — regarding the covered thing like mourners at a second, smaller funeral. Theo shifted his weight. Outside, a car passed, unhurried, suburban, indifferent.

"Why does it stay covered?" Ansel asked softly.

"Because the estate lawyers said nothing leaves the house until probate closes, and because—" Mrs. Adeyemi stopped. Started again. "Because whoever covers a painting doesn't want it seen yet. And I've honored a lot of that woman's wishes. I can honor one more."

It was a reasonable answer. It was also, Ansel noted, not the whole answer. The whole answer lived underneath the cotton, and it had a shape, and shapes were her native language.

* * *

They were being walked back toward the kitchen — ten minutes having expired with Prussian precision — when Theo, trailing slightly, whispered, "Ansel. The photographs."

She'd seen them. On the hallway wall between study and kitchen: a procession of frames. Young Eleanor Voss in graduation robes. Voss being sworn in. Voss at some judicial banquet, mid-laugh, holding a champagne flute like a weapon she'd decided not to use.

And one frame, smaller than the others, hanging slightly crooked — the only crooked thing in the entire immaculate house — containing a photograph of a young woman with dark hair and Voss's exact stubborn chin, laughing on a beach, squinting into sunlight twenty years gone.

There was no nameplate. There was no date. And the frame was crooked in a way that suggested it had been taken down and rehung many times, held often, wept over maybe, or simply touched — the way you touch a thing when touching it is all that's left.

"Lena," Mrs. Adeyemi said, without being asked. She said it the way people say names they've said over graves. "Her daughter. Died a long time ago. Car accident." A pause with weight in it. "The judge never believed it was simple. Never said so out loud, mind. But she kept the file. I saw it once, years ago — a box, in the bottom of her closet, labeled in her own hand. One word." Mrs. Adeyemi's voice went very dry. "*Questions.*"

Ansel's pulse picked up, steady and cold, the old familiar rhythm. *Questions.* A judge who spent her career ruling on other people's truths, keeping a private box of her own unanswered ones.

"Where is the box now?"

"Gone." Mrs. Adeyemi met her eyes squarely. "I looked, after. After the funeral. The closet was empty except for hangers. Whatever was in that box, someone took it before the house was sealed — or the judge moved it herself, near the end, because she knew she was running out of time to move things."

The kitchen was warm and lemon-bright and Ansel barely registered walking into it. Pieces were rearranging themselves behind her eyes: a judge checking into a hotel under a false name to feel free; a painter clearing afternoons; a box labeled *Questions* vanished from a closet; a confession letter that knew about lavender oil and a stopped clock; and now a dead daughter whose death the judge herself had doubted.

And a covered easel. *A portrait will tell you,* the letter had said. Ansel hadn't known what to do with that sentence yet. Now it sat in her chest like a lit coal.

At the back door, Mrs. Adeyemi held it open and fixed them both with a look that could have sealed a courtroom.

"You were never here. You saw a kitchen and a hallway and nothing else. If anyone asks, I will deny you with great fluency; I've had practice denying things in this house." Then, quieter: "But you — concierge. You said you can't make yourself stop asking questions."

"I can't."

"Mm." Mrs. Adeyemi reached into her cardigan pocket. What she pressed into Ansel's palm was small, brass, cool — a key, worn smooth at the bow, on no ring or fob, the kind of key that had lived in a pocket for years.

"The study," the housekeeper said. "The painters' entrance around the side has its own lock; this opens it. Probate closes in five weeks, and after that the house goes to a nephew in Scottsdale who will hold an estate sale and sell that painting to someone who'll hang it over a sofa." Her chin lifted a fraction. "Eleanor Voss spent her whole life believing the truth would come out eventually if somebody just refused to shut up about it. I am seventy-one years old, Miss Lockridge. I don't have time to wait for eventually."

Ansel closed her fingers around the key. "Why me?"

"Because you knocked on the door and told me the truth badly and honestly, and because you remembered her crossword." For one unguarded second, Mrs. Adeyemi's composure cracked and showed the grief underneath, raw and enormous. "Nobody remembers the crossword."

Then the door closed, gently, decisively, and Ansel and Theo stood on the back steps in the late-morning light, holding a stolen-not-stolen key between them like contraband.

"Well," Theo said at last. "So are we breaking in tonight, or—"

"Tonight," Ansel said. She turned the key over in her palm. Somewhere under the cotton in that study, a portrait was waiting to tell her something, and a judge who did crosswords in pen had trusted a stranger with the only door to it. "Bring the good flashlight. And Theo — bring your camera."

He grinned, quick and bright. "Knew honesty was just the opening act."
