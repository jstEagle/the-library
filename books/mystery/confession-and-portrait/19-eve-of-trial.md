## 19. Eve of Trial

The Grand Meridian's ballroom had been transformed overnight into a courtroom annex — folding tables in neat rows, a stenographer's machine humming like a contented insect, and a jury pool of forty-odd citizens arranged with the anxious politeness of people waiting for a dental appointment. Ansel watched them from the corridor through the glass doors, a coffee warming her hands and a knot tightening somewhere beneath her ribs.

Jury selection. Day two. In five days, twelve strangers would begin deciding whether Daniel Reyes spent the rest of his life in prison for a murder that — if the portrait told the truth Ansel believed it told — was committed to protect a lie twenty years old.

"You're doing the thing," Marisol said, materializing at her elbow with the tray she carried even when off shift. "The staring thing. The one where you look at people like they're crossword puzzles."

"I'm assessing," Ansel said.

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Brooding has no actionable outcome." Marisol followed her gaze through the glass. "Which one is he?"

"Third row, gray suit. Reyes."

Marisol studied him a long moment. "He looks like my brother after our father's funeral. Like someone already read the verdict." She sighed. "Okay. Tell me what you need. Theo has tomorrow off, Piotr is pretending his ribs don't hurt, which means they hurt, and I have been saving my tips since you first showed me that ledger page. So. What do we need?"

Ansel looked at her — at this woman who had once poured her a drink at midnight and listened to a theory about a teacup without laughing — and felt the knot loosen by one careful degree.

"We need witnesses," she said. "A lot of them. And we need them all in one room on Thursday evening, looking at a painting."

* * *

Ruth Calloway arrived at the hotel at seven that evening, out of uniform, which on Ruth meant a cardigan the color of wet cardboard and an expression suggesting she'd rather be anywhere else, including a tax audit. She took the corner booth in Marisol's bar, back to the wall, and didn't touch the drink Marisol set down until the bartender had withdrawn to a tactful distance.

"The probate viewing," Ruth said quietly. "Walk me through it again. Slowly. Because when you said it last week I assumed you were joking, and you don't joke, which I find deeply unsettling."

"The estate inventory requires a public viewing before distribution," Ansel said. "It's scheduled Thursday, six o'clock, at the Voss house. Creditors, claimants, interested parties — anyone with standing can attend. And here's the beautiful part: the estate's attorney sent notice to every party named in Judge Voss's files. Including Dr. Emil Brandt, who is listed as executor of her medical directives and a personal friend of thirty years' standing."

"He doesn't have to come."

"No," Ansel agreed. "But he will. Because the portrait will be there."

Ruth turned her glass a quarter-turn on the coaster. "The restored portrait. Which Iris Kwan gave you. Which contains, hidden in the background, Eleanor Voss holding a sworn statement naming Brandt as the man who falsified her daughter's autopsy and, twenty years later, murdered her in her own bathtub."

"Painted into the background," Ansel said. "Small enough that only someone looking would see it. She spent her last weeks making sure the truth survived her. It's the most stubborn act of love I've ever encountered, and I work in hospitality."

"And your plan is to hang it on a wall, invite the commissioner, a reporter, and Reyes's attorney, and wait for a doctor to have a nervous breakdown in front of a covered canvas."

"Not a breakdown," Ansel said. "A confession. He confessed to Adeyemi once already — or nearly. He came back to the house twice after the funeral. Men like Brandt don't fear punishment, Ruth. They fear being *seen*. He's spent twenty years being the respectable physician, the grieving family friend, the man who signed the autopsy because poor Lena was already gone and what difference could it make. If he walks into that room and sees himself painted into the record — seen, publicly, irrevocably — he'll crack. Or he'll run. Either way, with the right witnesses, we win."

"With the right witnesses," Ruth repeated. "Meaning me. Meaning I stand up in front of the police commissioner and testify — informally, off the books, career-suicidally — that I was ordered to close the Voss file."

Ansel said nothing. That was the arrangement they'd made, and they both knew the shape of it: Ruth's badge, traded for the truth. Ansel had offered to find another way three times. Ruth had told her, each time, to stop insulting her.

"I've been thinking about the order itself," Ruth said now. "Who gave it. I keep circling it. The call came through the commissioner's office the morning after I found her. Not from the commissioner personally — from an aide. 'Close it, natural causes, family request.' Family request. Whose family? Voss's family was a dead daughter and a dead marriage." She rubbed her eyes. "I should have pushed then. Twenty years ago I'd have pushed. You get older and you learn which fights are survivable, and then one day you realize you've learned it too well."

"You're pushing now."

"I'm pushing now," Ruth agreed. "Because a concierge with a warrant pending shamed me into it, God help us both."

The word landed between them like a dropped glass.

"Warrant," Ansel said.

Ruth's jaw tightened. "You haven't heard. It came through this afternoon. Complaint filed by Dr. Emil Brandt — harassment, trespass, intimidation of a protected witness. A judge signed it. There'll be an officer at your place of work by end of week, possibly sooner." She met Ansel's eyes. "Somebody tipped him, Ansel. Somebody inside."

The bar noise seemed to recede. Ansel thought of the ledger pages she'd copied, the motel where Adeyemi hid, the rolled canvas in her closet disguised as a yoga mat. Thought of how many hands had touched each of those threads.

"Tipped him how?" she asked. "What did the complaint say, exactly?"

"That you'd been asking questions about the death of Eleanor Voss, that you'd entered the sealed estate without authorization, and that you'd contacted him directly with accusations." Ruth slid a folded paper across the table. "That last part isn't true. Have you contacted him?"

"No."

"Then either he's lying—" Ruth stopped. Her face did something complicated. "Or someone else talked to him. Someone who knows what you know."

* * *

They went to the motel that night.

The Starlite Motor Court sat on the edge of the interstate like something waiting to be demolished, its vacancy sign buzzing a tired pink. Room 14. Ansel knocked the pattern she and Adeyemi had agreed on — two, pause, three — and got nothing.

She knocked again. Nothing.

"Mrs. Adeyemi?" she called softly. "It's Ansel. From Wren Lane."

Silence, but the wrong kind — the kind with listening in it. Then Ruth, who had hung back by the ice machine, said quietly: "Door's not locked."

Inside, the room was stripped. No suitcase, no coat on the hook, no Bible in the drawer — Adeyemi had taken even the Bible, which struck Ansel as either very devout or very practical. On the pillow lay a single sheet of motel stationery, the kind with a fountain printed on the border.

*I'm sorry. He found me. He was kind about it, which frightened me more than shouting would have. He says if I testify he will make sure my sister loses her visa. I cannot do this to her. Do not come looking. Burn the key.*

Below it, pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly, one more line:

*Everything I told you is true. Please remember that everything I told you is true.*

Ansel stood very still. Beside her, Ruth read the note twice and swore with the fluency of a thirty-year veteran.

"He got to her in forty-eight hours," Ruth said. "A federal immigration threat takes paperwork, takes connections. He didn't do this alone. This is the commissioner's machinery, or something wearing its clothes." She looked around the sad little room. "And now your star witness is gone, your second witness is terrified, and you've got a harassment warrant with your name on it. Ansel — I say this with enormous respect — we are losing."

Ansel picked up the note. Folded it. Put it in her pocket next to the brass key Adeyemi had slipped her three weeks ago, the one she'd never returned.

"No," she said. "We're not losing. We're finding out exactly how scared he is. Scared men make mistakes, and Mrs. Adeyemi just told us his favorite weapon: kindness. He'll have been gentle with her. Gentle men leave softer fingerprints, but they still leave them." She turned for the door. "Come on. We need to check on Iris."

* * *

Iris Kwan lived above a shuttered frame shop on Delancey Street, in a room that smelled of turpentine and instant coffee, with tarps over the windows and brushes standing in jars like a garden of thin flowers. She'd been there nine days. Ansel had visited twice. Both times, Iris had answered the door as though she'd been standing behind it the whole while, which Ansel suspected she had.

This time, the door was ajar.

Ansel's stomach dropped before her mind caught up. She pushed through. The room was empty — not tidied-empty, *emptied*-empty. The brush jars gone. The tarps folded. On the floor where the easel had stood, a rectangle of cleaner wood, pale against the sun-faded boards.

On the door, taped at eye level, a note in Iris's quick slanted hand:

*Don't look for me. They came asking at the frame shop downstairs — a man with a nice voice, said he was worried about me, said he was a friend of Eleanor's. Nobody who knew Eleanor says "friend of Eleanor's." I finished what I promised. The portrait is yours. Keep it safe and keep it hidden and when the moment comes, show it to everyone at once. Light is the only thing he can't buy.*

Underneath, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*I'm sorry I keep running. Some of us were built with doors instead of walls.*

Ruth read it over Ansel's shoulder. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Down on Delancey, a bus sighed past, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing with tremendous conviction.

"She's right, though," Ansel said finally, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "About the light. About everyone at once." She folded the note carefully. "He thinks he's dismantling us piece by piece. Witness gone, witness silenced, warrant on my head. But he's actually teaching us the lesson. We tried to move quietly, witness by witness, thread by thread — and quiet is his home turf. Quiet is where he's lived for twenty years."

"So what do we do?" Ruth asked. "Thursday's in three days. Your warrant lands before then, probably. My testimony dies with Adeyemi's courage. Iris is a ghost. All we have is a painting and a theory."

"All we have," Ansel said, "is a painting, a theory, a reporter who owes me a favor, a defense attorney who would sell her soul for exculpatory evidence, a police commissioner who doesn't yet know he's walking into a room with a covered canvas in it, and the entire staff of the Grand Meridian Hotel." She allowed herself a small, cold smile — the smile Marisol called her *funeral face*, though these days the funerals were metaphorical. "Ruth, I am a concierge. Arranging for many people to be in one room at the same time, all of them believing it was their own idea — that is not a desperate gambit. That is Tuesday."

Ruth stared at her. Then, slowly, she laughed — a short, rusty sound, like an engine turning over after years in a garage.

"God," she said. "Eleanor would have liked you."

"I think," Ansel said, "she counted on it."

* * *

Back at the Meridian, past midnight, the lobby glowed its familiar amber and Piotr stood at his post by the doors, upright despite the bruised ribs, reading glasses low on his nose over a crossword.

"They served the papers," he said, without looking up. "Officer came at ten. Very polite. Left a card." He tapped the desk, where an envelope sat beside the bell. "Also, a gentleman telephoned for you at eleven. Would not leave a name. Said to tell you—" Piotr peered at his own handwriting on the message pad— "'the estate viewing is such a lovely tradition. Families deserve closure.' Then he hung up."

Ansel took the message slip. The handwriting was Piotr's; the words were Brandt's. *Closure.* He knew about Thursday. Of course he knew — he'd known about the motel, about Delancey Street. Somewhere in the machinery of the city, a phone rang whenever Ansel Lockridge breathed too loudly.

She should feel afraid. She noted, distantly, that she probably would later, at three in the morning, alone with the ceiling. But standing here under the chandelier, with Piotr pretending to do the crossword and Marisol's radio murmuring jazz from the bar and Theo's jacket hanging on the hook where he'd left it, what she felt instead was something older and harder.

They had taken her witnesses. They had taken her painter, her housekeeper, her good name. Fine. Let them collect everything soft and scattered about her case, every loose thread, every private meeting.

Thursday, she would give them a room full of light.

"Piotr," she said, pocketing the message. "How do you feel about catering?"

He lowered the crossword. His eyes, behind the smudged lenses, were bright as a young man's.

"Madame," he said, "I have been waiting three weeks for you to ask."
