## 4. Regulars

The morning after I burned the letter, the bookshop smelled of smoke and old paper, and I had opened the door out of habit rather than courage. Habit is a strange mercy. It carries you through the hours when your own judgment has gone on strike.

I had retrieved the letter from the stove the night before, when the shop was dark and I was sure no one was watching. Half of it was gone—eaten to a lace of black—but the words that survived were worse for the burning, somehow, as if fire had concentrated them. *She did not slip. She was put under. And there is another one, in the boathouse, that no one ever counted.* I had smoothed it flat on my kitchen table with hands that would not quite obey me, and I had told myself that I would decide in the morning what kind of fool I was going to be.

The morning arrived, and with it, the regulars.

Felix came first, as he always did, at seven-fifteen sharp, though he hadn't slept—this was evident in the way he moved, like a man negotiating a truce with his own skeleton. He was seventy-three and had spent forty years at a city desk two hundred miles south, and when the paper let him go they let go of something in him too, and now he ran the crossword table in the back corner of my shop every day with the gravity of a man presiding over a small nation whose only export was two-letter words.

"Yara." He set his thermos on the crossword table, unscrewed the cap, and sniffed the air the way he used to sniff a press release. "Something burned in here."

"The stove's temperamental."

"Everything's temperamental when you lie about it." He lowered himself into his chair, unfolded the paper, and drew his pen in a slow ceremonial arc. "Seventeen across. *What the sea refuses to give up.* Six letters."

"Salvage," I said.

He looked at me over the top of his glasses. "That's the answer they printed. Doesn't make it true."

I left him to it.

* * *

Marisol arrived at eight, on foot, backpack slung over one shoulder, earbuds in but not playing anything—she'd told me once that she wore them so people wouldn't talk to her on the walk over, and then talked to me for forty minutes about the returns cart. She was seventeen, sharp-faced, and had started as a shelf-stockers two winters ago and annexed the job of reading the returns, which meant she read every book the town gave back and formed opinions about all of them, and delivered these opinions to me like dispatches from a war I hadn't known we were fighting.

"You have three copies of that harbor history," she said, dropping the cart by the register. "Three. Nobody needs three. I moved one to the window display."

"It's a local book. It sells."

"It *sat*. I moved it to the window and it sold twice in a week. This is called merchandising, Yara. You're welcome." She was already shelving, her hands moving on their own, her eyes flicking to me and away, to me and away. She'd been doing that since yesterday. "You okay? You closed early."

"Errands."

"You never close early." She slid a paperback home with unnecessary force. "Felix says you looked like you'd seen a ghost."

"Felix looks like he's seen a ghost every morning. That's what insomnia is."

"Uh-huh." She didn't push, which was one of the thousand things I loved about her and would never say aloud. She just kept shelving, and after a minute she said, quieter, "If it's money stuff, I can work more hours. I don't need the extra ones anyway."

"It's not money."

"Okay." And then, because she was Marisol: "But if it ever is, I'm here. Obviously. Where else would I go."

I turned to the register so she wouldn't see my face do what it was doing.

* * *

Old Piet came at ten, when the tide was low enough to fish the flats and high enough that he'd already been out once and given up on the morning's catch. He came in smelling of salt and diesel and the particular cold that lives inside a man who has spent sixty years on the water, and he took his chair by the window without asking, because the chair had been his longer than I had owned the shop.

He was the one I'd been waiting for, and I hated myself a little for how carefully I'd waited.

"Quiet out there," he said, unwinding his scarf. "Water's holding its breath. Bad sign, this time of year."

"Bad for fish?"

"Bad for everything. Fish know things." He accepted the coffee I poured without looking at it, the way he accepted everything, as if the world owed him small services and mostly paid up. "You look tired, girl."

"I'm always tired."

"Not like this." He drank. His eyes, pale as winter water, went past me to the back of the shop, to the stove, cold now, its door shut. "Burned something?"

"Paperwork."

"Mm." He was quiet a moment, and I felt the silence lean on me. Piet had a way of being quiet that was worse than other people's questions. "You know what my father used to say about the flats?"

"I can't imagine."

"He said the tide's a honest worker but a bad liar. Takes what it takes, gives back what it gives. But it keeps a ledger." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Fifteen years is nothing to a ledger like that."

I kept my voice level, kept it light, kept it the voice of a woman making conversation over coffee, and I have never worked so hard at anything in my life. "What does it give back, after fifteen years? The tide."

Piet looked at me for a long time. Long enough that Felix's pen stopped scratching in the corner. Long enough that Marisol, halfway up the stepladder with an armful of returns, went still.

"It gives back what it's made to give back," he said. "Or what it's allowed to. There's a difference, and men have drowned learning it." He set the cup down. "Why do you ask?"

"Idle curiosity."

"No," Piet said, not unkindly. "You've never been idle in your life. I've watched you two years. You rest like a horse—standing up, one ear turned."

The bell over the door rang, and a customer came in wanting a gift for a granddaughter, and the moment broke and scattered like a dropped plate. But Piet's eyes stayed on me as I rang up the sale, and when the customer left he gathered his scarf and stood, slow, joints cracking like rigging in wind.

"I'll be out at Gull Point tomorrow," he said, at the door. "Morning tide. If a person had a question for the water, that'd be the time to ask it." He wound the scarf around his neck with terrible patience. "And Yara. Whatever's in that stove—" he nodded, once, toward the back, "—the ash remembers too."

The bell rang behind him.

* * *

The rest of the day passed in the small mechanics of the shop—receipts, a delivery of new stock, Felix declaring the puzzle "a disgrace" and solving it anyway in eleven minutes. But I moved through it like a woman underwater, and I knew, watching them, that they knew.

That was the thing about the life I'd built here. I had constructed it to be invisible. A bookshop in a town too small to be worth corrupting, a name chosen from a ferry schedule, a history thin as tissue paper and never questioned because nobody in a town like this questions a woman who sells them their books and remembers their grandchildren's names. I had thought of them as scenery. Cover. The set dressing of a life.

But Felix had noticed the smell of smoke. Marisol had noticed the early closing and offered, without being asked, to work more hours. Piet had watched me for two years and learned how I rested. You cannot be scenery to people who love you. I had hidden inside their warmth the way the letter's writer had hidden inside an envelope, and the warmth had known, the whole time, that it was sheltering something.

At four o'clock I flipped the sign to CLOSED, and Felix looked up from his paper.

"Early again."

"Errands."

"Second time this week." He folded the paper along the crossword with a crisp violence. "I was a journalist for forty years, Yara. You know what the first thing they teach you is?"

"How to verify a source?"

"How to recognize a story that's already happening." He capped his pen. "Whatever it is—you don't have to tell me. But when you do tell someone, make sure it's someone who knows how to keep their mouth shut and their eyes open. Those are rarer than you'd think." He stood, joints popping, and gathered his thermos. "I'm at the crossword table every day at seven-fifteen. In case."

He left. Marisol lingered, pretending to alphabetize a shelf that was already alphabetized, and finally said, "I locked the back door. The bolt sticks, you have to lift the handle." And then she was gone too, into the blue evening, and the shop was mine and silent, and the half-burned letter waited in the drawer under the register like a coal that wouldn't die.

* * *

I took it out and read it again by the window light, what was left of it.

*She did not slip.* Teodora Voss. I had spent the evening before with the newspaper archive and the sealed case file's public shadow, and the shape of it was exactly the shape of things I had spent a career learning to distrust: a drowning off Gull Point, a grieving daughter, a ruling of accidental death issued with a speed that had nothing to do with the evidence and everything to do with who signed it. Sergeant Merrick Hale. My mentor. The man who taught me how to read a scene, how to work a witness, how to be—God help me—good at this. The man whose name was on the seal, and whose signature I would know anywhere, because he'd made me practice signatures in the academy, laughing, saying *a detective who can't forge is a detective who can be forged.*

*And there is another one, in the boathouse, that no one ever counted.*

I sat with that until the light went blue and then gray. Fifteen years ago I had testified against the precinct and died in a staged accident and been reborn as a bookseller, and I had told myself the bargain was clean: my old life for my new one, my silence for my safety. But the letter had my address. The past had found the one door I'd built, and it was knocking politely, the way Hale used to knock.

And Piet's voice, all day, turning over in me like a stone in a pocket: *it gives back what it's made to give back, or what it's allowed to.*

I thought about the tide flats at Gull Point. I thought about what a man who fishes them every morning for sixty years might have seen, and kept, and carried, the way you carry a hook in your thumb—too deep to dig out, too true to forget.

Tomorrow, Piet had said. Morning tide.

I put the letter back in the drawer, and I locked the shop, and I stood a moment in the dark street looking at the water past the rooftops, black and patient, keeping its ledger.
