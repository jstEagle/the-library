## 3. The Locked Crypt

I did not sleep well, which was unusual. I sleep the way I file: completely, and without dreaming. But the confession letter lay in my locked drawer all night like a stone in a shoe, and by morning I had made a decision that surprised me, which was to make one.

Decision, I mean. I am not a man who decides things. I am a man who notices things, and noticing is a coward's vocation — you watch, you record, you never stand between the fact and its consequences. For thirty years I had been the cathedral's memory and none of its conscience, and I had been proud of that, in the way a man is proud of a scar he doesn't discuss.

But the letter was dated the twenty-second of December. Three weeks out. A murder, scheduled like a service, in a crypt that had been sealed for forty years.

There are two kinds of archivists. The kind who believe paper describes the world, and the kind who believe paper commands it. I had always been the first kind, comfortably. The letter was trying to convert me.

I found Tomasin in the bell tower, feeding the jackdaws, which was against at least four separate regulations and which he did every morning with the serenity of a man who has never once read a regulation.

"Verger," I said.

"Archivist." He scattered another handful of crusts. "You look terrible."

"I need the crypt key."

The crumbs stopped. It was only a moment — half a heartbeat, the time it takes a jackdaw to swallow — but I have spent my life reading pauses the way other men read faces, and Tomasin's pause had a whole paragraph in it.

"The crypt key," he repeated.

"The undercroft door. Iron, double-locked, seal of Bishop Wulfric on the hasp. I'm told it hasn't been opened since 1984."

"Hasn't," Tomasin agreed. He brushed his hands on his cassock. "That's right. Hasn't."

"Then you won't mind showing me."

"Mind?" He smiled, and it was his ordinary smile, the one he gave to tourists and frightened children and me, and I realized I had never before wondered what it looked like when it wasn't ordinary. "Why would I mind? Come on, then. But you'll want a lantern. The stairs are murder."

He said it lightly. Murder. The word dropped between us like a coin into a well, and I listened all the way down for the splash.

* * *

The undercroft stairs descend from the north transept in a spiral of worn steps, forty-one of them — I counted, because counting is what I do instead of praying — and at the bottom the air changes. Cathedral air above is stone and beeswax and the ghost of ten thousand services. Below, it is older. Cold that has stopped pretending to be weather and become climate. The smell of lime and long iron and, beneath it, something sweetish and mineral that I have never found a name for and have privately called *the past*.

Tomasin went first with the lantern. He is a big man, the verger, big and slow and gentle, with a face like a badly repaired wall — handsome once, then something happened to it. He hummed as he walked. He always hums. I had never noticed what he hums. It was the solstice plainsong, the one they'd sing in three weeks' time, and the tune came down the stairs ahead of us and pooled in the dark like water.

"Here we are," he said. "Door."

The crypt door is iron over oak, and it is a door the way a verdict is a sentence: final. Two locks, one above the other, and across both, the lead seal of Bishop Wulfric, pressed with his crozier and the date — I didn't need the light to read it, I have read that seal a hundred times in the registers. *Wulfric, by divine permission, servant of the servants of God.* Underneath, in the bishop's own hand, the injunction that had kept the door shut through four deans and one minor earthquake: *Let no man open, till the matter be concluded.*

The matter. Forty years, and no one had ever written down what the matter was. That, more than anything, is what an archivist cannot forgive.

"Seal's intact," Tomasin said.

"Seal's intact," I agreed, and knelt.

The lock was intact. The hasp was intact. The hinges, which I could just reach, were intact — and that was the first wrong thing, though I didn't know it yet. Forty years of iron in damp air, and the hinges showed no rust worth the name. Someone had oiled them. Recently. You can smell machine oil in cold air the way you can smell a lie in a sentence.

I said nothing. I am very good at saying nothing. I took out my notebook and wrote: *hinges dressed — recent.* Tomasin watched me write it. I felt him watching the way you feel weather change.

"Lock's fine, then," he said, too quickly. "Dust will tell you the rest, if you're set on looking."

"I'm set on looking."

He sighed, and it was a strange sigh, half exasperation and half something that sounded almost like grief, and he took from his sleeve — I saw it clearly, I want that recorded, I saw it come from his sleeve and not from any ring of keys — a small iron key, old, black with age, and he knelt beside me and put it in the upper lock.

It did not turn.

"Rusted," Tomasin said. "Told you. Hasn't been opened since '84. Key's seized."

He tried again, with a show of effort, shoulder into it, and the key sat in the lock like a fact in a false statement — perfectly placed, perfectly useless.

"Let me," I said.

I took the key from him. Our fingers touched, briefly, and he flinched — actually flinched, this man who once carried me, drunk and half-frozen, up forty-one stairs on the night I first arrived at Saint Aldhelm's with nothing but a coat and a name I was trying to forget. He flinched from my fingers as if the key were hot.

I turned it. It turned.

Not easily. But it turned, smooth at the heart of its resistance, the way a well-oiled mechanism turns, and I looked at Tomasin and Tomasin looked at the lantern flame, and neither of us said anything for the length of three jackdaws' heartbeats.

"Lower lock's the real one," he said at last. "Bishop's seal. That we don't touch."

"No," I agreed. "We don't touch it."

But the upper bolt, released, had opened something other than the door. It had opened the question. And questions, once bolted back, have a way of rattling.

* * *

We did not go in. The seal held the door, and I am, whatever else, a man of procedure; breaking a bishop's seal without authority is the kind of act that ends careers, and mine is the only thing I own. But the door was not quite shut. The upper bolt's release had loosened it by the width of a fingernail, and from that fingernail's gap came the crypt's breath — cold, still, and carrying, faint but unmistakable, the smell of burning.

Not old burning. Not the mineral sweetness of candles burned out decades ago and sealed in with the dead. This was fresh. Tallow and a thread of smoke, the smell of a flame recently pinched.

"Tomasin," I said. "When was the crypt last entered?"

"1984," he said. "Before my time, even. Before Father Anselm's. Nobody goes down there."

"Then someone has been burning candles in it."

He was quiet for so long that I looked up from the gap. His face, in the lantern light, had done something I had never seen it do. It had closed. Not hardened — closed, like a book shut by a man who has decided you may not read further, and the hum was gone out of him entirely, and I understood that I was not looking at the verger anymore. I was looking at a door of my own.

"Draft," he said. "Old air moves. Smells carry."

"Old air doesn't smell of tallow. Old air smells of stone and forty years. That's smoke, Tomasin. Fresh smoke."

"Zephyrine." He said my name, and it was worse than the flinch. He has said my name perhaps a hundred times in fifteen years, always comfortably, the way you say the name of a chair or a coat — *Zephyrine, supper's on; Zephyrine, you'll catch your death in that archive.* This time he said it like a warning, or a plea, or both, and I could not tell which frightened me more. "Some doors are locked for kindness. You of all people should know that."

I stood. My knees cracked in the cold like the lower lock trying to turn.

"Kindness," I said, "or the matter being concluded?"

He didn't answer. He picked up the lantern, and the light swung, and for one second it fell full on the gap in the door, on the darkness beyond — and in that second I saw what I will see every time I close my eyes for the rest of this case, whatever it turns out to be.

On the floor of the crypt, just inside, perhaps a yard from the door: a candle stub. White wax, burnt to within a finger's width of its end, still standing upright on the flagstones. And beside it — I would swear this on any register you put in front of me — the dust was *swept*. Not disturbed. Swept. A clean path, narrow, deliberate, leading away from the door into the dark, the way you would leave a path if you wanted someone to be able to walk it without leaving a trace.

Or the way you would leave a path if you wanted someone to be able to walk it *again*.

Then Tomasin turned the lantern away, and the dark took it back.

* * *

Tomasin

He watched the archivist climb the stairs ahead of him, that thin, bent, careful man, counting his steps under his breath the way he counted everything, and Tomasin felt the lie sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He had lied. He had never lied to Zephyrine. Not in fifteen years, not when the man had wept his first winter over a charity supper like it was a coronation, not when he'd found the old scandal in the county records and come to Tomasin grey-faced with it and Tomasin had said, *that was another life, and this one is yours, and no one here will ever use it against you.* He had lied about the key — the key turned fine, had always turned fine, was oiled every month on the first by Tomasin's own hand, a duty passed to him with the verger's chain and a sentence he had never forgotten: *Keep it ready, lad. The day may come when she needs to leave quickly.*

She. Mirabel. Forty years in the dark below, or near enough to it, and the whole cathedral a conspiracy of cassocks and kindness, and now the archivist had seen the swept path and smelled the candle and asked his one question in that flat paper voice, and Tomasin had lied to him.

*Some doors are locked for kindness.*

He climbed. Above him, at the top of the stairs, Zephyrine stood in the transept light, thin as a bookmark, and looked back down into the dark with an expression Tomasin had never seen on him and recognized anyway, because he had seen it on Father Anselm's face forty years ago, and on the old dean's, and on his own in every mirror since.

It was the expression of a man who has just understood that the story he is living inside has another story underneath it — and who has decided, God help them all, to read that one too.

"Supper's at seven," Tomasin said, because it was the only true thing he had left to offer.

Zephyrine nodded. He did not say thank you. In fifteen years he had always said thank you.

That night, Tomasin went down the ossuary passage with a fresh candle, and knocked — three soft knocks, the old signal — and told her, "The archivist knows the door's been opened."

And from the dark, in a voice like paper, she said: "Then we have three weeks to decide what he is."
