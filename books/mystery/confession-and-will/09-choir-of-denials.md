## 9. Choir of Denials

The sacristan's name was Ambrose Pell, and he had held the office for thirty-one years, which meant he had held it longer than Zephyrine had been alive. He was a small man built like a folded letter — creased, compact, sealed at every edge — and he smelled perpetually of beeswax and the iron gall ink he used to label the vestry cupboards with a precision that bordered on devotion.

Zephyrine chose the hour carefully. Evening choir practice emptied the building of everyone except the singers, and the singers were upstairs under the organist's baton, their voices rising through the stone like water through a sieve. The sacristy itself sat in the cathedral's eastern flank, a low room of cedar presses and brass hooks, where the vestments hung in ranked colors like a calendar of solemnities.

Ambrose was mending a cope when Zephyrine came in. He did not look up. "If you've come about the thurible, it's been sent to the silversmith."

"I haven't come about the thurible."

"Then you've come about the questions." Ambrose set down his needle and finally raised his eyes. They were pale, unhurried eyes, the eyes of a man who had spent decades watching other people's hands. "Tomasin told me you'd get here eventually. Sit down, Mr. Yarrow. You make a room nervous standing in it."

Zephyrine did not sit. Standing gave him the door, and the door gave him the exit, and the exit gave him the illusion that he could still leave this whole affair behind him like a coat too heavy to carry. "I'll be brief," he said. "Mirabel Quill. When did she die?"

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full — packed tight, like a drawer forced shut on too much linen. Somewhere above them the choir reached a cadence and resolved, and the sound came down through the vaulting faint as memory.

"You've asked the others," Ambrose said.

"I have. Canon Loxley. Mrs. Ferrier at the organ. The two stewards who keep the west door. Four people, four conversations, and every one of them used the same words in the same order: *She died years ago, poor soul.* Not 'she passed.' Not 'we lost her.' *Died years ago, poor soul.* Word for word."

"People repeat what they've heard."

"They do," Zephyrine agreed. "When they're repeating gossip. But these four don't gossip together — Loxley can't stand Ferrier, and the stewards barely speak to anyone. For them to produce identical phrasing, the sentence must have been given to them. Taught. Like a response in the liturgy." He let that settle. "*Died years ago, poor soul* is not grief, Mr. Pell. It's a rubric."

Ambrose resumed his mending. The needle went in and out of the gold thread with terrible steadiness. "You were paid to prove something," he said. "That no murder has occurred in the crypt. That's your commission, isn't it? Prove the absence of a thing." He glanced up. "You're very good at proving the presence of things instead. It's a different trade. Perhaps you should consider which one you were hired for."

"I was hired to establish facts."

"You were hired to establish *one* fact, by a man who knew exactly what he was buying." The needle stopped. Ambrose set the cope across his knee and looked at Zephyrine fully now, and there was something in his face that hadn't been there before — not hostility, but a kind of weary recognition, as though he'd been expecting this conversation for forty years and had hoped, right up until tonight, to die before it arrived. "You want the truth. Very well. I'll give you half of it, because half is all I can give without breaking faith with people who trusted me with their lives. And then I'm going to ask you for something, and I want you to hear the asking before you decide how much of my half you believe."

Zephyrine felt his pulse change gear. Forty years of archival instinct told him that a man offering half a story was offering the half that would steer him; the other half was always the true one. But instinct also told him that Ambrose Pell was not steering. He was surrendering ground he'd held a long time, and it cost him visibly, the way retreat costs a soldier who has never once broken rank.

"Forty years ago," Ambrose said, "something happened in the crypt."

"I know. A killing. The confession describes—"

"Not a killing." Ambrose's voice dropped, though there was no one to hear. "*Something*. I choose the word carefully, Mr. Yarrow, because even now I don't know the whole shape of it, and I was here. What I know is this: a young woman saw it. Saw enough to end a powerful man's life if she ever spoke. And the people of this cathedral — ordinary people, choristers and vergers and a frightened young sacristan not much older than yourself — made a decision. They could hide her or bury her. Those were the choices. Hiding her meant killing her name. Burying her—" he paused "—burying her meant the other thing. The thing your confession letter promises. So we killed her name. We put it in the register with a date and a shovelful of fiction over it, and we taught ourselves to say *died years ago, poor soul* until the words wore smooth as river stones, and Mirabel Quill ceased to exist so that Mirabel Quill could go on living."

The choir above had begun a new piece — something slow, in a minor mode, the kind of anthem sung only at midwinter. Their voices threaded down through the floor and seemed to enter the room through the walls themselves, as if the building were singing along.

"She's alive," Zephyrine said. It wasn't a question, but he needed it said aloud, in this room, to this man, so that it became a fact with a witness.

Ambrose didn't answer directly. He rose, crossed to the press where the altar linens lay, and stood with his hand flat on the cedar lid as if taking an oath upon it. "I have kept her secret for thirty-one years," he said. "The ones before me kept it nine more. In all that time, do you know how many people have tried to find her? Two. Both hired by men who wanted her silent permanently. Both turned away at this door by nothing more than the same four words everyone gives you. *Died years ago, poor soul.* It's remarkable, Mr. Yarrow, what a sentence can do when a community agrees to carry it. It's the strongest lock in this cathedral. Stronger than the crypt door."

"And yet someone burns candles down there."

He saw the flinch. Small, quickly mastered, but real — the first crack in the river-stone smoothness. "Candles are lit for the dead," Ambrose said. "It's a cathedral. Candles are lit everywhere."

"In a locked crypt whose dust shows no footprints, behind a door whose lock has not been turned in years? Someone reaches that crypt, Mr. Pell. Someone prays down there regularly — the wax drippings are layered, weeks of them. Either your dead woman walks, or your living one does."

For a long moment Ambrose said nothing. Then he came back to his chair and sat, heavily, folding his hands on the unfinished cope as though pinning it down before it could fly away with him. "I'm going to ask you for the thing I mentioned," he said. "Stop."

"Stop?"

"Stop the inquiry. Return the retainer. Write to Lord Aldery and tell him the matter is beyond proof — that the records are ambiguous, the register unreliable, the trail cold. No one will fault you. No one will even know. And in nine days—" his voice caught, just slightly, on the number, and Zephyrine understood that the sacristan had done the same arithmetic he had, that everyone in this quiet conspiracy lived by the same calendar "—in nine days the statute expires, and everything that happened forty years ago becomes unprosecutable forever. Unpunishable, yes. Also unrepeatable. The past closes like water over a stone. She can come up out of the dark. She can have a name again — a new one, a true one, whatever she wants. Do you understand what you threaten, digging now? Not justice. Justice died forty years ago in that crypt with the man who bled there. What you threaten is the only life she has left."

Zephyrine stood very still. He was aware, distantly, of the anthem ending above, of the pause between movements, of the organist's cough. He was aware that everything Ambrose said was reasonable, humane, even noble — and that none of it answered the question that had been eating him since the archive, since the confession dated three weeks forward, since the candle stub burning in a sealed room.

"If she only needs to stay hidden for nine days," he said slowly, "why does the confession describe her death three days from now? Why schedule a murder at all? If the statute expires and frees her, the crime dies with it. Unless—" and here the thought arrived whole, cold, complete, the way the worst thoughts do "—unless the death in the confession is meant to happen *before* the deadline. Unless someone believes the case only closes if the witness dies inside the window. Unless hiding her was never the plan, Mr. Pell. Unless finishing her was."

"That's not—" Ambrose started, and stopped. And in the stopping, in the half-second where the sacristan's face went from argument to something older and more frightened, Zephyrine read the answer as clearly as if it had been written in the register in fresh ink.

"You didn't know," Zephyrine said quietly. "You thought the confession was a threat from outside. From whoever she saw that night, or his heirs, coming to finish the work. You've been guarding her against *them*." He leaned forward. "But the confession is signed by her own name, in her own registered hand. And the payment that hired me came from Lord Aldery. Tell me, sacristan — who exactly have you been keeping her safe from?"

Ambrose looked at him for a long time. The lamp between them guttered and steadied. Above, the choir began the final verse, voices climbing toward the high vaults, and the sacristan bowed his head as though the sound pressed on him physically.

"I keep her safe," he said at last. "That's all I've ever been asked to do, and all I've ever done. Who asks me — and why the asking changed three weeks ago — that's the half I can't give you. Because the person who asked made me swear on the altar, and I am an old man, Mr. Yarrow, and an old man's oaths are the only property he has left."

He gathered the cope into his arms like a sleeping child. "Nine days," he said. "Please. Whatever you think you owe your employer, owe the living more. Stop."

Zephyrine walked back through the cloister with the anthem still ringing faintly overhead, and the strange double knowledge turning in him like a key in a lock he couldn't see: sympathy on one side, duty on the other, and between them a gap exactly wide enough for a lie to fall through. Ambrose believed what he'd said. That was the trouble. Every word had been true, and every word had been arranged, and somewhere beneath both truths lay the third thing — the reason a dying magistrate paid a year's fee in advance, the reason a confession carried tomorrow's date, the reason a woman's name had been buried so lovingly and her body so carefully not buried at all.

He let himself into the archive office and lit the lamp. The shelves stood in their familiar ranks, patient and ordered, the world reduced to paper where paper obeyed rules. He sat down, pulled the burial register toward him, and opened it to the page he had flagged — Mirabel Quill's entry, the ink a shade too brown, the hand a shade too careful.

He would read it properly this time. Every stroke. Every hesitation of the pen.

He did not yet notice that the window latch, which he always checked twice, was already undone.
