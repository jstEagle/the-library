## 1. The Call Before the Body

The dead hours between two and four a.m. are when the city tells you its secrets. I've worked the night board at Harborline Dispatch for six years, and I can tell you that nothing good ever calls after two. Drunks who've lost their keys, nurses who've lost their rides, and once — memorably — a man who'd lost an entire goat on the viaduct. But mostly it's quiet. Mostly it's me and the coffee pot and the soft electrical hum of a city pretending to sleep.

The call came in at 2:47 a.m. on a Tuesday so slow I'd already alphabetized the fare log twice.

"Harborline Dispatch, this is Coral."

Static. The wet kind, like someone breathing through a soaked cloth. Then a voice, low and frayed at the edges, the way voices get when a man has been talking through pain for a long time and has finally stopped bothering to hide it.

"Fare's... nine-fourteen," he whispered. "Nine-fourteen to... eleven-oh-two. Then the bridge toll. You understand? The *bridge toll*."

"Sir, this is a dispatch line, not—"

"Nine-fourteen," he said again, gentler now, like he was sorry to be difficult. "Write it down. Somebody has to write it down."

There was a sound behind him — a door, or a wave, something heavy and rhythmic — and then the line filled with water noise, actual water, sloshing against what I can only assume was a hull, because the next thing I heard was gulls. Gulls at three in the morning, screaming like the world was ending in miniature.

"Where are you calling from?" I asked. "Sir? Can you give me a landmark?"

"I gave you everything," he said, and there was something almost tender in it. "Fare's paid." And the line went dead with a click so clean it sounded deliberate, like a ledger closing.

I sat there with my pen hovering over the log. Six years of taking calls from strangers at the worst moments of their lives teaches you things. It teaches you which silences mean the caller is deciding whether to hang up, and which ones mean they already have, somewhere inside themselves. That silence had been the second kind.

I wrote down 914–1102 anyway, because somebody had to write it down. Then I logged the call as a prank and hated myself for it, and went back to my coffee, and did not think about the gulls.

Mostly.

* * *

At 6:12 a.m., just as Marisol came in to relieve me smelling like vanilla and cigarette smoke in equal measure, the police arrived. Not one cruiser — three, plus an unmarked sedan parked diagonally across two spots like it owned them, which, I suppose, by then it did.

Detective Ibarra was the kind of woman who made you feel audited just by looking at you. Gray blazer, gray eyes, hair pulled back so tight it looked structural. She asked for Coral Quintana the way you'd ask for a suspect, not a witness, and when Marisol pointed her across the room, I felt every driver in the bullpen go still around me — Teo freezing mid-sip over his thermos, old Danny lowering his crossword like a drawbridge.

"Coral Quintana." She said it flat, checking it against something in her head. "You took a call at approximately 2:47 this morning."

"You have our recordings?"

"We have your recordings." She didn't smile. "We also have a body. Pulled out of the harbor near Pier Nine about an hour ago. Male, fifties, retired PD. Name of Edwin Marsh."

The name meant nothing to me. The rest of it meant everything.

"He had a cell phone on him," Ibarra continued, watching my face with the patience of someone who'd learned to wait people into confessions. "Last outgoing call, 2:47 a.m., duration forty-one seconds. To this number."

"That call was garbled," I said. "Whispering numbers. Fare numbers, maybe. Then it cut out."

"Fare numbers." She let that sit. "Ms. Quintana, can you tell me why a drowning victim would be wearing your brother's jacket?"

The bullpen got very quiet. Even the coffee pot seemed to stop percolating.

"I'm sorry?"

She held up a clear evidence bag, and inside it, folded with more care than she'd shown anything else that morning, was a jacket. Navy canvas, salt-stiffened, one button missing. I knew that button. I'd been meaning to sew it back on for three years, since the night after the funeral, when I brought the jacket home from Danny Jr.'s apartment because I couldn't stand the idea of Goodwill getting it. It had lived in the back of my closet ever since, behind the winter coats, where I put things I couldn't look at but couldn't throw away.

Except it wasn't in my closet anymore. I'd know if it were, because I'd looked at it last Thursday, moving coats around, touching the collar the way you touch a scar.

"That's mine," I heard myself say. "That's — that was my brother's. How did you—"

"There's a dry cleaner's tag inside the collar," Ibarra said. "Quintana. We ran it. We also found, in the victim's pocket, a slip of paper with a phone number written on it. This number. This dispatch office. And underneath it, one word: *Coral*."

The floor did something unsteady beneath my chair. Marisol's hand landed on my shoulder, warm and certain, and I gripped it without thinking.

"I never met him," I said. "Edwin Marsh. I don't know him. Someone stole that jacket out of my closet, Detective. My apartment was locked, I was here all night, you can check the logs—"

"Oh, I intend to." She set the evidence bag down on my desk, right on top of my neat little log book, right on top of the number I'd written at 2:51 a.m.: *914–1102*. Her eyes dropped to it, then came back up, and something in them sharpened like a blade finding its edge. "Interesting. You wrote that down before we told you there was anything to write down."

"It was part of the call. He was whispering fares. Nine-fourteen to eleven-oh-two. I thought it was a crank."

"A crank." She picked up my log book, turned it toward herself, and read my own handwriting back to me like scripture. "A man calls a taxi dispatcher at three in the morning, whispers route numbers, says 'fare's paid,' and drowns in the harbor wearing a dead woman's brother's jacket with your name pinned in his pocket. And you logged it as a crank."

"I didn't know what else to do with it."

"No," she agreed. "People rarely do. That's usually how it starts." She closed the book and tucked it under her arm like confiscated homework. "I'm going to need you to come down to the station this afternoon, Ms. Quintana. Don't make me come find you."

She left. The door swung shut behind her, and the bullpen exhaled all at once, a dozen drivers talking over each other — *did she say Pier Nine, that's Teo's old route, that jacket, Coral, honey, whose jacket* — and I sat very still, listening to all of it wash over me.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about spending six years listening to strangers' last words: eventually the job comes for you. Somewhere out there was a man who had spent his final forty-one seconds trying to give me something — numbers, a message, a *fare* — and I had filed him under prank and poured another cup of coffee while the tide took him apart.

He'd known my name. He'd worn my brother's jacket like a message, like a key, like a hand reaching up out of the water pointing straight at me.

And whoever put that jacket on him had been inside my closet. Which meant they'd been inside my life. Which meant, as I stared at the dispatch board blinking its little green lights at me — every one of them a voice I'd trusted, a colleague, a friend — the listener had become the listened-to, and I had no way of knowing yet which light on that board belonged to the person who'd killed him.

Marisol squeezed my shoulder. "Coral. Honey. Talk to us."

I looked at the log book under Ibarra's arm, at the numbers still swimming behind my eyes.

"Nine-fourteen to eleven-oh-two," I said quietly. "Somebody has to write it down."

I picked up a fresh page.
