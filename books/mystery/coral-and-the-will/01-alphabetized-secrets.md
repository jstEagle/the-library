## 1. Alphabetized Secrets

The microphone's red light had a way of making silence feel like an accusation.

Odile Kavanagh sat in the converted pantry he called a studio — four feet of shelving cleared of jam jars and replaced with acoustic foam, a secondhand condenser mic on a boom arm, a laptop running software he'd learned the way he learned everything: alone, at night, by trial and error. The house around him was quiet in the particular way old houses are quiet when they've been empty of other people for a long time. Not silent. A house like his creaked and settled and ticked in its walls like something turning over in sleep. He'd lived with those sounds so long they'd stopped being sounds and become furniture.

He pressed record.

"This is *Saltmere Unsound*, episode forty-one," he said, and heard immediately that his voice was wrong — too careful, too much like a man reading from a script he'd memorized rather than written. He stopped, deleted, started again.

"Episode forty-one. The last one. For now."

Outside the window above the desk, October was doing what October did to Saltmere: stripping the maples along Harbor Road down to their pale bones, dragging fog in off the water each morning like a sheet being pulled over something the town preferred not to look at. Twelve years ago this month, Coral Vance had driven her blue hatchback out of the school parking lot at 3:40 on a Thursday afternoon and been seen by no one again. Odile had been nineteen then. He was thirty-one now, and he had spent most of the years between organizing her absence into folders.

He opened the first one on his screen as he spoke, because it helped, saying the words while looking at them. There was comfort in the architecture of it. Everything in its place.

"For those just joining — though I know there aren't many of you left, and I know most of you are neighbors — here is where we stand." He clicked through the directory structure he'd built over six years: **01_CORRESPONDENCE**, **02_WITNESS_STATEMENTS**, **03_PHYSICAL_EVIDENCE**, **04_TIMELINE**, **05_THEORIES_DISCARDED**, **06_THEORIES_OPEN**. Each folder subdivided further, files named in a convention only he could parse, every document scanned at three hundred DPI and backed up twice — once to a drive in his sock drawer, once to a server somewhere he liked to imagine was underground in Iceland, cold and dark and indifferent.

"Coral Anne Vance. Born March ninth, 1968. Fifth-grade teacher at Saltmere Elementary for eleven years. Last confirmed sighting, October fourth, 1998, 3:40 p.m., school parking lot. Her vehicle was recovered nine days later from the pull-off at Gull Point — unlocked, keys absent, a library book on the passenger seat. *The Shipping News.* She was on page one-nineteen."

He knew that because he'd checked the book out himself, years later, from the little branch library that still kept its records on paper going back decades. He'd read to page one-nineteen and stopped, and the stopping had felt like a kind of prayer, or a superstition, or both. He'd never finished it. Some part of him believed Coral would finish it when she came home, and he was not a man who spooked easily, but he was a man who kept faith with small rituals, because small rituals were all that stood between an archivist and a madman.

"The investigation," he continued, "was conducted by Deputy Hollis Grady, who took the missing-persons call on the evening of October fifth, after Coral failed to appear for a faculty meeting. Sheriff Robert Okoye assumed oversight on October sixth. I want to be fair here. I want to be fair, because fairness is the whole point of this exercise." He paused, adjusted the gain, watched the waveform flatten and rise with his breath. "But fairness has to include the fact that the first seventy-two hours were treated as a runaway case. A woman leaving town. A woman starting over. Nobody considered — officially — that Coral Vance, who had a daughter, who had a mortgage she paid early, who had grading in her bag and a dentist appointment on the calendar, might not have left voluntarily."

A daughter. He said the word and felt the familiar snag of it, the hook beneath the surface of the file. Wren Vance. Nine years old in 1998, shipped east to an aunt in Massachusetts within the month, and never — as far as Odile's records showed, and his records were thorough — brought back for a memorial, an anniversary, a question. He'd tried once, early on, to find a current address. He'd gotten as far as a P.O. box in Gloucester and then stopped, pen hovering over the letter he'd drafted and never sent. What would he even say? *Your mother's disappearance is alphabetized. Would you like a copy?*

He'd filed the draft under **07_UNSENT**, which was, he admitted, the most honest folder he owned.

* * *

The Diner on Marsh Street — it had no other name; it had been The Diner longer than anyone living could account for — smelled of coffee burnt past redemption and bacon grease seasoned by four decades of use. Odile arrived at seven-fifteen the next morning, as he did every morning, and sat in the third booth from the door, the one with the view of the register and the window, because a man who spent his life cataloguing other people's movements liked to keep track of his own.

"You look like a man recording a eulogy," said Dot Ferreira, setting down his coffee without being asked. She was sixty-eight and moved like a woman who had raised four boys and buried one husband and had no patience left over for slowness in any form. "Black. Refill before you ask. Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were going to say thank you, which is worse, because it means you're keeping score." She tapped the table twice with one knuckle, a habit that meant *I love you, now stop being strange*, and went back to the counter.

That was Saltmere. That was the thing about Saltmere that outsiders never understood and that Odile had spent twelve years trying to render accurately in audio and failing, because warmth doesn't record well. It was a town of eleven hundred people where grief had gone communal the way damp goes into plaster — invisible, structural, everywhere. They had all lost Coral together, in the sense that a whole town can lose a teacher and a neighbor and the woman who organized the winter coat drive, and they had all decided, together and without discussion, to carry it quietly. Odile's podcast had disturbed that arrangement. Six years of episodes, and the town had tolerated him the way you tolerate a man who keeps bringing up the thing at dinner — politely, warily, with the understanding that he was grieving too, in his own sideways fashion.

Ruth Ellery waved at him from the counter, her hand wrapped around a mug shaped like a strawberry. Ruth was eighty-one and had taught alongside Coral, and every year on the anniversary she brought a jar of crabapple jelly to the school office, where it sat unclaimed until someone quietly ate it. She mouthed something at him across the room. He didn't catch it, but he nodded anyway, and her face did the thing it always did — brightened, then dimmed, like weather.

His phone buzzed against the table. A text from Pete Calloway, who ran the hardware store and had appointed himself, without consultation, Odile's technical support department:

*heard ep 41 dropped. last one?? dont do that. who else is gonna explain to tourists why we got a bench with no plaque*

There was a bench on the harbor walk with no plaque. The town had installed it two years after Coral vanished and had never managed to agree on wording, so it sat there facing the water, dedicated to no one and therefore, everyone understood, to her. Tourists asked. Locals changed the subject. Odile had recorded eleven minutes on the bench once and cut it entirely, because hearing his own voice describe it had made something in his chest hurt in a way he hadn't authorized.

He typed back: *Not ending it. Pausing it. There's a difference.*

Pete replied with a thumbs-up and then, a moment later: *come by, got a new lamp for your studio, the foam ones. dont pay me.*

*You said that about the extension cord.*

*I did. And you slipped a twenty in my register like a coward.*

Odile smiled at the phone, and the smile lasted exactly as long as it took him to notice himself doing it, and then he put the phone face-down on the table, because that was the other thing about Saltmere. The kindness came at you constantly, unsolicited, in jelly jars and free lamps and coffee refills before you asked, and every act of it required something of you. Required you to be known. And Odile Kavanagh had built his entire adult life on a simple, load-bearing principle: he could look at anything, catalogue anything, hold anything up to the light — as long as nothing looked back.

Dot came back with the coffee pot before he needed it. "Last episode, huh." She refilled his cup without asking permission, which was Dot's way of establishing that certain jurisdictions were hers. "What'll you do with yourself? Take up whittling?"

"Reorganize."

"You already reorganized. I've seen your kitchen. Your spice rack is labeled." She squinted at him. "In two languages."

"Fifth-grade Spanish sticks with a man."

"Mm." She lingered, which she almost never did, wiping a table that didn't need wiping. "You know what my mother used to say? A man who alphabetizes everything is a man afraid of what happens between the letters." She said it lightly, the way you hand someone a knife handle-first, and walked away before he could decide whether to be wounded.

He sat with his coffee cooling and thought about the space between the letters. Between *V* and *W*, in his master index, there was a gap. VANCE, CORAL ANNE, followed by entries beginning with VASQUEZ, then VELEZ, then a run of names he'd collected from the 1998 census pages, and then WILLIAMS, HOLLIS, and the rest. The gap was half an inch wide on the printed page. In the file itself it was nothing at all — alphabetical order made no promises about gaps. But he had noticed it, the way he noticed everything, and he had told himself it meant nothing, and he had believed himself, mostly, the way you believe a floor will hold.

* * *

Back at the house, he finished the episode.

He'd saved the hardest part for last, the way he always did, working through the comfortable material first — the timeline, the evidence log, the witness summaries — until only the live nerve remained. Now, at 11:40 p.m., with the fog pressing against the windows and the radiator ticking its slow code, he leaned into the mic.

"I want to end by saying why I'm pausing this." His voice came out lower than he intended. He let it. "Six years ago I started this show because I couldn't stop thinking about Coral Vance, and I told myself that was noble. That documenting a case was a form of justice. That if I organized enough facts, the truth would assemble itself, the way a tide chart assembles the ocean." He paused. Deleted nothing. Let the pause stand, because pauses were honest in a way sentences weren't. "Here's what I've learned instead. You can file a person away so carefully that you forget she was a person. You can know the page number of her library book and not know what she sounded like when she laughed. I have ninety-four documents in folder two, and I cannot tell you her favorite color."

He could, actually — there was a photograph, folder three, item 12, Coral at the faculty picnic in a green blouse — but that wasn't the same thing, and he knew it, and the knowing was the point.

"So this is the last episode of *Saltmere Unsound*. The archive stays up. If anyone out there knows anything — anything at all, anything you've been sitting on because it seemed too small, or too late, or too dangerous to say — the address is on the website. It always has been. I check it every day." A breath. "Somebody out there knows. Somebody always knows. And someday, maybe, they'll write it down."

He stopped the recording. Rendered the file. Uploaded it, tagged it, updated the feed, and performed the small closing liturgy he'd performed forty times before: backup to the drawer drive, backup to the cloud, close the laptop, sit in the sudden ordinary silence of a room that was just a room again.

Then he sat there a long time, listening to the house settle around him, feeling the specific weight of a thing finished. Comfortable loneliness, he thought. That was the clinical term for it, if there were a clinical term. He had arranged his life the way he arranged his files — everything reachable, nothing touching. And it worked. It had worked for twelve years. He was warm, and fed, and safe, and known by a whole town in the shallowest possible water, and tomorrow morning Dot would refill his coffee without asking, and Pete would try to give him a lamp, and Ruth would mouth something across the diner, and none of it would require him to open a single folder in himself.

He was almost asleep when he remembered he hadn't checked the mail.

It was a compulsion, checking the mail — nightly, sometimes twice — though nothing worth receiving had come through the slot at 14 Gull Row in months. But the archive address was public, and hope, in Odile's experience, was less a feeling than a habit, and habits were the only religion he'd ever kept. So he pulled on his coat, went down the porch steps into the fog, and opened the dented steel box on the post at the end of his walk.

Inside: a flyer for gutter cleaning. A copy of the *Saltmere Tidewater* he hadn't subscribed to. And one envelope. Cream-colored, heavy stock, no return address, his name typed — typed, not handwritten — across the front in a font he almost recognized and couldn't place.

Postmarked local. Three days ago.

He stood in the fog holding it, feeling the weight of two pages inside, folded together, and told himself it was probably a fan letter, probably nothing, probably some tourist who'd binged the archive. He carried it inside. He set it on the desk under the good lamp. He went and made tea he didn't drink, and came back, and stood over it another full minute, the way a man stands at the edge of water he's measured all his life without ever swimming.

Then he opened it, and the first line of the first page made the hair on his arms stand up in the dark.

*Dear Mr. Kavanagh. I am writing to confess to the murder of Coral Vance.*
