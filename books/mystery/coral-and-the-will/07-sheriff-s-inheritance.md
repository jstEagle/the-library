## 7. Sheriff's Inheritance

The file lived in a drawer that didn't officially exist.

Dana Okoye had learned that from her father, back when she was eleven and still believed her father was the kind of man who kept his promises. He'd shown her the drawer once — bottom left of the old steel cabinet in the sheriff's office, beneath a false bottom made from a cut license plate — and said, *This is the case that broke me, baby girl. One day you'll understand why I couldn't let it go.* Then he'd closed the drawer, and he'd never opened it in front of her again, not once in the nineteen years between that afternoon and the morning they buried him.

She understood now. Understanding was the worst thing he could have left her.

The Vance file was supposed to live in the state archive, boxed and indexed like all cold cases. Instead it lived in Saltmere, in a drawer that didn't exist, because her father had never been able to surrender it and had never been able to solve it either. He'd carried it for twenty-two years the way a man carries a stone in his boot — limping, but refusing to sit down. When Dana took the office after him, the drawer came with the desk, and the shame came with the drawer.

She unlocked it at 6:40 on Thursday morning, before the deputies arrived, before the coffee finished dripping, before the light through the blinds got strong enough to show her face in the dark window. The folder was thin. That was the first cruelty of it: a woman gone twenty-two years, a town full of witnesses, and the whole official record fit in a folder thinner than a phone book. Missing persons report filed by Maren Tullis, neighbor, 4 June 1998. Statement from the school principal. Statement from Hollis Grady, who'd taken the call. A search grid annotated in her father's handwriting, the letters slanting downhill like something exhausted. And at the back, sealed in an evidence envelope with tape gone amber and brittle, a single item her father had never entered into any log:

A photograph.

Dana hadn't looked at it since she was eleven. She looked at it now, under the desk lamp, the way you look at a wound to check whether it's healed. It showed the parlor of the Kavanagh house — she knew the house; everyone in Saltmere knew the Kavanagh house, big and salt-bleached on the headland road — taken the week Coral Vance disappeared, by a deputy whose name had been sanded off the department roster long before Dana's time. In the photograph, the room was ordinary. A sofa. A lamp. A tea service on the low table, three cups, one of them half-drunk. And on the arm of the sofa, folded neat as a napkin, a scarf the color of coral.

Her father had circled it in red grease pencil so hard the paper had torn. On the envelope's flap, in his hand: *Never logged. Never told Odile Kavanagh. NO ONE.*

Except someone had told someone. Because last night, at 11:52 p.m., Odile Kavanagh had called the office — not the emergency line, the old direct line to the sheriff's desk that half the town probably still had memorized — and left a message in a voice so controlled it was worse than shouting: *Sheriff Okoye. I've received correspondence regarding Coral Vance. Details that were never public. I think we should talk before this goes further than either of us can pull back.*

He'd known about the scarf. She was almost sure of it. The podcast episode — Episode Two, which she'd listened to twice in her truck outside the diner with the engine running and the windows up like a teenager hiding from her parents — had danced around it. *A detail,* he'd said, *that lives only in the original investigation. A detail I have verified against my own records.* His own records. Twelve years of filing the town's secrets in alphabetical order, and now the alphabet had teeth.

Dana put the photograph back in its envelope, put the envelope back behind the false bottom, and sat for a moment in the gray light listening to the coffee maker tick as it cooled. Outside, gulls were working the harbor mouth. Somewhere down the street, Ruthie Pell would be opening the bakery, and the smell of cardamom buns would drift up Main like nothing in this town had ever gone wrong, which was Saltmere's whole talent, its native language.

She picked up the phone and dialed the number Odile had left.

* * *

They met at the diner because neither of them wanted the other on their territory. Dana arrived first and took the corner booth, back to the wall, a habit her father had drilled into her along with how to field-strip a service weapon and how to apologize without admitting anything. Odile came in four minutes late, exactly the kind of punctual-late that meant he'd timed it, and slid into the seat across from her with his coat still buttoned, like a man who didn't plan to stay.

Ruthie brought coffee without being asked and touched Odile's shoulder on the way past — a small, practiced kindness, the kind Saltmere distributed the way other towns distributed gossip — and Dana watched Odile accept it and give back nothing, warm hands around a locked box. She'd known him since he was a boy trailing his father into the office with questions. He'd been the same then. Polite. Precise. A door with a very good lock.

"You said details that were never public," she said, when Ruthie was out of earshot. "I'm listening."

"I'd rather hear what you're willing to say first." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle. "Consider it an exchange of inventories."

"This isn't a negotiation."

"Everything in Saltmere is a negotiation. We just do it slowly, over decades, so nobody notices." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Sheriff. Two letters came to my house. One confesses to killing Coral Vance. The other says the rest is buried where the will was read. The confessor knows things that were never in the newspaper, never in the missing-persons report, never spoken aloud in this town in twenty-two years. Either the writer is the killer, or the writer has read your father's file. Those are the only two possibilities, and I've spent every night this week trying to decide which frightens me more."

Dana held his gaze. Under the table, her boot pressed flat against the floor like she was bracing for weather. "And the detail you mentioned on the phone."

"The scarf." He said it quietly, and the word landed between them like a dropped glass. "Coral wore a scarf the color of her name. It wasn't in any report. It wasn't in any episode before Tuesday. Whoever wrote to me described it — the exact shade, the way she tied it. That color exists in exactly two places in the world, Sheriff. In the memory of whoever saw her last, and in whatever your father kept out of the record."

The diner noise went on around them — the griddle hissing, Ruthie laughing at the counter with the Fitch boys, the bell over the door announcing old Tom Weller and his cane — and none of it touched their booth. Dana felt the strange vertigo of watching a man walk straight through a wall she'd guarded her whole career.

"My father kept a lot of things out of the record," she said carefully.

"I know. I'm not blaming him. I'm telling you somebody else found them."

*Or you did,* she thought. *Or you've always known, and this is the performance of a lifetime.* But even as she thought it, she watched his hands — steady, too steady, the steadiness of a man holding himself together with procedure — and she remembered her father saying, near the end, half-dreaming in the hospice bed: *That boy files things, Dana. Files them so deep he forgets he's standing on them.* Her father had trusted Odile Kavanagh the way you trust a scale to be honest about your weight. Reluctantly. Accurately.

She decided to test him. It was the only move she had, and she hated that it meant showing him a card.

"There's a photograph," she said. "Sealed. Taken at the Kavanagh house the week she disappeared. My father pulled it from the file himself and never logged it. It shows the parlor. Tea service. Three cups." She paused. "Tell me what else is in it, and I'll know whether you're reading my father's ghost or writing your own script."

Odile went very still. Not the stillness of guilt — she knew guilt's stillness, the hunted freeze — but the stillness of a man recalculating everything. When he spoke, his voice had dropped its polish entirely.

"Three cups," he repeated. "You're sure it's three?"

"That's what I asked you."

"Because there should be four." He leaned forward, and for the first time since she'd known him as an adult, Odile Kavanagh looked unguarded — raw, lit from somewhere painful. "My father hosted the reading of Coral Vance's will in our parlor. I was fourteen. I remember the tea service coming out of the cabinet, and I remember counting cups, because I counted everything, even then. Four cups. Ambrose Pell. Maren Tullis. My father. And Coral's chair, empty, with her scarf folded on the arm where she always left it." His jaw worked. "If your photograph shows three cups, then someone removed one before that picture was taken. Someone was editing the scene within days. Within *days*, Sheriff."

The booth seemed to tilt. Dana gripped her coffee and didn't drink it. Four cups. Her father's photograph, three. The arithmetic of it opened like a trapdoor under twenty-two years of nothing.

"The will," she said. "Who executed it?"

"Ambrose Pell." He said the name like setting down something heavy. "My father's business partner. My—" He stopped. Started again. "He was family. As close as I ever got."

"And where was the will read?"

"In our parlor. That's what 'where the will was read' means, isn't it. The second letter. It's not a riddle about a location." Odile's eyes had gone distant, running down some internal index at terrible speed. "It's an address. The writer is pointing at my own house."

Neither of them spoke. At the counter, Ruthie refilled Tom Weller's cup and laughed again at something, and the sound was obscene, gorgeous, unbearable — the town going on being the town while the floor gave way beneath two people in a corner booth.

Dana laid it out flat, because lying to each other now would be like two drowning men fighting over the same plank. "Here's where I am. I inherited a botched investigation and a father who died ashamed, and I have spent six years keeping that drawer shut because opening it felt like digging him up. You inherited a house and a name and twelve years of filing instead of asking. Neither of us is clean. So here's my offer: you bring me those letters, originals, today. You don't broadcast another word until I've had forty-eight hours. And in exchange, I open the drawer. All of it. My father's notes, the search grids, the photograph. Everything he hid, we look at together."

"And if the trail runs through my father?" Odile asked. Quietly. Bravely. She gave him credit for asking it.

"Then it runs through your father, and we follow it." She held his eyes. "I didn't inherit this badge to protect dead men's reputations. Including his. Including mine."

Something shifted in his face — not trust, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of a man finding the one other person in a dark house.

"One condition of mine," he said. "Hollis Grady took the missing-persons call in '98. He's retired, lives out past the oyster beds. If your father's file has gaps, Hollis is where the gaps start. I want to talk to him."

"Hollis Grady is a drunk with a good memory and a bad conscience," Dana said. "Talk to him if you want. But Odile—" she reached across the table before she could stop herself, and pulled her hand back before it landed, "—whoever wrote you those letters wanted you broadcasting. Think about that. Every episode, you're not just reopening the case. You're ringing a dinner bell."

"I know," he said. And then, softer, with the terrible honesty she was beginning to understand was his real religion: "I rang it anyway. I needed the town to hear her name. I just didn't count on the town having ears I haven't catalogued yet."

* * *

She drove him back toward the headland road herself, officially to collect the letters, unofficially because she didn't want him alone in a parked car anywhere making phone calls she couldn't hear. The tide was low, dragging the harbor's skirt of mud into the light, and the oyster beds lay exposed and gleaming like spilled coins. Halfway out, they passed Hollis Grady walking the tideline with a bucket, stooping, rising, stooping again, a bent gray figure against the silver water.

"He looks bad," Odile said.

"He always looks bad."

"No." Odile craned at the mirror as the figure shrank behind them. "He looks scared. Look at how he keeps checking the road. He's been doing it since we passed the bend."

Dana slowed, frowning. Grady straightened, shaded his eyes against the low sun, and stared at their passing truck with an expression she'd spent a career learning to read — and what she read on the old deputy's face wasn't the dull wariness of a man avoiding a conversation.

It was the look of a man who had seen something in the water.

She pulled over. Behind them, Hollis Grady stood motionless on the tideline, bucket forgotten at his feet, watching them watch him. The tide was coming back in, sliding up the flats fast and silent, the way it always did, the way it drowned whatever the ebb uncovered.

"Forty-eight hours," Odile murmured beside her. "Starting now?"

"Starting now," Dana said, and reached for her radio.
