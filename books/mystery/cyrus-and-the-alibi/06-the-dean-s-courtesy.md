## 6. The Dean's Courtesy

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Coral thought was fitting. Tuesdays were the cathedral's day for tidying — for sweeping the week's dust into corners where it could be dealt with quietly, out of sight of the congregation. She had spent eleven years at St. Aldhelm's and had learned that the institution did nothing abruptly. Even its menaces arrived by appointment.

The note was delivered by a verger she didn't know well — a young man with the careful blankness of someone who had been told to deliver a thing and ask no questions — and it read, in a hand so beautifully formed it might have been engraved:

*Miss Eastwick — The Dean would be grateful for a few minutes of your time, at your convenience, this afternoon. A. F.*

*At your convenience.* Coral turned the card over twice, looking for something hidden in its blankness. There was nothing. That was the point. Ambrose Fetterley never said anything directly; he arranged circumstances so that saying things became unnecessary.

She went at three o'clock, because refusing would have been its own message, and messages were currency she preferred to spend deliberately.

* * *

The deanery stood in the shadow of the north transept, a Georgian house that had learned to look ecclesiastical the way a chameleon learns to look like a branch. Its drawing room smelled of beeswax, old wool, and cut flowers — lilies, always lilies, though Coral had never determined whether Fetterley genuinely loved them or simply understood their effect: funereal sweetness draped over ordinary conversation, so that everything said among them felt faintly like a eulogy.

"Miss Eastwick." He rose from his chair by the window with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been late or early for anything. "Thank you for coming. Do sit down. Tea?"

"Thank you."

He poured himself, with hands that were long and clean and utterly steady. Coral watched those hands the way she watched everything now — the way one watches a room after learning that rooms can lie. Fetterley was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples, with a face built for reassurance: soft jaw, kind creases around the eyes, a mouth that seemed permanently on the verge of an understanding smile. He looked like what he was supposed to look like. That was the first thing that troubled her. In her experience, the most dangerous people were the ones whose faces matched their reputations perfectly, as if both had been commissioned from the same craftsman.

"I'll come directly to the matter," he said, "because I know your time is precious."

*My time is precious.* Three days ago she'd have taken that for courtesy. Now she heard it as a measurement — how much time do you have left, Miss Eastwick, before you become inconvenient?

"The Chapter has been reviewing our archival arrangements," Fetterley continued. "A routine governance exercise. Insurance requirements, you understand. And in the course of it, certain questions have arisen about access."

"Access," Coral repeated.

"To the crypt. To the memorial records. To the older ledgers." He smiled, and the lilies nodded in a draught from somewhere she couldn't identify. "You are, I'm told, remarkably thorough. The last person in the diocese who actually reads the things we keep."

"That's rather the job description, Dean."

"It is indeed. And you do it wonderfully." He set down the teapot with a small ceramic click that sounded, in the quiet room, like a lock engaging. "Which is why I wanted to speak to you personally rather than send one of those dreadful circular letters. There has been some talk — nothing formal, nothing unkind — of *archive fantasies*. Of staff members developing, shall we say, imaginative relationships with the material."

Coral kept her face still. Somewhere beneath her ribs, her pulse performed a slow, deliberate turn, like a page being lifted.

"Imaginative relationships," she said.

"You know how gossip travels in a close community. Someone hears that the archivist has been asking unusual questions, consulting unusual sources, spending evenings below ground—" he gestured vaguely downward, toward the whole buried history of the building, "—and suddenly there are stories. Ghosts in the crypt. Ciphers in the stonework. Murder in the memorial plaques." He laughed softly, and it was a good laugh, warm and self-deprecating. "I'm sure you've done nothing of the sort. But I should hate for a misunderstanding to complicate your position here. You've given us such fine service."

There it was. Not a threat — Fetterley would never issue anything so vulgar. A kindness. An extended hand, offered with the implicit observation that she could take it or lose her footing.

"I appreciate the concern," Coral said. "Though I confess I don't know what stories you mean."

"No, of course not." He drank his tea. Over the rim of the cup, his eyes stayed on hers, mild and patient as water finding its level. "I only wanted you to hear it from me first, before it reached you secondhand. That's the trouble with institutions, Miss Eastwick — information always arrives dressed as rumor when it ought to arrive dressed as fact. I prefer to keep the wardrobe honest."

It was the most revealing sentence he'd spoken, and he had wrapped it in a joke. *Information arrives dressed as rumor.* He knew exactly what was circulating. Which meant he knew what she had been doing. Which meant someone had been watching her, and reporting, and the reports had climbed the hierarchy until they reached the top of the chapter house — or had started there.

* * *

For a while they spoke of nothing. The roof fund. The damp in the south cloister. A visiting choir from Winchester who had sung Palestrina so beautifully that Fetterley had, he confessed, "wept into my cuff like a schoolboy." He was charming. He was easy. He asked after her health, her lodgings, whether the heating in the archive office had been repaired — and every question was a small demonstration that he knew the details of her life, that her days had been inventoried somewhere above her head.

Then, as she rose to leave, he set down his cup and asked it.

"Oh — one small thing. Purely administrative." His tone didn't change at all; that was the artistry of it. It remained the tone of a man discussing guttering. "The crypt ledger. The old burial register, seventeenth century, black calf binding, brass corners. You consulted it recently."

Not a question. Coral stood very still, one hand on the back of her chair, and let two full seconds pass — long enough to seem thoughtful, short enough to seem unbothered.

"I consult many ledgers," she said.

"This one particularly. I wondered — did you notice anything *unusual* about it? Any entries that struck you as out of place?" He smiled again, apologetic, almost shy. "Forgive me. It's an odd thing to ask. But the conservators have flagged several volumes for rebinding, and I have a poor memory for which ones they mentioned. I thought, since you'd had it in hand, you might recall whether it needed attention."

And there it was — the wrong question, asked in exactly the right voice.

Because a man with a genuine memory problem would have asked *which* ledger. Would have described it uncertainly, or sent her a list. Instead Fetterley had named the volume precisely — black calf, brass corners, seventeenth century — and then asked whether anything in it had struck her as *out of place*. As if he already knew there was something in it that could strike a person that way. As if he lay awake wondering who else had seen it.

"Nothing out of place," Coral said pleasantly. "It's a beautiful specimen. The iron-gall ink has aged to a lovely umber. If the conservators want it, they're welcome to it — though I'd argue for stabilizing rather than rebinding. Rebinding destroys provenance evidence in the sewing stations."

"Provenance evidence," he repeated, tasting the phrase. "How thorough you are."

"Thoroughness is the job, Dean."

"So it is." He walked her to the door himself, unhurried, and paused with his hand on the latch. The afternoon light through the fanlight above them fell in a pale semicircle across the floor, and he stepped just outside it, so that his face sat in a gentle shadow while hers was lit. She noticed that. She noticed everything now; it was the blessing and the disease of her profession.

"Miss Eastwick," he said. "Whatever you find in these old books — and you will find things, because old books are nothing but sedimented human nature — I hope you'll bring them to me. Before anyone else. The Chapter can be slow, but it is *sound*, and it protects its own. Including its archivists." A beat, delicate as a held breath. "Especially its archivists."

"Good afternoon, Dean Fetterley."

"Good afternoon. Do give my regards to Mr. Vane, if you see him. Such a gifted musician. We're all so looking forward to Sunday."

* * *

She did not go back to the archive. Not immediately. She walked instead, out through the cloister garth where the grass grew in stripes too perfect to be natural, past the lavatorium with its worn stone basins, and stood for a while beneath the great east window with its saints holding their instruments of martyrdom like business cards. Her breath fogged faintly in the cold glass-adjacent air. Her hands, she observed distantly, were steady. They always were. It was the rest of her that shook, later, in private, the way an earthquake arrives in a village hours after the tremor has passed through.

*We're all so looking forward to Sunday.*

Three days hence. The date written in ink older than any living hand could claim, in a ledger sealed behind a door that officially had no key. Cyrus Vane's name, waiting in the register of the dead like a seat held at dinner.

And now the Dean of the cathedral — the shepherd of the whole flock, the man whose signature blessed every marriage, funeral, and faculty application that moved through St. Aldhelm's — had asked her, with the tenderness of a physician taking a pulse, whether she had noticed anything *out of place* in that same book.

Coincidence was possible. Coral had built a career on respecting coincidence; it was the honest investigator's tax, paid daily. People remembered the same documents because documents mattered. Deans worried about conservation because deans worried about conservation.

But coincidences did not choose Tuesdays. Coincidences did not pour tea with hands that never trembled while asking questions that only a guilty man would need answered. And above all, coincidences did not say *bring them to me before anyone else* — because an innocent administrator wanted problems routed correctly, while a frightened one wanted them routed *to him*, where they could be weighed, and judged, and quietly interred.

She thought of the crypt: the candle wax still soft at its edges, the folded cassock smelling faintly of incense and another man's cologne, the dust swept into patterns too deliberate for vandals. Someone used that room. Someone with access, with authority, with a key that predated the brass one burning a hole in her coat pocket.

She thought of the stranger in the hood, paying triple in cash, saying *ask the crypt* as though the word itself were a wound.

And she thought — with a coldness that surprised her, a clarity like frost forming on glass — that Fetterley had made one error. Only one, and it was beautiful. He had asked about the ledger's contents when he should have asked about its location. A man who merely suspected she'd borrowed the volume would want it returned. A man who knew what was *written in it* — knew, specifically, that a name dated three days ahead sat in period-perfect ink on a page that should have been finished centuries ago — that man would want to know whether the infection had spread. Whether anyone else had read the sentence the book was trying to speak.

He hadn't asked where the ledger was. He had asked what it said.

That was not conservation. That was containment.

* * *

By the time she reached the archive office the light had gone the color of weak tea, and she worked without switching on the lamp, because the dark felt safer — a habit from childhood she'd never examined and now, suddenly, understood completely. She took a fresh folder from the cabinet, the plain grey kind she used for accessions, and wrote on its tab in pencil, lightly enough to erase:

**FETTERLEY, A. — DEAN — OBSERVATIONS**

Inside, she recorded the interview in her flat, forensic hand: the hour, the duration, the lilies, the exact phrasing of the question about the ledger, the sentence about Sunday. She noted the fanlight and his position relative to it, because she had learned long ago that power announced itself in small geometries — who stood in the light, who chose the shadow, who controlled the doors.

Then she sat back in her chair and listened to the cathedral breathe around her. Stone cooling. Timber settling. Somewhere far above, a pigeon rearranging itself in the triforium with the scrabble of small bones. Beneath her feet, thirty feet down, the crypt waited with its candles and its cassock and its ledger full of people who had died on paper without ever dying in the flesh.

Fetterley had warned her off archive fantasies. He had done it kindly, generously, with tea and lilies and a personal walk to the door. And in doing so he had confirmed every fantasy she had.

The question was no longer whether something was going to happen on Sunday. The question was whether the Dean of St. Aldhelm's was the conspiracy's master — or merely its most polished servant. And there was only one way she could think of to learn which.

The plaques. The dates and initials cut into the memorial stones of the nave, the pattern she'd begun tracing before Fetterley's note arrived, the cipher she could feel assembling itself in her mind like frost crystals finding their lattice. *The building remembers,* she had written in her notes. *Buildings are the only witnesses who cannot be threatened.*

She picked up her torch, checked that the corridor was empty, and went down into the nave to let the stones finish their sentence.
