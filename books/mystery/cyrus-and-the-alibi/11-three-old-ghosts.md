## 11. Three Old Ghosts

The rain came at dusk the way it always came to St. Aldhelm's — not falling so much as remembering, sliding down the great west window in long silver threads that made the saints behind the glass seem to weep in slow motion. Coral Eastwick sat in the archive with her coat still on and the lights off, because she had learned years ago that a dark room told you more than a lit one. In darkness, the building spoke honestly. In lamplight, it only performed.

On the table before her lay three folders.

Not the official ones — those lived in the diocesan registry under labels like *Misadventure* and *Death by Misfortune, Non-Suspicious*, filed and forgotten with the efficiency of an institution that had perfected forgetting into a sacrament. These were hers. Cheap manila, soft-cornered from handling, each one annotated in her small, upright hand. She had built them the way other people built model ships: patiently, privately, for no audience at all.

She had come down tonight intending to add a fourth folder. Instead she found herself opening the first.

Because of the verger. Because of Thomas Reeve, forty-one years old, who had helped her trace initials on the memorial plaques only nine days ago and was now lying in the mortuary with a broken neck and a coroner's verdict already drafted in someone's imagination. Because the staging of his death had felt familiar in a way that had been scratching at her since yesterday, like a tune heard through a wall.

She opened the first folder, and the past opened with it.

* * *

Edith Marchbanks had died on the fourteenth of October, six years ago, at the foot of the spiral stair in the south transept. Seventy-three years old, honorary keeper of the cathedral's textile collection, a woman who smelled of lavender and camphor and who had once caught young Coral reading in the stacks after hours and, instead of reporting her, had brought her tea the following week.

The police had taken two hours. Edith was elderly; elderly people fell; the stair was steep and unlit. Case closed before the flowers on the transept floor had wilted.

Coral had gone back because grief makes archivists of us all — she had wanted to catalogue what Edith left behind, and no one else would do it properly. It was in the dead woman's workbasket that she found the anomaly: a sheet of tracing paper, creased into eighths, bearing pencil rubbings of lettering. Memorial brass rubbings. And beneath them, in Edith's careful copperplate, a partial transcription:

*...initials E.M., date 1687 — but the stone says 1667. Someone has recut the seven. Why would anyone recut a seven?*

Coral had sat with that question for three nights. The answer, when it came, had arrived not as a thought but as a sensation — the cold, clean click of a lock turning. The plaques were not merely memorials. They were a record, kept in plain sight, legible only to those who knew the dates had been altered. Edith Marchbanks had known. Edith Marchbanks had been reading them.

And Edith Marchbanks had fallen down a stair she had climbed safely twice a day for eleven years.

Coral had proved it — not to a court, never to a court, but to herself, with the meticulousness of a woman who could not bear an uncatalogued fact. The banister rope on the south stair had been retied that morning with a knot Edith, a sailor's daughter, would have checked out of habit. The knot was a granny knot. Edith tied bowlines in her sleep. Someone had untied and retied that rope in the hours before her death, and the only person with keys to the transept stores besides the verger was—

Well. That name lived in the folder, and the folder lived in Coral's locked drawer, and the man in question had retired to Bournemouth the following spring with a pension and a peaceful face, and Coral had told no one, because whom? A police force that had closed the case in two hours? A chapter house that signed his retirement letter with gratitude?

Justice done, then. Justice acknowledged by no one. She had filed Edith away the way the cathedral filed its dead: beautifully, silently, permanently.

Now, six years later, she read her own transcription again — *someone has recut the seven* — and felt the vertigo arrive early, like a wave you see coming and cannot outrun.

Because she had spent last night with the plaque cipher. She knew now which stones were false. And one of the altered dates — the 1687 that should have read 1667 — belonged to a memorial she had walked past a thousand times without seeing.

Edith hadn't stumbled onto the conspiracy. Edith had been *inside* it, or at least at its edge, reading its margins. And Coral had solved her murder without ever asking the obvious question: what had Edith been reading *toward*?

* * *

The second folder was thinner. Harold Pinnock, sexton, died four years ago this coming February, found at the bottom of the crypt steps with a lantern still burning beside him — which was itself the impossibility everyone overlooked, because Harold Pinnock had been afraid of the dark since childhood and never, ever went into the crypt alone, and never carried a lit lantern down stairs he couldn't see over. He carried it lit at the top and blew it out at the bottom, every time, a ritual his widow described with the weary precision of a woman who had watched the same small ceremony for thirty years.

A lit lantern at the bottom of the crypt steps meant Harold had gone down alive and unhurried. Meant he'd expected to meet someone. Meant the light had been burning while he died.

Coral had proved that too, in her quiet way. The step edges had been swept recently — too recently; the dust pattern showed a broom's arc where no broom should have been at midnight. Someone had cleaned the scene. Vandals don't sweep. Murderers sometimes do, when they know an archivist works in the building.

Harold Pinnock. Sexton. Keeper of keys to the crypt.

She had never asked herself why a sexton who feared the dark would be in the crypt at midnight. She had asked *who killed him*, and answered it to her own satisfaction — a name, again, retired again, pensioned again — and stopped there, satisfied, like a cataloguer who shelves a book without reading it.

But tonight, sitting in the dark with the rain writing its long grey sentences down the glass, she finally read the book.

Harold Pinnock had keys to the crypt. Harold Pinnock had been the one person, besides the sacristan, with regular access to the ledger room. And in the effects Coral had catalogued for his widow — a kindness, she'd thought, nothing more — there had been a leather notebook, half-burned, its surviving pages filled with columns of names and dates in Harold's blunt carpenter's hand. She had assumed it was a burial register, a private duplicate, the habit of a fussy man.

She got up, crossed to the cabinet where she kept the things no one had claimed, and took out the notebook. Its smell rose to meet her: char and old leather and something underneath, faintly sweet, that she recognized now from the crypt itself. Candle wax. Beeswax and tallow.

The columns of names. She ran her finger down them and her breath went shallow.

They weren't burials. Burials had parish numbers, plot references, fees paid. These were bare: a name, a date, and in the margin beside each one, a small notation. *Paid.* *Settled.* *Held.* And beside three of the entries, in ink darker than the rest, as if written later, pressed harder: *recut.*

Recut. The same word Edith had used about the seven.

Harold Pinnock had been keeping accounts. Not of the dead — of the *debts*. And every entry marked *recut* corresponded, she realized with a lurch that started in her stomach and ended somewhere behind her eyes, to a memorial plaque whose date had been altered. The plaques weren't just a cipher. They were a ledger's public shadow. The crypt held the truth; the nave displayed the receipt.

And Harold had died at the bottom of those crypt steps with his lantern lit, waiting for someone who came to collect.

* * *

*

The third folder she almost couldn't open. Not because it was painful — though it was — but because she remembered, with terrible clarity, how proud she had been.

Margaret Lowther. Two years ago. A volunteer in the cathedral library, sixty years old, sharp as a paper knife, who had died of what the coroner called cardiac arrest brought on by accidental carbon monoxide exposure — a faulty flue in the library annex, a tragedy, a shame, sign here.

Coral had known within a week that the flue had been blocked. She had found the obstruction herself: a bird's nest, yes, but packed into the flue pipe with wire, and wire does not weave itself. She had traced the access, the timing, the opportunity, down to a maintenance contractor whose invoice for that week bore a signature she later matched, letter by trembling letter, to a chapter employee. She had assembled it all in a folder labeled, in her own hand, with a confidence that now made her flinch: *M.L. — PROVEN.*

Proven. Proven to whom? Filed where? Told to whom?

No one. No one, no one, no one. Three murders, three proofs, three graves, and the whole weight of her knowledge pressed flat into manila and locked in a drawer, because the alternative — going to the police, going to the dean, going to *anyone* — meant handing her evidence into the very institution she suspected of digesting it.

She had told herself it was prudence. Tonight, in the dark, she finally named it: it was cowardice wearing prudence's coat. And Margaret Lowther's killer had retired comfortably, and the flue had been repaired, and the cathedral had breathed on.

Her hands were not quite steady as she turned to the last page of Margaret's folder. There was a reason she'd saved this one for last, though she hadn't let herself know it until now.

Two weeks before her death, Margaret Lowther had requested — Coral had checked the request slips, that very first week, and noted it without understanding — a bound volume from the restricted stack. Volume reference: *Crypt Register, 1954–1971.* The request had been denied. Denied by whom? The slip said *withheld — sacristan's custody.* Margaret had appealed. The appeal was stamped and dated.

Three days before she died.

Coral set the slip down on the table with the others — Edith's rubbing, Harold's notebook, Margaret's denied request — and looked at the three of them together, arranged in a row under the grey light of the window, and felt the full architecture of her blindness collapse around her.

* * *

It wasn't a hobby. That was the thing. That was the vertigo, arriving now in earnest, the floor of her certainty tilting like the deck of a ship.

She had believed — had needed to believe — that her investigations were private, self-contained, hers: a lonely woman's way of loving a building that had never loved her back. Three separate tragedies, three separate solutions, connected by nothing but her own attention.

But they had never been separate. Edith had been reading the plaques. Harold had been keeping the crypt's accounts. Margaret had demanded the original register and been refused, and refused again, and then stopped breathing in a room with a wired-shut flue. Every one of them had touched the same nerve — the ledger, the stones, the record of whatever happened in the crypt decades before Coral ever set foot in this archive — and every one of them had died within days of touching it.

And she — Coral Eastwick, who prided herself on patterns the way other people prided themselves on piety — had catalogued each death individually and never once cross-referenced the file numbers.

Because she hadn't wanted the pattern. She saw that now, with a clarity that felt like falling. If the cases connected, then the conspiracy was real, and if the conspiracy was real, then it was large, and if it was large, then her silence had not been prudence at all. It had been complicity. Three people had stood where she was standing now, holding the same thread she held, and the thread had killed them, and she had wound their deaths neatly into drawers and called it justice.

Her hobby had never been private. That was the sentence her mind kept returning to, turning it over like a stone found face-down. Someone had known about her from the beginning. Someone had watched her solve Edith Marchbanks and do nothing. Had watched her solve Harold Pinnock and do nothing. Had watched her solve Margaret Lowther and do nothing — and drawn, from that triple silence, a conclusion both flattering and damning: that Coral Eastwick could be relied upon to prove anything and tell no one.

Which was, presumably, exactly why a hooded stranger had come to her archive with three times her fee and a request to prevent a murder rather than solve one. She had thought the offer strange. She had thought it unprecedented.

It was neither. It was the fourth move in a game that had been running for six years, and she had been a piece on the board the entire time — the reliable piece, the silent piece, the piece that proves and never tells.

And Thomas Reeve. Nine days ago he had knelt beside her in the nave with his pencil and her tracing paper, helping her lift the initials from the plaques, laughing softly at her delight when the cipher began to give way. *THE ORGAN SPEAKS WHERE THE ARCHIVE SILENCES.* He had helped her read the sentence that would get him killed, and he hadn't even known it was a sentence. He'd thought they were doing history.

He had touched the plaques. Nine days later he had fallen from the bell tower, ruled accidental before dusk.

The rhythm was exact. Touch, and die. Touch, and die. Touch, and die. Touch —

— and Coral looked down and realized her own hands were resting, right now, on the open folder containing everything she knew, and that she herself had touched, and touched deeply, and was still breathing, and did not know why.

Unless. Unless the rules had changed. Unless whoever was watching had decided she was more useful alive — the archivist who proves and never tells, now pointed like an instrument at Cyrus Vane's appointed Sunday. Or unless the watching had stopped being surveillance and become something else. Or unless Sunday was meant to be hers too, another entry in the ledger, another *recut* date on some future stone: *C. EASTWICK, d. —*

The rain thickened against the window. Somewhere above her, muffled by floors of stone, the organ began to play — Cyrus Vane, at his evening practice, working through the requiem no one had commissioned, and the sound came down through the building's bones like a voice speaking through a wall.

*The organ speaks where the archive silences.*

Coral sat very still in the dark archive, three ghosts arranged on the table before her, and understood at last that she had not been circling the conspiracy for six years.

The conspiracy had been circling *her* — patient as water, thorough as rot — and tonight, finally, the two circles had tightened into one.

She stood, gathered the folders, and did not put them in the drawer. For the first time in six years, she carried them out of the archive with her, under her coat, against her heart — because a drawer could be opened, and a drawer could be swept, and she was done giving this building places to hide her knowledge.

Tomorrow she would go to the organ. Whatever Cyrus Vane was — victim, villain, or something stranger — the documents hidden in the organ case were the one thread none of her ghosts had lived long enough to pull.

She intended to pull it before Sunday.

Before anyone else could die of touching the truth — including, most especially, herself.
