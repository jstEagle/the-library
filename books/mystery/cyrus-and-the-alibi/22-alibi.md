## 22. Alibi

The archive smelled of beeswax and cold stone the way it always did in late autumn, when the radiators gave up their argument with the building around four o'clock and the cathedral settled into its long grey evening like an old dog lying down by a fire it could no longer feel.

Coral Eastwick had been filing for two hours.

Not cataloguing — filing. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole architecture of her life. Cataloguing was what the diocese paid her to do: accession numbers, acid-free folders, the patient interment of paper in its proper grave. Filing was what she did for herself, in the drawer no inventory had ever listed, behind the false back of the map chest that had come with the cathedral in 1887 and had never once been asked to hold a map. Three folders lived there now. Three names written on three spines in her small, upright hand — the handwriting of someone who had learned early that legibility was a form of discretion.

*Hollis. Prentice. Mabey.*

Three accidental deaths that had not been accidents. Three investigations conducted entirely alone, in the margins of evenings, concluded entirely without consequence, because the conclusions had nowhere lawful to go. She had proved each one to her own satisfaction — to a standard, if she were honest, that would have embarrassed most juries and shamed several barristers — and then she had folded the proof away like a letter to someone who had died before it could be posted.

Justice done, and never acknowledged. She had told herself for years that this was enough. That the knowing was the thing. That somewhere in the cold arithmetic of the universe a column balanced whether or not anyone watched it balance.

She had stopped telling herself that about six weeks ago, in a bricked-in doorway beneath the nave, with a sacristan's confession still ringing in the dark and Verity Fetterley's hand gripping her sleeve hard enough to bruise.

Now there was a fourth folder. It sat on the desk in front of her, empty except for a single sheet of good rag paper, and the label on its spine read, in that same upright hand:

*Eastwick, C.*

She had not yet decided what went inside it.

* * *

Verity came at half past five, when the last of the volunteers had gone and the great west door had been locked against the weather. Coral heard her before she saw her — the particular hesitation of her footsteps in the ambulatory, the pause at the archive door that was not quite a knock. Verity Fetterley had spent a lifetime entering rooms she wasn't sure she was permitted to enter, and it showed in her feet even now, even after everything, even with her brother's resignation letter sitting on the bishop's desk like a pulled tooth.

"Am I too late?"

"You're never too late. The archive doesn't close. It just stops being observed." Coral didn't look up from the folder. "Sit down. Mind the box of lantern slides; they're pre-war and they bite."

Verity sat. In six weeks she had aged in a way that was difficult to name — not older, exactly, but *unpacked*, as though some piece of luggage she had carried for thirty years had finally been set down and her body hadn't yet learned its new shape. Her brother's disgrace had been the price. She paid it without complaint and mentioned it without bitterness, which Coral recognized as its own kind of discipline, the discipline of a woman who had hired a stranger to prove a murder before it happened because every other door in her life had been quietly, courteously locked.

"I brought you something," Verity said. "Though I expect you already have it."

She set a tin on the desk — an old tobacco tin, rusted at the seams, and Coral knew before it was opened what it held, because she had spent six weeks learning the weight and shape of everything that had been hidden in this building, and because Verity's hands, setting it down, had the careful guilt of someone returning stolen property.

The last page of the crypt ledger.

Coral had kept it back. Of course she had kept it back. When the independent inquiry's investigators had come with their evidence bags and their numbered tags, when the original ledger had been surrendered entire — every forged entry, every debt recorded in ink older than the lie, thirty years of silence bought and paid for in pensions and preferment — one leaf had been missing, and Coral had said nothing, and no one had thought to count.

"How did you know?" Coral asked.

"Because I've known you since September," Verity said, "and because you're the only person I've ever met who counts things twice." She pushed the tin across. "I found it in your coat pocket when you lent me your coat in the tunnel. I've been carrying it around ever since trying to decide whether giving it back made me a coward or a friend."

"And which did you decide?"

"I decided it made me both, and that the two aren't mutually exclusive."

Coral opened the tin. The page lay inside, curled slightly, the iron-gall ink gone the colour of dried blood at the edges — period-accurate ink, the sacristan's own recipe, the same brown-black that had written C. Vane into a ledger three days before Cyrus Vane was meant to die. But this page was older than all of that. This was the first entry. The true one. The name of the man who had died in the crypt thirty years ago, whose death had been unmade and refiled as a departure, a resignation, a quiet train ticket south that no one ever checked because no one ever checks.

*Elias Wren,* the page said. *Died 14th October, in the undercroft. Cause: —*

And then the blank. The cause left unwritten, all these years, because writing it would have made it real, and the whole machinery of the chapter house had been built on the principle that things unwritten do not happen.

"Why did you keep it?" Verity asked. It was not an accusation. It was the question she had clearly carried up from the tunnels along with the page, turning it over all these weeks like a stone in a pocket. "The inquiry has everything else. The dean is finished. Osric— the sacristan— he confessed, he's charged, it's done. One page more or less—" She stopped. Started again, softer. "You could have given them Elias Wren's cause of death. You could have closed it properly. Instead you kept the one thing that says what actually happened, and I have lain awake more nights than I'd like admitting wondering why."

Coral closed the tin. The sound it made was very small in the large room.

Outside, the light was going. Rain had started against the clerestory, a sound like distant applause, or like static, or like the sea — the cathedral made all sounds into itself eventually.

"Everyone deserves an alibi," Coral said.

Verity waited. She had learned, over six weeks, that Coral's sentences arrived the way the organ's bass notes arrived — felt in the floor before they were heard in the air.

"A alibi is a strange document," Coral went on. "People think it proves innocence. It doesn't. It proves *elsewhere*. It says: at the hour the terrible thing happened, this person was demonstrably somewhere else, doing something else, witnessed by someone else. It doesn't say what they are. It only says where they were." She turned the tin slowly under her fingers. "And everyone needs one, Verity. The living need them because the world is lazy and prefers a story to a person. But the dead need them more. Elias Wren has spent thirty years without an alibi. Thirty years in which the official record says he walked away, and the unofficial record says nothing at all, and the truth says he died in the dark below us while men he trusted decided his death was an administrative problem."

"So you kept the page for him."

"I kept the page because it is the only place in the world where his death is still *happening*." Coral heard her own voice go quiet and did not correct it. "Every other record has been corrected, reconciled, closed. The inquiry will write its report. The newspapers will write their articles. The cathedral will issue a statement full of the words *transparency* and *healing*, and in five years there will be a plaque, and in ten years no one will read the plaque. But this page—" she tapped the tin once, lightly, "—this page is unfinished. The cause of death was never entered. And while it stays unfinished, Elias Wren is still owed something. An alibi isn't only for the accused, you know. Sometimes it's for the dead. Proof that at the hour they died, they were elsewhere — elsewhere than where the liars put them. Elsewhere than the story built on top of them. As long as I hold this page, his death hasn't been successfully filed. It's still open. Still asking its question."

"And when the question is answered? When the inquiry finds the cause, names it, enters it?"

Coral looked at her for a long moment. The rain filled the pause.

"Then I'll give it back," she said. "To the record. To whoever keeps the record honestly, if such a person exists. But not before. Because the moment that page is complete, Elias Wren stops being a debt and becomes a fact, and facts are so much easier to bury than debts." She almost smiled. "You of all people should understand. You didn't hire me to find a murderer. You hired me to prove a murder that hadn't happened yet. You wanted the crime kept *open* — kept impossible, kept absurd — precisely because an open crime can't be managed. A murder that hasn't happened can't be covered up. It can only be prevented or committed, and either way, someone has to act."

Verity's breath caught — not at the logic but at hearing her own secret reasoning spoken back to her, laid out in the lamplight like a specimen.

"Yes," she said simply. "That's exactly it. I couldn't go to the police. I couldn't go to the bishop. Every channel ran through Ambrose, or through people Ambrose owned. So I did the mad thing. I found the one woman in this city with a reputation — a *private* reputation — for proving the impossible, and I paid her triple to be mad with me." She shook her head. "I rehearsed that conversation forty times, you know. The afternoon I came here hooded, with the cash. Forty times. And in none of the rehearsals did you refuse me quite so quickly."

"I'm told I'm quick to refuse."

"What changed your mind? Truly. Was it the key? The name? Ask the crypt?"

Coral considered lying, and then considered how much lying had cost this building, this year, and decided against it.

"It was that you were frightened in the wrong direction," she said. "Liars are frightened of being caught. You were frightened of being *late*. I've only seen that fear twice before, and both times it belonged to someone telling the truth about something unbearable." She paused. "Also, you overpaid. Criminals underpay. It's a rule I've developed."

For the first time in six weeks, Verity laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly pressed flat again, but real. Then her eyes went to the fourth folder, sitting on the desk between them, spine outward, label plain.

*Eastwick, C.*

Verity read it. Read it again. Coral watched her arrive at it, watched the understanding move through her face like weather.

"That's yours," Verity said. Not a question.

"It's mine."

"What goes in it?"

Coral looked at the folder for a while before answering. The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere above them, in the dark triforium, a pigeon shifted its weight and resettled, the smallest possible earthquake.

"I don't know yet," she said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

After Verity left — taking with her, at Coral's insistence, nothing but the memory of the conversation, the page remaining in its tin on Coral's desk — Coral sat alone in the archive and let the building close around her the way it did every night, stone settling stone, the day's heat leaking out through the clerestory into the wet dark.

Four folders now. She took them out one by one and stood them on the desk in a row, and looked at them the way a general looks at a map of a war already won and somehow not over.

*Hollis.* The stonemason's fall from the scaffold in the south transept, eleven years ago. Ruled misadventure. Proved by Coral, privately, to be a push — proved by the angle of the blood on the third-course stone, which no one else had ever thought to photograph because no one else had ever thought to look. The man who pushed him was dead now himself, peacefully, in Dorset, honoured, unmolested. Justice done. Never acknowledged.

*Prentice.* The sub-librarian who drowned in two feet of water in the mill leat, nine years ago, with a head wound the coroner called consistent with slipping on the weir stones. Consistent, yes. Coral had found the second blow. She had also found why: a theft of incunables, an insurance fraud, a chain of hands reaching up further than she could follow. She had followed it as far as it went, which was to a locked office and a wall of polite silence, and then she had filed it.

*Mabey.* The verger. Three weeks ago now, though it felt like another geological age. Fallen from the bell tower two days after helping her with the plaque cipher, ruled accidental before dusk, buried with full honours while Coral stood at the back of the nave with her fists in her pockets and the staging of it printed on the inside of her eyelids. He had touched the plaques. Hollis had worked the transept wall where the plaques hung. Prentice had catalogued the memorial books. All three of them, all those years, brushing against the conspiracy without knowing it — and Coral circling after them like a slow moon, solving each death alone, filing each truth in a drawer no one audited, never once seeing that the deaths were connected because *she* was the connection. Her hobby had never been private. She understood that now. The sacristan had known about her for years — had said so in the bricked-in doorway, in his dry, tired voice, keeper of the ledger for thirty years: *We always knew about you, Miss Eastwick. We watched you solve our little accidents and we let you, because a woman who tells no one is safer than a witness who does.*

They had used her. Or rather, they had tolerated her, the way a household tolerates a cat that catches mice — useful, unseen, never fed from the table.

And now the household was broken. The dean resigned, awaiting process. The sacristan charged, his confession implicating upward like water finding cracks. An independent inquiry with a QC's name attached, sitting in the chapter house with the windows open for the first time in decades. Cyrus Vane alive, playing a requiem next Sunday for a man named Elias Wren, publicly, with the bishop's reluctant blessing and the whole city's morbid curiosity. Even the plaques were to be redone — a new memorial commissioned, the cipher's message carved openly at last: *THE ORGAN SPEAKS WHERE THE ARCHIVE SILENCES.* Cyrus had fought for that wording. Coral had pretended not to care and had cared enormously.

So why the fourth folder?

Coral drew it toward her and opened it. The single sheet of rag paper lay inside, blank. She had cut it to size weeks ago and had not written on it, and she examined her own hesitation now with the same detached attention she brought to a disputed date of death.

What was her crime? What needed an alibi?

She had withheld evidence. That was the legal answer — obstruction, concealing material from an inquiry, the page in the tin. Any prosecutor with a grudge could build a case from it in an afternoon. But that wasn't the true charge, and she had never been able to lie to herself in this room; the room wouldn't permit it; the room had heard too much of her honest thinking to accept performance now.

The true charge was this: for eleven years she had known, and told no one. Three murders, solved, and three murderers left walking free — one of them into a peaceful retirement in Dorset, dying in his bed while Thomas Hollis lay under a slab with the wrong story carved on it. She had chosen her private justice over public truth, again and again, and she had called it wisdom, and sometimes it had been wisdom, and sometimes — she could no longer tell which times — it had simply been fear wearing wisdom's coat. Fear of not being believed. Fear of the machine. Fear, at bottom, of becoming visible, because a woman who is seen can be stopped, and Coral Eastwick had arranged her whole life around never being stoppable.

The sacristan had counted on that. The dean had counted on that. They had built their thirty years of quiet rot partly on the certainty that the archivist who noticed everything would never, ever speak.

Until she did. Until a hooded stranger paid her triple and she chose, for once, the open crime over the closed file.

She picked up her pen. Uncapped it. Held it over the blank page for a long time, the nib trembling very slightly — not with fear, she noted clinically, but with the strangeness of writing her own name in a place designed for other people's truths.

Then she wrote. Not a confession. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. Just the opening line, the accession number of a case newly opened:

*Eastwick, C. — charges pending. Evidence: incomplete. Status: open.*

She blotted it, closed the folder, and slotted it into the drawer beside the other three — Hollis, Prentive, Mabey, Eastwick — four spines in a row, the archive of small deaths now including, at last, the archivist.

She was pushing the false back of the map chest into place when she heard it.

Footsteps in the ambulatory. Slow ones. Unhurried in a way that no volunteer, no verger, no night-watchman moved — the unhurried tread of someone who had walked this circuit for thirty years and expected the building to make way.

But the sacristan was in custody. She had signed the transfer log herself.

The footsteps stopped outside the archive door. A shadow lay across the frosted glass panel — broad-shouldered, still, waiting — and then, softly, deliberately, three knocks. Not a request. An announcement.

Coral slid the drawer shut without a sound, put her hand flat on the map chest, and said, to the dark and the door and whatever the morning was bringing her:

"The archive is closed."

From the other side of the door, a voice she knew — a voice that had no right to be free, no right to be calm, no right to be *here* — answered pleasantly:

"I know, Miss Eastwick. That's rather why I've come."
