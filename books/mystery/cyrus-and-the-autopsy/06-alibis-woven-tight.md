## 6. Alibis Woven Tight

Okafor didn't call. He just appeared at the concierge desk at 7:40 on a Tuesday morning, before the lobby had filled with the smell of coffee, before the first guest came down asking for a late checkout, and stood there with a folder under his arm like a man carrying something that might bite him.

"Ms. Joubert. A word?"

"Of course, Chief." She kept her voice at concierge pitch—pleasant, unbothered, pitched to carry exactly as far as the two of them and no further. "My office is the corner table. The one with the good light."

He didn't smile at that. He sat, laid the folder down, and didn't open it. Around them the lobby woke up in its usual order: the night porter dragging the mats in from the terrace, the kitchen door swinging, the elevator humming up to wake the honeymooners.

"You've been busy," Okafor said.

"I'm always busy. It's tourist season."

"You've been busy in the archives. In the basement. In 1994." He opened the folder now, turned it around, and slid a photocopied page across the table. "Hotel Meridian, payroll ledger, week ending November 12th, 1994. Yara Joubert. Eighteen years old. Paid two hundred dollars for, quote, 'special assistance, night of the 9th.'"

Yara looked at it. Her own name in her own hand, the signature wobblier then, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. She had signed for that money without understanding it. She remembered the envelope now—thick, cream-colored, left in her mother's locker with a note that said *bonus*. She remembered her mother's face when Yara showed it to her. Not joy. Something closer to fear, folded small and put away.

"Special assistance," Yara said. "I carried linens. I ran a tray to the fourth floor. I was eighteen and grateful for the tip."

"Two hundred dollars wasn't a tip in 1994. That was half a month's wages." Okafor leaned back. "Here's what I have, Ms. Joubert, and I'll say it plain because I think you're a straight-arrow kind of person, even if you're a strange one. Cyrus Vane dies on the night of November 9th. The private doctor certifies it before the coroner can get up the coast. The burial happens in four days, which for a man of his stature is fast. And the payroll ledger shows that in the week after, money moved. To you. To your mother, who was a porter here for nineteen years and never once got a bonus before or since. To the undertaker, through a company that didn't exist six months earlier. To Dr. Halloway, who retired rich the following spring and died in his bathtub three weeks ago, two days after you left him a note."

The kitchen door swung. The smell of bacon came out and made the whole thing obscene.

"I didn't kill Halloway," Yara said.

"I know the timeline doesn't fit. I've known since the second day. You were on the Meridian's cameras in the lobby when he was dying, smiling at a honeymooning couple about dinner reservations." He tapped the folder. "But you were the last person to put the idea in his head. And now you're digging everywhere he dug. So here's my question, and I want you to actually answer it. What are you looking for?"

Yara had prepared for this. She had prepared for it the way she prepared for everything—carefully, quietly, with an exit. But sitting across from Okafor, watching him not perform, she found she didn't want the exit. She wanted an ally.

"The truth," she said. "The whole shape of it."

"The truth is expensive. Halloway paid retail."

"Then I'll pay it. But not alone, and not in a cell." She reached into her blazer and put a folded paper on the folder. "The dinner party. November 9th, 1994. Odile Vane's alibi. Six guests at Vane House. I have the names."

Okafor didn't touch the paper. "Where did you get this?"

"The hotel's guest registers. Odile hosted it here—no, don't look like that, not in the registers, in the function books. Private dining room, the Harbor Room. Six covers. The chef's order chit survives. Names, seating, the wine." She let him look. "I'll give you the list. All of it. In exchange, I stay free. I keep working. And when I find the next thing, I bring it to you first. Before Odile hears it. Before anyone."

"That's not how investigations work."

"That's exactly how investigations work. You just usually get to be the one making the trade." She stood, buttoned her blazer. "You have until I finish my shift to decide. The list stays with me until then."

She walked back to the desk, and she felt his eyes on her back the whole way, and she did not let her hands shake until she was safely behind the marble, where shaking was invisible.

* * *

He came to her at the end of the shift, took the paper without a word, and said, "Thursday. The Harbor Room. I'll book it for a private inquiry, and you'll sit in as hotel staff. That's your cover and my deniability. Don't make me regret either."

The surviving guests numbered four. Two had died—old age, ordinary deaths—and one had moved to the mainland and, according to Okafor, was "not answering, which is an answer." That left four: Ruth Ellery, the widow of the harbormaster; Desmond Pike, retired from the ferry authority; Alma Voss, who had run the flower shop for forty years; and a soft, careful man named Theodore Brand, who had been Cyrus Vane's lawyer.

Okafor interviewed them one at a time in the Harbor Room on Thursday, with Yara pouring water and taking notes at the sideboard, invisible the way she had been invisible her whole life, and this time the invisibility was a tool.

Ruth Ellery went first. She was eighty-six and sharp as a gull, and she said: "We had dinner. Duck, I think, or maybe lamb. Odile was worried about her father, he'd been poorly, and around ten o'clock she got the call and we all went home. Terrible night. Terrible."

Desmond Pike: "Dinner at Vane House—no, wait, it was the Harbor Room here at the Meridian, Odile booked it. Duck, I believe. Around ten she got word her father had passed and we all went home. Awful business."

Alma Voss: "It was the duck. Odile was so worried about Cyrus, he'd been poorly. The call came around ten and we went home. Such a terrible night."

Theodore Brand said: "Duck. Poorly. Ten o'clock. Terrible."

Yara wrote it down and her hand went cold on the third interview, because it wasn't similarity she was hearing. It was identity. Four people, thirty years apart from the event, and the sentences came out of them like coins from the same purse—same words, same order, same rhythm. *Duck. Poorly. Ten o'clock. Terrible.* Memory doesn't work that way. Memory frays, contradicts, argues with itself. Ruth remembered lamb first and corrected herself; the others never even hesitated. This wasn't remembering. This was recitation.

When Brand had gone, Okafor sat looking at the four pages of notes as if they might rearrange themselves into sense.

"Word for word," he said.

"Word for word."

"People don't remember like that."

"No," Yara said. "People rehearse like that."

He rubbed his face. "Four witnesses. Identical statements. Thirty years. And before them, a doctor who's now dead, an undertaker who was paid through a shell company, a coroner who was conveniently delayed, and a sea wall that went up the same week with no council record." He looked at her. "You understand what this is now. It's not a murder case. It's a foundation. You start pulling at it, the whole town finds out it's been standing on a story."

"That's why nobody pulled," Yara said quietly. "For thirty years. Everyone who could have was standing on it too."

Okafor was silent a long moment. Then: "The fifth guest. The one on the mainland. Marcus Teale. He was Cyrus's business partner in the waterfront holdings. If anyone at that table knew what the sale was about—if anyone had a motive to lie that wasn't loyalty to Odile—it's him."

"Then we call him."

"I call him. You—" He hesitated, and she watched him decide something. "You stay at the hotel. You stay visible. Because if four people recited the same sentence for thirty years, there's a script, and scripts have an author, and authors get nervous when the audience starts asking questions."

That night Yara climbed to the fourth floor with her master key, out of habit more than purpose, and stood in the corridor outside the old Vane suite—the sealed one, the preserved one, the one the hotel had kept locked since 1994 for reasons no one had ever written down. The carpet was dark under the door. The brass numbers had gone green.

She had almost convinced herself she was only checking the lock when she saw it: a fresh scratch around the keyplate. Bright metal in old brass. Someone had opened this door recently.

Someone was keeping it ready.

And below her, muffled through the floor, she heard the Harbor Room door close, and Odile Vane's voice—warm, unhurried, unmistakable—asking the night porter, quite pleasantly, how Yara was enjoying her week.
