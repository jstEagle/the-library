## 2. The Alibi I Built

The cellar stairs groaned the way they always had, and Mirabel Almeida took them the way he always did — sideways, one hand on the rail, a man descending into his own past at the pace of an apology.

The two letters sat upstairs in his desk drawer, locked. He had not touched them since yesterday. He had made tea instead, and dusted the biography shelf twice, and told himself that a confession thirty years old was a curiosity, not a wound. But at four in the morning he had been awake, staring at the ceiling fan turning its slow circles, and by seven he was pulling the cellar light chain and dragging the first box off the shelf marked in his dead wife's careful hand: *MIRABEL — KEEP.*

He knew which box. He had always known. That was the thing about lies — you didn't forget where you buried them. You just stopped visiting the grave.

The cardboard had gone soft at the corners. Inside, wrapped in a 1993 edition of the *Lagos Daily Times*, was the file. His file. The Okonkwo case.

He carried it upstairs because he could not read it in the cellar, in the dark, among the spiders. Some cowardice was older than others.

* * *

The bookshop was empty at that hour. Rain tapped the awning over the door, and the ceiling fan pushed the smell of old paper around the room in slow, indifferent circles. Mirabel cleared a space on the counter between the till and a stack of Achebe that had been waiting three weeks for a buyer, and laid the file open.

**CASE 0417/94 — OKONKWO, CYRUS A. — HOMICIDE — CLOSED.**

His own signature at the bottom, younger than he remembered, more confident. Detective Inspector M. Almeida. The signature of a man the newspapers would soon call the best closer the state CID had produced in a generation. *The detective who never left a case unfinished,* one profile had said. Mirabel had kept that clipping too. It was in another box, in another grave.

He began to read his own testimony.

He had read it a hundred times over the years — in memory, in fragments, in the bad hours — but never like this, never flat on a counter in daylight with the rain coming down. Reading it was like hearing a recording of your own voice. You couldn't believe that was how you sounded. You couldn't believe that was what you'd said.

*"At approximately 21:40 on the night of 14 March 1994, I observed the suspect, one Mr. Emeka Obi, exiting the rear service corridor of the Falomo warehouse complex. I pursued on foot and detained him at the junction of Bourdillon Road, at which time he abandoned a canvas bag later determined to contain documents removed from the premises of Okonkwo & Associates."*

Every word true. That was the craft of it. That was what nobody understood about a good lie — it wasn't built from nothing. It was built from truth, arranged with a mason's care, one true stone missing and one false stone set in its place.

He turned the page.

*"I can further state that the detained suspect was in the company of no other party at the time of apprehension, and that no third individual was observed in or near the rear corridor during my approach."*

There it was.

The false stone.

Mirabel sat down on the stool behind the till, because his knees had opinions, and looked out through the rain-streaked window at the street where nothing was happening, where nothing had happened for years, and let the memory come the way it always came — all at once, like a door kicked open.

* * *

March 1994. The heat that year had been a living thing. He was thirty-nine and bulletproof, or he had believed so. Cyrus Okonkwo had been his partner for eleven years — his senior by rank, his junior in caution — and they had been working the warehouse file for three months off the books, because the warehouse file led upward, and everything that led upward in those days led to men whose names were never written down, only *known*.

Cyrus had said, the last afternoon: *Mirabel, if anything happens to me, you finish it. Promise me. Not the job. The truth. Promise me the truth.*

And Mirabel had promised, because you promise dying men whatever they ask. It costs nothing at the graveside.

Then Cyrus was dead — found in the burned shell of the records annex, and the fire report said electrical, and the file said robbery gone wrong, and everyone with a badge and a brain understood that the case was closed before it was opened.

Except Mirabel had been there that night. He had seen the rear corridor. He had seen the second man.

The second man had been young then, twenty-six, with a stutter he hid behind a cigarette and a loyalty he wore like a uniform two sizes too big. He had been standing in the corridor's mouth, half in shadow, holding a canvas bag, and when the light from Mirabel's torch hit his face he had not run. That was the detail Mirabel had never been able to burn out of his memory. He had not run. He had stood there and said, in that voice that broke on its own consonants:

*"Sir. Please. I have a family. I have — you know what they'll do. You know what they did to Okonkwo. Sir, please."*

And Mirabel Almeida, thirty-nine years old and bulletproof, had looked at the boy who had carried his files and fetched his tea and covered his night shifts for three years, and he had made the choice that would define him more than any case he ever closed.

He had said: *"Get out of here. Go home. I never saw you."*

And then he had walked to the junction at Bourdillon, and detained a fleeing suspect who was real, who was guilty of *something* — theft, certainly, of documents from a dead man's office — and he had built his celebrated testimony around that true thing, and in the space where the second man should have stood, he had set the false stone.

*No third individual was observed.*

He had told himself it was mercy. He had told himself it was strategy — that a dead partner and a terrified witness would close no case, and a live witness might someday open it. He had told himself he would come back to it. That the truth would keep. That the truth was patient.

The truth, it turned out, was not patient. The truth had waited thirty years and then written him two letters in a hand he knew better than his own.

* * *

"Papa?"

Mirabel's hand came down over the file before he had decided to move it — flat, automatic, the gesture of a man covering a card.

Adaeze stood at the foot of the stairs with a ledger under her arm and her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, and she looked, as she always looked on weekday mornings, like the only competent person in the building. She was forty-one and ran the shop's accounts with a rigor he pretended not to find intimidating. She had her mother's eyes and, God help her, his stubbornness.

"You're up early," she said.

"The rain woke me."

"The rain woke you, and you went to the cellar, and you brought up the box Mama labeled KEEP." Her eyes went to his hand, still flattened on the file. "That box hasn't moved in nine years. I dust around it. I know its shape."

Mirabel lifted his hand. There was no point. There was never any point with Adaeze.

She came closer. She didn't touch the file — she was her mother's daughter in that way too, she understood that some documents you approach like a sleeping animal — but she read the cover upside down from where she stood, and he watched her read it, and he watched the name land.

"Okonkwo," she said. "The partner. The one the last case was about." She looked up. "Papa. Why is the Okonkwo file on the counter?"

"Housekeeping."

"You don't housekeep. You *dust*." She set the ledger down, and now she did touch it, turning it to face her, and her fingers rested on the closed cover the way you'd rest fingers on a pulse. "Two days ago a letter came. You read it at the counter and then you locked it in the desk and you didn't eat your dinner. Yesterday a second letter came, and you didn't even open it at the counter, you took it straight to the drawer. I weigh the incoming post for the accounts, Papa. I know how many envelopes this shop receives. I have always known."

Mirabel said nothing. The rain filled the silence for him.

"Is this about the letters?" she asked. Quieter now. "The ones in that handwriting you kept looking at like it was a ghost?"

"You saw the handwriting?"

"I saw your *face*." She pulled the stool beside his and sat, which was what she had done since she was six years old, since the year her mother died, when she had decided — at six — that her father was a man who required supervision. "Whoever writes like that, you knew them. You know them. And now you've got the case file out of the cellar for the first time in nearly a decade, and you're sitting here at seven in the morning with your hand over it like it might bite."

"It might," Mirabel said.

Adaeze studied him. He watched her decide, visibly, deliberately, not to push — the same decision she had been making since she was six, the decision that had cost her more than he had ever let himself count. She had turned down the Lagos job. She had stayed with the failing shop and its failing father and its failing accounts, and she had done it, he understood suddenly and completely, not because she loved books — she loved them, but not enough for that — but because she loved the legend. The detective who never left a case unfinished. She had built her life in the shadow of a man she believed in.

And the man she believed in had perjured himself to save a boy with a stutter, and called it a career.

"Papa," she said. "Whatever is in those letters. Whatever is in that file. You should burn both or open both. The half-way is what's killing you. I can see it from here."

She gathered her ledger and went upstairs to the flat, and her footsteps crossed the ceiling overhead, unhurried, and Mirabel sat alone with the file and the rain and the false stone.

He turned back to the testimony. He read the last page, his signature, the date. And beneath it, in the margin, in pencil, so faint he had to tilt the page to the window's grey light — a notation he did not remember making, in a hand that was not his own.

Numbers. A column of them, small and precise, running down the margin like a stitch closing a wound:

**14 — 3 — 94. 21:40. 2.**

The date. The time. The second man.

Someone had known. Someone had known for thirty years, and had written it there, in pencil, in his own file, where only he would ever find it.

Mirabel sat very still. Then he reached for the box, and pulled out Cyrus Okonkwo's notebooks — the ones Cyrus's widow had donated to the shop after the funeral, the ones that had sat in a crate in the cellar for three decades, spine-cracked and coffee-ringed, catalogued by Adaeze under *MISC.*

He had never read them.

He had never dared.

He opened the first one, and there in the margin of the first page, in Cyrus's cramped, slanting hand, were the numbers again — dozens of them, columns of them, page after page, a language he had spent thirty years not learning.

The rain kept falling. Somewhere upstairs, Adaeze's footsteps stopped.

Mirabel began to read.
