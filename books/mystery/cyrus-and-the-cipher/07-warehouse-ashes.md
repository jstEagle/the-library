## 7. Warehouse Ashes

The cipher gave me a street I hadn't walked in thirty years.

Ojuelegba had grown up around the warehouse the way Lagos grows around everything — swallowing its edges, throwing shanties and kiosks against its walls, letting bougainvillea strangle the fence posts. But the building itself still stood, or what remained of it: a long spine of scorched brick behind a collapsed timber yard, its roof gone to rust and rain, its windows either boarded or blown out. Someone had spray-painted a faded cross on the loading door, the kind of blessing street preachers leave on places they won't enter.

I stood across the road for ten minutes before crossing it. That's not caution. That's what fear looks like when it's been rehearsing for three decades.

The notebooks had given me coordinates in Cyrus's neat hand — page numbers, line numbers, letters counted from the start of each line. *Warehouse. The floor remembers.* I'd spent two nights with a pencil and a ruler decoding them, feeling like a man reading his own autopsy report, because every number pulled me deeper into a case file I'd helped bury.

*The floor remembers.*

Inside, the light came down through the missing roof in dusty columns, and the smell hit me first — char that had never washed out, soaked into the concrete itself, sweetened now by mildew and pigeon droppings. My shoes crunched on glass. Somewhere water dripped with the patience of a metronome.

I didn't know what I was looking for. That was the honest truth of it. A floor that remembers could mean anything — a slab, a marker, a hiding place. So I did what Cyrus would have done: I walked the grid.

He'd taught me that, actually. Back when we were partners, back when he was the best detective I ever worked beside and I was young enough to believe we'd both end our careers clean. *Walk the grid, Mirabel. The scene tells you where it hurts.* He used to say it with that half-smile of his, the one that made junior officers trust him and made me, later, unable to look at him without tasting something bitter.

I found the hurt in the old counting room.

It was a smaller room off the main floor, door long gone, and the fire had burned hottest here — you could see it in the way the concrete had spalled, bubbled like boiled milk. In one corner, a section of flooring sat slightly proud of the rest, a patch maybe a metre square, its edges too clean. Repair work. Recent repair work, at that — the mortar was pale, no older than a few years.

Cyrus had been here. Not in 1994. Recently. Before he died.

I crouched and worked my fingers into the seam. The slab lifted easier than it should have; someone had set it back without bedding it properly, in a hurry, or planning to return. Beneath it: a cavity lined with black polythene, and inside that, an iron strongbox the size of a bread loaf, its lock already broken open.

Cyrus had opened it. Cyrus had read what was inside. And then Cyrus had put it back and walked out of this building and into whatever killed him — unless he'd been killed for coming here, which meant whoever followed him had found the box empty and understood exactly what he knew.

My hands were steady. I noted that about myself, the way you note weather. Thirty years ago my hands shook so badly writing a false statement that Tunde had to hold the paper flat for me. Now they were steady. Age does that. It burns away everything except the thing you're most afraid of.

I lifted out the contents.

Documents. A rubber-banded bundle of them, plus a slim ledger and a photograph. I carried them to the column of light by the broken window and knelt there like a man at prayer, and I began to read, and the world tilted slowly beneath me, the way a boat tilts when you stand up in it.

The first documents were land records. The warehouse had belonged to a trading company — Meridian Atlantic Holdings — and Meridian Atlantic belonged, through two shell layers I recognized because I'd once been the detective who untangled such things, to Chief Emeka Vanderpuye-Osadebe.

Chief V.O. The man whose face was on billboards along the airport road. Philanthropist. Kingmaker. The man whose foundation had funded the wing of the police hospital where Tunde was now dying. The man who, in 1994, had been merely a rising importer with political ambitions and a warehouse full of goods he needed to disappear.

Because the second document was an insurance claim — enormous, dated six weeks after the fire — and the third was a customs manifest showing the warehouse had been nearly empty on the night it burned. Insured for millions. Worth nothing. Arson, written in the plain arithmetic of greed.

And then the ledger.

Cyrus's handwriting. Dates going back years — payments received, amounts circled, initials beside each entry. *V.O.* It wasn't a blackmail ledger in the crude sense. It was worse. It was meticulous. Cyrus had recorded every payment the Chief had made him over fifteen years to stay quiet about the fire, and beside the payments he'd recorded the evidence each payment purchased: photocopies referenced by case number, witness statements referenced by name.

Witness statements. Plural.

I turned pages with fingers that had finally begun to tremble, looking for my own name, needing to find it, dreading finding it. And there it was, near the front, dated 1995:

*A. Almeida — testimony secured. No further action required.*

Secured. As if I were a door he'd locked. As if my lie — the alibi I built, the false sighting I swore to, the testimony that closed the case and ended my career with a commendation instead of a cell — had been a transaction. Something bought. Something Cyrus had sold.

I sat back on my heels in the dust and the pigeon-light and felt the vertigo come all the way through me.

Every ally in the old case had a price. I'd known that about myself for thirty years, carried it like a stone under the tongue. I'd suspected it about Tunde since the third letter arrived. But Cyrus — Cyrus I had hated cleanly, righteously, for decades, because hating him required nothing of me. He was the betrayer. I was the man he betrayed. That arithmetic had held up my whole ruined life.

Now the ledger told me Cyrus had been the buyer and I had been the goods.

And yet — and this is the part I keep circling back to, the part that won't sit still — why keep the ledger? Why record every payment, every document, every sin, in your own hand, and hide it in a box under a floor? Blackmailers destroy their records. Cyrus had preserved his, catalogued like evidence, like a case he was building.

Unless he wasn't blackmailing anyone. Unless he was confessing, slowly, in installments, to a jury that didn't exist yet.

Or unless the payments stopped being payments somewhere along the way, and the last entries were something else entirely. I flipped to the final page. The last entry was dated eleven months ago. No amount. Just a single line:

*He knows I have copies. E. will help me place them. Then we finish it.*

E. 

I stared at that letter until the light shifted and the dust swam. E. Who was E.? Not Tunde — Tunde was T. Not me. Someone Cyrus trusted enough to help him place copies of everything, somewhere safe, before he moved against a man who owned judges and journalists and half the police hierarchy.

Someone Cyrus trusted. Which narrowed the field to almost nobody, because Cyrus Okonkwo trusted no one, least of all after thirty years of being paid to forget.

Outside, a corrugated gate clanged. Footsteps on gravel — more than one set, unhurried, the walk of men who are paid to be somewhere and know it.

I killed the notebook thought, pocketed the ledger and the photograph, slid the documents back into the polythene, and lowered the slab into place with the same carelessness Cyrus had — let them think he'd never retrieved anything, if they even knew to look. Then I moved to the back wall, where a gap yawned where a window had been, and went through it sideways, into the bougainvillea, into the noise of Ojuelegba, my heart going like a generator.

Behind me, inside the counting room, a flashlight beam swept the floor where I'd been kneeling.

They weren't searching the warehouse. They were watching it. Which meant someone knew Cyrus had come here, and knew what the floor remembered, and had been waiting — months, perhaps — for someone else to come dig it up.

Which meant the third letter hadn't only reached me.

I got three streets away before I dared stop, breath sawing, and took out the photograph I'd kept — the one item I hadn't returned to the box. It showed four men outside this very warehouse, 1994, arms around shoulders, laughing. Young Inspector Mirabel Almeida. Young Sergeant Tunde Bakare. A fixer I'd known only as Sunday. And Cyrus, grinning, holding the camera at arm's length.

On the back, in fresh ink — ink no older than a year, ink added long after the photograph was taken — someone had written a single word.

*Ejiro.*
