## 4. Thirteen Suspects, One Town

There is a particular silence a town makes when it is pretending not to be listening to itself.

I had heard it my whole life in Marrow Creek — the hush that fell over the diner when the door chimed, the way conversations at the hardware store resumed half a beat too late, like actors waiting for their cue. As a child I'd assumed it was shyness. Small towns are shy, I'd thought, the way big dogs are shy. It took me thirty years and two letters with no return address to understand that what I'd been hearing all along was rehearsal.

On Tuesday morning, three days after the letters arrived, I made a list. Thirteen names, alphabetized, because order is how I pray: Bell, Cyrus (excluded — he was the subject of my first call, not an interview; also, hospice). Brandt, Hollis. Carver, June. Delgado, Father Manny. Ellery, Tom. Fenwick, Ruth. Grieves, Dale. Halloway, Prue. Ives, Kenneth. Jarnow, Lila. Kettleman, Roy. Lindqvist, Astrid. Marsh, Delia (pending — she'd promised me a sit-down Thursday, after her menu tasting). Nye, Otis. Okafor, Beatrice.

Thirteen suspects, one town. The math of my childhood.

I started at the diner, because you always start at the diner. Every town has its confessional booth, and ours serves pie.

* * *

The Blue Plate Diner smelled the way it had smelled since 1987: coffee, bacon grease, and the faint industrial sweetness of the lemon cleaner Ruth Fenwick had used on the counter since before I was born. Ruth herself stood behind the register like a monument to the concept of standing behind registers, seventy-some years old, spine straight as a plumb line, reading glasses pushed up into a cloud of white hair.

"Mirabel Kavanagh," she said, before I'd fully cleared the door. "You look thin. Sit."

"I'm here about—"

"Sit anyway." She poured coffee I hadn't ordered into a mug I suspected she'd chosen for me. That was the thing about Ruth: she ran the diner the way air traffic control runs a runway. Nothing landed without her say-so.

I sat. I turned on my recorder, openly, the way I always did — secrecy gets you nothing in a town where everyone can see everyone's kitchen window. "Ruth, I want to ask you about Ada Wren."

And there it was.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only inheritance my mother left me. Ruth Fenwick did not gasp. She did not go pale or drop anything or perform any of the theatrics that guilt is supposed to produce. What she did was smaller and far more damning: she flinched. A single contraction of the muscles around her eyes, quick as a camera shutter, gone before the rest of her face could vote on it. Then she smoothed over, wiped the counter where no spill existed, and said:

"What about her?"

The name itself. Not the question. The *name*. Thirty years of saying *the teacher who disappeared*, *poor Ada*, *that poor woman*, and apparently the raw syllables still had teeth.

"Two letters came to my studio last week," I said. "One confessing to her murder. One promising more at the anniversary dinner. You've heard?"

"Whole town's heard. You told the whole town." Which was true — episode one of the season had dropped Sunday night, and by Monday morning the letters were the only weather anyone discussed.

"Do you remember the night she disappeared?"

"Everyone remembers the night she disappeared. That's the trouble with this town. We remember it out loud every year and never once to each other." She refilled my untouched coffee, which was either hospitality or a way to keep her hands busy, and I noticed her hands. Steady. Too steady. Hands that steady themselves on purpose.

"You closed early that night," I said. "May fourteenth, 1993. You never close early."

Something moved behind her eyes — not the flinch this time, something slower, like a shade being drawn. "I had a migraine."

"In thirty years of records, tax filings, and one very detailed diary your late husband kept for the historical society, you have never once had a migraine."

"First time for everything." She smiled at me, warm as the pie case, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Mirabel, honey. You were nine years old. You're digging in grown folks' business with a spoon."

"That's rather the point of a spoon."

She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and for one second I saw the woman who'd given me free grilled cheese when Dad was between jobs, who'd let me do homework in booth four through an entire gray winter. Then the shutter came down again, smooth as a well-oiled mechanism, and I understood that I wasn't talking to a witness. I was talking to someone who had practiced this conversation. Possibly out loud. Possibly with the other twelve names on my list.

"Ask your next question," Ruth said. "I know you have a list. You alphabetize everything. Always did. Used to alphabetize the condiments when you were small."

"Cyrus Bell," I said, watching her. "What do you know about him?"

"Retired deputy. Dying man. Sweetest soul God ever put in a uniform." No hesitation. No flinch. And that, too, was information: she could say his name without blinking. Whatever lived under this town's floorboards, it had a shape, and Ruth knew exactly which parts of it were safe to touch.

* * *

Hollis Brandt ran the hardware store the way his father had run it and his grandfather before him, which is to say he sold you exactly what you needed, nothing you wanted, and commentary on both. He was sanding a porch rail sample when I came in, sawdust drifting in the light like the store was snowing indoors.

"Letters," he said, before I asked. Word travels fast in a town with one topic. "Heard you got 'em. Heard one of 'em says who did it."

"Says they did it. Different thing."

"Huh." He blew sawdust off the rail. "Always figured she ran off."

"With what money? She had forty dollars in her checking account. I've seen the statements."

"Maybe somebody gave her some." He said it lightly, testing the weight of it, and then went very still, the way men do when they've said more than they meant to. I watched him decide, visibly, to become a simpler person. "Anyway. Sad business. Hope you find whoever wrote those things. Cruel, writing letters like that. To you, I mean."

"To me?"

"Well." He set down the sander. "They sent 'em to *you*, didn't they? Somebody out there knows you'll make noise about it. That's not confession, that's commissioning." He nodded at my recorder, running on the counter between us like a small black beetle. "You ought to be careful, Mirabel. Towns like this, we keep our own books."

"We?"

He smiled. Everyone smiled today. "We the town. Figure of speech." He walked me to the door, held it open with old-fashioned courtesy, and added, as I stepped out into the bright cold morning: "Tell Ruth her order came in. She'll know what I mean."

I did not tell Ruth her order came in. But I wrote it down, because everything goes in the file, and somewhere around mile marker three of my walk back across town it occurred to me that a hardware store owner and a diner owner had just exchanged a message in front of me, openly, trusting that I wouldn't understand it. Which meant either they thought I was stupid — possible; people have underestimated me my whole life, usually once — or they wanted me to see it.

Both options frightened me in different flavors.

* * *

By afternoon I'd collected six interviews and a pattern so clean it looked machined. Father Manny Delgado remembered May 14th, 1993 vividly — he'd been up all night with a "parish emergency," wouldn't say whose, offered instead a moving anecdote about forgiveness. Prue Halloway, who ran the flower shop, had been "at her sister's in Cedar Falls" — a sister I had called, twice, who confirmed it in words so identical to Prue's own that I checked whether they'd rehearsed on speakerphone. Ken Ives, the mail carrier, had been "doing his route," though his route that year, per postal records I'd filed years ago, ended at 4:40 p.m., and Ada Wren's car was last seen at the quarry at 9:15.

Every alibi fit. That was the problem. Alibis are like teeth — natural ones have irregularities. These had been straightened.

I sat on the bench outside the library — closed Tuesdays, like always, another fixed point in the town's orbit — and ate a sandwich and looked at Marrow Creek the way I imagined a set designer looks at a finished stage. Main Street, freshly painted shutters on the older buildings, the war memorial polished, geraniums in barrels even though it was barely spring. Pretty. Coherent. Slightly too coherent, the way a room looks when it's been staged for a photograph and nobody actually lives in it anymore.

That was the feeling I couldn't shake, walking from house to house all day. Not that these people were lying to me. That they were *performing* — performing innocence, performing grief, performing the fond exasperation of small-town folk dealing with the podcast lady — and performing it *together*, without ever appearing to compare notes. Six interviews, six different people, and underneath all of them the same rhythm, the same careful pauses in the same places, like variations on a single piece of sheet music.

You don't get that from conspiracy, I thought. Conspiracies leak; conspirators sweat; somebody always wants a deal. This was something else. This was a town that had agreed on a story so long ago, and told it so many times, mostly to itself, that the story had worn grooves. They weren't lying to me. They were reciting.

And the terrible, tender question underneath it all: *reciting for whom?*

Because here's what kept snagging in my chest, the splinter I couldn't work loose: I grew up with these people. Ruth fed me. Hollis cut keys for my first apartment and refused payment. Father Manny buried my father and then showed up every Sunday for a year afterward to sit with my mother, saying nothing, bringing nothing, just sitting, which was worth more than casseroles. If they had rehearsed their innocence together, they had done it the way families rehearse Christmas dinner arguments — not to deceive, but to protect the table. To keep the seats filled. To keep the family a family.

Someone had needed protecting badly enough that thirteen people — at least thirteen, probably more — had spent thirty years holding a story together like a quilt against the cold.

And Ada Wren was still under it. Or she wasn't. And both possibilities broke my heart in opposite directions.

* * *

My last stop was Otis Nye, retired, keeper of the town's unofficial archive in a garage that smelled of motor oil and history. Otis had been the one who taught me to file, back when I was eleven and obsessed with organizing his fishing lures by color. He answered the door in suspenders and a cardigan, took one look at my face, and said, "You found something."

"How do you know?"

"Because you knock like a person with something in her pocket. Come in before the heat gets out."

His garage was a cathedral of boxes. He cleared a stool for me, sat himself down on an upturned bucket with the ease of a man who had been sitting on buckets his whole life, and listened while I laid out the day: Ruth's flinch, Hollis's warning, the interlocking alibis, the recitation.

Otis didn't interrupt. When I finished he was quiet for a long moment, turning a bolt between his fingers the way other men turn worry beads.

"You think they're hiding something," he said finally.

"I think they're hiding *it* together. Whatever it is."

"Mm." He set the bolt down. "Mirabel, when your daddy died, do you know what this town did? Nobody said a word to your mama about the drinking. Not one word. Whole town knew, whole town watched him weave down Main Street every Friday, and whole town decided — all at once, no meeting, no vote — that she'd hear it from nobody. Was that a lie?"

"It was mercy," I said slowly.

"It was both. It's usually both." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Now you take that instinct — protect the widow, protect the kid, protect the family — and you give it thirty years and something worse than drinking, and you get what you got today. A town full of good people reciting a bad story because the alternative was letting one of their own be torn apart." His eyes, pale and sharp under white brows, held mine. "Question you ought to be asking isn't *what are they hiding*. You'll get to that. Question is: who were they hiding it *from*? Because whoever it was, they were scared enough of him to lock the whole town's mouth."

A cold thread went down my spine, and I thought — unbidden, unwelcome — of the anniversary dinner guest list, and of the name printed at the top of it in gold script. The man giving the toast. The town's beloved patriarch.

But that was a thought for later. Otis was already reaching into a box, pulling out a folder, and when he handed it to me I saw the label in his careful carpenter's print: *WREN, A. — MISC.*

"One more thing," he said. "Before you go asking your questions tomorrow. There's a page missing from this. Has been since '93. I never took it, and I never asked who did." He tapped the folder once. "But I'll tell you who had access to my garage back then. Everybody on your list, girl. Everybody. We were all in each other's pockets that summer, trying to figure out what we'd lost."

I drove home with the folder on the passenger seat like a sleeping animal, and it wasn't until I pulled into my driveway that I saw the light on in my studio window — the little converted garage where I record, where the letters had arrived, where the file cabinets stand in alphabetical glory.

I had locked it. I always lock it.

The door was unlocked now, and inside, on my desk, dead center, arranged with a precision I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting — someone had left a single place card from the anniversary dinner, gold script gleaming under the lamp.

*Seat Three,* it read. *Next to the Deputy Chief.*
