## 10. Delia's Kitchen

Delia Marsh worked out of the basement of the First Methodist Church, which was the kind of detail that would have meant nothing to me a month ago and now felt like a clue in itself. Everything in Harrow Creek happened in basements and back rooms. The truth of this town had always been stored below ground level, like root vegetables.

I'd called ahead — or rather, I'd called and been told, in a voice as smooth as melted butter, "Come by whenever, honey. I'll be elbow-deep in shortbread." So at ten on Thursday morning I descended the church steps into warmth and light and the smell of browning butter, and found Delia Marsh exactly as advertised: a broad, comfortable woman in an apron that said KISS THE COOK in faded iron-on letters, standing at a steel counter with flour to her elbows.

"You must be Mirabel," she said, without turning around.

"You know me?"

"Honey, everybody knows you. You're the podcast lady. My niece listens to you like it's scripture." She turned then, and smiled, and I did my best to smile back while every instinct I owned stood at attention. "You want coffee? It's church coffee, so lower your expectations."

"I've survived worse."

"Spoken like a true investigator." She poured two mugs from a percolator older than both of us combined and set them on a card table already laid out with three kinds of cookies. "Sit. Taste. Tell me if the almond ones are too almond. The committee said too almond, but the committee also thinks Jell-O is a food group, so."

I sat. I tasted. The almond cookie was, in fact, perfect, and I told her so, and she looked pleased in a way that made me feel like I'd passed some kind of test I hadn't studied for.

"So," she said, settling across from me with her own mug. "You're here about the letters."

Straight out of the gate. No circling, no small talk about the weather or the price of butter. I'd prepared for deflection and gotten a door flung open instead, and it knocked me off balance more thoroughly than any evasion could have.

"You've heard about them," I managed.

"Whole town's heard. Sheriff's office is calling it a hoax." She sipped her coffee. "What do you call it?"

"That depends on what the handwriting analysis says."

"And when will that be?"

"Friday."

"Mm." She nodded slowly, as though Friday were a person she knew slightly and didn't trust. Then she pushed the plate toward me. "Try the ginger one. It has candied ginger in it, which sounds fancy but isn't. You just chop it up small and pray."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Delia Marsh before that morning: she was fifty-something, widowed once and divorced once, depending on who you asked; she'd come to Harrow Creek eleven years ago with no history anyone could find and a catering business nobody remembered starting; she cooked for every funeral, wedding, christening, and Rotary luncheon within forty miles; and she was, by universal agreement, the kindest woman in three counties. Mrs. Pell at the diner had said it. Pastor Lund had said it. Even Deputy Chief Hollis — and I use his title now deliberately, because titles are how he likes to be handled — even he had said it, warmly, at the hardware store: *Best caterer this town's ever had. We're lucky to have her.*

Everyone agreed Delia Marsh was wonderful, and everyone agreed on it a little too quickly, the way people agree about things they've been coached on.

Here is what I learned about her over the next hour: she asked questions the way a surgeon asks questions. Not curious questions. Diagnostic ones.

"When Ada Wren disappeared," she said, somewhere between the second cookie and the third, "did they ever figure out what she took with her?"

"Took with her?"

"From her house. When she went. People always take something. A photograph, a coat, a book. Something they can't stand to leave behind." She was piping icing onto a sheet cake as she talked, her hands moving in a steady, hypnotic line. "Did they ever say?"

"The sheriff's report lists a missing suitcase," I said carefully. "And her car was never found."

"No car." She nodded. "So she drove. Or somebody drove her. Or—" the piping bag paused, a half-second, no longer, "—or there never was a car to begin with."

"That's not in any report."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It wouldn't be."

I set down my coffee. "Delia. Can I ask you something direct?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer is a separate negotiation." But she was smiling, and there was something underneath the smile that wasn't quite a smile, something watchful and tired and old.

"Why does a caterer care whether Ada Wren had a car?"

She considered me for a long moment. Flour drifted in the light from the basement windows. Somewhere above us, the organist ran through scales, badly.

"Because I cook for funerals," she said finally. "Thirty years of funerals. And do you know what I've learned? Everybody's story gets shorter after they die. Every single one. A whole life gets boiled down to a paragraph on a funeral card. *Beloved mother. Avid gardener.* And the parts that don't fit the paragraph — those just evaporate. Like they never happened." She set down the piping bag. "Ada Wren got boiled down to *missing schoolteacher, presumed dead.* That's a very short paragraph, Mirabel. Shorter than most. Somebody decided what fit and what didn't, and everybody in this town has been reciting it ever since."

"You think the paragraph is wrong."

"I think paragraphs are written by survivors." She picked the bag back up. "More coffee?"

* * *

We talked for another hour, and I want to be honest about how it went: it went nowhere, brilliantly. She gave me nothing and everything. She told me about the anniversary dinner menu (roast pork, because "the committee can't agree on anything except pork"), about her late husband ("a good man who was bad at being married, which is its own category of tragedy"), about the church basement ("dry in summer, damp in winter, haunted according to the youth group"). She was funny. She was warm. She made me laugh twice, genuinely laugh, and I resented her for it, because I had come armed with questions and she disarmed me with shortbread.

But underneath all of it, threaded through it like a wire through a loaf of braided bread, she kept asking. Small questions. Precise ones.

"What was Ada's classroom number?" — casually, while discussing the school's renovation.

"Did she wear glasses?" — while telling a story about her own cataract surgery.

"Was she left-handed?" — while handing me a knife to slice a lemon loaf, and watching, I realized afterward, which hand I took it with.

Each question landed softly and sank deep. By the time I noticed the pattern, I was three questions behind and pretending otherwise.

"They say she drowned in the quarry," Delia said at one point, apropos of nothing. "Is that the official version?"

"There was no body. There's no official cause. Just... presumed."

"Presumed." She tasted the word like a spice she wasn't sure about. "That's a strange word for a death. Presumed dead. Like the town assumed it into existence." She wiped her hands on her apron. "You know what I presume? I presume people are more complicated than their paragraphs. I presume a woman doesn't walk into a quarry without a reason. And I presume—" she looked at me directly, and held it, longer than politeness allowed, "—that whoever wrote you those letters wanted you here. In this town. Asking these questions. Right now."

"Do you think that's a trap?"

"I think," she said, turning back to her cakes, "that traps are just invitations you weren't ready for."

* * *

It happened near the end, and afterward I would replay it so many times I wore the memory smooth, the way you wear a coin by carrying it.

A delivery man came to the top of the stairs and hollered down about a produce order, and Delia went up to sign for it, and I was alone in the kitchen for maybe ninety seconds. Ninety seconds with her open recipe binder, her wall calendar, her corkboard of index cards — each one a job, written in her hand. *Rotary, 40 pax, no cilantro (Mr. Denny). Funeral — Okafor, keep it simple, they're grieving. School fundraiser — kids' table near the exits, ALWAYS.*

And one card, older than the others, soft-cornered and yellowed, pinned slightly apart from the rest:

*Patience first. The answer is usually already in the room.*

I read it twice. There was nothing remarkable about it. Catering wisdom, probably. Some platitude about letting guests settle before serving.

Except.

I knew that sentence. I had heard that sentence, or its ghost, a thousand times, in twelve years of archival tape. Because Ada Wren used to say it to her students — it was in the interviews, in the old news footage, in the testimony of half a dozen former pupils who all remembered it independently, the way you remember a teacher's odd little rituals decades later. *Patience first. The answer is usually already in the room.* She said it when a kid was stuck on a problem. She said it during fire drills. One witness, a woman named Carol Dempsey now, had told me in episode nine, laughing through tears: *"God, I still hear her saying it. Patience first. Thirty years and I hear it every time I lose my keys."*

It was never in any police report. It was never printed in any newspaper. It existed only in the memories of her students, and in my archive, and — apparently — on an index card in a church basement in Harrow Creek.

The delivery truck pulled away. Footsteps on the stairs. I stepped back from the corkboard and picked up my coffee and arranged my face into polite blankness, and Delia came down into the kitchen reading the delivery slip, and everything was normal, everything was fine, everything was shortbread.

"Find anything interesting?" she asked mildly, not looking up.

My heart performed a maneuver I'm fairly sure is illegal in most states. "Just admiring your system. I'm a bit of an archivist myself."

"Mm." She pinned the delivery slip to the board, right beside the yellowed card, covering it without appearing to cover it. "Archivists and cooks have a lot in common, you know. Both of us trying to keep things from spoiling. Both of us labeling everything so it can be found later." She glanced at me, quick and sharp as a bird. "Both of us knowing that what matters most is usually kept in the oldest container."

"Is that a riddle?"

"It's a recipe," she said. "They look similar from the outside."

* * *

I walked home the long way, past the school, past the shuttered quarry road, doing the thing I do when my mind refuses to sit still: narrating. *Mirabel Kavanagh, walking through a town where every friendly face might be a locked door.* The morning had given me nothing usable. No confession, no slip, no evidence. And yet I felt like a woman who'd been handed a live coal and told to carry it gently.

Because here's the thing I couldn't shake, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: the phrase. *Patience first.* Eleven years in this town, cooking for its funerals, hearing its stories — and using, idly, privately, on an index card, the private classroom litany of a dead woman that only her students would know.

Unless she wasn't dead.

The thought arrived the way absurd thoughts do — ridiculous, glowing, impossible — and refused to leave. Hope, I thought, disgusted with myself. This is what hope looks like when it's desperate enough to wear a disguise. Twelve years of chasing a ghost and now I'm seeing her in a caterer's handwriting, in a kindness, in a cookie. Get a grip, Mirabel. Half the women in America over fifty say things like that. It's a coincidence. It's a coincidence.

But I kept hearing Carol Dempsey's laugh. *Thirty years and I hear it every time I lose my keys.*

And I kept seeing Delia's hands. I hadn't mentioned it yet, hadn't let myself mention it, but I'd noticed them the moment we shook hands: a thin white scar across the back of her left hand, curved like a comma. And I knew — because I had read every page of the 1993 file four times, because obsession is just love with a filing system — that Ada Wren's sister had testified, in a statement sealed for reasons no one ever explained, that Ada had a scar on her left hand from a childhood dog bite. A comma-shaped scar. A detail that never made the papers. A detail I had never published, never spoken aloud, never put in any episode.

Two impossibilities. Two details that shouldn't exist outside a sealed file and a dead woman's classroom.

I stopped at the corner by the war memorial and stood there in the cold like an idiot, and let myself say it, silently, just once, just to hear how it sounded:

*What if she's alive?*

It sounded insane. It sounded like the plot of a soap opera. It sounded like the kind of thing a lonely woman tells herself when the archive starts talking back.

It also explained the second letter. Fresh ink. Someone alive, writing recently, promising *the rest* at the anniversary dinner. Someone who knew the case from the inside — not the file, the life. Someone who cooked for funerals and asked about cars and covered a yellowed card with a delivery slip the moment I looked at it too long.

Someone who had spent thirty years being patient.

My phone buzzed. A text from the lab, early: *Analysis done ahead of schedule. Call me — you'll want to hear this one verbally.*

I stared at the screen. Above me, the church bell rang ten-thirty, and somewhere in the kitchen beneath it, I'd have sworn I could smell shortbread.

I pressed call, and held the phone to my ear, and waited for the world to split in two.
