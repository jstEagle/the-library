## 6. A Child Saw Everything

The name came to Juniper the way most truths did in Millbrook: sideways, in a voice she almost didn't recognize, over food neither of them wanted.

Marcus Teague had been in her graduating class — a heavyset boy who'd grown into a heavyset man who ran the hardware store his father ran before him, and who had spent the last thirty years perfecting the art of being unremarkable. She found him at the Gas-N-Go, buying lottery tickets with the deliberate slowness of a man killing time he was afraid of.

"Marcus." She hadn't planned this. She'd come in for a phone charger and a bottle of water, her recorder still in her bag out of habit, and there he was, scratching a quarter against a silver ticket like he was trying to dig something out of it. "Got a minute?"

He didn't look up. "Depends on the minute."

"About the quarry."

The quarter stopped moving. That was the thing about small towns — everybody's grief had a physical tell, and Marcus Teague's was his hands. He set the quarter down on the counter with exaggerated care, as if it might go off.

"Juniper." He turned, and his face had gone the color of the store's fluorescent lighting. "I heard your episode. The one about the clothes. I've been waiting for you to come ask me something, and I've been praying you wouldn't."

"Then you already know what I'm going to ask."

"I don't." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, just air escaping. "That's the hell of it. I know *pieces*. Everybody in this town knows pieces. We've been carrying them around like shrapnel since '94, and nobody's ever laid them all out on a table."

The clerk pretended to restock cigarettes. Juniper lowered her voice anyway. "Then lay out yours."

Marcus looked at the door. He looked at the lottery tickets. He looked, finally, at her, and what she saw in his face frightened her more than the ransacked studio had — because the studio had been violated by a stranger, but Marcus was being violated by memory, thirty years late, right there in front of her.

"Ruthie," he said.

The name landed in Juniper's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for it to hit bottom.

"Cyrus Bellamy's daughter. You know her?"

"Knew *of* her." Juniper's mind was already racing through the archive in her head, the yearbooks, the class lists. Ruthie Bellamy. Quiet girl, braids, sat by the window in homeroom. Gone before graduation — transferred, people said, or moved, or something. Millbrook had a way of losing children and calling it logistics. "She left when we were eighteen."

"She left because she'd been leaving since she was eleven." Marcus picked up the quarter again, then put it down. His hands needed a job and had none. "That summer Delia Marsh disappeared — my mama made me stay inside the whole rest of July. Wouldn't say why. But I heard her and my aunt talking on the porch, low, the way they talked when they thought the screens were enough. And my aunt said—" he swallowed— "my aunt said the Bellamy girl was down at the quarry that night. That she *saw*."

"Saw what?"

"That's the thing." Marcus's voice dropped so low the clerk actually turned around, then thought better of it and turned back. "Nobody ever said. Not what she saw, not who she saw, not whether it was before or after. Just that Ruthie Bellamy was there, and after that summer she stopped talking. Not all the way — she'd answer you at school, yes and no, pass the pencil. But it was like somebody had taken the rest of her words and locked them in a box and dropped the box in the quarry. She never spoke about that night again. Not once. Not to anybody. Thirty years, Juniper. You think the sheriff's own daughter, a child witness, never got asked? Everybody asked. The state police asked. A counselor came out from Charleston. She'd just look at them. Look at you with those gray eyes like water over stone, and wait until you gave up."

Juniper's recorder was in her bag. She did not take it out. Some things you could not ask a machine to hold.

"Her father," Juniper said slowly. "Cyrus was a deputy then. He'd have known she was there."

"He *was* her father." Marcus said it like a correction, though it wasn't one. "He carried her out of that quarry, is what my aunt said. Carried her home in his arms like she was asleep, except she wasn't asleep, she was just — gone. Somewhere behind her eyes. And from that day on, Cyrus Bellamy never let that girl out of his sight. Walked her to school. Sat outside her classroom. When she turned eighteen, he put her in a car and drove her out of Millbrook before the sun was up, and as far as I know she never came back, not for Christmas, not for funerals, not for his retirement party two years ago. Whole town showed up for that party. His own daughter sent a card."

Juniper stood very still. The Gas-N-Go hummed around her — the cooler, the lottery machine, the bell over the door as somebody came in for cigarettes and left again. Ordinary sounds. But underneath them she could hear the shape of something enormous rearranging itself.

For seven years she had built her podcast around a simple architecture: a girl vanished, a town looked away, a truth waited to be found. The villain in that architecture had always been vague — the town itself, mostly, its silence, its willingness to let a Black woman disappear without a search party worth the name. She had never needed a face. The absence of a face had been the story.

Now the face had a daughter. And the daughter had been eleven years old, and she had been at the water, and she had carried the whole weight of it in her locked-up mouth for three decades.

"Marcus." Juniper heard her own voice come out strange, careful, like she was handling evidence. "Why are you telling me this now?"

He finally looked at her straight, and his eyes were wet, and he was ashamed of it, and he was thirty-eight years old and twelve years old all at once.

"Because somebody's dressing bodies in your clothes, Juniper. Somebody's doing *this again*. And I keep thinking — Ruthie's out there somewhere, and if she ever hears about what happened at that quarry last month, what's left of her is going to break. And I keep thinking maybe you can find her before it does. Because you find things. It's what you do." He picked up his lottery ticket at last, folded it once, and put it in his shirt pocket like a letter he'd never mail. "And because I'm tired. Thirty years of carrying my piece. I want to put it down."

* * *

She drove home with the windows down even though the air had gone cool, letting the wind push at her face like it could rearrange her thoughts into a shape she could use. The quarry episode was supposed to air Thursday. She had a script, an outline, a theory of the crime that had taken seven years to build — and now every load-bearing wall of it had a crack running through it.

Her grandmother's house sat where it always sat, patient and yellow in the late light, and Juniper sat in the driveway for a long time before she went in, because she understood, suddenly, that she was about to do something that could not be undone. Finding Ruthie Bellamy was not research. It was a detonation with a delay on it. Whatever Ruthie had locked in that box, opening it would break something — maybe Ruthie, maybe the town, maybe the version of Delia Marsh that Juniper had spent seven years keeping alive in her listeners' hearts.

She went in anyway. She always went in. That was the whole of her character, she supposed, the thing her grandmother had seen in her before anyone else did: the girl who opens the box.

The archive room smelled of paper and dust and the faint chemical sweetness of old magnetic tape. She pulled the 1994–95 binder and laid it open on the table, and there Ruthie was — not in a photograph; there were no photographs of her anywhere in seven years of files, which itself was a kind of evidence. Just a name in the margins of an interview transcript, in her grandmother's handwriting: *R. Bellamy, b. 1983 — quarry? — ask again.* Her grandmother had known. Her grandmother had written *ask again*, and then had died, or been made quiet, or simply run out of time.

Juniper sat down and did what she did. She worked.

It took her four hours and three databases. A change-of-name record surfaced first — Ruthie Bellamy had become Ruth Bellamy, then, six years ago, Ruth Bellamy-Ashford, a hyphen acquired somewhere along the way that suggested a marriage, or at least a document. A nursing license in Ohio, renewed every two years, current. Hospice certification. And then, buried in a continuing-education directory, a workshop listing: *Ruth Bellamy-Ashford, RN — "The Unspoken Patient: Communication at End of Life," Fairview Community Hospice, Cedar Falls, Ohio.*

Juniper read the workshop title three times.

*The Unspoken Patient.* Thirty years after she stopped speaking, Ruth Bellamy had built a career out of sitting with people who couldn't or wouldn't talk — out of listening to the silence on behalf of everyone who couldn't bear it. Juniper put her hand flat on the table and felt, absurdly, like she was reading her own future. Or her own reflection.

She booked a flight that night. Then she sat for a long while in the dark of the archive room, the binder open in front of her, the streetlight coming through the blinds in slats across her grandmother's handwriting.

*Ask again.*

"I'm asking," Juniper said out loud, to the room, to her grandmother, to the girl with the braids by the window. "I'm asking again."

* * *

Cedar Falls, Ohio, was flat and green and aggressively ordinary, the kind of place that had been assembled from a kit. Juniper rented a car at the regional airport and drove through subdivisions with names like Meadowbrook and Stonebridge — water names, she noticed, everywhere water names, as if the whole country were dreaming of quarries — until she found the hospice on a quiet street lined with sugar maples.

She had decided, on the flight, that she would not ambush her. Whatever else Ruth Bellamy-Ashford had endured, she deserved to be found the way you find a person and not the way you find a suspect. So Juniper did the cowardly, decent thing: she called the main line and asked for her, and when the receptionist asked the nature of her business, she said, "An old classmate from Millbrook," which was true, and "about a family matter," which was also true, and waited.

The call back came forty minutes later, to her hotel. The voice on the line was low and level and very tired, in the way of people who sit with the dying professionally and have learned to conserve everything.

"You're the podcast woman." Not a question.

"Juniper Okafor. Yes."

"My father listens to you." A pause long enough to drive a truck through. "He told me. He said you were thorough." Another pause, and in it Juniper heard something she couldn't name yet — something under the words, the way you hear water under ice. "You want to know about 1994."

"I want to know what you saw."

The silence on the line went on so long Juniper checked whether the call had dropped. When Ruth finally spoke, her voice had not risen or sharpened; it had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere flat and far.

"Do you know what I do for a living, Miss Okafor? I sit with people in their last days. And do you know what they almost all want, at the end? Not medicine. Not even family, half the time. They want *one person* to hear the thing they've been carrying. One confession, one apology, one name said out loud. And I sit there, and I listen, and I take their secrets with me when I leave the room, because that's the job. That's the sacred part of the job." A breath. "I have been the unspoken patient my whole life. And I will tell you what I saw. God help me, I have been waiting thirty years for someone to ask again."

Juniper closed her eyes. Outside her hotel window, an ordinary Ohio evening went on being ordinary — sprinklers ticking across a lawn, a dog barking twice and stopping, a child laughing somewhere out of sight.

"Tomorrow," Ruth said. "Not the hospice. There's a park on the river, Miller's Landing, the benches by the boat ramp. Come at four. And Miss Okafor —" the voice steadied itself like a hand gripping a rail— "before you come, you should know one thing. Whatever you think my father is. Whatever your whole town thinks he is. You're wrong about him. He's not the monster in this story."

A pause, and then, so quietly Juniper almost lost it under the sound of the sprinklers:

"He's just the man who's been burying it for me."

The line went dead, and Juniper sat in the blue glow of the hotel room, her heart hammering the way it had at the quarry's edge, understanding that she had spent seven years digging for a killer and had just been handed something far more dangerous:

a witness.
