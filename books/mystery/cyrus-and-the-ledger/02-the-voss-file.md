## 2. The Voss File

The rain came in off the creek that morning the way it always did in October — sideways, patient, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Ines Oyelaran watched it from the coroner's office window while her coffee went cold beside a stack of paperwork she had no intention of finishing before noon. Marrow Creek's dead were quiet this week. It was the living who kept arriving at her door with their small requests and their smaller patience.

She heard the cruiser before she saw it — the particular wet grind of tires on the gravel lot behind the municipal building, a sound she could have identified blindfolded, the way she could identify the creak of the third stair in her father's old house or the rattle of the furnace kicking on at two in the morning. Deputy Dana Ruiz climbed out with her collar up and her hat low, and stood for a moment in the rain looking at the office door as if it might be kinder to her than it was.

Ines knew why she'd come. She'd known since Tuesday, when the memo came down from the county: eleven years was the statutory horizon, the file was stale, the budget was tight, and Ada Voss needed to stop being a missing person and start being a closed case.

It was the word *closed* that sat wrong in her chest. Not *resolved*. Not *answered*. Closed, the way you close a drawer on something you don't want to look at anymore.

The bell over the door gave its tired jingle when Ruiz shouldered in, shedding water onto the mat that hadn't been new when Ines took this job nine years ago.

"Deputy." Ines didn't get up. There was a second chair across from her desk, and they both knew the ritual: Ruiz would stand a while first, dripping, as if standing made the visit unofficial.

"Dr. Oyelaran." Ruiz pulled off her hat and worked water out of the brim with her thumb. "You got my memo."

"I got your memo."

"And?"

"And I read it." Ines turned her chair toward the desk, folded her hands. She'd learned long ago that stillness unnerved people more than argument did. Her father used to say she got it from her mother, who could out-wait a blizzard without saying a word. "I'm not signing it."

Ruiz exhaled through her nose — not quite a laugh, not quite anything else. "Ines."

"Dana."

"It's a formality. Eleven years. You know what eleven years means. There's nothing left to find. The woman is gone, and whatever happened to her happened a decade ago, and the county wants the file weight off the books so the auditor stops flagging it every quarter." She set her hat on the corner of the desk, which was either an act of trust or of siege, depending on how you read it. "Your signature is the last one we need. Sheriff signed. Judge signed. Even the state liaison signed, and that man would sign his own death certificate if it saved him a phone call."

"Then you're one signature short of a complete set," Ines said, "and I'm afraid I'm going to keep being the short one."

The rain filled the silence between them, ticking against the window glass in gusts. Ruiz looked at her the way people in Marrow Creek had learned to look at her over the years — with a mixture of respect and the weary recognition that respect didn't move her an inch.

"You buried Tom Aldous on Saturday," Ruiz said.

"I did."

"Heart attack, per your report."

"Per the evidence available to me." Ines held her gaze level. She wasn't going to have that conversation here, in this room, with this much rain between them and the truth. Tom Aldous had come into her morgue with hands that told a different story than his chest did — knuckles split in ways a man doesn't split them falling down his own stairs, and a bruise along his jaw shaped like nothing accidental she'd ever catalogued. She'd written what she could prove and kept the rest in the ledger she kept in her head, the one with three names in it now that the county called accidents and she called something else. But that was a debt to be collected later, carefully, or maybe never. Today's fight was Ada Voss.

"Tom Aldous isn't why I'm here," Ruiz said.

"No. But it tells me what kind of week you think I'm having, if you thought mentioning him would soften me up."

A ghost of a smile crossed the deputy's face and died there. She finally sat, the chair complaining under her, and leaned forward with her forearms on her knees — the posture of a woman about to say the thing she'd rehearsed in the car.

"Ines. I'm asking you as a friend, not just as law enforcement. Sign the paper. Close the case. It doesn't mean we stop caring. It means the paperwork stops bleeding us."

"Do you know what's in the evidence annex?" Ines asked.

Ruiz blinked. "What?"

"The Voss belongings. From her classroom. From her rental house on Delaney Street." Ines rose and crossed to the filing cabinet against the wall — oak, older than both of them, one drawer that stuck unless you lifted while pulling, a trick her father had taught her when she was twelve and he was teaching her that everything broken has a technique. She opened the bottom drawer and drew out a manila folder gone soft and gray at the edges. ADA VOSS — MISSING PERSON — 11-04, said the label, in her own predecessor's handwriting, and beneath it, added in hers: *inventory pending.*

"Eleven years ago, when she disappeared, the department boxed up her things," Ines said. "Two days of processing, per the log. Then the boxes came here because the annex at the sheriff's office flooded, and they've been sitting in our storage room ever since. Sealed. Catalogued by box number only." She laid the folder on the desk between them like a card played face-up. "Nobody has ever done a full inventory. Nobody has ever opened those boxes and written down what's actually inside."

"So? They're boxes. Clothes, books, dishes. What are you going to find in a box of dishes?"

"I don't know. That's rather the point." Ines sat back down. "Dana, I sign that form, and the case closes, and within eighteen months those boxes go to county surplus or the incinerator, because that's what happens to the property of closed cases in towns like this one. I've seen it happen to four other files since I've been here. And once those boxes are ash, whatever Ada Voss left behind — whatever she wanted found, if she wanted anything found — goes with it."

"She's been gone eleven years. If she wanted something found—"

"Then someone failed her for eleven years," Ines said, "and I'm not going to be the twelfth year of it."

Ruiz stared at her. Rain ran down the window in long silver threads. Somewhere down the hall, the radiator knocked twice and settled, the building's old bones shifting the way they did whenever the weather turned.

"You really believe she's alive," Ruiz said quietly. "After eleven years. You believe it."

Ines considered the question honestly, because Ruiz deserved honesty even if the county didn't. "I don't believe anything," she said. "Belief is for churches. What I have is a body that was never recovered, a scene that was never properly processed, a witness list that was never fully interviewed, and a schoolteacher who, by every account anyone has ever given me, was methodical, careful, and beloved by her students — none of which describes a woman who wanders off into the creek bottoms and stays wandered." She tapped the folder once. "Missing persons cases close two ways, Dana. Somebody comes back, or somebody turns up. Neither has happened. Everything else is a town deciding it's tired of remembering."

"And if the inventory turns up nothing? Just clothes and lesson plans and a casserole dish?"

"Then I'll sign your form, and I'll write a letter to the file saying the closure was administrative, not evidentiary, so that if anyone ever reopens it, they'll know exactly what kind of closure it was." She met Ruiz's eyes. "That's my offer. That's the whole offer."

For a long moment the deputy said nothing. Then she laughed — a short, defeated sound, half admiration. "You know the sheriff's going to call you. He's going to call you tonight, and he's going to use words like *obstruction* and *not a team player*, and then he's going to remember that you're the only coroner in forty miles and he can't replace you, and he's going to hate that he remembers it."

"He can add it to the list of things he hates," Ines said. "It's a long list. It keeps him organized."

Ruiz stood, retrieved her hat, and turned it in her hands once before putting it on. At the door she paused, her back half-turned, rain-light catching the wet shoulders of her uniform.

"For what it's worth," she said, "off the record, nowhere near any record — I never liked how that case was handled either. First responding deputy was Ray Kettleman, and Ray processed that house in under an hour. Under an hour, Ines. I've spent longer than that on a stolen bicycle." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "And Ada Voss tutored the Harrow grandkids. Did you know that? Wednesday afternoons, out at the mill house. Half the town forgot that. I never did."

The bell jingled. Then she was gone, and the rain took the sound of the cruiser with it, and Ines sat alone in the office with the folder open in front of her and her coffee cold as creek stone.

* * *

The evidence annex occupied the back third of the municipal building, past the boiler room, behind a door whose lock had been installed sometime during the previous administration and never oiled since. Ines let herself in at four o'clock, after the clerks had gone home, because she wanted to do this without an audience and without interruption and without anyone asking her, in that tone Marrow Creek reserved for its difficult women, whether she couldn't just let sleeping dogs lie.

Sleeping dogs, she thought, flicking on the fluorescent tube that buzzed and stuttered before committing. Sleeping dogs were fine. It was the buried ones that concerned her.

The boxes were where the transfer log said they'd be: six of them, stacked on a steel shelf against the far wall, each sealed with evidence tape gone brittle and amber with age, each bearing a box number and a date in November, eleven years back. The cardboard had softened at the corners. Dust lay on them in a smooth, undisturbed gray felt, thick enough to hold a fingerprint, and Ines noted — with the part of her mind that never stopped taking notes — that no print had disturbed it. No one had touched these boxes in years. Perhaps no one had touched them since the day they'd been carried in.

She photographed each seal before breaking it. She wrote the time in her notebook. Procedure mattered; procedure was the skeleton that held a person upright when everything soft in them wanted to run. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd taught it about ledgers instead of bodies — *write it down, Ines, write it down while it's true, because memory is a liar with good manners.*

Box one: winter clothing, a woman's, wool and corduroy smelling faintly of cedar and something older underneath, something like paper left too long in damp. Box two: kitchen things, wrapped in newsprint from a paper that had stopped printing years ago. Box three: books. She lifted them out one by one — poetry, mostly. Mary Oliver. A battered Rilke with a cracked spine. A high-school literature anthology with sticky notes still clinging to its pages like pale moths.

Box four was heavier than it should have been.

Inside, wrapped separately in acid-free tissue that someone — not the department, the department used newsprint — had taken care with, lay a stack of notebooks. Composition books, the marbled black-and-white kind sold at the drugstore for a dollar ninety-nine. Seven of them. On the front of each, in a small, upright, unhurried hand, was a date range and a single word: *Diary.*

Ada Voss had kept diaries. This fact appeared nowhere in the case file. Ines was certain of it; she had read the file so many times she could recite the gaps in it like psalms. Either the original investigating officer had never seen these notebooks, or he had seen them and decided a missing woman's private thoughts were irrelevant to finding her. Knowing Ray Kettleman, Ines suspected the latter. Knowing Ray Kettleman, she suspected he'd have found her actual thoughts inconvenient.

She sat down on the metal stool by the shelf and, wearing gloves, opened the earliest diary to a random page.

The handwriting was lovely — precise without being fussy, the letters leaning slightly rightward, like a person walking briskly into wind. *October 3rd,* the entry began. *The Henderson boy finally finished his essay on the mill. He wrote that the creek "used to be louder." I asked him what he meant and he said his grandfather says the creek used to be loud because the wheel turned, and now it's quiet because the wheel doesn't, and quiet creeks keep secrets better. I gave him an A and didn't tell him his grandfather might be more right than he knows.*

Ines read it twice. Something in her chest tightened — not grief exactly, not yet, but the anticipation of it, the way the air tightens before a storm front arrives. Here was a voice. Eleven years of official silence, and here, suddenly, was a voice: wry, observant, fond of her students, watchful of her town. *Quiet creeks keep secrets better.* Had she meant it as a joke? Teachers made jokes. Towns like this one taught you to.

Ines turned pages. School terms passed. Winters. Springs. The entries grew denser in the final notebook — shorter intervals between dates, longer entries, the handwriting pressing harder into the page as though the writer had less time or more to say. Ines flipped toward the end of the last book with the growing, irrational urgency of someone reading letters from a ship they know sank.

The final dated entry was November 2nd, eleven years ago. Two days before Ada Voss vanished.

*I saw Wendell Harrow's truck parked at the mill after hours again. Third time this month. I am going to ask him directly about the 1994 file tomorrow. If he has nothing to hide, he'll tell me. If he does, well. Then I'll know why the creek is so quiet.*

That was the last line in Ada's hand.

Ines sat very still. The fluorescent tube buzzed overhead. The boiler ticked in the next room. Outside, the rain had thickened into the steady drumming roar that meant it would go all night, washing the streets clean of everything except what wouldn't wash away.

There were perhaps twenty blank pages left in the notebook. She knew this because she was looking at them — she could see the ruled lines running empty to the back cover, pristine, untouched.

Except they weren't untouched.

Because there, following the final entry, after a gap of several blank pages, the writing resumed.

Same pen. Same ink. Same slight rightward lean, same precise, unhurried letterforms — a hand so distinctive, so individual in its small habits (the way the *t*s were crossed with a single flat stroke, the way the *y*s descended with a little backward curl) that Ines felt her stomach drop before her mind caught up to what her eyes were reporting.

The entries continued. Dated months after Ada Voss disappeared. Years after. The handwriting walked on and on through pages that should have been empty, dated into seasons when every soul in Marrow Creek agreed the woman was gone —

And Ines Oyelaran, coroner of Marrow Creek, keeper of its dead and its records, sat alone in the buzzing annex holding a diary in her gloved hands, and recognized the handwriting on every one of those impossible pages.

It was her own.
