## 11. Truck, Keys, Blank Hours

The truck had always smelled like her father. Forty years of pipe smoke soaked into the bench seat's cracked vinyl, into the headliner, into the rubber floor mats he'd never once replaced because, he said, a man shouldn't have to look down to remember where he'd been. Ines had kept it after he died — kept it running, kept it gassed, kept the same tin of peppermints rattling in the glovebox long after the mints themselves had fused into a single pale brick. She drove it the way other people wore a dead man's coat.

Now it sat in the bay behind the coroner's office under fluorescent light, hood up, doors open, and two state forensic technicians moved around it with the careful, apologetic reverence of people handling a body.

She had asked for this. That was the part she kept returning to, the part she made herself repeat like a catechism: *I asked for this.* Three nights ago, after Ruthie Callow's front door had closed behind her with its soft, terrible click, Ines had sat in her own kitchen with the diary open to a page in her own handwriting and understood that if she did not turn the evidence over — all of it, including herself — she would never trust her own mind again. So she'd called Dana Ruiz at seven in the morning and said, in a voice she didn't recognize as hers, "I want my truck processed. And Dana — I want you to do it like I'm a suspect. Because I might be."

Dana had looked at her for a long moment across the desk. "You know what that means."

"I know what it means."

"It means if it's bad, I can't bury it. Not for you. Not for your dad."

"I'm not asking you to bury anything. I'm asking you to find it."

Now she stood at the edge of the bay with her arms crossed too tight, watching a woman named Okafor lift the driver's seat cushion with a spatula-shaped tool and a patience Ines could not have mustered. The morning was cold enough that their breath hung in the light. Somewhere a crow was working at something in the gravel lot, making the same three-note complaint over and over.

"Dr. Oyelaran." Okafor didn't look up. "You're welcome to stay, but I'll tell you now — the first hour is always the worst hour."

"I'm a coroner, Ms. Okafor. I *am* the first hour."

Okafor almost smiled. "Fair enough."

The first thing they found was nothing, and the nothing was its own kind of answer. No blood. No hair that wasn't hers. No sign of restraint, of struggle, of anyone else's weight in the cab. The truck was, in every way the eye could see, exactly the truck of a lonely woman who drove to work and to the cemetery and to Ruthie Callow's house and back.

It was the vacuum that found the rest.

Okafor ran a shop vac fitted with a collection canister along the seams of the floor, along the door tracks, into the channel beneath the seat where forty years of Marrow Creek had settled — grit, pine needles, the desiccated husks of June bugs, a nickel dated 1987. She sealed each canister and labeled it, and Ines watched the labels accumulate like a ledger being written against her: *DRIVER FLOOR, FRONT. DRIVER FLOOR, REAR. SEAT RAIL, LEFT.*

It was the seat rail sample that came back wrong.

Okafor spread the contents on a white tray under magnification and went quiet in a way Ines knew professionally. It was the quiet of a person who has found something and is deciding how to say it. Ines had made that same quiet herself, over bodies, a hundred times.

"Talk to me," Ines said.

Okafor used forceps to lift a pinch of fine powder and held it to the light. It was pale gray, almost silver, with a faint oily sheen, like the dust that gathers on the inside of a bell.

"You know what this is?"

Ines leaned in. She did know. Everyone in Marrow Creek knew; it got into the sills of every house within two miles of the place. "Sawdust. Treated lumber. From the mill."

"Old-growth pine, pressure-treated with a chromated copper formulation the mill stopped using in ninety-six when the regs changed." Okafor set it down. "It's not in the mats, where you'd expect tracked-in dirt. It's in the seat rail. Under the seat. Which means it came off something — or someone — that was *under* there. Or it was in the cab in quantity and settled into the one place nobody cleans." She looked up. "When were you last inside the Harrow mill?"

"I've never been inside the Harrow mill."

"Your father?"

"He did the death certifications for them for thirty years. He'd have been in and out." Ines heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for families. "But I haven't driven this truck onto mill property. Not once. I'd remember."

Okafor said nothing to that. She was too polite to say what they were both thinking: *would you?*

* * *

The fragment came out from under the passenger seat at 11:40 in the morning, and Ines would remember the time because she was looking at her watch when Okafor made the small sound — not a gasp, nothing so dramatic, just a short intake through the teeth, the sound of a professional finding exactly what she'd been afraid of.

"Forceps," Okafor said, and her assistant handed them over, and then there it was, held up in the fluorescent light between two steel tips: a triangle of paper, maybe two inches across, soft with age and damp-stained at one edge, the way paper gets when it has spent time somewhere with water in the air.

The mill. The flooded quarry behind the mill. Ines's stomach dropped through the concrete floor.

"Can you —"

"I can see it, Dr. Oyelaran. So can you."

She could. Even at two inches, even creased and foxed, the handwriting was legible, because it was a handwriting she had been staring at for weeks from both sides now — Ada Voss's neat, upright script on the older pages, her own slanting hand on the later ones. This fragment was neither. Or it was both. It was the transition, the seam where one hand became the other, and Ines felt her mouth go dry reading the three partial lines that survived the tear:

*...he watches the house on the nights...*

*...if anything happens to me it will look like I...*

*...Ines, if you are reading this, you were...*

The last line broke off at *you were*, and the absence where the rest of the sentence should have been was the loudest thing in the room.

"You were," Ines said aloud. "I was what."

"Dr. Oyelaran." Okafor set the fragment down on a fresh evidence card, squared it, photographed it twice. Her voice had changed — gentler, and behind the gentleness, careful. "I have to ask you to step back from the tray now. From here on, you're not the consulting coroner. You're the source."

"I know what I am." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I've known for a while. I just don't know *who*."

* * *

Dana arrived at noon, and Ines watched her read Okafor's preliminary report standing up, the way Dana read anything that mattered — fast, twice, lips moving slightly on the second pass. Dana had a habit Ines had catalogued over the years without meaning to: when something landed hard, she pressed her thumbnail into the cover of her notebook, hard enough to leave a crescent. Ines watched the thumbnail press down now, and hold.

"Mill dust under the seat," Dana said. "A page from the Voss diary under the passenger seat. And no signs of forced entry, because it's your truck and your keys." She looked up. "Ines."

"Don't."

"I have to say it."

"Then say it fast."

Dana closed the report. "There's a version of this file where you lured Ada Voss to the quarry eleven years ago, killed her, kept the diary as a trophy or leverage, and have been performing the grieving investigator ever since. There's a version where your father did it and you're covering for a dead man. And there's a version where somebody is building a frame out of your father's secrets and your missing hours, and they're doing it well, because they've had a long time to practice." She held up a hand before Ines could speak. "I'm not saying I believe the first two. I'm saying as of four o'clock today, I have to open a formal file, and your name goes in it as a person of interest. It goes in *with* the exculpatory stuff attached — the anonymous note, the drugging hypothesis, the timeline on the handwriting. But it goes in. If I sit on this and it surfaces later, everything I've done gets thrown out, and the Harrows' lawyers will eat this county alive. You know that."

"I know that." Ines sat down on the cold steel step of the bay, because her legs had quietly resigned without informing her. "Dana. That fragment. *If anything happens to me it will look like I.* Ada wrote that — or someone made her write it, or someone made *me* write it — and then it ended up under my passenger seat. Whoever put it there wants me to find it eventually. It's a message."

"Or it fell there eleven years ago and you drove on it for a decade without knowing."

"Under the seat rail, under the mat, in the channel? Dana, I have vacuumed that truck. My father vacuumed that truck. That paper was placed, and placed recently — the damp staining is consistent with the quarry, but the creases are fresh." She heard herself and stopped. "Listen to me. Listen to how I sound. I'm building the case against myself better than the state could. I've spent three weeks telling you the evidence in this town lies, and now I'm the evidence, and I can't even give myself the benefit of the doubt I'd give a stranger on my table."

Dana crouched down in front of her, which Dana almost never did — crouching was a concession, and Dana paid concessions like a woman counting coins. "You want to know the difference between you and a stranger on your table?"

"Tell me."

"The stranger doesn't ask for the autopsy." Dana's eyes were steady and tired and, underneath the tiredness, still loyal, which was the thing about Dana that Ines had come to depend on the way you depend on a load-bearing wall. "You called me. You said *treat me like a suspect.* Nobody building a frame does that, and nobody guilty does it either — not like that, not first, not before I had anything on you. So here's what's going to happen. I open the file. Your name goes in it. And then you and I are going to work it like I work any case where the obvious answer stinks — from the edges in. Which means the next thing I need from you is the one thing you can't give me."

"The night," Ines said. "The missing night."

"The missing night. Where were you, what were you driving, and why does your truck smell like a mill that's been shut for twenty years?"

Ines looked at her own hands. They were steady. She made them be steady. "I don't know," she said, and the words cost her more than any cause of death she had ever spoken aloud. "That's not an excuse, Dana. It's the finding. I have blank hours on the night Ada Voss disappeared, and the only witnesses are a truck full of dust and a diary written in my own hand, and every hour that goes by, whoever did this is deciding what I was."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon assembling the file, and there was a grim, liturgical quality to it that Ines recognized from the other side of a hundred death investigations. Photographs. Chain-of-custody forms. The fragment sealed in mylar. Okafor's dust report with its damning little chromatograph, the copper signature of 1990s mill treatment glowing off the page like an accusation. Dana typed with two fingers and did not soften anything, because Dana did not believe in softening, and Ines sat across the desk and watched her own name become an object.

*Oyelaran, Ines M. Person of interest.* Four words. She had written worse about better people. She had written *homicide, undetermined* over the bodies of men she'd loved, in a sense, and signed her name under it, and gone home and slept. This was different. This was her name in the machine, moving through the machine, acquiring weight and edges as it went. By Monday it would be in the county system. By Wednesday, if the Harrows' attorney requested discovery — and the Harrows' attorney would request discovery the way water requests a downhill grade — it would be in a law office on the second floor of the bank building, being read aloud to Wendell Harrow over coffee.

"They'll know everything we have," Ines said. "The moment this files, we've handed Wendell our whole hand."

"We're not handing him anything he doesn't already have." Dana saved the file, and the progress bar filled, and Ines thought: *that's it, that's how fast a life gets a second ledger.* "He's known since Ruthie Callow talked to you. He's known since before that, probably. The file isn't for him. It's for the day after the day he does something about it. It's so that when this breaks — and it's going to break, Ines, one way or the other — there's a record that you came in first, with clean hands, and asked for the autopsy."

"And if the autopsy says I did it?"

Dana stood, pulled her jacket off the chair, and looked at her for a long moment across the desk where eleven years of small-town paperwork had passed between them, and where, as of four o'clock, a file now lived with Ines's name on it like a diagnosis.

"Then I'll be the one who has to arrest you," Dana said, "and I'll do it, and I'll hate every minute, and I'll also keep working the case, because a person of interest isn't a conclusion. It's a question with your name on it. So answer it." She paused at the door, hand on the frame. "The psychologist in Asheville — the one who does the forensic memory work. Call her tonight. Don't wait for the file to catch up to you. The only evidence that can beat a planted one is the kind that's locked in your head before somebody gets to you again."

Then she was gone, and the bay was quiet, and the truck sat there under the lights with its hood up like a patient mid-surgery, holding its dust and its torn page and its forty years of her father's smoke, keeping the one secret Ines needed most.

Ines stood alone in the cold and said the fragment's last line out loud, to the empty room, to Ada, to whoever had put it there and was, at this moment, reading the same report she was.

"You were," she said. "I was *what?*"

The crow outside made its three notes and stopped, as if waiting for the answer too.

She got her coat. She had a phone call to make, and a night ahead of her, and somewhere out past the mill road, a man who had just learned that the county now had her name in a file — and who had spent thirty years making sure that names in files stayed exactly where he put them.
