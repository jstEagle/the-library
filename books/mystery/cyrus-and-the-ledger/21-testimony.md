## 21. Testimony

The courthouse in Marrow Creek had been built the same decade as the mill, and it wore its age the same way — a façade of respectable limestone over rot that had been patched and repainted until nobody could say anymore what was original and what was repair. Ines Oyelaran stood on the steps that first morning of the trial and looked up at the columns, at the scales carved into the pediment, their balance long since worn smooth into two shapeless cups, and she thought: *we pretend this building holds something it has never once held evenly.*

Inside, the air smelled of floor wax and old paper and, faintly, of the radiator's scorched dust. The gallery was fuller than any proceeding Marrow Creek had seen in thirty years. Farmers in their church coats. Women from the quilting circle who had buried husbands whose deaths Ines had signed. The Harrows' pew — because of course they had kept a pew, as if grief were a family seat — sat conspicuously empty except for one figure near the back: Marcus, in a gray suit the color of creek fog, his hands folded, his face arranged into an expression of patient sorrow that Ines now recognized as simply another room in the same house as his charm.

Dana Ruiz sat at the prosecution table beside the district attorney, a borrowed gun from the county seat named Prosser, who talked like a man reading a warranty. Dana caught Ines's eye and gave her nothing — no nod, no reassurance. That was the gift of Dana Ruiz: she did not lie to you with her face.

"Ms. Oyelaran." Prosser was standing. "The state calls you."

* * *

She had rehearsed this for eleven weeks. She had rehearsed it in the shower, in her truck at red lights, standing over the embalming table at two in the morning with her hands still wet. She had rehearsed the parts about the ledger, the shorthand, the prescriptions. She had not rehearsed what to do with her voice when it came time to say the true thing out loud, the thing she had promised herself she would not flinch from:

"I don't remember all of it."

Prosser had begged her not to say it that way. *Say* portions of my memory are unavailable to me. *Say* there exist gaps consistent with chemical coercion. Say it like a coroner, not like a confession.

But Wendell Harrow's attorney — a silver-haired man from the city named Callis, whose fees alone exceeded the town's annual road budget — would spend three days trying to make those gaps look like guilt. And Ines had decided, somewhere in the small hours, that she would rather hand him the truth whole than let him carve it into whatever shape served him.

"I drove to the mill road on the night Ada Voss disappeared," she said, her voice carrying in the hush the way sound carries over water at dawn. "I don't remember driving home. I don't remember most of the following two years. There are nights missing from me the way pages are missing from a book, and I know now why — I was drugged, systematically, by a man who is sitting ten feet away from me — but I want to be clear about something." She turned, deliberately, and looked at Wendell Harrow across the aisle of polished oak. He sat very straight in his good coat, his white hair combed, his hands quiet on his knees, an old man made almost entirely of patience. "Not remembering is not the same as not knowing. I know what he did. The ledger knows. And the dead know, and they have waited longer than any of us to say it."

Callis rose, smoothing his lapels. "Your Honor, we'll stipulate the witness is not a memory expert—"

"She's not testifying as one," Judge Amos said. He was seventy-four and had buried a wife and run unopposed for twenty years, and he looked at Ines now with the flat attention of a man who had signed too many easy verdicts in his life and knew it. "Continue, Ms. Oyelaran."

So she continued. She told them about the second ledger — the payments, thirty years of them, H.H. to E.O., entered in her father's cramped clerk's hand. She watched the gallery when she said her father's name; she watched old Tom Aldous's widow close her eyes, and she felt the cost of it go through her like a needle finding bone. She told them about Ada Voss, tutoring a Harrow grandchild, pulling at the thread of 1994, of the worker crushed under the stamping press while the safety guard lay disabled three feet away. She told them about the quarry behind the mill, the coordinates in her father's shorthand, the flooded dark where Ada had waited eleven years — or some portion of them — to be found.

And then Callis stood, and spent the afternoon doing what silver-haired men from the city do.

* * *

"Ms. Oyelaran." He approached the stand the way a man approaches a horse he intends to break. "You've told this court your memory fails you. Extensively. For years."

"For specific periods, yes."

"Specific periods." He smiled gently, the smile of a man about to be very reasonable. "Convenient periods. Periods that happen to include the night Ada Voss vanished, the nights the diary entries were written in your hand, the night — if I recall your own statement — when someone rearranged your kitchen. Isn't it remarkable, Ms. Oyelaran, how every gap in your memory happens to fall exactly where the evidence against Mr. Harrow needs it to be?"

Ines had thought about this question for eleven weeks too, though not as a lawyer thinks it. As a coroner thinks it. As a daughter.

"It isn't remarkable," she said. "It's the design. If you wanted to control a person — to make her doubt herself, to make sure that if her father's records ever surfaced she'd be too compromised to be believed — you wouldn't erase everything. You'd erase precisely enough. You'd leave her holding evidence in her own handwriting that she can't explain. You'd leave her truck smelling of your mill. You'd make her the perfect suspect so that no one ever looks past her." She paused, and let the silence do its work. "That isn't my theory, Mr. Callis. That's the architecture of what was done to me. I've lived inside it. I can describe the rooms."

A murmur moved through the gallery like wind through wheat. Callis waited it out, unhurried.

"You were prescribed medication after your father's death. A sleep aid. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"And that prescription came from Dr. Feld, your family physician."

"Yes."

"And Dr. Feld also consulted, informally, with a specialist in the county seat regarding your case. A specialist who happened to hold hospital privileges at St. Ambrose." He let that hang. "Where Mr. Harrow endowed the wing that bears his wife's name."

"The drug in my blood wasn't anything Dr. Feld prescribed," Ines said. "It was scopolamine derivative, compounded. It appears in exactly one dispensing record in this county, under a prescription account only Wendell Harrow could authorize. Your own expert confirmed the chain of custody. Ask him again if you like. I have time. The dead taught me patience."

Callis's smile thinned by a degree. "No further questions," he said, and Ines understood that she had won something, though she was too tired to name it.

* * *

The trial ran nine days. Ines attended every hour of it, sitting in the front row of the gallery with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral — which, she supposed, was accurate, since she had spent her professional life attending funerals that lied, and this was the first service in Marrow Creek's recent history that intended to tell the truth.

She watched Ruthie Callow testify with her hands shaking around a cup of water she never drank, describing the photograph — young Wendell, young Elias Oyelaran, and the mill worker Daniel Reeve together on the loading dock, 1993, all smiles, a year before the press came down. Ruthie's voice cracked on the word *frightened*, and Ines found herself gripping the rail in front of her hard enough to whiten her knuckles, because Ruthie had carried that fear alone for thirty years and was setting it down in public at last, and courage, Ines had learned, looks almost nothing like the movies. Courage looks like an old woman's trembling hands refusing to put down the cup.

She watched the forensic toxicologist, the records clerk, the diver who had brought up what the quarry held. She did not watch when they showed the photographs. She had already seen everything the photographs contained. She had been the one to kneel at the water's edge and begin the count of what had been taken from Ada Voss — the antemortem injuries, the healed fractures, the evidence of years. Not days. Years. Someone had kept her. Fed her. Kept her alive in the dark below the mill because killing her once had been unthinkable and keeping her had become the only alternative, and then, when the ledger surfaced, even that became impossible.

Wendell Harrow never took the stand. His counsel rested on procedure, on chain-of-custody quibbles, on the respectable fiction that a man of property does not do such things. On the ninth day, the jury — seven farmers, two teachers, a nurse, a mechanic, a retired mail carrier, and a young woman who worked at the feed store and had been Ada Voss's student — deliberated for six hours.

Ines waited in the hallway outside the courtroom, standing beneath the portrait of some judge dead a century, and Dana Ruiz came and stood beside her without speaking. They had passed beyond the need for speech somewhere around day four.

"They're going to convict," Dana said finally. "On the Reeve death and on Voss. The '94 cover-up might get pled down. Prosser's already talking about it."

"And Marcus?"

Dana was quiet a moment. "Callis filed to suppress the ledger's chain of transfer. The original passed through Marcus's hands before it reached us. Technically, that's tampering with evidence — or it makes every downstream exhibit fruit of a poisoned tree, depending on which judge wakes up grumpy. They're going to cut him loose. Maybe a misdemeanor. Probably nothing."

"He held the book that convicted his father," Ines said. "He handed it to me. He thought I'd burn it. When I didn't, he let it become evidence anyway, because a conviction serves him better than a secret. He walks away owning the mill, the land, the name — scrubbed clean by the very trial that dirtied it."

"That's the technicality," Dana said. "There's always a technicality. My academy instructor used to say the law is a net, and every net has mesh, and the ones who study the mesh longest are the ones who swim through." She exhaled through her nose. "He's leaving town, though. Sold the house on Caldwell Street last week. Quietly."

"Of course quietly. Loudness is the one luxury he's never allowed himself."

The bailiff opened the door. "Jury's back."

* * *

Ines had imagined this moment a hundred times during the nine days, and in none of her imaginings had Wendell Harrow looked the way he actually looked, which was: relieved.

That was the horror of it, and she made herself look directly at it, because looking directly at things was the whole of her profession. When the foreman read *guilty* — on the murder of Ada Voss, on the murder of Daniel Reeve, on the conspiracy that had dressed both as accidents — Wendell Harrow closed his eyes briefly, and his shoulders came down half an inch, and what crossed his face was not despair. It was the expression of a man setting down a load he had carried so long that its weight had become his posture. Thirty years of rehearsing alibis, of maintaining the fiction, of drugging a coroner's daughter and forging her hand — it had been work. Whoever Wendell Harrow had been before 1994, Ines suspected he had not enjoyed being what the work required. Conviction, for him, was not the end of his life. It was the end of his labor.

He did not look at her as the deputies led him out. But at the door, pausing, he said — quietly, to no one, or perhaps to everyone — "Daniel Reeve's guard was disabled before I got there. I want that known. I didn't disable it."

And then he was gone, and the courtroom breathed, and somewhere in the gallery a woman began to cry — not loudly, not dramatically, just the steady private crying of a person who has waited decades to be permitted it. Ines recognized her: Mrs. Reeve's sister, gray now, who had sat through every hour with her hands in her lap exactly the way Ines sat with hers.

Justice, Ines thought, watching the empty chair where Wendell had been. Imperfect, late, partial. The '94 records would be sealed pending review; Marcus would swim through the mesh; the town would spend a generation arguing about whether the Harrow name should come off the library annex. None of it would raise Daniel Reeve or Ada Voss or give Ines back her lost nights. And yet the word *guilty* had gone out into the air of Marrow Creek like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples would reach shores no one could predict, and for the first time in thirty years the official record of this town pointed at the truth instead of away from it.

That was not everything. But it was not nothing. She had spent her life among the dead learning the difference.

* * *

Afterward, the courthouse emptied slowly, the way churches do. People stopped to speak to her — Mrs. Aldous pressing her hand, the feed-store girl saying *Miss, you knew my teacher, didn't you, you're the one who found her* — and Ines received each of them the way she received mourners at viewings, with steadiness and a few true words, and only when the last of them had gone did she step out onto the courthouse steps into the late afternoon light.

Marcus Harrow was waiting at the bottom, beside a car packed for travel, suitcases visible through the rear window. He looked smaller out here, without the courtroom's architecture to frame him — just a man in a good coat in a fading town, holding a set of car keys.

"Ines." He said her name carefully, the way you handle something that might still cut you. "I wanted to say—" He stopped. Started again. "My father did terrible things. I'm not going to insult you by explaining them. And you did what you did, and I—" His jaw worked. Whatever speech he had prepared, it had died somewhere between the courthouse door and the bottom of the steps. "I held the ledger," he said instead. "I could have burned it. Part of me wanted to. Part of me wanted to be the kind of man who burns things to protect a name." He looked out at the street, at the shuttered hardware store, at the water tower with MARROW CREEK painted on it in letters going pale. "I couldn't figure out how to want it enough. So I gave it to you, and let you do the thing I couldn't. I don't know if that makes me a coward or the closest thing to an ally you had."

"It makes you a man leaving town," Ines said. "Which is what you were always going to do."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Recognition, maybe. The particular pain of being accurately described.

"There's one more thing," he said. He reached into his coat and produced an envelope, soft-cornered, handled often. "From my father's desk. It was addressed to you. Dated the week after they arrested him. He never sent it. I don't know if he meant to." He held it out. "I think you should have it. I think maybe it belongs to you more than to the family, and God knows the family doesn't deserve it."

Ines took it. Her name was written on the front in Wendell Harrow's careful, old-fashioned script: *Mrs. Ines Oyelaran, Coroner.* Not Miss. Mrs. — a courtesy from another era, or a small deliberate wound, or simply an old man's habit of granting dignity where he had taken everything else.

"Why give me this now?" she asked. "Why not let it burn with everything else?"

Marcus opened the car door. For a moment he stood half in shadow, half in the slanting gold light, and he looked, suddenly, very much like his father — the same long face, the same watchfulness — and very much not like him at all.

"Because you keep graves by name," he said. "All of them. Even the ones that shame you. Even mine, someday, probably." He got in. The engine started. Through the open window, as the car pulled away, he said the last thing she would ever hear him say: "Somebody in this town ought to."

* * *

She did not open the envelope that evening. She drove home along the creek road with the windows down and the autumn air moving through the truck, past the mill — dark now, its windows boarded, the county's padlock bright yellow on the loading dock where three people had once stood smiling in a photograph — and she parked in her own drive and sat in the cab a long while, watching her kitchen window, checking, out of habit, that nothing inside it had been rearranged.

Nothing had. The house was hers again. The nights belonged to her, all of them, even the empty ones — especially the empty ones, which she had decided to stop apologizing for. She had stood in a courtroom and said *I don't remember* without lowering her voice, and the world had not ended, and Callis had not devoured her, and somewhere in the gallery a girl who had been Ada Voss's student had written in a notebook, and Ines had seen the words upside-down: *she owned it.*

Owning it. That was the whole work, she thought. That was the entire ledger.

Finally she went inside, set the envelope on the kitchen table beneath the lamp, and stood over it with a cup of tea going cold. Wendell Harrow's handwriting stared up at her. Whatever was inside had been written by a man in the first week of his capture — before the trial, before the plea negotiations, before he settled into that strange relief — written while he still believed there might be something left to save, or something left to explain, or some last entry to make in a ledger thirty years long.

She picked up the letter opener her father had given her, years ago, for birthdays neither of them celebrated properly.

Outside, the first stars were coming up over the ridge, and down the road, barely visible in the dusk, the cemetery gate stood open the way it always did, patient, holding its names.

She slid the blade under the flap, and broke the seal.
