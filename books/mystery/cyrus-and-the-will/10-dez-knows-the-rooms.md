## 10. Dez Knows the Rooms

Dez Amari had worked the Grand Meridian for eleven years, and in that time he had learned that a hotel was not a building. It was a confession booth with better lighting. People told you things at three in the morning they'd never tell their priests — what room they were really sleeping in, whose ring was in whose pocket, how much of the champagne bill they could put on an account that wasn't theirs.

He'd learned other things too. That Mr. Okonkwo on four took his shoes off in the elevator and was embarrassed about it. That the widow in 612 watered a plant that had been dead since spring, because the dead plant had been her husband's. That Miss Brightwater drank her coffee black and bitter and always, always stood where she could see the doors.

Dez noticed things. It was the job. You couldn't be a porter at a hotel like the Meridian without noticing; you'd last a week. The trick wasn't noticing. The trick was knowing what to do with it afterward. Most of what he saw went into the same drawer in his head where he kept other people's secrets — shut, locked, never opened unless someone paid him in kindness, which almost nobody did.

Miss Brightwater paid in kindness. She'd been doing it for years, back when Dez was a kid from the harbor district pushing luggage for tips and pretending his uniform fit. She'd taught him the ledgers when the night auditor quit. She'd covered for him the winter he'd missed three shifts because his mother was dying and he hadn't told anyone why. She'd never once asked him to explain himself, and in return he had never once let her walk into something blind if he could help it.

Which is why, on the morning after the solstice, when Manager Corvane summoned Orsen to his office with a face like a closed ledger, Dez made a decision before his feet finished carrying him down the service corridor.

* * *

Corvane's office sat behind the front desk like a tumor behind a smile — all dark wood and framed certificates, smelling of lemon polish and old ambition. Dez had delivered packages there often enough to know the geography: the desk, the two guest chairs nobody sat in willingly, the window that looked out on the air shaft because Corvane believed views were for people who hadn't earned them yet.

Orsen was already inside when Dez arrived with a tea trolley he had absolutely no authorization to be pushing at nine in the morning.

"—and I want it understood," Corvane was saying, "that the Grand Meridian does not involve itself in police matters. We are a hospitality institution. Our guests' privacy is—"

"Your guest died in another man's house," Orsen said. She was standing, not sitting. She always stood in that office. "And your ledgers show his family has been staying here every winter for ten years. That's not privacy, that's a pattern."

"The Vane family are valued patrons—"

"The Vane family booked a private parlor every solstice for a decade and never once used the dining room." Orsen leaned forward. "You don't pay for rooms you don't use unless the rooms aren't the point."

Corvane's jaw tightened. Dez had seen that jaw work through union disputes, health inspections, and one memorable incident involving a duchess and a fire hose. It was a jaw built for saying no.

"This conversation is concluded," Corvane said. "And further, Miss Brightwater, given your... extracurricular activities of late, management has questions about your continued suitability for the concierge desk. Questions I suggest you answer carefully."

There it was. Dez had heard the rumor moving through the staff corridors all morning like weather: the concierge was mixed up in the Vane death, the police had come by, the family had made calls. Families like the Vanes didn't complain to managers. They *inquired*. And inquiries from people like that came with a weight you could feel through the floor.

Corvane wasn't evil. Dez wanted to be clear about that, even in the privacy of his own head. Corvane was a man who had spent thirty years learning that survival meant bending, and he bent beautifully, professionally, with certificates on the wall to prove it. He would not fire Orsen today. He would freeze her out, trim her hours, wait for her to bend or break, whichever came first.

Unless somebody put something on the scale.

Dez knocked.

Both heads turned. He pushed the trolley forward with the easy rolling gait of a man who had pushed ten thousand trolleys and would push ten thousand more, and said, "Beg pardon, sir. Tea for the manager's meeting? And Miss Brightwater — your nine o'clock is waiting at the desk. The gentleman from the shipping concern. Says it's urgent."

Orsen blinked at him. She was quick — quicker than anyone Dez knew — but even quick people needed half a second to catch a rope thrown in the dark. Then she saw the trolley, saw Dez's face doing its best impression of blank professional courtesy, and caught it.

"My nine o'clock," she said. "Of course. Forgive me, Manager Corvane. We'll continue this another time."

"We will conclude it another time," Corvane corrected. "There is no continuing."

"Conclude," Orsen agreed, already moving. At the door she paused beside Dez, and under her breath, so quiet only he could hear: "What shipping concern?"

"No idea," Dez murmured back. "Make one up. You're good at that."

* * *

They ended up in the linen room on three, because it was the only place in the Grand Meridian without ears. Housekeepers came and went, but the morning rush was over and the afternoon rush hadn't started, and the shelves of folded sheets swallowed sound the way snow swallows footsteps.

"You lied to a manager for me," Orsen said. She was holding a cup of the manager's tea she'd stolen off the trolley, which Dez felt was fair compensation.

"I delayed a manager," Dez said. "Lying's when you say something untrue. I just said a thing that hasn't happened yet. Timing's different."

"He'll figure it out."

"He'll suspect. Figuring out takes effort, and Corvane budgets his effort like a poor man budgets bread." Dez hopped up to sit on the folding table, because his feet hurt and the linen room was the one place a porter could sit like a human being. "Besides. You'd have done worse for me."

"That's not why I did it."

"I know." He looked at her. Orsen Brightwater, sharpest concierge in Vellamare, the woman who could find anyone's secrets out of a lobby full of strangers, sitting in a linen room drinking stolen tea because her case had turned into a corpse and her city had turned against her. She looked tired. She looked stubborn. Both of those were normal. What wasn't normal was that she looked alone, and Dez found he hated it more than he'd expected.

"So," he said, before the silence could get ideas. "The Vane family. You want to know about the suites."

Her head came up fast. "I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You were going to. You've got the look. You get the look when you're being polite, which means you're planning to ask, which means I can skip ahead." He pulled a folded paper from his jacket — actual paper, because some things shouldn't live in phones — and smoothed it against his knee. "I pulled the winter registers. Last ten years. Want to hear something funny?"

"Dez."

"The Vane family books the Meridian every solstice. Always have. But here's the thing." He tapped the paper. "They don't book as Vanes."

Orsen set down the tea. "Say that again."

"Every December, same week, the penthouse suite and the two adjoining rooms on seven get booked under different names. Different names every year, but the booking comes through the same channel — a private secretary's office, wired payment, cash settlement on checkout, no receipts itemized past 'hospitality services.' First year it was 'Mr. and Mrs. Harrow.' Then 'the Delacourtes.' Then 'Mr. and Mrs. Fen.' One year just 'A. Winters,' party of five." He shrugged. "Hotel folk see a hundred fake names a season. Rich people like pretending. But most rich people fake *down*, you know? Book as Smith so nobody bothers them. These names fake *sideways*. Same class, same style, just... wrong faces."

Orsen had gone very still, the way she did when a puzzle piece clicked somewhere deep. "Ten years. Every solstice."

"Every solstice. And here's the part I keep chewing on." Dez lowered his voice even though the linen room was empty. "Two years ago, the party changed. Before that, it was always five guests, matching the rooms. Two years ago it dropped to four. And last year—" he met her eyes "—last year, the booking was made twice. Same week, same floor, two separate parties. One under the family's false name. One under a name I'd swear I've seen before but couldn't place until this morning, when I went digging through my memory instead of the register."

"What name?"

"'C. Fell.'"

The name landed between them like a dropped glass. Dez watched Orsen absorb it — watched her lips move around it silently, once, the way she did when she was filing something into the architecture of a case.

"Cyrus Fell stayed in this hotel," she said slowly. "Last year. Solstice week. Same time as the family."

"Seventh floor. Room 704. The adjoining room." Dez held up a hand before she could speak. "Before you ask — no, I didn't connect it then. A porter sees a thousand faces. But I remember him now. Quiet fellow, paid cash, tipped too much, asked me odd questions about the building. Which elevator had the camera. Whether the seventh-floor fire stair locked from the inside." He grimaced. "At the time I figured him for a paranoid businessman. Now I figure him for a man casing the stage of a murder that hadn't happened yet."

Orsen stood up so fast the folding table rocked. She started pacing between the sheet shelves, three steps each way, turning something over and over.

"He wasn't predicting the murder," she said, mostly to herself. "He was *rehearsing* it. Or preventing it. He booked the adjoining room a year early — he knew the family's pattern, knew they'd be here, knew the study, the layout—" She stopped. Turned. "Dez. The year the party dropped from five to four. Which guest left?"

He shook his head. "Nobody checked out. That's the thing. Five people arrived that first year, four people were on the folio the next. Somebody stopped being counted." He hesitated, then gave her the rest, the part he'd been carrying since dawn. "And there's one more thing. The false-name bookings — they don't just cover the family. There's a standing reservation. Not annual. *Monthly.* Suite 712, booked the first Monday of every month for six years, under the name 'V. Ash.' Never used more than one night. Cash. And the guest who signs in—" Dez exhaled. "Older gentleman. Silver hair. Signs the register with a fountain pen like it's a ceremony. And Miss Brightwater, I've carried his bags twice, and both times he looked at me like he was memorizing my face in case he needed to deny it later."

"V. Ash," Orsen repeated. "Monthly. Six years."

"Aldous Vane has a standing room in my hotel under a name that isn't his," Dez said, "and until this week I thought that was just rich-man nonsense. Now a man with that name is dead in a locked study, and the police closed it in a day, and the family is staying here again right now under the name 'Pell' — third floor, they checked in yesterday — and I don't think any of those facts belong in the same drawer anymore."

Silence. Somewhere below them, the hotel went on breathing — elevators, luggage wheels, the low murmur of a lobby performing normalcy. Dez realized his hands had gone cold around the folded paper, and that he'd crossed a line sometime in the last five minutes and hadn't felt it happen. There was porter knowledge and there was everything else, and he'd just handed Orsen everything else.

"They'll check the registers eventually," he said. "If the family's lawyers come asking who talked, the trail ends at the winter ledgers, and the winter ledgers end at whoever pulled them. That's me."

"Then we pull them properly," Orsen said. "Official audit request, front-desk authority, your name nowhere near it. I'll sign whatever needs signing. If it costs me this job, it costs me the job I'm about to lose anyway."

"And if it costs more than the job?"

She looked at him straight. "Then you should tell me now, and I'll do it alone."

Dez laughed — short, surprised at himself. Eleven years of keeping his head down, of the locked drawer, of noticing everything and doing nothing, and here was this woman offering to carry the danger solo out of politeness.

"Miss Brightwater," he said, sliding off the table, "you taught me the ledgers. You covered my mother's shifts without asking why. You think I've been keeping a drawer full of this hotel's secrets for eleven years because I'm careful?" He shook his head. "I've been keeping them because nobody ever gave me a reason to open it. Consider this the reason."

He held out his hand. She took it. Her grip was cold and strong, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because some partnerships don't need speeches.

"One condition," Dez added. "When this is done — however it's done — you teach me how you spotted the widow's dead plant. I've walked past that room a hundred times and never seen it."

"It's not the plant," Orsen said, gathering the paper with the false names. "It's that she keeps watering it. People tell you everything by what they refuse to give up on." She tucked the page into her coat, next to Cyrus Fell's list of five names, and Dez understood suddenly that the two pieces of paper belonged together, though he couldn't have said how.

"Now," she said, "tell me about the Pell party on three. How many guests?"

"Four," Dez said. "Widow, nephew, daughter, and a lawyer. Checked in yesterday, requested no housekeeping, and—" he paused, because this was the part that had been itching at him since the morning register "—they asked for the room's telephone line to stay disconnected. All week."

Orsen's eyes narrowed. "Disconnected from what?"

"That's the thing," Dez said. "It's not disconnected from outside. It's disconnected from the *hotel switchboard*. They can call out. Nobody can call in. Not even us." He rubbed the back of his neck. "And one more thing, and then I swear I'll stop. This morning, before you came down, a courier delivered a package to the Pells. No return address. I signed for it, because the regular bell captain was busy. Heavy envelope, wax seal, no letterhead. And the name on it wasn't Pell."

Orsen went very still. "Whose name?"

Dez took a breath. "It was addressed to *A. Vane*," he said. "Third floor. This morning. Two days after his funeral."
