## 14. Second Funeral

The Cathedral of the Tides held two thousand mourners and smelled of lilies and cold stone, and every single one of them was performing.

I'd been to enough funerals in Vellamare to know the difference between grief and its costume. Grief has a texture — it's clumsy, it arrives late, it forgets to stand when everyone else stands. Costume grief is punctual. Costume grief wears good tailoring and remembers its lines. The pews of the Cathedral of the Tides were full of people who had rehearsed.

I sat in the back row with a catering badge in my pocket and a black dress I'd borrowed from Marta in housekeeping, who had pressed it without asking why and slipped a tin of ginger candies into my hand instead of questions. That's the thing about found family: they grieve you in advance, quietly, so you don't have to notice you're walking into danger.

Aldous Vane lay in an open casket beneath the rose window, and I made myself look at him properly this time. At the study, on solstice night, I'd only glimpsed him through a doorway of police shoulders. Now there was nothing between us but candlelight and twenty feet of marble aisle.

He was a handsome corpse. Too handsome. The mortician had given him a serenity that no dying man earns — cheeks smoothed, mouth composed into something almost smiling, hands folded over a rosary he'd never been photographed holding in life. The newspapers called him *the Saint of Vellamare*, and the mortician had clearly read the papers.

But it was the jaw that stopped me. I'd spent three weeks inside Aldous Vane's face by then — photographs from galas, charity pamphlets, a ten-year-old newsreel clip Dez had dug out of the hotel's archive room at two in the morning because I couldn't sleep and neither, apparently, could he. I knew the way his jaw sat slightly forward, the underbite the society pages never mentioned because flattery is a currency in this city. The man in the casket had a jaw like a drawing of a jaw. Correct. Symmetrical. Wrong.

The dead man in the box was not Aldous Vane.

I already knew that, of course. The doctor's confession had cracked the case open nine days ago; the sanatorium ledger had confirmed it. But knowing a thing in your head and seeing it laid out in satin under a rose window are different organs of knowledge, and the second one hurts more.

* * *

The widow went first.

Corinne Vane processed down the aisle on her nephew's arm — Julian, executor, mourner-in-chief, a man whose grief fit him like a rented tuxedo. Corinne herself was dressed in dove gray rather than black, which the obituary writers would call elegant and I called a message: *I am not finished being a hostess.* She walked the length of the nave at exactly the pace of a processional, paused at the casket for exactly eleven seconds — I counted, because counting is what I do instead of praying — touched the casket's edge with two fingers, and took her seat.

It was flawless. It was also, I realized as she settled her veil, identical to the way she'd greeted guests at the solstice gala three weeks earlier: the pause, the touch, the tilt of the chin. Corinne Vane had one choreography for all occasions, and death had merely been added to the program. When the priest said *our brother Aldous*, her shoulders shook at the right moments, in the right rhythm, like a metronome someone had set to *bereaved, moderate*.

Julian leaned toward her once, murmured something, and she nodded without turning her head. Whatever they were saying to each other, it wasn't comfort. It was logistics. I've watched enough couples at the Grand Meridian's front desk to know the difference between intimacy and coordination, and these two were coordinating.

Then came Sera.

The daughter walked alone. No arm offered, none taken. She wore black — real black, the kind that drinks light — and she moved down that long aisle like a woman crossing ice she didn't trust. Halfway to the casket, her step faltered. Not a stumble. A refusal. Her body simply declined to carry her closer, and for a moment she stood in the center of the nave with two thousand eyes on her, breathing.

She looked at the casket the way you look at a stranger wearing your father's coat.

Then she did the thing that told me everything. She laughed.

It was a small sound, quickly strangled — half a breath, half a sob, gone before the organ could cover it — but I was watching for it the way a locksmith watches a keyhole. People laugh at funerals. Nerves do it, shock does it, the terrible comedy of ritual does it. But they laugh *at* things: a eulogy's typo, a memory, the absurdity of the flowers. Sera Vane laughed at nothing. She laughed at the fact of the casket itself, at the audacity of it, and then she pressed her knuckles against her mouth so hard they went white, and she wept — genuinely, horribly, the kind of crying that has no rhythm and no dignity, her whole body folding over the pew rail like laundry dropped from a line.

The congregation mistook it for devotion. I knew better. You don't collapse like that over a body you believe in. You collapse like that over a lie you've agreed to live inside.

Sera Vane knew the man in the box wasn't her father.

* * *

The service ran ninety minutes, and I used every one of them.

The eulogies were a masterclass in the family's architecture. Julian spoke first — of course he did — and gave a speech about legacy that was really a speech about administration: the charity's programs, the endowment's growth, the *continuity of his uncle's vision*. He used the word *stewardship* four times. He never once used the word *love*. Behind the pulpit he had the relaxed shoulders of a man reading his own victory lap, and twice — twice — his eyes lifted from his notes and swept the congregation the way a landlord sweeps a property. Counting. Assessing. I ducked behind a pillar of the nave and let a funeral bouquet block my sightline until he moved on.

The lawyer from my list of five — Merrick Hale, silver-haired, silver-voiced — read a passage from Ecclesiastes with the smoothness of a man reciting terms he'd drafted himself. The archivist, a small dry woman named Pell, sat in the third pew and did not sing any of the hymns, though her lips moved throughout, and I'd have bet my retainer she was checking names off some internal register. And Dr. Ansel Roche, the charity's physician, the man who had confessed to me over cold tea that Aldous Vane had died once before — sat near the front and stared at the casket with an expression I can only describe as professional. He looked at the corpse the way a man looks at work he signed off on. There was no grief in his face at all. There was relief, carefully buried, and above it a thin frost of fear.

Five names, five functions. The lawyer to legalize, the archivist to record, the doctor to certify, the hotelier to house — and the fifth name, redacted, still a blank space where a person should be. They sat scattered through the congregation like punctuation marks in a sentence I hadn't finished reading.

And threaded through all of it, invisible to everyone but me, was Cyrus.

I didn't see him. That was the point. But I felt the shape of his attention the way you feel weather coming — a pressure at the back of the neck. He'd promised to stay away. *Funerals are for families,* he'd said in the dockside boarding house, in a voice that suggested he'd attended his own family's funerals from the outside his entire life. But Cyrus Fell had hired me to prevent a murder, then vanished, then reappeared just long enough to hand me the truth about himself, and a man like that doesn't miss the closing night of the show. Somewhere in those two thousand mourners, or in the gallery, or in the shadow of the bell tower, my client was watching his father's funeral — the second one, if you counted the one staged at a mountain sanatorium twenty years ago — and I found I couldn't decide whether I hoped he'd come closer or stayed away forever.

Both, probably. That was always the trouble with Cyrus. He wanted his father dead and alive at once, and now the world had granted him exactly that impossible wish, and there was nowhere to put it.

* * *

After the committal — a private affair at the cliffside cemetery, which I gate-crashed by carrying a tray of mulled wine I had no authority to carry — the family received condolences in the cathedral's side chapel, and that was where I got close to Sera.

The receiving line was a gauntlet: Corinne at the front, hand extended, cheek presented, murmuring thanks in a voice sanded smooth by practice. Julian behind her, working the crowd like a candidate. Then Sera at the end, holding a handkerchief she never used, shaking hands mechanically while her eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance where, presumably, her actual father was living out whatever remained of his life under another name, in another city, spending the liquid fortune the inheritance machine was designed to protect.

I joined the line. Marta's black dress did its work; nobody looks closely at a woman in mourning clothes at a funeral, which is the single greatest security flaw in the entire industry of grief.

When I reached Sera, I did the thing I'd decided to do somewhere around the fourth hymn, the thing that had been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone since the nave: I took her hand and held it one beat longer than condolence requires, and I said, very quietly, "You laughed."

Her hand went rigid in mine.

"In the nave," I said. "Halfway down the aisle. Before you cried. You laughed at the casket." I kept my voice below the organist's postlude, below the murmur of the line, in the register meant only for her. "People don't laugh at their fathers. They laugh at strangers."

For a moment she was perfectly still, and I watched her decide — watched the calculation move behind her eyes the way tide moves under harbor ice. Deny, and I'm a madwoman in a borrowed dress. Confirm, and she's handed a piece of herself to a stranger who might be anyone.

"You're the concierge," she said finally. Her voice was wrecked and level at the same time, which takes either enormous discipline or enormous damage, and I suspected both. "From the Meridian. You were at the gala. You asked my father's assistant about the guest suites."

"I ask about everything. It's my job."

"And your job brought you to my father's funeral?"

"My job," I said, "was to prove a murder wouldn't happen. It happened anyway. So now my job is to find out who's lying to me, and everyone is, Mrs. Vane, everyone in this building, including —" I hesitated. Empathy is a complication. I've built a career on noticing things, and noticing is easy; it's the caring that ruins you. But her hand was still in mine and it was trembling now, fine tremors like a struck wire, and I heard myself say the next part gently, which is not my native register. "Including you. You're lying with your face. It's exhausting. I know, because I do it professionally."

Something broke open in her expression — not the tears again, those were spent, but the thing underneath them. She glanced past my shoulder at Julian, thirty feet away, accepting condolences with his widow's-arm around a grieving aunt. Then she pulled me sideways, out of the line, into the alcove behind the baptismal font where the candles burn for the unnamed dead, and she stood so close I could smell the salt of dried tears on her veil.

"He told me a year ago," she whispered. "At the sanatorium. He thought I wouldn't understand, and he was right, I didn't, I —" She stopped. Started again. "He said he'd been living as another man since before my wedding. He said the man everyone thought was Aldous Vane was — a colleague. A double. He said he'd died, really died, for four minutes, twenty years ago, and when he came back he couldn't be that person anymore. That person belonged to a debt." Her voice thinned to thread. "I thought it was grief talking. Early grief. His brother had just passed. I told myself he was confused."

"And now?"

"Now there's a man in that casket," Sera Vane said, "who taught me to sail when I was nine years old, and it isn't him. It's his face. It's his hands. But it isn't him, and I stood in front of two thousand people today and mourned a stranger on purpose, because if I say it out loud —" She looked up at me, and her eyes were the exact gray of her father's, the real father's, the ones I'd studied in a newsreel clip at two in the morning. "If I say it out loud, then whoever killed that man killed him *for* my father. As my father. And my father let them."

The candles guttered in their alcove. Somewhere beyond the chapel wall, Julian's laughter rang out, warm and carrying, the laugh of a man whose machine was running smoothly.

"Sera," I said. "Who arranged the funeral?"

"The family. Julian, mostly. With Mr. Hale." She frowned, the frown of a woman assembling a picture from pieces she'd been refusing to look at. "Why?"

"Because whoever arranges a funeral controls what gets buried," I said. "And your father isn't the only one who's good at burying things."

She caught my wrist as I turned to go. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested, desperate, and for one unguarded moment she was not a philanthropist's daughter but a girl at a graveside who had just learned the grave was empty.

"Find him," she said. "Not the man in the box. My father. Find him before Julian does, because Julian —" She faltered, glancing again toward the sound of that warm laughter. "Julian keeps saying the estate will be *settled*. Like a debt. Like a score. And my father taught me that when Julian says *settled*, he means *finished*."

I covered her hand with mine, briefly, and promised nothing, because promises are inventory and I wasn't sure yet what I had to give.

Outside, the winter light was failing over Vellamare's harbor, and the bells began to toll the hour — six strokes, slow, the city counting something it didn't understand. I stood on the cathedral steps with the list of five names in my pocket and one new certainty pressing against my ribs like a hand:

Aldous Vane was alive, hiding, and running out of places to run. And somewhere between this funeral and the settling of the estate, Julian Vane intended to make sure the second death stuck — permanently, legally, and with paperwork.

I just had to find the dying man first.
