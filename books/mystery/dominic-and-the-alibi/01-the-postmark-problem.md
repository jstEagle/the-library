## 1. The Postmark Problem

The dead-letter tray lived at the bottom of the third drawer, beneath the wax seals and the box of brass paper fasteners that no one had used since the cathedral had installed its first telephone. It was a shallow wooden tray, its felt lining worn to a grey nap in the shape of forty years of other people's mistakes, and Wendell Fairweather had inherited it the way she had inherited the whole archive: without instruction, without ceremony, and without a single note from her predecessor explaining what any of it meant.

She liked it that way, mostly. An archive without notes was an archive that trusted its reader.

On the first Monday of November, with rain ticking against the vestry window like a patient finger, she pulled the tray out and set it on the long oak table under the lamp. There were eleven items in it. There were always between eight and fourteen items in it; the dead-letter tray of St. Aldric's Cathedral was a small, reliable graveyard, and she was its sexton. Letters addressed to saints. Letters addressed to the dead. One, every few years, addressed simply to *God, St. Aldric's, the town* — and those she read, because it seemed rude not to, and then she burned them in the vestry grate with a kind of borrowed solemnity, as though the smoke might count as delivery.

She worked left to right, the way she did everything. A christening gown order from 1987, never collected. A parish newsletter returned twice, its recipient deceased, then the forwarder deceased, then presumably the forwarder's forwarder deceased as well. A child's drawing of the cathedral with the spire drawn so tall it curled off the page and back onto itself, addressed to *the angels, care of the tower*. Wendell held that one a moment longer than the others. She did not file it in the dead-letter tray. She put it in the drawer where she kept things that were true even if they were not records.

The eleventh item was a long white envelope, softened at the corners as if it had been carried in a pocket for a long time before it was ever posted.

No name. No return address. The front bore only four words in a small, upright, careful hand:

*To the Keeper of Records.*

Wendell turned it over. The postmark was smudged but legible, and it did two things to her at once, the way a single chord can be both sweet and wrong. It named the sorting office at Millhaven, forty miles east. And it was dated the fourteenth of June — which was, she knew without looking it up, without wanting to look it up, the day after Edmund Voss's funeral.

She had catalogued that funeral herself. Not attended — catalogued. The parish burial register, volume nine, page two hundred and six: *Edmund Charles Voss, aged 81, interred in the Voss plot, south lawn, service conducted by the Rev. Canon A. Pryce.* She remembered the entry because the family had requested a line added beneath the standard formula, an unusual request, and the line had read: *Faithful servant of this parish.* She remembered thinking, as she wrote the marginal note in the finding aid, that it was the sort of phrase that could mean anything, and that most of the time it meant nothing, and that once in an archivist's life it meant something she would never be told.

She had not attended. Archivists did not attend; they preserved. It was a distinction she had made a rule of, and rules were how she kept the work from becoming grief.

Now she held a letter postmarked one day after that funeral, addressed to a title that had been hers for only fourteen months, and the rain ticked, and the lamp hummed, and she did not open it. Not yet. First she did the thing she always did, the thing that made Canon Pryce tease her gently at coffee hour — *Wendell, you catalogue your breakfast* — she documented the object before she disturbed it. Date of discovery. Condition of envelope. Postmark transcription. She wrote it all in the accession log in her small even hand, and only when the letter had been properly entered into the record did she permit herself to become a person again, and open it.

The paper inside was the same long white stock, two sheets, folded in thirds. The handwriting was the same as on the envelope — small, upright, careful, the letters standing like choristers who have been told, repeatedly, that they are being watched.

It began without salutation.

*You will have heard that Edmund Voss is dead. Everyone is relieved. I am writing so that relief does not become the last word, as it usually does.*

*I was at his funeral. I did not go in. I stood by the wall by the yews and watched them carry him past, and I thought: there goes the only man this town ever agreed to believe.*

*You keep the records, so you will know the story. The boy who drowned in the summer of 1994. Tobias Marsh, fifteen years old, taken by the water below the mill bridge on the last Friday of July. The inquest sat for two days. The whole of it rested on one witness — Edmund Voss, who swore he saw the boy slip from the bridge stones, saw him go under, saw nothing and no one else, and the coroner thanked him for his clarity, and the town thanked God it was an accident, and the case was closed before the blackberries came in.*

*I am writing to tell you that the witness never existed.*

*Not the man. The man existed; you may verify that in your burial register, and I am sure you have. But the witness — the clear-eyed man on the bridge who saw everything — that person was manufactured, the way a document is manufactured, the way a record is manufactured. Edmund Voss was not on the mill bridge that afternoon. He could not have seen what he swore he saw. He was somewhere else entirely, and he said what he was told to say, and the town was so grateful for a story with an ending that no one ever asked him where he had been standing, or why.*

*I know this because I know where he was. I know because I was there too.*

*You are the Keeper of Records. Keep this one.*

There was no signature. The second sheet was shorter. It contained a single paragraph, and a line that Wendell would find herself reading, over the coming weeks, at odd hours, in the way one touches a bruise to check whether it still hurts:

*I have been dead a long time now, and it is a comfortable life, but I find at my age that comfort is not the same as rest. Ask the register. The register remembers what the town agreed to forget.*

Wendell set the sheets down on the felt of the tray, aligning their edges, which was what her hands did when her mind was elsewhere.

*The witness never existed.*

She had read a great many strange things in fourteen months of keeping St. Aldric's records. She had read a 1921 dispute over the ownership of a pew that had ended in a lawsuit and a broken nose. She had read the minutes of a vestry meeting at which three grown men had shouted for forty minutes about the colour of a carpet. She had read confessions of a sort — the archive was full of the small, ordinary confessions people make to institutions because they cannot make them to each other: unpaid fees, unacknowledged children, a 1963 letter in which a man asked the cathedral to pray for a woman he declined to name, *because she knows what she did, and so do I, and that is between the three of us and God.*

But this was not that. This was a letter that claimed the founding document of a death was a forgery. That the inquest's single pillar was air. That a boy had gone into the water below the mill bridge and the town had buried not a body but a story.

And it was postmarked the day after the perjurer's funeral, which meant the writer had waited — what? Thirty years? More? — and had waited until the man who could contradict them was safely under the south lawn.

That was the detail that made her skin prickle, and she noted it with professional admiration even as it chilled her. The letter was not the outpouring of a guilty conscience. Guilty consciences do not keep calendars. This was a document released on a schedule. This was an archive acting.

She should have filed it.

That was the honest truth, and she admitted it to herself that evening as she locked the vestry, and again at home over soup, and again in bed with the lamp off, watching the ceiling go grey with the streetlight through rain. There was a folder in the dead-letter system — she had created it herself, in her first month, with the tidy optimism of a new appointee — labelled *ANOMALOUS / UNVERIFIED*, and this letter belonged in it the way a bone belongs in a cabinet. Unverified. Undated internally. Unsigned. The product, very possibly, of a grieving mind or a mischievous one; Millhaven had a long-stay psychiatric unit, and cathedrals received letters from it twice a year like clockwork. Canon Pryce had a gentle phrase for such correspondence — *the voices the stones attract* — and a gentle procedure, which was to say none at all.

She nearly filed it. She had the folder open. She had the letter halfway in.

What stopped her was not the content. It was the *hand*.

She had gone back to the letter a second time — archivists always go back; the first reading is for the story, the second is for the object — and she had been looking at the writing itself, the way one does, idly, the way one looks at a face. Small. Upright. Careful. And something in it itched. Not wrongness — correctness. An excessive, disciplined, almost anxious correctness, the letters formed with a precision that no one's natural hand achieves, a precision that is *taught*.

She knew that precision. She had spent fourteen months immersed in it.

She was out of bed before she had fully decided to be, and down the stairs in the dark, and she did not put the lamp on in her sitting room because she did not need it; she had the letter's first sheet spread beside her on the table and her laptop open to the digitized registers, and her finger moved between them, and the itch resolved into recognition with a click she felt in her teeth.

The 1994 baptismal register. Volume eight. She had digitized it herself in the spring — every page photographed, every entry indexed — and she had noticed then, without noticing, the beauty of one particular hand. Not the curate's standard entries, which were hurried, slanting, the writing of a man with a queue of families waiting in the drizzle. There was one entry — a baptism by emergency, a sick infant, performed at the font at odd hours — and beneath it, in the register's wide lower margin, a formal attestation of the witnesses, written not by the curate but by one of the witnesses, in a hand that stood at attention.

Small. Upright. Careful. The *a*s with that particular flattened bowl. The *t*s crossed with a single decisive stroke that never varied in angle, not once across four lines, not once across thirty years.

The attestation in the 1994 register was signed. The signature was a woman's: *U. Brant.*

The letter on her table was unsigned.

But the hand was the same hand. Not similar. The same. The same hand had written a witness attestation in the summer of 1994 and a confession in the spring of this year, and between those two documents lay Edmund Voss's entire afterlife as the sole witness to a drowning, and his funeral, and one day of silence, and then this.

Wendell sat in the dark with the rain ticking and felt the particular sensation that she privately called the itch — the itch of a record that refuses to stay alphabetized, that keeps surfacing in the wrong drawer, that will not lie flat no matter how many weights you set on it. Most records, once filed, behaved. They went to sleep. This one was awake. This one had been awake for thirty years, waiting for the one person whose whole job was to notice that a hand did not match its history.

*U. Brant.*

She typed the name into the index. The search returned the baptismal entry and nothing else. No marriage. No burial. No confirmation. No appearance in the school rolls, the electoral supplements, the choir lists, the flower rota — and Wendell had indexed the flower rota back to 1911, because the flower rota, she had learned, was where a parish kept its true social history.

A woman who signed a register in 1994 and then, in the records of St. Aldric's, ceased to exist.

The letter said: *I have been dead a long time now, and it is a comfortable life.*

Wendell looked at the two sheets in the lamplight she still hadn't turned on, their edges pale in the grey glow of the screen, and she understood that she was holding something that was not a letter. It was a key. And she understood, with the flat certainty that archivists develop about their own instincts — the certainty that had gotten her this job and would, she suspected, eventually get her into trouble — that whoever had written it had chosen her deliberately. Not the dean. Not the parish office. *To the Keeper of Records.* They had wanted the person who would check the hand.

Outside, the rain kept its patient time against the glass, and somewhere below her floor, beneath the flagstones and the heating pipes and two hundred years of quiet, the crypt of St. Aldric's held its locked mail slot and its one unaccounted key, and waited, as the stones always waited, for her to come down.
