## 3. The Drowning Year

The clippings lived in a box that smelled of another century's glue.

Wendell Fairweather found it on the third morning, on the bottom shelf of the archive's north wall, where the cathedral kept the things it did not quite know what to do with — the jubilee programmes, the choir rosters from dissolved parishes, the obituary scrapbooks assembled by some long-dead verger with more devotion than method. The box was labeled in faded copperplate: *MISCELLANEOUS — 1990–1996*. Miscellaneous. As if a boy's death could be filed under the same heading as a roof repair.

She carried it to the reading table beneath the lancet window and set it down carefully, the way one sets down something that might still be breathing.

Outside, October was doing its slow work on the plane trees of the close, stripping them gold by gold. Inside, the archive held its usual temperature — cool, dry, faintly mineral, like the inside of a stone. Wendell had come to love that smell over her four years as archivist of St. Aldric's. This morning it felt different. This morning it smelled like evidence.

She had not planned to open the box. That was the honest truth of it, and Wendell was a woman who made herself tell the honest truth at least to herself, even when no one else required it. She had come down to shelve a donation of hymnals and had simply — paused. Paused at the shelf where she'd left the confession letter locked in her desk drawer upstairs, its postmark burning a hole in her mental catalogue: *14 June 2019*, one day after Edmund Voss's funeral. One day after the town had buried the man the newspapers had once called *the sole witness*.

*There was never any witness,* the letter said. *I would know. I made him up.*

And beneath that, in the same careful liturgical hand: *Ask the river what it remembers.*

So she had paused at the shelf, and then she had pulled the box, because an archivist learns early that the past does not organize itself; someone arranges it, and the arrangement is always a kind of argument.

* * *

The first clipping was from the *Marshfield Gazette*, 21 June 1994. The paper had gone brittle to the exact color of weak tea, and the headline had faded from black to a bruised brown:

**BOY DROWNS IN MILL RACE; SOLE WITNESS DESCRIBES TRAGEDY**

Wendell read it twice, slowly, the way she read anything she intended to remember.

Tobias Marsh, fourteen years old, of Fenwick Lane, had gone into the water below the old Harrow Mill on the evening of 18 June. His body was recovered the following morning, two hundred yards downstream, snagged against the sluice gate. The coroner's finding was accidental drowning. The mill race, the article noted, was deep and fast in June, swollen by rains, and the boy was known to swim there despite warnings.

Then the article turned to its centerpiece, and Wendell understood why this clipping had been kept — why all of these had been kept, folded with a care that bordered on reverence:

Edmund Voss, thirty-one years old, a stonemason and parishioner of St. Aldric's, had been walking home across the mill bridge when he saw the whole of it. The boy slipping on the wet stones above the race. The flailing. The going-under. Voss had run for help, but the river had already done its work.

"I saw him fall," Voss was quoted as saying. "No one pushed him. No one touched him. He slipped, poor lad. I'd swear it on anything you like."

*I'd swear it on anything you like.*

Wendell sat back. The reading-room clock ticked somewhere behind her, patient as a chaperone.

A witness who swears on anything you like is a witness offering his oath in advance of the price. She had catalogued enough depositions in the diocesan records to know the shape of certainty, and this had a different shape — too smooth, too complete, a testimony with no rough edges, no hesitation, no *I think*, no *I couldn't say for sure*. Just the fall, just the slip, just the poor lad, sealed shut like a jar.

But that was a feeling, not a fact. Feelings didn't go in the catalogue.

Facts did. So she kept reading.

* * *

The second clipping was from ten days later. A shorter piece, tucked on an inside page: **MARSH CASE CLOSED; CORONER PRAISES WITNESS**. The inquest had returned a verdict of death by misadventure. The coroner had commended Mr. Voss for his "courageous and clear-eyed account," noting that without it, the matter might have lingered in uncertainty indefinitely. The family — the Marshes were a small family, a thin line on the town's map, a father who worked the timber yards and a mother who sang in the cathedral voluntary choir — had asked only for privacy in their grief.

Wendell turned the clipping over. On the back, in the dead verger's copperplate, someone had written a date and a single word: *Closed. Thanks be.*

Thanks be. She ran her thumb over the ink. Whoever had saved these articles had been grateful for the ending. Grateful the way a housekeeper is grateful when a stain comes out of the carpet.

The third clipping was different. It wasn't news at all but a notice — a funeral notice, printed in the same paper six days after the verdict. Tobias Marsh, beloved son. Service at St. Aldric's Cathedral. Interment in the north section of the churchyard, near the yew.

Near the yew. Wendell knew the spot. Everyone in the town knew the spot; children dared each other to touch the yew's trunk after dark, and every Hallowmas the vergers raked its black needles into drifts that smoldered sweetly in the bonfires. She had walked past that corner of the churchyard a thousand times on her way to ring the archive bell.

A grave she had walked past a thousand times.

She set the clipping down and pressed both palms flat against the table, as if the wood might steady whatever was beginning to tilt in her.

*The witness's testimony was the only thing that closed the case.* She heard her own thought arrive with the flat clarity of a catalogue entry. And the case, closed, had opened a grave — a real one, with a boy in it, near the yew, where the needles drifted. Everything about the end of Tobias Marsh's story flowed from one man standing on one bridge, saying *I saw.*

If the letter in her drawer was true — if Edmund Voss had seen nothing, had been nowhere near the bridge, had been *made up*, as the hand insisted — then the case had never been closed at all. It had been shelved. Misfiled. And Wendell Fairweather had spent four years learning exactly how much damage a misfiled document could do, how long a lie could survive in a well-ordered room, growing roots into every record that touched it.

* * *

By late afternoon she had read everything in the box twice and laid the clippings out on the green baize in chronological order, a little paper river of their own. The drowning. The inquest. The funeral. Then — and here the box grew strange — a gap of two years, and then three final items that did not belong to any scrapbooker's gratitude.

The first was a carbon copy of an exhumation licence, dated March 1996. Wendell stared at it for a long moment before she let herself believe the words. *Application for removal of remains: MARSH, TOBIAS A., interred St. Aldric's north churchyard.* Grounds cited: subsidence of the north wall requiring foundation work within the burial ground. Signed by the coroner. Countersigned by the incumbent — Canon Aylward, Pryce's predecessor, whose portrait hung in the refectory with the mild, watery gaze of a man photographed against his will.

Exhumed. The drowned boy had been dug up, two years after they'd put him under the yew, because of a *wall*.

The second item was a short note, undated, on cathedral letterhead: *Reinterment approved. Costs met privately. No further publicity desired. — A.*

No further publicity desired. Which explained the third item, or rather explained its absence: there was no newspaper clipping about the reburial. In a box maintained by someone devoted enough to save every printed word about the case, the second funeral went unrecorded. A boy buried twice, and the second time in silence.

Wendell stood, walked to the window, and watched the light fail over the close. Her breath had begun to fog faintly against the glass. The heating in the archive never quite reached this corner, and the cathedral around her was settling into its evening sounds — doors, footsteps, the far-off complaint of the organ being tested for Sunday.

She thought: *I should take this box to Canon Pryce.*

She thought: *Pryce will ask me why I was looking.*

She thought: *I was looking because of a letter that came through a slot only three keys can open, postmarked the day after Edmund Voss died, claiming the sole witness never existed — and I cannot yet prove who wrote it, and if I carry this box up those stairs, I may be carrying it into the very hands that want it forgotten.*

That was the trouble with archives, she told the darkening glass. They taught you caution. Every record was a door, and you learned which doors opened onto rooms people still lived in.

She returned to the table and copied out the essentials into her notebook in her own neat hand — dates, names, the licence number, the initials *A.* — and then she reboxed the clippings exactly as she'd found them, folding each along its existing creases. If someone came looking, the box would tell them nothing had been disturbed. She was getting good at thinking like that now, and she disliked herself a little for it.

At the bottom of the box, beneath the last folder, her fingers found something the inventory had missed: a photograph, small, glossy, its corners rounded by handling. Two boys on the mill bridge, arms slung round each other's shoulders, squinting into a summer sun. One was tall and fair, grinning wide. The other was slighter, darker, caught mid-laugh, one hand raised as if warding off the camera or blessing it.

On the back, in pencil, in a child's uneven capitals: *TOBY & E. VOSS, JUNE 1993.*

Toby and Edmund Voss. The drowned boy and the sole witness, best friends a year before the water took one of them.

Wendell looked at the laughing boy for a long time. *I saw him fall,* the man had sworn, a year later. *He slipped, poor lad. I'd swear it on anything you like.*

She slid the photograph into her notebook, between the pages, where no inventory would find it. Then she put on her coat, locked the archive behind her, and climbed the stairs toward the close, where the lamps were coming on one by one and the river, somewhere below the town, was running dark and patient toward the sea.

It was only in the doorway, pausing to lock the outer door, that she noticed it — pinned neatly beneath the archivist's brass bell, where notices for lost gloves and cancelled practices were usually left.

An envelope. Her name upon it. No stamp, no postmark.

Hand-delivered.
