## 4. Alphabet of Absences

The archive at St. Aldric's kept its own weather.

Wendell Fairweather had learned this in her first month as archivist, when she noticed that the north aisle of the undercroft stayed cool in August and warm in January, as if the stone remembered a different season than the one outside. The air smelled of vellum and cold iron and, faintly, of candle smoke from decades past — a smell that had seeped so deeply into the shelves it could never be cleaned out, only inherited. She had come to think of it as the smell of patience. Paper waited better than people did.

But on the morning after she found the confession letter, the archive's patience felt less like comfort and more like surveillance. Every shelf seemed to watch her work. Every folio she opened seemed to open an eye.

She had laid out the letter on the long oak table beneath the green-shaded lamp — the good lamp, the one with the brass chain that let you lower the light to a reading distance without scorching the page. Beside it, weighted flat with lead pellets sewn into canvas, lay the documents she'd pulled overnight: baptismal registers, marriage rolls, burial books, confirmation lists, spanning 1988 to 1999. A decade. The drowning year and its long gray aftermath.

The letter's handwriting was a small, upright, disciplined script — the kind taught by rote in church schools before the war and clung to afterward like a dialect on the edge of extinction. Round o's. A g with a closed loop descender. Crossbars on the t's that slanted slightly upward, as if every word were reaching for something just above itself.

Wendell had spent eleven years among these registers. She knew their hands the way a choir mistress knows voices — not by reading each one fresh, but by recognizing timbre. And this voice she knew.

She turned to the 1994 baptismal register first, because that was where she'd first heard it. March, the entry for a child surnamed Halloway. *Received into the congregation,* written in that same upright hand, those same reaching crossbars. Then April. May. June — the hand steady through all of them, patient as the stone above her head.

Then she turned to the 1994 confirmation roll.

And stopped.

* * *

A register has a body, and its body has a rhythm. Entries follow entries like heartbeats: name, date, officiant, sometimes a note in the margin — *father deceased*, *of the Mill Lane family*, *twins*. When you've read enough of them, you feel the pulse of the page even before your eye lands on any particular line. Wendell felt the arrhythmia immediately, before she understood it.

The 1994 roll was bound in quarter leather, marbled boards, the spine stamped CONFIRMATIO in faded gilt. She opened it with the bone folder, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you suspected might be hurt. The pages for spring and early summer were there, dense with names in several hands — the curate's, the dean's secretary's, one flamboyant contributor who dotted his i's with circles. Forty-one names between Easter and Pentecost.

Then came the autumn section. And the pulse went wrong.

There was no tear. No ragged stub where paper had been ripped away — that would have been violence, and violence leaves evidence. What there was instead was a seam. A whisper-thin line running vertically down the gutter of the binding, so precise it might have been a shadow, if shadows fell inside books. Wendell tilted the page toward the lamp. The line caught the light and held it: the faint silver kiss of a blade.

Someone had razored out a leaf. Not torn it — cut it, flush against the binding, with a steadiness that made her stomach turn. Then they had done something worse. They had rebound the stub. There was fresh-ish adhesive in the gutter, older than her tenure but newer than the book, and the remaining page had been trimmed along its inner margin to sit flush, so that the loss would read, to a casual eye, as nothing at all. Not a gap. An absence shaped exactly like presence.

She counted anyway. Archivists count; it is the first prayer we learn. The roll's index — a separate card in the wooden drawer upstairs — listed sixty-three confirmations for 1994. She counted the names remaining in the book.

Sixty-one.

Two names missing. Or one name entered twice, or a name and its sponsor, or — she wrote the arithmetic in pencil on a slip of acid-free paper — *at minimum, two lines of ink, gone.* Gone so cleanly that whoever catalogued the collection afterward, whoever microfilmed it, whoever trusted it for thirty years, had never once felt the page stumble.

Except her. Except now, because she was reading with a letter in front of her that claimed a celebrated witness had never existed, and because she had spent eleven years learning the heartbeat of these books until an skipped beat rang in her ears like a dropped tray.

Wendell sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere overhead, in the cathedral proper, a door closed — far off, soft, the building settling its bones — and she found herself listening to it longer than she needed to.

*Who has access?*

That was the question, and it had teeth. Because this was not a theft from a shelf in some public library. This was surgery performed on a bound volume, in place or after careful removal, followed by cosmetic repair — trimming, adhesive, alignment. It required time alone with the book. It required the confidence that no one would notice. It required, above all, access.

Access to the St. Aldric's archive meant three categories of person, and Wendell enumerated them the way she'd enumerate a shelf list, because enumeration calmed her and she badly needed calming. First: the archivist. Herself, since 2013. Before her, Mr. Ambrose Kettle, who had held the post for thirty-eight years and died at his desk — literally, at his desk, mid-catalogue, a man whose whole life had been a marginal note. Second: the clergy. Canons, deans, curates — men and women who came down for a christening record or a funeral date, always supervised, always logged in the visitors' book. Third: the keyholders. The people who could be here at two in the morning with a razor blade and no witness at all.

She thought of the crypt mail slot. Of the letter postmarked the day after Edmund Voss's funeral, written in a hand trained for liturgical script, posted into a slot that locked. Of Canon Pryce's warm voice yesterday: *These old buildings collect oddments, Miss Fairweather. Best not to make a mystery of every scrap.*

Best not to make a mystery. As though mysteries were litter, to be swept.

She looked down at the razored seam again, and the archive changed around her. That was the thing she could never have explained to someone who hadn't lived inside records: an archive is not a warehouse of facts. It is a body of trust. Every register is a promise that what was written was written, and what was not written was never there. She had built her whole working life on that promise the way the cathedral was built on its foundations — unthinkingly, structurally. And now she stood inside the structure hearing a crack travel up through the stones.

The crime scene was the thing she loved. That was the horror of it, and she let herself feel it fully, sitting alone under the green lamp: not fear, yet — but grief, sharp and specific, the grief of discovering a beloved room has been used for something unspeakable while she slept upstairs, so to speak, filing away.

*Before my time,* she told herself. *Kettle's era. 1996, maybe, once the case cooled.* But even as she thought it, her eyes went — traitorously, professionally — to the adhesive in the gutter. She touched it with the tip of a probe, barely. It flaked, yellowed, aged. Old glue. But how old? Glue aged dishonestly. Ten years could look like forty in a dry undercroft. The evidence refused to testify clearly, and she understood suddenly why criminals in novels always burned things: fire at least destroyed. This — this careful, silent, surgical absence — left a wound that healed over so well you couldn't date the knife.

She got up and walked the aisles, because sitting still had become unbearable.

* * *

The stacks of St. Aldric's ran in seven ranges under the south transept, steel shelving installed in the 1970s to replace the rotten oak, the old wood repurposed into wainscoting that still lined the reading room like a memory of itself. Wendell walked the ranges slowly, trailing her fingers an inch above the spines, not touching — never touching — letting the order of things steady her. Baptisms, 1541 forward. Marriages. Burials. Confirmations. Services. Vestry minutes. The great ledgers of the parish's self-regard.

At range four she stopped, because her feet stopped before her mind agreed to.

The visitors' log. The green cloth-bound book kept at the archive door since 1987, in which everyone entering signed their name and purpose. She carried it back to the table and set it beside the confirmation roll, and for a while she simply looked at both, feeling the way a chess player feels looking at a board where the losing move has already been made and only needs to be seen.

1994. She turned to it. The year of the drowning was a year of ordinary traffic: the diocesan surveyor in February; the organ restorer in June; a cluster of visits in September — *family history enquiry*, *photography permission*, *grave location, Marsh* — that last one dated the eleventh of September, six days after Tobias Marsh went into the water below the mill bridge. Someone had come down to find the Marsh grave within the week. She noted the signature: a firm, slanted, confident hand. *E. Voss.* The witness himself, locating the grave of the boy whose death he had seen. Or said he'd seen.

Then October. November. December sparse as winter grass.

And then — nothing. Nothing anomalous, nothing suspicious, nothing she could point to. The log recorded who came in. It did not record who stayed, or who had keys, or who needed neither because the door was theirs to lock and unlock at will.

She flipped back through the years around it, hunting the way you hunt a misfiled card: not knowing what it looks like, only knowing the alphabet is wrong somewhere. 1993. 1995. In 1996 there were two entries in March signed *A. Kettle, routine inspection* — Ambrose Kettle logging inspections of his own archive, which he had never done before 1994 and never did again after. Two inspections, ten days apart, in the spring of 1996. The year, she remembered from Chapter Three's clippings still stacked in her mind, the year the coroner's file formally closed and the press went quiet. The year, she would later learn, Tobias Marsh was dug up and put back down.

Was Kettle tidying? Was Kettle cutting? Or was Kettle logging his own movements precisely because someone else's movements needed covering — a loyal man leaving a trail of innocuous ink across a season of knives?

Wendell pressed the heel of her hand to her eyes. Speculation was a solvent; it dissolved everything it touched, including the speculator. She was trained better than this. Evidence first. Feeling later. She had a rule about it, pinned above her desk at home: *The record is the witness. Interrogate it; don't imagine it.*

So she interrogated.

She pulled the microfilm of the 1994 roll — filmed in 2009, she saw from the leader, during the diocesan digitization project. If the razor had come before the camera, the film would show the gap as original; if after, the film would preserve the lost names. Her hands were not quite steady threading the reel, and she hated that, hated the tremor as evidence of something she wasn't ready to name.

The film ran. Spring pages, summer pages, the same forty-one names marching past on the reader's pale screen.

Autumn.

The screen showed autumn pages intact. Full. Unrazored. Sixty-three names for the year, complete, the two missing souls present and accounted for in silver halide — a boy named Colm Frey and a girl whose surname began, the flickering frame suggested, with B.

Wendell sat very still.

The film predated the cut. Which meant the cut was younger than 2009. Which meant —

Which meant it happened on *her* watch. Between 2013 and now. In the years when the archive had one keyholder who used it daily, one dean who descended for records "for the commemorative exhibit," and one third key unaccounted for since 1994.

The undercroft's cold reached up through the floor then, real or imagined, and Wendell understood that the question she had brought down here — *who erased this page?* — had quietly changed into a worse one: *who erased this page while I was the archivist, and how many times have I walked past them?*

She thought of Pryce's warmth. She thought of the way he'd asked, casually, terribly casually, whether she'd finished cataloguing the nineties boxes. She thought of the letter writer — alive, afraid, literate in the archive's grammar, able to address a confession to the crypt slot with the certainty that it would land somewhere it might someday be read.

And she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I am being managed. The page is gone, the notes may be next, and the management has already begun.*

* * *

She worked until the lamp's circle was the only light in the undercroft and her tea, forgotten at the table's end, had gone the temperature of the stone. Before she left, she did the thing she should perhaps have done days ago, and which she would remember doing, afterward, as the moment the case became hers beyond recall.

She photographed everything. The razored seam, raking light and direct. The adhesive in the gutter. The film frames showing the lost names. The visitors' log, 1994 through 1996, every entry. Then she sealed the 1994 roll itself in an archival box, labeled it in her own unmistakable hand — *CONFIRMATION ROLL 1994: DO NOT CONSERVE. DO NOT REBIND. EVIDENTIAL* — and carried it up the stairs, past the wainscoting of dead oak, into the cathedral's evening gloom, to the safe in her office that only she could open.

On her way out, locking the archive door behind her, she paused. The cloister was dark, the fountain off for winter, and the whole great pile of St. Aldric's stood around her holding its breath. Somewhere above, in the deanery wing, a light burned in a window she could not see but knew was there.

Three keys, she thought. Hers. The dean's. And one, unaccounted for, since the drowning year.

Tomorrow she would start asking, very carefully, about the third key.

Tonight, walking home through streets that smelled of rain and coal smoke and other people's dinners, she kept hearing the sound the razor must have made — that long, quiet, deliberate whisper of steel through vellum — and understanding, at last, that it had not happened once. Archives do not lose single pages. Archives are lost by degrees, one clean cut at a time, by people who know exactly which absences will go unnoticed.

The only question left was how many cuts she hadn't found yet — and whether the next one was already waiting for her name.
