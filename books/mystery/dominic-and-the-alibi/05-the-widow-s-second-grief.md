## 5. The Widow's Second Grief

The note came on a Tuesday, folded inside a plain envelope with no stamp and no return address, which meant someone had walked it up the lane and posted it through the slot by hand. Marian Voss stood in her kitchen holding it the way one holds a wasp — carefully, at arm's length, waiting to see what it would do.

*Ask Wendell about the letter.*

Seven words in a small, upright hand. The ink had bled slightly where the pen had paused, as if the writer had hesitated over each word before committing to it, or perhaps after. Marian read it three times standing at the counter where Edmund used to leave his keys, and then she set it down beside the teapot and did not touch it again for two days.

Grief, she had learned, was a house with many rooms. There was the room of the first week, all casseroles and black wool and hands pressing hers at the graveside. There was the room of the first month, when the casseroles stopped and the house began to speak in Edmund's absences — his coat still on its hook, his half of the bed still cold in a way that felt less like emptiness than like held breath. And there was this new room, opening now without her permission: the room of questions. Because Edmund had died afraid. She had known it the way a wife knows weather, without being able to produce evidence. He had burned papers in the garden basin in March, feeding them in handfuls, watching the flames with an expression she had never seen him wear at any funeral, any graveside, any of the ten thousand deaths he had witnessed from his pew at St. Aldric's. He had been, near the end, a man watching a door.

And now a stranger's handwriting was asking her to open it.

She made tea she didn't drink. She sat in Edmund's chair because sitting in her own felt like agreeing that the house had only one occupant now. Outside the window, the yew hedge threw its permanent shadow across the lawn, and beyond it the cathedral tower stood against the grey sky like a finger raised in warning or in blessing — after forty years of living in its long shade, Marian had never decided which.

*Ask Wendell about the letter.*

She didn't know who Wendell was. That was the humiliating truth of it. Forty years in the parish and she knew the deans and the organists and the flower guild by their Christian names, but the archivists were a species that lived below stairs, among the ledgers, surfacing only when someone needed a baptismal certificate for a grandchild's school application. She thought there might have been a Mr. Something-or-other before, a stooped man with ink on his fingers. This Wendell must be the successor.

On Thursday morning she took out Edmund's good coat — her own, actually; he'd stopped wearing good coats his last year, had taken to dressing like a man trying not to be noticed — and she walked into town along the river path, past the mill ruins with their broken teeth of stone, and up the hill to St. Aldric's, telling herself the whole way that she was going for ordinary reasons. To arrange the memorial plaque. To discuss the flowers. Widow's business. Business widows were allowed.

The side door of the cathedral opened onto a corridor that smelled of stone dust and candle wax and, beneath both, something older — the smell of centuries of damp paper, of vellum slowly giving up its secrets to the air. Marian followed it the way she supposed a pilgrim follows a rumor of relics, past the vestry, past the locked door of the crypt stair with its brass plate worn illegible, until she found the archive: a low room off the cloister garth, its windows fogged with age, lit by two green-shaded lamps that turned everything the color of deep water.

A woman was at the long table, sorting papers into trays with the absorbed precision of someone defusing something.

"You'll be Wendell," Marian said.

The woman looked up. Younger than Marian expected — forty, perhaps, with the kind of face that keeps its thoughts behind glass. "Mrs. Voss," she said, and rose, and there was something in the way she said the name — not *Mrs. Voss*, exactly, but *Mrs. Voss* — that told Marian the woman had been expecting her, or at least expecting *a* her, some widow-shaped question walking in out of the rain.

"Everyone knows me," Marian said. "That's what being married to a parish for forty years does."

"Wendell Fairweather." A handshake. Dry, cool hands, ink at the second knuckle of the right forefinger. "I'm sorry about your husband. He was — " a pause, brief as a caught breath — "he was well known to the archive."

"Was he." Marian let it sit there, neither question nor statement, and watched the young woman's face for the flicker. There was a flicker. Small, quickly banked, but there — the look of someone who has just remembered a letter.

"Can I help you with something? The memorial plaque, perhaps — "

"The flowers are arranged. The plaque is arranged. Everything about my husband's death has been arranged by other people since the day he died." Marian heard her own voice go flat and hard and did nothing to soften it. "I've come about something else. Though I confess I don't know what. I received a note."

Something shifted in the room. Not the woman — the light, perhaps, or the pressure of the air, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere far off in the building. "A note?"

"Anonymous. It said — " and here Marian stopped, because she had rehearsed this in the kitchen for two days and now, faced with the actual ink-stained stranger, she heard how it sounded. Like madness. Like grief talking. "*It said, ask Wendell about the letter.*"

The silence that followed had texture. Marian had spent forty years reading silences in church — the silence before the sermon, the silence after the benediction, the terrible polished silence of the peace when two feuding families shook hands — and this one was none of those. This one was the silence of a person deciding, very fast, what to tell you.

"I don't know anything about a letter," Wendell Fairweather said.

Which was, Marian thought, the most interesting thing she could possibly have said. Because she hadn't asked whether Wendell knew about *the* letter. She had said *a* letter, indefinite, unmarked, and the archivist had reached straight past every letter in the world — condolence letters, letters of administration, forty years of Edmund's correspondence with the diocese — and picked out one particular letter as if it had been glowing in the dark between them.

"I see," Marian said mildly.

"I mean — people write to the archive constantly. I couldn't speak to a specific — "

"No, of course not." Marian smiled, and was aware, distantly, that it was her funeral smile, the one she'd worn for three weeks until her face learned it. "I must have misunderstood. It's the grief, everyone tells me. Grief makes you imagine things. Notes. Meanings. Husbands burning papers in the garden at midnight."

The green lamps hummed. Somewhere above, in the body of the cathedral, a door closed with a sound like a book shutting.

"Burning papers," Wendell repeated. Carefully. The way you repeat a word to see if it's load-bearing.

"He was always tidy," Marian said. "Tidy men don't burn things. Tidy men file them." She looked around the room — at the trays, the boxes, the ranks of grey archival cartons climbing the walls like a congregation of the patient dead. "He trusted this place, you know. Forty years of tithes and committees and he never once doubted the church. Then the last year — " She stopped herself. This was the trouble with circling: you gave away ground with every step and called it conversation.

"The last year?" Wendell asked. And her voice had changed — dropped its receptionist polish, gone quieter, closer to the bone. She wasn't performing sympathy anymore. She wanted to know. Whatever else the woman was hiding, whatever letter sat in whatever tray with its postmark like a wound, she genuinely wanted to know what had happened to Edmund Voss in his last year.

And Marian, who had come here to watch and probe and give away nothing, heard herself say: "He was frightened."

The words came out of her like water from a cracked jug, and once they started they would not stop.

"Not of dying. He made his peace with that early, he was very sensible about it, we chose the hymns together in February. Frightened of — being found. Of something catching up with him. He'd sit by the window in the evenings facing the wrong way, facing the garden, watching the gate. He stopped answering the telephone. He burned things. And once — " her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the weakness, hated the tenderness that survived everything — "once I woke at three in the morning and he wasn't in bed, and I found him sitting on the stairs in his dressing gown, in the dark, just sitting there with his head in his hands. And I said, Edmund, whatever is it? And he said — "

She had never told anyone this. Not the doctor, not the vicar who took the funeral, not her sister on the telephone. It had been theirs, hers and Edmund's, the last secret of a marriage, and she was giving it away in a basement to a stranger with ink on her hands because a note had told her to, and she could not stop.

"He said, *There's a girl under the stairs.*"

The lamps hummed. The fogged windows gave back nothing.

"That's what he said," Marian finished. "There's a girl under the stairs. And I said, what girl, whose stairs, and he wouldn't say another word. He went back to bed. And six weeks later he was dead, and I buried him, and I have been sitting in that house ever since listening to the stairs."

Wendell Fairweather had gone very still. Not politely still — arrested still, the stillness of a woman whose entire interior landscape has just rearranged itself. Her eyes had gone to the middle distance, to some shelf of thought only she could see, and her lips moved faintly, silently, testing the phrase the way one tests a key in a lock.

*Mrs. Voss,* she said finally, and stopped, and started again. "Did your husband keep papers? Anything at all, even — "

"Burned," Marian said. "All of it. Except — "

Except. And here was the thing she had carried for three weeks like a coal in her pocket, the thing she had told no one, the thing she had almost thrown away twice and twice retrieved from the bin like a madwoman, and now she heard herself saying, "Wait here," and walking out of the archive with her heart going like a girl's, across the cloister, out into the thin cold light, to her handbag left in the nave with the verger, because she had brought it after all. Of course she had brought it. Some part of her had dressed for this meeting a week before the note arrived.

When she came back, Wendell was standing, and there was color in her face that hadn't been there before.

Marian laid the thing on the table between the green lamps.

A corner of paper. Burned at three edges, curled and browned to the color of old tea, no bigger than a playing card. On it, in a hand she recognized — Edmund's hand, the hand that had signed the marriage register beside hers, the hand that had written her love notes in courting days and shopping lists in tired ones — a fragment of writing, cut off on every side:

*—ella*

"That's all," Marian said. "That's all he didn't burn. One corner. I found it behind the grate in the garden basin after the funeral, when I was — clearing. As if it had blown clear at the last moment. As if it didn't want to burn." She laughed, briefly, horribly. "Listen to me. As if paper wants things."

But Wendell wasn't laughing. Wendell was looking down at the scorched corner the way people look at relics, and her right hand — the ink-stained one — had risen halfway toward it and stopped, hovering, as if the fragment were hot, or holy, or both.

"—ella," she said softly. "Urs-ella. Mar-ella. Lu-ella. Prisc-ella. Est-ella." She was naming names the way Marian had heard her sort papers, alphabetically, methodically, a mind running down its shelves. Then she looked up, and for the first time their eyes met without either of them flinching, and Marian saw in the young woman's face something that answered the question she had carried up the hill: not friend, not threat. Something rarer and more dangerous than either.

A witness.

"Mrs. Voss," Wendell said quietly, "may I ask you something, and may I ask you not to answer it here?"

The lamps hummed. Above them, faintly, footsteps crossed the cathedral floor — unhurried, clerical, the tread of a man who owned the building and everything under it.

"Ask anyway," Marian said.

"Who else knows you came here today?"

And Marian Voss, widow of forty years' standing, felt the cold walk up her spine on small feet, because she realized she did not know — could not know — who had written the note, or why, or whether *ask Wendell about the letter* had been an invitation at all, or bait, or a test; and that somewhere in this building of stone and silence, someone had wanted these two women in this room together, watched and weighed.

"No one," she said. "No one knows."

"Good," said Wendell, and folded the burned corner into a sheet of acid-free paper with the tenderness of a nurse, "then let's keep it that way — at least until I know which of us the letter was meant to find."

* * *

They parted at the west door, awkwardly, two women who had exchanged more truth in twenty minutes than either had spoken aloud in months and who now had no protocol for it. Marian pressed her hand — dry, cool, ink-stained — and said she'd be in touch, and Wendell said yes, soon, and both of them lied beautifully about feeling better already.

Marian walked home the long way, along the river, past the mill. The ruined bridge stood over the water with its broken parapet, and she stopped, without quite deciding to, and looked at the dark pool below where the current turned slow and green. Tobias Marsh. She had been a young wife when the boy drowned; she remembered the bells, the hush, the way the whole town had leaned toward one family's grief like grass toward wind. She remembered Edmund coming home from the inquest pale as linen, and her asking what was wrong, and Edmund saying *nothing, love, it was dreadfully sad, that's all* — and she had believed him for thirty years, the way you believe a man who has never given you cause not to, right up until the night he sat on the stairs and spoke of a girl under them.

The water moved below the bridge. A fisherman downstream lifted his rod, cast, waited. Everything in this town waits, Marian thought. Everything holds its position and watches the surface.

At home, the anonymous note lay on the kitchen counter where she had left it. She picked it up again and held it to the window, and this time she did what she should have done on Tuesday: she looked at the handwriting properly. Small, upright, careful. A hand trained to be legible — trained, she thought suddenly, the way choirboys' hands were trained, or vergers', or anyone raised copying out psalms under a strict eye. And the ink had bled where the pen paused. After each word. As if the writer were left-handed, dragging the pen, fighting the smear.

Edmund had been right-handed all his life.

Marian set the note down beside the teapot, next to the ghost of his keys, and understood that she had spent the week asking the wrong question. Not *who wrote to me.* Not even *what did Edmund know.*

The question was: who in this parish, all those years ago, had learned to write like the church — and was still alive, and still afraid enough to reach into a widow's house?

Outside, the cathedral tower stood against the darkening sky, and somewhere high in it, or low beneath it, a bell that had not rung in years hung waiting in the gloom, keeping, like everything else in St. Aldric's, its own counsel.
