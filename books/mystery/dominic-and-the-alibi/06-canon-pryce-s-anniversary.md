## 6. Canon Pryce's Anniversary

The invitations went out on a Tuesday, which Canon Alistair Pryce considered the correct day for anything that required forgiveness in advance. Tuesday had none of Sunday's obligations, none of Friday's penitential weight. It was the day the Church of England did its laundry.

He stood at his study window in the deanery, watching the postman's bicycle wobble down Cathedral Close, and counted. One hundred and forty invitations, cream stock, gilt-edged, each bearing the cathedral's new sesquicentennial mark — a stylized spire inside a laurel wreath, designed by a firm in London that charged more per letter than the verger earned per week. One hundred and forty envelopes. One hundred and forty families whose names appeared somewhere in the stone of this building, carved into benefactors' plaits and memorial brasses and the donor wall by the north transept, going back to the wool merchants who had raised the first roof beams in 1874.

One hundred and fifty years. The town would expect a dinner. The diocese would expect gratitude. And Pryce — he straightened the fold of his cuff, an old habit, smoothing what could be smoothed — would give them both, flawlessly, because flawlessness was the only sermon he had ever truly believed in.

The trouble with anniversaries was that they were excavations. Everyone said so, everyone laughed about it — *oh, digging up the past* — without understanding how literally true it was. To celebrate one hundred and fifty years you had to produce one hundred and fifty years, whole and presentable, like a bride's family producing photographs. And photographs, in Pryce's long experience, were never neutral. Every archive was an argument. Every exhibit a verdict delivered in velvet.

He turned from the window to his desk, where the planning binder lay open — leather-bound, tabbed in eleven colors, maintained with the devotion other men gave to prayer. *Catering: Hartwell & Sons, confirmed. Wine: the 1998 vintage from the Dean's cellar, supplemented. Music: the Merbecke Quartet, deposit paid. Seating chart: draft four.* He ran his thumb down the seating chart and paused, as he always paused, at table nine.

Marian Voss. Recently widowed. The name sat there in his own handwriting like a splinter.

Edmund Voss had been dead eleven weeks, and the town still hadn't decided what Edmund's death meant, which suited Pryce fine. Let them wonder. Wonder was diffuse; wonder dissipated. What wonder must never be given was a shape, a narrative, a *witness*.

He closed the binder on table nine and went down to the close, where the morning light was doing its best with the west front, gilding the apostles whose faces the weather had long since made democratic.

* * *

The archive occupied the undercroft beneath the south cloister, and Pryce descended into it the way he descended into all uncomfortable places: briskly, with his hands clasped behind him, as though he were merely passing through.

Miss Iversen was at her worktable, and she did not hear him at first. She was bent over the dead-letter tray — he noted that, filed it away, the dead-letter tray, of all things — sorting envelopes with the absorbed concentration of someone who trusted paper more than people. She was younger than he'd expected when the chapter appointed her, and more thorough, and those two qualities together had begun, over the past months, to constitute a problem.

"Miss Iversen."

She started — actually started, shoulders jumping — and turned, and her face composed itself into courtesy a half-second too late. "Canon Pryce. I didn't hear the door."

"No," he said pleasantly. "No one ever does. That's rather the point of an archive, isn't it? Things kept where they can't interrupt anyone." He advanced into the room, letting his gaze travel over the shelving with proprietary affection. Racks of boxes, brown and patient. The parish registers in their cradles. The smell — dust and vellum and the ghost of incense rising through the floor from the Lady Chapel above — that smell was the smell of safety, if you understood it correctly. Everything here was already past. Nothing here, properly managed, needed to happen again.

"I'll keep you only a moment," he said. "As you know, we have the anniversary dinner approaching, and the chapter has approved a commemorative exhibit. *A Hundred and Fifty Years of Faithful Service.* Photographs, vestments, the founding charter, that sort of thing." He smiled the smile he used for fundraising. "I'll need the historical files for 1994."

Something moved behind her eyes. Small. Quick. A fish turning under ice.

"1994," she repeated.

"The drowning year," he said lightly, as though the words cost nothing, because they must not appear to cost anything. "Tobias Marsh. Terrible business. There was considerable press at the time, and the exhibit committee thought a respectful acknowledgment — a photograph of the memorial perhaps, the flowers the school lays each year. Closure, Miss Iversen. That's what anniversaries are *for*." He let the word sit between them, warm and reasonable. "Also, I'm afraid, a practical matter. With the dinner consuming so much of your time — the exhibit preparation, the display cases, liaising with the caterers about the cloister access — the chapter has agreed to reassign some of your routine duties. The register transcriptions will go to Mr. Dalgleish for the duration. You'll want to focus on the celebration."

"The registers," she said. Her voice was level. Too level; he'd heard that flatness before, in other voices, in other years, and it always meant the same thing: a mind closing around something and refusing to open. "You'd like me to hand over the current transcription work."

"Temporarily. Through the autumn." He spread his hands. "It's an honor, really, what you're being asked to do. Not many archivists get to build a cathedral's memory with their own hands."

"No," she said. "Not many do."

She agreed — of course she agreed; there was nowhere else for the conversation to go, not yet — and he left her among her boxes with the pleasant conviction, which he permitted himself the way other men permitted themselves a drink, that everything was now arranged.

Only in the stairwell, climbing back toward the daylight, did he allow himself to count what he had actually accomplished, item by item, like a man checking a bill: the 1994 files requested, formally, so that their removal — if removal became necessary — would appear administrative. The register transcriptions reassigned, so that no fresh hands need turn the pages of certain years. And Miss Iversen herself, buried under ribbon-cuttings and caterer's invoices, too busy to go looking through drawers he would very much prefer stayed shut.

Three moves. Clean as a liturgy. Menace dressed as ceremony worked precisely because ceremony was expected; no one questioned a dean who wanted the past tidied for a party. Tidying was the job. Tidying was the whole of the job.

At the top of the stairs he paused, one hand on the cold iron rail, and permitted himself ten seconds of the memory he never permitted more than ten seconds of: rain on the mill bridge, a boy's shoe, a woman's voice saying *I saw* in a register office while he stood witness and wrote his name in the margin of another man's lie.

Then he smoothed his cuff, and went up to write thank-you notes.

* * *

By Thursday the exhibit committee had metastasized, as committees do, into eleven members and a subcommittee, and Pryce chaired them all in the vestry room with the agenda projected onto the whitewashed wall. Mrs. Halloway wanted bunting. Colonel Renwick wanted the military band. The headmaster of the cathedral school wanted the choir to perform a piece commissioned for the occasion, and produced a sample recording on his telephone that made the organist wince audibly.

Pryce let them spend twenty minutes on bunting. Bunting was harmless. Bunting was the point. Every minute spent arguing about bunting was a minute not spent asking what else might be in the boxes.

"The 1994 materials," he said at last, tapping the agenda, "will be handled sensitively. A single case. Photographs of the memorial garden, the order of service from the funeral, a brief text. Nothing sensational. This is a *celebration*, not an inquest."

"Inquest," repeated Mrs. Halloway, with the relish of a woman who read the local paper cover to cover. "Well, there was never any question about poor Tobias Marsh, was there? That Voss man saw the whole thing. Sole witness, they called him. It was in the *Courier* for weeks."

"Sole witness," Pryce agreed, and poured the tea, and the phrase passed through the room and out of it, unexamined, the way it had passed through the town for thirty years, worn smooth as a pebble by handling. Sole witness. He had almost come to love the phrase, in the way one loves a load-bearing wall: not for its beauty but for what it held up.

It was Mrs. Halloway again, later, gathering her knitting, who nearly undid the afternoon. "And is it true about Miss Iversen?" she asked, in the doorway, in the confiding tone women used when they meant to be told something. "That she found a letter? In the crypt slot? My nephew is a verger, you know, and he says she's been up there half the night with the old registers—"

"A letter," Pryce said, "is not necessarily news, Mrs. Halloway. Cathedrals receive hundreds. Most of them are prayers, and the rest are complaints about parking." He smiled. She laughed. The door closed.

But he stood in the emptying vestry room for a long moment afterward, listening to the building settle around him — that vast creak and tick of old timber and older stone, the sound of an institution breathing in its sleep — and revised his arithmetic. The letter. Of course. The dead-letter tray. He should have anticipated the dead-letter tray; it was exactly the sort of unglamorous drawer a thorough person opened when she had nothing better to do, and he had just made sure she would have nothing better to do for six weeks.

Which bought him time, or spent it — he honestly couldn't tell anymore which.

* * *

Friday evening, after Evensong, he went down to the crypt alone. His key turned in the lock with the small obedient sound it had made for nineteen years, and he stood in the dark among the tombs with his hand flat against the mail slot's iron lip, thinking.

Three keys. His own. The archivist's, hanging now presumably on a hook in the undercroft. And the third — the third had gone into the river of ordinary loss years ago, lost with a set of vestry keys when the old sacristan died, or so the inventory said, and inventories were documents, and documents could be believed if you had no choice. He had checked the inventory twice this month. It still said what he needed it to say.

The slot itself was a modest thing, a brass-mouthed aperture in the crypt door, installed a century ago for the devotional notes of the poor — petitions, prayers, the occasional coin. People believed things posted into darkness reached God more directly. Pryce had never shared the belief. In his experience, things posted into darkness reached whoever held the key.

Someone had posted a confession into that darkness. Someone with a steady enough hand to write a full page and a frightened enough heart to do it anonymously, the day after Edmund Voss went into the ground — as though death had loosened something, as though a grave opening anywhere in the parish pulled a thread loose everywhere else.

He should have taken the letter when he'd had the chance. That was the error, and he catalogued it with the dispassion he brought to all his errors: he had assumed the letter would arrive addressed to the chapter, or to him, and instead it had arrived addressed to no one, which meant it belonged to whoever found it, which meant it belonged to Miss Iversen, who was young and thorough and had begun, he suspected, to compare handwriting.

He knew she had compared handwriting, because he had watched her do it — not literally, but structurally, the way one watches weather. She had requested the 1980s registers last month. Then the confirmation rolls. Then, three days ago, she had sat for two hours with the 1994 box and emerged with a look he recognized from mirrors: the look of a person who has found a page missing and cannot stop imagining the knife.

So: the anniversary. He had built the anniversary into a wall between her and the truth — duties reassigned, files requested, calendar stuffed with florists and seatings — and walls, he knew, were temporary. Walls were what you built while you decided whether to dig the moat deeper or open the gate.

There was a third option, and it had been sitting in his desk drawer for a week in a manila folder with her name on it, and he was not yet desperate enough to use it. But the folder was there. Institutions survived by keeping folders.

He locked the crypt, and the sound of the key was very loud in the dark, and above him the cathedral held its hundred and fifty years of silence the way a full glass holds its surface — perfectly, tremblingly, until someone's elbow touches the table.

In the morning he would call the caterers. In the morning he would approve the bunting. In the morning, if Miss Iversen came to him with questions, he would smile his fundraising smile and speak of closure, and exhibits, and the healing balm of a town remembering together.

And if she did not stop — well.

The dinner was in five weeks. He had planned larger events with less notice.
