## 9. Marian's Locked Room

The house on Candlewick Lane had two kinds of rooms, Marian Voss had learned in the eleven weeks since Edmund's funeral: the ones she could enter, and the ones she could not. The kitchen was an enterable room. The bedroom, mostly. The study was not. The study still smelled of him — of pipe smoke soaked into the wallpaper and the particular dusty sweetness of old paper, which had been, in the last year of his life, the smell of fear as much as habit. She had not opened the study door since the morning she'd found him gone from his bed and present, impossibly, in the chair by the window, as though he had climbed out of his own sleep to keep one last appointment with the light.

So when the knock came at the front door — three soft raps, the knock of someone accustomed to waiting — Marian was grateful for the interruption, and then immediately ashamed of the gratitude, and then, when she opened the door and saw the young woman from the cathedral standing on her step with the wind pulling at her coat, ashamed of something else entirely.

"You're the archivist," Marian said. "Wendell Fairweather."

"Yes, Mrs. Voss. I'm sorry to call without writing first. I know how it looks, a stranger at the door."

"You're not a stranger. Edmund spoke of you. He said you were the first archivist in thirty years who actually read what she filed." Marian stepped back to let her in, and the words came out more bitter than she intended, because everything came out more bitter than she intended now; grief had gotten into her mouth the way damp gets into a wall, invisibly, and ruined the plaster. "He said it as a compliment. He meant it as a warning, I think, though I didn't understand that until later."

Wendell stood in the hall with her hat in her hands, a narrow young woman with ink on the side of her right hand and the careful, listening stillness of someone who spent her days among things that could not answer. "Later," Marian repeated. "That's why you're here, isn't it. Later."

"May I ask you something, Mrs. Voss? And may I ask it plainly? I've found that in this town, plainly is the only language that doesn't have a second meaning."

Marian almost smiled. "Ask it."

"Did your husband write a letter? A confession. Posted through the crypt slot at St. Aldric's, the day after his funeral. It said that the witness to Tobias Marsh's drowning — the witness whose testimony closed the case — never existed. That Edmund Voss invented him."

The hall clock ticked. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice, the house settling into its bones. Marian found that she was gripping the newel post, and that her knuckles had gone the color of the marble.

"No," she said.

Wendell's face did not fall, exactly. It did something worse. It became patient.

"No," Marian said again, "I did not know he wrote it. But yes — yes, I believe he could have. Because in the last year of his life, my husband burned his papers. Not filed them. Not archived them, God forgive us both, but burned them, in the grate, a handful at a time, like a man destroying love letters. And he would stand at that window—" she pointed up the stairs, toward the locked room, toward the smell of old paper and fear— "and he would whisper to himself, over and over, the same three words. *The girl under the stairs.*"

* * *

They did not go into the study at first. Marian made tea she had no intention of drinking, because her hands wanted occupation and the kettle wanted filling, and because the ritual of it — cups, the good saucers, Edmund's mother's sugar bowl — bought her the few minutes she needed to decide how much truth a stranger could be trusted with. Wendell sat at the kitchen table with her hands folded, and did not rush her, and did not fill the silence with the small dishonest noises people make when they are uncomfortable. Marian decided that was a kind of honesty in itself.

"Tell me what you know," Marian said at last. "All of it. If you've come to my door, you've already been to the registers, and I won't have you piecing him together out of parish paper and calling that knowing him."

So Wendell told her. The letter, the postmark, the handwriting that matched a baptismal register from 1994. The razored page in the confirmation roll. The three keys to the crypt, one unaccounted for. The mill bridge, and the initials carved into the stone that belonged to neither the dead boy nor the celebrated witness. She told it plainly, as she had promised, in the flat careful voice of a woman reading an inventory aloud, and Marian listened and understood that this young archivist had been walking for weeks through the same country Edmund had lived in at the end — a country of documents that lied by omission, of polite men managing the past like a budget.

When it was done, Marian sat for a while with her hands around the cooling cup.

"He was a good man," she said. "I want that said in this kitchen, out loud, before I say anything else. Whatever he did in 1994, he was a good man who did one cowardly thing, and then spent thirty years paying interest on it. Do you know what debt does to a face? It doesn't show all at once. It shows in the way a man stops meeting his own eyes in the mirror."

"Mrs. Voss—"

"Marian."

"Marian." Wendell leaned forward. "The letter says the witness never existed. But someone testified. Someone's name is in the inquest record, in the newspapers, in the parish's own anniversary pamphlet. Edmund Voss saw the drowning from the mill bridge. That's the story the whole town stands on."

"Yes." Marian set the cup down. The porcelain clicked against the wood, a small precise sound, like a key turning. "And I have asked myself every day since you knocked whether I'm about to bury him a second time. But he's dead, and the dead can't be shamed, only misrepresented, and I have spent eleven weeks misrepresenting him by silence." She drew a breath. "Edmund did not see the drowning. Or rather — he saw it, but he was not alone on that bridge, and the person who was with him was not a man, and was not called Edmund, and by the time the constable came asking, that person had been made to disappear so thoroughly that the only witness left standing was my husband. He took the testimony. He was paid for it — not in a bag of coins, nothing so honest as that. His debts were paid. His father's debts. The Voss name was made respectable again, and all it cost was a lie and a girl."

"The girl under the stairs," Wendell said quietly.

"The girl under the stairs." Marian closed her eyes. "He said it the way other men say prayers. Not to inform anyone. To punish himself. In the last year, when the fear got bad, he'd say it and say it, and I'd ask him — Edmund, who is the girl under the stairs, what girl, what stairs — and he would look at me with such *terror*, such naked animal terror, and he'd say, *If I tell you, they'll have to do to you what they did to her.* And I believed him. God help me, I believed him, and I let him carry it alone, because I was a coward too, in my own smaller way. I preferred a haunted husband to a dangerous truth."

The wind came against the kitchen window and the ivy scratched at the glass, and for a moment neither woman spoke.

"He burned the papers," Marian went on, "in the last winter. I'd come downstairs at night and find him at the grate with the fire tongs, feeding it things out of a biscuit tin. Photographs. Letters. A ledger — I remember a ledger, the paper was different, finer, and the ink bled green when it burned. I asked him once what it was and he said, *History, Marian. I'm paying it back.* And I thought he'd gone soft in the head, and I was almost relieved, because a mad husband was a husband I could nurse. A guilty one I'd have had to *know*."

She rose. She had known, when Wendell's knock came, that this moment would arrive; she had perhaps known for eleven weeks, standing outside the study door with her hand flat against the wood. She climbed the stairs now, and Wendell followed, and at the study door Marian stood a moment with her palm on the panel, the way you'd touch the flank of a horse before saddling it.

"I haven't been in here since he died," she said. "You'll understand why in a moment."

She opened the door.

The room was exactly as he had left it — the chair by the window, the cold grate heaped with pale ash, the desk with its green leather blotter gone brown at the edges. Dust lay over everything in a fine grey veil, and in that dust, on the desk, were the marks of the last things he had handled: the ghost-print of a book, the crescent of a tin lid. The biscuit tin itself sat at the back corner of the desk, dented, its lid slightly askew, as if he had meant to come back to it and had run out of time the way men run out of time — not all at once, but in the middle of an ordinary morning.

"He left it," Marian said. "The night he died, he'd been at the grate. I found ash on his cuffs. But he left this tin, and I've never had the courage — because whatever he saved, he saved for a reason, and reasons frighten me now." She lifted the tin and set it in Wendell's hands. It was heavier than it looked. "You read what she files," Marian said. "So read this. And then tell me what my husband bought with his good name, and whether it was worth what it cost."

* * *

Wendell opened the tin on the desk, in the study, because Marian would not leave the room and would not have the contents carried elsewhere — *he kept them here; they'll be judged here* — and because, Wendell suspected, neither of them could have borne the walk down the stairs in silence.

Inside: a burnt smell, soft as a moth's wing. Layers of paper, most of them ruined — edges curled to carbon, whole pages gone to black lace that crumbled when the light touched them. Edmund had been thorough and Edmund had been interrupted; both truths were in the tin. Near the bottom, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth, was a small folded square of paper that had survived, and Wendell unfolded it over the desk with the tenderness she used on water-damaged registers, breath held, fingers hardly touching.

It was a corner. Only a corner — torn, scorched along one edge, the paper gone the amber of old varnish. Most of whatever had been written on it was lost to the fire. But in the surviving triangle, in a small, cramped, careful hand, three letters remained, preceded by the broken tail of a fourth:

**—ella**

Below them, half a line, in the same hand: *she saw it all.*

Wendell did not speak. Marian, watching her face, read the verdict there before any words came.

"Ursella," Marian whispered. "There was a girl at the cathedral school — a scullery maid — Ursella. Edmund brought her name up once, decades ago, when I asked about his work, and he went white as the communion linen and left the table. I never asked again. I never asked again about *anything* after that." Her voice cracked on the last words, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and for a moment she was not a widow in a dusty study but a young wife at a supper table, watching her husband choose a lie over her, and choosing, out of love or cowardice, to let him.

"Mrs. Voss." Wendell's voice was very quiet. "The handwriting on this corner — I need to compare it. There's a register from 1994 with a signature I've been chasing. If this matches—"

"Take it." Marian said it quickly, before the coward in her could object. "Take the whole tin. Take the corner, take the ash, take everything. I kept his secret for thirty years by not looking, and look what not looking bought this town. A boy dead twice over. A girl erased. My husband whispering to the window like a man in a cell." She turned to the window herself, to the light Edmund had kept his last appointment with, and her reflection looked suddenly very old. "You said the letter was written by someone still alive and still afraid. I used to think it was Edmund, writing ahead of his own death. But he died with the secret, didn't he. So it wasn't him."

"No," Wendell said. "The graphologist says the writer is in their seventies now. Left-handed. Trained hand — liturgical script."

"Liturgical." Marian turned from the window. Something moved behind her eyes, something that had been waiting eleven weeks for permission. "The dean. Canon Pryce writes the anniversary pamphlets himself, every one, in that beautiful looping hand. He's left-handed — Edmund mentioned it once, some small thing, the way you notice a man's hands." She stopped, and Marian Voss, who had spent a lifetime not asking questions, asked the largest one of her life. "Miss Fairweather. When you find her — the girl under the stairs, the —ella — when you find what's left of her, will there be anything left to find? Or did they do to her what they did to that page in the confirmation roll?"

Wendell looked down at the scorched corner in her palm, at the three small letters that had outlived the fire that was meant to eat them.

"I don't know," she said. "But someone carved initials into the mill bridge stone that belong to neither the dead boy nor the false witness. And dead girls don't carve their names into bridges."

She folded the corner back into its oilcloth, and the study door, which had been locked by grief for eleven weeks, stood open behind them both.
