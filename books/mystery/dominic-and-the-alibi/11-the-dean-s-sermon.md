## 11. The Dean's Sermon

Canon Alistair Pryce had learned long ago that a sermon was not a message but an instrument — that the pulpit was less a place of proclamation than a position of advantage, from which one could survey every face in the nave and know, with the precision of a man reading gauges, exactly who was listening and who was afraid.

He stood in the vestry at half past ten on Sunday morning, adjusting his stole in the tall mirror that had hung there since before his time, and allowed himself the small luxury of admitting he was tired. Not weary in the soul — he did not permit himself weariness of that kind; it was a luxury for men without responsibilities — but tired in the body, in the way a man is tired who has spent three weeks managing an anniversary dinner, a commemorative exhibit, a bishop's correspondence, and one archivist who would not stop digging where she ought to have been dusting.

Wendell Fairweather.

He said the name to himself the way he said all troublesome names: once, flatly, and then set it down like a stone he would pick up again when his hands were free.

The trouble with Wendell Fairweather was not that she was clever. The cathedral had employed clever people before, and cleverness, properly channeled, made excellent footnotes and better fund-raising copy. No — the trouble was that she loved the archive the way he loved the institution itself: wholly, jealously, without any sense of proportion. She treated the registers as if they were scripture rather than paperwork. She had been seen, more than once, sitting in the stacks after hours with a lamp burning, cross-referencing things no reasonable person cross-referenced. And she had asked him — directly, politely, with that unnerving flatness of hers — why the 1994 confirmation roll had been requested twice in October by an office that kept no such requests on file.

He had smiled at her then. He was very good at smiling. Thirty years in the church had taught him that a smile was simply a door held open, and that most people, offered a door, would walk through it without asking what room lay behind.

But Fairweather looked at doors the way she looked at everything else: as records of something having passed through.

* * *

The bells began at eleven, and Pryce processed up the aisle behind the crucifer with the measured tread he could have performed asleep, past the Voss pew — empty now, poor Marian Voss having taken to sitting further back since Edmund's funeral, as though grief required a change of address — past the Hollis family, past old Mrs. Trenoweth who slept through every sermon and woke precisely at the blessing, and up into the chancel where the morning light came through the east window in great slanted columns of ruby and cobalt, laying colored wounds across the stone floor.

The cathedral was full. It was always full in the anniversary season; the town liked its history best when it came with music. The founding families sat in their accustomed places like furniture that had learned to breathe — the Ashcombes, the Penhaligons, the Marsh cousins, three rows of them, because the Marshes were numerous and the dead are always more numerous than the living remember.

*Tobias Marsh*, Pryce thought, and put the name away again.

He mounted the pulpit. He arranged his notes, though he did not need them; he had written this sermon the way he wrote all his important sermons, twice over, the second draft being the one that sounded like mercy while meaning something else entirely.

His text was from Isaiah: *Remember ye not the former things, neither consider the things of old.*

A dangerous text, honestly handled. Which was why he did not intend to handle it honestly.

"Brothers and sisters," he began, and let his voice fill the vaulting the way water fills a cistern, slowly and from below. "We have come this year to a great anniversary. One hundred and fifty years of this house of God standing above our town like a lighthouse above a rocky shore."

He gave them the lighthouse. He gave them the generations of faithful hands. He watched their faces soften into the warm, unexamined contentment that anniversaries exist to produce, and only then — only when the congregation was thoroughly anesthetized by nostalgia — did he turn the text.

"And yet Scripture does not only command us to remember," he said. "It commands us, also, to forget. *Remember ye not the former things.* Consider what a strange mercy that is. God, who sees all things, who remembers every sparrow that falls — even God, in His kindness, chooses what to hold before His eyes and what to set aside. How much more, then, should we — we who see so little, who understand less — be gentle with the past? How much more should we resist the temptation to dig up what has been decently buried?"

He let the word *dig* fall into the silence and stand there a moment, small and sharp as a dropped pin.

"For there is a kind of remembering that is not faithfulness," he went on, his gaze moving across the nave with the unhurried sweep of a lighthouse beam itself, "but vanity. There are those who mistake curiosity for conscience. Who believe that turning over every stone is the same thing as seeking truth. But stones, my friends, were put down for reasons. The mason lays each one where it belongs. And when we lift them — out of restlessness, out of pride, out of some private itch we cannot explain — we do not find truth beneath. We find only darkness, and damp, and the small blind creatures that have made their home in the dark because the dark is where they belong."

Somewhere in the third pew a baby fussed and was hushed. Mrs. Trenoweth's chin dipped toward her chest. And Wendell Fairweather, seated alone near the north aisle where the archivists had always sat, looked up at him with her pale, patient eyes and did not look away.

Pryce held her gaze for exactly as long as courtesy permitted — two seconds, perhaps three, the length of time a shepherd might rest his eyes on a sheep that had wandered too near the cliff edge — and then moved on, warmly, inclusively, to the rest of the flock.

"So let us forgive," he concluded, spreading his hands, letting the light catch the gold thread of his cuffs. "Let us forgive the dead their silences and the living their mistakes. Let us bury what is buried, and raise our glasses on Friday night to one hundred and fifty years of a town that knows how to keep faith — which means knowing how to keep quiet. Amen."

The organ swelled. The congregation rose. And Alistair Pryce descended from the pulpit feeling the particular satisfaction of a man who has locked a door in full view of everyone and called it hospitality.

* * *

Coffee after the service was served in the parish hall, in urns and mismatched cups, under a banner sewn for the anniversary that read ONE HUNDRED AND FIFTY YEARS OF GRACE in letters that had faded to the color of weak tea. Pryce worked the room as he always did, shaking hands, laughing at jokes he had heard for decades, dispensing warmth in carefully rationed measures.

He saved Wendell Fairweather for last. This too was technique: a man approached last has had time to be observed, and a man observed waiting is a man already half-forgiven whatever he is about to say.

She stood by the window with a cup of coffee she was not drinking, watching the cloister through the glass. Up close she smelled, faintly, of the archive — of dust and old vellum and the lemon oil she used on the leather spines. She had ink on her left cuff. He noted it, filed it, and smiled.

"Miss Fairweather. Exactly the person I hoped to see."

"Canon Pryce." She turned. Her handshake was dry and brief. "That was a fine sermon."

"You're kind. Though I suspect you didn't agree with a word of it."

"I agreed with the part about the stones," she said. "Masons do lay them where they belong. That's rather my point, usually."

There it was — that flat, unarmored honesty that made her so difficult to manage. Most people, given a sermon aimed at them like a thrown dart, would flinch or flush or laugh it off. Fairweather simply picked the dart up off the floor and handed it back.

"The exhibit is coming along well?" he asked, steering toward open water. "For Friday?"

"I've prepared what you requested. The 1994 materials are boxed and labeled."

"All of them?"

"All of them that exist," she said, and looked at him with those pale eyes, and for one unguarded moment Pryce felt the sensation he most despised in himself: the sensation of being read. As though he were a document and she had found the page where something had been razored out.

He recovered. He had a lifetime's practice at recovering. He reached into his inner pocket and produced the envelope he had placed there that morning, thick and cream-colored, sealed with wax because wax made even bribery look like ceremony.

"But I didn't find you to talk about boxes," he said. "I found you because I have news. Wonderful news, actually, and I wanted you to hear it from me before it became gossip."

She took the envelope but did not open it. Of course she didn't. She waited, the way she waited over documents, for the contents to declare themselves.

"The diocese of Northmoor," Pryce said, warming his voice to the temperature of good news. "You know it? On the coast — wild country, beautiful. Their record office is a disgrace, frankly. Two centuries of parish registers rotting in a damp cellar, uncatalogued, unindexed, half of them water-damaged. A tragedy. An absolute tragedy." He shook his head with genuine feeling, because he did feel it, in the way he felt all disorder: as a personal affront. "And they've written to us asking whether we might recommend someone. Someone with real archival training. Someone with the vision to build a modern catalogue from nothing. Someone, frankly, whose reputation deserves a larger stage than one cathedral's crypt."

He watched her take it in. He watched her understand it — he could see the understanding arrive in her face the way frost arrives on a window, silently and all at once.

"A promotion," she said.

"A recognition," he corrected gently. "A post with your own budget, your own staff, your own title. Principal Archivist. They'd want you there by Advent. The stipend is nearly double." He smiled his widest smile, the one that opened doors. "I can think of no one better suited. I told them so myself, in the strongest terms."

The hall murmured around them. Somewhere a child laughed. Through the window the cloister lay in its Sunday stillness, the grass green inside the stone square, and Pryce thought: *she will take it*. They always took it. The gilded door, the distant post, the honorable exile dressed as elevation. He had removed three people this way in his career — a treasurer who asked about accounts, a curate who asked about a will, a verger who asked about nothing at all except too often — and not one of them had ever understood, until the train pulled away, that they had been carried rather than promoted.

Wendell looked down at the envelope. Turned it over. Read the wax seal as if it were a postmark.

"Northmoor," she said. "That's four hundred miles away."

"It's a new life, Miss Fairweather."

"It's a long way from this crypt." She looked up. "Who applied for it? Because I didn't."

And there it was — the question laid down between them like a card turned face-up, and both of them looking at it, and neither of them touching it.

Pryce let the smile fade by degrees, the way he dimmed lights after a service, until what remained was not hostility — he was far too skilled for hostility — but something kinder and much more frightening: concern.

"Wendell," he said, dropping the surname for the first time, letting the familiarity land like a hand on the shoulder. "May I speak plainly? As a friend?"

"You may speak however you like, Canon. I'll listen either way."

"This work you've been doing. These questions. The letter, the registers, the—" he waved a hand delicately, as if brushing away a moth, "—the 1994 business. I know you think you're serving the truth. I respect that. I respected it in your predecessor too, God rest him." A pause, precisely measured. "But archives are not neutral ground, whatever the textbooks say. An archive is an institution's memory, and an institution's memory must serve the institution, or the institution dies. You cannot serve two masters. It's the oldest lesson there is."

"I only have the one master," Wendell said. "The record."

"The record is a building," Pryce said, "and buildings need roofs, and roofs need money, and money comes from the families in that hall. The Ashcombes. The Penhaligons. The Marshes." He let the last name sit in the air between them, and saw — he was almost certain he saw — the flicker of it land somewhere behind her eyes. "If you go to Northmoor, you will do magnificent work. Work that matters. Work that no one will ever need to be hurt by. If you stay—" he sighed, and the sigh was genuine, and that was the worst of it, "—then I cannot protect you from what staying costs. I'm not threatening you, Wendell. I'm describing weather. A wise woman checks the forecast."

For a long moment she said nothing. The coffee in her untouched cup had stopped steaming. Behind her, through the window, a rook crossed the empty cloister square, low and slow, dragging its shadow along the grass.

Then she held the envelope back out to him.

"Thank you, Canon. But I haven't finished cataloguing here."

"There will always be more to catalogue."

"Yes," she said. "That's rather the point of me."

She set the envelope on the windowsill beside her — not thrusting it at him, merely setting it down, the way one returns a borrowed book — and walked away across the hall, nodding to Mrs. Trenoweth, sidestepping the children, unhurried, her left cuff still ink-stained, until the door swallowed her and the Sunday light took her place.

* * *

Pryce stood alone by the window for some time, holding his coffee, watching the cloister.

He was not a cruel man. He wished, sincerely, that he could have spared her this. He had offered her everything short of the truth, and she had declined it with the same mild stubbornness she brought to everything, and now the weather he had described would arrive on schedule, and it would be nobody's fault but the climate's.

He thought of his predecessor — old Dean Harrow, dead these twelve years, whose hands had taught Pryce everything worth knowing about the keeping of institutions. *The archive forgives nothing, Alistair,* Harrow used to say, *which is why the archivist must.* He had thought it wisdom when he was young. He still thought it wisdom. It was simply that lately the words had begun to sound less like instruction and more like prophecy, and prophecies, in his experience, were just warnings that arrived too early to be believed.

On the windowsill, the cream envelope sat where she had left it, wax seal unbroken, already gathering the first faint film of hall-dust.

Pryce picked it up, weighed it once in his palm, and slipped it back into his pocket.

Then he went to find the sexton, because the crypt mail slot was to be changed before Friday — new lock, new key, one key only — and there was very little time, and so much, so very much, that needed to stay buried.
