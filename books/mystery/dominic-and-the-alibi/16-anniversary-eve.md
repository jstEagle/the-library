## 16. Anniversary Eve

The cathedral had a smell for every hour of the day, and Wendell had learned them all the way other people learned the faces of their children. Morning was candlewax and cold stone. Noon brought dust warming in the light through the clerestory, a smell like old bread. But evening — evening was her favorite and her dread, because evening smelled of iron and incense settling into the floorboards, of everything the day had stirred up going still again, sinking back down through the nave and into the crypt below, where the town kept what it preferred not to look at.

On the eve of the anniversary dinner, that stillness had a different quality. The scaffolding along the south transept stood wrapped in white sheeting like something bandaged. Garlands of ivy and late roses climbed the pillars, put up that afternoon by volunteers whose laughter had echoed off the vaulting and then, one by one, gone home. The great doors would open tomorrow at seven. Two hundred place settings waited in the refectory. The founding families — Ashcombe, Marsh, Voss, Pell — would raise crystal to a hundred and fifty years of St. Aldric's, and Canon Pryce would stand at the head of the table with his glass held high, and everything buried would stay buried, because that was what anniversaries were for.

Wendell stayed late because she could not make herself leave.

She told herself it was work. There was always work. The Voss correspondence needed rehousing; the 1994 box — what remained of it after the night someone had stood in her stacks and taken her notes with ink-stained left hand — needed a fresh inventory. But the truth was simpler and she knew it: she did not want to walk home alone through the dark with the letter in her mind, the confession's missing pages folded against her ribs in Marian Voss's careful hand, naming names, naming Ursella Brant, naming Pryce's predecessor as the architect of a girl's disappearance. Those pages were the heaviest thing she had ever carried, and she carried them now only in memory, having hidden the originals somewhere no dean would think to look — not in the archive at all, but in the one place sacred even to men who managed the past like an event budget.

She was re-shelving a folio of burial fees when she heard his footsteps.

Pryce walked softly for a big man; she had remarked on it before, the way deans learn to move through their own churches like hosts at a party, present everywhere, startling no one. But tonight there was no softness in it. His shoes rang on the stone flags, unhurried, deliberate, a man arriving on schedule.

"Miss Iversen." He stopped at the mouth of the stacks, hands clasped behind his back, and smiled the smile she had come to think of as his vestment smile — warm, seamless, worn over something else entirely. "Burning the midnight oil before the great day."

"Someone has to," Wendell said. "The exhibit files were due back Tuesday."

"Ah." He let the syllable hang. "Yes. About the files."

He came further into the stacks. The lamps here were low, amber, merciful; they made even the ledgers look kind. Pryce set a folder on the reading table between them — a plain manila folder, the cathedral's own stock, the kind that held employment records and disciplinary summaries and all the small paper machinery by which institutions remembered people wrong.

"I thought we might have our conversation tonight," he said, "rather than tomorrow. Tomorrow will be crowded. Tomorrow everyone will be watching the toast."

Wendell looked at the folder and did not touch it. "Whose file is that?"

"You know whose it is." He said it gently, almost fondly, the way one reminds a child of a game's rules. "Sit down, Miss Iversen. Please. This needn't be unpleasant."

She sat. Not because he asked — because her knees suggested it, quietly, the way fear works when it has been building for weeks. She had faced the razored page, the disturbed box, the figure fleeing into the cloister with her own handwriting tucked under its arm. She had thought herself braced for anything the institution could do. Then Pryce opened the folder and turned it toward her, and she understood she had been braced for the wrong weapon entirely.

Her own name looked up at her from the first page. *Iversen, Wendell A. Appointed Archivist, 2019.* Her appointment letter, her references, her university transcript — and then, three pages in, an incident report dated fourteen months ago, from her previous post at the diocesan records office in Cawston.

She had never seen the document before. That was the first thing. The second thing was how good it was.

*Following a routine audit,* it read, *it was determined that Ms. Iversen removed original materials from the Cawston repository without authorization, specifically two leaves from the parish register of St. Botolph's (1971), which materials were subsequently recovered damaged.* There was a witness statement, signed by a name she half-recognized — a junior clerk who had since moved to Canada, conveniently beyond the reach of a phone call. There was a "summary of remedial supervision." There was a closing paragraph noting that the matter had been handled "informally, in consideration of Ms. Iversen's otherwise satisfactory service," and that this forbearance should be understood as contingent.

Contingent. The word sat on the page like a spider.

"This is fabricated," Wendell said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her; she heard herself as if from across the table, a woman in a story, and wondered briefly if that was how Ursella Brant had sounded once, telling the truth to men who already knew it wasn't true.

"Is it?" Pryce folded his hands on the table. He did not look angry. He looked tired, and sorry, and reasonable, and that was the most frightening thing in the room. "You'll forgive me, but I've learned that in matters of records, Miss Iversen, provenance is everything. And this document has excellent provenance. Diocesan letterhead. Signatures. A chain of custody any tribunal would accept." He tapped the page once, lightly, a bell-ringer testing a rope. "Of course, documents can be challenged. Experts consulted. Handwriting compared. It would take months. It would take lawyers. And by then—" he spread his hands, encompassing the stacks, the cathedral, the anniversary, the whole patient architecture of the town "—by then the toast will long since have been drunk."

The lamps hummed. Somewhere above them, in the tower, the bells hung silent in their frames, waiting for tomorrow.

"What do you want?" Wendell asked.

"The same thing I have wanted from the beginning," Pryce said. "For you to be excellent at your job and curious about nothing. You were nearly that, when you arrived. I watched you, you know. In those first months. You filed beautifully. You alphabetized like a woman at prayer." He shook his head, and the sorrow in it seemed genuine, which was somehow worse than menace. "But then you found the letter. And I thought — perhaps she'll be sensible. Perhaps she'll understand that some pages are missing for reasons, that archives are not temples of truth but treaties between the living and the dead, renegotiated every generation. Some archivists understand that. The best ones, honestly. My predecessor certainly did."

"Your predecessor struck a boy and drowned him," Wendell said, "and erased the girl who saw it."

Something moved behind Pryce's eyes — not guilt, she thought. Something more like bookkeeping. An entry he had not expected, entered anyway.

"Edmund Voss struck him," Pryce said quietly. "That is what the record says, and what the record says is the only thing I am able to administer. I inherited a settlement, Miss Iversen, not a crime. Dean Halloway made the arrangements in 1994. I was a curate of twenty-six. I witnessed a reburial. That is the whole of my signature on it." He leaned forward. "And yet here we are, thirty years on, and you propose to unseal all of it at a dinner — my dinner — in front of the families whose endowments keep the roof on this building and the lights on in your archive. For what? For a woman who doesn't want her name back. Have you considered that? Ursella Brant spent thirty years becoming Mrs. Pell. Thirty years building a life on the foundation of being no one. And you want to dig her up and display her beside the commemorative china, for your satisfaction. Is that truth, or is it vanity?"

It was a good question. It was the question, and it had teeth, and Wendell felt them.

Because she had considered it. Lying awake in her narrow bed above the chandlery, she had turned it over and over: whether dragging Ursella into the light was rescue or a second drowning. Whether the dead boy needed his grave corrected, or merely left alone at last. Whether an archive's duty was to the truth or to the people the truth would break.

But underneath the question, always, was the page. The razored stub in the confirmation roll, clean-cut, professional, the ghost of a name that had been *Ursella Brant* in a seventeen-year-old's careful copperplate. She had held that stub to the lamp. She had read the shadow of the letters pressed through from the reverse side. Someone had decided, with a blade and steady hands, that a girl had never existed — and the institution had paid for the blade, shelved the wound, called it order.

"That's not mine to answer," Wendell said. "It's hers. Which is why I'm not reading your file or anyone else's tomorrow. I'm giving her the choice you never gave her."

Pryce exhaled slowly. Then he reached into his jacket and produced a second document — a single sheet, already typed, already signed. *Letter of resignation, accepted with regret. Effective immediately. Grounds: personal.* Beside it lay an envelope, and in the envelope, when he slid it across the table, she saw the top edge of a cheque. A generous one. Generous enough to be insulting.

"The Diocese of Northumbria is expanding its records program," Pryce said. "A new purpose-built repository. Sea air, Miss Iversen. Quiet. No founding families, no crypts, no letters in mail slots. A directorship, actually — they asked me for a recommendation, and I told them you were the finest archivist of your generation. Which is true." He met her eyes. "Or there is the resignation, and the cheque, and a silence that everyone will call dignity. Or—" he closed the manila folder with a soft, terrible click "—there is tomorrow, and the incident report, and a hearing, and by Christmas you are a cautionary tale told to new hires about what happens to archivists who confuse themselves with avenging angels."

He stood. He straightened his cassock. At the mouth of the stacks he paused, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I don't think you're a bad person. I think you're a person who believes the alphabet tells the truth. It doesn't, Miss Iversen. It only tells you where things are supposed to go." And then he was gone, footsteps fading down the cloister, and the cathedral breathed out around her, and Wendell sat alone with the folder and the envelope and the sound of her own heart.

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. Long enough for the lamps to seem dimmer, though she never touched the switch. Long enough for the cold to climb out of the stone and into her bones, so that her hands, when she finally looked at them, were pale as vellum.

The envelope sat where he'd left it. The cheque inside was real; she could see the watermark when she tilted it toward the lamp. The resignation letter was real. The incident report was real — real paper, real signatures, real diocesan seal, a forgery with better credentials than most truths. That was the lesson of the whole wretched year, she thought. Forgery was just provenance with worse morals.

She could take it. The thought arrived not as temptation arrives, dramatic and burning, but the way exhaustion arrives — gently, reasonably, wearing a cardigan. Take the directorship. Northumbria. Sea air. A clean repository with climate control and no history heavier than tide tables. Start over where no one had ever drowned, where the registers were boring and complete, where the alphabet told you exactly where things were supposed to go and nothing ever bled through the page. She was tired. God, she was tired. She had been threatened and followed and disbelieved by her own instincts for months; she had eaten dinner standing up over boxes of dead men's papers; she had a bruise on her hip from the cloister door where the figure had shoved past her, and some nights she still woke at three with the sound of running feet in her ears.

No one would blame her. That was the terrible part. Even Marian, even the fisherman, even Ursella herself — Mrs. Pell, who had chosen thirty years of nobody — even they would understand. Institutions won. They always won. They had won in 1994, they had won in 1996 at the second funeral, they were winning right now, tonight, with a forged file and a cheque and a dean's tired, reasonable voice.

Wendell picked up the envelope.

She weighed it in her hand. It was astonishingly light. All that money, all that escape, weighing less than a single leaf of the 1994 register.

And it was the weight that undid her. Because she thought: a page weighs this much too. A page with a girl's name on it, written in copperplate by a curate's assistant on a Sunday in June, *Ursella Brant*, confirmed before God and the parish of St. Aldric's — and someone had decided that weight was intolerable, and cut it out, and filed the absence where the presence used to be. Thirty years the town had lived on that absence. Thirty years Tobias Marsh had lain under a stone that lied about how he died. Thirty years a woman had answered to a borrowed name because the truth cost more than anyone would pay.

Except Marian had paid. Marian had handed over her husband's last secret with shaking hands. And the fisherman had paid, remembering a woman who watched the water. And Ursella — Ursella had paid every day for thirty years, in the coin of her own name, and was coming home anyway, packing a bag and a gun she'd never fired, because some debts compound until even the frightened come to settle them.

Everyone had paid except the ones who cut the page.

Wendell set the envelope down. She slid the cheque back inside, uncreased, and wrote across the face of the resignation letter in her neatest archival pencil — the hand she used for annotations meant to outlive her — four words:

*Declined. See attached testimony.*

Then she stood, and took the manila folder with the forged incident report, and did not shred it, and did not burn it. She dated it. She annotated it: *Fabricated document received from Canon R. Pryce, eve of anniversary dinner, retained as evidence.* She initialed and dated the annotation, because that was the job, that had always been the job — not to avenge, not to forget, but to write down what happened while she was the one holding the pen. Then she locked it in the deepest drawer of the map case, beneath the flood plans, where no one had reason to look and she had every reason to remember.

The cathedral clock struck eleven. Tomorrow the families would come, and the garlands would smell of roses, and Pryce would lift his glass to a hundred and fifty years, and she would stand up in front of all of it with Marian's pages in her hand and her voice shaking, probably, and none of it guaranteed to work — the file existed now, the machinery of her disgrace was built and oiled and waiting — but it would be *true*, and truth was the only currency she had ever trusted.

At the door of the archive she paused, keys in hand, and looked back at the dark shelves. Somewhere in the stacks the 1994 register slept, with its razored wound and its pencilled facsimile waiting in her workroom to be sewn home. Somewhere two towns over, an old woman was spending her last night as Mrs. Pell.

"Ursella Brant," Wendell said aloud, to the empty stacks, to the whole patient alphabet of the absent. "Your page is ready."

Outside, the fog was coming in off the river, thickening around the mill bridge, the graveyard, the stones. And along the coast road, headlights crawled through it toward town — a car moving slowly, the way people drive when they are returning to a place they swore they'd never see again.
