## 22. The Last Letter

### Ursella

The sea at Pell's End did its two things in the morning, as it always did: it went silver, and then it went gray. Ursella Brant — Mrs. Pell, of the boarding house, of the forty years of borrowed weather — stood at the window with her tea going cold and watched it do both, and thought about letters.

She had written one letter in her life that mattered. The confession, posted through the crypt slot in the dark, her left hand cramping around the pen, the liturgical capitals rising off the page like something she'd been taught to carve into stone rather than paper. *Brother Aldous trained us well,* she'd thought, and then had not thought of him at all, because to think of him was to be nineteen again, standing on the mill bridge with Tobias Marsh's coat still warm in her arms.

Now there was a second letter. A last one. She had been drafting it in her head for three weeks, the way she drafted everything — twice, three times, burning the versions that told too much.

The boarding house was quiet behind her. Two widows and a retired schoolmaster, none of them curious, which was the whole art of the place. She had chosen them the way she chose her curtains: for their indifference to what was behind them.

She sat at the kitchen table — the good table, the one with the grain running like water — and took out the paper she'd bought in town, ordinary paper, and the pen that no longer cramped, because she had written so many letters as Mrs. Pell that the borrowed hand had grown into her like a root into a wall.

*Dear Miss Iversen,* she began, and stopped, because the name was wrong. Wendell. The woman had earned the Christian name, if anything could be earned in that town, which Ursella doubted, but which she was — lately, dangerously — willing to test.

*Dear Wendell.*

She looked at the two words a long time. Then she wrote.

*You will not remember me, and that is the point of me. I was the page that was cut out. You found the scissors' work and you put the page back — a copy, yes, with your own hand underneath it saying so, and I want you to know that the saying-so was the truest thing anyone has done for me in fifty years. An honest scar. My whole life I have been a scar with no story attached.*

She wrote about the white stone. She had not told anyone about the white stone, not even the fisherman, who had seen her lay it and had kept her secret with the decency of men who spend their lives watching water and learning what it takes. *I put it there because the stone knows how to stay. I did not, for a long time, and I am sorry for it, and I am not sorry for it, both at once, which I have decided is what survival is.*

Then she came to the part she had drafted and redrafted, the part that had kept her up two nights running, and she wrote it plainly, because plainly was all she had left:

*Here is what I know that you don't, and it is not much, and it is enough. Every town keeps one more crypt. Not under the church — the church's crypt is the honest one, the one with a slot in the door for exactly this kind of letter. The other crypt is in the town itself. In the founding families' parlors. In the mill that fell down conveniently. In the story everyone agrees on because agreeing is cheaper than digging. You opened ours. You will move on someday, or be moved, and the town will grow a new one, and some other archivist will find a letter in a slot, or a page with a razored edge, or a name that stops in the middle of a register like a sentence that was never finished. When you find it — and you will, because you are the sort who finds them — remember that the person it was cut out of may still be alive. We usually are. That is the whole horror and the whole hope of it.*

*Do not come looking for me. I know you won't. You filed my letter the way you file everything, which is to say you gave it a place and let it be what it was.*

*I am signing my own name. It is the first time in fifty years and my hand is not shaking, which surprises me. Ursella Brant. There. It exists on paper again, in a hand that is not mine, in a town that is not mine, and the world has not ended. The sea did its two things this morning and the kettle whistled and nobody died.*

*Thank you for the alphabet of the living. I did not know there was one until you started reading it aloud.*

*Yours in the borrowed light,*
*Ursella Brant (Mrs. Pell, if you must)*

She read it through twice. She did not burn any version of it. That, too, was new.

She walked it into town herself — four miles along the coast road, her lungs complaining, her good coat buttoned to the chin — and posted it at the pillar box by the chandlery, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the cold red iron, feeling the letter drop like a small stone into deep water.

Then she bought a paper, and fish for supper, and walked home, and the sea did not do anything remarkable at all, which was its own kind of blessing.

### Wendell

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the day the dead-letter tray was sorted, and Wendell Fairweather found it exactly where a letter should be found — in the tray, in the pile, ordinary as bread — and understood immediately that it was not ordinary, the way she had once understood a postmark was not ordinary, because her hands had learned the temperature of important paper. It was warm. Everything in this building was cold except the things that mattered.

She recognized the hand before she read a word. The liturgical capitals, the leftward lean, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath. The confession hand. The hand that had started everything, sitting on her desk like a returned debt.

She locked her office door. Old habit now; it would be a long time before she unlocked it without thinking.

She read it standing up. Then she sat down and read it again, and the second time she let herself feel it, which was not the archivist's way and was, she was beginning to suspect, the only way left worth having.

*Every town keeps one more crypt.*

She looked up at her own office — the shelves she had inherited from Aldous Pettibone, the ledgers in their numbered sequence, the facsimile page in the 1994 register with her note beneath it in her own blunt hand: *This leaf was removed by razor, date unknown, by person unknown. Restored in facsimile by W. Fairweather, archivist. The erasure is part of the record. The record is part of the town. The town is part of the wound.* Canon Pryce had resigned in the spring, quietly, a canon's quiet, and the diocese had sent an interim dean who said *wonderful* too often and touched nothing, which was, for now, a mercy.

She had thought, these last months, that she was finished. That the story had ended the way stories in her line ended: with a filing, with a shelf, with a small brass plaque if the town was feeling generous. She had not expected to be *thanked*. She had certainly not expected to be *warned*, and least of all warned so gently, the way you'd warn a child about ice on a pond you knew she'd walk on anyway.

Because Ursella was right. That was the terrible, quiet truth of it. Wendell had spent a year learning to read the gaps in the record — and now she could not stop seeing them. The 1931 burial ledger with its suspiciously uniform ink. The school's employment rolls, which began in 1952 for no stated reason, as though the school had been founded by adults with no childhoods. The way the mill's collapse was dated in one document and contradicted in a photograph. Gaps, gaps, everywhere, and each one shaped like a person.

She read the letter a third time. *We usually are. That is the whole horror and the whole hope of it.*

Then she did what she had been trained to do, and it was the right thing, and she knew it was the right thing even as she felt it was not the whole thing: she filed it. She gave the letter an accession number — 1994-URS-01, cross-referenced to the confession, to the register, to the grave — and she wrote a finding aid in her small precise script, and she slid it into the box, and she closed the box, and the box went onto the shelf in its proper place in the alphabet of absences, which was, she had decided, no longer a name for a problem but simply the name of the work.

The archive was quiet. The cathedral was quiet. Through her window the afternoon went silver and then gray, like some other sea entirely, and she sat with her hands flat on the desk and felt the silence do what it always did, which was to wait.

*We usually are.*

She thought about the letter in the box. Filed, numbered, safe. And she thought about Ursella Brant, seventy-some years old, walking four miles along a coast road to post her own name back into the world, and then walking home to a house where nobody knew it.

The record was complete. The record was *finished*. And it was wrong — not inaccurate, nothing she could cite — but wrong, the way a grave is wrong when the person in it would rather be having supper.

Wendell took out a sheet of paper. Plain paper. Her pen, right-handed, untrained, a hand good for nothing but saying so.

*Dear Ursella,* she wrote, and her own name for the woman looked strange and right, like the facsimile page, like an honest scar.

*Your letter is filed. 1994-URS-01. I gave it a place, as you predicted, and the place is a good one, and I want you to know that I hated doing it, which I think you also predicted.*

*You are right about the other crypt. I have started a list. It is not an accusation list — I learned that lesson at the dinner, at some cost — it is a list of gaps. Places where the record stops too cleanly. I am calling it the Alphabet of the Living, which is a phrase I am stealing from you and will deny stealing. Every entry is a name that stops mid-sentence. I do not know yet what I will do with the list. I only know that someone should hold it, and that the someone should be a person who knows what a razored page looks like from the inside, and I am not that person, and you are, and so I am writing to ask — not to come home, never that, you have paid for the name you live under and I would not take it from you — but to correspond. To read over my shoulder, from a distance, at the pace of the post. To tell me when a gap is a wound and when it is only wear. You know the difference. Nobody taught me the difference. I suspect nobody can.*

*The sea here does not do two things. It does one thing, which is to be a river with ambitions. But the light on it this evening was the color of your letter's paper, and I thought you should know that the town you left is being read, slowly, by someone who intends to keep reading.*

*The kettle is whistling. Nobody has died. I am told this is what peace feels like. It feels like an unfinished sentence, and I find I do not mind.*

*Yours in the un-borrowed light,*
*Wendell Fairweather, archivist (of the alphabet, both of them)*

She read it twice. She did not file a draft, which violated every rule she had, and she found that the violation sat in her chest like a coal, warm and slightly dangerous, and she decided to keep it.

She posted it herself, walking down through the close in the last of the light, the cathedral towers going violet above her, the pillar box by the lychgate cold under her hand. The letter dropped. Somewhere under her feet, in the dark of the slot's throat, it began its journey to a woman who had spent fifty years being a page that was cut out, and who had, in the end, written herself back in.

Wendell stood a moment with her palm on the iron. Then she turned back toward the archive, where the boxes waited in their patient rows — the whole alphabet of the town, the living and the absent, the scarred and the restored — and she climbed the steps two at a time, because there was a list on her desk with nine names on it, and a tenth she'd only just thought of, and the morning, when it came, would go silver first.

She had letters to answer.
