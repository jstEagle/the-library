## 4. The House That Remembers

The drive up to Dunmore Hall took twenty minutes and cost me a decade of composure.

I'd prepared for it the way I prepare for everything: obsessively. I had a folder of research on my laptop, a recorder in my coat pocket, and a cover story polished to a shine—I was doing an episode on historic Welsh estates, their architecture, their family archives. Idris Vaughan had agreed to the interview with a warmth that unsettled me more than refusal would have. *Come and see us, boy. The house likes visitors. It doesn't get enough.*

*Boy.* He'd said it on the phone like he'd earned the right to it, like he'd been saying it for years and I'd simply missed the intervening decades.

The lane narrowed. Hawthorn hedges pressed in, and then the trees opened like a curtain, and there it was: Dunmore Hall, grey stone under a bruised sky, its windows reflecting nothing so much as their own watching. It was the kind of house that appeared in the background of photographs of other, happier places. It had clearly decided, sometime around the reign of Victoria, that it would rather be looked at than lived in, and had committed to the bit ever since.

I parked beside a Land Rover with mud up to its axles and sat for a moment with my hands on the wheel, doing the breathing exercise my producer Beth had taught me for interviews with hostile subjects. *You're a professional,* she'd have said. *You've interviewed a man who buried his wife under the patio and a woman who forged her own death certificate twice.* But those had been strangers. This was the house my mother had been born in and never once described to me, and I was arriving under a false name and a false premise, and my hands were doing something on the steering wheel that I chose not to examine.

*Emrys Cole,* I'd told the housekeeper on the phone. Cole, my mother's other name, the one she'd shed like a coat. It felt, walking up the gravel, like wearing a disguise made of her.

* * *

Idris Vaughan received me in a room called, with the unselfconscious grandeur of old money, the Long Library, though it was perhaps forty feet and perhaps a third of the books were about shooting things.

He was smaller than I expected. Photographs from the eighties showed a broad-chested man with a jaw like a headland; the man who rose from his wingback chair was stooped and fine-boned, like the same sculpture after weather. But the eyes hadn't weathered at all. They were pale, quick, and they took me in with an efficiency that made me feel inventoried.

"Emrys Cole," he said, shaking my hand. His grip was dry and precise, a handshake like a signature. "You've got the look of the county about you. Pembrokeshire?"

"Cardigan way," I said, which was true, and a lie, and the truest thing I'd said all day.

"Good bones, the families down there." He waved me into the facing chair. "Tea? Mrs. Prydderch makes it strong enough to trot a horse across."

I said yes to the tea because declining felt like it would be entered into a ledger somewhere.

And then, because I was there under pretense, I asked my pretense questions. He answered them beautifully. That was the thing about Idris Vaughan—he was a *good* interview. He gave you architecture and genealogy and the provenance of the Long Library's worst paintings, and he did it with a raconteur's timing, and every so often he'd pause and look into the middle distance and say something like, "Of course, the Hall remembers more than we do," and I'd feel the hair on my arms stir and have to remind myself I was a professional.

"The house remembers," I repeated, pen moving. "That's a lovely line. May I use it?"

"Use whatever you like." He smiled. "It's true, though. Houses keep what people can't carry. That's why the family stayed, you know. Someone has to mind the remembering." He said it lightly, but his eyes went—just for a flicker—to a door at the far end of the library, and something in the room's temperature changed by a degree I couldn't measure but could feel.

"Your family's been here a long time," I said. "The Dunmores and the Vaughans both."

"Intermarried since before anyone thought to write it down." He sipped his tea. "Aneurin Dunmore was the last of the direct line on that side. My partner, once. In business." A pause, expertly measured. "A fine man. A difficult man. He died—" and here the pause again, the small theatrical grief, "—in 1984. Tragic business. Accident at the mortuary in Carmarthen. It rather finished the family's appetite for Carmarthen."

I kept my pen moving and my face doing nothing. Forty years of true crime had taught me that grief has a grain, like wood, and you can tell sanded grief from grief left rough by the person who actually felt it. Idris's grief was sanded to a polish. It had been handled so many times it had lost all splinters.

"Aneurin," I said. "No children?"

"None living." The pale eyes came back to me and rested there a moment too long. "The line scattered. My own family took up the slack, such as it was. I'm the patriarch now, God help us all. You'll meet the heirs at lunch. I'd apologize in advance, but they're mine and I'm fond of them."

* * *

The heirs were served to me with lunch in a dining room long enough to land aircraft in, at a table set for six though we were four.

There was Rhys Vaughan, Idris's son, sixty-ish and built like the old photographs of his father, with the same jaw and none of the same certainty. He talked about farming subsidies with the desperate fluency of a man who had rehearsed every conversation of his life in front of a mirror. His wife, Sian, had the brittle brightness of someone who had married a house instead of a husband and was still waiting for it to be habitable.

And there was Carys—Rhys's sister, I gathered, though she sat slightly apart, as though the table had a draught only she could feel. She was perhaps fifty-five, sharp-faced, dark-haired going grey at the temples, and she was the only person in the room who looked at me with anything other than rehearsed warmth.

"And what do you do, Mr. Cole?" Sian asked, for the second time, having forgotten the first.

"Podcasts," I said. "Audio documentaries. True stories."

"Like that American one," Rhys said, "with the—what was it—the *Serial*?"

"Somewhere in that family, yes."

"How marvelous," said Sian, in the tone of someone finding a spider marvelous.

"True stories," Carys repeated. She was turning her wine glass by its stem, slowly, not drinking. "Which stories?"

I felt the question land somewhere below my ribs. "Whatever's true," I said.

"My uncle died on a table in Carmarthen in 1984," she said, to her wine glass rather than to me. "That's a true story. Nobody's made a podcast about that."

The room went very quiet in the way large rooms can, the silence pooling at the far end like water finding a low spot.

"Carys," Rhys said, with the weariness of long practice.

"It was an accident," Sian said quickly, brightly. "It was all very sad and it was an *accident*, and Mummy said we weren't to—" She stopped herself, but not before the sentence had shown its seams. *Mummy said we weren't to.* Not to what? Discuss it? Remember it? I filed the unfinished sentence away with the care of a man pocketing a key.

"Aneurin's death was investigated thoroughly," Idris said, mild as milk, "and closed. Accidents happen in mortuaries, Mr. Cole. Bodies are heavy, tables are narrow, hearts are frail. The coroner was satisfied. The family was satisfied. It's only Carys who insists on keeping the wound open, because she was a girl then and girls romanticize."

"I was fifteen," Carys said, "and I don't romanticize anything." She looked at me then, directly, and her eyes were her father's pale eyes with something else behind them—something that had been awake a long time in a house where everyone else was asleep or pretending. "What's your podcast called, Mr. Cole?"

"*The Cold File*," I said.

"And what's in your cold file at the moment?"

"Carys," Idris said again, and this time it wasn't mild. It was a word with a hand on it, a hand on a shoulder, and I watched a woman of fifty-five straighten like a girl caught out of bed after hours. The room rearranged itself around his voice. Even the portraits above the sideboard—Vaughans and Dunmores, four centuries of jowls and collars—seemed to lean in.

I should have felt afraid. What I felt instead frightened me more: I felt *at home*. The weight of the house, the weight of the name, the weight of being *counted* at a table set for six with two empty chairs that somehow included me—some starved part of me that had spent forty years eating alone stood up and said *yes, this, more of this*. I understood, with terrible clarity, how families like this kept their secrets. Not with locks. With belonging. You didn't ask questions because asking questions meant you might be asked to leave, and the house made leaving feel like dying.

"I haven't chosen the next file yet," I said. "I'm looking."

"Look somewhere warm," Idris said, and smiled, and the smile was the warmest thing in the room and the coldest. "Wales is full of true stories, Mr. Cole. Most of them are about weather."

* * *

After lunch, Idris walked me out himself, through corridors that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, past staircases and closed doors, past a small door at the end of the east passage that he touched with two fingers as he passed—not a gesture of avoidance, I thought, but of *possession*, the way you'd touch a wallet to check it was still there.

"The archive," he said, gesturing at a room of filing cabinets and document boxes as we passed its open door. "Everything's in there. Deeds, letters, the lot. If you're serious about the house, Mr. Cole, come back. I'll open it for you properly." A pause. "I like a man who's serious about remembering."

"I'm very serious about remembering," I said.

"I thought so." He stopped at the front door and took my hand again, that dry signature of a grip. "You know, you remind me of someone."

"Oh?"

"Aneurin." He said the name easily, watching me. "Around the eyes. It's the county in you, I expect. These families repeat themselves like weather."

I laughed the laugh you laugh when a man hands you something enormous and watches to see if you'll drop it. "I'll take that as a compliment."

"Take it as an observation." He held the door. Gravel, grey light, the watching windows. "Mind how you go, boy. The lane's treacherous after rain. People have come to grief on it before now."

It was a pleasantry. It was a pleasantry the way a hand on the shoulder can be a hand on the shoulder, or a hand holding you under.

I drove back down the lane with my recorder full of architecture and my notebook full of nothing I'd dared write, and it wasn't until I reached the main road that I let myself say it out loud to the empty car, the thing I'd known since the tea was poured, the thing the whole house had been saying in its stone voice since I came up the drive:

*He knows who I am. He's known from the first ring of the telephone.*

And in my coat pocket, under the recorder, my fingers found the corner of the second letter I'd brought with me like a talisman—the one that promised *the second body is still warm*—and I thought about the small door at the end of the east passage, and the archive with its filing cabinets, and the ground behind the Hall that I had, on the walk from the car, noticed was disturbed in one long rectangle, freshly turfed, like a scar someone had tried very recently to make look like lawn.

I pulled over. I turned the car around.

There was one more question I needed to ask the house, and I wanted to ask it before dark.
