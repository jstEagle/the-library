## 6. What the Table Said

The retired mortuary attendant lived in a bungalow in Porthcawl that smelled of coal tar soap and the sea, and he answered the door holding a whippet under one arm like a rolled newspaper.

"You'll be the podcast man," he said.

"I will be the podcast man," Emrys agreed. "Emrys Dunmore."

"I know who you are. You've got the chin. The whole family had that chin." The man looked at it a moment longer than was comfortable, then stepped aside. "I'm Gethin Llewellyn. This is Beryl. She doesn't like strangers, but she doesn't like anyone, so don't take it personally."

Beryl fixed Emrys with the flat, ancient stare of an animal that had outlived several owners and expected to outlive this one too. He took it as a fair warning about the household's general policy on disclosure.

The front room was small and fiercely tidy, dominated by a television tuned to a muted horse-racing channel and a wall of framed photographs—Gethin at various ages, always squinting, always beside some vehicle or animal or other man squinting harder. Emrys counted four funeral directors' vans among them before Gethin caught him looking.

"Forty-one years at the mortuary," Gethin said, easing into an armchair with the sound of a man settling into his own biography. "Cardiff Royal first, then the county place. I've laid out more people than the census has names for. So you can believe me when I tell you I know what a body looks like."

"That's actually why I've come."

"I assumed so. Tea?"

"Please."

Gethin did not get up to make tea. He shouted "MARGED" toward the kitchen, and a woman's voice replied with something in Welsh that had the cadence of *get it yourself*, and Gethin shrugged as if to say the negotiation would continue without them. Emrys found himself liking him enormously, which made what came next worse.

* * *

He had spent three days finding Gethin Llewellyn, which by his standards was leisurely. The 1984 inquest file listed staff only by initials; but Emrys had learned from Chapter Four of his own podcast—the one about the drowned quarry keeper—that Welsh institutions were held together by a lattice of chapel committees, darts leagues, and grudges, and that anyone could be located through any two of the three. A former colleague of Aneurin Dunmore's solicitor remembered "young Llewellyn, the mortuary lad, terrible at cards, wonderful at gossip." From there it took one phone call and a promise not to record anything until invited.

Which was why the recorder sat, switched off, in Emrys's coat pocket, feeling like a stone.

"I want to ask you about the night my great-uncle died," Emrys said. "The eleventh of March, 1984."

Gethin scratched Beryl's ears. The dog tolerated it the way a queen tolerates a footman.

"Aneurin Dunmore," Gethin said. "I remember him coming in. Not many come in on a Friday night, and fewer still with that name attached. The Hall telephoned ahead. Very proper. 'The family requests discretion.' That phrase, word for word. Forty years and I can still hear it."

"Discretion from whom?"

"That's the thing, isn't it." Gethin looked at the racing, where nothing was happening, with gratitude. "You'd think discretion meant the newspapers. But we weren't ever going to ring the papers. Discretion, in that house, meant each other."

Emrys leaned forward. "Mr. Llewellyn—"

"Gethin."

"Gethin. The inquest recorded death by misadventure. Cardiac event during post-mortem examination. The pathologist signed it, the coroner sealed it, and the file stayed shut for forty years. Is there anything about that night that doesn't fit inside that sentence?"

For a long moment the only sound was the horses, running nowhere, and Marged putting something down in the kitchen with unnecessary force.

"There's a thing," Gethin said at last, "that mortuary men don't say out loud, because it sounds mad, and because saying it ends your career and possibly your marriage. We call it the table talking. Not literally. It's just—you spend enough years with the dead, you learn their grammar. How they lie. What they're telling you about their last hour. Bruises have tense. Rigor has an opinion. And Aneurin Dunmore—" he stopped, and started again, quieter. "Aneurin Dunmore didn't die on my table."

Emrys felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it does on a ship that hasn't moved yet.

"Say that again."

"He didn't die on the table. Or rather—he did, but not the way the paper says, and not the man the paper says, if you follow me, and I can see you half-following me, so let me do it properly." Gethin set down the dog, who relocated to the radiator with dignity. "The body came in Friday night. I received it. Signed for it. Big fellow, six-two, heavy through the shoulders. Farm build. I put him in the drawer, second from the top, because the top one sticks. Saturday morning the pathologist comes—Dr. Prichard, lovely man, hands like a concert pianist—and we bring out the body, and I unzip the bag, and I knew before I'd finished unzipping."

"Knew what?"

"That it wasn't the same man." Gethin's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reciting something he'd rehearsed against saying for four decades. "Six-two, yes. Heavy, yes. But the hands were wrong. Aneurin Dunmore had worked his own land till the year before—I'd seen him alive twice, once at my aunt's funeral, once buying seed potatoes, and a mortuary man looks at hands the way a jeweller looks at stones. Those were office hands. Soft. Manicured. And there was a scar, here—" he touched his own left forearm, "—a big old surgical scar, that the admission notes said should be there, and it wasn't. No scar at all. Smooth as a churchgoer's conscience."

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. "What did you do?"

"What does a twenty-four-year-old mortuary attendant do? I said, 'Dr. Prichard, I think there's been a mix-up.' And Prichard went white—not confused-white, frightened-white—and he looked over my shoulder at the door, and he said, 'You received this body last night, Mr. Llewellyn. This is the body you received.' Word for word. Like a line he'd been given. And then the phone rang in the corridor, and he went and answered it, and when he came back he wouldn't look at me, and we did the post-mortem on a stranger, and I signed the register, and forty years later I still sign the register in my sleep sometimes and wake up hating myself."

"The bodies were swapped," Emrys said. "Someone moved Aneurin out and put someone else in."

"Or moved someone else in and put Aneurin somewhere else entirely." Gethin spread his hands. "I've turned it over ten thousand times. Either way, somebody needed a Dunmore dead on paper, and somebody needed whatever was on that table to be nobody in particular. And everybody who might have asked questions stopped asking them within a fortnight. Prichard retired early. My supervisor got promoted to Cardiff. And I got a raise I hadn't earned and a warning I didn't need, delivered very politely by a solicitor with a car worth more than my mother's house."

"Whose solicitor?"

Gethin smiled, and it was an old, tired smile. "Come now, podcast man. You've read the file. Who had the money in 1984? Who had the reach? Whose signature is on every polite letter that ever closed a door in this county?" He let Emrys arrive at it himself, and Emrys arrived, and the name sat between them like a third guest: *Vaughan.*

* * *

Marged brought tea eventually—two cups, both for Gethin, one of which she pushed across to Emrys with the grudging grace of a woman conceding a point—and while they drank, Gethin fetched a biscuit tin from beneath the television, which contained no biscuits but did contain forty years of a careful man's quiet disobedience.

Photographs. A carbon copy of the receiving register, the entry for 9 March 1984 annotated in pencil: *hands wrong — see me — G.L.* A Christmas card from Dr. Prichard, 1991, unsigned, containing a single sentence: *You were right. Forgive my silence. Some tables are set by bigger hands than ours.*

And a visitor log. The mortuary kept one even then—sign-ins for families identifying remains, officials collecting documents. Gethin had kept his own copy, because, he said, "I decided early on that if they were going to make me complicit, I'd at least be complicit with receipts."

Emrys turned the pages with fingers that felt borrowed. March 1984. The twelfth. The thirteenth. Names he recognized from the file—solicitors, a police superintendent, Dr. Prichard three times in one week, which no pathologist needs unless he's checking his own story.

Then, dated the fourteenth of March, 1984, in a hand he had seen every day of his childhood on birthday cards, shopping lists, the margins of his schoolbooks:

*Nesta Dunmore.*

His mother. Nineteen years old. Two days after the swap. Signing herself into the room where her uncle's body should have been and wasn't, and signing out forty minutes later, and never—not once in forty years, not in a lifetime of silences exchanged across dinner tables—saying a word about it to anyone.

Including, Emrys realized with a cold that started in his sternum and radiated outward, to him.

"She was a child," Gethin said gently, watching his face. "Nineteen. I remember her standing in the corridor with her coat buttoned wrong. She asked to see her uncle. I told her visiting hours were past, which was true, and that the family had requested no viewings, which was also true, and she looked at me—" he paused, choosing, "—she looked at me the way people look at a locked door when they already know what's behind it. Then she thanked me. Politely. Dunmores are always polite. It's how you know they're frightened."

Emrys closed the log book. His hands were steady, which surprised him; everything else felt like weather.

"Gethin," he said. "Would you say all of this again? On the record?"

"I'm eighty-three and Beryl's fourteen. Neither of us is saving ourselves for anything." He reached over and tapped the recorder in Emrys's pocket. "But before you press that button, boy, think on this. If Nesta Dunmore saw what was in that drawer in 1984—if she's known all these years what I've known—then ask yourself why she's never said. Ask yourself who she's been protecting all this time. Because in my experience, a woman keeps a secret that size for forty years for one of two reasons. Fear." He drank his tea. "Or love. And in my experience it's usually both, and it's usually the same person."

Emrys drove back along the coast road with the windows down and the sea doing its ancient indifferent business, and thought about his mother burning something in the garden sink, and about the first letter's handwriting, and about how every road in this case, however far he walked it backward, seemed to end at a kitchen table where a woman was keeping a promise.

That night, at his flat, he laid the photograph from the second letter beside Gethin's register copy and understood, finally, that he wasn't investigating a murder anymore.

He was investigating his own family. And the family, he suspected, had been investigating him right back.
