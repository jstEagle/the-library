## 1. Two Envelopes, One Hand

The rain had begun at four o'clock and showed no intention of stopping by vespers. Marguerite Voss listened to it the way she listened to everything in the archive — attentively, cataloguing its variations without quite meaning to. It came first as a whisper against the leaded windows of the north gallery, then thickened into a steady drumming on the slate roof above, until the whole of Saint-Aubin's seemed wrapped in grey wool. Beneath her, in the nave, the evening organist was running scales, each note rising through the stone floors like a bubble through dark water.

She liked this hour best. The tourists were gone; the vergers had finished their rounds; the great doors had been locked with a sound like a full stop at the end of a long sentence. What remained was hers alone: three hundred years of parish registers, donation ledgers, correspondence, deeds, and dust — the sediment of a town that had never learned to throw anything away and had long since stopped looking at what it kept.

Marguerite sat at her desk beneath the green-shaded lamp, a pair of white cotton gloves on her hands, and worked through the morning's acquisitions with the methodical pleasure of a woman setting a room to rights. A bundle of nineteenth-century funeral invitations, tied with black ribbon gone brittle as spun sugar. A box of photographs from the Ferreol estate — she paused over those a moment longer than necessary, noting the family's habit of photographing itself at every christening and harvest fair while somehow never once appearing candid — and set them aside for tomorrow. A letter from the diocesan office reminding her, with ecclesiastical delicacy, that the annual inventory was due before Advent.

And two envelopes.

They lay at the bottom of the tray, face down, and she noticed them only when she lifted the last folder away. Ordinary envelopes, cream-coloured, slightly warped by damp. Both postmarked Saint-Aubin. Both postmarked the same day — the eleventh of October, six days past. No return address on either. Her own name and title written across the front in a hand she did not recognize: *Mademoiselle Marguerite Voss, Archiviste, Cathédrale Saint-Aubin.*

The handwriting troubled her before she could say why. It was not the hand of an uneducated person — there was nothing crabbed or uncertain in it — but neither was it the hand of anyone who wrote often. The letters stood upright and deliberate, like soldiers who had been drilled long ago and had since forgotten why. Each stroke seemed to have been considered separately, and then joined to its neighbours with something like reluctance.

She turned them over. Both sealed with plain adhesive; no wax, no crest. Whoever had sent them wanted nothing known about themselves except what the hand confessed: age, care, and a certain formality that belonged to another generation.

Marguerite had been archivist of Saint-Aubin for eleven years, and assistant for nine before that under old Père Goudard, and in twenty years she had received perhaps a dozen letters addressed to her personally rather than to *the archives* in general. People wrote to ask whether their grandparents' marriage record survived the fire of 1931. People wrote to dispute burial plots. Nobody wrote twice on the same day.

She slit the first envelope with the bone folder she used for everything, the blade worn smooth as a river stone.

Inside was a single sheet, folded once. She unfolded it, angled it beneath the lamp, and read:

*Mademoiselle Voss,*

*I have followed your work from a distance, as one follows a light in a house one dare not enter. I am told you are a woman who believes that everything has its place, and that a thing hidden is not a thing destroyed. I am writing to put something in your keeping, though I do not yet ask you to keep it.*

*Twenty years ago, in the month of November, a girl died in the crypt of your cathedral. Not fell. Died. There is a difference, and the difference was paid for by four families whose names you would know, though you have never seen them together in one document. I know because I was there. I saw what was done, and I helped make it into something else.*

*I am old now, and tired, and the crypt is still locked. You will want to know which girl. You will want to know which families. You will want to know my name. All of this can be yours, if you are the archivist they say you are. Find the witness who never died, Mademoiselle Voss. When you have found him, I will tell you everything.*

*Until then, burn this if you are wise. I confess I hope you are not.*

*— A penitent*

Marguerite read it twice. The second time, she read it aloud, very quietly, because some sentences will not sit properly in the mind until they have passed through the mouth.

*A girl died in the crypt of your cathedral. Not fell. Died.*

The rain filled the silence after her voice, drumming its indifferent applause on the roof.

Her first instinct — and she noted it, the way she noted everything, filing even her own reflexes — was professional irritation. Hoaxes arrived every few years. Usually they concerned treasure buried beneath the altar, or Templar secrets, or the inevitable letter claiming descent from some bishop of the fourteenth century. She had developed a tolerance for them, the way a doctor develops a tolerance for hypochondriacs: wearily, and without contempt, because behind most delusions there was at least a real ache.

But hoaxes did not write like this. Hoaxes were eager; they gave too much too soon, names and dates tumbling out like coins from a torn pocket. This letter withheld. It rationed itself. It knew exactly how much to promise and how much to keep, and that knowledge — that *architectural* sense of a secret, load-bearing walls and all — was not the knowledge of a prankster. It was the knowledge of someone who had lived inside the secret for a long time and knew which walls would fall if you pulled the wrong brick.

*Find the witness who never died.*

She set the page flat on her blotter, weighted its corners with four brass paperweights, and looked at it the way she looked at any document: as an object first, a text second. Cream laid paper, good stock but not expensive. Ink, not ballpoint — a fountain pen, medium nib, slight pressure variation suggesting an older writer whose hand tired toward the ends of lines. The French was correct, almost pedantically so, with one curious affectation: the writer had used the imperfect where the passé composé was natural, as though narrating from inside the memory rather than reporting it. *I saw what was done.* Not *j'ai vu*. *Je voyais.* As if it were a thing he had watched many times, over and over, all these years.

Marguerite took off her gloves, flexed her fingers, and put them back on. Then she picked up the second envelope.

It was heavier. Inside, folded around a small card, was a sheet of the same cream paper in the same upright hand — but the tone had changed entirely, the way a face changes when it thinks it is no longer observed.

*Mademoiselle Voss,*

*So you have opened the first one. Of course you have. I counted on it. An archivist cannot leave a letter unopened any more than a locked door can resist a key it was made for.*

*You are wondering about the first letter now — whether it is true, whether the girl was real, whether the crypt really keeps what I say it keeps. Good. Wondering is the beginning of finding. But let me save you a little time, since I admire economy: go to your own catalogue. Look up the year. Look at what your predecessors chose not to record. The truth has been sitting on your shelves for twenty years, Mademoiselle, filed and numbered and dusted by your own hands. You have touched it. You may even have shelved it.*

*When you understand what I mean, you will know how to reach me. Until then — mind the stairs.*

*— The same hand*

Marguerite sat back. The lamp hummed faintly, the way old lamps do, and somewhere below her feet the organist had stopped his scales and begun, softly, a hymn she half-remembered from funerals.

*Mind the stairs.*

It was such a small phrase. Such an ordinary, domestic, harmless phrase — the sort of thing one said to a child carrying soup, or to a guest leaving after dinner. And yet it landed in her chest with a cold weight entirely disproportionate to its size, because it was not advice. It was a demonstration. It said: *I know things about your daily life. I know which stair creaks. I know where you walk.*

Or — and here her mind, trained by twenty years of documents to distrust the obvious reading, offered the alternative — it meant nothing personal at all. It meant: *the danger is in the building. Watch where you step.*

Both readings were unpleasant. She disliked them both, and she disliked, most of all, how thoroughly they had succeeded in making her feel like a character in someone else's plot.

Marguerite rose and walked the length of the gallery, because she always thought better in motion. Rain streaked the windows in long silver threads. Below, the town of Saint-Aubin was dissolving into lamplight and wet cobblestone — the square, the café awnings dripping, the narrow streets climbing toward the vineyards where the old families' houses stood behind their walls. The Ferreol house. The Delmas house. The Roche-Sabran house. The Aubry house. Four names she had grown up among the way one grows up among mountains: always present, never examined.

Four families, the letter said. Whose names you would know.

She stopped at the window and made herself stand still, hands clasped behind her back, and did what she always did when a document disturbed her: she retreated into procedure. Procedure was her rampart. Procedure was the wall between Marguerite Voss, a woman of forty-one who liked chamomile tea and was afraid of deep water, and Marguerite Voss, Archivist, before whom chaos was obliged to organize itself.

Step one: establish provenance. Two letters, same postmark, same day, same hand. They had come in the ordinary morning delivery with the rest of the post — she remembered Berthe bringing the tray up at nine, shaking rain from her cardigan, complaining about her hip. Berthe had handled the tray. The postman had handled the tray. Anyone at the cathedral had access to the tray.

Step two: verify content. The first letter alleged a death in the crypt, twenty years ago, in November. Twenty years ago Marguerite had been twenty-one, a student in Lyon, home only for Christmas. She remembered nothing of any death — but then, she remembered nothing of most deaths, and that was precisely the point of archives. Memory forgot; paper didn't. If a girl had died in Saint-Aubin in November twenty years ago, there would be a register entry. A burial record. A coroner's report, probably, if the death had been sudden. Death left footprints in paper the way rain left footprints in dust, and no one — *no one* — had ever been able to sweep them away entirely. Not priests, not magistrates, not founding families with names like carved lintels.

Unless, said a small cold voice at the base of her skull, unless the footprints had been swept by someone who knew exactly where they fell. Someone with keys. Someone with access.

Someone, said the voice, like an archivist.

Marguerite turned from the window and went to the catalogue.

The catalogue of Saint-Aubin was her cathedral within the cathedral: forty-two wooden drawers of index cards, each card typed or handwritten in a system she had inherited from Père Goudard and refined until it ran like a Swiss watch. Every document in the collection had a shelf-mark, and every shelf-mark told a story — year, category, provenance, location — so that the whole history of the town lay encoded in strings of characters, legible to her alone. She loved the system the way other people loved a language. It had never once lied to her.

She pulled the drawer for the relevant year — twenty years back — and began to turn cards.

Parish registers: present, complete. Baptisms, marriages, burials, January through December. She found November easily enough. Three burials that month. An infant Delmas, a widower named Toussaint, and — she slowed — a card that read simply: *Vernet, Céleste. See cross-reference.*

There was no cross-reference.

Marguerite stared at the card. It was typed in the standard format, in the standard typeface, on the standard card stock — and yet something about it was wrong, the way a forged painting is wrong: every element correct, the whole dead. She held it to the lamp. The typewriter impression was shallower than Goudard's machine produced. The card was newer than its neighbours; she could see it in the whiteness of the edges, unfaded against the soft ivory of the cards typed decades ago. Someone had inserted this card into the drawer. Recently. Within the last few years, certainly. Perhaps within the last few months.

*Vernet, Céleste. See cross-reference.*

A pointer to nothing. A door painted on a wall.

Her heart had begun to beat in a way she recognized from the few times in her life she had been genuinely frightened — once in a cave in the Ardèche, once during a break-in years ago — a slow, heavy, underwater beating. She made herself breathe. She made herself continue.

She went to the shelf where the actual registers lived, following the shelf-mark, and found the volume for that year. It was there. It was always there; she had dusted it herself in spring. She carried it to her desk, laid it on the felt cradle, and opened it to November with the reverence she brought to all these books, and turned the pages — *the third, the ninth, the fourteenth* — to the week in question.

Pages ninety-one through ninety-four had been razored out.

Not torn. Razored — cut close along the spine with a blade, cleanly, expertly, by someone who understood that a torn page screams and a cut page only whispers. The stubs remained, thin slivers of paper bearing the ghosted imprint of whatever had been written on the removed leaves, mirror-writing pressed through from the surviving pages. She tilted the stubs to the light. Fragments. Half a date. The tail of a name that might have ended in *-net*. A word that might have been *accident* or might have been nothing at all.

Marguerite closed the book very gently, as though it were wounded, and stood with her palms flat upon its cover.

Someone had done this inside her archive. Inside her system. With keys that existed in exactly two places: the ring in the sacristy safe, and the ring on her own belt. The rot, if rot there was, was not outside the walls. It had been inside for years, moving quietly through her catalogue, typing false cards, cutting pages, wearing the shape of order the way a thief wears a uniform.

And the letter-writer had known. *Go to your own catalogue,* he had written. *You have touched it. You may even have shelved it.*

She thought of Berthe then — old Berthe, sixty-eight next spring, thirty years in the archive, who knew the catalogue better than Marguerite herself did, who had served under Goudard, who had served under Goudard's predecessor, who had been here twenty years ago when a girl named Céleste Vernet had supposedly died and supposedly been buried and supposedly left no trace at all.

Berthe, who had brought up the mail tray this morning, shaking rain from her cardigan.

Berthe, who had gone home at five o'clock as always, pausing at the door to look back at Marguerite with an expression she had not been able to read and had not tried to.

Outside, the rain intensified, flinging itself against the glass as if demanding entry. Down in the nave the hymn had ended, and the organist's footsteps echoed away across the crossing, and the great doors boomed shut behind him, and Marguerite Voss stood alone in the green pool of lamplight with two letters and a mutilated book, feeling the ordered world of twenty years tilt slowly beneath her feet.

She did not burn the letters. She was not wise, and the penitent had hoped she wouldn't be.

Instead she took a fresh card, dated it, and began — in her own careful hand, the hand that had never yet lied to a shelf — to write the first entry in a file that did not officially exist:

*Vernet, Céleste. Cause of death: unknown. Burial record: missing. Cross-reference: none.*

Then, after a moment, she added the line that would govern everything that came after, the line she would think of, much later, as the first stone of the whole edifice:

*The archive has been edited. Find out by whom — and how long they have had the key.*

Far below, in the dark of the crypt, behind a door that had not opened in twenty years, something waited to be catalogued.
