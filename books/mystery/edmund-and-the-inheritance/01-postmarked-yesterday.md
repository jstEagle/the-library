## 1. Postmarked Yesterday

The red light on the microphone was the color of a small, patient heart, and Ivo Galloway had spent two years learning to speak to it as though it were the only listener in the world.

"Episode forty-eight," she said. "The last one. Or so I keep promising myself, and so I keep lying."

She shifted in her chair—a secondhand thing with a wobble she'd never fixed because the creak had become part of the show's texture, the way Dev said, like a house settling, like someone thinking—and pulled the index card toward her. She didn't need it. She could have recited the Vane case in her sleep, and some nights, honestly, she did.

"For those of you just joining us: on the fourteenth of June, 1974, Edmund Vane—twenty-four years old, heir to half of everything her family touched—took a rowboat out from the boathouse at Vane House and never came back. The boat came back. The cushion didn't. The lake gave up nothing else, not then, not ever. No body. No note. A coroner ruled accidental drowning, which is a remarkable verdict to reach about a person you have not found."

She let that sit for a moment. Silence was a tool; her mother had taught her that without ever meaning to.

"Three people who might have known something are now dead. Arthur Pell, the groundskeeper, buried eleven days ago at the age of ninety-one—the last living witness. Before him, the family solicitor, Mr. Halloway—no relation, though I checked, God help me, I checked twice. And before him, Edmund's old school friend, who drowned in a bathtub in Torquay in circumstances the local paper described as 'tragic' and I have described, on this program, as 'insufficiently examined.'"

Dev would tell her to cut that last bit. Dev would say it sounded like she was auditioning for trouble. Dev was usually right, which was why she kept him around and why she ignored him roughly forty percent of the time.

"So this is where we end," she said. "Not with an answer—I've stopped pretending answers grow on trees in this county—but with a question, properly sharpened. Somebody out there knows what happened to Edmund Vane. Somebody has always known. And if you're listening—" she smiled, though there was no one in the room to see it, "—I'm not going anywhere."

She reached for the fader, and that was when the letterbox rattled.

It was nine-forty at night. Nobody posted letters at nine-forty at night. The rattle came again, softer, almost apologetic, and Ivo sat very still with her hand hovering over the mixing desk, listening to the house do its ordinary nighttime things—the fridge humming, the pipes ticking, the neighbor's dog conducting its nightly argument with a hedgehog—and telling herself that a late post was a coincidence, that delivery drivers ran strange schedules, that the world was full of innocent explanations and she had simply spent too long looking for guilty ones.

She paused the recording. She went to the door.

The envelope lay on the mat like something washed up by a tide.

Cream-colored. Heavy stock. Her name written across it in a hand so elegant it looked practiced—*Miss Ivo Galloway*—with no address above it and no return address, which meant whoever wrote it had walked up her path in the dark, posted it, and left. Her streetlight buzzed outside. The road was empty.

She stood there longer than she'd admit later, holding it without opening it, the way you hold a live wire you haven't decided whether to touch. Then she carried it back to the studio, set it on the desk under the lamp, and looked at it for a long time.

The postmark read: *Ashcombe, 12th of May.*

Arthur Pell had been buried on the eleventh.

* * *

"You're going to want to sit down," Ivo said into her phone the next morning, "and also possibly stand up, and also possibly lie down, because I genuinely don't know what posture this calls for."

"It's seven a.m., Ivo." Dev's voice was sandpapered with sleep. In the background, a kettle clicked off. "If this is another theory about the solicitor's golf clubs—"

"I got a confession."

Silence. Long enough that she checked the call hadn't dropped.

"A confession," he repeated carefully, "like a *confession* confession. To what?"

"To murder. To *the* murder. Eight pages, Dev. Handwritten. It's Edmund Vane's killer, or it's the most committed crank in the history of cranks, and either way I've read it six times and I need you to come look at it before I lose my mind entirely."

Another silence, but a different quality of silence—the kind that meant he was already pulling on shoes. "Don't touch it again. Don't photograph it with your phone, don't put it near your tea, don't—"

"I put it in a freezer bag."

"...You put it in a *freezer bag.*"

"The good ones. With the little seal." She was already smiling, and she noticed herself smiling, and part of her stood back and observed that smile with a certain clinical unease, because this was the feeling she knew best and trusted least: the hot bright thrill of being *chosen*. Two years of shouting into the dark about Edmund Vane, and the dark had finally shouted back. It felt like winning a prize. It felt, if she was honest, like being seen.

And underneath the thrill, cold as the bottom of a lake: *someone wanted me to have this.*

* * *

Dev arrived twenty minutes later with two coffees and the expression of a man arriving at a scene he'd been dreading in his dreams. He was thirty-four, built like a friendly wardrobe, and possessed of the single most useful talent in podcasting: he could hear a lie through a microphone. He'd heard hers often enough over the past two years.

"Okay." He set the coffees down, didn't pick his up. "Show me."

The envelope sat on the desk between them, sealed in its plastic bag, looking smaller than it had any right to. Dev turned it over twice with one finger, the way a vet might examine something that had recently been alive.

"Postmarked the day after Pell's funeral," he said.

"You noticed that."

"Ivo, everyone's going to notice that. That's the point. Whoever sent this wanted the timeline legible." He frowned. "They're not hiding. They're *corresponding.* There's a difference, and the difference scares me."

He put on gloves—actual cotton gloves, produced from his jacket pocket, because Dev prepared for contingencies the way other people prepared for weather—and slid the pages out onto the desk. Eight sheets, cream to match the envelope, covered in that same beautiful slanted hand. Blue-black ink. No corrections. Whoever wrote it had composed it elsewhere and copied it out clean.

Ivo watched him read. She'd read it six times herself and each time it landed differently, the way a chord changes depending on what note came before it. The first time, she'd read it as evidence. The second, as performance. By the sixth, she'd begun reading it as a letter—which was worse, somehow, because it was unmistakably a letter. Intimate. Almost tender.

*You have told her story faithfully,* it began, *which is more than anyone managed while she was alive to hear it. So I will tell you mine, and you may decide what to do with it, as I have decided what to do with yours.*

*I killed Edmund Vane on the fourteenth of June, 1974. Not with malice. With temper, which is worse, because temper is a thing you carry home with you and feed.*

Then it went on. Details. The argument at the boathouse—though the letter named no cause for it, only that words had been said that could not be unsaid. The boat. The water. The long night after. And then, in a paragraph that had made Ivo's hands go cold on the fifth reading:

*The detective who came asking questions was kinder than I deserved, and cleverer than I hoped, and she chose, for reasons of her own, to believe the story I told her. I have thought about her choice every day for fifty years. I expect you will think about it too, once you understand it.*

"She," Ivo said aloud, though Dev had reached that line himself. He'd gone very still.

"Your mum worked the original case," he said quietly.

"My mum worked every case in this county for thirty years."

"But she worked *this* one." Dev took off his gloves slowly, finger by finger. "And this letter says she chose. Chose to believe a story. That's not a detail, Ivo, that's an accusation wearing a detail's clothes."

Ivo looked at the page. *Kinder than I deserved.* Her mother had died three years ago taking whole rooms of memory with her, locked drawers and unreturned phone calls and a single flat sentence delivered over Sunday lunch—*the Vane girl fell in the lake, love, these things happen*—in a voice Ivo had mistaken, all this time, for boredom.

"Or," she said, and heard how thin her own voice sounded, "it's bait. Someone who knows my mother worked the case, dangling her in front of me to make sure I bite. Everything in this letter is calibrated, Dev. The postmark. The handwriting. The—" she gestured vaguely, "—the *tone*. This isn't a confession, it's a composition."

"Then test it." Dev tapped the page. "What's in here that only the killer would know?"

"That's the problem." Ivo pulled her notebook toward her—she'd filled four pages the night before, cross-referencing against the coroner's report, the newspaper archives, Marge Okafor's old columns. "Most of it matches the public record. Argument, boathouse, staged drowning—all consistent with what we've already reconstructed on air. Which means either our reconstruction was right, or whoever wrote this has been listening to the podcast and is feeding us back our own theories dressed up as revelation."

"And the rest?"

Ivo hesitated. There was one paragraph she hadn't shown anyone, hadn't even fully admitted to herself, because it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"There's a description," she said. "Of Edmund. On the water. What she was wearing, how she looked when—" She stopped. Started again. "It's not in any record. It's not in the papers, not in the inquest, not in anything Marge ever printed. If it's true, it could only come from someone who was there."

Dev was quiet for a moment. Outside, a delivery van went past, absurdly ordinary, and Ivo flinched at the sound of it before she could stop herself.

"Okay," Dev said at last. "Here's what happens now. We go to the police. Properly, today, with the letter in a bag and both of us looking respectable. Detective Sergeant Cole—he seemed straight, when you met him. Let him earn his salary."

"He'll take the letter and tell me to stop making episodes about it."

"He might. And you'll hate it, and you'll do it anyway, because whoever wrote this knows where you live, Ivo. They stood on your step in the dark. That's not a fan letter. That's a *doorbell.*"

She wanted to argue. The words were right there, lined up and eager—that Cole had dismissed her before, that the police had fifty years of practice not caring about Edmund Vane, that the letter was hers, addressed to her, *chosen* for her. But Dev was watching her with that expression, the one that meant he could hear the lie forming in her throat before she'd finished building it, and so instead she said:

"One episode."

"No."

"Not about the letter. I won't mention the letter. But I can't just—sit on this, Dev. Forty-eight episodes, and now I'm supposed to go quiet exactly when it gets real? Whoever sent this is *listening.* If I go silent, they'll know they've got to me. If I keep talking, normal, weekly, boring old me—" she spread her hands, "—then I keep them calm. I keep them talking. Letters want replies. Maybe the next one tells us something they didn't mean to."

Dev looked at her for a long moment. She watched him weigh it—watched him arrive, visibly, reluctantly, at the same place she'd already gotten to at three in the morning, staring at her ceiling.

"You've already planned the episode," he said.

"I've already *outlined* the episode."

"That's worse. Outlining is premeditation." He picked up his coffee at last, drank, grimaced at the temperature, drank again. "Fine. One episode. Nothing about the letter, nothing about your mum, nothing that tips our hand. And Ivo—" he waited until she looked at him properly, "—if another one comes, we go to Cole together. Deal?"

"Deal."

He gathered his coat, paused at the door. "For what it's worth," he said, not quite turning around, "whoever wrote that thing called your mother *kinder than they deserved.* People don't write that about cops unless the cop mattered. Your Ruthie mattered to somebody, and I don't think it was the good way round."

He left. Ivo sat alone in the studio with the letter in its freezer bag and the red light of the microphone glowing its patient heart-glow, and she found herself reading the first line again.

*You have told her story faithfully.*

Faithfully. As though faithfulness were the currency here. As though she'd been paid in advance.

She thought about Arthur Pell, ninety-one years old and eleven days dead, and about the three witnesses before him, eighteen months of funerals arranged in a neat descending line, visible only from above—only from the height where patterns lived. She thought about a cream envelope posted at night by someone who knew her name, her mother's case, and the exact day the last witness went into the ground.

Someone wanted her to have this. Someone had been waiting for her to have this.

And the worst part—the part she'd never say on air, the part that hummed under her ribs like a struck tuning fork—was how much she wanted the next letter to come.

She pressed record.

"Episode forty-nine," she said, to the small red heart, to whoever was listening. "A short one today. Just some housekeeping. Some thoughts on endings, and how they're rarely endings at all..."

Outside, the streetlight buzzed on in the grey afternoon, lighting an empty path where, sooner or later, she knew, another envelope would land.
