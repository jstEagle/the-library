## 12. Tea with Mr. Marsh

The invitation came on cream cardstock, which should have been my first clue. Nobody sends cream cardstock unless they want you to know they own a pen.

*Mr. Edwin Marsh requests the pleasure of Ivo Galloway's company for tea, Saturday at three, Thornfield House.*

No mention of the podcast. No mention of Edmund Vane. Just *pleasure* and *company*, as if we were old friends arranging to discuss hydrangeas. Dev read it over my shoulder twice and then said what we were both thinking.

"He knows."

"Of course he knows."

"Then why invite you into his house?"

"Because," I said, turning the card over as if the answer might be printed on the back, "he wants me to see it."

Dev wanted to come with me. I told him no, and we had the argument we'd been having in installments all week—the short version being that I was walking into the lion's den and the long version being that Dev thought I *liked* walking into lion's dens, which was unfair, because I didn't like it, I just couldn't stop doing it. There's a difference. It's an important difference. It is also, I'll admit, entirely invisible from the outside.

So on Saturday afternoon I drove out to Thornfield House alone, with a recorder hidden in my handbag and a head full of everything Marge Okafor had told me about Casper Vane's eyes.

* * *

Thornfield House sat on the good side of the river, all honeyed stone and climbing roses, the kind of house that made you understand why people stayed in small towns their whole lives. A woman in her sixties opened the door before I knocked—Mrs. Adeyemi, I'd learn, housekeeper for thirty years—and led me through a hall of framed photographs: ribbon-cuttings, charity galas, Edwin Marsh shaking hands with three different mayors and one bishop.

He was waiting in the garden room, a glass conservatory full of ferns and winter light, and when he rose to greet me I understood immediately why the town loved him. He was seventy-something and carried it like a well-cut coat—silver hair, easy smile, the particular warmth of a man who has spent fifty years being thanked.

"Miss Galloway." His handshake was dry and firm. "You're shorter than you sound."

"People say that."

"I imagine they do." He gestured me into a wicker chair across from his own. "Tea? Mrs. Adeyemi makes it properly. None of this bag-in-a-cup nonsense."

"Properly, please."

While he poured—actually poured, steady-handed, no spills—I took inventory. The room was beautiful in the way rooms are beautiful when someone with money hires someone with taste. But my eyes kept snagging on details that didn't belong to Edwin Marsh's biography. On the side table, half-hidden by a fern, a photograph of two young people on a dock, laughing. A woman with dark hair and a man whose face was turned away. And on the mantel, of all things, a small brass compass, polished bright, sitting where a clock might go.

"You have questions," he said pleasantly, handing me my cup. "I can see them queuing up behind your eyes like passengers at a bus stop. Ask the first one. It's rude to make them wait."

I decided to start polite. "How did you know about the podcast?"

"The whole town knows about the podcast. Mrs. Adeyemi listens in the kitchen. She thinks you have a lovely voice." He smiled. "She also thinks you ask impertinent questions, but she says that about everyone under sixty."

"That's not what I meant."

"No," he agreed, settling back with his own cup. "It isn't. You meant: how did *I* know, specifically, and why did I invite you here, and am I the man you think I am?" He crossed his legs. "Ask the second question first. It's more interesting."

"Why did you invite me?"

"Because you've been asking about me." He said it without a flicker of defensiveness, almost fondly. "The historical society, the bank records, poor Gerald Whitcombe at the trust—he telephoned me, you know, terribly apologetic. And last week you requested the Vane family photographs from the county archive. The archivist is my golf partner." He sipped his tea. "I thought it was time we met. It seemed discourteous to let you keep working so hard on my behalf without so much as a thank-you note."

"And what exactly are you thanking me for?"

"For taking Edmund seriously." Something moved behind his face—not quite pain, something older than pain, something that had been pain once and had since been polished smooth, like the brass compass. "Fifty years, Miss Galloway, this town has called her death an accident, and meant by it *let it lie*. You didn't let it lie. Whatever else happens, I'm grateful for that."

My mouth had gone dry. I drank tea to cover it and burned my tongue, which served me right.

"Tell me about your connection to the Vanes," I said.

"Ah." He set down his cup. "Straight to it. Very well. I was the Vane family's ward, in a manner of speaking. My mother was housekeeper at Vane House—before your time, before nearly everyone's time. When she died, old Ambrose Vane took me in. I ate at their table. I did my homework in their library. Edmund tutored me in Latin because she was bored and I was available." He laughed softly. "She was a terrifying teacher. She'd rap your knuckles with a ruler if you declined a noun wrong. I declined a lot of nouns wrong, on purpose, some weeks. It was worth it to watch her get exasperated."

"You loved her."

"I did." No hesitation. "Everyone loved Edmund. That was the trouble with her, if you want the truth. Loving Edmund was easy. Being loved *by* Edmund—that required references and a credit check." He looked out at the garden. "Casper and I understood each other better. Two boys in the shadow of a brilliant girl. We were friends."

"Were."

"Were." He let the word sit there between us like a stone dropped in still water. Then, gently: "You've seen the 1976 photograph. From solicitor Halloran's funeral."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. He watched me not answer it with evident enjoyment, a man watching a dog work out a puzzle box.

"The archivist mentioned you asked after it," he offered helpfully.

"Casper Vane died in 1971," I said. "Drowned off the coast of Portugal. There's a death certificate."

"There is indeed. I've read it. Very official-looking." He refilled my cup unasked. "And yet the man in that photograph had Casper's face. What do you suppose explains that?"

"I was hoping you'd tell me."

"Was you?" He smiled again, and this time the smile did something I wasn't prepared for—it changed his whole face, rearranged it around the cheekbones and the pale gray eyes, and suddenly I wasn't looking at Edwin Marsh, philanthropist. I was looking at the boy in the 1976 photograph. At the young man in the dock photo, face turned away but shoulders familiar. At the grainy school portrait Marge had described to me over the phone, her voice going strange: *he had eyes like winter water, that boy, you never forgot them.*

Marge hadn't exaggerated. I set down my cup because my hand wasn't steady enough to hold it.

"You knew," I said. "When you invited me. You knew I'd figured it out or you knew I'd figure it out sitting right here, and either way—" I stopped, started again. "Why?"

"Because I'm tired, Miss Galloway." For a moment the charm dropped away entirely, and underneath it was something vast and quiet and sad, like a lake in February. "Do you know what fifty years of being someone else does to a person? Edwin Marsh is a very good man. He endows the library wing. He remembers birthdays. He visits the sick. I invented him in 1972 and he has been so much better than I ever was that some days I forget which of us is real." He turned the brass compass over in his fingers—it had been within reach the whole time, I realized; everything in this room was within reach, arranged, curated. "But you dug him up. You and your microphone. So I thought: let her look at me. Let her see what fifty years costs. And then let her decide what she thinks justice looks like."

"This isn't a confession."

"Of course not." The charm snapped back into place, seamless. "This is tea."

* * *

We talked for another hour, and I want to be honest in these pages, because honesty is the only currency I've ever had: I liked him. That's the thing nobody warns you about. Monsters in the podcasts are voices and case numbers, abstractions you can hate safely. In person, Edwin Marsh was funny. He told a story about the bishop and a beekeeping incident that made me laugh before I could stop myself. He remembered Edmund with a specificity no archive could match—how she hummed when she rowed, badly, tunelessly; how she kept a list of everyone who owed her money, including family, *especially* family.

"She kept accounts," he said, and there was that flicker again, quick as a fish under ice. "Everything was a debt with Edmund. Every kindness was entered in a ledger somewhere, awaiting repayment. She'd have made a magnificent banker. Instead she inherited one." He gazed at the ferns. "I often wonder what would have happened if she'd been born plain. If nobody had ever found her fascinating. Cruelty needs an audience, you know. Take away the audience and most cruel people just become... tired."

"Are you saying she was cruel?"

"I'm saying she was human, and the record has made her a saint, and saints are very hard to live with." He met my eyes. "You'd know. Your mother's been a saint in this town for twenty years. How has that been, living with her?"

The temperature of the room changed. I felt it on my skin before I understood it in my head.

"You knew my mother."

"Everyone knew Ruth Galloway." He said it lightly, too lightly. "Fine officer. Terrible bridge player. She cheated, you know. Blatantly. I never had the heart to call her on it." He smiled at some private memory. "She'd have made a magnificent detective even if she hadn't been one. She had the gift of knowing when a person was lying and loving them anyway. That's rarer than you'd think. Most people can only manage one or the other."

"What did she know about Edmund Vane?"

"Ah." He stood, unhurried, and went to the window. Outside, a blackbird was bullying the bird feeder with tremendous commitment. "There it is. The real question. You've been circling it since you sat down." He turned back to me. "I'll tell you what I told your mother, the last time we spoke. Some debts aren't owed to the living. And some payments, once made, can't be unmade—you can only decide who carries them next."

"Is that a threat?"

"Goodness, no." He sounded genuinely surprised, and genuinely pleased. "It's arithmetic. You've read the letters—I know you have, don't look so fierce, I posted them, who else would have? You know things now that Arthur Pell took to his grave and that your mother chose to leave there. The question isn't whether you'll publish. Of course you'll publish; you're your mother's daughter, and duty runs in families like eye color. The question is what you'll cost, and who pays it." He came back toward his chair but didn't sit, standing over me with the light behind him. "Your mother paid for forty years, quietly, without complaint. I've always admired that about her. The Galloways pay their debts."

"I haven't decided you killed anyone."

"No," he agreed warmly. "You've decided I *might* have, and you're finding it far more interesting than certainty would be. Certainty ends stories. Possibility feeds them." He glanced at the recorder bulge in my handbag—he'd known about it since I walked in, obviously, had probably counted the minutes of tape—and his expression softened into something almost paternal. "Take the recording, Miss Galloway. Play it for your producer, your detective, whoever you like. There's nothing on it. A charming old man talks about Latin lessons and bridge. Meanwhile, ask yourself the question that actually matters."

"Which is?"

"If Edmund's body were ever found—if someone finally looked in the right place, after all these years—" he paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping a gift he'd been saving, "—what do you suppose your mother would have wanted you to do? And what do *you* want to do? Because I suspect those are two different answers, and I suspect you already know it."

Mrs. Adeyemi appeared in the doorway to say my taxi was waiting, though I hadn't called one, which told me exactly how long our conversation had been scheduled to last.

At the door, he pressed something into my hand. The brass compass. Warm from his pocket.

"A keepsake," he said. "Edmund gave it to Casper when they were children. *So you can find your way home,* she said. Fifty years, and I've never once been lost." His gray eyes held mine, bright with something that was not quite amusement and not quite grief. "Do give my regards to Dev. And to Detective Cole. And when you write your finale, Miss Galloway—make her human. Not the saint. Not the victim. The girl with the ledger and the terrible singing voice. She'd have hated being a saint. She'd have charged for it."

The door closed behind me with a click soft as a lock turning.

I stood on the gravel drive in the cold, holding a dead girl's compass, and it wasn't until I was halfway home that I understood what he'd done. He hadn't confessed. He'd handed me a map and dared me to follow it—*if someone finally looked in the right place.* He wanted me digging. He'd been feeding me clues for months like a man scattering breadcrumbs, and now, at last, openly, delightedly, he'd pointed me at the ground beneath his feet.

That night I spread every document I had across the kitchen table and put the compass in the middle of them, and Dev, arriving with takeaway and apologies, looked at it for a long moment and said, very quietly:

"Ivo. Where do you hide a body for fifty years and keep visiting it?"

I looked at the compass needle, swinging lazily, pointing nowhere useful.

"Somewhere you own," I said. "Somewhere nobody else would ever dare to dig."

Dev and I looked at each other, and I watched him arrive at it a half-second before I said it aloud, both of us thinking the same word, the same place, the same newly varnished wood in a boathouse that should have rotted decades ago.

Vane House.
