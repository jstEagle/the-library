## 22. The Last Episode

The studio was not really a studio. It was the spare room above Dev's garage, which he had spent three weeks and an unreasonable amount of money soundproofing with egg cartons and acoustic foam, and which still smelled faintly of paint and of the curry he'd ordered the night we finished episode nineteen. But it had a window that looked out on his garden, where a row of late crocuses had come up through the frost like small purple arguments against winter, and it had Dev in it, sitting close enough that our chairs touched when either of us leaned.

That was new. That was four months new. I had stopped pretending I didn't notice.

"Levels," he said, tapping the board. "Whenever you're ready."

I looked at the microphone the way you look at an old dog who has followed you into trouble more times than you can count. Two years of my life lived inside this machine's patience. The Vane case had begun here — or near enough, in a rented studio with bad coffee — and it ended here, in a room that smelled of Dev's paint choices, with a folder on the desk containing exactly one piece of paper.

"Take your time," Dev said, which was what he always said, and which meant: *I'm here, and I'm not going anywhere, and I know what this costs you.*

I pressed record myself. He let me. He understood that after everything, I needed to be the one to start it.

"This is *Edmund and the Inheritance*," I said. "Episode twenty-three. The last one. My name is Ivo Galloway, and if you've listened this far, then you already know how the story ends for everyone in it except me. So tonight is about the ending nobody got to hear. Tonight is about Edmund."

Dev slid his hand across the gap between our chairs and rested it over mine on the desk. Neither of us said anything about it. The red light burned on, steady as a heartbeat.

* * *

I want to tell you first about the well, because everything else comes out of it.

They opened it on a Tuesday in March, eleven weeks after Edwin Marsh — Casper Vane, I still have to correct myself some mornings — was taken away in the back of Detective Sergeant Cole's car. The boathouse floor came up in sections, and beneath it the old well shaft, dry these fifty years, capped with planks and covered in sacking so neat and deliberate that the workmen stood around it for a long moment before anyone would touch it.

She was there. Of course she was there. Fifty feet down, wrapped in the sailcloth Casper had used because it was what the boathouse offered him, because he was twenty-two and panicking and had perhaps ten minutes to decide how a person disappears forever.

I wasn't there when they brought her up. Cole called me beforehand and asked, gently, whether I wanted to be, and I said no, and then I sat in my kitchen for six hours staring at a cold cup of tea until Dev let himself in with his own key and made me a fresh one without asking. That is love, by the way. Not the grand gesture. The second cup of tea, unasked for, at nine o'clock at night.

But I was there at the reburial, in the churchyard at Ashcombe, on a bright cold morning in April, when they laid Edmund Vane next to her parents under a stone that finally said her name and her dates and nothing else, because what else is there to say? Half the town came. Some of them came because they loved her memory. Some came because they had honored her brother for fifty years and needed somewhere to put their shame. Marge Okafor came in a wheelchair, ninety-one years old and furious at the wheelchair, wearing the same green hat she'd worn to every Vane family event since 1968. She held my hand during the prayers and squeezed it once, hard, at the exact moment the vicar said *resurrection*, and neither of us commented on her timing.

Casper Marsh did not attend. He will not attend anything again. He pleaded guilty — quietly, almost gratefully, Cole told me, like a man setting down luggage he'd carried too far — and he will die in a room smaller than the boathouse he kept beautiful. I have not been to see him. I thought about it for a long time. In the end I wrote him a letter instead, telling him only that Edmund had been found, and given a stone with her name on it. I don't know if he'll write back. I found I didn't need him to. There is a kind of finishing that doesn't require the other person's participation.

The trust dissolved itself within the month — lawyers move fast when the alternative is newspaper photographers — and its remainder became the Edmund Vane Scholarship, awarded each year to a girl from the Ashcombe comprehensive going on to study science. Edmund had wanted to be a botanist. Almost nobody knew that. It was in her diary, the fragments we recovered, between observations about the estate's orchards and increasingly sharp notes about her brother's arithmetic. She'd underlined it twice. *Kew,* she wrote. *Someday Kew.* Someday, now, a girl from Ashcombe every year, carrying a dead woman's ambition forward like a lit candle passed hand to hand.

And the tape — my mother's tape — stayed sealed.

I want to talk about that, because people asked. People are still asking. A national paper offered me what Dev described as "a genuinely upsetting amount of money" for the recording of Ruth Galloway's interview with a terrified young Casper Vane on the night after his sister vanished. The historical society wanted it for their archive. Two documentary producers wanted it for their films.

Here is what the tape actually contains, for those keeping score: my mother, thirty-four years old, sitting across from a boy who has just destroyed his own life, and choosing — deliberately, with her eyes open, in violation of her oath and possibly several statutes — to believe that Arthur Pell's confession could stay buried long enough for Arthur's family to be safe. Because Arthur Pell had seen something on the water that night, and because Casper had spent two terrified days convincing himself Arthur would talk, and because Ruth understood — as only someone raised in a town like Ashcombe can understand — that a frightened man with money and no future is at his most dangerous precisely when cornered. She buried the confession to starve the fire. It worked for fifty years. It cost her everything else.

My mother was not corrupt. She was not even, I think, wrong. She was a woman standing in a burning house deciding which door to carry which child through, and she chose, and she lived with the smoke in her lungs for the rest of her life, and she died without ever telling her daughter why the case file in her locked drawer weighed so much.

I aired none of it. I told the story of what she did — episode twenty, if you were listening; you could hear my voice shake — but the tape itself stays in a safe-deposit box, and when I die it goes to the Ashcombe historical society under seal for seventy-five years, by which time everyone in it will be a name in a ledger and no one will be a wound. Context over cruelty. That was the phrase I used on air, and I stand by it, though I'll confess the cruelty part tempted me more than once. Grief makes you generous and vengeful in the same hour, sometimes in the same breath.

Marge, when I told her my decision, nodded slowly and said, "Your mother warned me off a story in 1975 and I resented her for forty years. Last month I finally forgave her. You got there faster than I did." Then she ate an entire scone and complained about the butter, and I understood that I had been dismissed with honors.

* * *

Which brings us to the letter.

You know by now that the well gave up more than remains. When the conservators lifted the last section of sailcloth, wedged into the shaft wall behind a loose stone at the twelve-foot mark, they found a tin — a tobacco tin, rusted shut, the kind that once held pipe tobacco and in 1974 held, instead, a sheet of paper folded eight times and wrapped in waxed cloth. Casper never knew it existed. I am certain of that. If he had known, he would have gone down that shaft himself rather than leave it. The police confirmed the handwriting against Edmund's diary within a week. It was hers. It had been hers since sometime before the eleventh of June, 1974.

Her family received copies. I received one too — Cole brought it to me personally, which I suspect bent at least three regulations, and which I have decided not to ask about. "You're the reason she has a stone," he said, putting it on my kitchen table. "Seems right someone should read it aloud."

So tonight, for the first time anywhere, I am going to read it to you. I transcribed it exactly — her spelling, her punctuation, her crossings-out. She was twenty-four years old. She wrote it in the spring of 1974, weeks before she died, and she hid it in the one place on earth she believed no one would ever look, and she addressed it, in her clear schoolgirl hand, *to whoever loved me enough to dig.*

Dev turned off the overhead light so only the lamp glowed, warm and yellow, and I unfolded my copy, though I no longer needed to. I know it by heart. I expect I always will.

* * *

"To whoever loved me enough to dig —

"If you are reading this then I am dead, and I am sorry, because I had plans. Kew Gardens. A flat in London with a bath that works. A dog, probably, something scruffy. I had plans the way other people have debts, constantly and with interest accruing.

"I am writing this because I believe my brother is stealing from me, and because I have decided to say so to his face tomorrow, and because I am not a fool. If I am wrong, I will burn this and laugh at myself. If I am right — if Casper has been bleeding the trust the way I think he has — then tomorrow will be an ugly day, and ugly days in families have a way of becoming permanent.

"So: instructions. Yes, instructions, even from beyond, because I am my mother's daughter and we do not go quietly.

"Whoever you are, you loved me. Good. I hope I deserved it. Do the following:

"One. Do not let them make me boring. They will try. This family buries its difficult women under adjectives — *troubled, headstrong, delicate.* I was none of those things. I was angry, and I was right, and I was going to fix it.

"Two. The orchard apples make better cider than Cook admits. She waters it. Tell someone.

"Three. Forgive Casper or don't, but understand him first. He has been the spare his whole life, the shadow of a girl who was better at everything, and shadows get hungry. That does not excuse him. Nothing excuses him. But hunger explains a great deal, and explanation is not the enemy of justice, whatever the magistrate says.

"Four. Live loudly. Whoever you are, you went digging for a dead woman, which means you are my sort of person, which means you are probably stubborn and inconvenient and much too curious for your own good. Stay that way. Curiosity is how the truth gets found, and truth is the only inheritance worth having. Everything else — the house, the money, the name — is just furniture in a room you can't take with you.

"If it was Casper — and I am afraid, writing this in daylight with the birds going, that it was — then tell him I forgive him for the theft. The rest depends on what he does next.

"Well. The light's going. Cook will ring the bell soon and I shall go in and say nothing at dinner, as usual, and watch him not meet my eyes, and tomorrow I will ruin us both, and perhaps that is the only honest thing left to do in this beautiful rotten house.

"Love, always, to whoever you turned out to be —

"Edmund Vane. Ashcombe. May 1974."

* * *

The microphone held the silence afterward the way a cupped hand holds water.

I hadn't realized I was crying until Dev handed me his handkerchief — an actual handkerchief, monogrammed, because Dev is Dev — without looking away from the mixing board, giving me the privacy of his profile. I blew my nose with tremendous dignity and pressed record again.

"Fifty years," I said. "She waited fifty years in the dark for someone stubborn enough to dig. And the thing that undoes me — the thing I keep coming back to at three in the morning — is instruction number four. *Live loudly.* She wrote that to a stranger. To whoever loved her enough to look. She couldn't have known it would be a podcaster with a cheap microphone and her mother's old case files. She just trusted that somebody, someday, would be her sort of person."

I looked at Dev. He was looking at me. The red light burned on.

"I spent two years being chosen by a killer," I said. "Letters on cream paper, clues fed to me like treats to a clever animal. It took me far too long to understand that I'd been chosen twice. Once by the man who wanted an audience. And once, fifty years earlier, by a girl who wanted a witness. Only one of them was worth answering."

I set Edmund's letter down on the desk, face-up, between the microphone and the window where the crocuses were doing their small purple work.

"So here's how it ends. Casper Vane will die in prison, and the town will argue about him for a generation, and eventually it will stop arguing and start forgetting, because that's what towns do. The scholarship will send its first girl to university in September — a chemist, Cole tells me, top of her class, ferociously stubborn. Edmund would have liked her enormously. The boathouse is coming down next month; the county's turning the shoreline into a nature reserve, which means that bit of water will belong, at last, to ducks and herons and children with nets, and to nobody's grief.

"And me? I'm going to live loudly. That's not a resolution, it's an inheritance. She left it to me in writing.

"My mother used to say that every case closes twice — once in the file, and once in the heart, and the second closing takes longer. Hers closed the night she buried that tape. Mine closed this morning, actually, at about seven-fifteen, when Dev brought me tea in bed and I realized I'd slept through the night for the first time in two years without dreaming about water.

"For everyone who listened, who wrote, who sent me their own unsolved things to carry for a while — thank you. Carry them carefully. Hand them on when you can.

"This is Ivo Galloway, signing off from *Edmund and the Inheritance*. Edmund Vane, 1950 to 1974. Botanist. Sister. Troublemaker. Right.

"Goodnight, everybody. Dig deep. Live loudly."

I reached over and pressed stop myself. The red light died. In the sudden quiet, the ordinary sounds of the world came back — a blackbird somewhere in Dev's hedge, the kettle beginning its low complaint in the kitchen, traffic on the ring road, all the vast indifferent machinery of a Tuesday evening going on without us.

Dev pulled off his headphones and looked at me for a long moment.

"So," he said. "What does a woman do the day after she finishes the only thing she's thought about for two years?"

I considered it. Outside, the light was going gold and long across the garden, the way it does in spring when winter finally concedes.

"She starts researching," I said.

He groaned. "Ivo—"

"Relax. Not a true-crime series." I pulled a folded page from my bag and smoothed it on the desk between us — notes, actual notes, in my handwriting, the first fresh ones in months. "Marge has been dropping hints for weeks that there's a story from 1962 she's 'never told anyone, dear, and won't, unless properly recorded.' She says it while looking directly at me. She's baiting me. She's been baiting me since the funeral."

"That's not a story, that's an ambush by a ninety-one-year-old in a green hat."

"Yes," I said. "It's going to be wonderful."

Dev laughed — that real one, the one that crinkles his whole face — and reached over and switched the microphone back on, just the two of us now, no red light, no audience, no case. Just a table, a lamp, a folded letter from a girl who wanted the truth found, and the two of us on the same side of it at last.

"Levels," he said softly. "Whenever you're ready."

I leaned toward the mic, and I was.
