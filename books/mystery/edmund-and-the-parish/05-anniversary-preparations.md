## 5. Anniversary Preparations

The invitation arrived on a Thursday, which Beatrix would later consider fitting, because Thursdays were the day the parish of Wrenlow had always set aside for remembering what it preferred not to speak about aloud.

It came by hand, not post. Beatrix found the envelope wedged against the door of their rented cottage on Fen Lane, weighted beneath a smooth grey stone that had been placed there deliberately—someone had stood on the step, arranged the stone just so, and walked away without knocking. The envelope was heavy cream stock, thick as linen, and pressed into its wax seal was the Vasse crest: a heron standing in shallow water, one leg lifted, head bowed as though listening for something beneath the surface.

Beatrix stood in the doorway a long moment before touching it. The marsh light came off the water in flat silver sheets, and somewhere behind the reed beds a curlew was calling its thin, rising note, over and over, like a question no one intended to answer.

They knew the crest before they knew anything else. That was the trouble with a memory like theirs—recognition arrived whole, complete, unbidden, dragging its entire history behind it like a net through deep water. The heron. The water. The particular weight of good paper. They were eleven years old again, standing in the long gallery at Vasse Hall while men in dark coats catalogued a dead boy's life into numbered lots.

Beatrix carried the envelope inside and set it on the kitchen table, and did not open it for forty minutes, because they made tea first, and washed the two cups already in the sink, and stood at the window watching Mrs. Petchy next door beat a rug against her fence with the rhythmic violence of a woman who had lived through four decades of Wrenlow winters and intended to be heard about it.

Then they opened it.

*Mr., Ms., or Mx. Delacroix* — the printer had hedged beautifully — *you are cordially invited to attend the Annual Dinner commemorating the legacy of the Vasse family, to be held at Vasse Hall on the eleventh of November, being the anniversary of the passing of Edmund James Vasse, beloved son of this parish.*

Below that, in smaller type: *As a named beneficiary of the estate settlement of 20—, your attendance is both requested and treasured.*

*Requested and treasured.* Beatrix read the phrase three times. It had the flavor of language drafted by committee—the vicar's softness laid over Sylvie's signature bluntness, or perhaps the other way around. Eleven years of these dinners, and Beatrix had never once attended. The invitations had come every year all the same, arriving with the regularity of the tide tables, and every year Beatrix had let them sit on the table until the date passed and the paper yellowed slightly at the edges, a small monument to a refusal no one ever acknowledged.

This year the envelope felt different. This year there was a dead man in the morgue wearing Edmund Vasse's clothes, and everyone in Wrenlow knew it, and still the dinner would go ahead, because in Wrenlow the dinner always went ahead. Grief here was not a weather; it was an institution. It had trustees. It had a budget line. It had a seating chart.

* * *

By Friday morning the whole parish had begun its annual transformation, and Beatrix watched it happen the way one watches a tide come in—inevitable, patient, erasing things.

At Hartley's shop on the square, the window display changed overnight. Out went the tinned goods and the coil of rope; in came white candles in glass hurricanes, a bolt of ivory ribbon, and a framed photograph of Edmund at perhaps sixteen, squinting into summer sun beside a rowboat, his collar open, his hair doing something reckless. Beatrix stopped in front of the glass longer than they meant to. In the photograph Edmund was laughing at someone outside the frame, and the laugh had caught him mid-breath so that his whole face was open, unguarded, a boy entirely unaware he had eleven days left to be alive.

*I never forget,* Beatrix thought, *that's the problem.* The photograph joined the archive whether they wanted it or not: the crooked framing, the price sticker half-peeled from the glass, the reflection of the square behind them—Mrs. Aldous crossing with her marketing basket, the church clock reading 9:14, a dog tied to the bench whose lead was blue.

"You'll be coming, then." Hartley himself said it from behind the counter, not quite a question, not looking up from the ledger where he was writing orders in his small upright hand. He was a broad, careful man who had sold Beatrix sweets as a child and had never once, in all the years since, asked them a personal question. "Dinner. Saturday after next."

"I haven't decided."

"Hm." He wrote another line. "Sylvie's ordered the good glasses out of storage. Forty covers. She wants the long tables down the length of the gallery, like the old days." A pause, the pen hovering. "First year she's done the gallery. Usually it's the hall proper."

"Why the change?"

Hartley looked up then, and his face did something complicated and brief, like a fish turning under water. "Reckon she wants room," he said, and went back to his ledger, and Beatrix understood that the conversation was over in the way conversations in Wrenlow ended—not with a door closing but with a curtain drawn, everything still visible in silhouette.

Outside, two women from the flower guild were wiring greenery to the churchyard railings, and their talk drifted across the square in the flat carrying voices of people who have known each other too long to bother lowering them.

"—forty white candles, Father Pell said, and not a one taller than the rest, he was very particular—"

"—well, he would be, wouldn't he, it's his evening as much as anyone's, more really, thirty years he's said the words over that boy—"

"—eleven years, Enid. Eleven. And still nobody tells you what to bring."

"Your appetite, Dora. You bring your appetite and your best black, same as every year."

They laughed, and the sound of it crossed the square and broke against Beatrix like small cold waves. *His evening.* As though the anniversary belonged to the living. As though Edmund had been a fixture of the calendar rather than a boy in the water.

* * *

The cottage on Fen Lane had been Beatrix's for eight months now—a temporary arrangement, they had told the letting agent, though both of them had known better. Investigators who grew up in a place did not rent cottages there by accident. Beatrix had told themselves it was proximity to the marsh, access to records, the practical business of a professional life. But the truth, which surfaced now and then like the heron on the crest, was simpler: Beatrix had come back to Wrenlow the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

And now the tooth was singing.

They spent the afternoon in the back room, which held the boxes. Not case files—those lived in the flat metal cabinet under the stairs, locked—but the other kind of box, the personal archaeology of a childhood spent partly in this parish and partly in the care of an aunt two counties away who had taken in her sister's orphan and never quite known what to do with a child who remembered everything. Beatrix knelt on the floorboards and opened the second box from the left, the one labeled in their own twelve-year-old handwriting: *AUCTION — THINGS THAT ARE MINE NOW.*

Inside: a brass compass with a dented lid. A paperback novel with a cracked spine, *The Moonstone*, Edmund's name inked inside the cover in a slanted, impatient hand. And beneath them, folded in tissue gone brittle with age, a page torn from an auction catalogue.

**LOT 47** — *Gentleman's wardrobe, comprising suit (charcoal wool), dress shirt (white, cotton), shoes (black leather), and accessories. Property of the late E.J. Vasse. Sold as a single lot.*

Beatrix had bid their entire savings that day—£14, saved across two years of birthdays and errands—and the auctioneer's gavel had come down like a small verdict, and the adults in the room had smiled at the strange child who wanted a dead boy's clothes, and Beatrix had carried the box home on the bus with it balanced on their knees the whole way, guarding it, though from what they could not have said.

They had never worn the clothes. That was the thing no one understood, the thing the police had found so difficult to grasp when they'd called three days ago and asked Beatrix, in the gentle voice reserved for witnesses who might be suspects, to confirm the garments found on the body in the marsh. Beatrix had confirmed them instantly, completely—the charcoal wool with its faint pinstripe, the shirt with the monogram at the cuff, the shoes with the stitched welt—and then had added, and seen the officers exchange glances: *But those aren't the clothes I bought. Those are copies. Very good ones, aged artificially, but the shoes are worn wrong-footed. Edmund broke his right heel outward. Whoever wore those shoes broke their left.*

A ghost had been manufactured in Wrenlow, and manufactured well enough to fool everyone except the one person in the county who kept perfect records of the past.

Now Beatrix sat back on their heels among the boxes, the auction page in their hands, and considered the invitation lying on the kitchen table in the next room.

Someone had dressed a stranger in Edmund's clothes and put him in the marsh eleven days before the anniversary dinner. Someone had wanted the parish to see a drowned boy rise from the water. And someone—this was the part Beatrix turned over and over, the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath—someone had known that Beatrix owned lot 47. Had known it well enough to copy it rather than steal it. Had known that if the clothes themselves were taken, Beatrix would notice the absence immediately, but a faithful reproduction might pass unnoticed until the police came asking.

Which meant the staging was aimed, in some measure, at Beatrix. Or through Beatrix. Or—Beatrix sat with this last possibility in the gathering dusk, the curlew calling again beyond the window—it meant the staging was designed to draw Beatrix in, the way the invitation had been drawn up with their name spelled carefully around their pronouns, *requested and treasured,* the way the stone had been placed on the doorstep by hands that knew exactly how heavy to make it.

Someone wanted them at the dinner. Someone wanted them in that gallery, under forty white candles, surrounded by every soul in Wrenlow who had been within a mile of the marsh on the night Edmund Vasse died.

*Come,* said the invitation. *Come and be remembered.*

* * *

On Sunday the bells rang for service, and Beatrix did not go, and heard it anyway—sound traveled across the flats here the way guilt traveled through families, reaching everywhere eventually, arriving softened but intact. Instead they walked out past the churchyard wall in the opposite direction, along the raised path toward the marsh, because the body had been found there and because walking a place was a form of reading it.

Wrenlow was dressing itself even out here. Someone had tied a ribbon—ivory, from Hartley's window—to the stile at the marsh gate, a small absurd gesture of commemoration that would be soaked and ruined by Tuesday's rain. Farther along, at the bend where the path overlooked the reed beds, Beatrix found fresh footprints in the mud: boot prints, sizeable, recent, going out toward the waterline and returning. Standing prints at the edge, two sets side by side, as though someone had brought a companion to look at the spot.

Or as though someone had brought a companion to explain the spot to them.

Beatrix crouched and read the mud the way other people read letters. The boots had a squared heel, deeply treaded—work boots, or boots chosen to look like work boots. The companion's prints were narrower, lighter, a dress shoe with a slight drag on the left side. Beatrix photographed both with the camera they now carried everywhere, and noted the time, and the cloud cover, and the fact that the ribbon on the stile had been tied with a bow rather than a knot, which suggested ceremony rather than utility.

On the walk back, the church bells finished and the congregation began to spill onto the path ahead, and Beatrix slowed, letting distance open between themselves and the black coats and good hats. But distance in Wrenlow was a fiction. Voices carried. Faces turned.

Sylvie Vasse saw them first. She was standing by the lych-gate in a coat the color of wet slate, arranging her gloves, and when her eyes found Beatrix across fifty yards of churchyard grass she did not look away, did not perform surprise, only lifted one hand—a small motion, halfway between greeting and summons—and held it there a moment too long before letting it fall.

Beside her, emerging from the porch into the pale light, stood Father Ambrose Pell, silver-haired, serene, his cassock buttoned to the throat, his hand resting briefly on the shoulder of a departing parishioner. He was smiling at something the woman had said. Then his gaze traveled, unhurried, across the churchyard, past the ribboned railings, past the slate-colored coat, and settled on Beatrix at the far gate with the mild, bottomless attention of a man looking into deep water.

He inclined his head. Beatrix inclined theirs.

And between them, unspoken, lay the whole arithmetic of the coming fortnight: the dinner, the candles, the forty covers, the gallery with its long tables, the anniversary of a drowning that had been ruled an accident by four witnesses who all still lived within a mile of the marsh. Beatrix would go. Of course they would go. The invitation had never really been a request, and the stone on the doorstep had never really been a courtesy.

That night, Beatrix pinned the invitation to the corkboard above their desk, beside the photographs of the marsh mud and the wrong-footed shoes and the artificially aged wool. They sat looking at the heron on the seal, head bowed, listening for whatever moved beneath the water.

Then they took out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote, at the top, in their small precise hand:

*Auction, June 20—. Who conducted the sale? Who consigned lot 47?*

Because memory kept many things, but it did not keep everything, and there was a gap in theirs—a child's gap, eleven years wide—right at the place where the gavel fell and the box changed hands. Someone had stood at that podium. Someone had signed the docket. Someone had handed a strange quiet child a dead boy's wardrobe and wished them a good afternoon.

Beatrix intended to find out who.

The clock in the church tower struck ten, each bell-note arriving across the flats a half-second apart, and in the silence after the last stroke, Beatrix reached for the telephone directory and turned to the page for St. Brannoc's parish office.
