## 7. Four Witnesses

The map of Wrenlow parish hung in Beatrix's rented kitchen like a specimen pinned to cork—every road, every lane, every crooked footpath rendered in the surveyor's faded ink—and by lamplight it had begun to confess.

Beatrix had drawn four circles on it that evening, small and precise, in red pencil so fine it barely disturbed the paper's grain. The first circle sat at Marsh End Cottage, where Mrs. Tallow, housekeeper to the Vasse family for thirty-one years, still lived in a house the estate had deeded to her "for services rendered." The second marked Ivo Brand's groundskeeper's lodge at the edge of the Vasse land, close enough to the water that his windows must have fogged with marsh breath every winter morning of his life. The third enclosed Vasse Hall itself, where Sylvie Vasse had inherited everything her cousin's death had left unclaimed. And the fourth—the smallest circle, drawn with a hand Beatrix noticed only afterward had gone very steady—sat over the vicarage of St. Aldhelm's, where Father Ambrose Pell had ministered for longer than anyone could remember without checking the brass plaque by the door.

Four circles. One mile of fen between them, give or take a hedgerow. Eleven years ago, all four of them had stood at the water's edge and told the coroner the same story: Edmund Vasse went out alone, the boat was found overturned, the boy could not swim, and grief was grief and accidents were accidents.

Beatrix stood back from the map with their arms folded and let the geometry speak.

A closed circle. Not metaphorically—literally, cartographically, a ring of houses around the place where a boy had drowned, each one within sight of the reeds, each one within calling distance of the others. If any one of them had wanted to say something in eleven years, they had only needed to walk next door. Instead the circle had held. It had held through inquests and anniversaries, through Sylvie's majority and Mrs. Tallow's retirement, through floods that reshaped the marsh and a new footbridge and two changes of constable. Circles like that did not hold by accident. They held because everyone inside them understood, wordlessly and completely, what would happen if the ring were broken.

Beatrix poured tea they did not drink and studied the map until the shapes burned themselves into memory—which took no time at all, and never did. That was the trouble with a mind like theirs. Nothing ever burned away. Every detail Beatrix had ever seen remained exactly where it had been placed, dustless and undimmed, waiting. Most people found their memories softened by time, blurred into something bearable. Beatrix's simply waited, patient as the marsh, ready to give up whatever was asked of them.

And what the map asked tonight was simple: *Who benefits from a closed circle?*

Everyone inside it, came the answer. Everyone.

* * *

Beatrix began, as they began everything, with paper.

The county archive kept its records in a building that smelled of cold stone and older paper, run by an archivist named Mr. Fenwick who had known Beatrix's grandmother and therefore extended a leniency he extended to no one else. Beatrix spread the Vasse file across a reading table beneath a green-shaded lamp and read it the way other people prayed: completely, and with a certain grim devotion.

The inquest transcript ran to nineteen pages. Beatrix read it twice, then once more aloud under their breath, because hearing a thing sometimes caught what seeing missed. Four statements, taken within forty-eight hours of the drowning.

*Mrs. Edith Tallow, housekeeper:* She had seen Master Edmund leave the Hall at half past seven in the evening, dressed for the boathouse, carrying his coat over his arm despite the damp. He had seemed "in fine spirits." She had not seen him again. She had heard nothing in the night. She slept, she said, "like the dead," which Beatrix noted she had said without apparent irony, or perhaps with a great deal of it; ink preserved no tone of voice.

*Ivo Brand, groundskeeper:* He had been mending a fence on the far side of the park and had seen Master Edmund cross toward the water "around eight, maybe a bit after." The boy had waved. Brand had waved back. He had gone home at nine. He had heard nothing. He had found the overturned boat the following morning while walking his dogs.

*Sylvie Vasse, cousin, aged fourteen:* She had been at the Hall all evening, doing her lessons. She had not seen Edmund after supper. She had heard nothing. Her statement was the shortest of the four—four sentences, Beatrix counted, four sentences from a girl who had lived in the same house as the drowned boy for her entire life—and it had the flatness of something recited rather than remembered.

*Father Ambrose Pell, vicar:* He had called at the Hall that evening on parish business, arriving at approximately seven o'clock and departing before eight. He had seen Edmund briefly, in the passage, "on his way out somewhere." The boy had seemed preoccupied but well. Father Pell had driven home to the vicarage and retired early. He had heard nothing in the night. His statement was the longest, the most fluent, the most helpful—and Beatrix read it three times trying to find a single concrete detail in it that could be checked against anything at all, and failed.

*On his way out somewhere.* A man who could not say where. A man who noticed the boy's mood but not his direction. In eleven years, apparently, no one else had noticed either.

Beatrix sat back in the green light and let the statements arrange themselves in memory like figures on a stage. Tallow: warm, detailed, useless. Brand: blunt, physical, useless. Sylvie: absent. Pell: eloquent, empty, everywhere at once—the only witness who placed himself at the Hall that night, the only one whose account could not be corroborated, the only one who had thought to mention Edmund's state of mind.

In a case built entirely on what people had *not* seen, the vicar had seen just enough to steer it.

Beatrix copied nothing down. There was no need. But they sat a moment longer with the file open, looking at the coroner's closing line—*I am satisfied this death was by misadventure, and I extend the court's sympathy to the Vasse family*—and thought about how much satisfaction could be purchased with four agreeable statements and a parish's willingness to grieve quietly.

* * *

The next morning Beatrix walked the mile.

It was the kind of errand that looked like nothing—a stranger strolling the lanes of a fen parish in October, hands in pockets, pausing now and then to look at hedges—but Beatrix walked it the way a surveyor walks, measuring. From Marsh End Cottage to the boathouse: six hundred paces along the raised path, the whole route visible from Mrs. Tallow's front windows unless the mist came up off the water, which Beatrix knew from eleven years of Octobers in this parish that it often did. From the groundskeeper's lodge to the same point: four hundred and fifty paces, downhill, unobstructed. From Vasse Hall's garden door: three hundred. From the vicarage, by the lane: nine hundred, though by the field path—unmarked on any map but worn deep into the turf by generations of churchgoers taking the shortcut—perhaps six hundred and fifty.

Every witness could have reached the water in under ten minutes. Every witness could have seen it, in daylight, from their own doorstep.

Beatrix stood at the top of the field path behind the church, where the grass grew in a ridge like an old scar, and looked down at the marsh. The water lay flat and pewter-colored under a low sky, the reeds standing in ranks, the boathouse a black tooth at the shoreline with its roof half fallen in. Somewhere out there, three days ago, a man had died wearing a dead boy's clothes. Somewhere out there, eleven years ago, another man had died wearing nothing but the parish's certainty.

"You'll catch your death standing there."

Beatrix turned. An old woman with a market basket had stopped on the path, wrapped in a coat the color of wet slate. She nodded toward the marsh as if it were a misbehaving child.

"Fog comes up quick this time of year. One minute you can see the church tower, next minute you can't see your own boots." She shifted her basket. "You're the one asking questions. The one from away."

"I'm looking at the parish," Beatrix said mildly. "It's a beautiful place."

"It's a place," the woman said, with the flat finality of someone who had buried a husband in it. She started past, then paused. "Whatever it is you're after, the Tallow woman won't tell you and Brand will set his dogs on you and Miss Sylvie will smile at you till you forget what you asked. And Father Pell—" she adjusted her grip on the basket, and for a moment her eyes went to the marsh instead of to Beatrix, "—Father Pell will pray for you. Which is worse."

She walked on. Beatrix watched her go, filing the voice, the coat, the exact cadence of *which is worse*, and turned back to the water.

A closed circle. And the whole parish, apparently, knew its shape.

* * *

That afternoon Beatrix made their first approach—not to any of the four, but around them, the way one tests ice from the edges inward.

At the post office, buying stamps they did not need, Beatrix remarked to the postmistress that it must be pleasant, living so close to the old Vasse lands. The postmistress, a cheerful woman named Dilly Harkess who had sold Beatrix their childhood sweets, agreed that it was, and added—unprompted, in the way of small villages where information moves faster than mail—that Mrs. Tallow hadn't set foot in the church since the anniversary dinner last year, that Ivo Brand spoke to nobody but his dogs, and that Miss Sylvie had let the east wing of the Hall go dark these two winters, "though whether it's economy or melancholy, who's to say." Of Father Pell she said only that he was "a saint," in the tone of a woman repeating a phrase she had been saying so long it had worn smooth of meaning.

"And they're all still there," Beatrix said. "After all these years. Nobody moved away?"

Dilly Harkess laughed, sorting stamps. "Move? Good Lord, no. Tallow's been in that cottage since before I had teeth. Brand inherited the lodge off his father, same as his father got it off *his*. And the Vasses—" she shook her head fondly, "—the Vasses have been at the Hall since the year dot. Sylvie'd sooner sell her own shadow." She leaned across the counter, lowering her voice into the register reserved for harmless gossip. "It's the vicar keeps them together, really. After poor Edmund, he saw them all through it. Called on Tallow every week that first year, I heard. Took Brand under his wing—Brand was the one found the boat, you know, and it fair broke him. And Sylvie—he practically raised her after, her parents being gone already. Lovely man. The parish wouldn't hold together without him."

*Called on Tallow every week.* Beatrix thanked her for the stamps and carried the sentence out into the street like a coal cupped in bare hands.

The vicar had not merely survived the drowning alongside his flock. He had tended it. He had positioned himself, week after week, year after year, at the center of every grieving household that might otherwise have compared notes. A shepherd does not walk among sheep to learn their secrets. He walks among them to make sure the flock stays a flock—and that no stray wanders toward the edge of the field, where the view is better.

Beatrix stood outside the post office in the thinning light and looked down the high street. At its end, past the war memorial and the lych-gate, St. Aldhelm's tower rose against the gray sky, and beside it the vicarage, its windows giving onto the churchyard, the churchyard giving onto the field path, the field path running straight as a plumb line down to the water.

Six hundred and fifty paces. Beatrix had counted them themselves.

* * *

Evening came early, as it did in October, and Beatrix spent it in the kitchen with the map again, adding what the day had yielded in pencil notes so small they were almost prayer.

*Tallow — 600 paces. Front windows command the path. Has not entered church in a year. Why does a devout housekeeper stop going to church?*

*Brand — 450 paces. Found the boat. Speaks to no one. Dogs.*

*Sylvie — 300 paces. East wing dark. Raised by Pell after the drowning. Four sentences in her statement.*

*Pell — 650 paces by the field path. Only witness to place himself at the Hall. Visited all three weekly for a year. Still visits.*

Beatrix read the notes over twice, then drew one final line on the map: a red arc connecting the four circles, enclosing the marsh, the boathouse, the water. It closed perfectly. Of course it did. Beatrix had known it would before the pencil touched paper.

There was a knock at the door of the cottage—two soft raps, apologetic even before it opened—and Constable Reeve's replacement, a young officer named Wilcox who had been assigned to liaise on the body-in-the-marsh case, put his head in with the expression of a man delivering news he wished were someone else's.

"Thought you'd want to know," he said. "We finished canvassing for the drifter—Reeve. Who he spoke to, where he went." He hesitated. "He didn't speak to many. Kept to himself at the inn. But the landlord says there was one visitor. Evening before last, the night before Reeve was found. Man in a cassock. Left him a pamphlet, landlord thought. Pastoral call, like."

Beatrix did not move. "Did the landlord know which clergyman?"

Wilcox shifted. "Only the one vicar we've got, isn't there?"

He left. Beatrix stood a long while in the lamplight, looking at the map—at the fourth circle, the smallest one, drawn in a hand that had gone very steady—and felt the circle draw tighter, one ring closer, like water rising in a well.

Tomorrow, Beatrix decided, they would begin with the housekeeper. Tomorrow they would knock at Marsh End Cottage and sit in Mrs. Tallow's immaculate rooms and ask their questions and watch her answer them too smoothly, too completely, in the voice of a woman reciting eleven years of rehearsal.

But tonight, Beatrix took down the map, folded it along its old creases, and put it away—and found, as they did it, that they were already memorizing the way the red circles overlapped, in case the map itself should disappear.

Some cases stayed closed, the anonymous note had said. Beatrix was beginning to understand that the note had not been a threat.

It had been a description.
