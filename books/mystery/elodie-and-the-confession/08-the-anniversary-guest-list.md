## 8. The Anniversary Guest List

Dev Okafor had a rule about anonymous tips: they were either gold or garbage, and the difference was usually legible in the first email. The good ones came from people who couldn't stop talking—paragraphs spilling over paragraphs, guilt leaking through every line break, a need to be heard so naked it embarrassed you to read it. The bad ones were one-liners, taunts, noise. The tipster who had opened season four of *The Coldest Case* was neither. That was what had bothered Dev for three weeks without quite becoming a thought.

The first email had arrived on a Tuesday in March, subject line: *Marguerite Vasseur did not run away.* No greeting, no signature. Just four clean paragraphs laying out the bones of the case—the anniversary dinner as ritual, the east wing sealed since 1994, the staff roster that the family's lawyers had kept out of every prior investigation—and then a single closing line that Dev had read a dozen times since:

*Some stories are buried where the digging is easiest.*

It read like an invitation written by someone who already knew the guest would say yes.

Dev sat in the production office above the laundromat on Rue Clémence, surrounded by the archaeology of a podcast season: printed transcripts fanned across the desk like tarot cards, two monitors glowing with waveform and spreadsheet, a cold coffee going slick with skin. The building hummed its afternoon hum—dryers tumbling below, a pipe knocking somewhere in the wall—and Dev pulled up the tipster's emails again, all eleven of them, arranged in chronological order like specimens pinned to a board.

That was when Dev saw it. Not with the eyes at first—with the spine, a cold walk down the vertebrae.

The murder had happened eleven days ago. The body in the marsh, wearing Coral's clothes. Everyone, including the police, including Coral themselves, had been working from the assumption that the killer had reacted to the podcast—that season four had poked something awake and the something had lashed out. Cause, then effect. Broadcast, then body.

But the first email was dated March 4th.

Coral hadn't announced season four until April 19th. The teaser hadn't dropped until May. The murder had come after—but the *baiting* had come before. Weeks before. The tipster hadn't found the podcast. The tipster had built it.

"Oh," Dev said aloud, to the empty office, and the word came out small and wrong, like a hiccup at a funeral.

[FROM THE ARCHIVE — *THE COLDEST CASE*, SEASON FOUR, EPISODE ONE, UNAIRED COLD OPEN]

**DEV:** Say the name for me again.

**CORAL:** Marguerite Vasseur.

**DEV:** And why her? Out of everything in the file room—every cold case in this country—why this one?

**CORAL:** *(laughing)* You sound like my therapist.

**DEV:** I sound like your producer. Which is worse.

**CORAL:** Because she vanished on the same night as someone else who never got a search party. Because thirty years is long enough that everyone's story has hardened into stone, and stone cracks if you know where to tap. Because—

*(pause; tape hiss)*

**CORAL:** Because I think I've been walking toward this house my whole life and only just learned its address.

**DEV:** Cut. Keep that. God help us, keep all of it.

* * *

Dev did what Dev always did when a feeling needed to become a fact: made a timeline. Paper, not screen—there was something about handwriting that shook loose what spreadsheets smoothed over. A legal pad, the top sheet, the pen that lived behind their ear.

March 4th: first email. Detailed knowledge of the estate, the sealed wing, the suppressed roster. No request for money, no demand for credit, no interest in being interviewed. Every follow-up question Dev sent went unanswered except by silence—or by another email arriving within hours, as though the sender were watching Dev type.

March 11th: second email, containing the exact date of the thirtieth-anniversary dinner. An event that, Dev confirmed with two phone calls to the Vasseurs' event planner under a flimsy pretext about catering archives, had not been publicly announced. It existed on no website, no invitation visible online. The tipster knew.

April 2nd: third email. This one contained a fragment of the 1994 staff roster—a photograph of a photocopied page, the kind of document that should have been locked in a lawyer's filing cabinet—and a single sentence: *Ask about the housekeeper.* Dev had passed it to Coral as a lead. Coral had gone quiet for a day and then said, in a voice Dev had never heard before, *I'll tell you soon. Not yet.* Dev had honored that. Dev was still honoring it, and hating it.

May 3rd: fourth email, sent the day the teaser dropped, ninety minutes after. *Good choice. She would have liked you.* Dev had assumed "she" meant Marguerite.

Sitting at the legal pad now, drawing the arrow backward from that line, Dev felt the assumption die on the page. *She would have liked you.* If the tipster knew the housekeeper—if the tipster knew enough to point at the roster, to know which name mattered before Coral had ever breathed a word of it—then "she" might mean someone else entirely. Someone whose name Dev still didn't know, because Coral hadn't told them yet, because Coral had said *not yet*, and Dev had waited like a fool waits, politely, outside a door that was already burning.

And the fifth email, June 1st, six days before the body surfaced in the marsh: *Tell Coral to pack for a long stay. The family will want them close.*

The family will want them close.

Dev put down the pen. The dryers below had gone quiet; the whole building seemed to be holding its breath along with them. On the monitor, the eleven emails glowed in their tidy column, and Dev understood suddenly that they had never been correspondence. They had been stage directions. Every message had moved Coral one step closer to the estate—one step closer to the marsh, to the dinner, to whatever the dinner was actually for. The tipster wasn't a source. The tipster was a director, and Coral had been cast without audition, and Dev had spent three weeks feeding their star lines written by someone else.

Lured, not chosen. Baited, not found.

* * *

The IP addresses were almost insultingly careful. Routed through a VPN chain that unwound through three countries and back into France like thread through a needle's eye. But whoever wrote those emails was meticulous about evidence and careless about vanity, and vanity leaves fingerprints even when gloves don't. Two of the eleven emails had been composed in a mail client that leaked the local network time alongside the spoofed headers—an old habit, the digital equivalent of a monogrammed handkerchief left at a scene.

Dev spent the evening with a friend from university, a woman named Priya who worked threat analysis for a firm whose clients preferred not to be named, and who owed Dev for a season-two favor involving a corrupt mayor and forty hours of audio nobody was supposed to have. Priya spread the metadata across her screens like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"Whoever this is," she said, tapping the timestamps, "they draft late. Between midnight and four, consistently. Eleven emails, eleven drafts, all in the same window." She looked at Dev over the top of her glasses. "Insomniac, or someone who works nights, or someone old enough that sleep stopped being negotiable decades ago."

"And the routing?"

"Clean. Too clean. Except—" She zoomed, and there it was, a single anomaly blooming in the sterile chain like a flower through pavement. "Except these two. The VPN exit nodes are supposed to randomize. These two emails exited through the same node twice, nine days apart. Statistically nothing. Practically—" she shrugged— "practically a habit. People return to the same node when the node is close. When it's convenient. When it's *theirs*."

"Where does the node live?"

Priya turned her monitor toward him. A map of the department, rendered in the flat colors of utility, and a pin dropped in green countryside maybe four kilometers—as the crow flew, and crows didn't care about gates or gravel drives—from the Vasseur estate.

Four kilometers. Close enough to see the chimney smoke on a cold morning. Close enough to watch cars come and go. Close enough, Dev thought, to watch a marsh.

"The node could be compromised," Priya said, in the tone of someone offering a life raft while pointing out the holes. "Could be coincidence. Could be anyone in that radius."

"Could be," Dev agreed, and neither of them believed it.

Walking back through the town afterward—the streets gone amber under the streetlamps, the smell of someone's dinner drifting from an open window, an ordinary evening in a town that had been ordinary for thirty years—Dev felt the horror arrive the way weather arrives, not all at once but in layers, each one colder than the last. First: the tipster was near the estate. Second: the tipster knew things only someone inside the family's gravity could know. Third: the tipster had timed everything—the announcement, the teaser, the invitation—to deliver Coral to that house at exactly the moment a dead woman was dressed in Coral's clothes and laid out in the reeds like a gift with a card attached.

And fourth, the layer that made Dev stop on the bridge over the canal and grip the cold iron rail: *if the tipster had planned all of it, then the tipster had planned for Dev too.* Every email Dev had answered, every question Dev had asked, every archive query Dev had filed—logged, read, anticipated. Dev looked down at the black water and saw, faintly, their own reflection looking back, and had the sudden vertiginous certainty that somewhere four kilometers away, in a dark room between midnight and four, someone else was looking at that same reflection's data shadow and smiling.

[FROM THE ARCHIVE — *THE COLDEST CASE*, PRODUCTION NOTES, VOICE MEMO, JUNE 9TH]

**DEV (recording):** Note to self. Note to future me, who is hopefully calmer than present me. Do not send anything else to the tipster address. Do not confirm receipt of anything. I went back through my replies tonight—all seven of them—and I counted what I gave away. I told them Coral was staying at the estate. I told them we'd secured interviews with all three siblings. I told them—I'm reading it now, I can hear my own stupid voice saying it—I told them *"this season feels different, like the case chose us."*

Like the case chose us.

I need to talk to Coral. Properly. Not in the kitchen with Yvonne Vasseur pouring wine and listening through the doorway. Somewhere with walls that don't belong to that family. Because here's the thing I keep circling and can't land: the emails predate the murder. The police don't know that. Coral doesn't know that. As far as the world knows, the tipster came forward *after* the body, some citizen moved by conscience. But the sequence says otherwise. The sequence says the body was part of the plan, and the tip was the map, and we walked it step by grateful step.

There's one more thing. The sixth email—the one I've never played for anyone—it says, and I quote: *"Your record is perfect because you only take cases that are already solved. You simply haven't been told the ending yet."*

I used to think that was a compliment.

* * *

Dev called Coral three times that night. The calls rang through to voicemail with a strange, seamless speed—no proper ring cycle, just the click into recorded silence—and on the third attempt Dev noticed the quality of the connection, a faint metallic undertone beneath Coral's recorded voice, the audio equivalent of a smudge on glass. Dev had spent fifteen years editing other people's phone calls and knew bad signal the way a sommelier knows cork taint. This wasn't distance. This was interference. Deliberate or otherwise, something between the estate and the outside world was eating the wires.

"Coral," Dev said to the voicemail, keeping their voice level the way you keep your voice level near a spooked animal, or a sleeping one. "Call me. Not text—call. There's something about the tipster I got wrong, and I need to tell you before—" They stopped. Before what? Before the dinner. Before Friday. Before whatever the tipster had scheduled next, because Dev no longer believed anything in this case was unscheduled. "Just call me."

They hung up and sat in the dark office a while, the legal pad in front of them, the timeline marching down the page in their own handwriting like a row of footprints leading somewhere terrible. At the bottom, beneath the last dated entry, Dev wrote a single line—not for the show, not for the record, just for the page:

*We weren't invited. We were delivered.*

Then, because dread unattended curdles into paralysis, Dev did the thing Dev knew how to do: work. They exported the full email chain, headers intact, to two encrypted drives. One went into the fire safe bolted beneath the floorboards—the safe Coral teased them about, the safe that had survived three offices and one flood. The other Dev wrapped in a cloth and carried downstairs past the shuttered laundromat, out into the blue dark, and drove twenty minutes to the chapel on the hill above town—the little stone chapel, deconsecrated decades ago, that rented space to a bell choir and a book club and, unofficially, to anyone who needed a hiding place more than they needed a receipt. Dev had recorded half of season two's best interviews in its vestry. Dev knew every loose stone in its floor.

Behind the third pew from the back, beneath the panel where generations of restless hands had worn the wood smooth, there was a cavity the size of a bread box. Dev slid the drive inside, replaced the panel, and sat for a moment in the chapel's cold quiet, listening to wind move through the yew trees outside with a sound like slow applause.

"If anyone's listening," Dev said to the empty nave, to the dust motes turning in the moonlight, to whatever patient intelligence had orchestrated all of this, "you should know we fact-check."

The drive stayed. The fear stayed. And on the walk back down the hill, Dev composed the message they'd send at dawn—not to Coral's phone, which couldn't be trusted, but through the one channel the tipster had never touched, the channel too old-fashioned and too boring to intercept: the show's public submission form, the one fans used to send corrections, buried in the site's backend, flagged to Dev alone.

The message was four words long. It looked, to any stranger, like a fan's typo-ridden nonsense. To Coral, who had helped build the show's cipher habits back in season one, it would read like a shout through a wall:

*TIPSTER PRE-DATES MURDER. TRUST NO INVITATIONS. CALL ME FROM TOWN.*

Dev hit send, watched the confirmation blink green, and stood a moment longer under the dying streetlamp, the town asleep around them, the estate invisible beyond the hills but present anyway, the way a tumor is present—unseen, and organizing.

Somewhere out there, between midnight and four, the tipster was drafting again. Dev could feel it the way sailors feel weather: a pressure change, a wrongness in the air. Twelve emails now, maybe. Thirteen.

And Dev, staring into the dark where the estate would be, understood at last what the anniversary dinner really was. Not a commemoration. Not a ritual of grief.

A guest list. Thirty years in the making. With Coral's name at the top of it, inked long before either of them ever knew—and Dev's own name just beneath, added the moment they'd answered that first beautiful, poisoned email.

The hook of the next chapter was already written; Dev could feel it in their hands, cold as the chapel key. All that remained was whether Coral would read the warning in time—or whether the dinner would begin with two empty chairs and one perfect toast.
