## 15. The Diary Reassembled

The notary's office smelled of cold paper and older secrets—dust, sealing wax, the faint vinegar tang of glue gone brittle in drawers that hadn't been opened since before Coral was born. Maître Perrin had agreed to see them at eight in the morning, before the town woke properly, before the Vasseur name could reach him through any channel faster than a telephone line.

"You understand," he said, unlocking a cabinet with two keys, one from his waistcoat pocket and one from around his neck, "that these letters were never part of the estate. Marguerite left instructions. *If anything should happen to me,* she wrote, *my correspondence goes to you, not to my family.*" He paused, keys still in the lock. "I assumed she meant shipwreck. Drowning. Something with weather in it."

"And instead it was family," Coral said.

Perrin did not answer. He opened the cabinet and lifted out a tin box, olive-green, dented at one corner, tied with string that had faded from red to the color of weak tea. He set it on the desk between them as though it might go off.

Coral's hands were steady. That surprised them. All night they had lain awake in their locked room listening to the house breathe—the tick of cooling radiators, Yvonne's slow footsteps above, Auguste's voice through the wall saying *the podcaster won't be a problem after tomorrow*—and somewhere in those hours the fear had burned down past panic into something harder and colder and more useful. Not calm. Clarity. The kind of clarity that comes when there is nothing left to protect except the truth.

They untied the string.

* * *

There were forty-one letters. Coral counted twice because counting steadied them, and because numbers were evidence in a way feelings were not. Most were addressed to a woman named Hélène Roux in Lyon—a school friend, by the tone, someone Marguerite trusted enough to be funny with, which made her trustworthy in every other way too. The earliest letters were light: complaints about her mother's dinner parties, jokes about suitors, a running grievance about the estate's heating that read like a woman writing from inside a beautiful refrigerator.

Then 1993 arrived, and the temperature of the letters dropped.

*"Hélène, I have done something enormous and I cannot tell anyone here. I went to Maître Perrin—you remember him, the notary with the sad eyes—and I have redrawn my will."*

Coral read the sentence three times. Around them the office filled with morning: light climbing the tall window, a pigeon muttering on the sill, Perrin standing very still by his cabinet like a man watching a fire he could not put out.

The next letter explained it. Marguerite had discovered the forgeries.

She wrote it plainly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. Their mother's will—the old matriarch's will, Yvonne's—had been altered. Pages substituted. A codicil manufactured, shifting the bulk of the inheritance away from Marguerite and toward Auguste, justified by a clause about "continuity of the estate" that Yvonne, in her final lucid year, would never have signed. Marguerite had found the original draft in the notary's own archived copies and compared them line by line. The signature was good. It was almost perfect. But the ink was wrong—two years newer than the paper it sat on—and Marguerite, who had spent her twenties cataloguing the family's art collection and knew provenance the way priests know scripture, had caught it.

*"Auguste thinks I don't look at things,"* she wrote. *"That is his great mistake and it has always been his great mistake. He thinks being overlooked is the same as being invisible. I have spent thirty years being overlooked too, Hélène. I know exactly how visible it makes you."*

Coral set the letter down. Their hands were no longer steady.

Because that was Solange's sentence. That was their mother's whole philosophy, worn smooth from use, handed to Coral across a kitchen table in a rented flat in Marseille: *people who are ignored see everything, mon cœur. We are the witnesses nobody counts.* Coral had built an entire career on that sentence. They had built a podcast, a reputation, a record of closures, on the conviction that the overlooked see everything.

And here it was, in Marguerite Vasseur's handwriting, dated fourteen months before she vanished.

* * *

"I need the diary," Coral said. "The one from the servants' wing. The pages that were cut out."

Perrin frowned. "What diary?"

Coral told him. The sealed room. The preserved bed, the folded nightdress, the child's drawing signed *Coral* in crayon. The diary with its razored stubs of pages, maybe sixty of them, cut out so cleanly it had to have been done slowly, deliberately, by someone who wanted the book to keep its shape—to keep looking like a diary—while having nothing left to say.

And then Coral took out their phone and showed Perrin the photographs they'd taken before the room was resealed, before the locks changed, before Auguste's lawyer descended on the east wing like weather. The stubs. The dates inked along the gutter where each page met the spine. March 1994 through October 1994. Solange had kept the diary for years; only the last seven months had been removed.

"Why those months?" Perrin asked.

"Because of what happened in them." Coral laid the letters out on the notary's green baize desk like a hand of cards. "Look. In January '94, Marguerite writes that she's hired a new confidante—not a friend this time, someone inside the house. She doesn't name her. She calls her *my witness*. She says she's going to redo the will properly, legally, with this person named in it as a witness to the forgery, so that even if something happens to Marguerite, the evidence survives in another person's memory."

Perrin had gone very pale. He was an old man and his hands, when he reached for the letters, trembled in a way that seemed less like age than like recognition.

"In June," Coral continued, "Marguerite writes that her witness is frightened. Someone has been asking questions about her. Checking into her past. In August, the witness wants to quit but Marguerite persuades her to stay until the new will is signed—it's scheduled for November. And then—" Coral turned over the last letter, dated the twelfth of October, 1994, nine days before the disappearances. "Then this."

The handwriting had changed. Not the hand itself but its pressure—digging into the page, the loops cramped, as though written fast or written afraid.

*"Hélène. If I am ever foolish enough to disappear, look to the will and look to the witness. Auguste knows. I don't know how—I was careful—but he knows, and he has stopped pretending to grieve our mother and started pretending to love me instead, which is infinitely worse. My witness says we should go to the police. I say we wait for November. I keep telling myself the family name protects us. That no one named Vasseur would dare. God, Hélène, what if being a Vasseur is exactly why he'd dare?"*

Nine days later, Marguerite Vasseur walked out of her brother's anniversary-adjacent world and never came back. Nine days after that, Solange Joubert packed nothing, told no one, and vanished from the servants' wing with her daughter's drawing still pinned above the bed.

Two women. One secret. One night—or near enough that the difference didn't matter.

"The witness," Perrin said quietly. "It was your mother."

"It was my mother." Saying it aloud felt like setting a bone that had been broken for thirty years. "Marguerite couldn't name her in the letters because if the letters were found, the name dies with the plan. So she hid it where only someone who already knew would look—in the gaps. In what she *didn't* say." Coral tapped the diary photographs. "Solange kept a diary. Marguerite knew she kept a diary. Sixty pages covering exactly the months of the conspiracy—cut out. Not burned. Cut. Because whoever took them didn't want to destroy the diary, they wanted to hollow it. Leave the shell on the shelf so the room would look untouched, look preserved, look like grief instead of surgery."

"But the pages themselves?"

"Gone. Or—" Coral looked up. "Or hidden. Because Auguste doesn't destroy things, Maître Perrin. That's the pattern. He keeps them. The forged will—he didn't burn the original draft, he let it sit in your archive where he assumed no one would ever compare. The portrait of my mother—he didn't destroy it, he took it *out of the frame* and left the frame hanging, empty, because an empty frame looks like a mistake and a missing painting looks like a theft. He erases people by subtraction. He leaves the shape of them behind."

Perrin sat down slowly. "You believe the diary pages are still on the property."

"I believe Auguste has spent thirty years visiting my mother's room." Coral's voice came out flat and strange. "Fresh footprints in the dust, remember. He goes back there. He sits with what he took. I think he reads them." They swallowed. "I think he needs them the way other men need trophies. Proof that he was cleverer than both of them. Proof that the two women who saw through him saw through him *together*, and he still won."

* * *

They worked through the morning, Coral reading aloud while Perrin took notes in a small copperplate hand, building a bridge of dates between the letters and the razored diary and the police file from 1994 that Coral now knew almost by heart. The reconstruction assembled itself the way Coral imagined archaeologists feel a city rising out of sand: fragment by fragment, each piece confirming the next.

January 1994: the forged will exists; Marguerite finds it. (Letter.) February: she confronts no one; she begins to watch. (Gap in diary—razored.) April: she recruits her witness, someone "who sees everything because no one bothers to hide from her." (Letter. Coral read that line twice and had to stop for water.) May through July: the plan takes shape—a new will, a legal challenge, the original draft retrieved from the notary's archive as physical proof. (Letters; matching gaps.) September: Auguste grows attentive, affectionate, suffocatingly kind. Marguerite writes, *"He has started bringing me flowers, Hélène. Our mother hated lilies. He has forgotten that, or he wants me to think he has. Everything he does now is a message in a language I'm not supposed to be able to read."*

October twelfth: the last letter.

And then the silence, and the marsh, and the file with its tidy conclusion—*no evidence of foul play; presumed voluntary absence*—signed off by a gendarme who retired to the coast and died comfortable, and whose doubt, Dev had learned, had been noted once in a margin and never pursued.

Coral stood at the tall window while Perrin sealed copies of everything into envelopes. Below, the town was waking properly now: a baker's van, two schoolchildren racing, the plane trees dropping early leaves onto wet cobbles. Somewhere beyond the ridge, invisible, the Vasseur estate sat in its grounds like a held breath. Tomorrow was the dinner. Tomorrow Coral would stand up in front of thirty years of rehearsed harmony and say their mother's name out loud.

"Maître Perrin." Coral turned from the window. "In 1994, did Auguste ever come to you? About the will? About anything?"

The old notary was quiet long enough that the pigeon on the sill flew off.

"He came," Perrin said finally, "three weeks after the disappearances. He asked whether his sister had left any documents with me. Recent ones. He was very polite. He brought flowers." His mouth twisted. "Lilies. I told him client confidentiality forbade me to answer, and he smiled and thanked me and said he'd assumed as much. And then at the door he turned around and said—" Perrin closed his eyes, retrieving it verbatim after thirty years, the way old men retrieve the sentences that frighten them. "He said, *'Whatever she may have left with you, Maître, I hope you appreciate that paper burns more easily than people.'*"

The words settled into the room like frost.

"And you never reported that?"

"To whom? The man who investigated her disappearance was his schoolfriend. The prefecture took the family's calls directly." Perrin's hands flattened on the baize. "I was a coward, Mademoiselle Almeida. I told myself I was protecting the documents. Perhaps I was. But I was also protecting myself, and I have paid for it in sleep for three decades." He pushed the sealed envelopes across the desk. "So take these. Take all of them. And tomorrow, when you stand up in that house—say my name too. Say that the law was offered the truth in 1994 and declined it. Let the record show the rot went deeper than one man."

* * *

Coral walked back toward the estate through the town because they didn't trust the car, didn't trust the road, didn't trust the particular quality of silence that had followed them since the boathouse. Théo was dead. Staged again, dressed again, arranged again—this time in Coral's scarf, Coral's gloves, items taken from a suitcase that had been locked in a room that had been locked from the outside. The police had questions now, and the questions had teeth, and Detective Brissac—retired, quiet, smuggled into the guest list by Dev like contraband—was the only officer among them whose face Coral trusted.

So Coral walked, and thought, and the clarity that had carried them through the notary's office began, mile by mile, to cool into something heavier.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't resolve. Marguerite had planned to disinherit Auguste and name Solange as her witness. Both women had been silenced within days of each other. Which meant the diary pages—the physical record of the conspiracy, the thing Auguste had spent an evening carefully cutting out with a razor—were either destroyed or kept. And if they were kept, they were the last missing organ of the truth, the piece that would turn Camille's testimony and Perrin's letters and a recorded toast from a strong case into an unanswerable one.

And Auguste would know, tonight of all nights, that the endgame had arrived. He would move whatever he needed to move. Burn what needed burning—if it could be burned.

But Auguste didn't burn things. Auguste kept things. Auguste visited a dead woman's room and left footprints in the dust because he needed to sit inside what he'd done, needed the audience, needed—even now, especially now—someone worthy of watching him be brilliant.

Which meant the pages weren't in a safe. They weren't in a bank. They were somewhere he could visit.

Coral stopped at the foot of the drive. Above the tree line, the chimneys of the estate stood against a bruised violet sky, and the east wing windows caught the last light and threw it back flat and blind, like eyes that had been painted shut.

Tomorrow was the dinner. Tonight, the house would sleep—or perform sleeping.

Coral pulled their coat tighter and started up the drive, already mapping the route to the servants' wing in their head, already hearing the particular sound a razor makes against old paper, already knowing—with the cold, warm-blooded certainty of the reassembled truth—that somewhere behind those blind windows, sixty pages were waiting to be found, and that Auguste Vasseur was waiting to be found with them.
