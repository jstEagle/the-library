## 1. The Will That Shouldn't Exist

The rain found Hollowmere before Gideon Dravenmoor did, and by the time his carriage ground to a halt beneath the town's leaning signpost, the whole valley had been rinsed to the color of pewter. Water sheeted off the coach roof in silver threads. Beyond the fog that clung to the hillsides like something unwilling to let go, he could make out the shapes of the town — a church spire, a scatter of slate roofs, a single street that curved away from him as though it had no intention of being followed.

He stepped down into a puddle that swallowed his boot to the ankle and did not remark upon it. Gideon Dravenmoor was a man who did not remark upon discomfort. Twenty-two years of estate law had taught him that discomfort was merely the body's opinion, and the body was rarely a reliable witness.

What the body did know, however — what it had begun to insist upon somewhere past the last crossroads, as the trees thickened and the light went thin and gray as old linen — was that Hollowmere did not want him here. It was not hostility. Hostility he could work with; hostility had edges he could grip. This was something quieter. The town had the stillness of a room in which a conversation has just stopped.

He paid the driver, collected his case — leather, brass-cornered, containing every document pertaining to the estate of Marguerite Ashcombe — and walked the last quarter mile on foot, because the road had turned to soup and the carriage horses were already unhappy. Rain dripped from the eaves of the cottages he passed in long, deliberate lines, like fingers counting him as he went. A woman drawing water at a well watched him with the flat, patient attention of a heron. An old man on a porch did not watch him at all, which was somehow worse.

*You are the executor of record,* Gideon reminded himself, squaring his shoulders against the wet. *You are the most meticulous practitioner of probate law in three counties. You have untangled estates that made grown magistrates weep. You are here to do a job.*

The job, as he understood it, was straightforward. Marguerite Ashcombe, founder of Hollowmere, benefactress, matriarch, the name on half the buildings in town, had died eleven days ago of heart failure at the age of eighty-one. Her physician had signed the certificate; the registrar had stamped it; the town had mourned with the tidy, practiced grief of a place accustomed to ceremony. She had left a will. Gideon had been named executor — a stranger, which was unusual, but not unheard of. Wealthy women sometimes preferred a professional to a relative. It kept the knives from coming out at the reading.

He had reviewed the file on the journey north. He knew it the way he knew all his files: completely, in order, with the particular intimacy of a man who trusted paper more than people. Paper did not lie. Paper could be forged, yes — he had seen forgeries that would have fooled a bishop — but paper always, eventually, told the truth about itself, if one knew how to listen.

The town clerk's office stood at the bend of the high street, a narrow stone building squeezed between a chandler's and a shuttered tea house. Its windows glowed amber against the gray. Gideon climbed three worn steps, let himself in, and stood dripping on the mat while his eyes adjusted to the lamplight and the smell — ink, dust, coal smoke, and beneath it all something faintly sweet, like old paper left too long in a damp room.

Behind a counter scarred by a century of elbows sat a young man with spectacles and the posture of someone who had been waiting all day for exactly this moment and dreading it.

"Mr. Dravenmoor," the clerk said. Not a question. "We expected you yesterday."

"The roads," Gideon said. "The fog. I'm here now, and I'd like to begin. I'll need certified copies of the death certificate, the burial permit, and the will itself, as lodged with your office."

Something flickered across the young man's face — there and gone, quick as a fish turning in dark water. He stood, smoothed his waistcoat, and disappeared into the back rooms. Gideon heard drawers opening and closing. He heard a long pause. He heard, very faintly, a drawer close more softly than the others, as though something had been slid back into it rather than taken out.

When the clerk returned, he carried a single folder and an expression of studied neutrality that Gideon recognized instantly, because he had worn it himself on the rare occasions when a case turned under his hands like a card.

"Here is the death certificate," the clerk said. "Certified. And the burial permit."

"And the will."

"The will is—" The clerk's throat worked. "The will is not lodged with this office, sir. It was delivered to you directly. By courier. As you know."

"I know it was delivered to me," Gideon said pleasantly. "I also know the law of this county, which requires a copy of every will to be lodged with the district registrar within fourteen days of execution. I am here on the eleventh day. I would like to see the registrar's copy, so that I may verify it against the document in my possession before I execute it. This is standard practice. It protects the estate. It protects the town. It protects *you*."

The last word landed harder than he'd intended. The clerk flinched, and Gideon, watching him flinch, felt the first true cold thread of unease slide down beneath his collar, past the wool, past the skin.

*Protects you from what?* the flinch seemed to ask. *You tell me.*

"I'll fetch the registrar's ledger," the clerk said, and fled again.

While he waited, Gideon did what he always did in unfamiliar rooms: he read them. The office was clean but not tidy — clean in the way of a place that had been *made* clean recently, surfaces wiped, papers squared, as though someone had spent the last eleven days preparing for an inspection. On the wall hung a framed photograph of Marguerite Ashcombe: a tall woman in her later years, silver hair pinned with severe elegance, a mouth that looked as though it had made a career of not smiling and had no regrets about it. Her eyes, even in a faded photograph, even in this light, were the eyes of someone who had looked at a great many things and been fooled by none of them.

Beneath the photograph, someone had pinned a black ribbon. The ribbon was new. The pin holding it was rusted.

The clerk returned with a ledger bound in green cloth and laid it on the counter with both hands, the way one sets down something fragile or something dangerous.

"Page forty-one," he said.

Gideon turned to page forty-one. The registrar's hand was small and exact. He read the entry for the death of Marguerite Ashcombe — cause, date, physician's attestation, registrar's stamp. Then he read the entry below it, the one recording the lodgment of her last will and testament.

He read it twice.

The date of death was the third of October. The will had been executed — signed, witnessed, and dated in Marguerite Ashcombe's own hand — on the sixth of October.

Three days after her death.

The rain against the window seemed suddenly very loud. Gideon became aware of his own heartbeat, steady and slow, and of the fact that his hands, resting on the ledger, had gone perfectly still — the stillness of a man standing very carefully on ice he no longer trusts.

"There's an error," he said.

"No, sir."

"Then it's a clerical mistake. A transposition. The registrar wrote the sixth and meant the third, or—"

"No, sir." The clerk's voice was quiet and did not waver, and that was the most alarming thing yet, because it was the voice of a man who had answered this question before, or rehearsed answering it. "The will is dated the sixth of October. The death certificate is dated the third. We have checked. The magistrate has checked. Everyone has checked." He paused, and his eyes, behind the spectacles, were young and frightened and resolute all at once. "Everyone has checked, Mr. Dravenmoor, and no one will say what it means. That's why they sent for you."

Gideon straightened. He was aware of the photograph above him, of Marguerite Ashcombe's unfooled eyes, of the rain writing its long gray sentences down the glass. He was aware, with a professional's humiliation, that his confidence — the meticulous, load-bearing confidence of twenty-two years, the thing his colleagues teased him about and his clients paid handsomely for — had developed, in the space of four minutes, a crack running through it like a flaw in fine china.

He made himself speak evenly. "A will cannot postdate a death. It is not merely irregular; it is a legal impossibility. Either the date of death is wrong — which would implicate the physician, the registrar, and the burial — or the will is a fabrication. And a fabrication, Mr. — I'm sorry, I didn't catch your name."

"Quill," said the clerk. "Elias Quill."

"A fabrication, Mr. Quill, would be a criminal matter. It would mean someone in this town has committed forgery of a dead woman's hand, and done it *badly* — or done it deliberately, in a way designed to be discovered." He tapped the ledger page once, gently, as though the paper might flinch. "Which is it?"

"I don't know."

"You must know something. You've had eleven days."

"I know," Elias Quill said slowly, choosing each word the way a man chooses footing on a river stone, "that Mrs. Ashcombe was the finest hand in this county. She wrote the town's charter. She wrote the inscriptions on the fountain and the schoolhouse and the church bell. Every child in Hollowmere learns to read from her letters. I have handled her correspondence my whole life." He looked up, and the lamplight caught the lenses of his spectacles and turned them briefly into two small blind moons. "The will is in her hand, Mr. Dravenmoor. Every letter of it. I would swear to it on anything you asked me to swear on."

"Then you're telling me a dead woman signed her own will three days after she died."

"I'm telling you," said the clerk, "what the documents say. I'm not telling you what they *mean*. No one in this town will tell you what they mean. You'll have to ask her."

"Her being—"

"The housekeeper. Mrs. Pell. And the nephew, Cyril Ashcombe, who has already retained a solicitor of his own, and—" Quill hesitated. His gaze slid sideways, toward the rain, toward some middle distance where a decision was being made. "And there's a girl. Arrived two days ago. Young woman. Says her name is Elodie. She was named in the will."

Gideon's eyebrows rose despite himself. "A granddaughter? The file makes no mention of grandchildren."

"No," Quill agreed. "It wouldn't."

"Because—"

"Because," said the clerk, and here his voice dropped so low that Gideon had to lean across the counter to catch it, and the smell of the old sweet paper rose up around them both like a tide, "as far as this town has ever known, Mrs. Ashcombe had no family at all. No children. No grandchildren. No one. She was the founder, and the founder was alone, and that is the story every one of us was raised on." He straightened, and the moment of confidence closed over like water. "The will names her anyway. Names her first. Before the nephew. Before anyone."

The clock on the clerk's wall ticked with the dry patience of a thing that had outlasted everyone who wound it. Outside, the rain went on. Gideon gathered his papers with hands that were, he noted with detached displeasure, not entirely steady, and told himself it was the cold.

"Where do I find Mrs. Pell?"

"Ashcombe House. The lane past the church, up the hill. You can't miss it." Quill hesitated again, and then, as Gideon turned to go, added — not loudly, not as advice, merely as a fact laid on the counter like a coin: "She was buried on the eighth, Mr. Dravenmoor. Closed casket, by her own instruction, lodged in the family crypt behind the house. The whole town walked behind the coffin. Every one of us." A pause. "It was the largest funeral Hollowmere has ever held. And it was the quietest."

* * *

The lane to Ashcombe House climbed through dripping beeches whose leaves had turned the color of brass coins and were letting go of the branches one by one, settling onto the mud with small, final sounds. The fog came and went in slow movements, opening to show him the house and closing again like a book being thought better of.

Ashcombe House, when the fog allowed it, was a long gray pile of a building, two centuries of stone and slate, with a bell tower — disused, Gideon guessed, from the ivy at its throat — and gardens gone soft and wild around the edges, as though the house had begun, gently, to be reclaimed. Behind it, on a rise, stood a small stone crypt with an iron door, and Gideon's eyes went to it without his permission and stayed there longer than he liked.

*Closed casket. By her own instruction.*

He was still looking at it when the front door opened.

The woman who stood there was sixty or older, straight-backed, gray-haired, dressed in black that was uniform rather than fashionable. She held the door with one hand and looked at Gideon with an expression he would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to classify. It was not grief. It was not suspicion. It was the look of a person standing at a window, watching a storm come, who has already counted the things in the house that the storm can break.

"Mr. Dravenmoor," she said. Her voice was low and even. "You'd better come in. There's tea, and there's the others, and there's no use meeting them wet."

"Mrs. Pell."

"Mr. Dravenmoor." She stepped back to admit him, and as he crossed the threshold, out of the rain and into the dim, high-ceilinged hall that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, she said, quietly, without any inflection at all:

"You've seen the date on the will."

It was not a question. Gideon stopped. Water ran off the hem of his coat and pooled on the flagstones, and somewhere deeper in the house a clock struck four, and he heard — or thought he heard, at the very edge of hearing, beneath the clock and the rain and the settling of the old house — a third sound, faint and rhythmic, like a page being turned somewhere it should not have been.

"Yes," he said. "I've seen it."

Mrs. Pell closed the door behind him with the soft, deliberate care of a woman shutting out the weather, or shutting something in.

"Then you'll want to know," she said, "why the whole town is pretending it isn't there."

She led him down the hall toward the light of the parlor, toward the tea and the heirs and the beginning of everything, and Gideon Dravenmoor followed her — meticulous, professional, certain of nothing anymore — into the house of the woman who had signed her own will three days after she died.
