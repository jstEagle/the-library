## 3. Exhumation Order

The summons came on the third morning, delivered by a boy who left the envelope on the doorstep of Gideon's lodgings without knocking, as though the paper itself were something that might bite.

Gideon read it standing in the narrow hall, rain still dripping from his coat onto the flagstones. The magistrate — one Aldous Fenwick, whose name Gideon had learned only the day before from the town clerk's grudging tongue — requested his presence at eleven o'clock at the Hollowmere courthouse, "regarding the matter of the Ashcombe estate." The word *matter* was doing quiet, terrible work in that sentence. Legal language was like weather to Gideon; he could feel a storm coming off it before the first drop fell.

He dressed with particular care. It was an old habit of his, armor before battle: the charcoal waistcoat, the watch chain laid just so, the cufflinks his mother had given him when he passed the bar. If he was about to lose an argument, he intended to lose it looking like a man who could not possibly be wrong.

The courthouse sat at the crook of Hollowmere's high street, a squat gray building with a clock that had stopped at some hour no one remembered and no one repaired. Fog pressed against its windows like a congregation waiting to be let in. Inside, the air smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and, beneath it, something faintly sweet — cut flowers, perhaps, or the memory of them. The whole town still smelled faintly of Marguerite Ashcombe's funeral, Gideon thought. Grief hung over Hollowmere the way the fog did: total, damp, and impossible to see through.

Magistrate Fenwick received him in a chamber lined with ledgers and portraits of dead magistrates, all of whom appeared to be frowning at him in advance. Fenwick himself was a dry, precise man with spectacles that magnified his eyes into something owl-like and unblinking. He did not offer his hand.

"Mr. Dravenmoor."

"Your Worship."

"You'll know why you're here." Fenwick tapped a folder on his desk. Gideon recognized the will's copy — he had lodged it himself, two days ago, with hands steady and heart already uneasy. "A document dated the ninth of October. A death certificate dated the sixth. Three days, Mr. Dravenmoor. Three days during which, according to this signature, a dead woman took up her pen."

"I'm aware of the discrepancy," Gideon said. "I've said from the first that the document should be examined by experts—"

"It has been examined." Fenwick removed his spectacles, polished them on a handkerchief with excruciating slowness, and replaced them. "The ink is period-consistent. The paper matches stock found in the deceased's own desk. And the hand—" he paused, savoring it, "—the hand is hers. Two graphologists agree. Marguerite Ashcombe signed that will after she died, Mr. Dravenmoor. Or before she was buried. Or—" his voice dropped into the register men use when they believe they are being reasonable, "—she was never in that coffin at all."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the stopped clock ticked anyway, out of spite or habit.

"There is," Fenwick continued, "one way to resolve which of those possibilities is true. An exhumation. The grave will be opened, the coffin raised, and its contents verified against the burial record. I have signed the order this morning."

Gideon felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does on a ship. He had argued cases before tribunals that made Fenwick look warm-blooded; he knew the sensation of losing before the argument began. But this — this was worse than losing. This was watching a town take hold of a shovel and drive it into the chest of a woman they claimed to love.

"Your Worship," he said carefully, "I must formally object."

"You may object. It is your privilege as executor."

"Marguerite Ashcombe is three weeks dead. The ground has barely settled. Whatever grief this town is carrying, you propose to tear it open along with the earth — and for what? To satisfy a question of dates that any competent forger could have anticipated?" He heard his own voice sharpen and did not rein it in. "If there is fraud here, Your Worship, exhumation is precisely what the forger wants. You'll be playing their game on their board."

Fenwick regarded him over the top of his spectacles for a long moment. Then, mildly: "Or the forger never anticipated it, because they never imagined we'd dare. Tell me, Mr. Dravenmoor — why does an estate lawyer from the city argue so hard to keep a coffin shut?"

And there it was. The turn. Gideon felt it pass through the room like a draft under a door — the small, cold shift from *who forged the will* to *why is this stranger so invested*. He had seen towns do this before, in smaller ways: grief curdling, needing somewhere to go, finding the nearest outsider.

"I argue because it is my duty to the estate," he said evenly. "And because disturbing the dead to settle the living's suspicions is a poor trade. But if the order is signed, I will not obstruct it. I'll attend. I'll verify. That is what I am for."

"That," said Fenwick, "is the first sensible thing you've said since you arrived in my town."

*My town.* Gideon noted the possessive and filed it away, the way he filed everything — in the vast, dim archive behind his ribs where he kept every detail that might someday matter.

* * *

The order was posted on the courthouse door by noon, and by evening it seemed the entire town had come to read it, standing in the fog in their black coats like a congregation of crows. Gideon walked past them once, hat down, and caught the fragments of their talk the way one catches rain in cupped hands.

"—the lawyer's doing, they say—"

"—never trusted a man who makes his living off the dead—"

"—if she's not in that grave, then where—"

That last question trailed off unfinished, and Gideon understood that the not-finishing was the worst part. Hollowmere had spent three weeks mourning a certainty. Now the certainty itself was being dug up, and every unanswered thing would come crawling out of the hole with the soil.

Mrs. Pell was waiting outside his lodgings when he returned, standing very straight beneath a dripping awning, her hands folded, her face arranged into its usual careful blankness. She had come, she said, to bring him a scarf against the graveyard damp. It was a thin excuse and both of them knew it.

"They'll blame you," she said quietly, without preamble. "Whatever they find. Or don't find. They'll need someone to blame, and you're the one who brought the will."

"And you?" Gideon asked. "What will they need you for?"

Something flickered across her face — quick as a fish under ice, gone before he could name it. "I keep the house," she said. "Houses are easier than people. They only ask to be dusted." She set the scarf in his hands and turned to go, then paused. "Mr. Dravenmoor. When they open it — whatever comes out of that ground — try not to look surprised. Surprises are what they'll punish."

She left him holding the scarf and the warning, and he stood a long while in the doorway, listening to the fog drip from the eaves like a slow, patient clock.

* * *

The morning of the exhumation broke gray and windless, the fog sitting so low it seemed the sky had come down to watch. By eight o'clock the churchyard of St. Brannoc's was ringed with townsfolk — more than Gideon would have guessed lived in Hollowmere at all. They stood beyond the low iron fence in silence, breath rising in pale plumes, and not one of them spoke. It was the silence of people attending a second funeral, or a trial.

Gideon arrived early, as was his habit, and stood at the graveside reviewing the burial record by lamplight until the light was unnecessary. Marguerite Ashcombe, interred the eleventh of October, witnessed by four names he had since checked and rechecked. The sexton, a stooped man named Hobbes with hands like tree roots, worked beside two hired laborers, and the constable — a broad, wary man called Tulliver — stood at Gideon's shoulder with the fixed attention of a man guarding a suspect rather than accompanying an officer of the court.

"Fine morning for it," Tulliver said, without warmth.

"There are no fine mornings for this," Gideon replied.

"No," Tulliver agreed, and said nothing else, but his eyes stayed on Gideon the way a dog watches a door.

It was near the fence line that Gideon saw Elodie.

She stood apart from the crowd, hood up, hands buried in the pockets of a coat too thin for the season. She had not been summoned; executors were not obliged to notify estranged kin of such proceedings, and yet here she was, drawn to the edge of it like everything else in this case seemed to be drawn to edges. Their eyes met through the fog. She did not look away, and neither did he, and between them passed something wordless and mutual: *we are the only two people here who don't know what we're grieving.*

Then the spades went into the earth, and there was no more time for looking at anything but the ground.

They dug well, Hobbes and his men — neat-sided, unhurried, the soil coming up in dark wet layers that steamed faintly in the cold. Gideon watched the depth markers and said nothing, though every foot of descent tightened something in his chest. He had attended exhumations before; they were administrative horrors, usually. Paperwork with a smell. But this one had a will dated after a death certificate, a town holding its collective breath, and a housekeeper's advice ringing in his ears: *try not to look surprised.*

At a little past nine, the spades struck wood.

The sound carried. Gideon saw it move through the crowd like a shudder — heads bowing, a woman near the fence pressing her knuckles to her mouth. Hobbes climbed down and cleared the soil from the coffin lid with his bare hands, gently, the way one uncovers a sleeping face, and then the ropes went round, and the winch groaned, and Marguerite Ashcombe rose out of the ground of Hollowmere for the second time in a month.

They set the coffin on trestles beside the open grave. Fenwick nodded. Tulliver produced the crowbar himself, refusing the laborers' help, and worked the lid loose nail by nail with a screech that set Gideon's teeth on edge.

The lid came free.

Inside lay white satin, funeral flowers gone brown at their edges, a small silk pillow —

— and nothing else.

No body. No remains. No weight of bone or ash. The coffin held its linings like a stage holds its props after the actor has slipped out the back, and the silence that fell over St. Brannoc's churchyard was so complete that Gideon could hear the fog dripping from the iron fence.

Then the silence broke, all at once, in every direction.

"She's alive—" someone gasped, and someone else shouted "miracle," and a third voice, harder, cut through: "Or she was never dead, and someone in this churchyard knows exactly why." Heads turned. Gideon counted the turns. Every one of them ended on him.

"The lawyer," said a man near the fence — Cyril Ashcombe, Gideon realized, the nephew, his face mottled with something between horror and vindication. "The lawyer brings us a will from a dead woman, and now there's no dead woman. Explain *that*, Dravenmoor."

"I didn't write the will," Gideon said, and his voice sounded strange to him, calm and far away. "I only read it."

"Then read us the rest," Cyril snarled. "Read us where she *is*."

Tulliver stepped between them, but his hand, Gideon noticed, closed on Gideon's arm rather than Cyril's. "Best you come with me, sir. Just for questions. Just for now."

Across the churchyard, Elodie had not moved. She stood at the fence with her hood fallen back and her face very pale, staring past the crowd, past the coffin, into the open grave — into the dark wet rectangle of earth that had given back nothing. Gideon met her eyes one last time before Tulliver drew him away, and what he saw in them was not accusation.

It was the look of a young woman watching a stranger defend a grandmother she'd never had — and wondering, with a hunger she clearly hated herself for, whether the empty coffin meant the grandmother might still be defended.

The fog closed behind them as the constable led him from the churchyard, and in Gideon's ears, all the way down the high street, rang the sound of the town beginning to talk.

By nightfall, he knew, they would have decided what happened in that grave. And whatever they decided, the story would not have him in it as a lawyer.

That night, long after Tulliver's questions had gone nowhere and Gideon had returned to his lodgings to sit awake in the dark, he heard footsteps on the stair — soft ones, deliberate, pausing at his door. He reached it in time to hear them retreat. Beneath the door, pale in the lamplight, lay a single sheet of paper.

He picked it up with fingers that were not quite steady.

It was a page from his own case file — the magistrate's copy, the one locked in the courthouse — and across the top, in handwriting he did not recognize, someone had written a single line:

*You dug. Now finish reading.*
