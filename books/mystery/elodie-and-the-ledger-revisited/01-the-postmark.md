## 1. The Postmark

The letter arrived on the slowest night of the year, which is the kind of coincidence I stopped believing in around the time I turned thirty and started believing in patterns instead.

It was 2:14 a.m. on the third of November, and Halcyon Cabs was running exactly one car — Dmitri, out past the fairgrounds, ferrying a drunk wedding DJ home in a silence that my radio feed rendered as breathing. The dispatch floor hummed its usual two-note song: the fridge in the break room, the space heater that clicked like a patient clock, the CRT-green glow of the fare screen that hadn't updated in forty minutes. Outside, Ashford Falls was doing what it does best after midnight, which is nothing, beautifully.

The mail slot rattled.

That alone was strange. Nobody mails anything to a cab depot at two in the morning. The postman comes at ten, hands Dolores-adjacent paperwork to whoever's on days, and the slot stays shut until then. But there it was — a single envelope lying on the mat by the door, white, ordinary, slightly warped as if it had been rained on and dried crooked.

I should mention that I don't get up for things. That's a dispatcher's cardinal virtue. You sit, you wait, you let the town come to you. But the heater had just clicked off and the silence it left behind felt staged, so I crossed the linoleum, picked up the envelope, and stood there holding it under the fluorescent buzz like a woman deciding whether to touch a downed power line.

No return address. My name typed — not handwritten, *typed*, the kind of even, mechanical spacing you only see from an actual typewriter or someone who really commits to Courier New — across the front: F. EASTWICK, DISPATCH, HALCYON CABS. Not Freya. Not Ms. Eastwick. Just the function. Whoever sent it knew where I sat but not who I was, or wanted me to think so.

The postmark read October 29.

I did the math without wanting to. October 28 was the funeral. Edmund Wren's funeral — the whole town had closed early for it, or at least every business with a Wren in its ledger history had, which in Ashford Falls is most of them. I'd listened to the procession go by from this same chair, three black Cadillacs and forty cars of grief, while I logged fares for people too practical to skip work for a man they'd never met.

So the letter had been mailed the day after we buried him.

I went back to my desk. I didn't open it right away. I want to be honest about that, because later — when everything came apart and people asked how long I'd known, how long I'd sat on it — I want the record to show that I hesitated like a normal person. I made coffee I didn't drink. I checked Dmitri's route twice. I watched the fare screen blink its patient green nothing. Then I slit the envelope with the letter opener my mother gave me for Christmas the last year she was alive, because she thought a dispatcher needed one, and she was right in a way neither of us could have predicted.

Two pages. Typewritten, single-spaced, no salutation, no signature. It began mid-thought, like the second half of a conversation:

*You will have heard that Edmund Wren is dead. He died in a bed, old and warm, which is more than can be said for what he did. On the night of September 17, 1994, a car belonging to the Wren family struck a pedestrian on the Corliss Road extension near Miller's Bend and did not stop. The pedestrian was Ruth Calloway, a journalist. She was killed instantly. The driver was not Edmund Wren. The driver was nineteen years old and Edmund Wren paid to make the record say otherwise.*

*I am not the driver. I am not the family. I am the one who kept the books.*

*There were witnesses. There were always witnesses, and they were bought or frightened, all but one. Your mother, Marion Eastwick, took the fare call that night. She logged the pickup on Corliss Road at 11:42 p.m., four minutes before the impact, and she wrote down what the passenger said to her over the radio, word for word, because that was her job and she did it honestly. That entry still exists. Or it did.*

*I am dying. I would like the truth to outlive me, since I could not. Ask the ledger.*

I read it three times. The third time wasn't reading; it was bracing, the way you brace before checking a tire you already know is flat.

My mother's name sat there in the middle of the page like a stone dropped in still water. *Marion Eastwick.* Typed in the same even keys as everything else, no emphasis, no apology. As if naming her cost the writer nothing. As if she were just another line item.

Here's the thing about my mother: everyone in Ashford Falls has a Marion Eastwick story, and every one of them is the same story wearing different clothes. Marion who worked doubles. Marion who remembered your standing fare before you said it. Marion who once drove through an ice storm to deliver a diabetic's insulin because the ambulance couldn't get up Harker Hill, and who came back and logged it like any other trip, eleven miles, $18.50, no comment. She ran Halcyon's dispatch desk for twenty-six years and she kept those ledgers the way monks keep scripture — complete, dated, uncorrected. When she died fourteen months ago, half the town came to the funeral and the other half sent casseroles, and the eulogy used the word *reliable* four times, which I counted, because counting was easier than crying.

Reliable. Honest. Word for word.

And now some stranger with a typewriter was telling me her honesty was evidence.

I did what I always do with a problem, which is turn it into work. I pulled the current log book toward me and started a new case file — we keep them on paper, green folders, because my mother believed computers were "a rumor with a keyboard" — and I wrote across the top, in my neatest block capitals: *LETTER, ANON. RE: E. WREN / 1994 INCIDENT.* Date, time received, condition of envelope, postmark. Procedure is a comfort. Procedure is a handrail bolted to the inside of a dark stairwell, and I have spent a lot of nights holding it.

Then I sat back and made myself look at the thing straight.

A confession letter, describing a hit-and-run thirty years old. Postmarked the day after the victim's funeral — except the victim in the letter isn't Edmund Wren, is it? The victim is Ruth Calloway, a name I didn't know, and Edmund Wren is something else in this story, something with a bed to die warm in. And the letter-writer claims to be "the one who kept the books," which is a strange way to describe yourself unless keeping books is a crime, and it isn't, it never has been, except apparently in Ashford Falls in 1994 it might have been.

And my mother was there. Four minutes before the impact. Logged the pickup. Wrote down what the passenger said, word for word.

I caught myself doing the thing I do — the professional thing, the safe thing — narrating it as a puzzle instead of a wound. *Interesting anomaly in the historical record,* I told myself. *Worth cross-referencing.* Professional curiosity. That's what I'd have called it if anyone asked. A dispatcher's brain is a sorting machine: urgent, routine, urgent, routine, and this went into the urgent tray with a nice clean label on it so I wouldn't have to feel the label burning through the folder.

Because underneath the curiosity, quieter, there was this: my mother never mentioned it. Fourteen months of grieving her, twenty-six years of watching her run that desk, and never once — not over Sunday eggs, not over the radio headset, not in the eleven boxes of her belongings I still haven't finished going through — never once did she say the words *Miller's Bend* or *Ruth Calloway* or *there was a night in September I need you to know about.* Marion Eastwick told me everything. She told me about the time she cheated on a fare test in 1978. She told me about my father leaving with a granularity I did not request. She told me things about Dolores from payroll that I actively begged her to stop telling me.

She did not tell me this.

Either it didn't happen, or it happened and she carried it alone for thirty years, and I genuinely could not tell you which possibility frightened me more.

Dmitri's voice crackled in my ear at 3:40 — DJ delivered, heading back to the depot, anything else on the board? Nothing else on the board. I signed him off and the depot settled back into its two-note hum, and I laid the two pages flat under the desk lamp and read them a fourth time, slower, hunting for tells.

Typewritten. Old typewriter, maybe — the *e*s struck slightly high, the ink uneven, the kind of machine that predates correction tape or whose owner disdained it. No fingerprints of personality in the language, though. No ranting. Confession letters, true ones, tend to leak — guilt finds the seams. This one was dry as a receipt. Almost like it had been written to be read by police rather than by me. Almost like a document.

*Ask the ledger.*

I looked across the dispatch floor to the archive room, where the old records live. Green folders, bound ledgers, decades of Ashford Falls reduced to columns of fares and times in my mother's upright hand. The door was closed. It's always closed. Nobody goes in there; the town runs on apps now and history runs on nobody.

September 17, 1994. If the letter was telling the truth, there'd be an entry. 11:42 p.m., Corliss Road, a pickup, a passenger, a sentence written down word for word. One page in one ledger in one locked room, and either my mother's handwriting would confirm a stranger's confession or it wouldn't, and either answer changed what I knew about her forever.

The heater clicked back on. Somewhere outside, a dog was losing an argument with the dark.

I want to say I felt dread. What I actually felt was the specific itch of an unclosed case, which is my oldest and worst instinct — the Eastwick inheritance nobody put in a will. My mother used to say I couldn't sleep until every fare was reconciled, every loose end tied, and she said it fondly, but she also said it carefully, the way you handle a tool you know can cut.

"Tomorrow," I said out loud, to no one, the way night-shift people talk. "First light."

But it was 4:15, and first light was two hours off, and the archive room key was on the ring in my pocket, and the letter was sitting there saying *ask the ledger* in its dry dead voice, and I have never in my life successfully told myself *tomorrow* about anything.

I got up. I crossed the floor. My hand was on the archive door handle when the radio squawked — Dmitri, ten blocks out, and behind his voice, faintly, the sound of the depot's front door swinging shut against its spring, the particular *thunk* of it that I've heard ten thousand times and never once at 4:15 in the morning.

I stood in the dark with my hand on the cold handle, listening to a building that should have been empty decide whether it was.

* * *

Dmitri came in at 4:31 smelling of cigarettes he swears he doesn't smoke, and whatever I thought I heard was gone — door latched, mat empty, night intact. I logged him out, locked the cash box, and told myself the wind had done it. Wind, in a building where the door takes two hands and a grudge to open.

Then I unlocked the archive room anyway, because tomorrow is a lie night-shift workers tell each other, and I found the 1994 shelf exactly where it should be, spines numbered, dust undisturbed in the gray lamplight.

Except for one gap.

Volume nine — September through December — was missing from its place between eight and ten. Not misplaced. Missing, the way a tooth is missing: a clean absence with a shape, a slot worn smooth at the edges by thirty years of sitting there and then, recently, not.

I stood there holding the letter in one hand and the empty space in the other, and I understood two things with total clarity. First: somebody had been here before me, recently, quietly, and they had known exactly which book to take. Second: whoever they were, they hadn't counted on my mother — because Marion Eastwick kept duplicates of everything, and I knew, with the certainty of a daughter who inherited both the ledgers and the stubbornness, exactly where she'd have hidden the copy.

The letter crinkled in my grip. *Your mother kept the ledger honest.*

Outside, the sky over Ashford Falls was going the color of dishwater, and somewhere in it, the day shift was waking up to a town that still believed its own story.

I turned off the lamp, locked the archive room, and went to find out what my mother had been keeping honest — and from whom.
