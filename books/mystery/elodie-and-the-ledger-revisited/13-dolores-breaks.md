## 13. Dolores Breaks

The phone rang at 6:47 in the morning, which was the first wrong thing. Nobody called Dolores Marsh at 6:47 in the morning unless somebody had died, and everybody who could die on her had already done it.

She let it ring four times. Old habit. In thirty-one years of dispatching she'd learned that the calls you answered too fast were the ones you regretted. The ones you made wait had a way of losing their nerve.

"Marsh."

A voice like static with intentions. "You've been talking about 1994."

Dolores sat down on the arm of her recliner, because her knees had opinions and they were all cowardly. "I don't know what you—"

"You told Freya Eastwick to leave it alone. That's talking. That's a public service announcement with your fingerprints on it." A pause, long enough for Dolores to hear a clock ticking somewhere behind the voice, or maybe just the sound of a man enjoying himself. "Here's how this goes. You tell the girl everything, or I tell the chief what you did in October of 2001. At Miller's Bend. With the tow rope."

The line went dead before she could say anything, which was fine, because she hadn't had anything. Her mouth had gone dry as old chalk and her heart was doing that arrhythmic stumble it did when the weather turned, except the weather wasn't turning. It was a bright cold morning in November and a dead man's name was sitting in her kitchen like an uninvited guest who'd brought his own chair.

She put the kettle on anyway. Ritual. When the world tilted, Dolores Marsh made tea, and if the world kept tilting she added the good whiskey, and if it kept tilting after that she called nobody, because calling people was how you ended up in other people's problems, and at seventy-three she had earned the right to her own problems exclusively.

Except she'd never quite managed it, had she. She'd spent twenty years keeping other people's problems like a woman hoarding canned goods for a war that never got declared.

* * *

Freya Eastwick came to the door at noon, and Dolores knew before she opened it. Knew by the shadow through the frosted glass — straight-backed, patient, the posture of a woman who'd learned to wait out drunks and liars on a night shift and saw no reason to change technique for one old lady.

"I figured you'd come," Dolores said, opening the door. "Sit down. I'll make tea."

"I can't stay—"

"You can stay." Dolores said it firmly enough that Freya sat. "Whatever you came to ask me, I'm going to answer it, and answers take as long as they take. Sit."

The girl sat. She had Maren's chin — that stubborn shelf of bone — and Maren's way of looking at you like you were a ledger entry she couldn't quite reconcile. God, Maren. Twenty years dead now, and still showing up in other people's faces to ruin Dolores's mornings.

"You got a call," Freya said. Not a question.

"This morning. Early." Dolores poured the tea, hands steady enough, thank God for small mercies. "Man wouldn't give a name. Said I'd been talking about '94. Said if I didn't tell you everything, he'd tell Chief Corliss what happened in October of 2001."

Freya went very still. It was a dispatcher's stillness — Dolores recognized it, the way you go quiet and flat when the radio brings you something bad and there's work to do first and falling apart later. "What happened in October of 2001?"

Dolores looked into her tea. Then at the window, where her late husband's roses had gone brown and leggy because she couldn't bring herself to cut them back. Then back at the girl.

"You know Marcus Vale is supposed to be dead," she said. "Officially. Car accident, 2001, Miller's Bend, same stretch where—" She stopped. Waved a hand. "Same stretch. Body identified by dental records, buried in Fairlawn under a stone that says MARCUS VALE, BELOVED COLLEAGUE. I sent flowers every year for eleven years until my arthritis got too bad for the florist's stairs."

"But he isn't dead," Freya said quietly.

"No," Dolores said. "He isn't. And I'm the one who killed him. On paper, I mean. I'm the one who wrote him into the ground." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Thirty-one years dispatching cabs and the hardest fare I ever booked was a man into his own grave."

* * *

It had been Maren's idea. That was the thing Dolores needed the girl to understand, needed her to hear it plain, because Dolores had spent two decades letting the town believe she was the loyal one, the discreet one, and loyalty and cowardice look identical from the outside and only feel different from the inside.

October of 2001. Vale had shown up at Dolores's kitchen door at two in the morning, soaked through, gray-faced, looking like a man who'd been running since 1994 and had finally run out of road. He'd heard something — he never said what, not then — some whisper that the Wrens' fixer had finally gotten orders to close the last open account. The journalist's ghost had a witness attached, and witnesses had expiration dates.

"He sat at my table," Dolores said, "and drank my coffee, and shook so hard the cup chattered against the saucer like teeth. And he said, 'Dolores, I need to be dead.' Just like that. Like he was asking to borrow the car."

"And you called my mother."

"Maren called *me*." Dolores set down her cup. "Two days before. Out of nowhere — we hadn't spoken since the funeral, since she stood up at the wake and told everyone the family wanted privacy, which was Maren-speak for *everyone shut up*. She called and she said, 'Dolores, if a man ever comes to your door asking to disappear, you call me before you call anyone else.' Swear to God, those were her words. *Before anyone else.* Like she'd been waiting seven years for exactly that knock."

Freya's hand had stopped moving around her cup. "She knew he'd come."

"She knew *something* would come. Maren always knew things three steps ahead of the rest of us; it's why she was such a good dispatcher and such a terrible neighbor. So I called her, and she came over that night, and the two of them sat at my table working it out like they were planning a route." Dolores's voice caught on the word *route*, of all things, and she pushed through it. "Maren had it mostly built already. That's what I couldn't get over, girl. She didn't hesitate. She didn't say *are you sure* or *is this safe*. She pulled a pen out of her purse and started drawing on the back of an envelope. A car at Miller's Bend. A fire hot enough to compromise remains. Dental records — she knew a man at the county office, don't ask me how, Maren knew men everywhere, men who owed her. An unidentified body — and no, I never asked whose, and you can hate me for that, but there were plenty of unclaimed souls passing through that morgue in those days, and one more John Doe going into the ground under a real name wasn't going to wake anybody."

"The police report," Freya said. "Somebody had to write it."

"Somebody did." Dolores met her eyes. "Corliss. He was a sergeant then, not chief. He wrote it clean and closed it clean, and I always figured he did it because Maren asked him, and I always figured Maren could ask him because of whatever she had in that ledger of hers. Leverage, girl. Your mother dealt in leverage the way other women deal in coupons."

The silence stretched. Outside, a truck went past, gears grinding down the hill, and Dolores thought: thirty years of listening to engines, and every one of them sounds like someone leaving.

"She did it for me," Freya said at last, and it wasn't a question either, and Dolores hated gently, kindly, that the girl already knew.

"For you, maybe. For him, certainly. For herself—" Dolores shook her head. "Your mother carried '94 like a stone in her shoe for seven years. She kept the ledger honest, she used to say, like that was a religion instead of a job description. But honest ledgers don't sleep well. I think faking Vale's death was the only sin she committed that she thought of as a good deed. Everything else she did after — the silence, the walking away from it — that was for you. To keep you out of it. And I helped her, and I have regretted it every single day since, and I would do it again tomorrow, and that's the part I've never been able to make peace with. Both things true at once. Isn't that a hell of a thing."

"What did you actually do?" Freya asked. "In it, I mean."

"Tow rope and a gas can." Dolores said it flat, wanting it over. "Vale drove his own cab out to the bend at midnight — I rode along, God help me, I rode along like we were checking a fare dispute — and he set it burning at the guardrail, and we watched it go up, and he cried. Big man, cried like a boy. And then Maren took him away in her own car, and I walked home three miles in the dark because I couldn't stand to be in any vehicle that night, and I filed the paperwork Monday morning like nothing happened, and I collected his last paycheck and mailed it to a PO box in Cedar Falls, and that was the end of Marcus Vale." She looked up. "Until somebody decided he should rise from the dead and start writing letters."

Freya was quiet a long moment. Then: "The caller. He knew about the tow rope. He knew details."

"He knew everything," Dolores said. "Which means he was either there or he's read something that was written by someone who was. And there were only three of us at that bend, girl, and one of them is dead and one of them is driving a cab under a false name, so you tell me who's been reading your mother's papers."

Freya stood. She looked older than her years in that moment, Dolores thought — looked like Maren at the end, carrying weight in the shoulders.

"Dolores. The letter named my mother as an accomplice. To the hit-and-run. Did she—"

"No." Dolores said it hard, faster than she meant to. "No, girl, listen to me. Whatever that letter says, your mother witnessed nothing but the aftermath. She wasn't at that bend in '94. She found the ledger entry the next morning and she understood what it meant and she spent the rest of her life standing between that town and the truth, holding the door shut with her body. That's not accomplice. That's something else. Something I don't have a clean word for."

Freya nodded slowly, absorbing it, filing it. Then, at the door, she paused.

"The caller threatened to go to Corliss. Why would Corliss care? He covered it up himself."

And Dolores, who had spent thirty-one years knowing when a question was a trap, felt the floor tilt anyway.

"That's what scared me, girl," she said. "That's the part I keep turning over. If Corliss already knows, then threatening to tell him is no threat at all. Unless—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "Unless the caller isn't threatening to expose the secret. He's threatening to expose that *I know it*. There's a difference. One burns the secret. The other burns the witnesses."

Freya's face went pale in the doorway light.

"Whoever's cleaning house," Dolores said, "has started sweeping."
