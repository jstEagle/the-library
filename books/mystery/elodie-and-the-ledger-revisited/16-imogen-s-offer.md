## 16. Imogen's Offer

There is a particular kind of silence that money buys, and I grew up inside it. It isn't the silence of empty rooms — anyone can have that, and my father's study was never empty anyway; it hummed with the particular static of a man pretending to read the same page of the same report for forty years. No, the silence money buys is the silence of other people deciding, in real time, not to say things to you. You can hear it if you listen closely: the small click of a sentence being swallowed at a dinner party, the way the word *father* went soft in everyone's mouth after last week, like they were handling it with tongs.

I had been handling my own grief with tongs for seven days when I decided to invite Freya Eastwick to Wren House.

Not invited, exactly. Summoned is closer, though I flattered myself at the time that it was an invitation. There's a difference, and Freya would be the one to point it out to me later, in her flat dispatcher's voice, the voice of a woman who has spent twenty years telling strangers where their rides are and has no patience left over for euphemism.

*Come by the house on Thursday,* I'd said on the phone. *I'd like to talk about your mother.*

Which was true. And also a lie. The way most true things are, in this town.

* * *

Wren House sits on the hill above Miller's Bend like a headstone nobody wanted to carve. My great-grandfather built it out of quarry stone from land he acquired through methods our family history book describes as "enterprising" and which the county records describe, in the places where pages haven't gone missing, as something else entirely. As a child I used to lie on the lawn and imagine the house was a ship and I was its captain. Now I know better. Houses like this aren't ships. They're anchors. They hold you in one place while the water rises.

I dressed for the meeting the way you dress for a negotiation you intend to win but feel bad about winning: good enough to signal respect, casual enough to signal warmth. The grey silk blouse. Mother's pearls, which I wear now more often than she ever did, because dead women's jewelry doesn't judge you. I put coffee on even though I don't drink it before noon anymore — since the funeral I've been drinking it constantly, actually, which suggests something about my relationship to ritual that I'm choosing not to examine.

Freya arrived eleven minutes early, which told me two things: she was eager, and she didn't want to admit it. She stood in the foyer looking at the staircase the way people always do — the carved balustrade, the portrait of Great-Grandfather Aldous glaring down like a man who suspects the help of theft — and I watched her decide not to be impressed.

"You have a nice house," she said, in the tone of someone reading a weather report for a town they're leaving tomorrow.

"Thank you."

"I'm not here about the house."

"No," I said. "I know what you're here about."

And here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because whatever else happens — whatever comes of all this, whatever the investigators find in the ledgers and the land grants and the police files with their suspiciously tidy holes — I want it recorded that in that moment, standing in my own foyer offering coffee to a woman whose mother my family helped erase, I felt something real.

Grief does strange things to the conscience. Mine had spent the week of the funeral doing calisthenics. Every condolence card was a small accusation. Every casserole delivered by a neighbor who'd worked for Wren Holdings was a reminder that half this town ate because of us, and the other half of the town — well. The other half had names I'd only recently learned, names like Marcus Vale and Dolores and Eastwick, names that appeared in my father's papers in contexts that made my hands shake so badly I'd had to put down the brandy I wasn't drinking either.

I had read the confession letter forty times. Not the copy Freya got — mine came separately, three days after hers, which was itself a message, though I couldn't yet read the language it was written in. Someone knew things. Someone had decided the thirty-year dam had a crack in it, and they were dripping water through it one letter at a time, patient as erosion.

My father was dead. The letter-writer was still out there. And the woman standing in my foyer holding a coffee she hadn't asked for was the only person in Ashcombe who seemed to be actively pulling threads instead of just nervously watching them unravel.

* * *

We sat in the morning room, which sounds genteel and is, in fact, the coldest room in the house — south-facing, all glass, designed by some architect in the seventies who believed transparency was a moral virtue. My mother loved it. I've often thought that explained a great deal about my parents' marriage.

"I'll come to the point," I said.

"Please."

"The letter you received. The confession." I turned my cup, not drinking. "You've shown it to people. The police, maybe. Or you're planning to."

Freya looked at me for a long moment. She has one of those faces that gives away nothing except intelligence — everything else is filed somewhere behind the eyes, cross-referenced, timestamped. A dispatcher's face. Twenty years of listening to strangers' voices and knowing within four seconds whether they're lying about where they need to go.

"Why do you care?" she asked. "It's your father he confessed to killing."

"It's my father's name being dragged through it. And mine attached to his." I heard my own voice tighten and made myself loosen it. Vulnerability, Imogen. Show her something real. "Do you know what it's been like this week? Sitting shiva for a man while anonymous letters arrive suggesting he was a murderer? People look at you differently. The butcher gave me the wrong change yesterday and wouldn't meet my eye. That's nothing — that's *nothing* — compared to what's coming if this goes public. But it's a preview."

"And you'd rather it not go public."

"I'd rather control how it goes public. There's a difference."

"Is there?" she asked, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. She was genuinely asking. This woman had spent her life in a dispatch booth listening to drunk people and lost tourists and once, according to Dolores, a man reporting his own car stolen from himself, and she had emerged from all that with a sincerity I found almost physically painful. She meant the question. She really wanted to know whether there was a difference between controlling a story and burying it.

"There is to me," I said.

* * *

Here is what I had prepared. I had prepared it the night before, sitting up in my father's study with his files spread around me like tarot cards, and I want to be honest about the fact that I rehearsed it. Out loud. To the portrait of Aldous, who disapproved.

The offer had three parts, because all good offers have three parts:

One: money. Enough to matter. Not insultingly large — Freya Eastwick did not strike me as a woman who could be bought, only perhaps rented — but substantial. A consulting retainer. Legal-sounding. Money with paperwork, because dirty money offends her and clean money might not.

Two: information. Whatever I had in my father's files that pointed toward the letter-writer. Because I had convinced myself — God help me, I had *worked* at convincing myself — that whoever wrote those letters was the real threat. Blackmail, I called it, in the phone call, in my rehearsal, in the little speech I'd built. Someone extorting a grieving family. Freya and I, natural allies, both victims of the same anonymous hand.

Three: belonging. This was the part I believed in least and counted on most. Freya Eastwick had lived in Ashcombe her whole life and belonged to it the way a splinter belongs to a thumb — present, irritating, not part of the body. Her mother had been a dispatcher. Her mother had died alone. And I was going to sit in my glass room and offer her a seat at the table of the family that had, per the letters, destroyed a man on the road below my house, and I was going to make the offer sound like rescue.

I rehearsed the belonging part twice as long as the money part. That should have told me something. When you have to practice sincerity, it's because you're borrowing it.

* * *

"Your mother kept books," I said. "For Halcyon Cabs. Thirty years of them, I understand. Meticulous."

Something moved behind Freya's eyes. "Everyone keeps telling me my mother was meticulous lately. It's starting to sound like a warning."

"Maybe it is." I set down the cup. "Ms. Eastwick. Freya. May I call you Freya?"

"You may attempt it."

A flicker of humor. Good. Humor means a person is still negotiating with themselves. "Whoever wrote that letter knows things about 1994 that only a handful of people could know. Names, times, routes. They know about the ledger. And I think—" I paused, and here I let the truth leak through, deliberately, a controlled spill, "—I think they know about your mother's part in it. Which makes her either their ally or their target, and either way, Freya, it makes *you* the person standing closest to the fire."

"That's very dramatic."

"I'm a Wren. Drama is the family business. Land was the other one."

She almost smiled. Then didn't. "What are you offering?"

Straight to it. I admired that and resented it in equal measure, the way you resent anyone who refuses to let you take the scenic route to the thing you're ashamed of.

"A retainer. Substantial. For your discretion and your assistance identifying the letter-writer." I slid the envelope across the glass table — I had prepared the number on cream stationery, because my father taught me that numbers written on cheap paper look negotiable. "Plus access to my father's files. He kept... extensive records. If there's a name in them that helps you find who's sending these letters, it's yours."

Freya didn't touch the envelope. She looked at it the way you'd look at a snake that had gotten into the house — respectfully, from a distance, calculating exits.

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, when you find them, you bring what they've sent you to me first. Before the police. Before the paper. We assess it together."

"Together," she repeated.

"I'm offering you a place in this, Freya. Not a payout. A partnership." And then, because the rehearsal demanded it, the line I'd practiced to Aldous's disapproving portrait: "You've been outside this town your whole life. Looking in. I'm offering you a window that opens."

* * *

I want to describe her face at that moment, because I've thought about it every day since, and every day I see something different in it.

That day, I saw temptation. I'm fairly sure I saw temptation. She looked at the envelope, and then at the windows — the whole gray valley laid out below, the bend in the river, the road where, if the letters were true, a journalist named Thomas Reeve had died under the wheels of a car my father's fixer drove — and something in her expression softened, or loosened, or lowered its guard. Loneliness will do that. I recognized it because I've been fluent in loneliness since I was six years old and understood that my friends' parents smiled at me the way employees smile at the boss's daughter.

"You think I want to belong to this," she said quietly.

"I think everyone wants to belong somewhere."

"My mother belonged here her whole life." She picked up the envelope now — finally — and weighed it in her hand without opening it. "Thirty years behind that dispatch desk. She knew every street, every driver, every fare. And do you know what I found in her apartment after she died? Nothing. No photos of her with friends. No letters. A woman who spent her whole life connecting people to each other, and nobody connected back." She set the envelope down again, untouched. "So no. I don't want a window into your house, Ms. Wren. I've seen what your house does to the people who live in it. Your father sat up there for forty years and died with a confession letter in the mail. That's not belonging. That's siege."

The words landed exactly where she intended them, which is to say: in the wound.

"He didn't write that letter," I said. "He received it. He was a victim too."

"Was he?"

"Freya—"

"Somebody killed a man on that road in 1994. Somebody covered it up. Somebody kept paying for the cover-up long enough that it became infrastructure — roads named after your family, a police department on your payroll, a whole town eating from your hand. Your father wasn't a victim of that letter. He was the *address* it was mailed to." She stood. She took her coat off the chair back with unhurried precision, and I understood that the offer was already dead, had probably been dead since the foyer, and that she had stayed this long only to give me the courtesy of hearing the verdict in full.

"Keep your retainer," she said. "But I'll take the files."

I blinked. "The—"

"Your father's records. You offered access. I accept that part." At the door she paused, and for one moment the dispatcher's mask slipped and I saw underneath it something that frightened me more than any threat could have: pity. "Imogen. Whoever is writing these letters — I don't think they're blackmailing you. I think they're finishing something. And I think when I find out who they are, you're going to wish you'd never met me."

Then she was gone, and the glass room filled back up with its purchased silence, and I sat alone with my mother's pearls and my father's envelope and the specific, exquisite humiliation of having misjudged absolutely everything.

* * *

That evening I went into the study and opened the files I had promised her, intending to box them up, to honor the bargain even in defeat — a Wren honors her bargains; it's the only religion we've kept.

Instead I found the folder at the bottom of the drawer. The thin one, unlabeled, the one I'd been avoiding for a week the way you avoid the top step that creaks.

Inside: a photograph of a young woman I didn't recognize, standing in front of Halcyon Cabs, squinting into the sun. On the back, in my father's handwriting, a single word: *Handled.*

And beneath it, a carbon copy of a payment authorization dated March 1994 — the month of the accident — signed not by my father.

Signed by Chief Corliss, twelve years before he became chief.

I sat in my father's chair until the light went out of the valley, holding the page, understanding at last that the letter-writer hadn't been blackmailing my family at all.

They'd been keeping receipts. And they'd just handed me one.
