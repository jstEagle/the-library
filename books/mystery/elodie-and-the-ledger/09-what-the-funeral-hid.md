## 9. What the Funeral Hid

The undertaker's name was Cyril Pargetter, and according to the parish records he had buried half of Saint Aldhelm's dead between 1978 and 2014 — which meant that if anyone knew what had gone into Elodie Vane's coffin eleven years ago, it was him.

Finding him took Hazel two days and one humiliating conversation with a woman at the funeral directors' registry who kept calling him "love" and asking if he was sure he didn't want a quote for himself while he was at it. ("Pre-planning is very sensible at your age," she said. Hazel was forty-one.) But eventually the trail ended at a bungalow on Fenwick Lane with a garden so immaculate it looked apologetic about it, and a brass door knocker shaped like a lily.

Cyril Pargetter opened the door before Hazel finished knocking, which suggested either excellent hearing or a man who had spent forty years anticipating what people wanted before they knew it themselves.

"You'll be the archivist," he said. "Mrs. Okonkwo rang me Tuesday. Said you'd come sniffing."

"Did she." Hazel filed that away. The verger had warned Pargetter of his approach — which could mean loyalty to Elodie's memory, or something less comfortable.

"She said you were thorough and polite and not to tell you anything I'd regret." Pargetter stepped back from the doorway. "Come in anyway. I've been expecting this visit for eleven years, and it's rude to keep a man waiting on his own doorstep."

* * *

The sitting room smelled of bergamot and furniture polish. On the mantelpiece stood a row of small framed photographs — funerals, Hazel realized, decades of them, each one a service Pargetter had evidently been proud of. A life measured in other people's endings, arranged in order of significance.

"Tea?" Pargetter asked, already pouring. "I find people talk better with something warm in their hands. Occupational habit. Grief makes people fidget."

Hazel accepted the cup. It was heavy, good china, and far too hot to drink, which he suspected was the point — something to hold instead of saying the first thing that came into your head.

"Mr. Pargetter—"

"Cyril. Everyone calls me Cyril. Well. Everyone who isn't arranging a funeral calls me Mr. Pargetter. There's a distinction."

"Cyril, then. I'm trying to understand what happened at Elodie Vane's funeral. You conducted it?"

"I did." Pargetter settled into an armchair across from him with the unhurried gravity of a man who had sat through ten thousand of these conversations and intended to enjoy this one at his own pace. "Lovely service, as I recall. Canon Merrick officiated. Full church — she was well liked, the ledger-keeper. Quiet, but well liked. People notice the quiet ones once they're gone."

"Who arranged the funeral?"

Pargetter's eyes, pale and steady as a winter sky, came up from his tea. "Now that's the right question. Most people would have asked *who died*, and I'd have said Miss Vane, and we'd have both nodded, and you'd have gone away none the wiser. You asked who *arranged* it." He set down his cup. "Canon Merrick arranged it. Personally. Came to my office the day after she disappeared — disappeared, mind you, when there was still every reason to hope — and gave me instructions."

"Instructions for a funeral for a woman who hadn't been found?"

"For a coffin," Pargetter corrected gently. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. He told me the family had identified remains." He paused. "I should say here that I checked. Of course I checked. Forty years in the trade teaches you that families in shock will sign anything, but they don't usually identify remains that haven't surfaced. So I asked. Politely. And Canon Merrick produced a death certificate."

Hazel felt the room tilt slightly, the way it always did when a case shifted its weight. "You saw the certificate."

"I saw it. Signed by Dr. Ellery — retired now, moved to Whitstable, deaf as a post, God rest him eventually. Cause of death listed as drowning. Date of death the fourteenth of March." Pargetter recited it without looking at any notes, and Hazel understood that he had carried those details around for eleven years like stones in a coat pocket. "And the signature at the bottom authorizing the burial — the next-of-kin line, or rather the *no*-next-of-kin line — that was Canon Merrick's own hand. He signed as executor of her estate."

"There was no estate."

"No," Pargetter agreed. "There wasn't. Which struck me as odd at the time, because executors are appointed for estates, and empty pockets don't need administering. But I was sixty-eight years old, my wife had died the spring before, and Canon Merrick was a man of the cloth telling me a sad story in a soft voice. When a man like that tells you a thing kindly enough, you want it to be true. That's how clergy get away with murder, if they ever do get away with murder. Not cleverness. Kindness."

"You're saying it was murder?"

"I'm saying nothing of the sort." Pargetter held up a hand. "I'm saying the coffin was light. That's all I ever said, and it's why I retired early, and it's why Mrs. Okonkwo's phone call on Tuesday made me put out the good china."

Hazel's mouth had gone dry despite the tea. "Light."

"Miss Vane was a small woman, but not that small. Six pallbearers, and four of them doing the work of six. I noticed. You notice everything in my trade — the weight of a box tells you more than the face of a mourner ever will. Mourners perform. Coffins don't." He looked at the photographs on the mantel, at some middle distance beyond them. "I mentioned it to Merrick afterward. Just a word, man to man, over the sherry he insisted on buying me. And he said—" Pargetter's voice changed, taking on another man's cadence, warmer and smoother than his own, "—'the sea gives back what it likes, Cyril, and keeps what it doesn't. We bury what we're given.' And then he smiled, and I thought: either this man believes every word he's saying, or he's rehearsed it. And I decided I didn't want to know which."

"What did you do?"

"What does any old man do with a stone he can't lift and can't put down? He carries it." Pargetter spread his hands. "I sealed the coffin myself. My instruction, my hands, my seal. If anyone had ever asked me under oath what went into that grave, I'd have had to perjure myself or ruin a canon of the Church of England, and I couldn't do either, so I made sure nobody asked. No inquest was ever held — certificate said accidental drowning, body recovered, all tidy. Tidier than any drowning I ever dealt with, actually. Real drownings are paperwork nightmares. This one was spotless. Spotless things worry me more than messy ones."

"There was no grave," Hazel said quietly. "I checked. No plot in the parish register, no interment record."

"There's a memorial plaque in the north transept," Pargetter said. "Paid anonymously, I heard. Anonymous payments to cathedrals aren't anonymous, you know. Somebody writes a cheque, and cheques have names on them, even if the name gets politely ignored." He fixed Hazel with a look. "You'll have found that already, though. You strike me as a man who finds things. The question is whether you've found the right things, or just all of them."

* * *

They sat for a while after that, the tea going cold between them, and Hazel let the silence do its work. He had learned long ago that witnesses filled silence the way water fills a hole — you only had to make the hole deep enough and wait.

It was Pargetter who broke first.

"I kept something," he said. "Eleven years, I kept it, telling myself it was professional habit. Record-keeping. But it wasn't habit. It was insurance. A man buries an empty box, he wants proof someday that he didn't imagine it." He rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and went to a bureau in the corner — oak, Victorian, the kind of furniture that outlives its owners on purpose. From a drawer lined in green felt he took a flat envelope, yellowed at the edges, sealed with tape that had aged to the color of weak tea.

He laid it on the table between them but kept his hand on it.

"Copy of the burial authorization. I signed the original over to the diocesan office, standard practice. But I photocopied it first. Old habit — I copy everything. My wife used to say I'd photocopy my own funeral if I could." His mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "Look at the signature line."

Hazel opened the envelope with the care of a man handling something that might go off. Inside was a single sheet, the photocopy grainy but legible: the standard diocesan form, INTERMENT AUTHORIZATION, the particulars filled in a neat, confident hand. *Elodie Mary Vane. Drowned. Interred in consecrated ground, Saint Aldhelm's, 21st March.*

And at the bottom, the signature.

Hazel had spent three weeks — nearly — inside the parish ledgers of Saint Aldhelm's. Three weeks of marginal codes and shifted dates and payments that appeared twice under different names. Three weeks of learning a hand the way you learn a face: not its letters but its habits, the way it leaned, where it pressed hard and where it hurried.

The signature read *A. Merrick*. But that wasn't what stopped Hazel's breath.

It was the small mark after the final letter — a tiny downward tick, almost invisible, the kind of flourish a pen makes when a hand lifts too quickly. He had seen that tick forty times in the ledgers. Beside the name *T. Vane* on the page where a bequest dissolved into nothing. Beside dates that had been nudged backward by days. Beside sums entered twice, once real and once invented.

Merrick didn't just sign the burial authorization. Merrick's hand was all over the forgery — the same hand, the same pressure, the same impatient little tick, like a man tapping his foot while he rewrote other people's lives.

"He signed it," Hazel said. His voice sounded strange to him, very calm, very far away. "In his own hand. He authorized the burial of a coffin he knew was empty, in writing, and left the paper in the diocesan archive for eleven years."

"He was never worried," Pargetter said. "That's the thing I want you to understand, because I think you're a young-ish man and you'll want to believe he was clever, covering tracks, sweating. He wasn't. He signed his name like a man signing a Christmas card. Because he believed — and maybe he was right — that nobody would ever look. Who audits a funeral? Who counts the dead?" He shook his head slowly. "The living count money. The dead are assumed. That's the bargain he made, see. With himself, with God, with whoever else needed making it with. He buried a story, and stories stay buried if everyone agrees to stop digging."

"And you agreed."

"For eleven years." Pargetter met his eyes without flinching. "I'm an old man who wanted to die in peace, and peace seemed worth one lie. Then my wife died — properly, I mean, I did that funeral myself and the coffin was the right weight, I checked, God help me, I checked — and I started thinking about what she'd have said. She'd have said, 'Cyril, you put an empty box in holy ground and called it a woman, and that woman might be anywhere, including alive, and you helped tuck her in.'" He tapped the table once, softly. "So. There's your paper. Do what you like with it. But hear an old undertaker out first."

Hazel waited.

"That signature convicts him of signing a form. That's all. A lawyer will yawn at it. What you need is the chain — the certificate, the coffin, the money, the *why*. And the why—" Pargetter's voice dropped, and for the first time the old man looked genuinely afraid, not of Merrick, Hazel thought, but of the shape of the thing itself, "—the why is standing somewhere breathing, or it isn't, and either answer frightens me. If she's dead, someone killed her and dressed it as drowning. If she's alive, then she let a whole parish mourn her, let that poor ward of hers grieve like an orphan, and chose it — chose the empty box, chose the lie — because the truth was worse than dying." He pushed the envelope an inch toward Hazel. "Either way, Mr. Brightwater, somebody made a bargain in that crypt of yours, and bargains like that don't stay quiet forever. They sit. They sit like a coffin that weighs too little. And then one day somebody picks them up and notices."

* * *

Hazel walked back across town as the light went long and amber, the envelope inside his coat, against his chest, like a second heartbeat.

He should take it to the police. He knew that. A forged death, a false burial, a canon's signature — this was past the reach of archivists and into the country of magistrates. And yet his feet carried him, instead, along the familiar route to the cathedral, where the evening bell was ringing and the windows were going gold, and where somewhere in the ledgers' margins that small impatient tick was waiting for him, forty times over, patient as a buried thing.

At the west door he stopped. Mrs. Okonkwo was locking up for the night, keys chiming, and she looked at him with an expression he couldn't read — sympathy, warning, or something older than both.

"You saw Cyril," she said. Not a question.

"I did."

She nodded slowly, and her eyes went past him, up the nave, toward the north transept where an anonymous plaque bore a name that belonged to no grave.

"Then you know the coffin was empty," she said quietly. "And now, Mr. Brightwater, you have to decide what you think that means. Because there's only one person in this world who can tell you — and she's very good at being dead."

The last bell faded. Somewhere below the floor, beneath the flagstones and the dust and eleven years of careful silence, the crypt waited with its locked annex and its missing volume, and Hazel understood suddenly, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with heights, that the handwriting in the second diary hadn't reminded him of his own by accident.

Elodie Vane had learned to write like the person she trusted most.

And he had never met her at all.
