## 19. Counting Backward

Hazel Brightwater had always believed that truth, like dust, settled in layers — and that if you brushed it away carefully enough, in the right order, you could read the whole history of a room in the strata. He had spent thirty years reading rooms. Now, at three o'clock in the morning in his flat above the antiquarian bookshop, he read a table.

The table was covered.

It was covered the way a battlefield is covered, if the armies had fought with ledger pages, photocopied receipts, a legal pad, three colours of string, and a mug of tea that had gone cold sometime around midnight and now wore a skin like a pond in November. Hazel stood over it in his dressing gown, glasses pushed up into his hair, and looked at the thing he had built, and felt — for the first time in eleven days — something that was not dread.

It was hope. Methodical hope. The best kind. The kind you could audit.

"Right," he said to the empty room, because he had long ago stopped being embarrassed about narrating his own deductions to the furniture. The furniture had never once interrupted him, which was more than could be said for Canon Merrick. "Let's count backward."

He sat down, pulled the legal pad toward him, and wrote at the top of a fresh page, in his small, exact, archivist's hand:

**What we know. What we can prove. What we only believe.**

Three columns. The discipline of it steadied him. Anyone could have a theory; theories were cheap, theories were what got innocent people hanged and guilty people canonised. What Hazel had was a chain, and a chain was only as good as its weakest link — so he would test every link, one at a time, the way you test ice before you trust it with your weight.

He began at the beginning, which in a forgery case was never the forgery itself. It was the money.

* * *

The parish ledgers of Saint Aldhelm's ran back to 1887, and Hazel had now read the relevant volumes four times. Not skimmed. Read. The way Elodie Vane had read them, presumably, before she'd taught herself to hide her findings in the margins the way other people hid letters in books.

The marginal marks were the key, and he had Tomas to thank for the key to the key.

*Two strokes beside a name: a payment received. One stroke with a hook: a payment made and disguised. A dot in the upper margin: a date moved. Two dots: a name changed on a document. And when a mark was enclosed in a small circle —* Tomas had gone very quiet when he'd explained this one, turning his big stonemason's hands over on his knees — *that meant* **do not let this person see this entry**.

Elodie had built herself a private language out of bookkeeping, and she had used it for years, right under everyone's nose, in a ledger that anyone in the parish was technically entitled to consult. She had hidden her discoveries in the one place she knew would never be searched: the open record.

Hazel laid out the pages now, in chronological order, and read the story the margins told.

March, eleven years ago. A dot in the upper margin beside the entry for the Vane bequest — the trust established by Elodie's late uncle for the benefit of his ward, Tomas. The date of the trust's execution had been *moved*. Moved backward by nine days, so that it appeared to predate a codicil that had, in truth, come first.

He had the original codicil. He had found it in the diocesan registry, misfiled — misfiled, he now understood, deliberately — under a year that didn't apply, in a box that hadn't been opened since. The codicil was unambiguous. The trust was irrevocable. Tomas Vane was the beneficiary, full stop, in his uncle's own hand, witnessed by two people whose signatures Hazel had verified against the electoral rolls. One witness was dead. The other, Mrs. Okonkwo, had confirmed it over tea in her little flat, her hands wrapped around a mug she never drank from: *"That's my signature. I watched Mr. Vane sign it. I watched Elodie watch me watching. She missed nothing, that girl."*

So: the codicil was genuine, and it came first. The later document — the one that had gutted the trust and redirected its income to a discretionary fund *administered by the office of Canon Merrick* — was the forgery.

That was the skeleton. Now for the flesh.

* * *

Hazel turned to the second column of his pad: **What we can prove.**

The forged instrument itself, first. He had spent an entire afternoon with it under his desk lamp, and it had confessed everything. The ink was right — Merrick had been careful, or had hired someone careful — but the *paper* was wrong. Not catastrophically wrong; wrong the way a forged banknote is wrong, in ways you feel before you can name them. The watermark of the stationer who supplied the diocese had changed suppliers in the intervening year. The forged document, dated before the change, was written on paper bearing the *new* watermark. Hazel had the stationer's invoices to prove it. The stationer, a nervous man named Pellow who kept his shop the way other people kept their consciences, had needed only one look at the invoice numbers before going grey and saying, "I'll write it down, shall I? Signed? You'll want it signed."

People, Hazel had learned, wanted very much to write things down and sign them, provided you asked in the voice of a man who already knew the answer.

Second: the substitution of the administering office. The forged instrument had not simply redirected money — it had rewritten the *mechanism*, and the mechanism it chose was Merrick's own office, at a time when Merrick had held that office for only four months. A genuine instrument drafted by Elodie's uncle would have named the office as it stood. The forgery named the office as it would *conveniently* become. Forgers always reached for the present tense of their own advantage. It was their one universal tell, and Merrick — meticulous in ink, careless in time — had reached for it.

Third, and this was the link Hazel had spent two days verifying because it was the link that turned suspicion into architecture: the payments. The discretionary fund, once established, had disbursed money in small, regular amounts, recorded in the ledger with the two-stroke mark Elodie had circled eleven times over three years. The disbursements had no stated purpose — *contingencies*, the ledger said, *parish contingencies* — and they had stopped, precisely and permanently, in the month Elodie Vane disappeared.

Hazel had traced the bank routing. The contingencies had gone to a private account. The account had been closed the same week. And the closure authorisation — obtained, after some gentle persuasion, from a bank clerk who owed Hazel a favour dating back to a rare-books appraisal — bore a signature.

He set the photocopy of the signature on the table, next to the empty-coffin receipt, next to the death certificate, and looked at the three of them together for a long moment.

The same hand. Of course it was the same hand. Canon Alistair Merrick signed his name the way he did everything else — with a flourish on the final letter that was just a little too pleased with itself, the *k* kicking out like a heel. The receipt from the undertaker, authorising the sealing of a coffin that the undertaker, Mr. Hobbes, had sworn on his retirement and again over a great deal of sherry was *light as a bread basket, Mr. Brightwater, I sealed it and I knew, I knew, but he was a canon and I was a man with a mortgage*. The death certificate, witnessed by a signature Merrick had presumably assumed no archivist would ever cross-reference against a ledger.

Eleven years ago, Merrick had not committed one crime. He had committed a *set*. A forged instrument to take the money. An empty coffin to close the question of the inconvenient ledger-keeper. A certificate to make the coffin official. Each piece covering the next, each signature a stitch in a shroud — and Elodie Vane, alive and watching from the dark, had stitched her own counter-record into the margins of the parish books, one circled mark at a time, waiting eleven years for someone to read it.

*She buried the evidence in the one place he'd never look,* Hazel thought. *In the accounts. Because to a man like Merrick, the accounts are furniture. They're the thing that's supposed to be true.*

He picked up his pen and wrote, in the third column — **What we only believe** — a single line, and then, after a moment, crossed it out. *That Merrick tried to have Elodie killed.* He couldn't prove it. Elodie herself had never claimed it; she had said only that she *understood what would happen to a woman alone who made trouble*, and that she had chosen to die on paper rather than find out. The absence of a body, the emptiness of the coffin — these were consistent with a bribe, with a warning, with a mercy Hobbes had never been told he was performing. Hazel left the line crossed out. A chain was only as strong as what you left out of it.

What remained was enough. More than enough. The forged instrument, the paper evidence, the codicil, the witness, the disbursement trail, the closed account, the signature on the coffin receipt, the undertaker's sworn statement, and — the crown of it, the link he had fitted only that evening — the diary volume from the restricted shelf, in Elodie's hand, describing the forgery as it happened, dated, detailed, and naming the canon in her own careful cipher that Tomas had decoded at Hazel's kitchen table with shaking fingers and tears he didn't apologise for.

The chain was complete.

Hazel sat back and looked at it all — the whole architecture of eleven years of silence, laid out on a table in a flat that smelled of cold tea and lamp oil — and he allowed himself, briefly, the pleasure of it. The pure, professional pleasure of a thing *finished*. Every archivist knew that pleasure; it was the reason any of them did the work. Somewhere under the dust, the truth had been waiting in order, and he had found the order.

Then he looked at the window, at the grey beginning of a November dawn, and the pleasure curdled into the other thing. The practical thing.

The chain was complete. But it was complete *in this room*.

* * *

He made fresh tea and thought about the problem like the archivist he was, because the problem was, at bottom, an archiving problem. He had assembled a record. Records existed to be preserved and produced. And every instinct he had — every scar from his precarious employment history, which Merrick had so kindly reminded him of — said the same thing: *a record kept in one place is a record that can be lost.*

Merrick had already suspended his commission. Merrick had already changed the crypt lock. Merrick had already delivered his little speech about Hazel's references, about how difficult it was for a man of a certain age and a certain reputation for *difficultness* to find placement, delivered with such exquisite politeness that it had taken Hazel a full minute to realise he was being threatened. The message had been clear: stop, or be erased too.

And Hazel had smiled and thanked him for his candour and gone home and started counting.

But Merrick was not a stupid man. A stupid man's forgeries would have been crude. Merrick would know, by now, that Hazel had not stopped. He would know it the way you know weather is coming. And a man who had once made a woman vanish so completely that the whole parish attended her funeral would not hesitate to make a set of documents vanish — along with, if it came to it, the archivist who was foolish enough to keep them all in one flat above a bookshop with a back stair that anyone could climb.

Hazel got up and checked the back stair lock anyway. Then, feeling slightly foolish, checked it again.

No. The answer was not better locks. The answer was *distribution*. You did not preserve a record by hiding it; you preserved it by making it plural. He spent the next hour making it plural.

The undertaker's sworn statement: copied, the original deposited with his solicitor, a second copy posted to Mrs. Okonkwo with instructions to keep it somewhere Merrick would never think to look — her hymnal, perhaps, given the state of her devotional life. The stationer's signed invoice: same treatment. The bank authorisation: same. The diary volume itself — that he did not copy, because a copy of a diary was a violation he couldn't stomach, and because the original, in Elodie's hand, was evidence in a way no copy could be. But he photographed every page with the old camera he used for cataloguing, and the film went into an envelope addressed to his solicitor, to be opened only on his instruction or his disappearance, whichever came first.

He wrote the instruction out plainly, because he had learned from Elodie that plainness was its own kind of courage: *If I am unable to account for myself, take this to the heritage inspectors and to the police. The key to the cipher is Tomas Vane.*

There. Plural. Distributed. A truth that now lived in five places and could not be killed by locking one door.

Which left the last problem, the one he had been circling all night the way a moth circles a bulb.

The chain was complete, but a chain in a private flat was just jewellery. To matter — to free Tomas, to convict Merrick, to give Elodie back her name without taking her freedom — it had to be produced *in the open*, before people who could not be threatened, at a moment when Merrick could not make it quietly go away. And Hazel knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent eleven days learning how Merrick thought, that if he simply walked the documents to the police, they would travel slowly, through channels, with warnings sent ahead — and somewhere in those channels, papers would be delayed, misfiled, lost. Merrick had misfiled a codicil for eleven years. He was very good at misfiling.

No. It had to be *witnessed*. It had to happen in a room Merrick could not control, at a moment Merrick could not predict, with eyes on him when he understood what was happening — because Merrick's danger, Hazel had come to believe, was greatest precisely when he was cornered, and a cornered man must be cornered in front of witnesses or not at all.

And there was only one room in Saint Aldhelm's that Merrick could not close, one gathering he could not cancel, one date circled on every calendar in the diocese.

Hazel looked at the letter he had received that morning, the embossed one, the one he had set aside unread while he worked because he had known, in the way you know a bill is bad news, what it would say. He opened it now. *The heritage inspection,* it announced, in Merrick's own gracious hand, *will proceed Thursday next. The Canon looks forward to demonstrating the cathedral's exemplary stewardship of its records and its dead.*

*Its records,* Hazel thought, *and its dead.* The vanity of the man. He was going to walk the inspectors through the crypt himself. He was going to stand beside the empty niche and the memorial plaque and speak movingly about Elodie Vane, the poor lost ledger-keeper, while Hazel stood in the corner with a complete chain of evidence in his briefcase.

It was almost too perfect. It was the kind of trap that set itself.

Hazel took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote two notes. The first, to the lead inspector, a woman named Dr. Ferris whose reputation for incorruptibility was the only reputation in the diocese longer than Merrick's: a request for a private word before the formal tour, *regarding the provenance of certain records*, signed with his title and nothing else. The second note was shorter, and it was not addressed to anyone at the diocese.

It was written in the marginal code — two strokes, a hooked stroke, a circled dot — and it said, in the language Elodie had built out of bookkeeping: *Thursday. The crypt. Bring your ledger. I am bringing mine.*

He folded both notes. He looked out the window at the dawn coming up over the rooftops, grey going to pearl, and he felt the methodical hope settle into something firmer, something with a date attached.

Then the telephone rang.

At five in the morning, the telephone in Hazel's flat had never once rung in eleven years of residence. He looked at it the way you look at a spider on the ceiling. It rang again.

He picked it up, and said his name, and there was a pause on the line — a long, careful pause — and then a voice he knew, low and roughened by sleep and by something worse than sleep, said:

"Mr. Brightwater. It's Tomas. She's gone. Elodie — her room in the annex is empty, and the candle's still burning, and there's a note in the margin code that says only one thing." A breath. "It says *he knows. Thursday is a trap. It was always going to be.*"

Hazel stood very still, the two folded notes in his hand, the dawn coming up over the city, and heard himself say: "Then we change the date. Tomas — we change it to tonight."
