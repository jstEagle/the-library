## 1. The Returned Book

The bell above the door of Thornbury's Books had not rung in eleven days, and Lior Thornbury had been counting.

Not obsessively. He wanted that understood, if anyone had asked — which no one did, because the only people who came into Thornbury's Books were the postman, who didn't count as a customer so much as a weather event, and Mrs. Petch from the bakery, who came in every other Thursday to buy whatever romance novel had the most shirtless men on the cover and to complain about the price of everything, including, once memorably, the concept of Tuesdays. Eleven days without even Mrs. Petch was unusual, but October in Ashmoor was like that. The rain came sideways off the moor, the tourists evaporated, and the town folded itself up like a deckchair at the end of summer.

So when the bell rang on a grey Tuesday afternoon — a Tuesday, Mrs. Petch would have noted with vindication — Lior looked up from his ledger with the mild surprise of a man interrupted mid-prayer.

The young man in the doorway was pale in the way of things kept out of sunlight: pale hands, pale face, hair the color of wet sand. He stood there dripping slightly, though it wasn't raining hard enough to justify it, holding something wrapped in a tea towel against his chest the way you'd carry an injured bird.

"Are you open?" he asked, in the tone of someone who hoped the answer was no.

Lior gestured at the room around them — the sagging armchairs, the shelves organized according to a system only he understood and barely defended, the cat-shaped hole in the dust where Bramble used to sleep before Bramble had gone on to whatever great library cats go to. "Debatable," he said. "But I'm here."

The young man came forward carefully, as if the floor might be load-bearing in ways he hadn't been warned about. Up close he was younger than he'd first appeared — early twenties, maybe — with the particular rawness around the eyes of someone recently bereaved. Lior knew that look. He'd worn it himself for a while, some years back, before he'd learned to file grief under *miscellaneous*.

"I found this." The young man set the tea towel bundle on the counter and unwrapped it with the exaggerated care of the unpracticed. "In my gran's attic. She died in August. Ruth Ellery — she used to run the inn, out by the crossroads? Anyway. I've been clearing the house, and there was a box, and this was under a lot of Christmas decorations and a taxidermied stoat, which — sorry, that's not relevant."

"It's a good detail, though," Lior said. "Stoats don't come up enough in conversation."

That earned him half a smile, quickly extinguished. The young man pushed the bundle across the counter.

It was a book. A diary, Lior saw immediately — the cheap kind sold in stationery shops, vinyl-covered, with one of those elastic bands that had long since given up its ambitions. Water damage had bloomed across the cover in continents of brown and grey, and the spine was soft as old bread. Someone had loved it once, or at least carried it often; the corners were rubbed round and silvered.

"We're not really a buying shop," Lior said gently. "I mean, I'll take donations for the charity shelf, but—"

"You donated it," the young man said. "Or — someone did. There was a sticker on the box. Thornbury's Books, donation, 1994. My gran must have bought it at your jumble sale, or taken it home by mistake, or—" He stopped, ran a hand through his damp hair. "I'm sorry. I'm doing this badly. I started clearing her house because I thought it would be simple, and nothing in that attic is simple, and I just want it to be somebody else's problem. Somebody who knows about books."

Lior looked down at the diary, and something cold walked up the back of his neck, quiet as a spider.

He opened the cover.

The flyleaf had a name written in blue ink, in a hand that slanted forward eagerly, letters leaning into the future: *Maren Voss.* Below it, smaller, almost shy: *Property of me, obviously.*

The shop went very still. Or perhaps it was Lior who went still — the distinction had never mattered much to him. Outside, the rain ticked against the window like a polite but persistent visitor. Somewhere down the street, the bakery's extractor fan hummed its eternal note. And inside Lior Thornbury's chest, behind a door he had boarded shut with considerable skill and thirty years of practice, something knocked.

"Maren Voss," he said aloud, to test whether saying it changed anything.

It did not.

"Yeah," the young man said. "I looked her up. That's why I brought it here instead of just binning it. She's — she's the missing woman, isn't she? From ages ago. They did a thing about it in the local paper last year, one of those 'thirty years on' pieces. Thirty years this autumn, actually." He swallowed. "My gran kept her diary in her attic for thirty years and never said a word about it. To anyone. I checked with my mum."

Lior closed the diary. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Hands were the last part of a detective to retire, he'd found. Everything else went soft and forgetful, but the hands remembered how to hold a thing like this — lightly, by the edges, as if it might still be evidence.

"What was your grandmother's name again?" he asked, though he'd heard it perfectly.

"Ruth Ellery. She ran the Drovers' Rest until it closed. Everyone said she was the best innkeeper the town ever had." The young man hesitated. "She was also the most private person I ever met. You could know Ruth Ellery for forty years and not know her middle name. So finding this felt like..." He searched. "Like finding out a locked room in a house you grew up in."

*Yes,* Lior thought. *Exactly like that.*

"I'm Tomas, by the way," the young man said. "Tomas Ellery."

"Lior Thornbury." He nodded at the room. "Hence the sign, such as it is."

Tomas glanced at the window, where THORNBURY'S BOOKS was painted in gold leaf that had faded to the color of weak tea. "Did you used to be something else? Before books? Gran said — well, Gran didn't say anything, but my mum said you were a police detective once. In the city."

"A lifetime ago," Lior said. "In another country, practically. London might as well be Narnia."

"But you're from here originally?"

"I'm from here originally," Lior agreed, and left it there, the way you leave a door closed that you have every intention of keeping closed.

Tomas looked at the diary, then at Lior, and Lior watched him arrive at the question the way you watch someone approach a frozen pond — wanting to warn them, knowing they won't listen.

"Do you think I should take it to the police?" Tomas asked. "Only it feels mad, doesn't it? Handing in a thirty-year-old diary like it's a lost umbrella. And they'd probably just lose it anyway. No offense."

"None taken. It's accurate."

"So I thought — you'd know what to do with it. Being a detective. Formerly."

*Tomas,* Lior thought, *you have made the single worst decision available to you today, and I include the taxidermied stoat in that assessment.* But what he said was: "Leave it with me. I'll have a look. If it turns out to be significant, we'll decide together what to do. Fair?"

Tomas exhaled, and some of the pallor left his face, replaced by the ordinary exhaustion of a young man carrying boxes of a dead woman's life. He wrote his phone number on the pad by the till in handwriting that looped and sprawled — a generation raised on keyboards, Lior noted, writing like the words might escape — and thanked him twice, and left.

The bell rang behind him. The shop settled.

Lior stood at the counter for a long moment, the diary sitting between his hands like a small drowned animal, and told himself all the reasonable things. It was a jumble-sale donation. Half of Ashmoor's attics contained relics of the Voss case; the disappearance had been the biggest thing to happen to the town in living memory, bigger than the flood, bigger even than the year the Calloway mill shut. People had kept newspaper clippings, memorial tea towels, God knew what else. A diary surfacing after three decades meant nothing. It was flotsam. It was coincidence.

He was a bookseller. He was sixty-eight years old. He had a bad knee and a good kettle and absolutely no remaining obligation to the truth of anything.

Lior put the kettle on, made tea he forgot to drink, and carried the diary to the back room, where the light was better and the door locked.

* * *

He read it the way he used to read statements — twice through fast, then slowly, then again with a pencil, marking nothing, changing nothing, letting the document speak first.

Maren Voss had been twenty-two in the spring of 1994, and she wrote the way some people breathe: constantly, urgently, joyfully. The early entries were full of the shop where she worked (the chemist's, stocking shelves, alphabetizing nothing), her father's silences, her sister Odile ("Odile says I dramatize. Odile owns four cardigans and a moral philosophy degree, so she would know"), and a friend referred to only as W., whose entries multiplied through June like spring itself arriving.

*W. laughed at me in the churchyard today and I nearly died of it. If dying of laughing were possible I'd be dead already and very happy about it.*

Lior read, and the shop darkened around him, and he did not get up to turn on the lamp.

He remembered Maren Voss. Of course he did. You didn't forget the faces from the cases that broke you; they stayed filed in the soft tissue somewhere, surfacing at odd hours. He'd been twenty-eight, a junior detective seconded down from the city for the search, walking the riverbanks with a stick and a bag of hope, and he remembered her photograph on the noticeboard — the eager slant of her smile, the same lean-forward energy as her handwriting — and he remembered the morning the Calloway solicitors arrived with their alibi like a rolled-up carpet, and how it unrolled over everything, and how everyone quietly agreed to stand on it.

He remembered signing the report. He remembered the weight of the pen, absurdly, the specific cheapness of it, station-issue ballpoint, and how the signature had felt less like writing his name than surrendering it.

The diary's dated entries ran through June, July, August. On the ninth of September, 1994, Maren Voss vanished. Every person in Ashmoor over the age of forty could tell you where they were when the news came, in the way of towns that have few stories and ration them carefully.

The diary in Lior's hands continued to the thirtieth of September.

Three weeks past the end of the world.

He turned the pages with fingers that had finally, after thirty years, begun to shake.

The entries after the ninth of September were still signed Maren. Still first person. But the hand was wrong — Lior saw it immediately, the way you recognize a forged signature because the forger has been *careful*, and care is the telltale; real handwriting is lazy, confident, unconscious, while imitation leans too hard on every letter. These letters leaned. The slant was too consistent. The ink pressure varied like someone thinking about pressure. And the content —

*September 12th. Woke early. The gulls here are louder than home. I am learning to be nobody, and it is harder than it sounds, because I was never very good at being somebody either.*

*September 19th. Do not look for me. Please. Some doors are better shut.*

*September 30th. She is safe where no one will look.*

Lior sat back in the creaking chair. The lamp he'd never switched on wasn't needed; the last of the daylight had gone grey-gold through the back window, catching the dust motes, and the room smelled of old paper and rain, and Lior Thornbury sat very still while thirty years of careful boarding splintered quietly inside him.

Because the entries weren't a forgery meant to deceive. That was the thing his hands understood before his head caught up. A deceiver writes convincingly or doesn't bother. This was imitation done *badly on purpose* — a message in a bottle, disguised as a fake, addressed to whoever might someday hold this book and know what they were looking at. *She is safe where no one will look.* Not *I*. *She.* Whoever wrote those lines was talking about Maren in the third person, hiding the truth inside a lie, trusting that eventually — ten years, thirty years, a hundred — someone would turn these pages and feel the wrongness like a stone in a shoe.

Someone had wanted the truth found. Just not yet. Just not by just anyone.

And someone else — Ruth Ellery, best innkeeper the town ever had, most private person alive — had kept that message sealed in an attic for thirty years, under the Christmas decorations and the stoat, and had died in August without breathing a word.

Lior got up, found the pencil, and wrote on a scrap of ledger paper, in a hand far lazier and more confident than any forgery:

*Maren Voss. Entries continue 3 weeks past disappearance. Deliberate imitation. Kept by R. Ellery. Why?*

He looked at the list, which was really one question wearing three hats, and felt the old injury wake along his spine — not pain exactly, more the ache of a break long healed, announcing a change in the weather.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Down the street, the bakery lights glowed yellow against the dusk, and Ashmoor arranged itself into its evening shape: curtains drawn, suppers cooked, the whole town settling into the story it had agreed upon, the one where a girl ran away and the sea or the road or the world took her, and no one was to blame, and everyone had been very kind about it, and it was all thirty years ago now, thank you.

Lior locked the diary in the drawer beneath the till — the shallow one, the decoy — and put the real one inside the hollowed-out copy of *Bleak House* on the top shelf, spine out, where it had waited through two previous decades of nobody asking questions about anything.

Then he took down the returned-book ledger, meaning to log Tomas Ellery's visit like a normal bookseller, like a man with nothing in his past but stock and slow Tuesdays.

His pen hovered. Because there, in his own handwriting, dated the eleventh of October, 1994 — two days after Maren Voss disappeared, one day after the jumble sale where the diary had first passed through his hands — was an entry he had no memory of making:

*Donation received: 1 diary, water-damaged. Returned to family. — L.T.*

He stared at it for a long time.

He had no memory of returning it. He had no memory of any family coming to claim it. And he had spent thirty years believing the book had gone to the jumble sale and scattered into the town like all the other paper ghosts of that autumn.

Which meant that either his memory was failing —

— or, thirty years ago, a younger Lior Thornbury had held this diary in his hands, read those impossible entries, understood exactly what they were, and chosen to write *returned to family* in a ledger and say nothing to anyone.

The kettle clicked off in the back room. The shop held its breath.

"Well," Lior said aloud, to the empty chairs and the fading light and the boarded-shut door inside his chest, now hanging by one hinge. "Hello again."
