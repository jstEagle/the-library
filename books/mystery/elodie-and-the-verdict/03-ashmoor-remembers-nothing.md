## 3. Ashmoor Remembers Nothing

The trouble with small towns, Lior Thornbury had learned over a career spent in them, was not that people lied. It was that they remembered selectively, the way a river remembers stones — smoothing off the inconvenient edges until everything left is round and pleasant and fits in the palm without cutting anyone.

He set out on Tuesday morning with the diary zipped into an inner pocket of his coat, which was absurd, because he had no intention of showing it to anyone, and also sensible, because he had every intention of showing it to someone if the right face appeared. The morning was one of those Ashmoor mornings that seemed designed by a tourism board: pale gold light on the wet cobbles, gulls arguing over the harbour wall, the smell of bread drifting from Pell's bakery like an apology for everything else.

Garrick Pell had been baking in Ashmoor for forty years. He was shaped, now, rather like his own sourdough — broad, floury, and dense at the centre — and he greeted Lior through the open door with the enthusiasm of a man who considered all human interaction a form of advertising.

"Lior! You look terrible. Rye? On me."

"Rye would be excellent." Lior accepted the loaf and leaned against the counter, adopting what he thought of as his browsing voice — casual, unhurried, the voice of a man with nowhere to be and no questions to ask. "Busy week?"

"Harvest festival coming. Forty years I've done the harvest loaves. You'd think after forty years they'd let me sleep." Garrick laughed at his own joke, a sound like a bellows with a cold.

"You've seen a lot of festivals, then. Do you remember the midsummer fundraiser? Must have been — oh, ninety-four, ninety-five. Big do, wasn't it? Marquee on the green?"

Garrick's hands kept moving, but something behind his eyes went very still, the way water goes still when a shadow passes over it. "Fundraiser," he said. "For the church roof, that would have been. Good year for it. We raised a packet."

"I heard Maren Voss helped organise it."

The bellows laugh did not come. Garrick looked down at his counter, wiped a spot of flour that had not been there, and said, "Voss. That's a name you don't hear."

"It used to be everywhere," Lior said mildly. "There were three Voss families on Harbour Lane alone."

"Well." Garrick straightened, and the smile came back up like a shop shutter, smooth and total. "People move on, don't they. That's the thing about towns. People move on." He pushed a second loaf across the counter, unasked. "On the house. Tell your customers about us."

Lior walked out with two loaves he hadn't needed and one answer he hadn't got, and stood on the pavement feeling the particular chill of a man who has just watched a door close from the inside.

* * *

*14 June 1994. Wren says I write too much about weather, so: the weather today was him. Grey all morning, then breaking into something bright and sudden around four, when she climbed through my window with her shoes in her hand and her hair full of garden. We lay on my bed and read the same page of the same book and neither of us turned it for an hour. If this is what the weather is going to be like all summer, I could die happy.*

*Except that I can't say things like that anymore, even in here. Even in here, someone might read.*

Lior sat on the sea wall with the second loaf torn open and ate it slowly, reading the entry twice. The handwriting was loose, looping, confident — a young woman's hand, unguarded. The ink had feathered where damp had found it, but the words held.

Wren. He turned the name over. There had been a Halloway family out past the mill, he thought. A girl who worked summers at the inn. He filed it away, in the drawer of his mind marked *things the town will not offer voluntarily*, which was, he reflected, becoming a crowded drawer after only three days of looking.

He brushed crumbs from his coat and went to find the vicar.

* * *

St. Aldhelm's smelled of candle wax and old stone and, faintly, of the particular despair of a building fund. Father Anselm was up a ladder in the nave, polishing a brass sconce with the concentration of a man who preferred brass to people, which Lior respected. Anselm was seventy-something and built like a question mark, and when he saw Lior below him he descended with the slow ceremony of a barge docking.

"Mr. Thornbury. Come to confess?"

"Nothing worth the paperwork. I wanted to ask you about the church records. Midsummer 1994 — there was a fundraiser for the roof."

"There was rain," Anselm said promptly.

Lior blinked. "I — yes, possibly, but—"

"The whole weekend. Rain like the Flood's rehearsal. The marquee leaked, the cake tent flooded, and Mrs. Calloway's hat was ruined, which she mentioned to me eleven times over the following decade." Anselm lowered himself onto a pew with a sigh that seemed structural. "Why do you ask?"

"Do you remember who organised it?"

Anselm's eyes, which were quick and grey and missed very little for a man who claimed to remember only weather, rested on Lior with gentle attention. "You're asking about Maren Voss," he said.

The name hung in the nave like incense. Lior kept his voice level. "Am I?"

"You're the third person in thirty years to say her name to me in this church. The first was her father. The second was a police officer with a clipboard." He folded his hands. "And now you, with a baker's bag and a policeman's walk. So. Ask."

"What do you remember about her?"

"Rain," said Anselm. "I remember rain, Mr. Thornbury. I remember that the roof fund was short that year because half the town stayed home, and I remember that a young woman with kind manners took it upon herself to cheer everyone up, and I remember that by August she was gone, and I remember that when I asked questions about it — quietly, pastorally, as one does — I was visited by a solicitor representing the Calloway family, who informed me that speculation was unhelpful to a grieving community." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I have been remembering rain ever since. It's safer. Rain doesn't have lawyers."

"And the third time?" Lior asked quietly. "Who was the second officer?"

Anselm looked at him for a long moment, and Lior watched the old man decide something, weighing it like communion bread. "A junior detective," Anselm said. "Young. Decent-looking. Stood exactly where you're standing and asked me almost exactly what you just asked, and then went away, and nothing changed, and I told myself that was the world working properly." He rose, joints announcing each stage of the ascent. "It's good to see you again, Mr. Thornbury. I'm sorry about the circumstances. I'm sorry about most things, at my age. It's practically a hobby."

* * *

*3 July 1994. Father says the engagement announcement goes in the paper this week and I should be proud. Proud. I stood in the parlour while Edmund's mother described my future to me like a menu — the house, the position, the name — and I nodded, and nodded, and inside my head I was counting windows. Eleven windows on the ground floor of this house. I have checked every latch. I know which ones stick.*

*Wren didn't come today. She said she couldn't watch it. I understand. I can't watch it either, and I'm the one standing in the room.*

By afternoon, Lior had collected a small museum of non-answers. The postmistress remembered the fundraiser's raffle ("a fruit hamper, first prize; the Hendersons won it and the plums went off before they could eat them all, which Mrs. Henderson took as a moral lesson"). The barber remembered the marquee ("green and white stripes; cheap canvas; you get what you pay for"). The woman at the chandlery remembered that it had been hot, or possibly cold, and that someone had brought a dog, and that the dog had been lovely.

Nobody remembered Maren.

That was the thing. It wasn't forgetting — forgetting is passive, forgetting is a hole. This was a wall painted to look like sky. Every person he spoke to performed their ignorance with such warmth, such cooperative willingness, that it became its own kind of testimony. *We are all agreeing together, Mr. Thornbury, to have nothing to tell you, and we are doing it as a favour, and we expect you to appreciate it.*

He bought a coffee he didn't want from the café on the square, mostly to sit somewhere public and be seen being ordinary, and eavesdropped on the local frequency. Harvest festival logistics. Someone's hip operation. A complaint about parking that had apparently been running since the Thatcher administration. And underneath it, threaded through the chatter like wire through a hedge: awareness of him. He caught his own name twice, both times followed by a pause and a change of subject so smooth it might have been choreographed.

Ashmoor remembered nothing, and it remembered its nothingness collectively, and it had rehearsed.

* * *

*9 July 1994. Last night Edmund put his hand on my knee at dinner, in front of both families, and everyone smiled as though he'd shown me a photograph of a puppy. I excused myself and was sick in the garden among the roses. Wren found me. She didn't say anything clever. She just sat down in the dirt next to me and held my hair back and after a while she started laughing — quietly, helplessly — and said, "Maren Voss, you are the worst liar in three counties," and I laughed too, filthy and silent, with my forehead against her shoulder, and I thought: whatever I have to do, however long it takes, I am getting out of this house alive, and I am taking her with me.*

Lior read it standing under the clock tower in the square, and had to fold the diary away for a moment and look at the gulls until his throat behaved.

Then he noticed the boy.

Across the square, outside the ironmonger's, a young man stood pretending to examine a window display of padlocks with the intensity of a scholar studying scripture. Pale, early twenties, the same young man from the bookshop — Tomas, according to the ledger, though Lior suspected that was roughly as true as the rest of what he'd been told. When Lior looked directly at him, the boy performed a masterful pantomime of having just remembered an urgent errand elsewhere, and walked away at a pace calibrated to seem casual to everyone except anyone actually watching.

Lior let him go. Chasing him would only spook him, and besides — frightened boys who follow retired detectives around market squares usually want to talk eventually. They just need to be certain, first, that talking won't kill them. Lior knew the type. He had been the type, once, standing outside a police station in 1994 with a report he didn't believe in his hand and a superior officer's voice in his ear saying *the family's cooperated, Thornbury, the file's closed, don't make waves you'll regret.*

He finished his coffee. It had gone cold and bitter, which felt appropriate.

* * *

His last call of the day was the least promising and therefore, in his experience, the most likely to yield something. Miss Ivy Trench ran the newsagent's, was eighty-one, claimed to be seventy-four, and had never in living memory agreed with anyone about anything. If the town had decided collectively to forget, Ivy Trench would have forgotten purely out of contrariness.

She was behind the counter sorting newspapers into a rack with the violence of a woman settling scores, and she looked up at Lior with the expression she reserved for customers she'd already mentally billed.

"Thornbury. Your paper's late again. Blame the delivery van, not me."

"Noted. Miss Trench, a question about the midsummer fundraiser, 1994."

Her hands stopped. Not dramatically — she simply finished slotting the newspaper she was holding, and then did not pick up another one, and the silence stretched just long enough to be eloquent.

"That was a long time ago," she said.

"It was."

"People forget things."

"They do," Lior agreed. "Though I notice you remember the price of everything since 1971."

"Prices matter." She fixed him with eyes like two black pins. "What is it you're actually asking, Thornbury? Because you've been round the square all day asking it sideways, and I've watched you do it, and I'll tell you now I haven't got patience for sideways."

Lior met her gaze. Here, at last, was a door that might open — or slam. He chose honesty, carefully rationed. "Someone left a book in my shop. It belonged to Maren Voss. I'm trying to find out what happened to her."

Ivy Trench looked at him for a long, long moment. Then she reached beneath the counter and pulled out a tin — an ancient biscuit tin with a picture of the Queen's silver jubilee on it — and from the tin she took a folded newspaper clipping, yellowed soft as cloth at the creases, and laid it on the counter between them without letting go of it.

The headline read: LOCAL WOMAN MISSING. Below it, a photograph of a young woman with a wide smile and wary eyes. Maren Voss. And below that, a smaller paragraph noting that the search had been "concluded" and that the family requested privacy.

"You kept it," Lior said.

"I keep everything," Ivy said. "It's why nobody tells me anything." She slid the clipping back into the tin, back under the counter, and her chin came up. "Thirty years ago a policeman came in here and asked me questions, and I answered them, and a fortnight later two nice men from the Calloway estate bought the freehold of this shop and dropped my rent by half, and I understood the transaction perfectly well, thank you, even if nobody wrote it down." Her voice was dry as old rope. "So don't ask me to remember, Mr. Thornbury. But I'll tell you one thing for free, since you're the first soul in thirty years to say her name out loud like it means something."

She leaned across the counter, and the shop seemed to lean with her.

"If you want to know what happened to Maren Voss, don't waste your time on the town. The town signed nothing and promised nothing and forgot nothing — we just stopped being asked. Go and see the sister. Odile. Magistrate now, if you please." Something flickered across the pin-sharp eyes — not quite fear, more like the respect one pays a dangerous animal. "Immaculate woman, Odile Voss. Never puts a foot wrong. And in my experience, Mr. Thornbury, it's the immaculate ones you want to look at. The rest of us are too busy dropping things."

She picked up her newspapers and resumed sorting them with tremendous force, and Lior understood the interview was over.

Outside, the light was going amber and long, and the gulls were settling on the rooftops like a jury retiring. Lior stood on the pavement with the diary against his ribs and Ivy Trench's words turning over in him like a stone in a tide pool.

*Go and see the sister.*

He had been circling toward it all day without admitting it — the way the town circled around the hole where Maren used to be. Tomorrow, then. He would make tea with a magistrate, admire her grief, and watch her hands.

In his inner pocket, the diary pressed against his heart like something with a pulse.
