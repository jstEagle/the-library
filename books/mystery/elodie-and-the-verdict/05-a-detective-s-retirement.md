## 5. A Detective's Retirement

The rain had a way of finding Ashmoor in October that felt less like weather and more like an opinion.

Lior Thornbury was twenty-six years old and already tired of it. He stood under the awning of the Voss house on Bellwether Lane with his collar up and his notebook going soft at the edges, watching Detective Sergeant Harrow smoke his third cigarette of the morning and pretend the house wasn't looking back at them.

"Missing persons," Harrow said, exhaling. "You know what missing persons means, Thornbury?"

"It means someone's missing."

"It means *so far*." Harrow flicked ash into the gutter. "Give it a week. Half of them come home on their own, embarrassed. The other half turn up somewhere worse, and then it's not our problem anymore either — it's the coroner's."

Lior wrote that down, because he was twenty-six and still wrote down everything his seniors said, including the things they said to be cruel, because he hadn't yet learned which words were instructions and which were just weather.

The Voss house was narrow and tall, painted a blue that had once been cheerful and was now merely stubborn. A woman opened the door before they knocked — Odile Voss, twenty-nine, in a grey cardigan buttoned to the throat, her hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had decided grief would be conducted indoors, privately, according to schedule.

"Detectives," she said. "You've come about Maren."

"We have, miss. May we—"

"I've made tea." She stepped aside. "You'll want to sit. Everyone says they don't want to sit, and then within ten minutes they're sitting."

Harrow muttered something about women and their tea, and Lior followed him into a front room that smelled of lavender and old paper. There were books everywhere — not arranged so much as accumulated, stacked on chairs, wedged between the radio and the window. Lior caught himself reading spines as he sat: Hardy, Brontë, a fat anthology of folk ballads with a broken spine.

"Maren's?" he asked.

Odile paused in the doorway to the kitchen. For just a moment her face did something complicated — a flinch folded inside a smile.

"Everything in this house is Maren's," she said. "That's rather the problem, isn't it."

* * *

In 1994, the town of Ashmoor had exactly two speeds: Sunday, and waiting for Sunday.

Lior learned this over the following eleven days, walking its streets with a notebook full of statements that all agreed with each other in the way that only rehearsed things do. The baker remembered Maren Voss buying a lardy cake on the Saturday she vanished — remembered it fondly, in detail, twice. The publican remembered her not coming in, which he offered as though it were a contribution. The vicar remembered rain. Everyone remembered rain. It was as if the entire town had been issued a single memory and told to share.

"She was quiet," said the woman from the post office. "Kept herself to herself."

"Sweet girl," said the woman from the bakery, in a tone suggesting otherwise.

"Close to her sister," said everyone, identically, like a chorus that had been given one line.

And through it all ran the name nobody would quite say aloud but everybody said anyway, in the pauses, in the lowered voices, in the way eyes slid toward the big house on the moor road and then away again, guilty, as though looking at it too long might cost something.

Calloway.

Edmund Calloway was thirty-one that autumn, heir to a shipping fortune that had mostly finished being shipped, and engaged — the engagement announced in the parish newsletter with a photograph in which Maren Voss smiled the way people smile when a camera is pointed at them by their future mother-in-law. Lior had looked at that photograph longer than was professional. There was something in her eyes he couldn't name yet. He'd have called it patience, if patience didn't usually look so much like a held breath.

"Talk to him gently," Harrow instructed, parking the unmarked car outside the Calloway estate gates. "Gently, Thornbury. That family has more solicitors than the county has roads."

"Are we investigating a disappearance or a diplomatic mission?"

"We're investigating whatever we can afford to investigate." Harrow ground out his cigarette. "Welcome to policing, son."

* * *

He met Edmund Calloway on the terrace, because of course a man like Edmund Calloway took meetings on terraces. He was tall and fair and handsome in the way of portraits — a face designed to be seen from across a room or across a century. His hands were elegant. His handshake was brief.

"You're the detectives," Edmund said. "I want you to know I'll help in any way I can. Maren is—" He stopped. Started again. "Maren is everything to me."

Lior wrote: *cooperative. distressed.* And underneath, in smaller letters he'd later scratch out: *rehearsed?*

"When did you last see her?"

"Saturday evening. Dinner at my mother's house. She left around nine, I believe — she wanted an early night. She'd been tired lately." A small, sad smile. "Weddings are exhausting even before they happen."

"And after she left?"

"I stayed. We had port. Mother and I talked about seating arrangements until nearly midnight. Riveting stuff." He laughed, and the laugh was warm, and Lior hated how warm it was, how easily warmth could be manufactured by men who'd been taught the recipe young.

"Anyone else at this dinner?"

"Just family. You know how it is." Edmund spread those elegant hands. "Ask anyone. Ask my mother. Ask the staff."

*Ask anyone.* In eleven days of asking anyone, no one had ever invited Lior to ask anyone. People got specific when they were telling the truth — they gave you the awkward details, the burnt roast, the argument about the car. Perfection was a costume, and Edmund Calloway wore his beautifully.

But Lior was twenty-six, and twenty-six-year-old junior detectives do not get to call beautiful men liars on the strength of a feeling. Feelings weren't evidence. Harrow had told him that too, and for once Harrow had been right.

* * *

The alibi arrived nine days after Maren disappeared, delivered not to the police station but to the office of the Chief Constable, which told Lior most of what he needed to know before he read a word of it.

Four witnesses. Four signed statements. Edmund Calloway had dined with his mother, Vivienne; the family solicitor, Corin Blake; and the local magistrate-in-waiting, a Mr. Fenwick, on the night in question, and had remained in their company until midnight. The statements were typed, immaculate, mutually corroborating, and signed within a two-hour window of one another — Lior checked the times three times, feeling the cold thrill move up his spine and not knowing yet what to call it.

It was airtight. That was the phrase used in the office, in the papers, in the pub. *Airtight.* Nobody seemed to find it strange that a man's innocence should arrive gift-wrapped, notarized, and addressed to the Chief Constable personally.

"They've done us a favour," Harrow said, feet on his desk. "Case closed before Christmas. You know what that means? Means I'm home for the turkey."

"She's still missing."

"People go missing, Thornbury." Harrow lit a fresh cigarette off the stub of the old one, a trick Lior had never mastered and suspected took decades of disappointment. "She's twenty-four, she's got cold feet written all over her, and her fiancé's family has produced four witnesses who say he was forty miles away eating cheese. What exactly would you like me to do? Arrest the truth?"

"The truth doesn't need four signatures."

"No." Harrow exhaled. "But neither does a lie, apparently. Sign the report, lad."

Lior signed the report.

He wanted, for the rest of his life, to be able to say he hadn't. That he'd thrown his notebook on Harrow's desk and demanded they pull the dinner's receipts, interview the kitchen staff, check the mileage on the Calloway motorcars. He composed that version of himself many times over the years, late at night, behind the counter of a bookshop, and it never once rang true. Here is what actually happened: he was twenty-six, he had a probationary report pending, and a detective sergeant with nineteen years of service told him to sign. So he signed. His signature went onto the bottom of a document that closed a door on a living woman, and the pen barely hesitated, and that — the barely — was the part that would keep him up at night for thirty years. If he'd fought and lost, there'd at least be a fight to remember. Instead there was only the smooth, frictionless sound of a career choosing itself over a stranger.

He did manage one small rebellion. He kept his notes. Every statement, every time-stamp, every spine he'd read in the Voss front room, every line of *rehearsed?* scratched out but not erased. He put them in a folder, and the folder in a box, and the box in the attic of a bedsit, and told himself he'd do something with them someday, when he was senior enough, when it was safe, when—

When. The great white flag of the English language.

* * *

The last time he saw Odile Voss was outside the inquest, in the rain, naturally. She stood on the courthouse steps in the same grey cardigan, buttoned to the throat, watching the Calloway motorcar glide away like a ship leaving harbour.

"My sister loved books," she said, without turning around. Lior hadn't known she knew he was there. "Did you know that? She read everything. She'd have liked you, I think. You had ink on your fingers the whole time you were in our house, and you kept looking at her shelves instead of at us."

"I'm sorry," Lior said. "About the verdict."

"There wasn't a verdict." Now she turned, and her face was dry and composed and terrible. "That's rather the point of your profession, isn't it, Detective? To make sure there's never a verdict. Just paperwork. Just *missing, presumed.*" She pulled her collar up. "You signed it, I hear. The report."

He had no answer. She hadn't asked a question.

"One day," Odile Voss said, "someone will ask the right questions in this town. I hope, for your sake, you're not standing next to the person who should have asked them first." And she walked down the steps into the rain without an umbrella, upright as a mast, and Lior stood there letting the water run off his nose, feeling precisely the size he was.

* * *

Thirty years later, in the flat above Thornbury's Books, Lior set down the diary of Maren Voss and did not sleep.

He tried. He went through the motions — kettle on, light off, the familiar creak of the bed that knew his shape better than any person now did. But the entries kept replaying behind his eyes. Three weeks past the date she vanished. Written in a hand that was hers and wasn't, slanted wrong, hurried wrong, *deliberate* wrong. And that line, that one line, sitting in the middle of the page like a stone in a shoe:

*She is safe where no one will look.*

At three in the morning he got up, went down the dark stairs past the dark shelves, and unlocked the cabinet under the till where he kept the things that were not for sale: his father's watch, a photograph of a woman who'd had the good sense to leave him before he could disappoint her properly, and a cardboard box gone soft and brown with age.

THORNBURY — WORK MATERIALS, said the marker pen on the lid, in his own younger handwriting, back when he'd still believed in filing systems and justice.

Inside, wrapped in a carrier bag from a supermarket that no longer existed, was the folder. His notes. Thirty years of attic damp had curled the corners but not the contents. He sat on the floor of his own shop, an old man among old paper, and read his twenty-six-year-old self like a stranger's diary.

There it all was. The baker's lardy cake. The vicar's rain. The four statements, the two-hour signing window, the times copied out in his neat junior hand with little question marks beside them — four question marks, marching down the margin like footprints.

And at the bottom of the last page, in pencil, faint as a confession:

*Alibi too clean. Who typed it? Same typewriter for all four? CHECK.*

He had never checked. He had written *check* and then signed the report and gone home, and the question mark had waited in the dark for thirty years, patient as a bookmark.

Lior sat on the cold floorboards and laughed — one short, ugly syllable with nothing funny in it. All these years he'd thought retirement meant the guilt was over, that closing the shop at five and arguing with the coffee machine constituted a life paid in full. But guilt didn't retire. It just went part-time. It worked quietly, behind the counter, dusting shelves, handing customers their change — and all it had ever needed was one water-damaged diary walked through the door to clock back in full-time.

Because here was the thing he couldn't stop seeing now, the thing that made his hands unsteady as he squared the pages: the forged entries in Maren's diary weren't random. They were *addressed*. Slanted wrong on purpose, hurried on purpose, planted where a certain kind of mind would eventually trip over them. Someone had written those entries the way you leave a note under a door — and the only kind of person who reads a room that carefully, who notices a hand that's almost right, is a detective.

Someone, thirty years ago, had written a message and addressed it to a man like him.

And a man like him had signed the report instead.

Lior got up off the floor, knees cracking their old complaint, and carried the folder to the lamp on the counter. Outside, October rain came down on Ashmoor with its usual opinion, and inside, a retired detective opened a fresh page in a notebook he hadn't used in a decade, and wrote, at the top, in a hand steadier than he felt:

*1994 inquest file. County records office. First thing tomorrow.*

Underneath, after a moment, he added:

*This time, check.*
