## 12. The Young Man Returns

The bell over the door of Thornbury's Books had a voice like a coughing canary, and for ten years Lior had found it comforting precisely because it announced almost no one. On a wet Thursday in November it announced Tomas Ellery, and Lior knew before he looked up that something had gone badly wrong.

The young man stood in the doorway as though he'd forgotten what doors were for. Rain had gotten into his pale hair and turned it dark at the roots of his parting, and his coat — the same inadequate grey coat from three weeks ago — was soaked through at the shoulders. He didn't come in so much as leak in, one step at a time, his eyes doing a quick, frightened inventory of the shop: the window, the back room curtain, the stairs.

"Tomas," Lior said, setting down the rag he'd been using on the counter. "You look like a man who's seen a ghost."

"I wish," said Tomas, and then seemed to hear himself, and went whiter, if such a thing were arithmetically possible.

Lior came around the counter and steered him by the elbow toward the two chairs by the radiator, the ones he kept for customers who read standing up too long and needed to be told to sit. The boy sat. His hands were shaking. Not dramatically — not the way people shake in films — but with the small persistent tremor of someone who has been shaking for hours and has stopped noticing.

"Tea," Lior said. It wasn't a question. He put the kettle on in the back room because the ritual of it gave them both thirty seconds to breathe, and when he came back with two mismatched mugs, Tomas took his in both hands like a man warming himself at a fire that might go out.

"Mr. Thornbury." The boy's voice cracked on the second syllable and he started again. "Mr. Thornbury, I need to tell you something, and I need you to not interrupt me, because if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve, and I've already lost it twice on the walk here."

"Then I'll be a lamppost," Lior said. "Lampposts are famously silent."

That earned him half a smile, there and gone, like a fish surfacing. Tomas drank some tea. Outside, the rain ticked against the window in the patient way rain does when it has decided to stay the night.

"Three weeks ago I brought you my grandmother's diary. The water-damaged one. Maren Voss."

"I remember."

"You asked me where it came from and I told you the attic. That was true, mostly. What I didn't tell you—" He stopped. Started again. "What I didn't tell you is *why* it was in the attic."

Lior said nothing. Lamppost. But inside him, the old machinery — the thing he'd spent a decade oiling shut — gave a single slow turn, like an engine turning over after a long winter.

"My grandmother was Ruth Ellery. She ran the Anchor Inn until her hips gave out. She died in March." Tomas stared into his tea. "She was one of those grandmothers who was very particular about things. Labels on everything. Her tins labeled, her boxes labeled, her Christmas decorations labeled by year. And she left me instructions. Written instructions, because she said I had the organizational instincts of a golden retriever."

"Gentle humor is permitted even now?" Lior asked softly.

"It's the only thing keeping me upright, sir."

"Then carry on. Golden retrievers are excellent under pressure, incidentally. They just need someone to throw the stick."

Tomas almost laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, and he set the mug down because his hands weren't cooperating. "Among her instructions was a box. Top shelf of the attic, behind the Christmas 1993 decorations — she'd written that specifically, *behind Christmas 1993*, which I thought was odd because why would the year matter? Inside the box was the diary. And underneath the diary was an envelope. Sealed. With wax, Mr. Thornbury. Who uses wax? My grandmother, apparently. And on the envelope, in her handwriting, it said—"

He couldn't say it. Lior watched the boy's throat work.

"It said, *For when someone asks about Maren.*"

The radiator ticked. Somewhere out on the high street a delivery van changed gears. Lior sat very still, the way he'd learned to sit thirty years ago in interview rooms, when stillness was the only tool they let juniors use.

"And have people asked about Maren?" he asked quietly. "Before me?"

"Not in thirty years. Not once. That's the thing — that's the thing that's been sitting on my chest all night." Tomas looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified all at once. "She wrote that envelope sometime around 1994. I know because of the box, because of the labels, because everything she ever did was dated. She sealed a letter and waited thirty years for someone to ask a question, and nobody did, and then *you* asked, and I brought you the diary without opening the letter, because — I don't know why. Because it felt like hers. Because opening it felt like eavesdropping on the dead."

"But now?"

"Now somebody else knows I have it." The words came out flat and fast, like stones skipped across ice. "Two nights ago my flat was broken into. Nothing taken. Nothing *touched* except the box shelf in my bedroom closet — everything pulled down, everything gone through, the Christmas decorations scattered across the floor. They were looking for the letter. And Mr. Thornbury, here's the worst part, here's the part I keep circling like a dog trying to bite its own tail—" He breathed. "I'd moved it. After your questions started, after I read enough of the diary to feel creepy about it, I moved the envelope out of the flat. I put it in a locker at the leisure centre. Twelve pounds a month and I've never once used the gym. So they tore my home apart and found nothing, which means they'll figure out eventually that I'm not stupid, just slow, and they'll come back, or they'll follow me, and I don't—"

"Stop." Lior held up a hand. "Breathe. When did you move it?"

"Sunday."

"And the break-in was Tuesday night?"

"Tuesday, yes."

"Then whoever it was searched your flat before you moved the letter, found nothing, and doesn't yet know where it is. You're ahead of them. Barely, but ahead." Lior heard his own voice settle into the old register, low and even, the register that used to make witnesses stop crying and start talking. Ten years of dust fell off it. "Now. Do you have the key on you?"

Tomas patted his jacket pocket with a hand that wasn't steady. "Yes."

"Good. Finish your tea. Then we're going to go and get it, together, right now, while it's raining and the streets are empty and anyone watching us is watching through water."

* * *

They walked the long way, because Lior insisted on the long way, doubling back through the arcade by the shuttered wool shop and coming at the leisure centre from the car park side. Tomas kept glancing at him sideways, and finally, somewhere between the chip shop and the bus shelter, he blurted:

"You've done this before. This — walking. Looking."

"I was a policeman for twenty-two years," Lior said. "Ashmoor CID, mostly. Retired eleven years ago."

"You were *police*?" Tomas stopped dead under the awning of the closed bakery. "You sold me a secondhand copy of *Watership Down* and you were police?"

"The book trade attracts all sorts." Lior nudged him back into motion. "And yes, Tomas. I knew Maren Voss's case. I worked it, at the bottom of the ladder, holding the torches for men who'd already decided how the story ended. Which is why your grandmother's letter matters more than either of us can afford to say out loud on a public street."

"Why does everyone talk about it like that? Like the case itself is dangerous. It's thirty years old. Everyone involved is old or dead or—"

"Or alive," Lior said, "and comfortable, and would very much like to stay that way. Comfortable people, Tomas, are the most dangerous creatures in Ashmoor. Ask anyone who's tried to take away their parking space."

It got the ghost of a laugh out of him, which had been the point. Fear is a gas; it expands to fill whatever container you give it, and a small joke makes the container smaller. Lior had learned that from a sergeant named Doyle who'd died of a heart attack at fifty-four, comfortably, which proved nothing either way.

The leisure centre smelled of chlorine and floor polish and the particular despair of a Tuesday-to-Thursday gym. A teenager at the front desk barely looked up from her phone. Tomas signed them both in as guests with hands that shook only a little, and led the way down the corridor to the lockers, past a poster encouraging residents to TRY A NEW YOU THIS JANUARY.

Locker 47. The key turned. Inside, wrapped in a plastic carrier bag from a shop that had closed in 2019, was a plain white envelope, sealed with a bead of candle wax gone amber with age. The handwriting on it was firm, schoolmarm-neat, each letter standing at attention:

*For when someone asks about Maren.*

Below that, smaller:

*Tomas. If you are reading this, then God help you, lad.*

"She knew," Tomas whispered. "Thirty years ago, she knew that someday it would land on me."

"Grandmothers know things," Lior said. "It's the job description." He looked at the envelope for a long moment. "This is yours. Legally, morally, every -ally there is. But I'm going to ask you anyway: may I be there when it's opened? Because whoever searched your flat confirms what I've suspected since the day you walked into my shop — this letter isn't just family history. It's evidence. Possibly the only honest document left from 1994."

Tomas looked at the envelope. Looked at Lior. And Lior saw the exact moment the boy made the decision — saw it arrive in his face like weather, the fear not leaving but being *outvoted*.

"There's a café across the road," Tomas said. "With booths. High backs. My gran liked booths. She said you could cry in a booth and only the person opposite you would know."

"Your gran," said Lior, "sounds like she understood architecture."

* * *

The café was called The Steaming Mug, which was ambitious, because the coffee was merely warm and the mugs were chipped in identical places, as though they'd all been dropped by the same person. But the booths did have high backs, and the window fogged nicely, and the girl behind the counter brought them two teas without being asked because Tomas was a regular and regulars were the only religion the place observed.

Tomas held the envelope in both hands for a full minute. Steam rose between them.

"Do you want me to step away?" Lior asked. "Truly. This might be private."

"No." Tomas's jaw set. It was the first time Lior had seen anything in the boy's face besides fear, and what arrived instead was stubbornness, which is fear's braver cousin. "If I read it alone, I'll convince myself I misunderstood it. I do that. Read it with me. Please."

He broke the wax seal. It crumbled like old toffee. Inside were four pages, both sides, in Ruth Ellery's attentive hand, and Tomas smoothed them flat on the table and began to read aloud, quietly, his voice steadying as it went, as though the words themselves were holding him up.

*"Tomas, my love, if you're reading this then someone has finally asked about Maren Voss, and I am sorry for what that will cost you, because I know better than most what it cost me.*

*"You were too young to remember the autumn of 1994. The town called it a drowning, then a runaway, then a tragedy, and then it called it nothing at all, which was the plan. I was there the night it was arranged. Not the death — there was no death, lad, hold on to that — the arrangement. Four of us sat in the back room of my own inn and signed statements saying Edmund Calloway was at dinner with us forty miles away, and he was not, and we knew he was not, and we signed anyway because our debts were forgiven and our fears were fed, and Vivienne Calloway could feed a fear like nobody I've ever known.*

*"I have regretted it every day for thirty years, which sounds noble and isn't. Regret is cheap. I kept living in the house the money built.*

*"Here is what I know and no one else living knows. Maren Voss did not drown in the mill race. Maren Voss walked out of Ashmoor alive, on her own two feet, helped by someone who loved her, and I have never known who, though I have my suspicions and I will take them to my grave because naming them would get someone killed. I know because I saw her. Three days after the town stopped looking, I saw her on the Leeds road with a rucksack, getting into a green car, and she looked frightened but she looked ALIVE, and I drove past because I had signed my name to a lie and I couldn't bear to look at the truth.*

*"Keep the diary safe. The diary is the message. Someone wrote those last entries knowing — KNOWING — that someday a person with eyes would read them and start asking. It was left where it would be found. That was deliberate. Whoever wrote it wanted a detective, even if it took thirty years to send for one.*

*"And Tomas — if the asking has started costing people their lives again, then burn nothing and trust no one whose comfort depends on the story staying buried. There are only three of us left who signed. Watch the other two. And watch Corin Blake, though I cannot tell you why, only that whenever Vivienne Calloway's name comes up in this town, Blake's face goes smooth as milk, and smooth faces are hiding something.*

*"I'm sorry, love. I should have spoken in 1994. Instead I sealed this and waited for a braver soul to come along. Don't be cross with me. Be careful with yourself. Some debts skip a generation, and I'd have paid this one gladly if my heart had let me live long enough.*

*"Your gran, who loved you, Ruth."*

The café noise rushed back in — the espresso machine, the radio, a woman laughing near the counter — all the ordinary sound that had somehow stopped existing while Tomas read. His hands were flat on the pages as though pinning down something that might fly.

"'Watch the other two,'" he repeated. "Gran said watch the other two. Mr. Thornbury — Father Anselm was one of the four, wasn't he? You told me you'd been asking questions, and then last week he—"

"Fell down the church stairs," Lior said.

"Fell," said Tomas, in a voice that made the word sound like the filthiest word in English.

"Yes." Lior took off his glasses and cleaned them on his jumper, which bought him five seconds. Through the fogged window, the high street went by, grey and wet and utterly unremarkable, and he thought: *a town can look like anything. It can look like this.* "Which means, by Ruth's arithmetic, two signatories remain. And one of them stopped answering her phone yesterday."

"Ruth said watch Corin Blake. Why would she say that? He's just a solicitor. He did our conveyancing when Gran sold the inn. He sent flowers. He sends flowers to *everyone*—"

"That," said Lior, putting his glasses back on, "is exactly what worries me. Men who send flowers to everyone are men who want to be remembered fondly. And men planning to leave the country want to be remembered fondly *very much indeed*." He slid the letter back across the table, folded along its original creases. "Tomas. I'm going to ask something of you that I have no right to ask, and you should refuse it, and I'll understand."

"What?"

"Stay visible. Keep your routines — the gym you never use, this café, your shifts. A missing witness frightens people; a present one is just a nuisance. And keep that letter somewhere new tonight, somewhere I don't know about, so that neither of us can give up what we don't have." He paused. "Because here's the truth of it, lad, and I won't dress it up: I can't protect you. I'm a bookseller with bad knees and a warrant card that expired when you were in primary school. Anyone who tells you they can protect you in this town is selling something. All I can offer is that whoever is tidying up 1994 will now have to go through me to get to you, and I am — I'm told — a remarkably irritating obstacle."

Tomas looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the rain had thickened, erasing the street in soft grey strokes, and the fogged window turned the whole town into something smudged and unfinished, a sketch of itself waiting for details.

"You knew her," Tomas said suddenly. "Maren. In 1994. That's why you asked about the diary. That's why you walk like that, like you're listening to the ground."

Lior was quiet long enough that the espresso machine screamed and died.

"I signed a report saying she drowned," he said at last. "I was twenty-six years old and I signed it because a senior officer told me to and because the alternative was a career that ended before it started. Your grandmother signed a lie for her debts. I signed mine for my future. Between us, Tomas, we've got the whole human condition covered." He stood, dropping coins on the table. "Come on. I'll walk you back the long way. And tomorrow morning, first thing, we're going to find out why a solicitor named Corin Blake has been sending flowers to a town that's forgotten how to ask questions."

Tomas gathered the letter, and then hesitated, one hand flat on the table.

"Mr. Thornbury. The green car on the Leeds road. Gran saw her alive." His voice dropped. "Do you think she's still—"

"Don't," Lior said gently. "Not yet. Hope's a load-bearing wall in this business. We lean on it carefully or the whole house comes down." He held the door, and the bell above it gave its canary cough, and the two of them stepped out into the rain together — the retired detective and the golden retriever, carrying a dead woman's confession between them like something hot.

Behind them, at the corner of the high street, a man in a good coat finished a phone call and did not follow, which was, in its way, more frightening than if he had.
