## 19. Two Funerals and a Wedding

The boathouse had given up its secrets the way old men give up opinions: reluctantly, and all at once.

Lior sat in his back room with everything spread across the desk that had once held his ledgers of stock-takes and supplier invoices. The real diary. Two train tickets to Inverness, dated the fourteenth of November, 1994 — three days after Maren Voss officially ceased to exist. An envelope of cash, brittle and yellowed, still banded. And the photograph: two young women on a beach he didn't recognize, arms slung around each other's shoulders, laughing at something outside the frame. On the back, in ballpoint: *M & W, June '95.*

June 1995. Seven months after both women were dead.

He'd been up most of the night with the real diary, and it was a different animal entirely from the forgery Odile had planted for some future detective to find. Where the forged entries were careful, this one was careless in the way only private writing can be — ink blots, crossed-out lines, a pressed flower between pages that had stained the paper brown. It stopped abruptly on the eleventh of November, mid-sentence, as if the writer had been interrupted by something more urgent than words.

Lior read it twice, then a third time, taking notes in the margins of a newspaper, because he refused to write in the diary itself. Some books you don't annotate. Some books you stand witness to.

And slowly, entry by entry, cross-referenced against the inquest file open on his laptop and the town he'd walked for thirty years without truly seeing, the shape of it came clear. Not the shape the town had agreed upon. Not even the shape Odile's forged entries had gestured toward.

The true shape was smaller than a conspiracy and larger than a crime. It was two girls and a rowboat.

* * *

**November 1994.**

The plan, such as it was, had been drawn up over three nights in the Voss kitchen, in pencil, on the back of a church raffle flyer — because Odile insisted that anything worth doing should be done on paper that could be burned without sentiment.

"You understand," Odile said, tapping the pencil against her teeth, "that if this goes wrong, I go down with you. Accessory before the fact. Obstruction. Probably wasting police time, which in this town is treated as blasphemy."

"I understand."

"And Father will disown you. What's left of him to do it with."

"I understand that too."

Maren did understand. What she understood, sitting at the kitchen table with her sister — serious Odile, twenty-six, already wearing cardigans like armor, already practicing her magistrate voice on the cat — was that understanding and being ready were different animals. Her hands wouldn't stop moving. She kept straightening things that were already straight.

"Stop tidying," Odile said. "You're about to be dead. Dead people don't fold tea towels."

That got the first laugh out of either of them in weeks, and it cracked something loose, and then they were both laughing — badly, hysterically, the laughter of people standing on a ledge — until Odile wiped her eyes and said, "Right. The boat."

The boat was the whole trick of it. Ashmoor's river was deep and cold and, in November, fast. A rowboat found capsized downstream of Miller's Bend, an oar washed up, one shoe on the bank — the shoe mattered, Odile insisted. Towns don't grieve bodies; they grieve shoes. A body would have required questions, an inquest with teeth. A shoe required only a headstone and a casserole.

"The Calloways will want it closed fast," Odile said, and there was something cold and precise in her voice that Maren had never heard from her before, something that sounded almost like their father. "Edmund's engagement dinner is in March. Vivienne will not have a drowned girl's inquest dragging scandal into February. They'll help us bury you, Maren. That's the joke of it. They'll do half the work themselves."

She was right, which was the worst part. When the boat was found — when Constable Peake stood on the muddy bank holding the single shoe with the reverence of a man handling a relic — the town performed its grief so efficiently, so *gratefully*, that no one thought to ask why a girl who couldn't swim had taken a boat out alone at dusk. Edmund Calloway sent flowers. Vivienne Calloway sent a larger wreath. The vicar spoke of the river taking whom it will, and everyone nodded, because the alternative was to ask what a Calloway engagement had to do with a Voss girl in the water, and asking that meant looking at things.

Three weeks later, at the funeral — Maren watched it from the churchyard wall, hood up, heart hammering, close enough to hear the vicar call her *a bright and dutiful daughter of this parish* — she saw her father weep into his handkerchief and felt the first cost of the plan arrive like a bill.

"He'll get over it," Odile whispered later, though they both knew he wouldn't, and they both knew Odile would spend thirty years bringing him his Sunday paper and watching him not get over it.

"You're crying," Maren said.

"I'm at your funeral. It's expected." Odile blew her nose with tremendous dignity. "Now come along. The dead don't linger in churchyards. It unsettles the living."

* * *

Lior set down his notes and rubbed his eyes. In the file, the timeline was clean: disappearance November 8th, boat recovered November 9th, inquest December 2nd, verdict accidental drowning, closed. He had attended that inquest himself, junior and silent, holding Edmund Calloway's alibi like a shield someone else had forged. He remembered thinking the whole thing moved too smoothly, like a stone skipping water — never quite sinking, never quite stopping.

Now he knew why. The stone had been thrown by the girl herself.

But the diary told him what the file couldn't: that the plan had accounted for everything except Wren Halloway.

* * *

**December 1994.**

Wren found out the way Wren found out everything — by refusing to be lied to.

They met in the ruined folly above Harrow Lane, the one with the ivy coming through the roof, where they had been meeting since they were sixteen, since the summer Maren had kissed her under the broken arch and the world had quietly rearranged itself around that fact without anyone's permission.

"You let me stand at your grave," Wren said. She wasn't crying. That was the frightening thing. Wren angry was loud and quick and over in a minute; Wren quiet was a weather system. "I put *flowers*, Maren. I cried on your headstone and apologized to you for not saving you, and the entire time you were — what? Watching? Was that funny?"

"It was the worst day of my life."

"Your life which *continued*."

"Wren—"

"No." She held up a hand. "Explain it to me like I'm stupid, because apparently I am. You faked your death rather than marry Edmund Calloway. Fine. Good. Applause. But you let *me* believe it, and you planned to just — go? To Inverness? Alone? Without telling me?" Her voice finally cracked, and she hated it, heaving in a breath to steady herself. "You decided my grief for you. You didn't even ask."

"There was nothing to ask. If you knew, you'd be in danger. If you came—"

"If I *came*?"

The silence between them went on so long that a blackbird started up somewhere in the ivy, absurdly cheerful.

"I'm coming," Wren said at last. "I want that understood. I'm not asking. I've spent nineteen years being managed by this town — my mother managing me, the vicar managing me, Edmund Calloway's friends managing me with their little smiles. Everyone in Ashmoor has a plan for me and none of them involve asking. So here's mine: I'm coming to Inverness, and we're going to be poor and cold and nobody will know our names, and it will be *magnificent*."

Maren looked at her — furious, tear-streaked, radiant — and felt the strange lightness that comes when a decision is taken out of your hands by someone who loves you.

"Odile will say it doubles the risk."

"Odile can pencil it onto a raffle flyer and burn it, like everything else."

* * *

In the present, Lior made tea he didn't drink. The kettle had clicked off ten minutes ago.

Here was the thing he kept turning over: the forged entries in the returned diary had said *she is safe where no one will look.* He'd assumed the line referred to Maren. Reading the real diary now, he understood it might have referred to all of them — to the whole apparatus of the lie, hidden in plain sight inside a town that had signed a collective agreement not to look. Ashmoor hadn't failed to notice the truth. Ashmoor had *declined* it, politely, the way you decline a second helping.

And one other thing nagged at him, small and cold: the photograph was dated June 1995. The tickets were for November 1994. Between those dates, according to every record Ashmoor possessed, Wren Halloway had gone on living — working at the inn, singing in the choir, being seen — and then, six months after Maren's drowning, she had drowned too. Suicide, said the verdict. Grief, said the vicar. Nobody asked why a grieving girl's suitcase was never found, or why her mother refused for years to have the grave marked.

The real diary explained it. The last entries weren't about escape anymore. They were about *movement* — a feeling of being watched in Inverness, a man asking questions at a boarding house, a car with southern plates parked too long. And then the final entry, the interrupted one:

*Odile says we should split up but I won't, I won't, whatever is coming we—*

Whatever was coming had a name, and Lior already knew it, because Ruth Ellery's letter had named the family that paid for silence: the Calloways. Not Edmund, who had perhaps genuinely loved her in his shallow, proprietary way. Vivienne. Vivienne Calloway, who had arranged a false alibi to protect her son from a scandal, and who had discovered — how? A sighting? A letter intercepted? — that the drowned girl was breathing air somewhere north, and understood instantly what that meant. Not that Maren was alive. That the *drowning was fake*. That the Calloway name was attached, forever, to a fraud that any vindictive girl could detonate at will.

Vivienne hadn't hunted Maren to punish her. She'd hunted her to *manage* her. There was, Lior was learning, very little difference.

* * *

**Spring 1995.**

They ran for six months, and by every account the diary gives — and it gives them generously, the pages filling with a joy that hurts to read — they were the best six months of either of their lives.

A cottage outside Ullapool, rent paid in cash, landlady incurious. Wren waiting tables and singing in a pub where nobody corrected her accent. Maren working at a bookbinder's, of all things, her hands finally making things instead of hiding them. They walked the cliffs. They learned the names of seabirds. They fought about money and dishes and whose turn it was to fetch the coal, gloriously ordinary fights, the kind of fights you can only have when no one is watching.

"We should get married," Wren said one night, apropos of nothing, drying her hair by the fire.

"With what? Rings? We're eating potatoes for the fourth time this week."

"Potato weddings are traditional in Scotland. Look it up."

"I *am* Scottish now, technically. I'd know."

"Maren." Wren knelt on the hearth rug, dripping, ridiculous, dead these seven months in the eyes of God and Ashmoor. "Marry me. Badly. With no rings and no witnesses and terrible potatoes."

So they did — a handfasting on the cliff path at dawn, a ribbon borrowed from a flour sack, vows they wrote themselves and laughed through half of. No register, no license, no legal weight whatsoever. Two dead girls marrying each other in the wind.

It was, the diary says, the happiest day of her life, and she wrote it knowing — because Odile's letters, forwarded through three hands, had begun to carry warnings — that happiness on borrowed time accrues interest.

* * *

Lior had to stop reading after that page. He took off his glasses and sat in the dark of his back room, listening to the shop tick and settle, and thought about the word *verdict.* Thirty years ago a coroner had said *accidental death* and a town had said *how sad* and a family had said *good, that's settled,* and none of it was a verdict. This was the verdict: two girls on a cliff with a flour-sack ribbon, loving each other in defiance of every institution that had jurisdiction over their lives. That was the finding. That was what should have been entered into the record.

Instead the record said: *Wren Halloway, found in the water off Crag Point, October 1995. Death by misadventure.*

The diary's final pages tell what actually happened, in handwriting that deteriorates from sentence to sentence, as if the pen were being gripped harder and harder.

Vivienne's man — a solicitor's investigator, patient and polite — had traced them through a pharmacy. Wren's mother's heart medication, ordered monthly, forwarded north. A kindness that killed. On the third of October, the man came to the cottage while Maren was in town. He didn't threaten. He offered terms: return to England, sign a statement recanting the fraud, accept a settlement, live quietly under supervision. Or Wren's presence — Wren's *survival*, Wren's complicity — would be reported, and both girls would face charges, and the Halloway name would be dragged through exactly the mud the Voss name had been spared.

Wren heard the car coming up the track. Wren, who had spent her whole life being managed, made the one decision that was entirely hers: she ran the other way, out the back, down toward the sea road in the dark, to draw him off, to buy Maren time.

The coast road above Crag Point was wet. Whether she slipped, whether she swerved from headlights, whether — and here the diary simply stops speculating, because Maren could not bear to speculate — the car followed her too closely, no one ever established. She was found the next morning by a fisherman.

Maren buried her. There was no other option; a missing person inquiry would unravel everything. The diary describes it in eleven words, and Lior has never been able to forget the eleven words: *I dug until my hands broke and then I kept digging.* A cairn above the tide line, a ribbon from a flour sack weighted with stones. Then Maren burned the cottage's traces, took the name Elodie from nowhere and everywhere, and walked south with a rucksack and a grief the size of a county.

She did not come home. Coming home meant handing Vivienne Calloway the power of exposure forever. Staying away meant staying dead. She chose dead, and she chose it for thirty years, and somewhere in those thirty years she must have learned what became of her father, her sister, her grave with its plastic flowers refreshed each November by persons unknown.

Persons unknown. Odile, Lior thought. Every year. Of course.

* * *

He finished the tea, cold, in one swallow, and wrote a final note on the newspaper margin: *Verdict delivered 3 Oct 1995, Crag Point. Court: the sea. Judge: circumstance. Nobody appealed.*

Then he sat with the photograph again — M and W, June '95, laughing — and let himself feel the full weight of what the town had done, what *he* had done, signing that report, closing that file, going back to his desk while a living girl's grave was being dug in a churchyard by proxy.

His phone buzzed against the desk. Tomas.

"Lior—" The boy's voice was wrong, thin and fast. "I tried Ruth's number again, and then I went round. The care agency's got a notice on the door. They won't say anything else on the phone, they said next of kin only, but Lior — Mrs. Ellery's son lives in Carlisle, and he's *on his way*, and they've moved her to the hospital, and nobody will tell me if she's—"

Lior was already reaching for his coat.

"Tomas. Listen to me. Witness number two doesn't get to become witness number one's encore. Which hospital?"

There was a pause, and then, very quietly: "The one in Ashmoor. They brought her back here last night. Lior — she came home to die, and I think somebody knows I've been asking about her."

Lior looked at the photograph of two girls laughing on a beach, seven months past their own deaths, and picked up his keys.

"Hold the door of that hospital," he said, "and don't let anyone in who looks like paperwork."
