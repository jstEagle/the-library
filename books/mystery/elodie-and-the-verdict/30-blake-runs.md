## 30. Blake Runs

The letter ran in the *Ashmoor Courier* on a Thursday, tucked between an advertisement for gutter cleaning and the parish council's ongoing dispute about the war memorial's hedge. Lior had insisted on that placement. A confession hidden in plain sight was safer than one shouted from rooftops, and besides, he'd learned long ago that nobody in Ashmoor read past the second page unless there was a funeral in it.

*To the people of Ashmoor,* it began. *I am Maren Voss. I did not die in 1994. I have lived, and I remember everything, and on the thirtieth anniversary of my disappearance I intend to come home and tell you all of it — including the names of those who helped me vanish, and those who tried to make certain I stayed vanished.*

It was a lie, of course. Or rather, it was a lie built on top of a truth so large it could bear the weight. Lior had written it at his kitchen table at two in the morning, the scorched diary open beside him like a witness, and he had felt something he hadn't felt since his last day at the Ashmoor constabulary: the clean, cold pleasure of setting a trap he actually believed would close.

Now it was Saturday morning, the eve of the anniversary vigil, and the trap was closing.

"He's moving," Tomas said.

They were sitting in Lior's ancient Vauxhall, parked with criminal nonchalance outside the bakery on Fore Street, which opened at five to supply the hotels and therefore provided perfect cover for two men drinking terrible coffee and pretending to read a newspaper. The fog had come off the estuary overnight, thick as wool, swallowing the streetlamps whole. It turned Ashmoor into a rumor of itself: gables floating, gulls crying somewhere overhead like ghosts with opinions.

"Define moving," Lior said.

"Lights on. Car out of the garage. He's loading things into the boot." Tomas lowered the binoculars — actual binoculars, purchased specially, because Lior had refused to let him use his phone camera after explaining, at length, what metadata was. "Two bags. Maybe three. He's wearing — is that a cravat? Who wears a cravat to flee the country?"

"A man who has never once been inconvenienced in his life," Lior said. He took the binoculars. Through the fog-blurred glass, Corin Blake's house glowed pale and orderly, and Blake himself moved through his front garden with the unhurried precision of a man closing out accounts. Which, Lior thought, was exactly what he was doing. Thirty years of quiet payments, sealed records, and tidied deaths, and now the ledger wouldn't balance. So he was liquidating. Running the numbers one final time and taking the remainder abroad.

"He'll go to the marina," Lior said. "His boat's moored there. *Persephone.*"

"You know the name of his boat?"

"I know the name of every boat in that marina. I've had thirty years and very little else to do." He handed the binoculars back. "Here's what happens now. He drives down. He loads whatever documents and cash he couldn't bring himself to burn — and he won't have burned them, men like him keep receipts even for their sins, especially for their sins — and then he waits for first light, because Blake will not take *Persephone* out in fog he can't see through. He's careful. Careful men are slow."

"And we follow him."

"We follow him." Lior looked at the boy — because that was what Tomas still was, under the fear and the stubbornness; a boy who'd found a diary in his grandmother's attic and hadn't yet learned that some doors are better left shut. "Tomas. Last chance. You could be home asleep. Nobody would think less of you."

Tomas gave him a look that was, Lior had to admit, almost insultingly familiar. It was the look Ruth Ellery must have given people, once, before the years wore her polite.

"You asked me to help," Tomas said. "You don't get to un-ask me when it gets interesting."

Lior sighed. "That's exactly what I was afraid of."

* * *

Blake left at ten past six, driving too slowly, which told Lior more than speeding would have. Guilty men either crawl or bolt; the crawling ones are the dangerous ones, because they're still managing themselves. The Volvo slid down Fore Street and turned toward the water, wipers ticking against fog that had no intention of clearing.

Lior waited ninety seconds — long enough to lose sight, short enough not to lose him — and followed.

The road to the marina ran along the estuary's edge, hedged on one side by blackthorn and on the other by nothing at all, just the drop to the mudflats where the tide went out and exposed the town's old bones: rusted mooring rings, a half-sunk dinghy, the ribs of some boat dead so long it had become geography. In the fog it was all suggestion. Lior drove with his lights off and his heart going like a metronome set by someone nervous.

"He's turning in," Tomas said, twisted around in his seat. "Gate's open. There's a night watchman — old Bill Ferris, I think, he used to drink with my gran—"

"Everyone in this town drank with your gran."

"She was popular," Tomas said, with dignity.

Bill Ferris waved Blake through without so much as lifting his thermos, which meant either that Blake had paid him or that Bill had reached the age where the entire world was someone else's problem. Lior parked fifty yards back, behind a boat trailer furred with moss, and killed the engine.

The marina in fog was a strange, drowned place. Masts rose out of the white like the masts of a sunken fleet, ropes knocking against aluminum in small irregular applause. Somewhere a halyard clanged, patient and tuneless. The water lay flat and gray-green, holding its breath.

Blake walked the pontoon to his berth, unlocked *Persephone*'s cabin, and began carrying things aboard. Bags first. Then, carefully, wrapped in what looked like a plastic document sleeve, a folder — thick, the kind solicitors used, the kind that held lives folded into triplicate. Lior watched him stow it beneath the helm seat and felt the old detective's certainty settle over him like a coat: that folder was the case. Thirty years of it. Payments, instructions, the names of everyone who'd ever signed anything they shouldn't have. Men like Blake didn't destroy evidence they might need for leverage; they packed it.

"He's going to wait for the fog to lift," Lior murmured. "Sunrise is at seven-twelve. He'll want visibility all the way down the estuary and out past the point. That gives us—" he checked his watch— "forty minutes."

"Forty minutes for what?"

"For Odile to get here."

Tomas went very still. "She's coming? Here?"

"She insisted." Lior kept his eyes on the pontoon. "I told her to stay away. She informed me that she had spent thirty years staying away, and that her days of taking direction from men who thought they were protecting her were over. Her words. I'd quote her more precisely but there was profanity, and I was raised to be shocked by it."

Despite everything, Tomas smiled. Then the smile faded. "And if Blake sees her? If he realizes—"

"Then he runs early, and we're standing on a pontoon watching him go, and I spend the rest of my life writing letters to newspapers that nobody reads." Lior flexed his hands against the steering wheel. They ached in the cold, the way they always did now, the arthritis arriving punctually as any witness. "But he won't see her. Not in this. Nobody sees anything in this."

As if to contradict him, the fog chose that moment to thin — just slightly, just enough that the far shore became a smudge instead of a theory, and the bell buoy at the harbor mouth began to sound, mournful and regular, counting out the tide.

Blake appeared on deck. He stood at the rail for a long moment, looking out at the water, and even at this distance Lior could read the set of his shoulders: not fear, exactly. Calculation. A man measuring a fog against a schedule.

Then Blake did something unexpected. He took out his phone, made a call — brief, thirty seconds, maybe less — and put it away.

Lior's stomach dropped through the hull of the car.

"That wasn't a goodbye call," he said quietly.

"How do you know?"

"Because he smiled at the end of it. People don't smile at goodbyes." He started the engine, low, barely a purr. "He called someone. Someone who's supposed to be handling something for him. Which means either he has help waiting downriver—" 

"—or he's tying off another loose end," Tomas finished. His face had gone the color of the fog.

Lior pulled out his own phone and dialed Odile. It rang four times. Five. Six. He watched Blake move briskly along the pontoon, casting off lines with practiced efficiency, and the phone rang on, and on, and each ring was a small cold stone dropping into him.

Pick up. Pick up, you magnificent, impossible woman—

"Odile Voss," she said at last, crisp as a court summons.

"It's me. Are you on the water yet?"

"I'm at the slipway. Wren's old uncle's boat, if you must know — the man has never once asked me why I want to borrow it, which tells you everything about this town." An engine noise behind her voice, the rattle of an elderly outboard being coaxed awake. "Is he there?"

"He's casting off. And he made a call, Odile. Right before departure. Somebody's doing something for him right now, and I don't know what."

There was a pause. When she spoke again, the magistrate's composure was still there, but underneath it Lior heard the other thing, the thing thirty years of silence had compressed into a hard bright core. "Then we stop guessing and we follow him. Lior. Whatever happens out there today — whatever he says, whatever he offers — do not let him be the only one telling the story. That's how this town got the way it is. One person telling the story, everyone else agreeing to listen."

"Poetic," Lior said. "Get on the water."

He hung up. On the pontoon, *Persephone*'s engine coughed, caught, settled into a low diesel thrum. Blake stood at the helm in his ridiculous cravat, and the boat began to slide backward out of its berth, slow and stately, a man leaving a country he'd spent his whole life quietly owning.

"Go," Tomas said. "Go, go, go—"

"I'm going, I'm going." Lior swung the Vauxhall out from behind the boat trailer and down toward the public slip, gravel popping under the tires. Ahead, the fog was beginning to lift in ragged strips, dawn coming up the color of pewter, and the bell buoy rang on, and somewhere out on that gray water an old woman in a borrowed boat was already moving.

* * *

The estuary at dawn, when the fog breaks, is the most beautiful lie in Devon. The water goes silver and pink at the edges, the mudflats shine like hammered metal, the birds wheel and cry as though the whole scene has been arranged for their benefit. Lior had grown up on this water. He knew its moods the way he knew the spines of his books: the way it lied about depth, the way the channel shifted after every winter storm, the way the current past the point could take a small boat and make it someone else's problem forty miles out to sea.

He also knew that Corin Blake knew none of this. Blake was a man of ledgers and offices. His boat was a toy he kept for fine summer Sundays, and Lior would have bet the bookshop — what remained of it — that Blake had never once taken *Persephone* out in anything worse than a light chop.

Which was why, twenty minutes later, watching *Persephone* wallow through the swell past the harbor mouth, Lior allowed himself the first real hope he'd felt since the fire.

"He's fighting the helm," he said. They were in Ruth Ellery's nephew's fishing boat — Tomas had produced it with the sheepish air of a boy revealing a rabbit from a hat, and Lior had decided not to ask questions he didn't want answered. The boat was old, ugly, and utterly at home in the estuary chop, which was more than could be said for either of them. Tomas was at the tiller, white-knuckled, and Lior stood braced against the gunwale with the wind pulling tears sideways across his face. "Look at him. He's overcorrecting. Every wave, he corrects, then overcorrects again. He's driving it like a car."

"Shouldn't we be closer?"

"We should be exactly this far. Close enough to keep him, far enough he doesn't think about us. He thinks we're ash, Tomas. He thinks the shop burned and the old man gave up. Let him keep thinking it." Although, Lior admitted privately, the arithmetic was uncomfortable: one dying ex-detective, one terrified apprentice, one borrowed fishing boat, versus a solicitor with a fast cabin cruiser, a folder full of sins, and presumably money enough to buy whatever came next. It was not, on paper, a pursuit. It was a complaint.

The bell buoy fell astern. Past the point, the estuary widened and shallowed, channels braiding through the flats, and Blake made his choice: he turned down the deep-water channel, heading for the open sea, and Lior felt his hope snag on something.

Because the deep-water channel was not the fastest route out. Anyone local knew that. At this state of the tide, with the ebb running, the smart play was the eastern passage — narrower, trickier, but it saved twenty minutes and got you past the bar before the swell built. Blake was taking the long way. The safe way. The way a man takes when he doesn't know the water and doesn't trust it.

Or the way a man takes when he's planning to meet someone.

"There," Tomas said suddenly, pointing. "Off the starboard side. Is that—"

A small boat, low in the water, riding at anchor near the channel marker. Gray hull, no name visible, one figure aboard. As they watched, the figure lifted an arm — not waving. Signaling.

Blake's cruiser altered course toward it.

Lior's mouth went dry. The transfer. Of course. Documents and cash passed mid-channel, the courier boat taking the evidence one direction while *Persephone* continued to sea, so that even if Blake were stopped, he'd be clean. It was elegant. It was exactly the sort of arrangement a man inherits from Vivienne Calloway and polishes for thirty years.

And it meant that in approximately four minutes, thirty years of evidence was going to change hands, and after that it would be gone — sunk, scattered, burned — and the law would have nothing, again, the way it had nothing in 1994, and the way it had nothing after the fire.

"No," Lior said aloud. It came out rougher than he intended. "No, you don't."

"What are we doing?" Tomas's voice cracked on the last word. "Lior, what are we *doing*?"

What they were doing, Lior thought, was the thing he'd sworn off thirty years ago in a report room that smelled of stale tea: playing judge and jury and executioner of other people's arrangements. But Odile's words were in his ears — *don't let him be the only one telling the story* — and the bell buoy was ringing behind them like a verdict, and the fog was burning off the water in long silver banners, and Corin Blake was about to hand his life's work to a stranger in a gray boat.

"We're introducing ourselves," Lior said.

He grabbed the tiller from Tomas and opened the old outboard all the way. The fishing boat surged, wallowed, planed, spray coming green over the bow, and ahead of them *Persephone* slowed beside the anchored gray boat, and Blake stepped to his rail with the document folder in both hands, and the distance between them began, at last, to close.

Behind the gray boat, half-hidden in the last rag of fog, Lior saw something that made his chest seize — a second hull, small and silent, an elderly outboard ticking over, and a familiar straight-backed silhouette at its tiller.

Odile had arrived first.

"Hold on," Lior shouted, to Tomas, to the morning, to thirty years of silence. "Hold on!"

Blake looked up from the folder in his hands — and saw them coming.
