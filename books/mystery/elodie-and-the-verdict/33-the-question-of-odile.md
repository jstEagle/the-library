## 33. The Question of Odile

The county prosecutor's office smelled of toner and old coffee, a smell Lior had always associated with the particular kind of tiredness that comes from believing in paperwork. He sat on a plastic chair outside Conference Room B with his hat — he had bought himself a hat for the occasion, a soft brown thing that made him feel like a man who attended hearings — and watched through the frosted glass as three shapes moved behind it like fish in a tank.

One of the shapes was Odile Voss.

She had dressed for it the way she had dressed for everything in the thirty years Lior had known of her: immaculately, correctly, in a grey suit that said *I am sorry for your inconvenience* before anyone had spoken. Her hair was pinned. Her hands were folded. She looked less like a woman facing charges than a magistrate waiting for a late clerk.

Which, of course, was exactly what she had been, right up until six weeks ago, when she had stood in the ashes of his bookshop and told him everything.

"You can go in now," said a young woman with a clipboard, pronouncing it the way one announces a dental appointment.

Lior went in.

* * *

There were three of them: Prosecutor Halloway — no relation to Wren, though Lior had watched him flinch when the name came up in the first session, everyone in Ashmoor flinched at names now — and two of his people, a brisk man called Dench and a younger solicitor named Priya Nair, who had been assigned to the case because, Lior suspected, she was the only person in the building under fifty who could read a 1994 inquest file without laughing.

"Mr. Thornbury," Halloway said, gesturing at a chair. "Thank you for coming back."

"You keep asking me to."

"We do." Halloway rubbed his eyes. He was a decent man, Lior thought, which was worse than being a bad one; bad men slept well. "Let me summarize where we are, and then you can tell me — again — why I should tear up my own file."

On the table between them lay the anatomy of Odile Voss's guilt, arranged with loving precision:

Item one: the forged diary entries. Twenty-three of them, written over three weeks in 1994 in an imitation of her sister's hand — slanted, hurried, deliberately imperfect — describing Maren Voss's continued existence after her disappearance. Entries designed, eventually, to be found.

Item two: obstruction. Thirty years of it. The sealed records, the steered questions, the quiet word here and there whenever anyone drifted too close to the truth. A garden, tended.

Item three: the boathouse cache, which Odile had maintained, visited, and protected since 1994, containing Maren's real diary, train tickets, cash, and a photograph of two women laughing by a lake, dated four months after both their deaths.

"And against that," Halloway said, tapping the pile, "we have what she *gave* us. Her confession. Corroborating testimony in the Blake prosecution. The recording apparatus at the marina — hers. And the fact that without her, Edmund Calloway's alibi would still be sitting in a drawer looking airtight, and Corin Blake would be retiring to Portugal with three dead witnesses behind him and no one the wiser." He looked up. "So you understand my problem."

"I understand it better than you do," Lior said. "I've had it for thirty years."

Dench made a small sound, the bureaucratic equivalent of clearing one's throat. "Mr. Thornbury, with respect, you're not a party to these proceedings. You're a witness. A helpful one, but—"

"He's the reason any of this is happening," Nair said quietly, and then seemed surprised at herself for saying it out loud.

Halloway held up a hand. "Go on, Mr. Thornbury. Make your case. You've clearly been rehearsing it."

He had. He'd rehearsed it into the dark ceiling of his bedroom, into the kettle's steam, into the ears of Tomas, who had heard it so many times he now mouthed along. But in the room, with the folder sitting there like a body on a slab, he found he didn't want the rehearsal. He wanted the truth, plain, the way he'd finally learned to say it.

"Prosecutor," he said, "when I was twenty-six years old, I signed a report closing the Voss case. I didn't believe a word of it. Four witnesses, all lying, all paid, and I knew it, and I signed anyway, because a senior officer told me to and because the Calloway name was worth more in Ashmoor than the truth was. That signature is the single worst thing I've ever done, and I include the year I sold romance novels at full markup." Nair almost smiled. "Everything I've done since — reopening the case, losing my shop, standing in front of you now — has been an attempt to unsign that report. It can't be done. Signatures don't work that way."

"No," Halloway agreed.

"But Odile Voss found a way to do something like it. Look at those forged entries again. Really look. She wrote them badly *on purpose*. Slanted, hurried, wrong. Any competent forger — and she was a law student at the time, she understood documents — could have produced a convincing imitation. Instead she produced one that a half-attentive detective would catch within a week. She wasn't hiding the truth. She was leaving a door open in a house the town had bricked shut. Those entries were not lies. They were lifelines. They were a message in a bottle thrown every few years, hoping someday the sea would give it to someone who could read."

"The sea gave it to you," Dench said. "Thirty years later. Three people died in the meantime."

Lior felt that one land, because it was true, and he let it sit for a moment before answering.

"Yes. Corin Blake killed three people because he thought the lie needed maintaining. Do you know what would have happened if Odile had never written those entries?" He leaned forward. "Nothing. Nothing forever. Blake retires rich. Ruth Ellery dies with her letter unopened. Maren Voss stays dead on paper until she's dead everywhere else. And the town of Ashmoor goes on being proud of how well it remembers nothing." He tapped the folder. "You're holding the murder weapon, Prosecutor. But you're also holding the distress flare. Same object. It matters which end you look at."

* * *

They broke for an hour. Lior ate a sandwich on the steps of the courthouse, watching the traffic go by — Ashmoor traffic, which meant a tractor, two dogs, and a delivery van whose driver honked at him in a way that might have been recognition or might have been the road narrowing.

Tomas found him there, pale as ever, carrying two coffees like they were evidence.

"They're going to charge her," Tomas said. "Aren't they. I heard Dench on the phone. 'We can't be seen to condone it.' Condone. Like she's a parking violation."

"She obstructed justice for thirty years, Tomas. From where they sit, that's not a parking violation. It's the whole car park."

"But she's the reason—" He stopped, sat down heavily, coffee sloshing. "My gran spent her last years afraid of her own telephone. Because of what she did for the Calloways. And Odile Voss spent hers afraid of her own conscience, which is worse, if you ask me, because at least Gran got to forget between phone calls." He stared at the cup. "Is it wrong that I want them to leave her alone? She read me stories when I was small. At the library. She did all the voices."

"That's not wrong," Lior said. "That's the whole question. The law is very good at counting what people did. It's terrible at weighing why. That's why we have people like Halloway, and why we argue with them."

"Will it help? Arguing?"

Lior thought about the folder, about signatures, about the difference between a lie and a lifeline.

"It helped once already," he said. "It just took thirty years to reach the right desk."

* * *

The afternoon session was different, because Odile spoke.

She had declined to speak in the morning — "my counsel will address the facts" — but something in Lior's little speech, or perhaps simply the exhaustion of thirty years of silence, had loosened whatever clamp she kept on herself. When Halloway asked her, gently, whether she understood the gravity of forging documents, she set down her pen, aligned it precisely with the edge of the table, and said:

"Do you know what my sister asked me for, Mr. Halloway? Not money. Not a plan. She asked me to write her eulogy. In advance. In her own hand, so the town would believe she was gone." Her voice was level, which somehow made it worse. "She was nineteen. She had been told she was going to marry a man she didn't love because loving the person she did love was, in Ashmoor, in 1994, a disease to be cured. Our father said so. The vicar said so. Edmund Calloway's mother said so while smiling, which is its own special kind of cruelty. So yes. I forged documents. I forged a death. I forged twenty-three diary entries over three weeks with my hands shaking so badly I had to write each one twice." She unfolded her hands, and Lior saw, for the first time in thirty years of knowing her, that they were not steady either. "And then I kept the lie alive for three decades because the alternative was letting the town dig up my sister and hand her to the people who broke her. If that is obstruction, then obstruct me. But I will not apologize for the only honest work I've ever done, which was dishonest from the first stroke of the pen."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a photocopier finished its run and sighed.

Nair was writing fast. Halloway was looking at the folder as though it had changed weight.

"Mrs. Voss," he said at last — Mrs., not Magistrate; even he had stopped knowing what to call her — "the entries. The ones in the returned diary. The ones Mr. Thornbury received. Did you write those recently?"

Odile looked at Lior. It was the first time she'd looked at him all day.

"No," she said. "Those are the originals. 1994. I've rewritten them eleven times over the years — better paper, better ink, fresher mistakes — and buried each version somewhere it might be found. The attic. The church jumble sale. A donation bin in Leeds. This last one, I put in a box marked for the bookshop, because I'd heard the owner used to be a detective." A pause, dry as August. "I'd also heard he was retired. It was a gamble on rust."

"You gambled three deaths on my curiosity," Lior said.

"I gambled thirty years of silence on it," she answered. "The deaths belong to Corin Blake. I'll carry mine. But I won't carry his, and neither, Mr. Thornbury, should you. Guilt is not a communal pot. Everyone contributes their own."

* * *

They adjourned again. Decisions, Halloway said, would take days. There would be consultation with the Crown Prosecution Service, with the judge in the Blake trial, with "stakeholders," a word Lior had come to loathe, since the stakeholders in question included a woman living under another name who could not legally exist.

He walked out past the conference room one last time and found Odile alone at the table, repacking her bag with the same precision she brought to everything.

"They'll charge me," she said, without looking up. "Something suspended, probably. They have to. The town needs to see the scales move, even a little."

"Does that frighten you?"

"Good heavens, no." She snapped the bag shut. "Do you know what frightens me, Lior? That it's nearly over. For thirty years I've had a purpose, even a shameful one. Guard the door. Keep the garden. Every morning I woke up and knew exactly what I was for." She stood, and for a moment she was not a magistrate or a defendant or a sister keeping a grave-shaped secret; she was just a tired woman in a good grey suit. "Soon I'll wake up and there will be nothing left to guard. Just an ordinary day. I genuinely don't know how people bear them."

"You'll learn," Lior said. "I did. It takes about ten years and a bookshop."

That earned him the ghost of a smile — the first real one he'd seen from her since the ashes. Then it faded, and she said, quietly, "There's something you should know. Something I haven't told the prosecutors, because it isn't theirs yet."

He waited.

"She reads the papers. My sister. She always has — I send them, or I did, before all this. Which means she's read every word about Blake, about the trial, about the shop." Odile's eyes came up to his, and they were bright and fierce and terrified all at once. "And yesterday, for the first time in thirty years, she wrote to me. One line. No return address. It said—" Odile's voice caught, steadied. "It said, *'Tell the bookseller I'm thinking of coming to see what he built.'*"

Lior stood very still, the way he'd learned to stand when something precious was nearby and might startle.

"When?" he managed.

"Any day," Odile said. "Maybe tomorrow. Maybe in an hour. Thirty years, Lior, and she's decided — because of you, because of a burned bookshop and a newspaper letter and God knows what stubbornness — that she wants to come home." She picked up her bag. "So whatever you're doing when she arrives, make sure it's worth walking into."

She left him alone in Conference Room B, with its toner smell and its folders and its fish-tank light, and Lior Thornbury realized his hands were shaking — not with fear, and not quite with joy, but with the strange vertigo of a man who has spent a decade hiding behind a counter and is about to hear the bell above the door ring for the most important customer of his life.

He bought flowers on the way home. He couldn't have said for whom.
