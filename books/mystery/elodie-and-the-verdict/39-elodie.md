## 39. Elodie

The sign arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day Lior had always done his accounts, back when the accounts were worth doing, and there was a symmetry to it he suspected Maren Voss would appreciate. Symmetry had been her trade, after all. She had spent thirty years building a life out of rearranged letters and borrowed names, and she had done it with the patience of a woman setting type by hand.

The carpenter, a broad, cheerful man named Denny Falk who had once, in another life, sold Lior a secondhand ladder and never let him forget it, climbed down from the scaffolding and squinted up at his work.

"Well?" he said. "Say something. I've been up there since eight and my arms have opinions."

Lior looked at the sign.

**ELODIE'S BOOKS**

The letters were painted a deep, quiet green — the green of the moor in early summer, the green of the boathouse door before the weather ate it. The apostrophe was small and slightly crooked, and Lior found he liked it better for that.

"It's good," he said.

"Good," Denny repeated, wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white and now belonged to the trade. "I repaint a man's shop after it's been burned to the frame, and I get *good*." He grinned to take the sting out of it. "Your apprentice said you'd want it exactly that shade. Wouldn't budge on it. Made me mix it three times."

Tomas, inside, was shelving with the fierce concentration of a young man who had discovered that work could be a form of penance and had decided to overpay. He'd been at Thornbury's for four months now, since the spring, since the arrests, since everything. He still flinched a little at loud noises. He still checked the door twice before locking up. But his hands had steadied, and when he talked about books — which he did, endlessly, with a convert's zeal — his voice had found a floor it hadn't had before.

Lior stepped inside. The shop smelled of new timber and old paper, the two smells arguing politely, neither winning. Half the shelves were stocked. The other half waited, patient as an audience before the lights go down.

"Tomas," Lior said. "The sign's up."

Tomas came around the biography section at a speed that suggested he'd been waiting all morning for permission to look. He stood in the doorway a long moment.

"She picked the green," he said. "I just remembered it."

"You remembered it exactly."

Tomas shrugged, embarrassed. "It was on the photograph. In the boathouse. The one of her and Wren." He hesitated. "She's wearing a green coat. Laughing. You can tell the coat's the best thing she owns and she doesn't care even a little."

Lior nodded. He had spent a long time with that photograph, in the months when it had been evidence and then, mercifully, stopped being evidence and become what it had always been: two young women on a sea wall, dated the summer after the town had buried them both, alive and squinting into the sun with the particular insolence of the living.

The bell over the door rang. It was a new bell; the old one had melted. Maren had brought it herself, wrapped in newspaper, and said, "Every shop should sound like itself," and Lior had hung it without asking what she meant, because he was learning that some things you simply waited on.

She came in on Tuesdays now. She had been coming in on Tuesdays since March, and every week she bought nothing, browsed everything, and stayed for exactly one cup of tea, and every week she left without saying the thing she had come to say, and Lior let her. He was a patient man by long training. He had once waited eleven months for a forger to slip on a single letterform, and he could certainly wait for a woman to decide who she was going to be in her own town.

Today she stopped in the doorway and looked up at the sign through the glass, and Lior watched her do it, and watched the moment land.

Her hand went to her mouth. Not the way people do in films — no theatrical gasp — but the small, involuntary way, the way you touch your face when something reaches you before your manners can get in front of it.

Then she looked at Lior through the window, and her expression was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she should have done months ago.

She came in.

"You knew," she said. Not a question. Her voice had a lowland burr now, thirty years of a different county settled into it like silt, but underneath it was still the voice from the early diary entries, the one that had written about Wren Halloway as though the name were a place she lived.

"Knew what?" said Lior, which was cowardly, and they both knew it.

Maren — Elodie, he corrected himself, and the correction felt less like a betrayal than he'd expected — walked to the counter and set both hands flat on it, the way she did when she was about to be precise.

"The name. *Elodie's Books.*" She tapped the counter once. "I never told anyone that name. Not Odile. Not the solicitor who did the new papers. Not the woman in Cornwall who rented me the cottage and asked, the way people do, *and what shall I call you?* I chose it out of a book when I was nineteen and I never spoke it aloud to another living soul. It was mine. It was the one thing in the whole world that was only mine." Her eyes were bright and level. "So how does a retired policeman in Ashmoor come to paint it over his door?"

Lior poured the tea. He'd learned that she took it strong, with too much milk, the way the diary said Wren had made it, and that she never commented on this, and that he was never to comment on it either.

"Chapter two," he said.

"What?"

"Of your diary. The first entry. The one dated the twelfth of June, 1994 — three weeks before you vanished." He set her cup down. "You wrote: *'Elodie is what I would be, if I were brave. I have decided to keep her somewhere safe, the way you keep a dress you can't wear yet. Elodie reads all day in a shop that smells of rain. Elodie is nobody's daughter and nobody's fiancée and nobody's mistake. Elodie is mine.'*"

Maren had gone very still.

"You wrote it in your own hand, in your own diary, and then you hid the diary in your sister's attic, and then you spent thirty years being a person who was not brave enough to be Elodie — until, one day, you were." Lior sat down across from her. "I read that entry the first night. Before I knew anything. Before the forged entries, before the inquest file, before any of it. And I remember sitting in that chair—" he nodded at the armchair by the poetry section, the one with the burnt-and-reupholstered cushion, "—and thinking, *that's not a wish, that's an itinerary.* People who write like that are writing instructions to themselves."

The bell rang as someone passed outside. Neither of them looked.

"You knew since the beginning," Maren said slowly. "Before the boathouse. Before Odile confessed. You knew the name I'd choose."

"I knew the name you'd *already chosen*. That's different." Lior wrapped his hands around his own cup. "Knowing a thing and taking it from someone are very different crimes, and I've spent my life learning the difference. It wasn't mine to say. It wasn't anyone's. A name you keep in a diary for thirty years is a seed, and seeds come up when they come up. All I could do was make sure there was soil."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, startled, real laugh, the first one Lior had ever heard from her, and he understood that he was being given something.

"You let me walk in here every Tuesday for five months," she said, "drinking your tea, browsing your stock, working up to it, and the whole time the sign was—" she gestured helplessly at the window, "—*waiting*."

"Since Tuesday last," Lior admitted. "Denny hung it eight days ago. I told him to keep the scaffolding up so it looked unfinished. He thought I was mad. He's thought I was mad since 1987, so it made no difference to him."

"You waited for me to *claim* it." She said it carefully, testing the weight of it. "You didn't hand it to me. You didn't say, *I know who you really are.* You put it on the building and let me find it."

"Identity isn't something you take from a person," Lior said. "I spent thirty years watching other people try — courts, inquests, families, whole towns. Everyone in Ashmoor had an opinion about who Maren Voss was. The Calloways did. The inquest did. Half the town did, and most of them were wrong, and the ones who were right kept quiet about it, which was worse." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "The only person who ever got to say who you were was you. So the sign went up, and I swept the floor, and I waited. That's the whole trick. It's not a very impressive trick. Mostly it's sweeping."

Maren picked up her tea. Her hands, Lior noticed, were not entirely steady, and she let him notice, which was its own kind of trust.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I used to walk past this shop. It was a butcher's then. Old Mr. Prewitt. And I'd look in the window and think, *one day I'll have a shop, and it will be full of the only thing my father couldn't take a hand in.*" She smiled down into the cup. "I never imagined it would be a bookshop. I never imagined it would be *this* bookshop. Life is either very cruel or very funny, and at my age you learn it's usually both."

"Both," Lior agreed. "That's the house style."

* * *

Tomas came back from the post office at half past eleven carrying a parcel the size of a paving stone and the expression of a young man bearing news.

"Three boxes of stock from the Whitcombe estate," he announced, "and—" he paused, and Lior saw him decide, deliberately, to say the thing, "—and Mrs. Aldous from the bakery wants to know when we're opening. Properly. With a ribbon or whatever. She says the whole high street's talking."

"The whole high street," Lior said, "has spent thirty years not talking, and now it intends to make up for lost volume."

Maren laughed again. It was getting easier for her, Lior thought. Everything was getting easier, in the way that things do after a long illness — not suddenly well, but less effortful, the body remembering its own weight.

"I still don't—" Tomas shifted the parcel to his hip. "I still don't entirely understand how you knew. About the name. Mr. Thornbury said the first entry, but I read that entry, I read the whole diary twice, and I don't see how you'd *bet* on it."

"You don't bet on a thing like that," Maren said. "You wait for it. He told you. It's not a very impressive trick."

"Mostly sweeping," Tomas said, and went to open the parcel, shaking his head with the resigned affection of someone who had learned that his employer's methods would never fully be explained to him, and had made his peace with it.

They worked through the lunch hour. Maren stayed. This was new — usually she left by noon, back to the cottage on Fell Lane, back to the garden she was bringing back from neglect with the same patience she'd brought to everything. But today she took down her coat again after nearly leaving, and asked, in a voice pitched carefully casual, whether she might shelve the poetry section, since she had Opinions, and Lior said that the poetry section had been waiting thirty years for someone with Opinions, and that was that.

By three o'clock the shop had a heartbeat. Boxes opened, paper everywhere, Tomas on a step-ladder calling down titles and Maren calling back verdicts — *bottom shelf, nobody respects poetry at eye level, poetry should make you stoop, it's good for the soul* — and Lior stood at the counter with the ledger open in front of him and did not write a single thing in it, because some afternoons the ledger could look after itself.

At half past three she found it.

Lior heard the small sound she made — not a gasp, nothing so dramatic, just a caught breath, the kind you'd miss if you weren't a man who had spent his life listening for caught breaths — and he did not turn around. He gave her the moment. It was hers.

She had found the shelf. The one he'd built in February, in the corner by the window where the light came through best. The one with the hand-lettered label, in his own careful capitals:

**MISSING PERSONS — ALL FOUND**

It was a small shelf. Eleven books. He had chosen them one at a time over the winter: memoirs of people who had walked out of one life and into another; novels about women who reinvented themselves and were, at the end, allowed to keep it; a slim, strange volume about a lighthouse keeper's daughter who had been given up for lost and walked out of the sea two years later, tanned and furious and refusing to explain. At the center of the shelf, spine out, in a protective sleeve that Tomas had made with more care than he'd ever shown a schoolbook, was a diary. Water-stained at the corners. The handwriting on the first hundred pages small and slanted and alive.

The real one. The one that had started everything. Maren had given it to him in April — *keep it here, where it happened, where it can do some good* — and Lior had shelved it among the found, which was the only place it had ever belonged.

"Mr. Thornbury," Maren said. Her voice was not entirely level.

"Lior," he said, for perhaps the fortieth time.

"Lior." She came to the counter. She was holding the label card, the spare one, the one he'd lettered and not yet tacked up. She turned it over in her hands. "This shelf. Did you build it for me?"

"I built it for the shop," Lior said. "The shop needed a shelf. The town needed a shelf, if I'm honest. Ashmoor has spent thirty years pretending people don't leave and don't come back, and neither half of that is true, and it seemed to me a bookshop ought to be the place in town where the truth gets a window seat." He paused. "But yes. Also for you."

Maren set the card down. She looked out the window at the sign — *ELODIE'S BOOKS* — green letters on cream, the apostrophe crooked, the whole thing catching the late afternoon light.

"You know what I keep thinking," she said. "In the diary. That first entry. *Elodie is what I would be, if I were brave.* I was nineteen, and I thought bravery was a thing you were born with, like height. And then I spent thirty years finding out it's a thing you do, one Tuesday at a time, mostly when you're tired." She looked back at him. "And I keep thinking — the girl who wrote that entry, she'd have walked past this window every day and never once come in. Too frightened. Too sure the shop was for other people." Her voice caught, and steadied. "It took me thirty years and a fire and a funeral and a trial to walk through the door. But I did walk through it. And the name was already on the building. Like the town had been holding it for me."

"The town owes you a great deal more than a name," Lior said. "But it's a start. Towns pay their debts in installments."

Maren laughed, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way women of her generation did, as though crying were a chore to be got through efficiently.

"Odile comes home Friday," she said. "Suspended sentence. She'll want to see the shop." A pause, and then, with the air of a woman deciding to stop circling: "She'll want to see it with me standing in it. Under the sign. She's spent thirty years being the sister who stayed, and I'd like her to see what staying built." She looked at Lior. "Will you be here?"

"Where else would I be?" Lior said. "I live above the shop. It's a very short commute."

Maren smiled. Then she reached across the counter and did something Lior had not expected: she took the ledger out from under his hands, opened it to the first blank page, and wrote, in a small, slanted, unmistakably alive hand:

*Elodie's Books, opened by Lior Thornbury, kept by Tomas Ellery, named by no one but me.*

She turned it around so he could read it.

"For the record," she said. "You of all people should appreciate a proper record. Signed, dated, in the right hand, by the right person." She tapped the page. "No forgeries in this shop. We've had quite enough of those."

Lior looked at the entry for a long moment. Thirty years ago he had signed a report he didn't believe, and it had cost him a career's worth of sleep, and he had spent a decade behind this very counter trying to make the signature mean something else. Now a woman he had helped find — or who had, more accurately, arranged her own finding and simply required a clerk — had written his name into the founding record of the thing he loved best in the world, in ink, in truth.

"It's a good entry," he said, and his voice was not entirely level either, and neither of them remarked on it, which was its own courtesy.

* * *

He locked up at six. Tomas went home whistling, which was new, and good. The lights of the high street came on one by one, and the sign over the door caught the last of the light and held it, green and patient.

Lior was halfway up the stairs to the flat when he heard the bell.

It rang once — the new bell, the one Maren had brought, the one that sounded like itself — and he stood on the landing with his hand on the rail, and he knew, the way he had known things his whole working life, the small cold way, before the knowing had a shape.

Through the frosted glass of the door, a figure. Not Maren. Not Odile. Someone standing very still, the way people stand when they have come a long way to say something difficult, or when they are not entirely sure they will be let in.

Lior went back down and opened the door.

A stranger. A woman in her sixties, maybe, in a good coat gone shabby at the cuffs, with the look of someone who had rehearsed a speech on the drive over and was now, at the threshold, watching it desert her. She looked at Lior. She looked past him, at the shelves, at the new timber, at the label on the corner shelf — *MISSING PERSONS — ALL FOUND* — and her face did something complicated.

"Are you Thornbury?" she said. "The one who found out about Maren Voss?"

"I'm the one who swept the floor," Lior said. "Come in. It's closing time, but I find the interesting cases always arrive at closing time."

The woman stepped inside, and out in the dark behind her, at the edge of the lamplight, a car sat with its engine running — and its driver watching the shop with the stillness of someone who has been watching for a long time.
