## 40. Open for Business

Spring came to Ashmoor the way forgiveness does — slowly, then all at once.

Lior noticed it first in the gutters outside the shop, where crocuses had forced their way up through the rubble dust the fire had left behind, pushing through grit and charred paper ash as if they'd been waiting all winter for permission. Then the light changed. It came earlier each morning and lingered longer each evening, pooling in the big front window of Thornbury's Books like something poured, and for the first time in thirty years Lior did not feel that the light was looking for him.

The rebuilt sign had gone up on a Tuesday. The carpenter — a broad, cheerful woman named Hesta who had once, Lior learned, borrowed forty-three books from him over a decade without ever quite paying her late fees — had asked twice whether he was sure about the lettering.

"Elodie's Books," she'd said, squinting at the sketch. "Not Thornbury's?"

"Not Thornbury's."

"It's your shop."

"It's her name," Lior said, and Hesta, being a sensible woman, carved what she was told.

The letters went up in gold leaf, and when the morning sun hit them they caught fire in the good way, the way names should burn: briefly, brightly, and leaving nothing behind but themselves.

* * *

The reopening was not a ceremony. Lior had put his foot down about ceremonies. He'd spent enough of the past year standing in rooms full of people looking at him — courtrooms, memorial vigils, the town meeting where he'd read Maren's letter aloud while half of Ashmoor held its breath and the other half pretended not to — and he wanted no more of it. What he got instead was worse, or better, depending on how you counted: an ordinary Saturday in April with the door propped open and a hand-lettered sign in the window reading OPEN, AT LAST, COME IN.

They came in all day.

The baker arrived first, before the flour smell had even cleared from his apron, carrying a box of almond twists and the expression of a man who had rehearsed an apology for eleven months and found, upon delivery, that it fit badly. "I told you once I didn't remember Maren Voss," he said, setting the box on the counter with unnecessary precision. "That wasn't true. I remembered her every day. I just agreed not to say so."

"I know," Lior said.

"The twists are an apology. The recipe was her mother's. I never told anyone that either."

"Then it's two apologies," Lior said, and the baker laughed — a startled, cracked-open sound — and bought three novels he would never read, because some transactions are really about other things entirely.

After him came the vicar, who had replaced Father Anselm and who brought, along with his condolences, a small congregation's worth of book requests. Then came people Lior had known for decades and people he had never seen before, and among them were the ones he'd been waiting for without admitting it: the borrowers. They appeared throughout the afternoon, one by one, sheepish as cats bringing home birds, pressing into his hands books gone soft at the corners, spines creased at favorite pages, one volume with a pressed violet marking chapter nine where someone had stopped reading in 1987 and apparently decided life was too short to finish *The Moonstone*.

"You kept it thirty years?" Lior asked the woman who returned it.

"I kept meaning to bring it back," she said. "And then there was always a reason. And then—" She gestured vaguely at the whole of Ashmoor, its long silence, its slow thaw. "Well. It seemed like time to stop keeping things."

By closing, the counter was stacked with returns like driftwood after a tide, and Lior stood among them feeling something he couldn't immediately name. It took him until he was locking up to find the word. Not joy. Not relief. *Settled.* The way a bone feels when it finally knits.

* * *

Tomas stayed on.

He had never formally been hired; he had simply begun appearing, the way he always had, pale and earnest and slightly damp-looking even in fine weather, until one Tuesday in May Lior came downstairs to find the shelves alphabetized, the window display rebuilt around a theme ("Books That Lie To You Beautifully"), and a handwritten rota taped inside the till suggesting which days Tomas intended to work.

"You know I can't pay you much," Lior said.

"I don't want much." Tomas straightened a stack of hardcovers with the delicacy of a man defusing something. "I want to learn the trade. My gran used to say a bookshop is just a town that decided to be quiet for a while." He paused. "She said a lot of things. I'm only now hearing most of them."

Ruth Ellery's letter had done its work in Tomas slowly, the way water works on stone. He still visited her grave on Sundays. He still flinched at the name Corin Blake, though Blake's trial had come and gone and the man was now a number in a prison up north, appealing on procedural grounds with the same tidy patience with which he had once administered murder. But the flinching was shorter these days. And some mornings, shelving in the back room, Tomas hummed — tunelessly, cheerfully, a habit Ruth must have had — and Lior let the humming stand in place of everything neither of them knew how to say.

There was one shelf Tomas did not touch. It stood near the back window, waist-high, painted a deep green that Lior had mixed himself from three tins none of which matched. Above it, in gold paint applied by Lior's own unsteady hand, were the words:

**MISSING PERSONS**

It was not a large shelf. It held perhaps sixty volumes, and every one of them was a lie in the best sense: stories of people who vanished and came home. *The Return of the Soldier.* *Jane Eyre*, because Bertha didn't count but Jane came back anyway. A dog-eared copy of *Cold Comfort Farm*. A whole row of mysteries whose solutions Lior had personally vetted, striking from the stock any book in which the missing person turned out, at the last page, to be dead. Those went in the cellar. The shelf above the green paint held only homecomings.

Customers asked about it constantly. Children asked first, usually — children noticed things adults had trained out of themselves — and Lior gave them all the same answer.

"It's for people who were lost and found their way back. If you ever feel lost, start here. Someone in these books has already made the trip."

"And if they're not lost," a girl of about eight had asked last week, "but they're hiding?"

Lior had looked at her for a long moment. "Then they'll find this shelf," he said, "when they're ready to stop."

* * *

Maren came in most Thursdays.

She had taken a cottage on the far side of the moor — close enough to walk in, far enough that the town could get used to her gradually, the way you introduce a cat to a house. Elodie Voss-Halloway, her papers said now, both names restored by a court that had spent thirty years refusing to pronounce either one. She wore her hair short and silvering and she had Wren's laugh, though only Odile was left alive who could have confirmed it, and Odile confirmed it every chance she got.

Odile herself had moved to a small flat near the harbor and taken up, of all things, gardening. The suspended sentence sat on her conscience the way weather sits on a coastline — present, permanent, occasionally stormy. She had resigned from the bench in a letter of four sentences, the shortest document she'd produced in thirty years, and Lior suspected it had cost her more than the trial. But she came to the shop on Thursdays too, trailing her sister, and the two of them would argue gently about poetry in the corner armchairs while Lior pretended to do inventory, and the sound of it — two voices, sisters, decades late and still going — was worth more than any verdict.

On the first Thursday of June, Maren brought him a parcel wrapped in brown paper and string.

"What's this?"

"The last thing I owed you." Inside was a notebook — new, blank, its cover the deep green of a certain shelf. "You keep lending out books about people who came home," she said. "I thought the shelf ought to have one written by an actual customer."

He understood then, and had to look out the window for a moment at the crocuses, the gutter, the ordinary street, until his eyes behaved.

"It's not finished," Maren warned. "Homecomings aren't. You just stop writing the bad parts down and start writing the rest."

"That's the whole trick of it," Lior said. "Everything else is commentary."

* * *

Summer settled in. The shop found its rhythm: Tomas opening, Lior closing, Thursdays belonging to the sisters, and the green shelf quietly doing its work in the background, its stock thinning and thickening like a tide. Lior dusted it every morning first, before any other shelf, and if that seemed sentimental he saw no reason to apologize to himself about it.

He was dusting it, in fact, on the last Tuesday of July, rag in hand, when the bell over the door rang.

He knew before he turned around. Thirty years will teach you the sound of a certain kind of entrance — the pause in the doorway, the way the air changes when someone crosses a threshold carrying more than a bag. He set down the rag, folded it once, and laid it on the shelf's edge.

The stranger was a woman in her sixties, travel-worn, with the particular posture of someone who has driven a long way rehearsing what to say and is now discovering the rehearsal was useless. Her eyes went around the shop — the new shelves, the old smell of paper and glue, the gold-leafed name above the door that she had surely read on her way in — and then found Lior and stayed there.

"You're Thornbury," she said. "The detective."

"Retired."

"So was I." She crossed the floor slowly, reading the room the way he read it, cataloguing exits and witnesses out of pure incurable habit. When she reached the green shelf she stopped. Read the lettering. Her jaw worked once, soundlessly.

"My daughter disappeared," she said. "Fourteen years ago. Up north, a coastal town — you won't have heard of it. The police closed the file in seven months. Everyone agreed she'd run off. Everyone was very kind about it, in the way people are kind when they've already decided." She touched the shelf's edge, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave marker or a doorbell. "Last month somebody sent me a newspaper clipping. About this shop. About a case here. About a woman who came home." She looked up, and her eyes were dry in the terrible way that eyes go dry after fourteen years. "I don't know why I drove five hours. I don't actually believe you can help me. I think maybe I just needed to stand somewhere it happened once — the impossible version, the one where they come back."

Lior looked at her. At the shelf between them, holding its sixty homecomings. At the rag lying folded where he'd left it, and beyond it the door, propped open to the summer street, the light coming through it warm and unhurried and asking nothing of anyone.

He thought about a signed report he had never believed, and a career spent avoiding another one, and a shop that had burned and been rebuilt with a dead girl's living name over the door, and a diary that had started all of it by knocking quietly, one rainy evening, on a counter he'd boarded shut behind.

"Sit down," Lior said, pulling out the chair across from his desk — the good chair, the one he saved. "Tell me her name. Tell me everything, in whatever order it comes. We have time."

The stranger sat. Outside, the bell swayed once above the empty doorway, and the spring that had become summer went on becoming whatever it was going to be next, and Lior Thornbury — bookseller, retired detective, keeper of a shelf of homecomings — put down his rag, and listened.
