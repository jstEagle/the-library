## 7. The Woman at the Lighthouse

The cannery had been dead for thirty years, but someone was keeping it alive.

Ines stood across the road from it at seven in the morning, coffee going cold in her hand, watching the fog lift off Marrow Bay in slow gray sheets. The building sat on pilings at the water's edge, its corrugated skin rusted the color of dried blood, windows boarded, a NO TRESPASSING sign so weathered it read as a suggestion. Everything about it said abandoned. Everything except the fresh tire tracks in the mud of the gravel lot — two sets, narrow, recent — and the padlock on the side door that was brass and bright and newer than anything else on the structure.

She'd traced the call here the way she traced everything: unglamorously. The number from Sefa's matchbook was a burner, but burners still ping towers, and Dev had a contact at a telecom analytics firm who owed him for a story they'd never run. The ping put the phone within two hundred meters of this stretch of coastline. The call had lasted eleven seconds. *Stop looking, or she dies sooner.* Eleven seconds was long enough to say something else, if you were careful. Ines had listened to the recording forty times, and she was almost certain there was a second voice under the first — not words, just breath, the sound of someone standing close by while another person spoke.

Someone had been holding the phone. Someone else had been talking into it.

The padlock said people came here. The tire tracks said they'd come recently. Ines finished her coffee, crossed the road, and did what she always did when a door was locked: she looked for the door nobody thought to lock.

It was around back, half-hidden behind a collapsed stack of fish crates — a maintenance entrance, the kind of thing a town forgets because the town that remembered it is gone. The hasp was rusted through. She slipped the latch with a flathead screwdriver from her glovebox and stepped into the dark.

Inside, the cannery smelled of salt and old machine oil and, faintly, improbably, of woodsmoke. Her flashlight found conveyor lines frozen mid-shudder, rusted baskets, a wall of lockers with names stenciled in flaking paint — HALLOWAY SHIFT, 1974 — and then, near the far end, where the floor sloped toward the water, something that stopped her cold.

A broom, leaning against a wall. Clean bristles.

And footprints in the dust. Recent. Small. A woman's size, or a teenager's, leading from the maintenance door toward a section of wall that shouldn't have been a door at all.

Ines followed them to a panel of corrugated steel that swung open on oiled hinges when she pressed it. Behind it: stairs. Concrete, swept, descending toward a glow of lamplight below.

She should have called Dev. She knew that even as she started down them.

* * *

The tunnel ran maybe sixty meters under the bluff and came up through a hatch into daylight so sudden it made her eyes water. When she could see again, she was standing in a stand of wind-bent cypress at the top of a headland, and above her, white against the gray sky, stood the old Marrow Point lighthouse.

Not the automated beacon — that was farther out on the point, solar-powered, municipal. This was the original keeper's house, decommissioned since the sixties, listed in the county records as structurally unsound. It looked sound enough from here. It looked lived-in. Smoke curled from a chimney. Laundry hung on a line strung between two cypresses, snapping in the wind — work shirts, a child's jacket, sheets gone soft with age.

Ines stood very still and did the math she'd been avoiding since the matchbook. Off-grid community. Cannery access. A phone answered by a woman who knew Elodie Voss's name before Ines said it.

*They're hiding her,* she thought. *Or they're hiding from me.*

Both, probably. With these people, both would feel like the same act of love.

She was still deciding whether to knock when the door opened and a woman looked out at her — sixties, silver hair cut short and practical, forearms like someone who'd hauled crab pots most of her life, an apron dusted with flour. She didn't look surprised. That was the first lie, Ines thought later. Nobody opens their door to a stranger on a fogbound headland without surprise unless they've been expecting her.

"You're the podcaster," the woman said.

"I'm—"

"Ines Oyelaran. You solved the Kessler case." The woman's eyes were pale green and entirely unfooled. "Sefa called ahead. Come in before the wind does. Soup's on."

* * *

Her name was Birdie. She said it like it was the only name she'd ever needed. She sat Ines at a long pine table scarred by decades of knives and elbows, set down a bowl of fish chowder thick with potatoes, and a slab of bread still warm enough to steam, and then she lied to her beautifully for the better part of an hour.

The lies were gorgeous. That was what unsettled Ines most. Birdie didn't stammer, didn't over-explain, didn't do any of the hundred things guilty people do. She told a story about a homesteading community — nine adults, four kids, off the grid by choice, selling smoked fish and preserves at the Saturday market in town — and every detail interlocked with every other detail like joinery. She named neighbors. She named a school schedule. She described the diesel generator's maintenance cycle with the weary specificity of a woman who actually bled a fuel line last Tuesday.

"Elodie Voss," Ines said, between spoonfuls. "Does the name mean anything?"

Birdie buttered her bread. "Should it?"

"She drowned in 1998. Or she didn't. I'm trying to find out which."

"That's a strange line of work." Birdie's tone was mild, almost kind. "Proving people aren't dead."

"It pays better than you'd think."

"Mm." Birdie ate a spoonful of soup, unhurried. "We don't take much interest in town matters here. Town never took much interest in us. That suits everybody fine."

"Your smokehouse sells at the Saturday market. That's town."

"The market isn't town. The market is money changing hands." Birdie smiled, and it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "You're welcome to buy a jar of preserves, Ms. Oyelaran. You're welcome to walk the headland. But I'd take it kindly if you didn't come back through my cannery again. The latch you broke is coming out of somebody's allowance."

"Whose?"

"Mine, likely. I own everything here, including the mistakes." She stood, collected Ines's empty bowl, and refilled it without asking. "Eat. The road back gets foggy after four."

It was masterful. Ines had interviewed killers with less composure. And underneath the performance — she was nearly sure it was a performance — was something real: the fierce, territorial warmth of a woman guarding a table where everyone got fed. Birdie wasn't lying because she was cruel. She was lying the way a hen lies, spreading herself flat over something hidden.

Ines just couldn't see what was under her yet.

* * *

It happened on the way out.

Ines had gotten as far as the yard, the laundry line snapping overhead, when she noticed the girl.

Fifteen, maybe sixteen, sitting on the porch steps with a battered paperback she wasn't reading. Dark hair, sun-browned, wearing a man's flannel shirt rolled at the sleeves. She'd been watching Ines since the door opened — Ines felt it before she confirmed it, that particular weight of being studied.

But it wasn't idle teenage curiosity. The girl stared at her the way you stare at a photograph of someone you've only heard described. Like she was checking Ines against something. A voice, maybe. Ines had done three seasons of episodes; her voice was out there, attached to cases, attached to a reputation. If Birdie's community listened to podcasts the way everyone did—

"Your book's upside down," Ines said gently.

The girl turned it right side up without looking at it. "I know," she said. "It's a test."

"A test of what?"

"If you're observant." Something flickered in her face — not quite a smile. "You passed."

"What's your name?"

"Nell." Then, quickly, like closing a gate: "You should go. Birdie doesn't like people lingering."

"Birdie doesn't seem to like much of anything."

"She likes everything." Nell's voice dropped, and for a second the teenager fell away and something older and sadder looked out of her eyes. "That's the problem. She likes all of us too much to let anything happen to us. So nothing ever happens to us. Nothing new. Nothing true." She glanced past Ines, toward the window where Birdie's silhouette moved behind the glass, and lowered her voice further. "Why are you really here?"

"You know why."

"I know what Birdie told you. I want to know what you know."

Ines held her gaze. "I know a woman named Elodie Voss is supposed to be dead, and everyone who could tell me otherwise keeps lying to me with their whole hearts. I know your community sits on top of a cannery that connects to a phone that threatened me. And I know you looked at me like you recognized me, which means you've heard my show, which means somebody here has a radio, or a phone, or a secret."

Nell went very still. Down the path, a man — broad, dark-bearded, moving with a deliberate casualness that fooled no one — had appeared near the woodshed and was watching them. Tomas, Birdie's son, Ines would learn later. Right now he was just a shape putting itself between her and the house.

"You should go," Nell said again, but softer now, and she pressed something into Ines's hand as they shook — quick, practiced, invisible from the porch. A folded scrap of paper. "For the fog," she said loudly. "Directions. The road's confusing."

Ines walked to her car with the paper burning in her palm. She didn't unfold it until she was a mile down the coast road, pulled over, engine idling against the wind.

It wasn't directions.

It was a single line, in a teenager's careful print:

*SHE ASKS ABOUT YOU TOO.*

* * *

Ines drove back toward town with the note on the passenger seat and her mind running hot. She played the day back like tape. Birdie's flawless lies. The swept tunnel. The laundry — four kids' worth, though Birdie had counted only four children total, and one of those jackets on the line had been sized for an adult woman, medium, worn soft. The chowder had been made for more people than sat at the table. Nine adults, Birdie said. Ines had seen place settings for eleven.

And Nell. Nell, who tested strangers, who spoke in gates and locks, who had slipped her a note with hands that had clearly done it before. *She asks about you too.* Not Birdie — Birdie asked about nothing, Birdie gave away nothing. Which meant there was someone else in that house, someone who hadn't been brought out to meet the visiting podcaster, someone who wanted to know about the woman on the radio who proved accidents were murders.

Someone who had spent twenty-five years listening to the world through walls.

Ines pulled over at the overlook above Marrow Bay and let the engine idle. Below, the town arranged itself along the shore like a stage set: the diner, the church, the Halloway Building with its brass letters, the marina where a drowned girl's name appeared on no memorial because there was no body and therefore, officially, no grave.

Everyone who could identify Elodie Voss insisted she died in 1998. Everyone had rehearsed the same grief. But grief, Ines had learned across three investigations, is the one thing liars can't fake convincingly for twenty-five years — and these people weren't grieving. They were *keeping*. Keeping a story alive the way Birdie kept the generator running: routinely, carefully, generation to generation.

Which meant the story mattered to more than one person. Which meant somewhere in that lighthouse, behind Birdie's beautiful lies and Nell's careful gates, was a woman who had been dead for twenty-five years and was asking questions about a stranger on the radio.

Ines took out her phone and photographed the note, then the lighthouse road behind her, then the cannery lot with its two sets of tire tracks. Evidence habits died hard.

Then she dialed Corin Ash. He picked up on the second ring, as if he'd been holding the phone.

"I found the community," she said. "Off-grid, up past the point. They're protecting someone."

A pause. Long enough to be deliberate. "Did they feed you?"

"...Soup. Why?"

"Good," Corin Ash said, and there was something in his voice she'd never heard before — not calm, not control. Relief. "Eat there again tomorrow. And Ines — whatever you do next, do it slowly. Some things die faster when you look straight at them."

The line went dead. Ines sat in the fog with the note in her lap and understood, finally, the shape of the trap she was inside: she wasn't hunting a killer anymore.

She was being introduced to a witness — by everyone around her, one careful lie at a time.

And tomorrow, she was going back for seconds.
