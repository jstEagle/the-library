## 11. The Producer's Dilemma

Ines heard the rental car before she saw it — tires grinding the gravel outside the lighthouse keeper's cottage where Birdie had grudgingly let her sleep, and a door slamming with the particular frustration of a man who had driven eleven hours to be angry.

Dev.

She was down the stairs before he knocked. He stood on the porch in his city jacket, fog beading on his shoulders, holding a leather portfolio like a shield.

"You didn't answer your phone," he said.

"You didn't call."

"I called four times." He pushed past her into the cottage, took in the candles, the wood stove, the maps of Marrow Bay taped to the wall with red string, and exhaled through his nose. "You're living in a lighthouse. You've become a metaphor. I hate it."

"Say what you came to say."

He set the portfolio on the kitchen table and didn't open it. That was how she knew it was bad. Dev opened everything immediately; he narrated his own life like a cold open.

"A lawyer came to the studio Tuesday," he said. "Name of Prentiss. Suits that cost more than my car. He asked for you by name, by episode number, by the exact date you arrived in Marrow Bay. Then he slid me a card and said the Halloway family would like to discuss an exclusive."

Ines leaned against the counter. "An exclusive on what? There's no story yet. Not one they'd admit exists."

"That's what I asked." Dev finally opened the portfolio. Inside was a single sheet, heavy stock, embossed letterhead. "Prentiss said — and I'm quoting because I wrote it on my hand in the parking garage — 'Ms. Oyelaran has developed a reputation for finding murders where none were intended. The Halloway family admires her work and would like to commission a definitive account of the town's history. Full access, full cooperation, full editorial control retained by Ms. Oyelaran.' Full editorial control, Ines. In writing."

"How much?"

"Two hundred thousand against. A podcast series. Six episodes minimum. Distribution guaranteed. They'll buy ad slots across the whole network." He turned the sheet toward her. "And there's a rider."

She read it standing up so her hands wouldn't shake. *The commissioned work shall not address matters predating 2010 without prior written consent of the family.* Predating 2010. Elodie drowned in 1998. Everything — the witness, the cover-up, the man in the water — sat neatly inside the exclusion like a body at the bottom of a bay.

"They're not buying a story," Ines said. "They're buying my silence and putting my name on it."

"I know what it is." Dev's voice cracked on the second word, and he hated himself for it; she could see him hating himself. "I'm not stupid, Ines. I did the math before I got in the car. Two hundred thousand keeps the show alive for three years. Three years is two more seasons, twelve more cases, maybe twelve more people who don't stay dead on a technicality. One town's ghost versus—"

"Don't." She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting, and he stopped. "Don't do the arithmetic out loud. I've heard it. I hear it every night. It always ends the same place: someone offered me money to look away, and I'm deciding whether the looking away is worth it."

"It's not just money." He pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, the way men sit when their knees have started keeping score. "Prentiss knew things, Ines. Things I only told you. He mentioned the raw files from the Delgado case. The ones we never aired. Nobody outside this room knew those existed."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, gulls argued over something dead.

"So either they have someone inside the network," Ines said, "or they have someone inside us."

* * *

Dev had left Los Angeles at four in the morning, and the whole drive north he'd rehearsed the argument he was going to win.

That was the thing about Ines nobody understood who hadn't worked with her: she wasn't reckless. Reckless people were easy. Reckless people burned bright and burned out and you mourned them and moved on. Ines was something harder to produce around — she was *certain*. She walked into rooms carrying her certainty like a lantern, and it lit everything, including the things you'd been comfortable not seeing, and once she'd shown you them you couldn't unsee them, and then you were hers.

He'd built a career on that light. Eight years. He'd found her doing a true-crime segment on a college radio station, a fifteen-minute piece about a hit-and-run in Fresno that she'd solved from traffic camera timestamps, and he'd heard in her voice the thing audiences starved for: not glee, not voyeurism, but *obligation*. She talked about victims like they were owed something. Listeners could feel the debt being paid in real time.

Three cases. Three accidents that weren't. Eleven million downloads. And now this — a story that hadn't happened yet, a woman declared dead twenty-five years ago, a client who paid double and vanished, a town that closed around her like a fist. Dev had read her notes. All of them. He'd read them twice, and both times he'd arrived at the same conclusion he was now sitting in a candlelit cottage trying not to arrive at:

She was right. And being right was going to get her killed, or ruined, or both, and he would be the one holding the microphone when it happened.

So he'd taken the meeting with Prentiss. He told himself it was reconnaissance. He told himself a lot of things on the drive north, each one less convincing than the last, until somewhere past Santa Rosa he admitted the truth: he'd taken the meeting because part of him wanted to say yes, and he needed to find out how big that part was.

It was big. That was the horror of it. Two hundred thousand dollars was not abstract. Two hundred thousand was health insurance for his mother's dialysis. Was the studio lease. Was the fact that their last three pitches had been rejected and the network had used the word "fatigue" about true crime, about *their* show, the word fatigue applied to the genre Dev had helped invent. The offer wasn't a bribe. It was a lifeline shaped exactly like a noose, and the terrible genius of it was that accepting it would feel, for months, like survival.

"Ines," he said now, and made himself say the rest. "If you walk away from this — if you publish nothing, if the story dies quietly — I can get us the deal anyway. Minus the rider. I can negotiate. They want you neutralized; neutrality doesn't require the rider if you never go near 1998 again."

"You'd trust them to honor that?"

"No." He laughed, short and ugly. "I'd trust that you couldn't help yourself. That's been my entire business model for eight years."

She almost smiled. Almost. "And if I do help myself?"

"Then I need to know what I'm producing. Because right now I'm producing a woman's death that hasn't happened yet, based on evidence that burns in record offices, sourced to a community that lies beautifully, funded by a client nobody can find." He stood, paced to the window, looked out at the fog swallowing the headland. "I can't air speculation, Ines. I can't even *hold* speculation. If this goes wrong, it's not you alone in the wreckage. It's me. It's the network's lawyers. It's everyone who ever trusted the name Oyelaran."

"The name Oyelaran is why Prentiss came to *you*," she said. "Think about that. They didn't approach me. They approached my producer. They went for the person who signs the checks and holds the files. They studied us, Dev. They know the show isn't just me. They know the show is the machine, and machines have parts, and parts can be bought."

He turned around. "Is that what you think I'm doing?"

"I think you drove eleven hours to say yes and you haven't managed to say it yet. That's not nothing. That's the last honest part of you fighting the first dishonest one."

The words landed like a slap, and he let them, because they were true, and because the alternative was defending himself, and he had nothing to defend with except the portfolio on the table, which was the enemy's document, which he had carried here like a fool, like a courier, like exactly the part he was afraid he'd already become.

"I should burn that," he said.

"You should leave it. Evidence has a way of mattering later." She picked up the letterhead, studied the embossing, and something shifted in her face — the hunter's shift, the one he'd learned to fear and love. "Halloway family counsel. Prentiss. Get me everything on him. Bar admissions, client lists, especially anything connecting him to the network or to Corin Ash."

"Corin Ash." Dev said the name carefully. "Your invisible client. You think the lawyer and the client are connected?"

"I think everyone in this story is connected, and the connections are the case." She folded the letter into quarters. "You want to know what you're producing? Here it is. A living witness to a murder committed by the family now offering us two hundred thousand dollars to never mention the year it happened. That's the pitch. That's the whole pitch. And I'm going to air it."

"Not without corroboration you're not. Not without her. Does she even exist? Have you seen her?"

Ines was quiet a moment too long.

"That's what I thought," Dev said. "You've got a feeling and a matchbook and a town full of liars, and you want me to bet the show on it."

"I want you to bet it on the truth. The truth is that she's alive, and there are people in this town who intend to correct that error, and the correction is scheduled." She stepped close, and her voice dropped to the register he'd heard exactly three times in eight years — the one that preceded every episode that changed their lives. "You can take the deal, Dev. I mean it. I will not hate you. Take the money, take your mom off the dialysis waitlist anxiety, take the lease. But if you take it, take it clean. Don't bring that paper back into my orbit, and don't stand between me and the year 1998."

* * *

He didn't sleep that night. Birdie's community had one spare room and it smelled of cedar and old rope, and Dev lay on the narrow bed listening to the foghorn and doing the thing he did worst: nothing.

At some point he got up, found his laptop, and pulled the file he'd sworn he wouldn't open until he'd decided. Prentiss's card. The firm's website. And beneath the polished bios, the thing he'd noticed in the parking garage and told himself meant nothing: a junior associate listed on the firm's roster, photographed at a charity gala two years ago, standing beside a donor whose name Dev had run through Ines's research database on a whim weeks ago.

Marcus Halloway.

Not the family. Not the foundation. Marcus, personally, as the gala's underwriter, smiling beside the young lawyer whose firm now offered Ines a fortune to skip a decade.

He sat very still in the dark, the screen the only light, and understood that the decision had already been made for him — not by Ines's certainty, not by his conscience, but by the simple fact that the other side had made a mistake. They'd put Marcus's name too close to Prentiss's, and Dev had seen it, and once Ines saw it she would never stop, and the only question left was whether he handed her the match or watched her strike it blind.

His phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number:

*Ask your partner what she paid for the room. Some hospitality isn't free.*

He looked at the message for a long time. Then he looked at the door, and the thin line of candlelight beneath it, and the sound of Ines moving around downstairs, awake too, and he thought: *they're watching the house. They're watching all of us. Which means we're close enough to hurt them, and they know it.*

He typed back: *Who is this?*

The reply came instantly, as if the sender had been waiting:

*A friend of the schedule.*

Dev deleted the message, closed the laptop, and went downstairs to find Ines standing at the map wall, pinning a photograph of Marcus Halloway next to a photograph of the burned records office, red string already in her hand.

"I need to tell you something," he said. "And then I need to decide if I'm in."
