## 13. Tomas Confesses

I found him at the boatyard, which was either stupidity or an invitation.

Tomas Reyes — Birdie's son, forty-one years old, hands like rope and a face that gave away nothing except when it gave away everything — was hosing down the deck of a crab boat that hadn't gone out in weeks. The fog had burned off by nine, and Marrow Bay's harbor lay exposed in flat gray light, every rusted hull and tarped mast suddenly legible, like the town had been caught undressed.

He saw me coming from thirty yards away. He didn't stop hosing.

"You're trespassing," he said without turning around.

"Private dock," I said. "You want to call someone? Sheriff's department has two deputies left, and I've met both of them."

The hose shut off. He set it down on the deck with exaggerated care, the way you put down something you'd rather throw. Then he turned and looked at me, and for the first time since I'd arrived in this town, Tomas Reyes looked tired. Not guilty. Tired, the way a man looks who has been carrying something heavy up a long hill and can finally see the top.

"Nell came back," he said.

"She did." I climbed onto the dock proper, boots ringing on wet planks. "Held for two days by men working for Marcus Halloway. Seventeen years old. She walked back into your mother's kitchen yesterday morning and you didn't even ask her where she'd been."

"I asked." His jaw worked. "She didn't tell me anything either. That family keeps its secrets."

"That family," I said. "Interesting word choice. *Keeps.* Present tense."

He wiped his hands on his thighs, slow, buying time. Around us the harbor ticked and groaned — hulls against pilings, a gull arguing with something invisible. A town of four hundred people pretending to be asleep.

"You should talk to your mother," he said.

"I did. Birdie lies beautifully. It's her best quality. But she lied *around* you, Tomas. Not about you. There's a difference. She kept steering me away from the clinic. Away from the money. And when I asked how a community of eleven people off-grid affords a satellite phone, a diesel generator, insulin, and a schoolteacher's salary, she told me they fish." I let that sit. "Nobody fishes anymore, Tomas. The boats are rotting. I counted."

The tide slapped the pilings. Somewhere behind us, a truck door slammed and neither of us flinched, though we both heard it.

"How much?" I asked.

He said nothing.

"Corin Ash pays six people in this town to keep watch on Elodie Voss. I know that now. I've seen the ledger structure — not the ledger itself, but the shape of it. Monthly disbursements, escalating after any contact with outsiders. Which means after I arrived, somebody's payments went up." I took a step closer. "So I'll ask again. How much is your mother's clinic worth to you?"

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own defense spoken aloud by a stranger, and hating how reasonable it sounds.

"You don't understand what it was like before," he said.

"Then tell me."

* * *

We ended up in the wheelhouse of the dead crab boat, because he wouldn't talk on open ground and I wouldn't leave. Inside it smelled of diesel and mildew and old coffee. He sat on the bench beneath the fogged windows like a man in a confessional that had run aground.

"My daughter got sick when she was six," he began. No preamble. No name for the daughter, and I understood immediately there would be none — that the girl was one of the things he could not say out loud without breaking. "Leukemia. The kind they treat now, the kind with the eighty percent survival rate. Except we were forty minutes from a hospital that had closed its pediatric ward in 2004, and we had no insurance, because you can't get insurance when your address doesn't exist."

"The lighthouse community isn't on any registry," I said. "I checked."

"We're ghosts. That was the point. Elodie Voss drowned in 1998, and eleven people drowned with her, on paper, so that no one would ever come looking. No tax records. No school enrollment. No utility bills. You live off the grid, you stay alive." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all week. "It works great until somebody gets sick. Or somebody's kid needs a dentist. Or somebody breaks a hip on the cliff path and you have to decide whether to carry them forty minutes to a hospital that will ask questions or hope they die quietly."

"What happened to your daughter?"

"Treatment cost two hundred thousand dollars." He said it flatly, a number worn smooth from handling. "A friend of my mother's — a woman I'd known my whole life as Aunt Ruthie, who turned out to be a woman named Corin Ash's payroll clerk — made a phone call. Three days later there was an envelope. Cash. And a note that said: *This continues as long as the reports continue.*"

"Reports on what?"

"On whether anyone new came to the light. On whether Elodie—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "On whether *Ruth* went anywhere, talked to anyone, seemed restless. On whether strangers were asking questions in town." His eyes came up to mine, and they were dry, which somehow made it worse. "On whether a podcaster named Ines Oyelaran had found the cannery."

The wheelhouse felt smaller. I sat down across from him, deliberately, making myself heavy, making myself hard to dislodge.

"So it was you," I said. "The leak. Nell goes missing, Marcus Halloway's men hit the road before we moved, and Corin knew our timeline because you fed it to him."

"Yes."

"No hesitation. No lawyer. Just yes."

"What do you want me to do, lie? You already know. You've known since the night at the cannery, when those men were waiting for us and nobody in this town had warned us — because the only person who knew the full plan besides Birdie and me was me." He spread his hands. "Yes. It was me. For nine years. Every month, an envelope, and every month, a report. And every month, the clinic stays open. The teacher gets paid. The kids get their vaccinations. Eleven people stay alive because one man pays for the privilege of watching another woman be afraid."

"Do you hear yourself?"

"Every day." His voice cracked on it, just once, then steadied. "Every single day, Ms. Oyelaran. You think I don't know what I am? I'm the reason Nell was taken. I'm the reason Birdie took a bullet in the shoulder on that road. My own mother bled because of reports I wrote in a notebook and mailed from Crescent City like a good little informant." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment he looked exactly like Birdie — same stubborn architecture in the face, same refusal to look away. "And if you put me in front of a judge tomorrow, I'd write one more report if it kept the lights on at that clinic. That's what you came here to hear, isn't it? That's the story. The traitor confesses and feels bad. Except I don't feel bad. I feel *used*, which is different, and worse."

"Explain the difference to me."

"Feeling bad is about you. Feeling used is about the person who built the trap." He stood, restless, and paced the three steps the wheelhouse allowed. "You think Corin Ash is some monster running a protection racket. I thought so too, at first. Nine years ago. Then I started paying attention. Do you know what he does with my reports? Nothing. He files them. He reads them, and he files them, and once — once — he sent back a note telling me to stop reporting on Ruth's trips to the cemetery because it was 'unnecessary distress.' Unnecessary *distress*. This man who owns us writes like a hospice nurse."

"Because he loves her," I said. "That's the part you still don't get, Tomas. His tenderness is real. It's also completely compatible with selling her. Those aren't contradictions to a man like Corin Ash. They're a portfolio."

"He's the only reason she's alive."

"He's the reason she's been *kept* alive. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference." I stood too, because sitting across from him felt like agreeing with him. "Elodie Voss spent twenty-five years in a room your money helped build. Yes. Your daughter lived because of it. Other people's children will live because of it. I'm not going to pretend that doesn't matter — God knows I've spent enough time around real monsters to know the difference between a man who pays for insulin and a man who drowns whistleblowers. But you didn't take Corin Ash's money to save your daughter. You took it to save your daughter and never have to choose. And the choice didn't go away. It just got billed to other people. To Nell. To Birdie. To a woman who hasn't used her own name since 1998."

He flinched at the name. Actually flinched, like I'd thrown something.

"You think I don't know that?" he said. "You think I don't see her at dinner every night, this quiet woman who teaches the little ones their letters, who fixed my roof after the storm, who — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Who thanked me. Last month. Out of nowhere. Said, *Whatever happens, Tomas, I know you tried.* Like she knew. Like she'd always known, and had decided a long time ago that hating me cost more than it bought."

"Maybe she did know. People in cages learn their keepers' schedules."

"She's not in a cage."

"Then why does everyone who could identify her insist she died twenty-five years ago?" I let that land. "Why does her freedom require eleven people living as ghosts, a boy held hostage, a matriarch shot on a coastal road? Tomas — if it walks like a cage and costs like a cage—"

"It kept her breathing!" He shouted it, and the gulls outside scattered off the pilings, and for a second I saw the man underneath the confession: not a traitor, not a saint, just a father who had once sat in a hospital corridor doing arithmetic that ended with his child's life on one side and a stranger's freedom on the other, and had chosen, and had been choosing again every month for nine years, and would keep choosing until someone stopped him. "You solve murders from a studio in Oakland. You get to be righteous at a distance. I did the math with my hands."

The silence afterward was long. The harbor filled it — water, wind, the far-off grind of the cannery's dead machinery shifting in the tide.

"I'm not here to absolve you," I said finally. "Or condemn you. I need something else."

His shoulders dropped half an inch. Suspicion, but listening.

"Corin's payments. The escalation clause. After I arrived, your rate went up — I'd bet the ledger on it. Which means Corin expected contact. Expected *me*. Which means this was never about whether I'd find Elodie. It was about when, and under whose control." I stepped closer. "But Marcus Halloway doesn't pay you. Marcus Halloway has his own people, and they grabbed Nell off a public road, which means Marcus has sources Corin doesn't control. Somebody in this town is reporting twice, Tomas. Maybe without knowing it. Maybe Birdie's letters home. Maybe the supply runs. Maybe the doctor who fills the clinic's prescriptions in Crescent City and talks too much over coffee."

Slowly, Tomas shook his head. "There's nobody else."

"There's always somebody else. That's the lesson of every case I've ever worked. The cover-up is never one man; it's a weather system." I pulled out my phone, hesitated, then turned the screen toward him. A photo Dev had pulled from a county archive before the records office conveniently burned: a young deputy, 1998, standing at the edge of a crowd at the Voss memorial service. Young, square-jawed, uncomfortable. "Recognize him?"

Tomas went very still.

"That's Deputy Kellerman," he said. "He comes to the clinic. Quarterly. Brings the prescription refills from the county program. Been doing it for years. He's — " and I watched the arithmetic start behind his eyes, the same terrible math, the realization arriving in real time that the quarterly visit he'd never questioned, the friendly deputy who asked after the kids and lingered over coffee, might have been counting heads for a different employer entirely. "He asked about Ruth last month. Whether she'd been sleeping well. Whether she seemed settled."

"And what did you tell him?"

"Nothing. I barely spoke to him." But his voice had gone thin, and we both knew the next sentence before he said it. "Birdie talked to him. Birdie always handles the handoff. She likes him. She's known him since he was a rookie walking the memorial every year."

I closed my eyes for one second. One second of pure, cold clarity: the ledger, the payments, the leak inside the sanctuary — and maybe it had never been one leak. Maybe Corin Ash ran his channel, and Marcus Halloway ran his, and the community at the lighthouse had been a junction box all along, wired from both directions, carrying current to two masters who each believed they owned the line.

"Tomas," I said. "When is Kellerman's next quarterly visit?"

He looked at me, and the tiredness in his face curdled into something like fear.

"Tomorrow," he said. "He's due tomorrow morning. And Birdie's expecting him."
