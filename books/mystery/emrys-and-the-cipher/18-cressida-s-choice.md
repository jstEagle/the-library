## 18. Cressida's Choice

The rain had stopped by the time Cressida Vane made up her mind, and she was grateful for it, because she did not want the weather to have any part in what she was about to do. She wanted the night to be ordinary—mild, grey, smelling of wet stone and salt from the harbour—so that nothing could be blamed on anything but herself.

She had dressed plainly, which for a Vane meant merely expensive rather than ostentatious: a dark coat, no jewellery except her grandmother's locket, which she wore now not as ornament but as evidence. Inside the locket, folded to the size of a postage stamp, was a slip of paper she had copied out by hand three times before she trusted the copy. Her father's study safe had a combination she had known since she was nine years old, when she'd watched him open it over his shoulder under the pretence of fetching his spectacles. Children see everything, she thought as she let herself out through the garden door. That is their one advantage over their parents, and it expires.

Ravensworth's lodgings were above the chandler's shop on Cordwainer Lane, in a room she knew because everyone in Ravensmere knew everything about everyone, or believed they did. The light was on behind the curtain. She stood across the lane for a full minute, watching the shadow move against the glass—a man pacing, stopping, pacing again—and understood that he was doing with his feet what she had been doing all evening with her thoughts: walking the same small circuit, looking for a way out of a room with no doors.

She crossed the lane and knocked.

The shadow stilled. A long pause followed, long enough that she imagined him deciding whether to answer at all, and then the lock turned and Sebastian Ravensworth stood in the doorway with the lamp held high, as though expecting to find a delegation of enemies on the step. He looked worse than he had at the Ashcombe dinner—or perhaps she had simply learned since then how to read him. The composure was still there, the investigator's smooth surface, but beneath it she could see the strain like a crack running through glazed porcelain: the sleeplessness around his eyes, the faint bruising of exhaustion, the way his gaze went past her shoulder to scan the empty lane before it settled on her face.

"Miss Vane." His voice gave away nothing. "It's late."

"It is," she said. "May I come in? I'd rather not say this on your doorstep. Ravensmere has excellent hearing."

He hesitated only a moment, then stepped aside. The room above the chandler's was spare and orderly in a way that told her more about him than any conversation might have done: papers squared into precise stacks, each one weighted; a wall of small cards pinned in grids, covered in symbols she now recognised with a lurch—the roses, the anchors, the broken columns of the graveyard, transcribed and cross-referenced until they must have haunted his dreams. On the table, the ledger lay open under the lamp, its marginal flourishes crawling like ivy across two centuries.

"You've been busy," she said.

"I've been thorough. It isn't the same thing, but it looks better." He closed the ledger—not hiding it, she noticed, simply ending the display—and gestured to the room's single chair. She remained standing. Sitting would have made this a negotiation, and she had not come to negotiate.

"I know about Quill," she said.

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise exactly—confirmation. "Do you."

"He met you at the tannery. He confessed to you. And four days later he fell from the harbour stairs and cracked his skull on the pilings, and the police wrote *accident* in their book because the police in Ravensmere write what they are asked to write." She kept her voice level. It cost her something to do it; her hands, hidden in her coat pockets, were not level at all. "I also know my father sent Quill to you. I know about the letter offering you money. I know about the search of your office. I know—" and here she paused, because this was the sentence she had rehearsed twenty times and it still came out wrong, so she said it plainly instead, "—I know that whatever my father ordered, he did not order what happened to Mr. Quill. And I know you don't believe me. You shouldn't. But I'm going to tell you why I believe it, and then I'm going to give you something, and then you can do what you like with both."

Sebastian set down the lamp. For a moment he said nothing, and the silence between them filled with the tick of the cheap mantel clock and the distant slap of water against the harbour wall. Then he pulled the second chair from beneath the table, turned it to face her, and sat down himself upon the bed's edge—an arrangement that put them at an angle, neither above nor below, and she understood it as a courtesy extended by a careful man to a woman he did not yet trust.

"Tell me," he said.

* * *

She began with her childhood, because there was no other place to begin.

"I grew up in a house where certain questions were never asked. You'll know the kind. We didn't discuss money in front of the servants—not because we were ashamed of it, but because the shape of it was strange. There was the bank, which was ours, respectable and dull. And there was the other thing, the thing that paid for my schooling and the clinic roof and the seawall stones, and that thing had no name. It was 'the trust,' always, as if there were only one trust in the world and we were its priests." She smiled without warmth. "When I was twelve I asked my mother where the trust's money came from. She looked at me the way people look at a child who has asked about death in a crowded room, and she said, *From the Founder's foresight,* and she never spoke of it again. But she squeezed my hand very hard under the table, and I remember thinking even then: that is not the grip of a woman describing foresight. That is the grip of a woman holding on."

Sebastian listened without interrupting. She had expected him to take notes—he was famous, by now infamous, for the memory that needed none—but his hands stayed still in his lap, and she realised he was memorising her instead. It should have been unsettling. She found, strangely, that it steadied her. Here, at least, was a man who would not lose a word of what she said, who would carry it whether she lived to repeat it or not. In Ravensmere, that was a kind of sanctuary.

"When I came home from school, my father began preparing me. Not telling me—preparing me. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole disease of this town. He taught me the cipher without ever calling it a cipher. Little games with gravestone carvings on our Sunday walks. *What does the broken column mean, Cressida? What does the anchor mean?* I thought it was sentiment. Founders' families are sentimental about their dead; it's practically a profession. It wasn't until I was twenty-two and he brought me into the bank's back office and showed me the disbursement schedule—fifty years of payments to a man named E.T., drawn against a fortune that officially died with him—that I understood I hadn't been learning flowers. I'd been learning my inheritance."

"And you took your place in it," Sebastian said. Not accusingly. Simply establishing the fact, the way he would establish a date.

"Yes." She did not soften it. "I signed the renewal. Every generation signs; Elias Ashcombe told you that, I imagine, in his own fashion. I told myself what everyone tells themselves: that the money does good, that the town lives on it, that the sin—if there was a sin—was old and cold and belonged to people whose bones had finished arguing about it. I was good at telling myself things. My family breeds for it, like horses bred for a particular gait." She drew a breath. "But then you came. And things began happening that were not in the gait."

"The letter."

"The letter. The search of your rooms—I heard Quill report it to my father, verbatim, because my father has me take minutes at meetings he thinks are too delicate for a secretary. Photographed, not stolen. He was proud of that. *We show him we can reach him without touching him.*" She watched Sebastian's face for the flicker she expected and found it: the investigator's quiet fury, banked but alive. "And then Miss Idris disappeared, and Mr. Quill died, and I sat at my father's breakfast table watching him eat kippers and read the shipping news, and I understood that I was looking at a man who was frightened. My father has been many things in my life—stern, vain, occasionally cruel in the way powerful men are cruel without noticing—but I have never once seen him frightened. Whatever he ordered, Mr. Ravensworth, it has slipped its leash. And a frightened man with a slipped leash will do anything, including things he never intended, and then stand at the graveside looking surprised."

"You're defending him."

"No," she said. "I'm indicting him. Can't you hear it? I'm telling you my father is a man who hired intimidation, who threatened you, who drove an old schoolteacher from her home—and that I stayed silent while he did it, because he was my father and the money was mine and the town was ours. I'm not asking you to acquit him. I'm asking you to be accurate. If you hang the wrong murder on him, the right murderer walks free, and my father becomes a martyr to every family in Ravensmere who ever signed a renewal. Accuracy is mercy, in this case. Accuracy is also strategy. I learned that at my father's knee, along with the flowers."

Sebastian rose and walked to the window, standing where the lamplight wouldn't reach his face. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—less the interrogator, more the tired man beneath.

"Miss Idris. Do you know where she is?"

"Alive," Cressida said. "Quill told you true, I think, insofar as Quill ever told anything true. She was put on a boat. Coerced, terrified, but breathing. My father's instruction was that she be *persuaded to visit relatives*, and I believe persuasion is all she suffered—Quill was a solicitor, not a butcher; his instruments were paper. But someone else's instruments may not be paper, and if she comes back to testify, whoever killed Quill will want her silenced properly. So if you mean to find her, find her quickly, and don't tell anyone—including, for a while, me. I love my father, Mr. Ravensworth. Love is not the same as trust, and I have finally learned the difference at an age when most people learn it from a broken engagement rather than a body on the harbour stairs."

He turned from the window. "Why now? Why come to me tonight?"

Because I dreamed of Miss Idris's cottage, she almost said, of the teacup left half-drunk, of the shawl folded neatly over a chair as if tidiness could make an abduction look like a holiday. Because I have begun to understand that silence is not neutrality, it is a payment, and I have been paying it my whole life without once seeing the bill. Because you looked at me across my father's dinner table as though I were a locked drawer, and I have spent a month being a locked drawer, and I am tired of it.

What she said was: "Because you're going to finish this with or without me, and I would rather it be with. Because the town deserves to choose, and it cannot choose what it doesn't know. And because—" she reached up and unclasped the locket, and the small sound of it was louder in the little room than the harbour bell, "—because I would rather betray my father than bury another secret in this town. There are enough graves here already. Some of them are empty, and some of them aren't, and I am done adding to either kind."

She opened the locket and drew out the folded slip of paper. The Vane segment. Fourteen lines of symbols transcribed in her own hand from the ledger page her family had guarded for a hundred years—the roses and anchors and broken columns that, joined to the Halloway piece and the Merriweather piece and the Cole piece and the Ashcombe piece, would make the record whole. Names. Dates. The truth about what had happened to Emrys Thorne when he came back from the dead to reclaim what was his.

She held it out. For a moment neither of them moved, and the paper hung in the air between them like a small pale bridge.

"If I take this," Sebastian said quietly, "there's no going back. Not for you. Your father will know within a day who gave it to me. The other families will know. You'll be a traitor to five dynasties and a curiosity to the sixth part of the town that isn't one of them. Ravensmere forgives many things, Miss Vane. It does not forgive a daughter who opens her father's safe."

"My father built a town on a lie and called it foresight," Cressida said. "Let me build one small thing on the truth and call it mine."

He took the paper. She watched him unfold it, watched his eyes move across the symbols with that terrible swift completeness of his—one pass, and it was in him forever, filed among the exhumed coffin and the wax seals and the tannery plot and every other thing he could not forget even if he begged to. Then he looked up at her, and for the first time since the Ashcombe dinner she saw him look at her not as a locked drawer but as a person: wary still, and grieving for her already in the practical way of a man who knows what exposure costs, but *seeing* her.

"There's something else," she said. "Before you assemble it all. Before you go to the vault, because I know that's where this ends—everything in this town ends at the vault eventually. When you have the whole record, when you know every name and every date and what was done in the tannery yard a century ago—give the town a chance. Don't hand it to the newspapers first. Don't hand it to the police, who write what they're asked. Bring it to Ravensmere itself. Let them read it. Let the Halloways and Merriweathers and Coles and Ashcombes and Vanes stand in front of their neighbours and hear what their inheritances cost. Some of them will choose the lie. Some of them will choose each other over the truth, the way families do. But some of them—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—some of them are like me, Mr. Ravensworth. Waiting all their lives for someone to make it possible to stop. Give them that chance. If you publish it over their heads, you'll destroy the town to save it. If you give it to them, they might just surprise us both. It's the only thing my father never planned for, you know. In a hundred years, no one ever asked Ravensmere what *it* wanted. Everyone decided for it. Even Emrys Thorne, in his clever dead man's cipher—even he decided. Be the first one to ask."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog shifted in its sleep, and the harbour water kept up its patient applause against the wall.

Sebastian folded the paper along its original creases and placed it inside the ledger, between the pages where the cipher lived, as though returning a stray leaf to its tree.

"All right," he said. "The town gets its verdict. But hear my condition, Miss Vane, because I'll only make it once. Tomorrow morning I go to Mrs. Halloway, and then I go to the vault, and I walk in carrying all five segments and the truth about Quill. If your father reaches for that ledger to burn it—if he so much as warms the cover with his hands—your chance for Ravensmere dies with my patience. I have buried one teacher and one solicitor and a founder who wouldn't stay dead. I will not bury the truth too. Not for anyone's redemption. Not even yours."

"Fair," Cressida said. "My father would respect the terms, if nothing else. He respects strength; it's the one language he never learned to counterfeit." She fastened the empty locket back around her neck—a strange lightness, wearing a vessel that no longer carried its secret—and moved toward the door. At the threshold she paused, her hand on the latch, and looked back at the lamplit room, at the man sitting very still amid his grids of carved flowers, at the ledger closed over its recovered fragment like a wound closed over a splinter it would one day work to the surface.

"Mr. Ravensworth," she said. "When you decode the last of it—you'll find a final entry. In Thorne's own hand, after his own death. My father has never let me read what it says. He goes white whenever it's mentioned." She managed something that was nearly a smile. "Whatever it is, I think it's the reason he's frightened. And I think it's the reason you should hurry."

The latch clicked shut behind her. Below, in the lane, her footsteps faded toward the harbour, unhurried, a woman walking home through the ordinary night she had chosen over the extraordinary silence.

Sebastian sat alone with the ledger, and after a long moment opened it again to the final pages—the ones the cipher had not yet yielded—and read, for the hundredth time, the last dated entry in a hand that was not Thorne's:

*The account closes when the last keeper can no longer bear the cost.*

And beneath it, in fresh ink, barely a week old, a new line had been added.
