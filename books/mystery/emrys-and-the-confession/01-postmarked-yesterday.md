## 1. Postmarked Yesterday

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt wrong from the start. Bad news in my experience prefers the end of the week — it likes to settle into you over a weekend, to have time to grow roots before Monday comes asking questions. But this one came on a Tuesday, in a plain white envelope with no return address, and it sat in my mailbox between an electric bill and a flyer for gutter cleaning like it had every right to be there.

I almost didn't open it that night. I want to be honest about that, because everything that came after began with the small, ordinary decision to sit down at my kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold beside me and slide a butter knife under the flap. Three years of making a podcast about the dead teaches you a certain superstition: that some envelopes are doors, and once opened, they don't close again.

The postmark read GREYHARROW, and beneath it a date that stopped my hand mid-motion.

The day after Edwin Marsh's funeral.

I hadn't gone to the funeral. I'd thought about it — I'd thought about a great many things involving Greyharrow in the weeks since I learned a stranger by that name had died alone in a rooming house four hundred miles up the coast — but thinking and going are different animals, and I told myself I had no business there. No family left in the town. No story yet, only a name that snagged on something in my memory like a fishhook in a sweater.

So whoever mailed this letter had mailed it the day after a funeral I did not attend, in a town I had not visited in fifteen years, addressed to me.

*Lior Eastwick.*

Not printed. Handwritten, in a slanted, careful script, the kind of penmanship taught in schools that no longer exist, each letter formed with the patience of someone who had all the time in the world and knew exactly how little of it was left.

I turned the envelope over twice before opening it. Checked the weight of it. Held it up to the lamp as if paper could be made transparent by wanting hard enough. Then I cut it open, and my life divided cleanly into the part before and the part after, the way lives do — quietly, without thunder, over a kitchen table, while the tea goes cold.

* * *

There were three pages. The handwriting was the same throughout — that same patient slant — but the ink changed partway down the second page, blue giving way to black, as though the writer had set the letter down and come back to it another night. Or several nights. As though it had been written the way confessions are actually written: not in a rush of guilt but in the slow, grinding way a stone is worn smooth, a little at a time, over years.

It was unsigned.

It began:

*You won't know who this is from, and that's the point. By the time you read it there'll be no one left to ask, and I've spent forty years being asked the wrong questions by people who wanted answers more than they wanted truth. You're different. I've listened to you. Every episode. Twice, some of them. You ask questions like you're sorry to be asking them, and that's rarer than you'd think.*

*My name doesn't matter anymore. What matters is what happened at Greyharrow in October of 1984, and what I'm about to tell you is the whole of it, or near enough. I've carried it so long it's grown into me like a splinter works itself into the pad of a thumb. You stop feeling it as separate from yourself. That's the trick of guilt nobody warns you about — it doesn't stay a thing you carry. It becomes the hand that carries it.*

*I killed no one. Understand that first, because everything else I say will sound like the words of a murderer, and I need you to hear past them. But I know who died, and I know where, and I know why the sea was allowed to keep it, and I know the names of everyone who helped make sure it stayed kept.*

*Ask your mother about the winter lights.*

I read that last line four times. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below my window a car passed, tires hissing on wet asphalt, and I became aware that I had been holding my breath long enough for the quiet of the apartment to feel physical, pressurized, like the inside of a diving bell.

*Ask your mother about the winter lights.*

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing that took the letter out of the category of crank mail and hoax and podcast-obsessive and dropped it somewhere much colder.

Nobody alive knew about the winter lights.

* * *

Let me explain what that means, because it will mean nothing to you and it meant everything to me.

When I was six years old, before my mother Sable packed our life into two suitcases and drove us out of Greyharrow forever, before the estrangement calcified into silence and then into habit and then into something neither of us knew how to name anymore — when I was six, she used to tell me a story at bedtime. Not a real story. A made-up thing, the kind parents invent from spare parts, and hers was about the winter lights: little lamps that came on along the harbour wall on the coldest nights of the year, lit by no one, glowing green-gold through the fog, and if a child was very still and very quiet, the lights would lean toward them like flowers toward sun.

It was nonsense. It was lovely nonsense, the private mythology of one woman soothing one child in one rented room above a chandlery, and I forgot it for thirty years the way you forget most things — completely, without ceremony, until something drags it back up dripping.

She never wrote it down. She never told anyone else; I've asked, in the careful sideways way I've learned to ask her anything, and she went quiet in that particular way of hers, the silence with walls in it. The story existed in exactly two places in the universe: her mouth, and my memory. And my memory of it was so buried that when I read those five words at my kitchen table, my first reaction wasn't recognition. It was vertigo — the sensation of a floor you trusted turning out to be a trapdoor.

*Ask your mother about the winter lights.*

Whoever wrote this letter knew a bedtime story invented in a rented room in 1987, told to a six-year-old, repeated to no one, recorded nowhere.

Unless.

Unless the writer had been there. Unless the writer had heard it through a wall, or a window, or from the mouth of the woman herself, back when she was young and frightened and telling stories to keep a child's mind off the sound of the gulls fighting over the harbour refuse. Unless the writer had known my mother when my mother was still a person and not just the wound I carried around under my ribs.

And there was only one person the letter could be from, if any of its other claims were true. Only one person whose knowledge of Greyharrow ran deep enough, whose absence ran long enough, whose voice — and I use the word loosely, for ink has no voice, and yet — whose voice sounded, as I read on through the second and third pages, like something I had been listening to for three years without knowing it.

* * *

Emrys Vale disappeared in October 1984. He was seventeen. His father kept the lighthouse at the end of the north spit, a grey tower the town pretended to be proud of, and Emrys was the lighthouse keeper's son in the way that defined him entirely in Greyharrow's imagination: solitary, odd, always walking the spit alone with his coat collar up, always looking at the water instead of the town. When he vanished during the storm of the twelfth, the town searched for three days and mourned for one and then, with the efficiency of coastal places that have learned the sea takes what it takes, got on with things.

His body was never found. His file was never closed. And in the forty years since, his case had become the sediment at the bottom of Greyharrow's collective memory — silted over, walked on, built upon, invisible and load-bearing all at once.

Three years ago I started a podcast about him. *The Light at Greyharrow.* Twenty-two episodes and counting, though I hadn't known when I started how many it would take, or how far the story would reach past the edges of one boy's disappearance and into the foundations of the town itself. I documented everything. The search. The inconsistencies in the constable's report. The witness statements that contradicted each other like strangers describing different storms. The fact that Emrys's father, Aldous Vale, stopped speaking to half the town afterward and died in 1991 still officially waiting for his son to come home.

I knew the case the way you know a house you've lived in alone for years — every creak, every draft, every place the light falls strangely.

Which is why the second page of the letter did what it did to me.

*You'll want proof,* it said. *Everyone wants proof. So here is something only I could know, something I've never said aloud to any living soul. On the night I went missing, I wasn't on the spit. I was at the old cannery. I saw what I saw through the east loading door, the one with the chain, and the chain was new that autumn — someone had replaced it that September, and the old chain is still coiled inside the doorway under a drift of rope, because I put it there myself and no one ever moved it.*

*You said in your seventh episode that the cannery door was chained. You were wrong about why. Everyone's wrong about why. The chain wasn't to keep people out.*

*It was to keep something in.*

I set the letter down. My hands were not steady, and I watched them not being steady with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to a character in one of my episodes rather than to me.

Here is what I remembered, sitting in my kitchen with the letter lying on the wood grain like a dead bird: Episode seven aired fourteen months ago. In it, I described the cannery — condemned since the nineties, a long low hulk of rusted corrugated steel at the mouth of Miller's Creek — and I mentioned, in passing, in a single sentence I nearly cut in editing, that the east loading door had been secured with a chain. I speculated, on air, that the chain was recent, probably municipal, probably liability-driven.

I was wrong about why. The letter said so with the flat certainty of firsthand knowledge, and then it added a detail I had never broadcast, never researched, never even imagined: the old chain, coiled inside the doorway, under a drift of rope. Placed there by the writer himself.

If that was true — if there really was an old chain inside the cannery door — then the writer had been inside the cannery. Before the storm. Before the disappearance. And he had known, for forty years, exactly what that chain was for, and he had chosen this moment, this stranger's funeral, this postmark, to begin telling me.

But it was the third page that undid me. The third page, where the ink changed from blue to black, where the handwriting pressed harder into the paper as though the pen were being driven by something heavier than a hand.

*I've listened to every word you've made about me,* it said. *Yes. About me. You never guessed, and I don't blame you — I made sure of that a long time ago. But you should know that when you talk about the boy on the spit, you're talking to him. You've been talking to him for three years, Lior, and he's heard all of it, and there's a part of him — there was a part of him — that looked forward to your voice every month the way sailors used to look forward to the supply boat.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you sleep, though I doubt you will.*

*You sign off every episode the same way. You say: "For the ones the sea keeps." You think you made that phrase up. You didn't. Your mother used to say it. She said it standing on the harbour wall in November of 1984, the week after I went missing, and I heard her say it, and I have never forgotten it, and now neither will you.*

*Come home, little wick. Ask her about the winter lights. Ask her what she saw.*

Little wick.

I did not move for a long time. The apartment darkened around me by degrees, the city outside doing its indifferent glittering, and I sat with the third page in both hands like something hot, reading the two words over and over until they lost their shape and became pure sound.

*Little wick.*

A nickname. My nickname. Given to me by my father — Thomas Eastwick, drowned off Greyharrow when I was four, a man I remember as a pair of hands lifting me onto a shoulder and a laugh I can no longer reconstruct — because I was small and pale and, he said, I glowed. *Our little wick.* My mother used it sometimes, after, softly, the way you touch a bruise to check whether it still hurts. She stopped using it when I was nine or ten. She has not used it in thirty years. My father has been dead for thirty-six.

No one alive calls me that. No one living has ever heard it except my mother, and my mother does not write letters in a schoolroom hand, and my mother is eighty miles away and has not spoken more than fifty consecutive words to me in two years.

The letter was signed with nothing. No name, no initial. Just a final line, smaller than the rest, pressed so lightly into the page I had to hold it to the lamp to read it at all:

*Postmarked yesterday, because tomorrow was never promised to a man my age. Forgive the haste. Forgive everything. Come home.*

I looked at the postmark again. Greyharrow. The date. The day after Edwin Marsh was lowered into the ground in a plot nobody had paid for, in a service nobody claimed, attended — according to the clipping I'd found — by a handful of curious townspeople and one woman from the rooming house.

Edwin Marsh. A stranger with no family, no history anyone could produce, no photographs younger than twenty years. A man who had appeared in Greyharrow sometime in the distant past, lived quietly, left quietly, and died quietly, leaving behind a rented room, a few possessions, and — though I would not learn this for days yet — a name that more than one mourner whispered didn't belong to him at all.

I stood at my kitchen window for a long time that night, the letter folded on the table behind me, the city burning its indifferent lights against the dark. Somewhere in my chest, under three years of professional detachment and thirty years of practiced forgetting, something had begun, very quietly, to knock.

*Ask your mother about the winter lights.*

I picked up my phone. I put it down. I picked it up again. Her number sat in my contacts under *Mum*, untouched for months, a small grey stone in the palm of my hand.

Outside, far off, a ship's horn sounded across the water — one long note, rising and fading, the sound every coastal child learns before they learn their own name, the sound that means someone is leaving, or arriving, or warning.

I dialed.

It rang once. Twice. On the third ring, I heard her pick up, heard the small intake of breath she always made before speaking, and I opened my mouth to say *Mum, it's me* —

— and stopped. Because the letter was on the table behind me, and the words were in my ears, and I understood suddenly, with total clarity, that whatever I said next would change which side of the trapdoor I stood on.

"Little wick," I said instead. Just to hear it. Just to see what she would do.

The line went silent. Not empty — silent, the charged silence of someone listening hard, of a held breath, of a door being held shut from the inside. Ten seconds. Twenty. Long enough for me to hear, faintly, beneath the nothing, a sound I couldn't place — a low, rhythmic creaking, like a floorboard, or a swing, or a boat knocking against a dock.

Then my mother spoke, and her voice was not angry, and it was not guilty.

It was afraid.

"Lior," she said. "Whatever you've been sent — burn it. Don't read it again. Don't come here."

"I haven't—" I began.

"Don't," she said, and the word cracked down the middle. "Don't go to Greyharrow."

The line went dead.

I stood in my dark kitchen holding a phone that had stopped being a phone and become a verdict, and I understood two things with perfect certainty. First: my mother knew what the letter was before I told her a single word about it.

And second: I was already packing.
