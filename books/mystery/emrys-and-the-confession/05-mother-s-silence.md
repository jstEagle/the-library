## 5. Mother's Silence

The boarding house phone sat on a small table in the hallway, its cord curled like something that had once tried to escape. Maren had pointed it out to Lior the night before with a shrug — *mobile signal's poor up here, love, you'll want the landline* — and now Lior stood before it in the grey morning light, holding a slip of paper with a number they knew by heart and hadn't dialed in three years.

Fog pressed against the window at the end of the hall. Beyond it, Greyharrow's harbor was a rumor: the sound of water slapping pilings, a gull's cry muffled to almost nothing, the groan of a boat somewhere out in the white. The town existed today only as sound, and all of it sounded like breathing.

Lior had rehearsed this call on the walk back from the funeral yesterday, pacing the cliff path while the mourners' whispers still clung to their coat. *Mum. It's me. I'm in Greyharrow.* No — too blunt. *I've been doing some research, and—* No, worse; Sable would hear the podcast in the word *research*, would hear three years of episodes she'd never once acknowledged, and the line would go dead before Lior finished the sentence.

There was no good opening. There was only the number, and the dial tone, and the fact that a dead man had written Lior's childhood nickname in handwriting gone soft at the edges, like something left out in sea air.

They fed coins into the slot. The line rang four times — long, oceanic rings, each one stretching further than a city ring should — and then clicked open onto silence. Not an answering machine. A held breath.

"Mum."

Another silence, longer. Then, faintly, as if from the bottom of a well: "Lior."

Not *love*. Not *darling*. Just their name, flat as a stone laid on a grave. Lior gripped the receiver tighter. Even now, even after everything, the voice went through them like cold water — that low, smoke-and-salt voice that had sung them to sleep once upon a time, before the leaving, before the years of postcards with no return address, before the estrangement hardened into a fact of the family, like a birthmark.

"You got my letters," Lior said. "The ones from the funeral home. I know you did, because the notice came back marked forwarded."

"I read them." A pause. Somewhere behind Sable's voice, a clock ticked — the old kitchen clock, Lior realized, the one with the brass pendulum shaped like a fish. Their mother had kept the clock. She'd taken almost nothing when she left Greyharrow, but she'd taken the clock, and Lior had spent their whole childhood listening to it tick without knowing where it came from or why it mattered to her. "You shouldn't be there, Lior."

"I'm working."

"Working." The word came out with a thin edge of laughter that wasn't laughter at all. "Is that what we're calling it now. That show. Those—" a breath, controlled, "—those stories you tell strangers about people who can't answer back."

"They can't answer back because most of them are dead, Mum. That's rather the point."

"Don't." Sharp now, and under the sharpness something else, something quick and wet. "Don't be clever with me. Not about this."

Lior closed their eyes. The fog breathed against the glass. Down the hall, Maren's radio murmured through the kitchen door — shipping forecast, wind speeds, sea states — the litany of a town that lived by weather the way other towns lived by scripture.

"I'm not calling about the show," Lior said, and made themselves say it plainly, no cleverness, no armor. "I'm calling because I went to a funeral yesterday. Edwin Marsh. You might have seen the name. He died alone, Mum. No family. No one to claim him. And I think—" the words caught, and Lior pushed through the catch, "—I think he wasn't who he said he was. I think he was Emrys Vale."

The tick of the fish-clock, transmitted down forty years and four hundred miles, seemed to stop.

When Sable spoke again, her voice had changed entirely. All the iron had gone out of it. What remained was quiet and careful and very young, a girl's voice wearing a woman's throat, and it said only one word:

"Who told you that?"

"So it's true."

"That is not what I asked." And there — there it was again, that strange inversion, the fear running the wrong direction. Lior had imagined this call a hundred times. In every version, Sable denied, deflected, grew cold, hung up in guilt. Guilt had a shape Lior knew how to fight. But this — this was not guilt. This was a woman standing at a window, watching for something coming up the road.

"Nobody told me," Lior said slowly. "That's the thing, Mum. Nobody tells me anything in this town. I worked it out. The dates, the age, the hands — he had a scar, in episode seven I described a witness account of a boy who burned his hand on a lamp housing, and the undertaker mentioned Marsh had an old burn scar, and—"

"Lior."

"—and the letter, Mum. Someone sent me a letter. Postmarked the day after the funeral. It's a confession. It names things only Emrys Vale could have known, and it calls me by—" their voice betrayed them, thinned out, "—it calls me by my nickname. Nobody alive knows that name. Nobody except you."

The silence on the line stretched so long that Lior checked whether the call had dropped. The connection hummed, alive, patient. Somewhere far away, waves were hitting a shore Lior couldn't see.

"Sable Eastwick," Lior said, using the full name like a key turned in a lock. "Did you write to me?"

"No." Barely a breath. "No, love, I didn't—"

"But you know who did."

The pendulum resumed, or perhaps Lior had only stopped hearing it. When their mother finally answered, each word arrived separately, placed down like stones across deep water.

"You need to come home. Away from there. Today."

"I'm not leaving."

"You don't understand what that place does." Her voice cracked along an old fault line. "You think it's fog and quaint little plaques and sad old mysteries you can put in people's ears. It isn't. It keeps things. It has been keeping a thing for forty years, and everyone who lives there has agreed, without ever saying so, to let it keep it, and the ones who didn't agree—" She stopped. Lior heard her swallow. "The ones who didn't agree are in the churchyard, Lior. Or they're nobody at all, like poor Mr. Marsh, buried with strangers singing hymns over a name that was never his."

"So you admit it. He was Emrys."

A long exhale, the kind that empties a person. "I admit nothing. I'm telling you to come home."

"I am home!" The words burst out of Lior louder than intended, echoing down the boarding-house hall, and they lowered their voice, pressed a hand flat against the wallpaper — damp, cold, faintly salted — as though steadying the building itself. "You left. Fifteen years ago you packed a bag and took the fish clock and you left me with Aunt Iris and a forwarding address that changed four times, and you never once said why. Never once, Mum. Do you know what I did with that? I built a whole career out of it. Every episode I've ever made is just me asking other families your question. *Why did you go? Who did you leave behind? What are you keeping?*"

On the other end, Sable made a small sound — not quite a word, not quite a sob. Something worn through from rubbing against itself for decades.

"There was a girl," she said at last, so quietly Lior had to lean into the receiver. "In 1984. After the storm. There was a girl, and I—"

And then, sharp as a gull striking water:

"*Don't.*"

Not Sable's voice. Not aimed at Lior. Aimed past them, over their shoulder, into whatever room their mother stood in — someone else present, someone close enough to touch her, close enough to hear. The word came clipped and male and afraid, and then came the small scuffle of a hand covering a mouthpiece, a hiss of whispered conference Lior could not make out, and Lior stood in the fog-lit hallway of Maren Quill's boarding house with their heart slamming against their ribs, understanding two things at once and both of them unbearable:

Their mother was not alone.

And whoever was with her had known, instantly, exactly which subject to kill.

"Mum?" Lior's voice came out steady, which surprised them. Three years of interviewing grieving people had taught their body this trick — calm above the waterline, panic below. "Mum, who's there with you?"

Static. Breathing. Then Sable again, composed now, too composed, the composure itself a confession. "It doesn't matter. Listen to me. Listen carefully, because I'll only say this once, and then I'm going to hang up, and you're going to be angry, and that's fine, be angry, anger keeps you moving." A breath. "There are initials on the memorial plaques by the harbor wall. Third plaque from the lighthouse steps, the one with the crack through the anchor. Count the dates backward from 1985. Don't write it down anywhere. Don't tell the deputy — especially not the deputy. And Lior—" the fish-clock ticked twice, three times, marking out her hesitation, "—if a man named Calloway asks you what your mother remembers about October 1984, you tell him nothing. You tell him your mother was a child and remembers nothing but rain."

"Calloway," Lior repeated. "As in Constable Calloway? The one from the case files? Mum, he's dead, his grandson's a deputy now, why would—"

"Nothing but rain," Sable said, and her voice broke clean through the middle of it, and beneath the break Lior heard it — unmistakable now, undeniable — not guilt, not shame, but terror, pure and protective and ancient, the terror of a woman who had spent forty years standing between something enormous and her child. "I love you. Come home. Please. Before the sea gives back what it's keeping."

The line went dead.

Lior stood holding the receiver to their ear long after the dial tone rose to fill the silence, that flat unbroken note, like the sound the horizon makes. Fog moved slowly past the window. Down the hall, the shipping forecast gave way to music, something old and gentle, and Maren's footsteps crossed the kitchen floor, and the world continued being ordinary, which felt suddenly like the strangest thing of all.

*Before the sea gives back what it's keeping.*

Lior set the receiver down gently, precisely, the way you set down something that might still be live. Then they took out their notebook — then stopped, remembering: *don't write it down anywhere.*

So they stood instead in the dim hallway and repeated it to themselves until it lodged: third plaque from the lighthouse steps, crack through the anchor, count the dates backward from 1985.

Third plaque. Crack through the anchor. Backward from 1985.

Outside, the fog was beginning to lift off the harbor in slow rags, and as it thinned, the outline of the memorial wall emerged piece by piece — granite, lichen-eaten, patient — waiting at the bottom of the lane the way it had waited for forty years.

Lior put on their coat.

* * *

The walk down took four minutes. Lior spent all of them not thinking about the male voice in the room with their mother, and failed completely.

*Calloway,* the voice had feared. Not the podcast. Not the confession letter. Not the police, even — though Rhys Calloway *was* the police, in his way, in this town's way. Whoever stood in Sable's kitchen had heard one word through the receiver and moved to cut the call like a surgeon. Which meant the name meant something to him. Which meant either he was protecting Sable —

— or he was watching her.

Lior reached the harbor wall. The fog had retreated far enough now to show the whole sweep of it: grey granite running along the curve of the bay, salt-scoured, topped with iron railings rusted to the color of dried blood. And set into the wall at intervals, flush with the stone, the memorial plaques — dozens of them, a century of drowned fishermen and lost ships and named griefs, each one engraved and darkened and quietly ignored by every passerby, because that is what towns do with memorials: they stop seeing them.

Lior found the lighthouse steps. Turned. Counted.

One. Two. Three.

The third plaque bore a relief of an anchor, and through the anchor ran a hairline crack, old and deliberate-looking, filled with a century of black grime. The inscription honored men lost in some storm of the 1920s, names worn shallow by weather. And below the names, along the plaque's lower edge, in a row of small carved numerals that any casual eye would take for more dates of loss—

Lior crouched. Pulled off a glove. Traced them with a bare fingertip, cold stone biting cold skin.

Dates. A sequence of them, spaced evenly, marching along the plaque's base like stitches closing a wound. 1949. 1953. 1961. 1968. 1972. 1977. 1981. 1984.

Then a gap.

Then 1985 — and after 1985, nothing. The carving simply stopped, mid-row, the final numeral abandoned half-finished, as though the hand that cut it had been interrupted.

Or as though the payments had.

Lior knelt there in the wind with their finger resting on the broken last date, and the dread that had been curdling in them since the phone call finally took its true shape. Because these weren't memorial dates. Memorial dates commemorated. These counted. Regular intervals, then a gap, then one more — the rhythm of a ledger, of sums logged and debts tracked, and the gap fell precisely where Emrys Vale walked into the sea of history and vanished.

Someone had been keeping accounts on this wall. Someone had been paying, or being paid, for years before the storm of 1984 — and then, for one terrible year after it, once more.

And their mother had told them to count backward from 1985. Had known, without seeing it, without touching it, exactly which stone held the arithmetic of her whole life.

Lior looked up. Across the harbor, half-veiled in the last of the fog, the memorial wall ran on toward the lighthouse steps, and beyond it the old cannery stood black against the brightening water, its broken windows like a row of closed eyes.

Somewhere inland, a phone was ringing in a kitchen with a fish-shaped pendulum, and a woman was deciding whether to answer it, and a man Lior could not picture was standing too close to her shoulder.

Lior took out their notebook after all — and, remembering, wrote nothing.

But they photographed the plaque, every numeral, and as the shutter clicked, a shadow crossed the wall. Lior looked up sharply.

Deputy Rhys Calloway stood ten feet away, hands in his pockets, watching them with an expression that was not surprise. It was something wearier. Something that had been waiting.

"My grandfather's files," he said quietly, "have a page torn out. I think you should see what's missing."
