## 16. The Trap Closes

The tapes arrived at the station at 9:14 on a Tuesday morning, in a padded envelope with no return address, addressed to Detective Ilsa Vann in careful block capitals that reminded her of ransom notes from old films.

She was standing at the evidence intake window when the courier handed it over, and she felt the weight of it before she opened it — not physical weight, though there was some, two cassettes and a sheaf of photocopied pages — but the other kind, the kind that settles into a detective's stomach when something arrives too neatly. In eleven years she had learned to distrust gifts. Evidence that came to you unbidden was either worthless or a weapon, and rarely anything in between.

"Sign here," said the courier.

She signed. She carried the envelope back to her desk, set it down beside her cold coffee, and put on nitrile gloves before she touched it again. Around her, the bullpen went on with its morning: phones ringing, someone laughing near the break room, the radiator ticking like a slow clock. Hallowmere PD did not know yet that its week was about to be decided by what was inside this envelope.

Two cassettes. TDK brand, the kind sold in every corner shop until the mid-nineties and nowhere since. Handwritten labels: *V.L. — ASHCOMBE — SIDE A* and *V.L. — ASHCOMBE — SIDE B*. And beneath them, folded twice, six photocopied pages of what appeared to be Jonah Amberdale's own notes — his handwriting, his abbreviations, his obsessive little system of cross-references she'd spent three months learning to read. Except these notes said things his published research had never said. These notes described the millrace. They described where Vera Loxley's body would have entered the water. They described, in one line that Ilsa read four times before her eyes would accept it:

*If the tape surfaces, I control the narrative. Record B is my insurance.*

She sat very still.

The trouble with knowing a man was innocent, she thought, was that it made you worse at your job. Not lazier — she would never be lazy — but slower at the moments when speed mattered most, because you kept stopping to ask whether the thing in front of you was true. A good detective, a clean detective, would look at these tapes and these notes and see a suspect who had found Vera Loxley's recordings years ago and had been sitting on them, curating them, building a podcast empire on a murder he'd committed or commissioned. A good detective would already be drafting the warrant.

Ilsa Vann looked at the tapes and saw a stage.

Because that was the word for it. She had stood in Jonah Amberdale's flat — searched it twice, once formally and once under a pretext so thin she was still slightly ashamed of it — and she had seen how he lived with the case. It was everywhere, yes, but it was *ordered*, and order was not the same as theater. His archive was a man building walls against grief, brick by indexed brick. He filed Vera Loxley because he could not bear her. That was not a killer's relationship to evidence. Killers didn't file. Killers rehearsed.

And the handwriting on those labels — she turned the cassettes under her desk lamp — was too legible. Jonah wrote like a man running; she'd seen pages of his notes, letters slanting forward, crossings-out abandoned mid-word. This label was drawn, not written. Each letter placed like a stone in a garden path.

Someone had studied him. Someone had studied him the way he studied the Ashcombes.

She bagged the envelope, logged it, and then did the thing she would think about later, in the long hours after everything broke open: she made a copy of the photocopies on the office machine before submitting the originals to the lab. Procedure said the evidence went straight to forensics untouched. Procedure also assumed the evidence was honest. Ilsa wanted her own set, because she no longer trusted anything that arrived in Hallowmere addressed to her by name.

*

By Thursday, the lab had done enough to ruin her week.

The cassette shells were old — genuinely old, aged deliberately or scavenged from estate sales, the plastic yellowed, one screw head stripped and re-seated. But the magnetic tape inside Side A had splice marks. Two of them. Clean splices, professional ones, the kind made with a proper editing block rather than a razor blade and steady hands. Vera Loxley, if she had ever recorded such a tape, had recorded it in one frightened sitting; Ilsa had listened to the recovered fragment from the parish archive enough times to know the texture of that woman's voice when it was real — the breath catches, the way she repeated names to herself like a rosary. This tape had none of that texture. This tape had seams.

And the voice on it — God help her, the voice on it was close. Close enough that a jury wouldn't hear the difference. A woman saying Edmund Ashcombe's name. A woman saying *he knows I know*. A woman saying, in a sentence that made Ilsa's skin crawl, *Jonah understands what happened to me, Jonah always understood* — which was impossible, because Vera Loxley had vanished ten years before Jonah Amberdale bought his first microphone, and whatever acquaintance they'd had was a tutoring arrangement and nothing more. Vera had never mentioned him in the authentic fragment. Not once.

But a jury wouldn't know that. A jury would hear a dead woman naming her killer's confederate, and the photocopied notes, and the coat — dear God, the coat, still sitting in the evidence locker wearing Jonah's dry-cleaning tag — and they would deliberate for forty minutes over lunch.

The handwriting analysis came back Friday. "Consistent with," the report said. Consistent with Jonah Amberdale's hand. Not identified as. Not conclusive. *Consistent with*, which was forensic language for *we can't rule it out*, which was prosecutorial language for *good enough*.

Chief Okafor called her into his office at four o'clock and had the warrant application already drafted on his desk.

"Talk to me, Vann," he said. "Tell me why I shouldn't take this upstairs tonight."

She stood in front of his desk and looked at the photograph clipped to the application — Jonah at the memorial vigil last month, holding a candle, looking like a man at a funeral, which was what he was, in his way.

"The splices," she said. "The handwriting's 'consistent with,' not confirmed. The notes reference details only the scene would contain, but they reference them in his vocabulary, which means whoever wrote them has read every transcript he's ever published — publicly available, Chief. Anyone could imitate him. Anyone who wanted to."

Okafor leaned back. "Or he's clever."

"He's not clever." She heard herself say it and realized it sounded like affection. She corrected course. "He's thorough. There's a difference. Clever men build alibis. Thorough men build filing systems. If Jonah Amberdale killed Vera Loxley, he would have needed to be clever ten years ago and thorough ever since, and what he's actually been is loud. Loud isn't guilt, Chief. Loud is a man trying to make the world hold still long enough to look at it."

Okafor was quiet for a moment. Through his window she could see the river, brown and patient, going about its business.

"You've spent a lot of time with this guy," he said.

"I've spent a lot of time with the case."

"Same thing, lately." He tapped the warrant. "The DA wants movement. We've got a body in this man's clothes, we've got recordings implicating him, we've got public pressure you could cut with a knife. If I sit on this and it turns out he's guilty, it's my neck. If I move on it and he's innocent—" He shrugged, and the shrug said everything procedure couldn't: *then we'll have moved on an innocent man, and the paperwork will call that diligence.*

"Give me forty-eight hours," Ilsa said.

"I'll give you until Monday morning." He slid the warrant into his drawer, and she watched it go the way you watch a loaded gun leave the room — relieved and not relieved at all.

*

She spent the weekend doing the thing she hated most: working around the evidence instead of with it.

Saturday she drove out to Ashcombe House. Officially, to follow up on the estate document request that had been pending for three weeks. Unofficially, because a thought had been growing in her all week like mold in a damp wall, and the thought had Marcus Ashcombe's name on it.

He met her at the door himself, in a grey sweater, holding a mug of coffee, the picture of a man interrupted from something blameless. The house behind him smelled of woodsmoke and furniture polish. Somewhere deeper in, a clock struck eleven.

"Detective Vann," he said warmly. "Come in out of the rain. My father's sleeping, so we'll have to be quiet, but I'm at your disposal."

She followed him into a sitting room where the portraits hung straight and gilded, except — she noted it again, the way you note a scar — the one space on the wall where paint was a shade darker than the rest, the ghost-outline of a frame turned elsewhere. Turned to face a wall somewhere in this house. She'd asked about it on her first visit and gotten a smile. She didn't ask now. Asking told people what you were looking at.

"I need to ask you about your movements over the past month," she said. "Informally."

"Informally." He settled into a chair across from her, crossing one ankle over his knee. "Of course. Though I confess I'm not sure what I could tell you that the newspapers haven't already invented."

"The envelope sent to me Tuesday. Two tapes, photocopied notes. You're aware of Mr. Amberdale's accusations regarding your family."

"Aware and heartbroken, frankly." His face did something skillful — sorrow folding into weariness. "That poor woman. That poor family of hers, if there is one. And now this podcaster dragging it all up again, and apparently implicating himself in the process. It's grotesque. I hope you arrest him soon, for everyone's sake. The town needs peace, Detective. Ten years is enough."

*The town needs peace.* She wrote it down in her notebook without looking, the way you write down a confession when the confessor doesn't know he's giving one. Because that was the phrase, wasn't it? That was the whole architecture of Hallowmere's silence, ten years of it, the civic project everyone had signed without reading: peace. Comfort. Enough. Every person in this town who had decided Vera Loxley slipped into the river had decided it in service of the same god, and the god's name was Enough, and its temple was this house, and its priest was sitting in front of her being kind to her.

"And your movements?" she prompted.

"Here, mostly. Caring for Father." He recited a fortnight of days with the ease of a man who had prepared them, each one anchored by a delivery, a doctor's visit, a neighbor's call. Corroborable. All of it corroborable, if the neighbors cooperated, and the neighbors would cooperate, because the neighbors had been cooperating with this family since before Ilsa was born.

She thanked him and left. On the doorstep, turning up her collar against the rain, she heard him say, pleasantly, behind her:

"Do give Mr. Amberdale my regards. When you see him. Wherever that ends up being."

She did not turn around. Turning around would have given him the moment, and she was beginning to understand that Marcus Ashcombe collected moments — small proofs of deference, small confirmations that the machinery of the town still answered to his hand. She walked to her car in the rain and let him watch her go, and only when she was behind the wheel did she let her hands shake.

Not fear. Anger. Anger with a specific shape: the shape of a warrant sitting in a drawer, waiting for Monday.

*

Sunday night she called Jonah.

It was a violation of about six different protocols, and she did it from her kitchen, on her personal phone, with a glass of wine she wasn't drinking. He picked up on the second ring, awake — of course he was awake; she suspected he hadn't slept properly in a decade.

"They sent me tapes," she said, without greeting. "Forged. Your handwriting, more or less. Vera's voice, more or less. You, implicated, more or less."

Silence on the line. Then, quietly: "Side A or Side B?"

"What?"

"Which side did they send first? A or B?"

"B. Why does that matter?"

A sound that was almost a laugh, hollowed out. "Because whoever made them numbered them like a series. Like episodes. Ilsa — I number things. That's mine. They took even my *ordering system*." His voice cracked on the last word, and she heard in it what she'd heard in his flat that first search: not a killer caught out, but a man watching his private grammar get counterfeited. "Do you believe me?"

She looked out her kitchen window at the dark street, at the rain coming down on Hallowmere like it had come down for ten years, indifferent and endless.

"I believe the tapes are forged," she said. "I believe the splices are real and the voice is manufactured and the handwriting is traced. What I believe doesn't matter as much as it should. There's a warrant drafted. It goes upstairs tomorrow morning unless I stop it, and I don't have anything to stop it with except doubt, and doubt doesn't photograph well in court."

"Then don't stop it," Jonah said.

She pulled the phone from her ear and looked at it, certain she'd misheard. "Say that again."

"Don't stop it. Let them arrest me." He was talking fast now, the way he talked when an idea was assembling itself faster than caution could vet it. "If I run, I'm guilty forever. If I fight it with lawyers, it takes a year and Marcus wins by attrition — that's what he's built for, Ilsa, patience, inheritance, the long game. But if I'm in custody, I'm contained. Contained is *safe*. And while I'm contained, you're the only person outside the walls who knows the tapes are fake, which makes you the freest investigator in this town. Go find the real half of Vera's recording. Go find what the clerk shredded. I can't do any of it from a cell, but you can, and right now you're the only one who will."

"This is insane. You're asking me to let procedure bury you on the chance I dig you out."

"I'm asking you to trade a pawn," Jonah said, "for the board."

She stood in her kitchen for a long time after the call ended, the wine untouched, the rain writing its long grey sentence down the window. She thought about the oath she'd taken, the neat machinery of it — probable cause, warrants, custody, process — and how beautifully that machinery ran, and how it ran equally well for truth and for lies, because it was built to run, not to see. Procedure didn't care whose hands fed it. Feed it a forged tape and a photocopied page and it would grind toward a conviction with the same serene efficiency it would bring to justice, if justice ever managed to arrive in a form the paperwork recognized.

Monday morning at 8:40, she unlocked the chief's drawer herself, took out the warrant, and carried it upstairs.

At 11:15, she stood in the parking lot of the Hallowmere public library and watched two uniformed officers walk Jonah Amberdale out in handcuffs. He looked smaller than his podcasts made him sound. As they passed her, he turned his head — just slightly, just enough — and their eyes met, and he gave her the smallest nod, a nod that said *you know what to do*, and then the door of the cruiser closed and the town began, visibly, almost audibly, to relax.

Ilsa got in her car and sat with both hands on the wheel.

Somewhere in this town was a real tape — nineteen minutes, maybe less, of a dead woman telling the truth — and somewhere in this town was a man who had spent ten years making sure it stayed buried, and who had just, with two forged cassettes and a photocopied page, committed the arrogance of believing the machinery would do the rest.

She started the engine.

The clerk's shredded files. The parish archive. The east wing of Ashcombe House, sealed since before she'd joined the force. She had until the trial — weeks, maybe — to find what the law's slow grinding never would.

She pulled out onto the wet road, toward the parish, and behind her the rain kept falling on Hallowmere like it knew something.
