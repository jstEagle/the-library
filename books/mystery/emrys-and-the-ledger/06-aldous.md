## 6. Aldous

The paper trail was supposed to be Theo's specialty. That was the joke, anyway—the archivist who couldn't stop following paper, who saw provenance the way other people saw faces. So when the donation file for the Marlowe-adjacent estate collection crossed his desk at the Ashford Public Library, he did what he always did: he pulled the acquisition record, and he read it the way he read everything, twice, once for content and once for what it was hiding.

The collection had come to the library eleven months ago. Forty-one boxes of estate papers, mostly agricultural ledgers and correspondence from a farm on the Vane side of the valley, donated by a holding company with a name so generic it was practically a confession: Ashford Heritage Trust, LLC. Theo had catalogued the boxes himself, and it was in the third box, in a folder of miscellaneous correspondence, that he'd found the letter recommending the library hire a consulting appraiser for the rare volumes—*a Mr. Theo Brandt comes highly regarded*—which was how Theo had come to be standing in Freya Zhou's bookshop on a Tuesday in October, holding a first edition of *The Wexford Poets* and feeling, for reasons he couldn't name, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was there.

Now, three weeks after the coffin had been opened and found empty, Theo sat at the microfilm reader in the library basement and followed the thread backward.

Ashford Heritage Trust, LLC. Registered agent: a law firm in the county seat. The firm's founding partner, according to the county historical society's own centennial publication, was a man named Edmund Vane—Cornelius Vane's grandson. The trust's registered address, when Theo drove out to look at it on his lunch break, was a converted mill office on the Vane estate road, all fieldstone and slate, with a brass plaque so tastefully understated you'd miss it unless you were looking. Theo was looking.

He sat in his car across the road for twenty minutes, watching a man in a green waxed jacket walk a dog along the estate's perimeter wall, and thought about what he was doing. He was thirty-one years old. He had a master's degree in archival science, a one-bedroom apartment above the pharmacy, and a job that consisted mostly of explaining to patrons that no, the library could not appraise their grandmother's Bible. And he was sitting in a borrowed hatchback, staking out a foundation that had, without his ever knowing it, been steering him for a year—toward a bookshop, toward a retired detective, toward a woman whose shop window had been shattered two nights ago by someone who hadn't left a single print.

The dog-walker passed out of sight. Theo drove back to town with his hands at ten and two and his heart going like a bird in a box.

* * *

The invitation came the next morning, and it came the way everything came from that quarter of Ashford: beautifully, and without fingerprints.

A cream-colored envelope, hand-addressed in fountain pen, left at the library's front desk with the morning mail. *Mr. Theo Brandt requests the pleasure of his company at lunch, Friday the fourteenth, one o'clock, the Vane House. — A. Vane.*

"He's never done that before," said Mrs. Okonkwo at the front desk, turning the envelope over in her brown hands as though it might bite. "Not once in thirty years. He sends the library a check at Christmas and a card when someone retires, and that's the whole of it. What have you done, Theo?"

"I have absolutely no idea," Theo said, which was true, and also the most useful lie he'd told in years.

He spent Thursday night deciding what to wear and hating himself for it. He spent Thursday night also reading, for the fourth time, the decoded entries Freya had left with him for safekeeping—photographs on his phone, printed and locked in his desk drawer, of ledger lines in a cipher hand that resolved, under her pencil-work, into sums and dates and a phrase that recurred like a heartbeat: *the arrangement.* Eleven names. Eleven sums. A foreman's name, Marlowe, written in the margin in a different ink, as though added later, as though someone had needed to remember who to blame.

He thought about not going. He thought about it seriously, lying in the dark above the pharmacy, listening to the delivery trucks at the bakery below. But not going was a kind of answer too, and Theo had learned—recently, uncomfortably, from watching Freya Zhou's face as she decoded the word *arrangement*—that in a town like Ashford, silence was a currency, and someone was always keeping the books.

* * *

The Vane House sat on a rise above the mill pond, and it was not a mansion so much as an argument—a long, low statement in gray stone that the Vanes had been here before the town, would be here after it, and saw no reason to raise their voice about it. Theo's cab crunched up a gravel drive lined with bare sycamores. A housekeeper met him at the door and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of eloquence.

Aldous Vane was waiting in a glass-walled room overlooking the pond, and he was smaller than Theo expected, and older, and more alive. Eighty-one years old, according to the centennial program, and he looked maybe seventy—white hair cut short, a cardigan the color of wet slate, eyes a pale, watery blue that nonetheless fixed on Theo with the unhurried attention of a man who had never once needed to hurry at anything.

"Mr. Brandt." He did not get up. He extended a hand instead, dry and cool as paper money. "Thank you for coming. Sit. The trout here is better than it has any right to be, considering the pond is mostly decorative."

Lunch was served by the silent housekeeper: trout, new potatoes, a salad with things in it Theo couldn't name. Aldous talked about the library. He talked about the roof, which needed replacing, and the special collections room, which needed a climate system, and the reading room windows, which were original to 1911 and leaked heat "like a colander." He talked numbers—precise, affectionate numbers—and Theo understood, with a sinking clarity, that this was the point of the first course. Aldous Vane was showing him the machinery. The library's operating budget, Theo realized, was roughly sixty percent Vane money. The police pension fund, he'd learned from the county filings, drew on a Vane endowment. The mill itself, the handsome brick ruin being converted to luxury lofts—the development company's parent was the Ashford Heritage Trust. Aldous Vane did not own Ashford. Aldous Vane was Ashford, the way water is a river.

"You catalogued the estate collection," Aldous said, in the middle of a sentence about the roof. Not a question. "The farm papers. Forty-one boxes. Tedious work, I imagine."

"It was interesting work, sir."

"Was it." Aldous buttered a piece of bread with terrible gentleness. "And did you find it interesting that the collection was donated at all? Most families burn that sort of thing, you know. Or sell it off piecemeal to dealers who scatter it to the four winds. We chose to keep it whole. Local. In the town's own keeping." He smiled, and it was a warm smile, a genuine one, Theo thought, and that was somehow worse. "I believe archives matter, Mr. Brandt. I believe a town that forgets its own history is a town that can be lied to. Wouldn't you agree?"

"I would, sir."

"Mm." Aldous looked out at the pond. A heron stood in the shallows, gray on gray, motionless as a monument. "Then you'll understand my concern. Since the exhumation—since that regrettable business at the cemetery—there's been a great deal of digging. Not literal, mostly. People going through old records, old papers. Asking old questions." He set down the knife. "And here is the thing about old questions, Mr. Brandt. They are like old wells. Everyone assumes that because the water is deep, it must be clean. But a well that hasn't been drawn from in a century—" he lifted his glass of water, considered it, drank—"is mostly silt and things that fell in and drowned. You don't drink from it. You cap it. You put a stone over it, and a fence, and you let it be."

The heron struck the water and came up with nothing.

"Mrs. Zhou," Aldous said.

Theo kept his face still. He'd had practice; he'd grown up with a mother who could read a lie off him at ten paces.

"She was a fine detective once, I'm told. Before. And now she keeps a bookshop that loses money every year—which is a kind of honesty, in its way—and she has taken it into her head that the empty coffin is a mystery, when in fact it is a tragedy, which is different. A mystery wants solving. A tragedy wants grieving." He turned those pale eyes back on Theo, and there was nothing watery in them at all. "You've been helping her. Don't trouble yourself denying it; the man who broke her window was seen, and the man who broke her window talks."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. He drank the water.

"I'm not angry," Aldous said, and he sounded it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "I'm offering you a career, Mr. Brandt. The special collections room will need a full-time archivist. A proper salary, a proper budget, a proper future—here, in the town where you were born, instead of whatever city you were saving up for. All I ask is that you help me keep the collection honest. If Mrs. Zhou brings you things to decode, I'd know about it. If she finds things in the margins of books, I'd see them first. That's all. Housekeeping. Stewardship." He smiled again, and spread his hands, and for a moment he looked like every benefactor's portrait in every library foyer in the county. "A town this old has only one thing worth protecting, Mr. Brandt, and that's its name. Everything else can be rebuilt."

"And if I say no?"

Aldous considered him for a long moment, and Theo watched the warmth drain out of the old man's face like water out of a sink, leaving something smooth and geological behind.

"Then you're a young man with a fine education and no prospects," Aldous said pleasantly, "in a town with one employer. People who dig up the past, Mr. Brandt, generally intend to bury something in the hole. I'd only ask that you be careful which end of the shovel you're holding."

He rang a small bell. The housekeeper appeared. Lunch was over.

* * *

The cab let Theo off in the square at half past two, and he stood for a while in front of Freya Zhou's bookshop with its plywood window and its hand-lettered sign—*OPEN, DESPITE EVERYTHING*—someone's joke, probably hers.

He thought about what Aldous had offered, and what Aldous had threatened, and understood finally that they were the same thing. The job was the trap. The salary was the leash. And if he took it and reported nothing, Aldous would know; if he took it and reported everything, Freya would be finished, and the eleven names in the ledger would stay buried under a stone that drank. And if he refused—

He thought about the man who had broken the window. *The man who talks.*

Theo pushed open the shop door. The bell over it gave its small, bright, absurd ring, and Freya looked up from behind the counter, a pencil behind her ear and a decoded page under her hand, and he saw her read his face in one glance, the way she must once have read a crime scene.

"Aldous Vane asked me to lunch," Theo said.

Something moved behind her eyes—not surprise. Recognition. "And?"

"And I think I just met the keeper of the ledger," Theo said. "He offered me a job. And Freya—" he pulled the folded cream envelope from his jacket, the invitation, and laid it on the counter between them like evidence, "—he knew about the window before I told him anything. Which means whoever broke it didn't just steal your books. They reported back. And there's one more thing." He took a breath. "The donation that brought me to your door. The forty-one boxes. The letter recommending me for the appraisal. It all traces back to the Ashford Heritage Trust. To the Vanes. He didn't send me to you by accident, Freya. He's been running me at you for a year."

Freya was very still. Outside, across the square, the afternoon light lay on the courthouse steps like something spilled. When she spoke, her voice was quiet and level and cold.

"Then he's been watching us longer than we thought," she said. "Which means we've got less time than I hoped. Theo—did you tell him no?"

Theo looked down at the envelope, at the fountain-pen flourishes of *A. Vane*, at the whole weight of a town balanced on one old man's signature.

"I told him I'd think about it," he said. "He gave me until Monday."

Freya nodded slowly, and reached under the counter, and came up with a folded map of the cemetery, creased white along a line drawn in red pencil.

"Then we'd better find what he's afraid of before Monday," she said. "There's a grave on Vane land that isn't in any record. And I think Cornelius Vane is still in it."
