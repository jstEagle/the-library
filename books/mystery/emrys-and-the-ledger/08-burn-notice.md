## 8. Burn Notice

The notice arrived the way bad weather arrives in Ashford: first a stillness, then a pressure, then everything at once.

Freya found it taped to her front door at seven in the morning, when the fog off the river was still thick enough to swallow the far side of the square. A single sheet, official letterhead, the town seal embossed in gold that caught no light in the grey. *Notice of Inquiry. Re: Freya Zhou, proprietor.* The words beneath it were careful, bureaucratic, and vicious — an investigation into "the possible involvement of the named party in recent incidents of theft and property damage," with a request that she "refrain from activities that may compromise said inquiry."

She read it twice. Then she peeled it off the glass with one fingernail, folded it into quarters, and stood for a long moment watching her own reflection waver in the door's old panes.

Behind her reflection, across the square, the police station's windows were dark except one. Chief Harlan Doss's office. She knew the shape of that light. She'd sat under it herself, twenty years ago, in the last case she'd worked before they'd gently, firmly, made it clear she was finished.

*They always use your own lamp to blind you,* her old sergeant used to say.

She went inside and put the kettle on. Her hands were steady. That surprised her more than anything else.

* * *

By nine, the phone had rung four times and stopped mid-ring each time — callers losing their nerve before she could answer. By ten, Ruth Marlowe had not come by for the Tuesday tea they'd begun, without ever quite agreeing to it, sharing. By eleven, the man who delivered her paper stock drove past the shop without slowing, and she saw him glance at the notice on the door as he passed, and she saw him decide not to see it.

The town had a language for this. It had no words, but it had a grammar: the way conversations ended when you entered a room; the way eyes slid to the middle distance; the way Mrs. Okafor at the bakery wrapped Freya's bread in silence and would not take her money, which was worse than taking it, because it meant pity, and pity meant the town had already written its verdict.

At noon, a second document arrived, this one by courier from the county licensing office. Her business license — the one she'd held for eleven years, renewed without comment every spring — was "under review pending resolution of outstanding inquiries." Thirty days. After thirty days, if the inquiry remained open, the shop would be closed.

She sat at her worktable among the un-stolen remnants of her stock — the common editions, the paperbacks, the books nobody at auction had wanted — and understood, with the clarity of cold water, what Aldous Vane was doing. He wasn't trying to frighten her. Frightened people run, and running people are hard to find. He was trying to make her *legible* — to write her into the town's records as a criminal, so that whatever she said afterward would sound like the ravings of a cornered thief. He was editing her the way his family had edited Cornelius out of a coffin and a foreman out of history.

The thought should have terrified her. Instead it settled over her like a coat she'd once worn well.

*You're erasing me,* she thought, addressing him across the square, across the money, across a hundred years of Vanes. *But I've spent my whole life reading marginalia. I know what erased pages look like. And I know how to make them speak.*

* * *

In the afternoon she did the small defiant things. She swept the glittering remains of the broken window from the threshold and stacked the boards she'd nailed over it. She watered the fern in the front bay that had survived the vandalism by virtue of being unremarkable. She opened the shop, put out the OPEN sign, and sat behind the counter where anyone passing could see her — visible, unhidden, refusing the shape they'd cut for her.

Three people came in all day. One wanted directions. One browsed without buying and left quickly, ashamed. The third was a boy of maybe twelve who bought a used paperback of sea stories with coins counted out of a sock, and when he left he looked back at her from the doorway and said, "My granddad says you're the only honest person left in this town."

She didn't ask which granddad. She was afraid the answer would be someone who'd gone quiet too.

When the light began to fail she turned the sign to CLOSED and stood in the dimness listening to the building settle around her. The empty places on the shelves where the auction books had been were like pulled teeth — the tongue keeps going back to the gap. Eleven years of quiet trade, and in two weeks she'd become a rumor with a file number.

That was when she heard the knock. Not at the front — at the back, the delivery door, three quick raps and then silence, the knock of someone who'd learned to be brief.

She went through the storeroom with her heart going hard and slow, and opened the door on Theo Brandt.

He looked terrible. His glasses were smudged, his coat was wrong for the weather, and there was a bruise-yellow shadow along his jaw that hadn't been there at the library. He clutched a canvas messenger bag against his chest the way children hold pillows during storms.

"Let me in," he said. "Please. Before someone sees."

She stepped aside and he came through the door like something blown in, and she locked it behind him and drew the bolt, and only then noticed his hands were shaking.

"They searched my office," he said, before she could speak. "This morning. No warrant shown, no inventory taken — just two men and the chief, going through my accession files. They took the donation paperwork. The provenance records. Everything connecting the collection to—" He stopped, swallowed. "To whoever donated it. Which is everything connecting it to Aldous Vane."

"You saved copies."

"I save copies of everything. It's archiving." He almost smiled; it collapsed halfway. "I scanned the ledger pages the first night. All of them. And I kept the scans off the library network, off any cloud, on a drive in my apartment, inside a hollowed-out copy of *Bartlett's Familiar Quotations*, because nobody has ever opened a Bartlett's in the history of the world." He set the bag on the worktable and unzipped it with fingers that wouldn't cooperate. "But Freya — they know I have them. Or they think I do. Someone followed me home last night. A grey sedan. It sat outside until three in the morning."

"So you brought them here." She said it without accusation. It was simply the fact, and they both knew it, and he flinched anyway.

"I didn't know where else to go. Everyone else is—" He gestured vaguely at the town beyond the walls, at the whole apparatus of quiet money and quieter fear. "The chief's pension fund. Half the library's endowment. The new wing at the hospital has Vane carved over the door. I called my mother in Corvallis and she asked me, sweetly, if I was sure I wasn't overworked." His voice cracked on the last word. "I'm twenty-six years old and the most powerful man in this county thinks I'm a problem, and the only person I know who isn't afraid of him is standing in a bookshop with a notice on her door."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. He was right that she wasn't afraid — or rather, the fear was there, but it had been processed into something else, something with edges, something usable. Twenty years ago they'd taken her career by making her doubt herself. She had sworn never again to doubt herself on another man's schedule.

"Sit down," she said. "There's tea. It's terrible. Drink it anyway."

* * *

Under the worktable lamp, surrounded by the smell of dust and glue, they laid the printouts out in sequence. The scans were good — sharp enough to catch the pencil-faint cipher in the margins, the columns of figures, the dates marching backward into 1904 like footsteps receding down a corridor.

"The final entry," Theo said. His voice dropped as though the pages might overhear. "I decoded it last week but I didn't understand it until today. Until they came for my files. Look."

He turned the last page toward her. The cipher resolved under her eye the way it always did now, the substitution key living somewhere behind her sternum:

*The arrangement does not end. The arrangement passes to the keeper. What was buried must stay buried, and the keeper must keep it.*

Below that, a date — not 1904, but 1931 — and a single initial rendered in the old style, a letter that could be read two ways depending on the hand: **A**.

"Aldous's grandfather," Freya said quietly. "Edmund Vane. He'd have been a young man in 1931. Old enough to inherit the secret. Old enough to enforce it."

"But that's not the part that matters." Theo tapped the page. "Read the entry before it. The one dated 1904, the month after the mill collapse."

She read it. *Paid in full to close the matter. Eleven souls accounted. The founder departs; the name remains.* And beneath it, in a different ink, added later, pressed hard enough to score the paper: *He tried to come back. The stone drinks.*

Her skin prickled. "The second grave."

"He went back," Theo said. "Cornelius. Years later, or months — whenever it was — he came back to Ashford, and someone met him on Vane land, and—" He couldn't finish it. He didn't need to. She'd stood over that disturbed earth herself, seen the fresh footprint beside a century-old crime.

"And the keeper," Freya said. "The arrangement passes to the keeper. Four generations of keepers, Theo. Edmund, then his son, then—" She stopped. The arithmetic completed itself with a click she felt in her teeth.

Aldous Vane was not covering up a crime committed by an ancestor.

Aldous Vane was the current holder of an office. A living institution, older than the police department, older than the library, funded by the same quiet money that paid the chief and licensed the shops and carved names over hospital doors. He wasn't destroying evidence because he feared exposure.

He was doing his job.

"That's why he can't stop," she said aloud, testing it. "That's why he escalated — the thefts, the notice, the search. A man protecting a secret can be bargained with. A man performing a duty can't. As long as he believes he's the keeper, every move we make just confirms the arrangement's necessity." She looked up at Theo. "We can't beat him with evidence alone. Evidence goes into files. Files get searched. Files get lost."

Theo's face had gone pale in the lamplight. "Then what do we do?"

Outside, the fog had thickened again, pressing against the boarded window like something leaning in to listen. Somewhere across the square, a light burned in the chief's office, patient as a held breath.

Freya gathered the pages, squared their edges, and felt the old pull settle into something new — not reluctance now, not curiosity, but purpose, cold and clean as a blade being honed.

"There's a centennial gala on Saturday," she said. "Every family in Ashford. Every camera. The mayor giving speeches about heritage." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Aldous thinks he's erased us both by Saturday. Let's show him what happens when you erase the wrong page."

Theo stared at her. "You're going to stand up in front of the entire town and—"

"I'm going to do what I've done my whole life," Freya Zhou said, and switched off the lamp. "I'm going to read aloud."
