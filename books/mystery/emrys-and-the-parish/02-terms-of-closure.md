## 2. Terms of Closure

The rain had followed Marguerite Dravenmoor from the train station all the way to the cathedral close, as if it too wished to see what was inside St. Aldhelm's. They stood a moment beneath the lychgate, letting it fall, because there was a discipline to arriving wet and unhurried — it told clients you were not a person who could be rushed.

Cordelia Loxley was waiting in the north porch, exactly where the letter said she would be, holding an umbrella she did not open. She was perhaps forty, with her aunt's long jaw and none of her patience, and she watched Marguerite cross the close with the expression of someone who has rehearsed a speech and is already losing faith in it.

"Miss Dravenmoor."

"Dravenmoor," Marguerite said, which was not a correction so much as a habit. "You wrote that the estate cannot settle."

"Eleven years." Cordelia folded the umbrella under her arm like a weapon she'd decided against. "Eleven years, and the solicitors won't declare her dead without a body, and they won't release the trust without probate, and they won't do *anything* without—" She stopped herself, breathed. "I'm sorry. You came a long way. Let me at least buy you tea before I make my case."

"You've already made it," Marguerite said. "The case is the tea. Show me the crypt."

* * *

There is a rule, and Marguerite had learned it the way one learns most rules — by breaking it.

Nineteen years earlier, in a narrow flat above a bookbinder's shop in Bath, a woman named Hester Vane had sat across from them with a photograph of her missing son and said, *I know it's been four years. I know what people say. But you'll look, won't you? You'll be the one who looks.*

Marguerite had been twenty-six and hungry for a reputation, and they had looked. They had looked for eleven months. They had found the boy's coat, his bicycle, a witness who swore she'd seen him on the canal towpath on the last night anyone saw him alive — and then the trail had gone into the water and stayed there, and Marguerite had stood in Hester Vane's kitchen with their hat in their hands and said the only honest sentence of their career: *I can't close this.*

Hester had thanked them. That was the unbearable part. She had thanked them, and pressed five pounds into their palm, and closed the door, and Marguerite had stood on the step listening to her cry through the wood, quietly, the way people cry when they have gotten used to it.

On the walk back to the station they had made themselves a promise shaped like a professional maxim, because archivists trust systems more than vows: *Never take a case you cannot close.* Not never fail — failure was weather, it happened to everyone. Never take. Never accept a commission whose ending you could not already see, dimly, the way you can see the far end of a corridor before you commit to walking down it.

In nineteen years they had refused forty-one cases. They kept the list. Refusal number thirty-one still woke them some nights — a missing bell-founder in Suffolk, a straightforward matter, they'd thought, until it wasn't.

So when Cordelia Loxley's letter arrived — *eleven years, no body, no verdict, no settlement* — Marguerite had read it three times and set it aside twice, and on the third reading had noticed something that changed the arithmetic entirely.

*They found her diary,* Cordelia had written. *In the crypt. Locked since the night she vanished. The last entry is dated three years after she disappeared.*

Marguerite did not believe in impossible documents. They believed in misfiled ones, forged ones, misdated ones, wished-for ones. But every one of those categories had an author, and every author left a seam.

A document that could not be true but existed anyway was not a dead end. It was a door.

That was why they had come. Not out of hope for Cordelia's inheritance, and not — whatever they might tell themselves later — out of any debt to Hester Vane. They had come because somewhere under St. Aldhelm's Cathedral was a piece of paper that lied about time itself, and Marguerite Dravenmoor had spent their whole life learning how paper lied.

* * *

The crypt smelled of cold stone and candle grease, the way crypts are supposed to smell, and beneath that something fainter — dust disturbed recently, and often. Marguerite noted it without comment while Cordelia led them down the stair with a lantern held too high, its light jumping along the vault ribs like a nervous animal.

"She was found here." Cordelia stopped before a recessed alcove behind the altar of the lower chapel, where a saint's reliquary — gilded, heavy, thirteenth-century by the tooling — sat on a stone plinth. "Wedged behind that. The sexton found it when they finally opened the room for the quinquennial survey. Two years ago now. The door had been locked since the night she went missing. The key was in the verger's keeping."

"And no one thought to look inside for eleven years?"

"It's a storeroom, mostly. Broken chairs, old flower stands." Cordelia's laugh came out wrong. "Who searches a locked cupboard for a living woman?"

Marguerite crouched by the plinth and did not touch the reliquary. Behind it, the wall bore a pale rectangle in the dust — the diary's ghost, still printed there even after the book had been removed. They measured it by eye. Octavo. Laid flat, spine outward. Placed deliberately, not dropped; a dropped book falls spine-in or pages-splayed, but this had been slid into the gap with care, the way one files a thing one means to be found.

Or means to be found *eventually*. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and Marguerite would learn it soon enough.

"The diary is with the solicitors?" they asked.

"A copy. The original is in the parish archive. Mr. Pell keeps it." Cordelia hesitated. "Mr. Pell — the verger. He's been here since before Aunt Verity vanished. He knew her better than anyone. He's been very helpful. Almost too—"

She caught herself again, and Marguerite filed the truncation away beside the disturbed dust. People truncated precisely where the truth began.

"Almost too helpful," Marguerite finished for her, mildly. "Go on."

"He quotes her. At the memorial dinners. He remembers things about her that I don't remember, and she was *my aunt*. Sometimes I think he loved her." Cordelia's voice flattened. "Sometimes I think that's the whole problem with this parish. Everyone loved her, and no one looked for her."

Marguerite straightened, knees cracking in the cold, and looked up at the vaulted dark above the chapel altar. Somewhere overhead, muffled by six feet of stone, the cathedral choir had begun evensong, and the sound reached the crypt as vibration rather than music — a pulse in the bones of the building.

"Terms," Marguerite said. "I work alone. I ask questions that will offend people, including you. I will tell you what I find or I will tell you honestly that I have found nothing, but I will not tell you what you wish to hear. And if I take this case, I will close it. One way or another, the estate settles, the diary explains itself, and Verity Loxley stops being a question this parish asks itself every November. Do you understand what you're buying?"

Cordelia looked at them for a long moment. In the lantern light her face did something complicated — relief and fear occupying the same features, the way they do when a person has wanted a thing for so long that getting it feels like danger.

"I understand I've got nothing else," she said.

"That's usually why people hire me." Marguerite extended a hand. "Then we're agreed. I'll want lodging in the close, access to the parish archive, and a list of everyone who had a key to this room in the last eleven years."

"The key list is short. Mr. Pell. The late dean. And—" Cordelia counted on her fingers, an oddly childlike gesture in the gloom. "That may be it. That may genuinely be it."

*A short list,* Marguerite thought, descending back toward the sound of the choir. *Short lists are either blessings or accusations.*

* * *

That evening, in the guest lodgings overlooking the cloister garth, Marguerite unpacked with the ritual economy of twenty years on the road: magnifier, calipers, the good lamp, the bad lamp for situations requiring humility. From the bottom of the case they took a small tin box, and from the tin box a photograph gone soft at the corners — a boy of twelve on a canal bank, squinting into sun he would not live to see again.

They did not pray over it. They were not certain prayer took. But they set it facing the window, where the floodlit tower of St. Aldhelm's rose against the rain, and they let it watch them work, which was its own kind of vigil.

The copy of the diary arrived at nine, carried up by a porter with instructions from Mr. Pell — *he hopes it finds you well, miss, and begs you treat it gently, as it is the last voice we have of her.*

*The last voice we have of her.* Marguerite turned the phrase over. It was a strange thing to say about a book full of writing that could not all be hers.

They waited until the porter's footsteps faded, then cleared the desk, laid a white cloth down, washed their hands, and opened the diary to the first page with the tenderness they reserved for things that had been waiting a long time to speak.

Verity Loxley's hand was beautiful — copperplate disciplined into near-print, the hand of a woman who had been taught penmanship by someone unforgiving. The early entries were domestic and dry: accounts, visitors, the price of candles, a running grievance about the organist's tempo. A life in ledger form. Marguerite read quickly, cataloguing, letting the rhythm of the hand settle into memory the way a musician lets a theme settle before variation begins.

Outside, the rain thickened. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, in the cloister, footsteps crossed the garth — slow, deliberate, pausing once beneath Marguerite's window — and moved on.

They reached the final dated entry at half past midnight.

Three years after the disappearance. The handwriting continued past it, page after page, growing gradually less sure of itself — the letters leaning, the ink pooling where the pen had lingered too long, as though written by a hand that was tired, or old, or afraid.

Marguerite sat back. On the windowsill, the boy in the photograph squinted into his permanent sun, and Marguerite looked at him and then at the diary and felt the old corridor open ahead of them — long, dim, and, for the first time in nineteen years, with light at the far end.

*Never take a case you cannot close.*

They picked up the pen, drew a fresh card from the box, and wrote the first line of the working file:

*Day 1. The dead woman writes on. Find out who holds the pen.*

Below the window, in the dark of the garth, the footsteps passed again — going the other way now, unhurried, as if their owner had all the time in the world and intended to use all of it.
