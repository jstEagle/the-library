## 3. Entries After Death

The reading room of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral archive occupied the north transept's upper gallery, a place where the light came down in long amber columns through glass that had not been cleaned since the war. Marguerite Dravenmoor had claimed the table beneath the east window on their first morning and had not surrendered it since. They worked the way they did everything: with instruments laid out in strict order — calipers, a raking lamp, cotton gloves so thin they felt like absence, a linen tape for measuring paper, and a notebook whose pages were gridded into columns no one else could read at a glance.

Verity Loxley's diary sat before them under the lamp.

It was a modest object. Bound in oxblood leather gone soft as chamois at the corners, its brass clasp long since broken, the whole thing smelling faintly of stone and candle smoke and something older underneath — the mineral breath of the crypt itself. Eleven years wedged behind a reliquary of Saint Aldhelm's fingerbone had done less damage to it than a decade in a damp attic would have. The crypt kept its own climate. The crypt kept its own secrets.

Marguerite opened to the first page and began.

The method was simple and merciless. One entry per line in the notebook: date, page number, ink classification, hand assessment, paper stock, any anomaly. They had catalogued four hundred diaries in their career, three hundred and eleven of them posthumous, and the work had a rhythm like tide — out with attention, in with understanding. By noon they had reached the second year. By two o'clock, the fourth.

And by half past two, Marguerite Dravenmoor stopped writing altogether and simply looked at what lay open beneath the lamp.

* * *

The facts were these, and they arranged themselves in Marguerite's mind with the terrible neatness of a proof completing itself:

Verity Loxley vanished on the night of the fourteenth of March, eleven years ago. This was not disputed. The police file — which Marguerite had read twice through, once for content and once for texture — recorded her last confirmed sighting at the cathedral's evensong, her coat found folded on a bench in the cloister walk, her house locked from within, her bank account untouched ever after. A woman who stepped out of her life between one hymn and the next.

The diary's entries continued for three years beyond that date.

Not sporadically. Not as a few stray lines someone might have added later in a fit of sentiment or fraud. They continued weekly, then monthly, then in the slow faltering rhythm of a person growing tired — forty-one further entries, each dated, each written in a hand that was recognizably, unmistakably Verity Loxley's, and each one shakier than the last.

Marguerite turned back to the beginning to compare.

The early hand was confident, upright, a schoolroom italic disciplined by decades of keeping accounts. Verity had been the parish treasurer for nineteen years; her numbers marched across ledgers like soldiers on parade. The letters in the first volume of the diary were small and even, the crossbars level, the descenders precise.

By the entries written *after* the disappearance — after, Marguerite thought again, feeling the word turn over like a stone in the stomach — the hand had begun to lean. The uprights tilted five degrees eastward. Then eight. The ink grew wetter where the pen lingered too long; there were places where a letter had been begun twice, the nib hesitating on the page until the paper drank a small dark bloom. In the final entries, written in a brownish iron-gall mix different from the earlier blue-black, the words themselves began to fail — a *t* left uncrossed, an *h* that became something between an *n* and a wound.

Forgery could imitate a hand. Marguerite had seen forgeries good enough to fool auction houses. But forgery imitates competence; it reproduces the strong version of a script, because the forger is working from exemplars and exemplars are always the best of what survives. What no forger would think to reproduce — what no forger *could* reproduce without living inside another body for three years — was decay. The slow collapse of fine motor control. The way a hand forgets itself from the inside.

Unless the forger was very clever indeed, and had understood that the truth of a life is written in its deterioration.

Marguerite sat back. The amber column of light had moved; dust drifted through it like slow snow. Somewhere below, the cathedral organist was running scales, the notes rising through the floorboards as if the building itself were humming.

"Impossible," Marguerite said aloud, to no one, because some findings required saying.

But it was not impossible. It was documented. It was dated, paginated, and now catalogued in a grid of Marguerite's own devising, forty-one entries marching toward a final line dated the ninth of January, seven years ago, which read in full:

*He thinks I have forgotten. Let him keep thinking it. The dead keep vigil better than the living.*

Then nothing. The remaining pages blank.

Marguerite read it four times. Then they took up their pen and wrote, in the anomaly column, a single word: *who?*

* * *

"You'll want the incident photographs for comparison."

The voice arrived without footsteps. Marguerite had not heard the door — had not, in fact, registered that anyone had entered the gallery at all — and yet Mr. Pell stood at the head of the table with a folder under his arm, a slight man in his sixties dressed in the verger's sombre grey, his white hair combed with a precision that suggested devotion rather than vanity. He had a face built for sympathy: soft around the eyes, quick to crease at the corners, the face of a man who had spent a lifetime standing beside grieving people and learning exactly how long to be silent.

"The photographs," he repeated gently, when Marguerite didn't answer. "From the original search. Of the cloister bench. You'd want them if you're establishing a timeline."

"I haven't established that I'm making a timeline."

"No." Pell smiled — apologetic, self-deprecating, the smile of a man aware he has overstepped and asking pardon for the habit of helpfulness. "Forgive me. I've spent thirty years in this archive. I know where everything is, and I've never quite learned that other people prefer to find things themselves." He set the folder down anyway, at the table's edge, equidistant between them, and did not push it closer. "Page fourteen has the bench. Page twenty-two, the coat. There's a discrepancy between the constable's note and the photograph regarding which side the buttons faced — the note says left, the photograph says right — that nobody ever resolved."

Marguerite looked at him.

It was a long look, and Marguerite let it be long, because watching what a man does under a sustained gaze tells you more than anything he says. Pell bore it well. Too well, perhaps. Most people fidgeted by the third second. Pell simply waited, hands folded over the folder, patient as furniture, his eyes kind and still and giving back nothing but a mild professional concern.

"That's a very specific detail," Marguerite said. "Button-side."

"It's my business to know the files." He said it lightly, the way one apologizes for knowing the names of all the stones in the churchyard. "When a family comes asking about a missing person, they don't want to wait while I look things up. They want answers while the grief is still hot. So I learned them. All of them. Every case this parish has ever opened." A pause, and then, softly, with genuine warmth: "Especially hers. Verity Loxley was the best of us. Whatever I can do to help you close her account, Mr. — Ms. — forgive me, I don't actually know how you prefer—"

"Dravenmoor is sufficient."

"Dravenmoor, then." He inclined his head. "Whatever I can do. I mean that. This parish remembers its dead, and she deserves to be remembered properly."

He left the folder and went, and this time Marguerite listened for his footsteps and heard almost none of them — just the faintest pressure of shoes on old boards, fading down the stair, a man who moved through his cathedral the way water moves through limestone: everywhere, invisibly, wearing grooves.

Marguerite looked at the folder for a long moment. Then they put on fresh gloves, opened it to page fourteen, and noted in the margin of their own notebook, beneath the word *who*, a second word: *him.*

* * *

That evening Marguerite walked the cloister.

It was habit rather than deduction. When a document troubled them, Marguerite walked, and the cloister was the nearest thing St. Aldhelm's had to a thinking-corridor — a square of covered walkway around a garden gone pleasantly wild, the pillars worn smooth on their inner faces by centuries of shoulders leaning exactly there. The evening light came low and gold across the grass. A blackbird was conducting its territorial business in the hawthorn. On the bench where Verity Loxley's coat had been found folded, an elderly woman sat feeding crumbs to pigeons with the serene entitlement of the very old.

Marguerite sat at the far end of the bench, uninvited but tolerated, and opened the notebook to the timeline.

Here was the shape of the thing, drawn now in columns and arrows. Verity disappears on the fourteenth of March. The diary continues. The hand decays believably, progressively, across three years — which meant either that Verity lived secretly somewhere, writing by lamplight in a hiding place no search ever found, her strength failing, her messages growing stranger; or that someone else wrote those entries, someone with a forger's patience and a scholar's understanding of how hands die.

And if the latter — why?

To fake a survival? To suggest the missing woman was alive, and thereby stop the search from becoming a murder inquiry? That had a cold logic to it. A body was never found. Without a body, without evidence of violence, the case had settled into that particular English purgatory: the open file, the annual anniversary, the memorial plaque. Someone had wanted it to settle exactly there.

Or — and Marguerite turned the thought over slowly, feeling its weight — the entries were real in intent if not in flesh. A message maintained. A signal kept lit. *I am not forgotten. I am not finished.* The final line came back to Marguerite with its strange, deliberate grammar: *the dead keep vigil better than the living.*

Vigil against what? Over what?

Marguerite closed the notebook. Across the grass, the old woman had run out of crumbs and the pigeons had dispersed into the hawthorn to complain. The blackbird had won. Marguerite stood, nodded to the woman, and walked the rest of the arcade, counting pillars out of idle discipline — and stopped at the eleventh.

There, cut small into the stone at shoulder height, worn nearly to a shadow by weather and touching hands, was a mark. A circle with a vertical stroke through it. Marguerite had noticed it on their first day and filed it among the parish's thousand anonymous scratches — masons' marks, pilgrims' crosses, the graffiti of the devout and the bored. But now, taking out a pencil and copying it precisely into the back of the notebook, Marguerite saw what they should have seen immediately.

The same mark appeared in the diary's margins. Faintly. Repeatedly. In the later entries especially, pressed so lightly the nib had barely kissed the paper, as though the writer feared even the ink might betray them — a little circled stroke beside certain dates, certain sums, certain names, like a flag planted on a map.

Marguerite stood in the gold light with the notebook open and felt the pleasure of pattern arriving, and beneath the pleasure, for the first time in this case, something colder. Because a mark like that was not decoration. A mark like that was a key waiting for its lock. And whoever had written those trembling entries — whether Verity Loxley alive behind some wall, or a killer curating her ghost — had hidden a message in plain sight and trusted only one reader to find it.

"Dravenmoor."

Marguerite turned. Cordelia Loxley stood at the cloister entrance in a raincoat despite the dry sky, holding a paper bag of chips gone cold, looking like a woman who had walked here on impulse and regretted every step. She was perhaps fifty, with Verity's jaw and none of Verity's composure, and she glanced at the bench, at the pigeons, at the mark on the pillar, with the flinching familiarity of someone returning to a house where something bad had happened.

"You're still here," she said. "Working."

"I'm still here."

"Is it — do you know yet?" She came closer, lowered her voice as though the pillars might repeat it. "Aunt Verity. Whether she—" She couldn't finish it. She never could. In their three meetings she had approached the sentence from four directions and retreated from all of them.

"I know the diary continues past the date she disappeared," Marguerite said. "In a hand that decays convincingly. And I know someone in this building knows more about the files than the files know about themselves."

Cordelia blinked. "Mr. Pell?"

"He's been helpful."

"They're all helpful here." Something flickered across her face — not suspicion exactly, but its cousin. "That's the thing about this parish, Dravenmoor. Everyone helps. Everyone remembers. My aunt used to say they remembered too much, some of them. That some people here kept accounts the way other people keep grudges." She hugged the chip bag to her chest like a shield. "She said it laughing. But she stopped coming to committee meetings after that. And then she stopped coming at all."

"When was that?"

"Six weeks before she vanished." Cordelia looked at the pillar, at the small worn mark, and Marguerite watched her eyes pass over it without seeing it — eleven years of blindness, or eleven years of choosing not to see. "Why are you looking at that stone?"

"Habit. Archivists collect marks the way magpies collect foil."

It was a lie, and a smooth one, and Marguerite noted its smoothness with mild self-disgust. The case was already teaching them to withhold. That was how it always began — the small economies of truth, each one reasonable, each one a coin saved toward a reckoning.

Cordelia nodded vaguely, thanked them for nothing in particular, and went. Her footsteps crossed the grass and faded, and the blackbird resumed its song, and Marguerite stood alone in the colonnade with the copied mark burning a small hole in the back of the notebook.

* * *

They returned to the reading room as the light failed, and worked by lamp.

The plan was methodical: transcribe every marginal mark, log its position against the text, hunt for repetition, frequency, structure. Cipher work was patience wearing gloves. Marguerite had broken three private ciphers in their career and each had yielded not to brilliance but to boredom — to the willingness to count the same symbols ten thousand times until the counts confessed.

They were an hour into the transcription when the lamp flickered.

Not the bulb — the flame of the candle Marguerite kept lit beside the lamp, an affectation borrowed from monastic practice, useful for testing drafts of air. The flame leaned hard toward the stairwell door, held, and righted itself. Someone had opened the door below and closed it quickly. Someone who did not wish to announce themselves.

Marguerite did not turn around. They kept writing, transcribing a mark from page 212 — the circled stroke beside the name *Hollis*, one of the trust's trustees, dead nine years now of a heart attack the coroner called unremarkable — and listened.

Footsteps on the stair. Slow. Unhurried. The tread of a man who belonged here and knew it.

"Late hours," Mr. Pell said warmly, appearing at the edge of the lamplight with a tray. Tea, two cups, a small plate of biscuits arranged with geometric care. "I saw your light from the vestry. Nobody works this room after six. Not in eleven years." He set the tray down at a respectful distance from the documents. "Milk's separate. I didn't know how you take it."

"Thank you."

"Page 212." He said it casually, pouring tea, not looking at the notebook. "You're in the spring of year six. That's when the entries start thinning out. Once a fortnight, then once a month. The last one's the ninth of January." He smiled at the cup, at the steam. "People always find that entry the most affecting. Even the police inspector wept, and he was not a weeping sort of man."

Marguerite's pen had stopped moving. They made it start again, finishing the transcription with a steady hand, and only then looked up.

"I hadn't told anyone which page I was on."

Pell paused, teapot suspended, and for one unmeasurable instant his face did something Marguerite would spend many nights afterward trying to classify. Not guilt. Not fear. Something more like a pianist striking a wrong note and hearing it hang in the hall — a flicker of pure professional dismay, the horror of the craftsman who has erred in front of an audience.

Then it was gone, smoothed away like a wrinkle passed over by a warm hand, and he laughed softly at himself.

"So you hadn't. Forgive me — I saw the ribbon marker from the doorway yesterday, when I brought the photographs folder up. Position of the bookmark, you understand. Old habits." He set the pot down. "I'll leave you to it. Do ring the vestry if you need anything at all. Anything. I sleep badly, and the cathedral sleeps worse."

He went. His footsteps descended the stair, soft as snowfall, and the candle flame stood perfectly straight, and Marguerite sat motionless in the pool of lamplight with the tea cooling untouched and the truth assembling itself one quiet detail at a time.

The ribbon marker. Marguerite looked at the diary. The ribbon lay at page 212, exactly where it had lain when Marguerite opened the book that morning — because Marguerite did not use the ribbon. Marguerite used slips of acid-free tissue. The ribbon had not moved since the day the diary was found in the crypt.

Which meant Mr. Pell had not seen it from a doorway yesterday.

Which meant Mr. Pell knew the diary's contents — knew them intimately, knew the rhythm of the entries, knew which page held the affecting final line, knew the exact cadence of Verity Loxley's decline — from somewhere other than this table.

Marguerite blew out the candle, closed the notebook, and sat for a long time in the dark with the amber column gone and only the distant red glow of the sanctuary lamp below, like a pulse in the body of the building.

Then they took up their pen and wrote, beneath *who* and *him*, a third word, small and cold:

*watches.*

Outside, across the close, a light burned in the vestry window long past midnight. Marguerite noted the hour, noted the light, and turned back to the margins — to the little circled strokes, the flags on the map, the cipher waiting for its key — because whatever Verity Loxley had hidden, and whoever had kept her diary breathing for three years after her death, the answer was not going to be found in the files.

It was going to be found in the ledger.
