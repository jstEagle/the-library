## 4. The Benefactor's Ledger

The parish archive of St. Aldhelm's occupied a narrow gallery above the north transept, reached by a spiral stair that smelled of cold iron and pigeon droppings. Marguerite Dravenmoor climbed it at half past eight on a Tuesday morning, carrying a thermos of black coffee, a box of cotton gloves, and the particular hunger that had kept them in this profession for twenty-two years — the appetite for paper that lied.

Archives never lied outright. That was the first thing they had learned, back when they were still young enough to believe archives were honest. Archives simply arranged themselves around the truth like water around a stone, and if you wanted to find the stone, you had to learn to read the current.

The Loxley Trust files occupied four shelf-boxes in the benefactors' section, wedged between the Harrowgate Memorial Fund and a crumbling run of vestry minutes from the 1970s. Marguerite pulled the first box down and set it on the reading table beneath the gallery's single window, where the morning light came through glass so old it turned everything the color of weak tea.

Eleven years of records. Annual reports, bank statements, correspondence with the diocese, receipts for stained-glass restoration, roof repairs, the new boiler that had been installed the year after Verity vanished and had failed within three. Marguerite worked the way they always worked: chronologically first, then thematically, then — when the shape began to emerge — forensically.

The shape took until noon to emerge.

It emerged the way shapes always did in Marguerite's experience: not as a smoking gun but as a small, persistent wrongness, the kind of thing a casual reader would skim past a hundred times without snagging. The Loxley Trust received donations — that was its purpose. Verity Loxley had endowed it with a substantial principal in 2004, and parishioners had added to it over the years, some in sums large enough to warrant acknowledgment letters. The trust then disbursed funds to approved purposes: fabric repairs, the music fund, the poor box, the school.

Straightforward. Except.

Marguerite laid six annual reports side by side across the table, 2010 through 2015, and drew a line with their finger down the column of disbursements. In each year, there was a line item labeled only *Administrative — external review*. No vendor named. No invoice attached. No purpose specified beyond those two words. And in each year, the amount was not trivial: four hundred pounds, then six hundred, then nine hundred, climbing steadily, until by 2014 the *external review* line exceeded what the trust paid for actual repairs to actual windows.

Marguerite turned to the bank statements.

The payments went to an account at a building society in Taunton. An account name, not a company name: *R. A. Fennimore, consultant.* Marguerite wrote the name on an index card and stared at it. There was no R. A. Fennimore anywhere else in the file. No engagement letter, no contract, no report delivered, no letter of thanks. A consultant who consulted invisibly, for eleven consecutive invoices, and whose fees rose every year like a tide coming in.

And every cheque — Marguerite checked this twice, then a third time, because the third time was where the stone under the water finally showed itself — every cheque bore two signatures. That was proper; trusts required dual authorization. The first signature was consistent throughout: a firm, upright hand belonging to Aldous Crane, the parish treasurer, whose signature appeared on hundreds of documents in the archive and could have been identified by any bank clerk in Somerset.

The second signature changed.

From 2010 to 2012, it belonged to Verity Loxley herself — fluid, slanted, unmistakable once you'd spent three days with her diary. Then, beginning in March 2013, seven months after she disappeared, the second signature became someone else's: *Edmund Gorse*, trustee. His hand was cramped and careful, the writing of a man who had learned penmanship in a strict school and never relaxed since.

Marguerite sat back and let the arithmetic settle.

Verity disappeared in August 2012. Her signature continued on trust cheques through February 2013 — Marguerite found three more, dated after the disappearance, which matched the diary problem exactly and would need to be dealt with separately. Then Edmund Gorse signed in her place. Fine. Trustees died or resigned; replacements were appointed. But there was no appointment record. No vestry minute electing Gorse to the trust. No resignation letter from Verity. No letter from her estate's executors transferring her trusteeship. The file simply skipped, the way a film skips, one frame missing, and everyone watching politely pretended not to notice.

And Edmund Gorse himself — Marguerite pulled the burial registers, because death was the archivist's oldest index — Edmund Gorse had died in November 2013. Fifteen months after Verity vanished. Fifteen months after he'd begun signing cheques to R. A. Fennimore. He was buried in the extension of the churchyard, plot 214, beneath a modest stone that read *Faithful Servant of This Parish*.

After Gorse's death, the second signature on the Fennimore cheques became — Marguerite squinted at the photocopy, tilted it toward the window — became something worse than a forgery. It became a performance of Edmund Gorse. The same cramped letters, the same careful loops, reproduced year after year on cheques written by a dead man's hand, authorizing payments to a man who did not exist, from a trust that had been built by a woman who could not object.

Marguerite poured the last of the coffee, gone cold, and drank it anyway.

The total, summed across eleven years, came to just under nineteen thousand pounds. Not a fortune. Not enough to kill for, a cynic might say — and that, Marguerite thought, was precisely the elegance of it. Nineteen thousand pounds was too small to attract forensic auditors, too small to interest the Charity Commission beyond a form letter, too small to make anyone in the parish want to look. It was theft calibrated to the exact threshold of collective indifference. Whoever had designed it understood something Marguerite had learned long ago: that the perfect crime was not the one nobody saw, but the one nobody could be bothered to see.

But the money was almost certainly not the point. Money like this was a symptom, a trail of breadcrumbs leading somewhere larger. Marguerite turned back through the boxes looking for the body of the trust itself — the founding deed, the schedule of assets, the inventory of what Verity had actually given.

The founding deed was there, dated 2004, and it named the property: not cash alone. Verity Loxley had conveyed to the trust, among other holdings, *the freehold of Loxley Cottage and appurtenant lands, together with all rights of way, timber, and mineral interests therein.*

Mineral interests.

Marguerite read the phrase four times. A cottage, yes — Cordelia had mentioned it, the house nobody lived in, shuttered since the disappearance. But mineral interests, in a corner of Somerset where the old maps showed a seam of blue lias and, further east, the ghost-works of a quarrying concern that had gone bankrupt in the nineties? Marguerite pulled the tithe maps, the geological survey sheets, the planning correspondence. And there it was, in a letter from 2009, from a firm of land agents in Bristol, addressed to *Mrs. V. Loxley, c/o St. Aldhelm's Parish Office*: an inquiry regarding test drilling on the eastern field, an indication of commercial aggregate deposits, and a preliminary valuation that made Marguerite's eyebrows climb.

The valuation was redacted. Not crossed out — redacted, with a heavy black marker applied sometime after filing, by a hand that had wanted the number forgotten. But the agent's covering sentence survived: *"...a figure which we trust will be of considerable interest to Mrs. Loxley and her fellow trustees."*

Considerable interest. Marguerite looked out the gallery window, down into the transept where a verger was moving through his morning rounds below, small and dutiful as an ant on a cathedral floor, straightening chairs that did not need straightening.

Someone had known. Someone had known in 2009 what the eastern field was worth, and by 2013 that same someone was signing a dead man's name to cheques and bleeding the trust dry in increments too small to notice. And Verity Loxley — meticulous, watchful Verity, who coded her fears into hymn numbers in the margins of her own diary — Verity had been asking questions about her own trust in the last months before she walked out of her life and into a locked crypt.

Marguerite closed the box, replaced it on the shelf, and stood for a moment with their hand flat against the cardboard, feeling the faint vibration of the bells being tested far above.

The stone under the water was bigger than they'd thought. And somebody had spent eleven years making sure the water stayed stirred.

* * *

Cordelia Loxley had learned to hate Tuesdays, because Tuesdays were when the solicitors' office in Wells sent its letters, and the letters were always the same letter wearing different dates. *We are unable to progress the administration of the estate pending resolution of matters relating to the Loxley Trust.* Eleven years of that sentence. She could recite it in her sleep, and sometimes suspected she did.

She sat in her kitchen in Bristol with the newest letter unopened beside the marmalade, because opening it felt like agreeing to it, and instead watched the rain write its illegible script down the window.

Aunt Verity had promised her nothing. That was the strange part, the part Cordelia couldn't explain to the solicitors or to her sister or to herself at three in the morning. There had never been a conversation about inheritance, never a will waved meaningfully over Sunday lunch. What there had been was a September afternoon when Cordelia was nineteen, and Aunt Verity had taken her walking along the eastern field with the wind flattening the grass in silver combers, and had stopped at the boundary stone and said, in her dry, amused voice: *"If anything ever happens to me, Cordelia — anything at all — don't let them tell you it was simple."*

Cordelia had laughed. *Anything happens to you? You'll outlive the cathedral.*

*"Promise me anyway."*

She had promised, the way you promise children's promises, lightly, and then Aunt Verity had vanished fourteen months later, and the promise had hardened overnight into something with weight and edges. Because the police said she'd likely left of her own accord — a woman of independent means, some unhappiness perhaps, a middle-aged crisis — and the parish said it gently, sorrowfully, and the solicitors said it in writing, and everyone agreed with such smooth unanimity that Cordelia had sometimes wondered whether she was the only person alive who remembered how much her aunt had loved St. Aldhelm's. You didn't walk away from a place you loved like that. You didn't leave your garden, your hens, your pew on the north side, third row, where the light came through the Saint Aldhelm window in the late afternoon and laid a ribbon of ruby across the hymn books.

You especially didn't leave a diary behind. Her aunt had written in that diary every day of her life since girlhood; Cordelia had seen the shelf of them, forty years of cloth-bound volumes, each inscribed with the year. And the last one — the one found in the crypt — had entries in it dated after she disappeared. Cordelia had read about that in the newspaper coverage and had felt something turn over in her chest that she'd never managed to name. Either her aunt was alive and hiding, which meant she had chosen to abandon Cordelia specifically, having extracted a promise first — or someone else was writing in her diary, which was unthinkable, which was worse than death, which was desecration of a kind Cordelia had no vocabulary for.

So she had hired the archivist. Dravenmoor. A strange, precise person with ink-stained fingers and eyes that catalogued you while shaking your hand. Cordelia had told them a version of the truth: that she wanted closure, that the estate couldn't settle, that the uncertainty was eating her alive. All true. But underneath the truth, in the cellar of it, was the other thing she hadn't said aloud: that she needed the money. That her marriage had ended in a settlement that favored neither party, that her job had ended in a restructuring that favored no one, that she was renting a flat above a nail salon and lying to her sister about how well things were going. That Aunt Verity's promise — *don't let them tell you it was simple* — had become, over eleven lean years, tangled hopelessly with the practical, humiliating fact that Cordelia needed the estate to settle the way a drowning woman needs air.

And now Dravenmoor had telephoned, that morning, in a voice carefully stripped of everything, and asked her one question:

*"Tell me about the eastern field."*

Cordelia had stood in her kitchen holding the phone and felt the September wind come back across eleven years, flattening the grass in silver combers, and heard her aunt's voice: *don't let them tell you it was simple.*

"It was just a field," she said. "She used to walk there. Why?"

There was a pause on the line, the particular pause of a person deciding how much truth a witness can carry.

"Because," Dravenmoor said, "someone has been paying themselves out of your aunt's trust using the signature of a man who has been dead for eleven years. And because I think the field is why your aunt is in that crypt."

Cordelia sat down slowly on a kitchen chair that wobbled, that had always wobbled, that she'd never fixed because fixing things required believing in a future.

"There's something else," Dravenmoor went on. "I'm sending you photographs of certain documents tonight. When they arrive, don't open them where anyone can see. Don't discuss them on the telephone. And Cordelia—" the archivist's voice dropped into a register she hadn't heard from them before, quiet and level as a plumb line— "has anyone ever contacted you about the trust? Anyone at all. A letter, a visit, a phone call. Think carefully. It may matter more than you know."

"No," Cordelia started to say, and then stopped, because the word snagged on something.

On a note. Years ago — five, six years ago, shortly after she'd first begun pressing the solicitors — a plain envelope, no return address, containing a single sheet on which someone had written, in handwriting she had recognized instantly and refused to recognize for a week afterward, because recognizing it meant believing impossible things:

*V.L.*

Two initials. Nothing else. She had kept it taped inside a book on a high shelf, telling herself it was a cruel joke, a coincidence, a trick of similarity — her aunt's distinctive hand, the sharp-tailed V, the L with its little serif foot, reproduced by chance or malice by some stranger who knew the family history.

She had never shown it to anyone. Not the police, not the solicitors, not her sister. Because showing it meant saying aloud that either her aunt was alive and choosing silence, or that someone was wearing her aunt's hand like a glove — and both possibilities were doors Cordelia could not afford to open in a flat above a nail salon, alone, at night.

"Dravenmoor," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "I need to tell you about a note."

Outside, the rain kept up its illegible script, and somewhere south of Bristol, in a village she had loved her whole life, a man was winding the church clocks and straightening chairs and keeping, with infinite tenderness, another woman's story exactly as he wanted it told.
