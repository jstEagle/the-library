## 7. The Anniversary Committee

The parish hall smelled of beeswax and old roses, the kind of scent that insists on reverence whether or not anyone present feels it. Marguerite arrived early — a habit, not a tactic — and stood a moment in the doorway of the Wycliffe Room, watching the anniversary committee assemble itself around Mr. Pell like iron filings around a magnet.

There were nine of them. Marguerite counted automatically, the way they counted everything: chairs, candles, hymnals, years. Eleven years since Verity Loxley had walked out of St. Aldhelm's on a rain-slicked October evening and dissolved into the particular silence that parishes reserve for their dead who are not yet dead enough to mourn properly.

"Dravenmoor." Pell's voice found them across the room, warm and unhurried. He was arranging place cards on the long trestle table, and his hands moved with the economy of a man who had performed this ritual so many times it had become a kind of prayer. "You've come to observe. Good. An archivist should see how memory is made, not merely stored."

"I came to see the guest list," Marguerite said.

"Then you've come to the right man." He lifted the seating chart — an enormous sheet of cream card, ruled in pencil, annotated in three colors of ink — and spread it before them like a surgeon exposing an organ. "Every soul who loved her. Every soul she loved. And every soul who owes her a debt they'd rather not discuss over soup."

Marguerite studied the chart. The handwriting was Pell's — of course it was Pell's; Marguerite had by now seen enough of it in the case files to recognize the way his letters leaned slightly forward, as if perpetually about to stand — but the *arrangement* was something else. This was architecture. The mayor and his wife at the head table, flanking the bishop's empty chair. Cordelia Loxley at the family table, isolated from the trustees by two full tables of ordinary parishioners, a buffer of harmless mourners. And at the table nearest the door, nearest the kitchen, nearest the exit — the two surviving trustees of the Loxley Trust, Mr. Harrow and Mr. Quill, seated with their backs to the room.

"You've put the trustees with their backs to the door," Marguerite observed.

Pell did not look up. "They prefer it. Men who handle money like to see whom they're speaking to, not who's arriving." He smoothed a place card flat with one thumb. "Besides, it's tradition. They've sat there every year since the first memorial. Mr. Harrow insists the sight of the kitchen door reminds him of Verity — she was forever sneaking in to help the cooks when the speeches went on too long."

It was a lovely detail. It was exactly the kind of detail that made a story breathe. And it was, Marguerite suspected, invented — or if not invented, then curated, polished, worn smooth by eleven years of retelling until no one could say anymore whether it had happened or merely been needed.

*He doesn't just remember everything,* Marguerite thought, watching him tilt the chart a degree to the left, correcting some imbalance only he could perceive. *He decides what everything means.*

* * *

The meeting began at seven with a prayer led by the chairman, a florid, well-meaning solicitor named Mr. Bexhill who had known Verity only in the way that men of committees know benefactors: as a signature, a sum, a name to raise a glass to. He spoke for six minutes. Marguerite timed him.

Then the machinery engaged, and Marguerite saw immediately why the anniversary dinner had survived eleven years without scandal, without drift, without the slow decay that kills most parish traditions. It survived because Mr. Pell would not permit it to decay. He was not the chairman. He was not, officially, anything at all — the verger's duties ended at the cathedral door, and this was the parish hall. But he had the minutes. He had the previous years' programs, bound and indexed. He had the correspondence with the florist, the caterer, the printer, going back a decade, each letter filed in its own folder in his own hand.

"The hymns," Bexhill said, consulting nothing. "Shall we keep the same as last year?"

"Number 341 and 218," Pell said, before the question had fully landed. "As we've done since the third anniversary. Verity requested 341 herself, in her last letter to the parish council — I have the letter, if anyone would like to see it. And 218 was chosen by the choir because it was her confirmation hymn." A pause, delicately measured. "Though I should note, for the record, that the choir's reasoning is oral tradition rather than documented. I've never found it in writing."

*He footnotes his own memories,* Marguerite thought. *He distinguishes the documented from the remembered, and he does it out loud, in front of everyone, so that no one else ever has to wonder.*

It was, Marguerite admitted, masterful. It was also a cage. Because once a community accepts one man as the keeper of its records — the man who knows which hymn was requested and which was merely assumed, the man who can produce the letter, the program, the seating chart from any given year — that community stops remembering for itself. Why should it? Remembering is labor. Pell had taken the labor on. And with it, he had taken the authority.

"Flowers?" Bexhill continued.

"Lilies and rosemary, as always. The lilies from Hollis & Sons, who gave the first arrangement free in the year she vanished and have refused payment ever since — I write them each February to renew the arrangement, and they write back each March. Rosemary for remembrance. Verity's own suggestion, from the same letter."

"Speeches?"

"Three. Yours, Chairman, no longer than eight minutes — you went eleven in year six and Mrs. Ashcombe's husband fell asleep audibly." A ripple of gentle laughter around the table; Bexhill flushed and laughed with it. Pell had delivered the rebuke as a joke, which made it land twice as hard and left no bruise that could be resented. "Then Cordelia Loxley, if she's willing — she spoke last year and it was very well received. And then the reading."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when everyone knows what's coming and wants to be the first to treat it lightly.

"The reading," Bexhill said. "Yes. Do we — that is, do we use the same passage again?"

"The same," Pell said. "Page 214 of her diary. The entry from the spring before she — " he did not falter; Marguerite watched closely and he did not falter — "from the spring before she left us. *'A parish is not its stones. It is the agreement between its living and its dead to go on keeping each other's accounts.'*" He recited it from memory, without looking down, without the smallest hesitation, and Marguerite felt the hair rise along their forearms.

Because Marguerite had the diary. The real one. It sat in a locked drawer in the archive, wrapped in acid-free tissue, and Marguerite had read every word of it three times. There was no entry on page 214 that said anything of the kind. There was an entry for the fourteenth of March, eleven years ago — a dull, domestic entry about a leaking roof and a dispute with the stonemason — and then the page had been torn out at the gutter, cleanly, by someone who knew exactly what they were doing.

The passage Pell had just recited did not exist. It had never existed. He had written it himself, or commissioned it, or assembled it from fragments, and for eleven years the parish of St. Aldhelm's had wept annually over words Verity Loxley had never set down.

And no one in the room could know that. No one except Marguerite — and the man who had made it.

Marguerite kept their face still. It was the oldest discipline of the archive: the face that reads without reacting, because a reaction is a record too, and records can be read.

"Beautiful," said Mrs. Ashcombe, dabbing her eyes. "Every year. Every single year it undoes me."

"It's meant to," said Pell, gently.

* * *

Afterward, while the committee dispersed into coats and umbrellas, Marguerite lingered by the seating chart. Pell was folding it with the care of a priest folding an altar cloth.

"May I?" Marguerite asked, and took the chart before he could answer — a small theft, deliberate, watching his hands as they surrendered it. His fingers did not tighten. His expression did not flicker. But there was a half-second, a single frame of stillness, in which the whole room seemed to hold its breath, and Marguerite catalogued it the way they catalogued everything: *Pell, resistance to touch of the chart, negligible but present. He does not like others handling the record.*

"Year one," Marguerite said, tracing the head table. "Where did Cordelia sit?"

"Third table, near the windows. She was twenty-three and would not come near the family table — she said it felt like sitting at a grave." He said it without looking at the chart. He didn't need to. "She moved to the family table in year four. She's been there since. She'll want the same seat this year; I've already spoken to her about it."

"You spoke to her?"

"I speak to everyone about their seats. It saves disputes on the night." He smiled. It was a kind smile. It was, Marguerite thought, a genuinely kind smile — and that was the worst of it, that the kindness appeared to be real. "People think a seating chart is furniture, Dravenmoor. It isn't. It's a sentence. You're telling every person in the room what they are to the story. Put the wrong man next to the wrong widow and you'll have a quarrel that outlives the dinner. Put them right, and they'll leave believing the evening was a gift." He held out his hand for the chart, and Marguerite returned it. "Verity understood that better than anyone. She used to say the parish was a story the living told on behalf of the dead, and that the only sin was to tell it badly."

"Did she," Marguerite said. "In the diary?"

"In conversation." Pell's eyes came up, and for a moment — one clean, unguarded moment — they were the eyes of a man standing at a door he did not intend to open. "You've been reading the diary, of course. For the estate."

"For the estate," Marguerite agreed.

"Then you'll know how much of it is ordinary. Roofs and masons and the price of candles. People imagine a diary is a confession. It rarely is. It's mostly weather." He folded the chart into its folder, squared the folder against the table's edge, and looked at Marguerite with that terrible, gentle patience. "I only say it because I'd hate for you to be disappointed. She was a woman, not a mystery. The parish remembers her better than any document could."

*The parish remembers what you permit it to remember,* Marguerite did not say. *And you have been writing its memory in three colors of ink for eleven years.*

"Good night, Mr. Pell."

"Good night, Dravenmoor. Do come on the night itself. Table six, by the pillar — I find archivists prefer a view of the whole room."

Marguerite walked out into the rain and did not feel the cold. Their mind was elsewhere: back in the archive drawer, back on page 214, back to the clean tear at the gutter of the binding.

He had quoted a page that did not exist, to a room full of people who had no way of knowing, and he had done it with the confidence of a man who had never once been contradicted — because he had memorized every file, every program, every letter, and had quietly removed or rewritten anything that might contradict him. He was not the keeper of the parish's memory. He was its author.

And if he had authored a passage of Verity's diary — a passage the whole parish held sacred — then the diary in Marguerite's drawer was not merely evidence.

It was the one manuscript he had never been able to finish editing.

Marguerite stopped under the lych-gate, rain threading through the ironwork, and looked back at the cathedral rising black against the sodium glow. Somewhere beneath it, behind the door that had been locked since the night Verity Loxley vanished, the crypt held whatever he had never found — or whatever he had found, and sealed away, and told himself he was protecting.

*Where would a woman who understood that memory is a story hide the one page that could not be rewritten?*

Marguerite turned up their collar and began, already, to plan a night visit to the crypt.
