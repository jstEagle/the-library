## 8. Where the Dead Keep Vigil

The cathedral at midnight was a different animal entirely.

By day, St. Aldhelm's performed itself — the tourists shuffling through with their folded maps, the choir rehearsing in the transept, Mr. Pell polishing brass until it sang. But after the last lock turned and the last lamp died, the building stopped performing and simply *was*: nine hundred years of stone breathing in the dark, holding its cold like a held note.

Marguerite Dravenmoor came down the north stair without a torch, counting steps by habit. Forty-one to the ambulatory. Twelve more to the crypt door. They had memorized the route on their second day, because an archivist who needed light to navigate their own case file was an archivist who hadn't done the work.

The key to the crypt was where Pell kept it — on the ring in the vestry, third hook from the left — except Marguerite had borrowed it that afternoon while he was arranging flowers for Sunday, and returned a duplicate in its place. The original sat now in their coat pocket, warm as a stolen thing.

*Which it is,* Marguerite admitted, letting themselves through the door. *You've become a burglar of your own archive.*

The crypt received them the way it received everything: without comment. The air tasted of stone dust and old candle fat and something faintly sweet — the lilies someone still left at the base of the Loxley memorial every year on the anniversary, though no one ever admitted to leaving them.

Marguerite had waited three days for this night. Three days of decoding marginalia by daylight, under the pleasant hovering shadow of Mr. Pell, who had taken to bringing them tea at eleven and again at three, and who asked, each time, in his mild way: *"Found anything more in her ladyship's diary?"*

Each time Marguerite had shown him something true but small. A cipher keyed to hymn numbers. A reference to "the one who watches." Nothing that named names.

Because the margins had finally given up their secret that morning, and what they gave up had sent Marguerite home to sleep badly and wake resolved.

*Evidence hidden where the dead keep vigil.* Verity Loxley's own words, decoded from a lattice of hymn references — 142, 87, 231 — that mapped, when plotted against the floor plan of the crypt, onto a single bay. The bay of the reliquaries.

And among those reliquaries, one saint above all others kept vigil over this parish: St. Aldhelm himself, whose gilded casket stood at the heart of the crypt, too heavy to move, too sacred to open.

Too sacred for anyone, perhaps, except the woman who had funded its restoration.

* * *

Marguerite worked by penlight, hooded with a strip of black cloth so the glow wouldn't leak up the stair. The reliquary casket sat on its plinth like a sleeping animal, gold leaf gone dull in the dark, the saints carved along its lid gazing down with expressions that had looked serene by daylight and looked, now, like patience.

"Forgive me," Marguerite murmured to them. "I know how this looks."

The casket's base was iron, older than the gilt. Marguerite ran their fingers along the join where lid met body and found what they were looking for on the third pass: a seam too clean, a line of solder newer than everything around it. Verity had paid for the restoration in 1998. She would have had access. She would have had craftsmen who took discreet payment and asked nothing.

The question was whether she'd had time to finish whatever she'd started before she vanished.

Marguerite took out the tools they'd bought that afternoon from the ironmonger on Cordwainer Street — a slim pry bar, a jeweler's screwdriver, a cloth to muffle sound. Their hands were steady. Their pulse was not.

*If you're wrong about this,* said the voice that sounded like their old mentor, Ruth Askeland, dead eleven years now and still the sharpest critic Marguerite owned, *you'll have desecrated a listed reliquary for nothing, and you'll deserve whatever comes.*

*I'm not wrong,* Marguerite told her. *She told us where to look. She spent three years writing in code in a diary no one was supposed to find. People don't do that unless the thing they're hiding is worth hiding.*

The screws fought. Two came free; the third was painted over, fused by decades of neglect, and Marguerite worked it in slow quarter-turns, listening past their own breath to the sounds of the cathedral overhead — the tick of cooling stone, the occasional sigh of wind in the tower, once, distantly, a door.

They froze.

Listened.

Nothing followed. Old buildings spoke; that was all. Marguerite made themselves believe it and went back to work, though some cold thread at the back of their mind noted the door-sound again, filed it, refused to discard it.

The fourth screw surrendered. The fifth. Marguerite slid the pry bar into the seam, wrapped their hand in the cloth, and lifted.

The false base came away lighter than it should have been — hollow beneath, a cavity maybe four inches deep, lined with lead sheeting against damp. And inside, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed string, lay a book.

Not the diary. Smaller. Squarer. A ledger, bound in green morocco gone stiff with age, and on its cover, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood:

*V.L. — Private Accounts.*

Marguerite did not touch it for a full ten seconds. They knelt there in the dark with their heart going like a trapped bird, because eleven years of professional discipline had taught them this exact moment — the moment before the evidence speaks — and taught them also that it never came twice. Whatever was in that book had been waiting since before Marguerite took this case. It could wait ten more seconds for them to be worthy of it.

Then they lifted it out, unwrapped the oilcloth with surgical care, and opened it under the hooded light.

* * *

Verity Loxley had been an accountant before she had ever been a benefactor. That much declared itself in the first pages — columns ruled true, figures set down in a hand so disciplined it might have been typeset. Marguerite read fast, then slower, then went back to the beginning and read again, because the shape of it assembled itself gradually and then all at once, the way a face emerges from a crowd.

The Loxley Trust had been bleeding for years before her disappearance. Donations earmarked for the restoration fund, the choristers' school, the alms houses — rerouted, in increments small enough to escape any single year's audit, through a secondary account under the signatures of two trustees: Harold Vane, who had died conveniently within eighteen months of the first irregularity, and—

Marguerite turned a page and found a name written not in the ledger's neat columns but scrawled across the margin in Verity's hand, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*E.P. knows. E.P. has known since March. He found the Vane signature pattern himself — clever boy. He is frightened, but he is honest. We will go to the diocese together after Michaelmas. I will not let him carry this alone.*

Marguerite's penlight trembled.

*E.P.*

Edmund Pell. Thirty-one years old the spring Verity vanished. Junior verger then, not yet the beloved institution he would become — the man who had memorized every case file, who quoted page numbers, who brought tea at eleven and three and asked, gently, endlessly, what Marguerite had found.

*He is frightened, but he is honest.*

Verity had believed that. Had staked everything on it. Had written it in ink and pressed it into paper with the force of certainty.

Marguerite read on, and the ledger's final entries told the rest in Verity's increasingly hurried hand — the diocese meeting postponed once, twice; a note that *someone has been in my study*; a last entry, dated nine days before her disappearance, that stopped Marguerite's breath:

*E.P. says we should wait no longer. He has copied the ledgers himself and hidden them. He says if anything happens to either of us, the copies will surface. He says he will protect me. God forgive me — I believe him. I have no one else to believe.*

Marguerite closed the ledger and sat back on their heels in the dark of the crypt, and the vertigo came up through the stone floor like water rising in a well.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not resolve. If Edmund Pell had been Verity's partner — her co-conspirator in exposure, her protector, the keeper of copied ledgers — then either he had failed her, or he had *been* the thing she needed protection from. And if the latter, then everything since had been performance: the hovering, the helpfulness, the encyclopedic memory of her case file. Not a man guarding a secret he didn't understand.

A man curating a story. Keeping the diary alive, entry after impossible entry, year after year — not to preserve the truth of Verity Loxley, but to preserve a *version* of her. The version in which he was the faithful friend. The version in which the mystery stayed safely mysterious, unsolvable, honored annually with candles and speeches and a dinner in her name.

*Where the dead keep vigil,* she had written. And all this time, Marguerite thought, the dead had kept vigil over him. Every saint in this crypt had been staring at the place where he hid the proof of what he was, and he had walked past them every day of his life and called it devotion.

Or —

Marguerite made themselves consider it, because rigor demanded it — *or he is exactly what she believed he was. Frightened, honest, and broken by her loss. Perhaps the diary entries after her disappearance are his grief-madness, his way of keeping her voice alive. Perhaps the copied ledgers surfaced long ago and were destroyed by others. Perhaps—*

But no. The sacristan's shifting memory. The file details quoted unprompted. The tea at eleven and three. The gentle warning that would come, Marguerite now understood, eventually — the way it always came from men who curated other people's stories.

And one more thing. The detail that settled it the way a keystone settles an arch.

Marguerite reopened the ledger to the final entry and held the penlight close. *He has copied the ledgers himself and hidden them.* Hidden them *where?* Verity hadn't known. But Marguerite knew the parish archive the way Pell claimed to know the case files, and there was exactly one place in St. Aldhelm's where a junior verger in 2002 could have concealed documents no auditor would ever think to search — a place Marguerite had catalogued on their fourth day and flagged, privately, as *anomalous: contents unverified.*

The deed boxes in the muniment room. Box 114, sealed, labeled in a young man's careful hand: *Loxley Restoration — Duplicate Specifications.* Filed, dated, and forgotten by everyone except the man who filed it.

Everyone except the man who had memorized every record in the building.

Marguerite wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth — they would not move it tonight; moving it tonight would tell him, if he was watching, that the vault had given up its secret — reset the false base, drove the screws home as best they could in the dark, and stood.

The saints gazed down. Somewhere above, the great clock began to strike one, and the sound fell through nine hundred years of stone into the crypt like coins dropped down a well.

Marguerite counted the strokes. Twelve. Then silence.

Then — faint, from somewhere in the body of the church above — the unmistakable sound of a door closing.

Not the wind. Not the building settling. A door, latched with intention, by a hand that knew which doors latched quietly.

Marguerite killed the penlight and stood motionless in the absolute dark, the ledger's weight ghosting against their chest where it wasn't, listening to their own heartbeat count out the seconds. One. Five. Ten.

Footsteps, far above, crossing the nave. Unhurried. Familiar in their rhythm — the measured tread of a man who had walked these floors for thirty years and knew every board that creaked.

Moving toward the crypt stair.

Marguerite had perhaps ninety seconds and two exits: the main stair, wide and lit and fatal, or the old bone-shaft passage behind the Vane memorial — barely shoulder-width, choked with rubble, unmapped on any plan younger than 1850. Marguerite had found it on their first week and dismissed it as a curiosity.

Tonight it was a lifeline.

They slipped into the dark behind the memorial, ledger clutched to their chest, and had just wedged themselves past the first fallen slab when the crypt door opened above and light spilled down the stair in a long yellow blade.

And a voice came with it — soft, warm, carrying in the stone silence the way voices do at midnight, pitched for whoever might be listening and for no one at all:

"Goodnight, my lady," said Edmund Pell. "Sleep well. I'll see you Sunday."

The footsteps descended. The light swung across the reliquary bay, paused — Marguerite felt it pass over the Vane memorial like a hand passing over their grave — and moved on, unhurried, toward the far end of the crypt where the oldest tombs stood.

Where the dead kept vigil.

Marguerite pressed deeper into the passage, into the cold and the dust and the dark, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, two things at once.

That tomorrow, before first light, they would open Box 114.

And that Edmund Pell had just come down here, at one in the morning, to say goodnight to a woman he had every reason to want forgotten — which meant he had come to check on something.

And Marguerite Dravenmoor had just left fingerprints on the only thing in this cathedral worth checking.
