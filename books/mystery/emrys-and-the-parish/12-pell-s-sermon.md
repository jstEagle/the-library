## 12. Pell's Sermon

The memorial address was scheduled for the eve of the anniversary dinner, a tradition Cordelia had explained with the weary patience of someone reciting liturgy she no longer believed in: *Verity's Vespers*, the parish called it, an evening service held every year on the Saturday before the dinner, when the congregation gathered not to mourn — mourning was considered unseemly after the first decade — but to *remember*. The distinction mattered to them. Marguerite could see it in the way the flower guild arranged white lilies rather than the funereal gladioli, in the way the order of service printed her name in gold rather than black.

*In Loving Remembrance of Verity Loxley, Benefactor of St. Aldhelm's, 1974–2011.*

Marguerite took a seat at the back of the nave, beneath the gallery, where the shadows pooled thickest and the acoustic carried oddly — a quirk of the cathedral's medieval geometry that meant they could hear everything while remaining, themselves, half-heard. An archivist's instinct. You learned early that the most valuable position in any room was the one where you could observe without being observed, and Mr. Pell, Marguerite suspected, knew every acoustic quirk of this building better than anyone alive.

He was standing at the lectern now, adjusting the microphone with small, precise movements, and even from forty yards away Marguerite could see the transformation. This was not the verger who hovered over research tables with his damp hands and his apologetic coughs. This was not the man who quoted file numbers like scripture. The man at the lectern stood the way the great east window stood — upright, luminous, inevitable.

The nave was nearly full. Three hundred souls, perhaps more. Old Mrs. Harkness from the flower guild sat in the front row, her hands folded on her handbag. The churchwardens occupied their customary pew. Cordelia sat among them, rigid as a fence post, wearing black despite the parish's preference for softer colors, and Marguerite noted that too — a small rebellion, or perhaps just grief finally finding its costume.

The organ faded. Pell waited for the silence to settle completely, the way a conductor waits, and then he began without notes.

"I first met Miss Loxley," he said, "on a Tuesday in October. I remember it was a Tuesday because the Tuesday volunteers were stacking hymnals, and one of them — young Thomas Reed, who has since emigrated to Australia, God keep him — dropped an entire cartload in the south aisle. A terrible clatter." A ripple of gentle laughter moved through the congregation. "And Miss Verity Loxley, who had come to discuss the restoration of the crypt windows, knelt down and began picking up hymnals alongside him. No hesitation. No sense that the work was beneath her. She simply knelt."

Marguerite watched the congregation lean forward, almost imperceptibly, the way wheat leans into wind.

"That was Verity," Pell continued. "She knelt. In a parish full of people who preferred to stand at a distance and write cheques, she knelt. She knelt in the crypt when the drains flooded. She knelt in the Sunday school when we were short a teacher. She knelt beside the dying, more than once, though she never spoke of it, because Verity understood something that took me years to learn — that service is not what you give. Service is what you *lower yourself* to carry."

The phrase landed in the nave like a stone dropped down a well. Marguerite heard a woman two pews ahead sniffle, then weep quietly.

*He is good,* Marguerite thought, with a cold professional admiration that felt almost like fear. *He is very, very good.*

And then the thought sharpened, turned, showed its other face: *Of course he is good. He has had eleven years to rehearse.*

Pell spoke for twenty-two minutes. Marguerite timed it out of habit. He told stories — the flooded crypt, the Christmas Eve when Verity had stayed until two in the morning helping an elderly parishioner home through the snow, the time she had quietly paid a curate's dental bills and made him swear never to tell. Each story was specific, textured, anchored in names and dates and sensory detail. Each story was, so far as Marguerite could determine, true. That was the horror of it. He wasn't inventing a saint. He was curating one — selecting, arranging, polishing, the way he curated the case files, the way he curated the seating charts, the way he curated, year after year, the parish's memory of the woman he had killed.

"And some of you will ask me," Pell said, and his voice softened, descended, became almost intimate, "as you ask me every year — *do we know what happened to her? Will we ever know?* And every year I give you the same answer, and I want to explain why I give it."

He paused. The candles guttered in the draught from the west door.

"I say: we know what matters. We know who she was. The rest — the circumstances, the questions, the *not knowing* — that is not our burden to solve. That belongs to God, who saw everything, and to Verity, who if she could speak would tell us not to torment ourselves. She would tell us to kneel. She would tell us to pick up the hymnals." He looked out across the faces, and his gaze seemed to rest on each one individually, a trick of light and sincerity that Marguerite recognized as technique and the congregation received as grace. "Grief wants answers. But love — love wants only that the beloved be remembered rightly. And she is. Every year. By all of you. That is her true memorial, and no archive, no investigation, no file in any diocesan office can add one thing to it."

*Amen,* said three hundred voices, and the sound rolled up into the vaulting like water finding its level.

Marguerite did not say amen. Marguerite watched Cordelia, who also did not say amen, whose lips had gone white, whose hands gripped the pew rail as though the floor were tilting. Cordelia had heard the speech eleven times, presumably. But tonight she was hearing it differently — hearing it the way one hears a familiar song after learning what the lyrics conceal. Her eyes found Marguerite's across the nave, briefly, wildly, and then fixed on the floor.

The service closed with a hymn — number 368, *Christ Whose Glory Fills the Skies*, and Marguerite felt the number register somewhere below thought, in the place where ciphers lived now, in the place where hymn numbers had become a language. 3-6-8. It appeared in Verity's marginalia twice, both times adjacent to passages about trust. Coincidence, probably. Everything was probably coincidence, right up until it wasn't.

The congregation filed out into the cold. Pell stood at the door shaking hands, and Marguerite watched him do it — the two-handed clasp, the slight bow of the head, the way he said each person's name as though retrieving it from a well he kept inside himself. He knew all their names. Of course he did. He knew their names, their anniversaries, their bereavements, their sins, probably. A man who memorized case files memorized people. People were files too, in the end. Files that walked and wept and came back every year to be read aloud to.

Marguerite lingered, letting the crowd thin, letting the shadows do their work. And when the last of the flower guild had shuffled out into the night and Pell turned to begin extinguishing the candles, Marguerite stepped out of the gallery's shadow and into the open nave.

"Mr. Pell."

He didn't startle. That was the first thing Marguerite noticed, and it confirmed everything. A guilty man startles. A man with nothing to hide startles. Only a man who has been expecting you — who has mapped your approach the way he maps everything — remains perfectly still, candle-snuffer in hand, turning with the unhurried grace of a host greeting a late guest.

"Miss Dravenmoor." His smile was warm. It reached his eyes. That was the second thing. "You stayed for the whole service. I'm glad. Most of the professionals who come through here — journalists, mostly, in the early years — they'd slip out during the hymn. As if remembering her were the tedious part and the mystery were the interesting part." He set the snuffer down on the lectern. "You didn't slip out."

"It was a remarkable address."

"You're kind." He began moving down the aisle toward her, unhurried, and Marguerite was struck again by how the space rearranged itself around him — how the cathedral, which had felt vast and indifferent for weeks, now felt like a room he owned. "Twenty-two minutes. I trim it every year. The first year I spoke for forty and old Canon Wetherby fell asleep, which I've never quite forgiven him for, though of course I forgave him immediately, because that's rather the point of the thing." He stopped four feet away. Close enough for warmth. Far enough for courtesy. "Did anything strike you? You have an archivist's ear. I imagine you hear speeches the way I hear them — structurally."

"Structurally," Marguerite said, "it was flawless."

"Ah." Something flickered behind his eyes — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a fellow craftsman admire the joinery. "But?"

"But nothing. It was flawless. That's what struck me."

For a moment neither of them moved. Somewhere above them, the great clock in the tower gathered itself and struck nine, each note falling through the dark nave like a coin dropped into deep water. Pell waited out all eight strokes before he spoke again, and when he did, his voice had changed — not colder, nothing so crude as that. Gentler. The gentleness of a nurse preparing an injection.

"May I tell you something, Miss Dravenmoor? Not as verger to investigator. Just as — one person who loves this building to another."

"You may."

"I've watched you these past weeks. In the archive. In the crypt. I expect you knew that; you're far too careful not to have known it." He smiled again, ruefully, as if confessing a small vanity. "And I want you to know that I don't resent it. I understand why Cordelia hired you. I understand why you came. There's a wound in this parish, and wounds make people reach for instruments. You're an instrument. A fine one. Perhaps the finest that's ever been pointed at us." He paused, choosing words the way he chose candles to snuff, one by one. "But I've watched instruments come and go for eleven years. And I've noticed something about them. They always believe the truth will heal the wound. And it never does. It only makes the wound larger, and teaches everyone in the parish exactly how deep it goes."

"The truth usually manages," Marguerite said, "regardless of what it heals."

"Does it?" He tilted his head, genuinely curious, and that was somehow worse than menace — the sense that he was interested in the answer, that he collected answers the way he collected file details, that somewhere in the vast memorized archive of his mind there was already a shelf reserved for her. "I knew a woman who believed that. Completely. Utterly. She believed truth was a kind of disinfectant — that you could pour it into any wound, however old, however deep, and the body would thank you for it." His voice softened further, and Marguerite realized with a lurch that he was speaking of Verity in the present tense, that in some sealed chamber of himself she was still alive, still arguing with him, still kneeling in the flooded crypt. "She was braver than me. Kinder, too, in her way — kinder the way fire is kind. But she never learned what I learned here, tending this parish. Some truths are not disinfectant. They're salt. And the question a careful person asks is not *is it true* but *who will it burn, and are they the ones who deserve burning*."

"Everyone deserves the truth," Marguerite said. "That's rather the point of archives. Memory isn't a possession. It's a trust."

"A trust." He repeated the word slowly, tasting it, and for one vertiginous moment Marguerite wondered whether he knew — whether he had already found the hollowed reliquary emptied, whether the second ledger was at this moment in his coat pocket, whether this entire gentle speech was being delivered over a grave he had already dug. Then the moment passed, or seemed to, and he was only a tired verger in a cold church. "Yes. I suppose that's what you'd say. Well." He glanced toward the west door, where the last candlelight leaked out onto the wet pavement. "It's late, and you'll want your supper, and I have candles to finish. Eleven of them, always eleven. One for each year. The flower guild thinks it's morbid. I think it's honest."

He picked up the snuffer and moved past her toward the chancel, and Marguerite stood alone in the nave, listening to the small silver sounds of flames going out, one by one, the darkness advancing up the aisles like a tide.

At the fourth candle he spoke again, without turning, his voice carrying perfectly in the acoustic dead spot beneath the gallery — the quirk of geometry he knew better than anyone alive.

"Oh — one more thing, Miss Dravenmoor. A small thing. Professional courtesy." The fifth candle died. "Some cases are better left unclosed. Not because the truth is bad. Because closure is. A closed file stops being read. And a thing that stops being read—" the sixth candle, the seventh, "—stops being *kept*. And I have spent eleven years keeping her. Whatever you find, whatever you think you've found — ask yourself whether the parish survives it. Ask yourself whether *she* would have wanted it. Ask yourself—"

The eighth candle went out.

"—whether you're serving the dead, or merely disturbing them."

Silence. Marguerite turned. Pell stood at the chancel step, the last three candles burning behind him like witnesses, and his expression was the same warm, sorrowful, patient expression he wore at every funeral, every memorial, every anniversary — the face of a man who grieves beautifully and forever.

"Goodnight, Miss Dravenmoor. Do wrap up warmly. The crypt gets terribly cold at night." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "So I'm told."

He finished the candles. The nave went dark. And Marguerite Dravenmoor stood in the blackness listening to his footsteps recede up the aisle — steady, unhurried, certain — and understood, with total clarity, that the warning had been delivered, the terms stated, and that whatever happened next would happen because she had refused them.

She also understood something else, something that arrived a heartbeat later and settled in her chest like swallowed ice:

*So I'm told.* 

Pell had never given her reason to believe he visited the crypt at night. She had never mentioned her own searches. Which meant either he was guessing —

— or he had been watching her watch him, all along, and the trap she was building had a second architect.

Marguerite stood a long moment in the dark, then walked out into the cold, and began, in her head, revising the plan.
