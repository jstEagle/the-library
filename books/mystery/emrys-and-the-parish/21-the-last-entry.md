## 21. The Last Entry

The crypt was cold in the way that stone remembers winter, and Marguerite Dravenmoor had come to think of that cold as a kind of honesty. It did not flatter. It did not console. It simply held the temperature of the truth beneath St. Aldhelm's, and for eleven years that truth had been Verity Loxley's body, and above it, layered like sediment, the careful fiction of a parish that preferred its grief tidy.

They had waited three days after Pell's arrest before coming down here alone.

The police tape still clung to the arch at the foot of the stairs, sagging where someone — Pell's solicitor, perhaps, or an officer with more sentiment than procedure — had pushed it aside. Marguerite ducked under it without ceremony. The lamps they carried threw long amber circles on the flagstones, and the saints in their niches watched with the patient indifference of carved things who had seen every kind of confession and been moved by none of them.

The reliquary stood open now. The hollow where Verity's second ledger had slept for a decade gaped like a pulled tooth. Marguerite had photographed it, measured it, catalogued it, and then, yesterday, returned the ledger itself to the diocese solicitor with a receipt signed in their own small, precise hand. Evidence belonged to the living now. To courts, to Cordelia, to the slow machinery of restitution.

But the diary stayed.

It lay on the worktable Marguerite had carried down two days ago — a folding table, incongruously modern against the Norman pillars, its legs shimmed level with folded card because Marguerite could not abide a surface that rocked. On it: the diary, opened to its final written page; a magnifying loupe; three pens whose inks Marguerite had spent the evening testing under raking light; and a single sheet of paper, aged in tea and ash over the past week, cut to match the diary's leaves exactly.

Marguerite sat, and did not touch anything for a long moment.

This was the part no one had asked them to do. The inspector had not asked it. Cordelia had not asked it. No court would ever require it, and indeed, if any court learned of it, there would be questions Marguerite did not entirely welcome — questions about an archivist who had already forged one page, however righteous the purpose, now preparing to forge another.

But Marguerite had a rule about accounts. They had learned it the hard way, in another parish, another decade, another failure whose name they still did not say aloud even in the privacy of their own skull: *an account left open is a wound left open.* The diary of Verity Loxley ended mid-sentence, in a hand gone shaky with fear or illness or the simple erosion of hope, dated three years after her disappearance, trailing off into marginalia that said only *he knows* and *the hymn numbers will hold* and nothing more. Eleven years, the book had hung there, unfinished, a sentence without its period.

Pell had kept it alive because he could not let her die. Marguerite understood that now with a clarity that felt almost like tenderness and was not. He had loved her the way ivy loves a wall — completely, destructively, unable to distinguish between holding and strangling. He had continued her handwriting because stopping would have meant admitting she was gone. He had memorized every file because memory was the only place she still lived, and he had killed the sacristan's memory, and then the sacristan, because a witness is just a rival archive.

Marguerite closed their eyes briefly. *An account left open.*

They picked up the pen.

Not to imitate Verity — that artifice was finished, its purpose served, its page burned in a brass dish by a man who wept as he fed it to the flames. This entry would be different. It would be honest about what it was. Marguerite would write it in Verity's voice because the diary demanded a voice, but the entry itself would declare its authorship plainly, in the old convention of amanuenses: *written for her, because she cannot.*

Marguerite dipped the pen — iron gall, mixed fresh, because Verity's later pages had darkened with oxidation and a modern ink would have shouted across the decades — and began.

---

*14th of October.*

*I am not Verity Loxley. My name is Marguerite Dravenmoor, and I keep the archives of this cathedral, which means I keep its debts as well as its treasures. This diary has been maintained falsely for eleven years, first by a man who could not bury the woman he loved, and now by me, who could not bear to leave her account unsettled. What follows is the true final entry, and it is written so that anyone who finds this book will know where the lies end.*

*Verity Loxley died in this crypt on the night of the 3rd of March, eleven years ago. She came here to move her ledger — the second ledger, the one that named the fraud in the Loxley Trust — because she had begun to doubt her partner in the exposure. She was right to doubt him. He met her here. He argued with her. And when she would not yield the ledger, he killed her, and he hid her body where the dead keep vigil, among the saints she trusted more than people.*

*His name was Mr. Pell. He was the verger of this parish for thirty years, and he was beloved, and his being beloved is part of how he managed it. He confessed on the night of the anniversary dinner, before witnesses, while burning a page he believed was hers. It was not hers. It was mine. I made it to catch him, and I do not repent of it, though I expect I will be asked to.*

*The ledger is with the diocese solicitor. Cordelia Loxley has her inheritance. The sacristan, Mr. Ambrose Fenn, who saw more than his shifting memory could hold, is dead, and his name belongs on the memorial roll beside hers, because he was murdered for remembering, and remembering is holy work.*

*That is the truth. It is not comfortable, and it does not make a moving sermon, and Mr. Pell would have hated it, because Mr. Pell believed that a story should be tended like a garden — pruned, watered, shaped toward beauty. But Verity Loxley did not stage her disappearance to be beautiful. She staged it to be heard. So hear her: the trust was robbed, the trustees were complicit or convenient, and the woman who tried to stop it was killed by the man who lit the candles for her memorial every year with his own hands.*

*I have checked every fact in this entry twice. I have cross-referenced it against the coroner's findings, the trust ledgers, and the testimony taken at his trial. Where my knowledge ends, I have said so. An archivist's first duty is to the difference between what is known and what is wished.*

*Rest, Verity. Your account is closed.*

*M.D., archivist, this 14th day of October.*

---

Marguerite set down the pen and read it through three times, the way they read everything — once for sense, once for error, once for the errors that sense itself might hide. The third reading found nothing. The second found one thing: *I do not repent of it,* and Marguerite paused there, pen hovering, and finally left it. Precision did not require false modesty.

Then they tipped a little of the remaining iron gall onto a cloth and worked it into the corners of the new leaf, aging the edges to match its neighbors, and pasted it in with wheat starch paste, the same adhesive the binders of the fifteenth century had used, because some standards were not negotiable. The diary closed with a sound like a held breath released.

Marguerite sat for a while in the lamplight, hands flat on the table, listening to the crypt breathe its stone breath.

Three days ago, in this same room, Pell had knelt among the candle stubs with the burning forgery in his hands and told the empty saints everything. Marguerite had stood in the doorway with Cordelia behind them and Inspector Hale beside them, and had listened to a man dismantle himself with the same meticulous care he brought to polishing brass. He had spoken of Verity in the present tense until nearly the end. He had apologized to the saints — *to the saints*, not to her — for the mess. And at the last, cuffed and rising, he had looked at Marguerite with something that was not hatred and was worse than hatred: recognition.

"You understand," he'd said. "That's the worst of it. You understand exactly."

Marguerite had said nothing. There had been nothing true enough to say.

Now, alone, they allowed themselves the thought they had suppressed: *yes.* Yes, they understood. They understood what it was to love an account so much you falsified it. They had done it smaller, years ago, in a case they could not close — had softened a report, rounded a certainty into a probability, because the truth would have broken a widow who had already lost too much. And the softened report had let a guilty man walk, and the widow had died not knowing, and Marguerite had built their whole rigid life out of that single crack. Never take a case you can't close. Never soften. Never again.

And yet here they sat, having forged a document, having baited a trap, having used a dead woman's voice as a weapon. Justice outside the law. Inspector Hale had looked at them across the interview room table yesterday and said, "Your forgery doesn't taint the confession. His own words, witnessed by three people, hold on their own. But don't do it again, Dravenmoor." And Marguerite had answered, "No," and both of them had known the word was a coin of uncertain denomination.

Peace earned through precision. That was what this was supposed to feel like. Marguerite searched for it and found something adjacent — not peace exactly, but steadiness. The sense of a surface leveled true, a shelf bearing exactly what it should bear, no more.

They rose, gathered the tools, folded the table.

At the foot of the stairs they stopped, turned back, and took one last look. The niches. The reliquary, empty now, its door ajar. The worktable gone, the floor swept, every trace of the investigation erased except the truth itself, which now lay in the diary like a cornerstone laid straight.

Marguerite locked the crypt door, tested the lock twice, and recorded the time in their logbook to the minute: *9:47 p.m. Crypt secured. Contents: complete.*

* * *

Cordelia was waiting in the vestry when Marguerite came up, sitting in one of the hard chairs with her coat still on, as though she hadn't decided whether she was staying or fleeing. She looked older than she had four months ago, and lighter, the way stone looks lighter once the moss is scraped off.

"It's done?" she asked.

"The entry is written. The diary is sealed in the crypt archive. The lock is changed — new key, one copy, held by the diocese registrar, one by me." Marguerite set the logbook on the table between them. "You may read the entry whenever you wish. I've transcribed it."

Cordelia didn't reach for the transcription. She looked at her hands instead. "Aunt Verity used to say that a lie repeated often enough becomes architecture. You live in it. You forget it has walls." A pause. "He built us all a house, didn't he. The whole parish. We lived in it for eleven years and called it home."

"He was a gifted architect," Marguerite said. "That's not forgiveness. It's diagnosis."

"No." Cordelia smiled faintly, and it was Verity's smile, the one from the photographs, tilted and unsentimental. "But it helps." She stood, buttoned her coat. "The trust settlement clears Friday. I'm directing the restoration funds to the crypt archive — proper shelving, climate control, a catalogue worthy of the collection. I'd like you to write the catalogue."

Marguerite blinked. "I'm not a cathedral employee."

"You're the only person alive who knows what's down there and hasn't lied about it. Write the catalogue, Dravenmoor. Make it honest."

"I will," Marguerite said, and found that the words cost nothing, which was itself remarkable.

Cordelia paused at the vestry door. "One more thing. Ambrose Fenn's sister wrote to me. She wants his name read at Sunday service. Some of the parish council are resisting — they say it will upset people, reopen things." Her jaw tightened. "I told them the truth isn't a door you get to close because it's heavy."

"Tell them," Marguerite said, "that memory is holy work. Someone should have told him that while he was alive."

She went. Marguerite remained a moment in the vestry, in the smell of beeswax and old linen, and let the silence settle around them like a garment cut correctly.

Outside, the evening had turned. Rain had come and gone, leaving the close shining, and the cathedral rose black and certain against a sky rinsed to a deep, clean blue. Marguerite walked to the lychgate and stood there, looking back, doing the thing they always did at the end of a case: the accounting. Four months. One murder solved, one murder avenged too late, one fraud unwound, one inheritance delivered, one man in custody awaiting trial, one confession witnessed and sound. One forgery, unrepented. One old failure, examined and re-examined and finally, perhaps, beginning to scar over rather than bleed.

The bells began.

Not the full peal — the practice ring, Tuesday evenings, eight ringers working through changes with the cheerful imperfection of amateurs. But the sound rolled out over the parish roofs and down into the streets where people were lighting their lamps, and Marguerite thought: *they will remember differently now.* Not comfortably. Not beautifully. But truly. Somewhere below the ringing, in a locked room where the dead keep vigil, a diary rested with its true last entry inside it, and the account was closed, and the period at the end of the sentence was set straight.

Marguerite turned up the collar of their coat and stepped through the lychgate onto the lane.

Halfway down, near the corner where the noticeboard stood with its faded posters for harvest suppers and choir practices, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the yew tree. A woman, sixtyish, rain-spotted tweed, carrying a leather portfolio clasped shut with a strap worn soft at the edges. She had the look of someone who had rehearsed an opening line several times on the walk over and was now abandoning all of them.

"Dravenmoor?" she said. "Forgive me. I know you've only just — everyone says you've only just finished. But I have a document." Her hands tightened on the portfolio. "It's a letter. Dated 1962. Signed by a man who was buried in 1958."

Marguerite stood very still under the fading bells.

The old rule rose in them — *never take a case you can't close* — and beside it, newer and quieter, the amendment earned in the crypt: *but never refuse one because it frightens you.*

"Show me," Marguerite said.
