## 9. Insurance Against Conviction

The offices of Blake & Associates occupied the second floor of the old Mercantile Bank building, which was the sort of architectural joke Noor had come to expect from Ashcombe. The bank had failed in the eighties; the Vales had bought it for pennies and installed their lawyer above the vault. There was a metaphor in there, Noor thought as she climbed the marble stairs, but she was too angry to bother polishing it.

She hadn't called ahead. Calling ahead would have given Corinne Blake time to arrange her face, and Noor wanted to see what it looked like unarranged.

What it looked like, when the receptionist — a young man with the haunted patience of someone paid to intercept difficult visitors — ushered her through double doors into a corner office with a view of the river, was: mildly surprised. Pleasantly so. Corinne Blake sat behind a desk of dark walnut that had probably witnessed more sins than most confessional booths, and she looked up from her papers with the expression of a woman whose tea had arrived one degree cooler than preferred.

"Ms. Fairweather." She set down her pen with care, aligning it parallel to the desk's edge. "I wondered when you'd come."

"You *wondered*," Noor said.

"I estimated." Corinne gestured to the chair across from her. "Please sit. You've walked up two flights of stairs in this heat, and you strike me as someone who argues better seated."

Noor sat, mostly because refusing would have felt like conceding something, though she couldn't have said what. The office smelled of lemon polish and old paper and, faintly, of money — not new money, which shouts, but old money, which merely hums.

"I'll save us both some time," Noor said. "You hired me."

Corinne didn't blink. She reached for a carafe on the sideboard, poured water into two glasses — crystal, of course, because even her glassware was testifying to something — and slid one across the desk.

"Yes," she said.

Just like that. No denial, no deflection, no lawyerly fog. Noor had prepared herself for an hour of fencing and found instead a woman simply opening the gate.

"That's it? 'Yes'?"

"Would you prefer a more elaborate confession? I can provide one, but I find brevity builds trust." Corinne folded her hands. "Yes, Ms. Fairweather. My firm retained your services through Meridian Holdings, which is a shell company, which you already know, because you're here rather than still digging. Ask your questions. I'll answer the ones I choose to, honestly."

Noor picked up the water mostly to have something to do with her hands. Her fury was a live thing in her chest, and it kept whispering *throw the glass, throw the glass*, and she kept telling it they didn't make furniture sturdy enough for what she wanted to do to this office.

"Why?" she said. "Why hire the one detective in fifty miles who might actually find him?"

"Because finding him is the point." Corinne rose and moved to the window, hands clasped at the small of her back, surveying her town the way a general surveys terrain. "Do you know what happens, legally speaking, when a dead man turns up alive?"

"He stops being dead."

"Clever. But incomplete." She turned. "Emrys Vale was declared deceased eleven months ago. His death certificate is filed, stamped, and — critically — *authenticated by this firm*. If Emrys Vale walks into a courtroom tomorrow and says 'the portrait is a forgery and I painted it,' do you know what opposing counsel says?"

Noor said nothing. She could feel the shape of it coming, the way you feel weather change against an old scar.

"They say: 'This man faked his own death. This man has spent a year living under a false identity. This man admits to forging a painting. Why should this jury believe a single word from a confessed fraudster who staged his own demise, quite possibly for insurance or inheritance purposes?'" Corinne smiled, thin and precise as a paper cut. "His testimony doesn't just become questionable. It becomes *poison*. Everything he touches, he contaminates. The sketches, the receipts, the pattern of accidents — all of it arrives pre-discredited, gift-wrapped in reasonable doubt."

"And you needed me to find him first."

"I needed *you*," Corinne said, "specifically. Do you know why? Because you're good. Genuinely, irritatingly good. If a private investigator of lesser quality located Mr. Vale, the family's people might handle it quietly and we'd be no worse off. But if *you* found him — publicly, verifiably, with your reputation attached — then his existence becomes a matter of record before he ever reaches a courtroom. And once it's a matter of record, the defense writes itself." She returned to her chair and sat, smoothing her skirt. "You weren't hired despite being the best detective in the county, Ms. Fairweather. You were hired *because* of it."

The words landed somewhere beneath Noor's ribs and sat there, cold and heavy, like a stone swallowed whole.

Twenty-nine years. Twenty-nine years of catching liars, and she had been handed a script and read her lines beautifully. *Prove the murder hasn't happened yet.* She'd thought she was working against the family. She'd been working *for* them, free of charge to them — well, not free, triple rate, paid out of accounts they controlled — doing the one thing they needed done: converting a ghost into a documented fraudster.

"The three accidents," Noor said quietly. "The fall. The fire. The drowning."

"What about them?"

"If I publish my findings — if I go to the press with what I know about those deaths—"

"Then you attach yourself to a conspiracy theory built on the word of a self-admitted forger who faked his own death, and every serious journalist in the country files it under 'eccentric local woman, disgraced former detective, unreliable narrator.'" Corinne's voice never rose, never sharpened. It simply proceeded, like a train that had never once needed to hurry. "You'd burn whatever credibility you have left, and you'd burn *his* with it. You'd do our work for us a second time."

Noor set down the glass. Very carefully. Because if she didn't set it down carefully, she was going to put it through the window, and the window looked expensive, and she was suddenly, viciously aware that she could not afford the window.

"You killed four people," she said. "Or your clients did. And you sat there and designed a trap where the only witness left alive destroys himself the moment he opens his mouth."

"I designed a trap where nobody else has to die." For the first time, something moved behind Corinne's composure — not guilt, Noor decided. Something more like fatigue. "Understand what I'm offering you, Detective, even if you won't accept it. Every path except this one ends with Emrys Vale in the river. This path ends with him discredited and *alive*. I have made my peace with the trade. Have you?"

"There's a third option."

"There's always a third option in novels." Corinne almost smiled. "In Ashcombe, there are two: theirs and theirs."

Noor stood. Her knees were steady, which surprised her; inside, everything was ringing like a struck bell. "You're very good at your job, Ms. Blake."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment." She walked to the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, and turned back. "One question. When you hired me — did you think I wouldn't figure it out?"

Corinne considered this with the seriousness of a chess player acknowledging a decent move.

"I thought you might," she said. "Eventually. That was accounted for. But I also thought you'd understand that knowing changes nothing. You now know exactly how the machine works, and the machine works precisely the same whether you know or not. That's what makes it a good machine." She picked up her pen again, aligned it with the desk's edge, and looked down at her papers. "Give my regards to Mr. Vale. Tell him the offer stands until it doesn't."

* * *

Outside, the heat hit her like a wet towel, and Noor stood on the Mercantile steps letting it, because moving required deciding where to move *to*, and every direction felt compromised.

She'd been played. Not beaten — played. There was a difference, and the difference mattered, and she turned it over in her mind the whole walk back down Fore Street, past the bakery, past the shuttered cinema, past the newsagent who nodded at her the way everyone in Ashcombe nodded at everyone: pleasantly, and without seeing.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop admiring, and the admiration made her angrier than the trap itself. It was *elegant*. No violence necessary. No risk. They'd taken the last witness and arranged for him to testify himself into oblivion. They'd taken the one honest investigator and made her the instrument. And if she refused to play — if she simply walked away and told no one — then Emrys stayed a ghost, the portrait stayed genuine, and the family waited him out until the chapel wall or the river or simple despair finished the job.

Every branch of the tree led down.

Except.

She stopped outside her own shop, key in hand, and stood looking through the dusty glass at the spines of books she loved and could barely afford to keep, and let the thought finish forming, slow and cold and clear as ice water.

A trap only works if the prey moves along the paths provided.

Corinne had planned for Noor to find Emrys. Had planned for Noor to confront her. Had planned, presumably, for outrage, for threats, for a detective storming off to lick her wounds. What Corinne had *not* planned for — couldn't have planned for, because it wasn't in any file, wasn't in any ledger — was what Noor had learned in a derelict chapel from a terrified man who trusted her with the only truth he had left.

She didn't have to prove Emrys was alive.

She had to prove the *painting* was a lie. And a painting, unlike a man, couldn't be discredited by the history of its painter. Pigment didn't perjure itself. Canvas didn't fake its own death. Somewhere in that varnish, in that grief that was too modern, in the chemical signature of paint bought at a hardware shop eleven days after a funeral — somewhere in the object itself was a testimony no lawyer could poison.

Noor unlocked the door, stepped into the cool dimness of the shop, and reached for the phone.

There was a constable she owed a favor to, and a police file she needed to read, and a dead archivist whose drowning deserved better than the word *accidental*.

But first — she glanced at the note she'd left herself that morning, the one with the gala date circled in red ink — first, she was going to need Emrys Vale to do the bravest thing a coward had ever done.

She dialed the number for the florist next to the chapel, the one Emrys used for messages, and asked for a dozen white lilies delivered to a derelict church outside town.

The card read: *They know I know. Time to talk strategy. — N.F.*

Then she made tea, sat down among her unsold books, and began, methodically, to plan how to spring a trap on the people who'd built one for her — starting with the assumption that Corinne Blake was watching everything she did.

Let her watch. Noor had spent twenty-nine years learning the difference between evidence and theater.

It was time somebody taught Ashcombe the same lesson.
