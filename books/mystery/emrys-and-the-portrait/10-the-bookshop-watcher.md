## 10. The Bookshop Watcher

The bell above the door of Fairweather & Sons had been broken for three years, producing instead a sound like a spoon dropped in an empty bathtub, and Noor had long ago decided this was fitting. A proper chime suggested welcome. The spoon-in-bathtub suggested: *enter at your own risk.*

Marcus Vale entered at his own risk on a Tuesday morning, ten minutes after she'd flipped the sign to OPEN, which was eleven minutes earlier than Noor had ever served a customer in her life.

He was taller than his photograph in the *Gazette* made him look, and better dressed than any man had a right to be in a town where the second-most-popular footwear was Wellington boots. Charcoal overcoat, cashmere scarf the color of expensive regret, shoes that had never once known mud. He looked around the shop the way a man looks around a room he's already memorized from blueprints.

"Good morning," Noor said, because she was raised polite even when armed.

"Morning." He smiled. It was a good smile, professionally installed. "I'm looking for a book."

"You've come to the right place. It's the only thing we sell." She came around the counter. "What sort?"

He blinked, as though the question were a trap. "Oh. Um. Something... literary."

"Ah," said Noor. "Literary. My favorite genre."

She walked him past the poetry, past the classics, past the shelf she privately labeled *Books People Buy to Feel Smarter and Never Open*, and pulled out a handsome hardcover of something about a lighthouse and grief. Marcus Vale took it, weighed it in his hand like contraband, and paid cash.

He did not come back for nine days. Then he came back and bought a biography of Admiral Nelson that he held upside down for the entire transaction.

* * *

By the third visit, Noor had stopped pretending it was commerce.

He came every week now, sometimes twice, always alone, always between two and four in the afternoon — the dead hours when the shop was emptyest and Noor was most alone. He browsed without browsing. He asked her opinion on books he clearly hadn't read. Once he stood in front of the local history section for twenty full minutes, and Noor, watching him from behind the till, watched him not read a single spine.

"You know," she said pleasantly, ringing up his fourth purchase — a book on beekeeping, God help her — "we do offer a subscription service. I could just pick books for you. Save you the trip."

Something flickered across his face. Not embarrassment. Calculation.

"I like coming in," he said. "It's peaceful here. Hard to find peace these days."

"Mm," said Noor. "Peace is very hard to find. Especially if you're looking for it somewhere it isn't."

He laughed, warm and easy, and left with his beekeeping book, and Noor stood at the window and watched him cross the street, get into a car worth more than her entire inventory, and sit there for six minutes before driving away.

Six minutes. She timed it. She'd started timing everything now — how long he stayed, what time he arrived, which shelves he touched. There was a page in her notebook for it, wedged between the payment trail and a grocery list, and the page was getting crowded.

Her grandmother had kept bees. That was the only reason she knew the beekeeping book was wrong for him. Everything else about Marcus Vale was wrong too, but the bees had a certain clarity.

* * *

"He's casing you," said Dev the following Friday, from his usual post at the shop's one armchair, where he had been failing to finish the same P.D. James novel since March.

Dev was seventy-eight, a retired railway signalman, and the closest thing Fairweather & Sons had to a security system. He came in most afternoons, bought nothing, drank tea Noor provided free of charge, and observed the town with the patient omniscience of a lighthouse.

"Casing me," Noor repeated. "This isn't a bank job, Dev."

"No?" He turned a page he hadn't read. "Man comes in four times, buys four books, reads none of them. Sits in his car outside twice that I know of — and I only know twice because I only come in twice. Checks his phone every time the door opens. That's not a reader, love. That's a watcher."

Noor poured the tea. "Maybe he's lonely."

"Maybe he is. Lonely men don't buy beekeeping books." Dev looked at her over his spectacles with the mild, terrible certainty of a man who had spent forty years watching signals and knowing exactly which ones lied. "Your gran kept bees, didn't she? Down at the cottage."

"How did you—"

"Everybody knew your gran's bees. Best honey in the county. Point is, if that man wanted a book about bees, he'd have asked you. You're the book woman. Instead he picked it off the shelf himself, wrong edition and all, like a man picking a prop." He sipped. "In my day we called that dressing a set."

Noor sat down across from him. Outside, the afternoon light was doing its best, slanting gold through the window glass and catching the dust motes, making the shop look exactly like what it was supposed to be: a safe, sleepy, harmless little place full of dead people's words.

That was the trouble with sanctuaries. They only worked if everyone agreed they were sanctuaries.

"Dev," she said, "if I told you I was in some trouble — nothing dramatic, nothing you need to worry about — would you worry anyway?"

"I'd worry efficiently," said Dev. "What kind of trouble?"

"The kind that buys books."

He nodded slowly, as if this confirmed everything. "Worst kind," he said. "They never read them. Nothing so sinister as a man who buys books and doesn't read them."

* * *

That night Noor took inventory of her defenses and found the list embarrassingly short.

One: a back door with a lock that worked if you lifted the handle while turning the key, a trick she'd learned by necessity and never taught anyone. Two: a telephone. Three: a cricket bat belonging to her late father, propped behind the counter, more sentimental than tactical. Four: Dev, who went home at six.

Against this: a family that owned the town, a lawyer who hired detectives as weapons, four deaths ruled accidental, and a man in a charcoal coat who checked his watch every time the door opened.

She thought about calling Emrys. She'd meant to tell him about Corinne Blake days ago — about the shell company, about being hired as cover, about the whole elegant machine of it. But every time she picked up the phone to arrange a meeting, she imagined Marcus Vale's car idling outside the chapel road, and her hand drifted away from the receiver. Every call she made was a thread someone might pull. Corinne had hired her precisely because Noor was findable, traceable, *legible*. Which meant everything Noor did was legible too.

So she wrote a letter instead. Three drafts. In the final one she managed, with great effort, to say almost nothing: *The weather is turning. Consider moving your easel somewhere with two exits. — N.* She addressed it to the hardware shop's general delivery, the dead drop they'd agreed on, and posted it after dark, walking the long way round and checking reflections in shop windows like a spy in a film she'd have mocked a year ago.

When she got home to the flat above the shop, she stood at the window with the lights off and watched the street for an hour. Nothing moved except a cat with ambitions. Eventually she went to bed and slept badly, in the shallow way of a person listening for a sound that hasn't happened yet.

The sound happened at 3:17 in the morning.

* * *

Glass has a particular voice when it breaks in the dark. Noor had heard it before, in another life, in other rooms — the high bright shatter followed by the long gravel whisper of fragments settling. Her body knew it before her mind did. She was upright, bat in hand, heart slamming, before the second sound came: a heavy, dull knock from the shop below, like a fist against wood.

She did not go down immediately. This was the detective in her, the part that had survived thirty years of other people's violence: *wait, listen, count.* She counted to two hundred. The house settled. The street stayed silent. Whoever had thrown whatever they'd thrown was gone or very good at waiting, and she judged the odds and went down the stairs with the lights off, bat first.

The front window of Fairweather & Sons had a hole in it the size of a grapefruit, star-cracked at the edges, the night air pushing through cold and smelling of rain. On the floor, amid the glitter of glass across the travel section — three guidebooks dead, a Baedeker wounded — lay a brick.

Not a large brick. A clean one, new, no mortar on it. Someone had bought it for the purpose.

Noor stood there in the dark with the bat hanging loose at her side and looked at it for a long moment. There was no note attached. No paint, no message, no symbol. Nothing written anywhere. Just the brick itself, sitting on her father's shop floor like a piece of punctuation.

And that, she understood, was the entire text. A note could be denied, misread, traced. A brick said only what a brick says: *we can reach you. We reached you tonight. We can reach you again whenever the mood takes us.* It was a sentence written in the grammar of men who never had to write anything down because everyone already understood the language.

She crouched, careful of the glass, and picked it up. Cold. Factory-clean. She turned it over twice, found nothing, and set it on the counter — evidence, or a paperweight, depending on how the next few weeks went.

Then she did something she wasn't proud of. She sat down on the floor of her ruined shop, in the glass, with her back against the counter, and let herself shake for exactly ninety seconds. She timed it on her watch. Ninety seconds of fear, allotted, contained, like medicine. It was a trick from her working years and it still worked, mostly. When the watch beeped she got up, fetched the broom, and began sweeping her sanctuary back into shape.

The bell — the broken one, the spoon-in-the-bathtub — hung crooked above the door, and when the draft moved it, it gave its sad little clank, and Noor found, absurdly, that it comforted her. Still hers. Still broken in the same way.

* * *

Dev arrived at half eight, took one look at the boarded-over window — Noor had nailed up plywood from the shed, crooked, unapologetic — and lowered himself into his armchair with the gravity of a judge taking the bench.

"Brick?" he said.

"Brick."

"Note?"

"No note."

Dev nodded. "Message is the message." He'd been a signalman forty years; he knew all about messages that consisted entirely of what they were. "Police?"

"And tell them what? Someone expressed an opinion about my window display?" Noor poured the tea stronger than usual. "Half the constabulary closes cases for the Vales before the tea goes cold. I'd be filing my own evidence into their shredder."

"So you're going to handle it yourself." It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm going to think," said Noor. "There's a difference. Handling implies I know what to do."

"Do you?"

She looked at the plywood. Morning light leaked around its edges in thin white lines, striping the carpet, striping the travel guides with their dead Baedeker. Her shop, her father's shop, with a hole nailed over it. Fear had moved house overnight — it no longer lived in the abstract country of ledgers and shell companies and drowned clerks. It lived here now. It had thrown a brick through her window and moved in.

"Yes," she heard herself say, and was surprised to find she believed it. "Because here's what the brick actually tells me, Dev. Underneath the threat. It tells me they're rattled. Men who are winning don't throw bricks at bookshops. Men who are winning smile at you from cars and buy beekeeping books. The brick means the clock is running *for them*, not me. Something's coming that scares them enough to get sloppy."

"Or it means they want you to think that," said Dev placidly, "while the real thing happens somewhere else."

Noor paused with the teapot halfway to her cup.

"That," she said, "is why I keep you on staff."

"I work for tea and the pleasure of your company. Mostly the tea." He opened his P.D. James at last, apparently to a new page. "So. What's coming that scares them?"

And Noor, standing in her boarded-up shop with her hands wrapped around hot china, let herself actually consider it — properly, in order, the way she used to. Corinne had hired her to destroy Emrys's credibility, and Noor had refused the role, and Corinne knew she'd refused, which meant the plan needed replacing. Which meant either they'd found Emrys themselves—

—or they were afraid *she* was close to him. Afraid of what a stubborn ex-detective with nothing left to lose might dig up. The brick wasn't a warning about the past. It was panic about the future.

Which meant Emrys was still hidden. Still alive. Still the last witness, sitting in a derelict chapel with his photographs of sketches, believing — after her confrontation with Corinne had splashed across the town's quiet channels like oil on water — that she might have sold him out.

"Oh," Noor said quietly. "Oh, you idiot. Both of us."

"What's that?"

"Nothing." She was already reaching for her coat. "Dev, mind the shop. If a tall man in a charcoal coat comes in, sell him the beekeeping book again and memorize his car registration."

"Already have it," said Dev. "Been memorizing it since Tuesday week."

Noor stared at him.

"Well," said Dev, turning his page. "Somebody round here had to be paying attention."

She was out the door before the spoon-in-the-bathtub finished clanking, letter unsent, dead drop abandoned — because if the Vales were watching her, then the hardware shop was burned, and if the hardware shop was burned, then Emrys might already be running, alone, into a city full of people paid to make accidents happen.

And the last thing she saw, pulling away in her ancient car, was Marcus Vale's dark sedan sliding into the space she'd just vacated, patient as tide, settling in to wait for a bookshop that wasn't open yet — and wouldn't be, not today, not for anyone looking for Noor Fairweather.

She drove toward the chapel with her foot down and her heart going like a hammer, praying she wasn't already too late.
