## 12. The Drowned Archivist

The phone rang at seven in the morning, which was an hour Noor Fairweather considered a theoretical concept rather than a lived experience.

She answered it with the grace of a woman who had once taken calls at crime scenes before coffee and could therefore survive anything. "Fairweather Books. We're closed. Also, possibly, we're open. I haven't decided."

"Noor." The voice belonged to Constable Wren Halloway, the last functioning conscience in the Ashcombe police department, and she sounded like she'd been awake considerably longer and enjoying it less. "I thought you'd want to know before the paper does. They pulled a body out of the reservoir this morning."

Noor sat up. Somewhere in the flat above the shop, a pipe knocked twice, as if it too were listening. "Who?"

"Hollis Finch. County records clerk."

Noor closed her eyes. She could see him without trying — a small, precise man with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, who had once spent forty minutes explaining to her the difference between a certified copy and a notarized one, and had seemed genuinely wounded that she didn't find the distinction thrilling. He had authenticated the provenance papers on the Lavinia Vale portrait. He had signed his name to the lie, whether he'd known it was one or not.

And now he was in the reservoir.

"Accidental," Noor said. It wasn't a question so much as a prophecy.

"Slipped on the boat ramp, hit his head, went into the water. That's the working theory." Wren's voice did something careful around the word *theory*, the way a person's voice does when they're carrying something fragile and don't want to drop it in front of witnesses. "Noor, I'm not supposed to be calling you."

"I know."

"His car was still in the lot at the records office. Locked. His lunch was in it. A man doesn't drive to work, leave his lunch, and then go drown himself at a boat ramp six miles away."

"No," Noor agreed. "He doesn't."

"I shouldn't have told you any of this."

"I know that too. Wren — thank you." She paused. "Was there water in his lungs?"

A silence, long enough to have an opinion. "That's not in the report I've seen."

Which meant it was in a report Wren had seen, and it said no.

Noor thanked her again, hung up, and sat on the edge of her bed in the gray light, listening to the pipes knock, and felt the grief arrive ahead of the rage the way thunder arrives ahead of the storm — no, she corrected herself, the other way around. Lightning first. The flash before the sound. Something in her chest had already been struck; the noise of it was still traveling toward her.

* * *

She opened the shop at nine because the shop did not care about drowned men, and neither did the mortgage, and neither did Mrs. Pemberton, who arrived at 9:04 sharp every Tuesday to complain about the ordering of the mystery section and to buy, invariably, a romance.

"They should be alphabetical by *feeling*," Mrs. Pemberton announced, "not by author. Nobody falls in love with an author whose name starts with Z."

"I'll take it under advisement." Noor rang her up and watched her go, and then stood for a moment in the middle of her own shop, breathing the smell of old paper and dust, and let herself feel the thing she was feeling.

It wasn't only Hollis. It was the shape of the thing. Four now. The fall, the fire, the drowning — and now a second drowning, as if the conspirators had run out of ideas or, worse, had never had any and simply kept a catalog. *Cause of death, see page one. See page one again.* Each of them a person who had touched the portrait's lie and then stopped being a person and started being a file with the word ACCIDENTAL stamped on it.

Hollis Finch had once given her a butterscotch from a tin he kept in his desk drawer, back when she'd been a detective and he'd been a witness in a case about forged land deeds. "For the wait," he'd said. "Bureaucracy is a kind of weather. You dress for it."

She had eaten the butterscotch. She remembered the exact taste.

"Right," she said aloud, to the shop, to the shelves, to no one. "Right."

The word came out harder than she intended. Mrs. Pemberton, mercifully, had already left.

* * *

The reservoir was a twenty-minute drive out of Ashcombe, past hedgerows going copper at the edges of autumn, and Noor spent the drive rehearsing all the reasons this was a stupid thing to do. She was not police. She had no standing. If Marcus Vale's people were watching — and they were, because a brick through her window two nights ago had communicated that message with the eloquence of all brick — then driving to the site of the fourth "accident" was roughly the equivalent of standing in the town square with a sign.

She went anyway. Some instincts you don't argue with. You just put on sensible shoes.

The boat ramp was cordoned with two sawhorses and a strip of tape that had already given up on life and lay in the grass like a shed skin. A single constable stood at the perimeter with the posture of a man guarding nothing in particular. Noor recognized him — young, earnest, the kind of officer who still thought the job was about the job.

She walked up to the tape and looked at the water. It lay flat and gray-green and perfectly indifferent, holding the sky the way a liar holds a straight face.

"Ma'am, I'm going to have to ask you to step back."

"I'm just looking at the water."

"It's a crime scene."

"Is it? I heard it was an accident." She let the word sit there between them, doing its work. The constable's ears went slightly pink. "Hollis Finch. Did you know him?"

"I — a bit. Everyone knew him. He'd bring biscuits to the office when it was his turn." The young man shifted. "It's a shame."

"A shame," Noor repeated softly. "Yes. It is a shame. It's also a pattern, but I suppose that's not in the report either."

"Ma'am—"

"I'm going, I'm going." She raised her hands and retreated a step, then paused, struck by something at the edge of the ramp. "One question. Purely academic. When they found him — was he wearing his glasses?"

The constable blinked. "I... don't know."

"His reading glasses. He wore them pushed up on his head like a little crown. He'd lose them constantly. He once called three offices looking for them and they were on his face the whole time." She smiled at the memory and found it hurt. "If a man slips on a boat ramp and hits his head, the glasses go one way and he goes the other. They'd be near the body. They'd be in the water. They'd be *somewhere*."

The constable said nothing. But his eyes flicked — briefly, involuntarily — toward the sawhorse on the left, and Noor followed the flick with the discipline of thirty years and found, tucked behind the sawhorse's leg, half in the weeds, a pair of small wire-rimmed glasses, folded neatly.

Folded.

Nobody folds their glasses while falling.

She didn't touch them. She wanted to, with a physical ache, but evidence in her hand was evidence the Vales could make disappear; evidence in a constable's sightline was evidence he'd now have to explain. She simply looked at it, then looked at the young constable, and let him watch her look at it.

"Academic question," she said. "How long has that been there?"

His throat moved. "I'll... make a note."

"You do that." She turned to go, then added, over her shoulder, gently: "Hollis Finch dressed for the weather, son. Somebody should dress for his."

* * *

The bookshop at midday was empty in the way that had lately become fashionable with her finances, and Noor used the quiet to do what she did instead of sleeping: she laid it all out.

Not on the table — she'd learned that lesson. On paper in her head, in the old architecture she'd built across a hundred cases. Four deaths. A portrait. A dead man who bought paint thinner. A lawyer who hired a detective to *prove* a murder hadn't happened yet, which was either madness or the most elegant alibi Noor had ever been invited to participate in. And somewhere in the city, Emrys Vale — alive, terrified, holding the one thing that could undo all of it — probably reading a newspaper this morning with Hollis Finch's name in it and drawing the only conclusion available to a man who trusted no one.

*They'll think I did it,* she thought. *Or that I led them to him.* And he'd run again, deeper, and she'd lose the only witness left.

Unless.

She got out the phone book — she kept one out of spite — and began the slow, unglamorous work of calling every secondhand bookshop, cheap café, and flophouse-adjacent establishment in the district where a man with no money and a scholar's habits might hide. It was like searching for a specific gray stone on a gray beach. Most people hung up. One laughed. A woman at a café called The Copper Kettle said, "We don't discuss our customers, dear," in a tone that meant *but I am discussing one right now, aren't I, because you said the magic word* — the magic word having been "he draws, always in the corner seat, pencil sketches."

"He was here," the woman admitted finally. "Week ago, maybe more. Paid in coins. Left a book behind by accident — art monograph, dreadful condition. I kept hoping he'd come back for it."

"Did he?"

"Not yet." A pause, freighted. "But he asked me something, before he left. Asked if I knew anywhere a man could sleep cheap without questions. I said the Anchor Rooms, by the fish market, but that I'd never say so." Another pause. "I never say so, do you understand me? I have never said so to anyone."

"Understood," Noor said, and meant it, and hung up, and sat with her heart going harder than a phone call warranted.

The Anchor Rooms. By the fish market. She wrote it on a slip of paper and then, after a moment's thought, did not put the slip in her pocket. She burned it over the sink and watched the words curl and go.

* * *

It was late afternoon when the second call came, and this one she took standing up.

Wren Halloway again. No preamble this time, which from Wren was itself the preamble. "The glasses turned up."

"I know. I saw them."

"You — " A breath. "Of course you did. Noor, they've been entered into evidence as *recovered from the scene*. Which is fine. Except the pathologist's preliminary notes say the head wound was consistent with the ramp, and there's a note — an internal note — questioning whether the wound geometry matches a slip-and-fall at all. And that note has been flagged for review by someone at the county level."

"Flagged by whom?"

"Redacted in the copy I have." Wren's voice was flat now, the voice of a woman reading a menu in a restaurant where she already knew the food was rotten. "Noor. Four accidental deaths connected to one family, every file closed inside a week, and now a records clerk drowns with his lunch still in his car and his glasses folded neatly by a sawhorse, and the review gets kicked upstairs. I've been a copper for eleven years. I know what a closed door sounds like. This whole building is one."

"Who's above the police, Wren?"

"That's the question, isn't it." A long exhale. "I can't give you the files. Not the real ones. But there's a constable's copy of the Finch preliminary — unredacted except the flag — sitting in a tray that a careless officer might have left unattended, and a careless officer might have photocopied it, and that photocopy might find its way into a bookshop that sells, I don't know, true crime."

"Wren."

"I was never here. You were never here. And Noor —" her voice dropped, and for a moment the careful flatness cracked and something younger and angrier showed through — "Hollis gave me biscuits my first week. He remembered my name. Whatever you're doing, do it fast. Because whoever is closing these files isn't finished, and the next name on that list might not be a clerk."

The line went dead.

Noor stood in her empty shop with the evening coming down outside and the streetlamp buzzing on, and she let the grief and the rage finish their journey and meet somewhere in the middle of her chest, where they fused into a single dense, cold, load-bearing thing. Not fury that burned. Fury that *built*. The kind you could put a wall on.

Four people. A fall, a fire, two drownings. Witnesses to a lie, erased one by one, each file shut like a door in a corridor, and behind the last door — behind all the doors — a portrait of a dead woman smiling with grief that was too modern, and a family that owned the town, and a lawyer who had paid Noor triple to stand in front of the whole machinery as a human shield.

They thought she was their weapon.

They were about to find out what she'd been sharpening.

She turned the sign on the door to CLOSED, though it was hours early, and went upstairs, and took from the bottom of her wardrobe the box she hadn't opened since she'd left the force: the favor markers, the contacts, the old case notes, the tools of a trade she'd sworn off. She set them on the kitchen table like a surgeon laying out instruments.

First, the Anchor Rooms, before Emrys ran again. Then the photocopy Wren had "never" made.

And then Corinne Blake was going to learn the difference between hiring a detective and aiming one.

Noor put on her coat. Outside, the first rain of autumn began to fall, soft and patient, and she thought of Hollis's voice — *bureaucracy is a kind of weather, you dress for it* — and she dressed for it, and went out into the dark.
