## 22. The Portrait in the Window

The bell above the door of Fairweather & Sons had been broken for three years, and Noor had never fixed it, because the silence it made when someone entered had become a kind of privacy she treasured. Now, of course, people came in specifically to hear nothing at all.

"Excuse me," said a woman in a raincoat far too cheerful for the weather, "is this the bookshop where the detective works? The one from the Vale case?"

Noor looked up from her ledger, where she had been performing the ancient ritual of pretending the numbers were better than they were. They were, actually. That was the strange part. The numbers had turned honest around the same time the newspapers did.

"That depends," Noor said. "Are you here to buy a book, or to buy me?"

The woman laughed. "A book. And maybe a look."

"A look is free. Books cost money. I know which one I prefer you choose, but I'm professional enough not to say so out loud."

The woman bought two — a mystery novel Noor had once described as "competent" and a slim history of forgery that Noor kept stocked for exactly this sort of customer — and then, on her way out, stopped in front of the front window and stood there for a long moment.

The portrait hung there now, where the display of discounted atlases used to sit. It was unlabeled. It was, in fact, deliberately resistant to labeling: no title card, no artist's name, no date. Just a woman of perhaps forty, seated in a chair that seemed slightly too formal for her posture, wearing a dark green dress and an expression that could only be described as *amused patience* — the look of someone who has just heard a joke she intends to repeat later, privately, to someone who will appreciate it properly.

Her name was Lavinia Vale. She had been dead for thirty years, and alive again for about eight months, depending on how you counted such things.

"Who's the artist?" the woman asked.

"It's a story about what people survive," Noor said.

The woman waited, clearly expecting more. Noor returned to her ledger with the serene insolence of a woman who had learned, over one long terrible autumn, exactly how much information was worth and exactly when to stop giving it away.

* * *

The trouble with fame, Noor had decided, was that it arrived precisely when you'd stopped wanting anything, and therefore couldn't be traded away. For twenty years she had run this shop the way a lighthouse keeper runs a lighthouse: faithfully, invisibly, waiting for storms that mostly didn't come. Then the Vale case broke — *ledgers, recordings, sketches, four deaths, one dynasty* — and suddenly Fairweather & Sons was on walking tours. Actual walking tours. A man with a clipboard had appeared in June and charged tourists five pounds apiece to stand outside and point at her window while saying the words "and this is where the detective planned the sting that brought down Aldous Vale." He had not asked permission. He had not offered a cut. Noor had gone out, listened to his entire speech, corrected three factual errors, and then charged him rent.

He paid it. Business, she reflected, was simply detection with better manners.

The shop itself had changed in small ways. There was a new armchair by the poetry section, because customers kept lingering and her old knees kept objecting to standing. There was a shelf labeled *TRUE CRIME (TASTEFULLY)*, which sold absurdly well and which Noor restocked with the grim satisfaction of a woman monetizing her own trauma. And there was the portrait, which she dusted every morning with a soft cloth Emrys had left behind, along with instructions so detailed they ran to four pages and included a diagram.

She followed none of the instructions except the dusting. But she kept them in the drawer under the till, because some things you keep even when you don't need them. Especially when you don't need them.

* * *

On Thursdays, Mrs. Okafor came in to argue about fiction.

This had been happening since before the case, back when the shop was failing and Mrs. Okafor's arguments were the only regular income of conversation Noor could count on. Nothing about the arrangement had changed except its frequency; success had given Mrs. Okafor more ammunition.

"You've got a signed first edition of *The Glass Harbour* behind the counter," she said, setting down her bag like a duelist laying out pistols. "I've seen it. Don't deny it."

"I don't deny it. It's not for sale."

"Everything's for sale. You're a shopkeeper."

"I'm a retired detective who happens to sell books. It's a different profession with overlapping equipment." Noor slid the ledger closed. "And that edition belonged to a county records clerk named Thomas Wrenn, who was murdered for doing his job correctly, and it's staying behind my counter until I decide otherwise."

Mrs. Okafor absorbed this. She was one of perhaps six people in town who knew the full story — not the newspaper story, but the true one, with the drowned archivist and the flophouse and the night two professionals had come through a safehouse window and very nearly ended everything. She knew because Noor had told her, once, over tea, in the flat voice reserved for the worst chapters.

"Thomas Wrenn," Mrs. Okafor said quietly. "Yes. All right. Keep your book." She paused. "Though if you ever change your mind—"

"I won't."

"—I'd offer a fair price."

"Get out of my shop, Delia."

Mrs. Okafor smiled, bought a novel she would complain about next week, and left. Through the window, Noor watched her pause in front of the portrait, as everyone did, and tilt her head, as everyone did, trying to work out what the woman in green found so funny.

Noor knew. She'd never say. Some jokes are between the painter and the dead.

* * *

The trial had lasted eleven days and ended the way trials end when the evidence includes hidden ledgers, a recorded confession attempt, a torn jacket traced to a security firm, and the voluntary testimony of a lawyer who had realized, rather late, that she was climbing a ladder being sawed off below her. Corinne Blake had taken the stand in a grey suit and given testimony so precise it sounded rehearsed, which it was, because Noor had rehearsed her — not the facts, those were Corinne's own, but the spine of them, the order, the places to hold firm.

"You coached her," the prosecutor had accused Noor afterward, half admiring.

"I interviewed her," Noor had said. "Coaching implies I told her what to say. I merely suggested she tell the truth in an order that survived cross-examination. There's a difference. It's the difference between architecture and forgery."

Aldous Vale had gone down without theatrics, which somehow made it worse — a man in his seventies listening to a verdict with the expression of someone reviewing a disappointing quarterly report. Marcus had wept, but only, Noor suspected, because he'd been caught. The security men had gotten years apiece. The family's holdings had been frozen, auctioned, scattered. The Ashcombe gallery had closed its doors within a month, unable to survive the discovery that its centerpiece was a lie painted by a man who was, inconveniently for everyone, still breathing.

Emrys Vale had not attended the verdict. By then he was already someone else.

* * *

His letters came every few weeks, postmarked from a coastal town whose name changed periodically, though the handwriting never did. Small, careful script, the letters of a man who had spent years signing other people's names and had developed, as compensation, an almost religious precision with his own.

*Dear Noor,* the latest began.

*The studio has a window now. An actual window, facing actual water. I restored a seascape last week for a woman who cried when she picked it up — her mother had owned it before the flood took the original frame. Honest work. Honest paint. I mixed the varnish myself and it behaved.*

*I read about the shop. The article called you "the bookseller-detective," which I suspect annoys you, and quoted your line about what people survive, which I suspect annoys you more, because you said it to me first and now it belongs to strangers.*

*Marcus wrote to me from prison. Did you know that? Three pages. Half apology, half threat, all Marcus. I burned it unread. Well — I read it, obviously, and then I burned it. Some rituals matter even when they're dishonest.*

*Lavinia sends her regards. You'll understand that sentence however you need to.*

*— E.*

Noor read the letter twice, folded it into the drawer beneath the till — alongside the varnish instructions and Thomas Wrenn's book and the torn scrap of jacket she'd never quite thrown away — and went back to work.

* * *

Autumn came the way it always did to the town: sideways, with rain that fell at insulting angles. Business stayed good anyway. On a Tuesday afternoon in October, a young man came in shaking water off his coat and asked, in the tone of someone completing a dare, whether the famous portrait in the window was "the real fake or the fake real."

"Neither," Noor said. "It's the true one."

"The true—" He frowned. "But the Vale portrait was the fake."

"The Vale portrait was a forgery of a painting that never existed. This is a painting of a woman who did. Work it out."

He worked it out slowly, the way young people do when they've grown up on stories where everything is either authentic or counterfeit. He stared at Lavinia for a long time. The rain ticked against the glass above her head.

"She looks like she knows something," he said finally.

"She knows several things. Most of them funny. One of them is why she's hanging in a bookshop instead of a gallery."

"Why is that?"

"Because galleries charge admission, and the truth should be free to anyone willing to stand in the cold and look." Noor rang up his purchase — he'd grabbed a paperback at random, a western, entirely wrong for him, but Noor had long ago stopped rescuing people from their reading choices; the wrong book teaches you something the right one can't. "That's four fifty."

At the door he turned back. "Is it true the detective lives above the shop?"

"No comment."

"Is it true she keeps a gun in the till?"

"No comment."

"Is it true she solved the whole thing because a stranger paid her too much money to prove a murder hadn't happened yet?"

Noor set down the pen. Outside, the rain had softened into something almost polite. In the window, Lavinia sat in her too-formal chair with her amused patience, watching the street watch her.

"That part," Noor said, "is true. Though the version they tell leaves out that I nearly died twice, lost a friend to paranoia for three weeks, and burned a perfectly good kettle during the stakeout. Fame is a summary. Summaries are a kind of forgery too — they leave out the varnish."

"What varnish?"

"The parts that make it look finished." She smiled. "Come back when you've read the western. Tell me if I was right about you."

* * *

Closing time. The ritual was unchanged after all these months: lights off in the back rooms first, then the till counted twice, then the long walk past the shelves to the front door, past the window, past her.

Noor stopped, as she always stopped, in front of the portrait.

Eight months of dusting, and she still noticed new things. Today it was the hands — how Emrys had painted them relaxed but ready, a woman's hands that had signed documents and held grief and possibly, once, thrown something at a Vale. The original commission, decades ago, had wanted a saint. What Emrys had eventually delivered — first as forgery, then, in this version, painted from memory in a rented room while the world hunted him — was a person. That was the whole crime, really, and the whole redemption, compressed into oil and canvas: the family had needed a symbol, and Emrys had given them, in the end, a human being.

"They'd have hated this one most," Noor told her. "It's the only version they couldn't use."

Lavinia, naturally, said nothing. She just went on knowing what she knew.

Noor locked the door, flipped the sign, and climbed the stairs to the flat, where the kettle — a replacement, unburned — waited faithfully. Through the window above the sink she could see the street going dark, the tour guide's clipboard crowd long dispersed, the town settling into its ordinary evening, which was, she had come to believe, the entire point of everything she'd done. Not justice as spectacle. Justice as the restoration of ordinary evenings.

The phone buzzed on the counter. A message from an unknown number, no greeting:

*Ms. Fairweather. My name is Iris Chen. I'm a researcher at the National Gallery. We've recently acquired a painting with provenance problems, and your name came up — more than once, and always with the word "discreet" attached. Would you be available to consult?*

Noor looked at the message for a long moment. Then at the drawer under the stairs, where a certain file lived. Then out the window, toward the coast she couldn't see but could easily imagine: a studio with an honest window, a man mixing honest varnish, a life assembled carefully from chosen pieces.

Retired, she reminded herself. Retired detectives who take consultations are like reformed forgers who sketch for fun. Everyone knows how it ends.

She typed: *Tell me about the painting. And Ms. Chen — how much are you prepared to overpay me?*

Somewhere below, in the dark shop, Lavinia laughed silently at whatever joke she'd been saving.

Noor put the kettle on.
