## 14. Why She Stayed Gone

Ruth Ellery had spent thirty years learning how to hold a secret the way one holds a hot coal—carefully, at arm's length, with a smile that said *nothing to see here*. Now, sitting in her own kitchen with Jonah Okafor across the table and the letters spread between them like a hand of cards she had been losing for decades, she found that the coal had finally burned through her palms.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Rain worked at the window in long, patient fingers. Outside, the cathedral rose against the gray afternoon, its tower half-dissolved into cloud, and Ruth thought: *thirty years I have looked at that tower and thought of it as a refuge. When did it become a wall?*

"You knew," Jonah said. Not accusing. That was the worst of it—the gentleness. The archivist's voice, soft as tissue paper laid over something broken.

"I knew." She wrapped her hands around her mug because they wanted to do something else—tear the letters, or reach across the table and shake him, or weep. "I have known since October of 1994. I have known longer than I have known my own ordination date. There are things a body remembers better than calendars."

"Tell me."

*Tell me.* As if it were simple. As if the story were a drawer she could open and lay out in order. But Ruth was a deacon; order was her trade too, in its way—order of service, order of prayer, the ordered procession of Sundays marching through a life. And so she did what she always did when the truth was too large to speak whole.

She began at the beginning.

* * *

"Maren Voss was not a caretaker's daughter," Ruth said. "She was a caretaker's daughter the way I am a deacon—which is to say, it was true, and it was also the smallest thing about her."

The rain filled the pause. Jonah waited. He was good at waiting; she'd noticed that about him from his first week at St. Brannoc's, cataloguing donations with the patience of a man planting an orchard he would never sit under.

"We grew up on the same street, you know. Before the close. Her father kept the cathedral grounds and my mother cleaned for the Pikes, of all people—God has a sense of humor, though I've stopped finding it funny. Maren and I used to climb the buttresses on summer evenings, when the stone held the day's heat like a loaf holds warmth. She'd sit up there with her legs dangling over the drop and read aloud to me. Poetry, mostly. Rilke. She said angels were just attention, perfected." Ruth laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "I became a deacon partly because of her, if you want the truth. All that talk about attention. About looking closely at the world as a form of prayer."

"And then 1994."

"And then 1994." Ruth set down the mug. Her hands were not steady, but they were steady enough. "You have to understand what that autumn was. Aldous Pike was buying up the river parcels—half the town didn't even know it was happening, it moved through solicitors and shell names like water through sand. And there was a man named Edmund Harrow who stood in his way. The verger. Before your time, before Silas took the bells full-time. Edmund had documents—old deeds, boundary papers, things Pike needed buried. And Edmund trusted people. It was his great flaw and his great grace, trusting people."

Jonah's breath caught. She watched him arrive at it—the way he arrived at everything, one careful step at a time, like a man crossing ice. "The former verger. Silas said—"

"Yes. Silas told you the name. I'm telling you what Maren saw." Ruth looked down at the letters, at the topmost envelope, postmarked eleven months ago, addressed in handwriting she had loved since she was nine years old. "Maren was at the fete that night. Everyone says so, and everyone is right—that's the elegance of the lie, isn't it? She *was* at the fete. She left early. She went back to the cathedral to fetch her brother's coat—he'd torn his sleeve and was crying about it, he was only fourteen—and she came around the east cloister and she saw Pike and Edmund Harrow by the crypt stair, and she saw what happened next."

"She saw him killed."

"She saw him killed." The words tasted like ash and iron. "And then she ran, and she hid, and by morning she understood two things with perfect clarity. First: no one would believe her. A caretaker's daughter, no father living, a reputation for fancifulness—Pike would have her painted as hysterical before the ink dried on any statement. And second—" Ruth's voice failed, and she pressed on through the failure, because she had pressed on through worse. "Second: Pike knew someone had seen. He didn't know who. And he started asking questions. Quiet ones. And the second person he asked about was Tam."

"Her brother."

"Her brother." Ruth nodded slowly. "Fourteen years old. Sweet as new bread, that boy. He mowed the churchyard for pocket money. Pike sent a man to speak to him—not a threat, nothing so crude—a friendly man, offering Tam work, asking after his sister, mentioning how families can be broken up by trouble, how boys without parents end up in care if their guardians prove... unstable. Maren heard about it within a day. And she made her choice before the week was out."

"She disappeared."

"She disappeared. Or rather—" Ruth corrected herself, and the correction cost her something visible, a flinch behind the eyes, "—she *left*. Disappearing is what happens to you. Leaving is what you do. She left so that Pike would stop looking near Tam. A missing woman draws searches; a missing woman with a younger brother draws *questions*, and every question aimed at Tam was a danger to him. So she cut herself loose from the town entirely, clean as a sail from its mooring, and she let the current take the story where it would. Runaway. Ungrateful girl. Gone off with someone. The town supplied its own cruelty; she only had to stay quiet and let it."

"And you helped her."

"I drove her to the coast road at dawn in my mother's car. I gave her the money from my confirmation savings—forty pounds, it was all I had in the world, and she cried over it and I told her to stop being sentimental, which was a lie, because I cried too, after." Ruth's mouth twisted. "And then I came back to St. Brannoc's and I stood in the nave and I lied to God, which is a strange thing to do in a building where He is supposedly everywhere, and I have been lying to Him every Sunday since, or so it has felt. Thirty years of sermons preached by a woman standing on a trapdoor."

* * *

Jonah turned one of the letters toward himself, carefully, by its edge, the way he handled everything—vellum, ash, other people's hearts.

"The early ones are hers," he said. "I compared them against the diary. The slant of the *g*, the way she crosses her *t*s low. It's her hand."

"For the first ten years, yes." Ruth drew a breath that seemed to come from somewhere below her ribs. "Every year, on the feast of St. Brannoc—always that day, never her birthday, too traceable—a letter would come. No return address. Postmarks from all over: Inverness, once. Cork. A village in Brittany whose name I had to look up. She never said where she was. She never could, and I understood that—it wasn't secrecy for its own sake, it was survival. If I didn't know, I couldn't be made to know. But she wrote about Tam. Every letter, she asked after Tam, and I wrote back through a poste restante box in Bristol that she checked maybe twice a year, and I told her he'd finished school, that he'd apprenticed as a joiner, that he'd married a kind woman named Sofia and had two girls. And each year she wrote back and said the same thing in different words: *next year. Next year I'll come home.*"

"But she didn't."

"But she didn't." Ruth stood, abruptly, needing her body to do something while her voice did this other thing. She went to the window. The rain had thickened; the cathedral was a smudge now, a charcoal ghost of itself. "Do you know what hope does, Jonah, when it's rationed? One letter a year. Three hundred words, perhaps, to last three hundred and sixty-five days. You read them until the paper goes soft. You memorize the postmark. You learn the weight of an envelope by heart so that when the thin season comes—February, March, the long gray stretch—you can hold it and remember that somewhere, in some northern town or foreign street, Maren Voss is alive and drinking tea and reading Rilke, and the world has not entirely eaten her."

She turned back. Her face in the gray light looked older than it had an hour ago, older than it had in years.

"After year ten, the letters changed. I didn't see it. God forgive me, I *wanted* not to see it. The handwriting was the same—I'd know that hand blindfolded, I thought—but the letters got shorter. Safer. They stopped asking real questions. Year twelve, the letter mentioned a café in Inverness that I later learned had closed two years before. Year fifteen, she called me 'Ruthie'—and Maren never called me Ruthie, that was my mother's name for me, Maren teased me about it exactly once and I hated it. Small things. Wrongnesses, like a note played flat in a hymn you've sung ten thousand times. And I ignored every one of them, because the alternative was to believe that the letters had stopped, and if the letters had stopped, then either Maren was dead, or—"

"Or someone else was writing them."

"Or someone else was writing them." Ruth came back to the table and sat, and her hands lay flat on the wood as if pinning something down. "It was Pike. It had to be. Somewhere along the line he learned that Maren lived—that she'd surfaced enough to send mail—and he understood what I apparently hadn't: that those letters weren't comfort. They were a leash. As long as Maren believed her brother was safe and her friend was faithful, she stayed away. Stayed hidden. Stayed *silent*. And the moment she wavered—every year she wavered, she told me so, every single year she nearly bought the ticket home—the letter would arrive, perfectly timed, warm and vague and just loving enough, and it would say, between its lines, what it never said on its face: *stay gone. He's fine. Don't test us.*"

Jonah was very still. "You're saying he weaponized her hope."

"I'm saying he understood her better than I did, in one terrible particular." Ruth's eyes stung, and she let them. "He knew that Maren Voss would spend thirty years in exile to keep one boy safe. He didn't need to hunt her. He only needed to write to her. Hope was the cage, Jonah. He built it out of her own love, one envelope a year, and posted it first class."

The rain drummed. Somewhere in the house a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"And Tam?" Jonah asked softly. "Did he ever—"

"He never knew. Not until this month, not until Priya came knocking with her fierce little face and her questions. He's fifty now. He has daughters older than Maren was when she left. And when I told him—last Tuesday, in this kitchen, sitting where you're sitting—he put his head down on the table and wept like the boy he was when she left, and then he lifted his head and said, 'Is she coming home?' And I couldn't answer him. Because I don't know. Because for thirty years I have not known whether the woman writing to me was my friend or a forgery, and now the diaries are stolen and returned with pages missing, and Pike knows something is moving, and—" Ruth stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Drew the deacon back up over the woman like a vestment. "Forgive me. This is not composure."

"It doesn't need to be."

"No," she said. "Perhaps that is the sermon, finally. After thirty years of preaching composure." She looked at the letters, all of them, the true and the false lying together in their neat chronological row, indistinguishable now except to the trained eye—and even the trained eye had needed the diaries to be sure. "Here is what I understand, Jonah, and what I want you to carry out of this kitchen. Maren did not stay gone because she was a coward. She stayed gone because love, in the wrong hands, becomes a chain, and hers were the wrongest hands in the county. Every year she nearly came back. Every year something—or someone—pulled her back to the far shore. Thirty years. Do the arithmetic of it sometime, when you can bear to. Thirty years of Sundays. Fifteen hundred days of almost."

* * *

When Jonah rose to leave, he paused at the door with the rain behind him, and Ruth saw the question form in him before he spoke it—the archivist's question, the one that was really the only question left.

"The forged letters," he said. "The last three. Do you still have the envelopes?"

"Every one. Why?"

"Postmarks," Jonah said quietly. "Dates. If someone was writing to Maren from inside this town—if Pike or whoever he used posted them locally, on or near the feast day, every year for twenty years—then there's a pattern. And patterns," he added, with the faint, grim light she had seen in him once before, in the archive, holding the diary, "are what I do."

He stepped out into the rain. Ruth stood in the doorway watching him go, watching the cathedral loom up gray and enormous through the wet, and she thought of Maren at nine years old on the buttresses, reading Rilke to the wind: *angels are just attention, perfected.*

Someone had paid a great deal of attention to Maren Voss for thirty years. And none of it had been holy.

Ruth closed the door, gathered the letters into her arms—all of them, true and false alike, because they were all she had of her—and began, for the first time, to count the postmarks.
