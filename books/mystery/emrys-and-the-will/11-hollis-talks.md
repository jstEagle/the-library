## 11. Hollis Talks

Hollis Greer had spent seventy-one years learning how to keep his mouth shut, and he'd gotten so good at it that most days he didn't even notice the effort anymore. It was like carrying a stone in your coat pocket. After a while you forgot it was there. You just walked a little tilted, and everyone in Hollow Creek assumed that was just how Hollis stood.

The break-in at Vesper Delacroix's place had knocked the stone loose.

He heard about it at Dot's Diner on Thursday morning, same as everybody else, because news in Hollow Creek traveled faster than the coffee. Earl Prewitt was holding court by the window, saying somebody had torn up the podcast lady's studio and taken nothing, which Earl found personally offensive.

"Who breaks into a house and steals *nothing*?" Earl demanded, slapping the counter for emphasis. "That's not burglary. That's a *message*."

"That's what I said," said Dot, refilling his cup without being asked, because Earl's opinions required lubrication.

Hollis sat two stools down with his eggs going cold and felt the stone in his pocket turn over. Because he knew exactly what kind of message got sent by taking nothing. He'd helped send one himself, once, sixty-odd years ago, though the message back then had been delivered with a shovel instead of a crowbar.

He ate his eggs anyway. A man his age learned not to waste a good breakfast on a bad conscience. But when he got home to the little house on Tannery Road—the one with the rusted truck out front and the wind chime made of old horseshoes that nobody had ever heard ring, because the wind around Hollis's place seemed to have better manners than that—he sat down in his chair by the window and did not get up for a long while.

The phone sat on the side table like a dare.

He'd been thinking about the Delacroix girl for three days now. Ever since she'd come out to interview him about the bicentennial reburial, all polite questions and careful eyes, and he'd opened his fool mouth about Augustus Emrys being buried twice before his grandson walked in and Hollis had snapped shut like a tackle box. He'd watched her face when he clammed up. She hadn't been angry. She'd looked at him the way you look at a dog tied up too long in a hot yard—like she wanted to help but knew better than to reach over the fence.

And then somebody had gone through her things and left them in order but wrong, and every instinct Hollis had—the ones that had kept him alive and quiet through two generations of Emrys money—told him the clock was running now. Not for her first. For him. Old men who talked were a problem. Old men who almost talked were worse.

He picked up the phone before he could talk himself out of it, which was the only way Hollis Greer had ever done anything brave: fast, before the sensible part of him woke up.

She answered on the second ring. "This is Vesper."

"It's Hollis Greer." His voice came out gravelly. "You still want to know what I buried twice?"

There was a pause on the line, and he could hear her sitting up straighter, could practically hear the recorder coming off the shelf.

"Mr. Greer," she said carefully, "I want that more than I've wanted anything in three years of doing this show."

"Well." He cleared his throat. "Then you'd best bring that little machine of yours and come sit on my porch, because I'm not saying any of this indoors. Walls in this town have ears, and mine are no exception. My walls especially. I built 'em myself in '68 and I've regretted the acoustics ever since."

* * *

She came at four o'clock, and she came alone, which he appreciated, and she brought a plate of cookies from Dot's, which he appreciated even more. Oatmeal raisin. She'd remembered from the first interview that he liked oatmeal raisin and disliked being given chocolate chip by people who assumed everybody liked chocolate chip. That kind of remembering told Hollis something about a person. It told him she listened even when she wasn't recording.

They sat on the porch swing, which creaked in protest at both their combined weights, and Hollis held his cookie like a man holding a rosary.

"You understand," he said, "that what I'm about to tell you, I've never told a living soul. Not my wife, God rest her, not my boy, not my grandson who thinks I'm half senile and is mostly right. The only reason I'm telling you is that whoever went through your place last night is the same breed of people who put me to work with a shovel when I was fourteen years old, and I'd rather set this down while I've still got the wind to do it."

Vesper set the recorder between them on the swing. The red light glowed steady. "Take whatever time you need, Mr. Greer."

"Hollis," he said. "A man confessing ought to be on a first-name basis with his priest, and I expect you're as close as I'm going to get."

The porch swing creaked. Somewhere down the road a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Summer of 1961," Hollis began. "I was fourteen, working summers for my uncle Dewey, who was the caretaker for the Emrys family before anybody remembers there ever being an Emrys family worth taking care of. Augustus was still alive then. Old, but alive, and mean the way only a rich man can be mean—quiet about it, patient, like frost coming up through the ground. You never saw it arrive. You just woke up one morning and everything green was dead.

"Now, the official story—and you'll find it in every book and plaque in this county—is that Augustus Emrys died in March of 1974 and was laid to rest in the family mausoleum with full ceremony, founder of Hollow Creek, benefactor, saint of the ledger book, all of it. And that part's true as far as it goes. He did die in '74. He does lie in that mausoleum. Or he did, until somebody emptied him out last week, which frankly surprised me less than it surprised anybody else in this town."

Vesper leaned forward. "But?"

"But that mausoleum already had a floor under its floor," Hollis said, "and I know because I helped pour it."

He took a breath, and the stone in his coat pocket—he wasn't wearing a coat, but the weight was there all the same—shifted again.

"In the summer of '61, my uncle woke me before dawn. No explanation. We drove out to the cemetery with a wagonload of tools and a coffin covered in canvas, and there were four other men there I recognized from church and none of whom would look at each other. Augustus Emrys was standing off by the trees in a black coat in July heat, sweating through his collar, and he never said a word the whole time. Uncle Dewey gave the orders. We dug beneath where the mausoleum foundation was going to go—we weren't building the mausoleum yet, mind you, we were digging *under* where it would stand—and we lowered that coffin down, and we poured concrete over it, and then we built the real floor on top, thirteen years later, when the old man finally died and they needed somewhere to put him that wasn't under it."

Vesper's voice was very quiet. "Whose coffin was it?"

"I don't know." Hollis held up a hand before she could press. "I don't, and I've had sixty years to wonder, and I'll tell you what I do know, which is maybe worse. I know it was heavy for one man. I know it was longer than a child's box and shorter than a grown man's needs, if you follow me, and I know that when we lowered it, Uncle Dewey crossed himself, and Dewey hadn't crossed himself since Korea. And I know that Augustus Emrys watched us fill that hole the way a man watches a fire he started—close, and cold, and making sure every last ember was covered."

The recorder's red light burned on. A moth had begun bumping against the porch lamp, patient and stupid, and Hollis found he envied it.

"My uncle made me swear on my mother's grave that night. Said the ground under the Emrys name was sacred ground and what went into it stayed in it, and that a Greer who talked was a Greer who'd find work hard to come by in this county. And he was right. Man doesn't dig graves in Hollow Creek for forty years without understanding whose dirt he's standing in." Hollis turned the cookie over in his fingers. "I kept the oath. I hated keeping it. There's a difference, and I expect you know it."

"I do," Vesper said softly.

"Here's the part that's been eating me since your episode twelve, though. Since I heard that lullaby on your tape." He watched her go very still, and nodded, satisfied. "Yes, I listen to your show. Every week. I'm old, not dead, and I like hearing people ask questions I'm too cowardly to answer. Anyway. You asked me once whether Augustus was buried twice, and I shut my mouth when my grandson came in. But that's not actually the thing I've been carrying. The coffin—that's a secret. This next part is a *witness*."

He set the cookie down. Some things you couldn't say while holding food; his mother had taught him that, along with everything else worth knowing.

"Spring of 1976. Two years after the funeral. I was twenty-nine, married, still digging graves for the county. And I went into the records office downtown to pull a deed—some plot transfer, I don't recall—and there was a young woman at the filing cabinets. Dark hair, glasses, cardigan buttoned wrong, which I noticed because my wife did the same thing every blessed morning and I'd stopped telling her. And she turned around to hand me the file, and I knew her face."

Vesper had stopped breathing. Hollis could hear it—not the silence of someone waiting, but the silence of someone afraid to move.

"Elena Voss," he said. "Alive. Filing deeds in the county records office, plain as Sunday, two years after every soul in this town believed she'd run off or died in the mountains or been carried off by the same bad luck that follows people who cross the Emrys family. And here's the thing, Miss Delacroix—she knew I knew. I saw it land in her eyes, that flicker, the deer-in-the-lantern look. And she looked at me—long, and level—and she didn't run, didn't scream, didn't deny it. She just said, real quiet, 'You're Dewey's nephew. You were there.' And I said yes, ma'am. And she said, 'Then you know why I'm nobody.' And I took my deed and I left, and I never spoke of it, and I never saw her again—or I didn't know I was seeing her again, which comes to nearly the same thing."

"What do you mean?" Vesper whispered. "You saw her later?"

Hollis looked out at the darkening yard, at the horseshoe chime hanging silent in the windless evening, and felt the last of the stone slide out of his pocket and land between them on the porch boards.

"For forty years," he said, "there's been a woman working in the basement of the courthouse. Archivist. Ruth Calloway. Kind as they come, keeps to herself, knows where every body in this county's history is buried—figurative and otherwise. And I'll tell you something I've never said out loud, because saying it makes it real and real things demand things of you." He turned to face Vesper fully, and his old voice steadied itself the way a hand steadies on a rail. "Ruth Calloway has Elena Voss's hands. Same hands, same way of squaring a paper against the edge of the desk before she files it. I watched those hands file a deed in 1976 and I've watched them file land records every year since, and a man who digs graves for a living learns that faces change but hands remember. And last month, when I brought her a box of my uncle Dewey's old papers, she hummed a tune while she worked. A lullaby. And I have heard that exact lullaby exactly one other place in my whole long life."

Vesper's face had gone white in the porch light. "Where," she said. Her voice cracked on the single syllable. "Where else?"

"On a tape," Hollis said gently, "in a courthouse basement, behind a young woman asking questions about land records. Your episode twelve, Miss Delacroix. The background voice. I'd bet this whole property—and it isn't much, but it's mine—that the woman humming on your recording and the woman filing papers in 1976 and Ruth Calloway are one and the same. Elena Voss didn't die in the mountains. She filed herself away in the one place in this town nobody ever looks. The past."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The moth gave up on the lamp and drifted off toward the yard. Then Vesper reached over and clicked off the recorder, and her hands were shaking.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Why now? Why me?"

Hollis considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty after hauling sixty years of silence out into the open air.

"Because somebody ransacked your home and took nothing," he said, "and I know what that means, because I was on the giving end of that kind of message once, and I know the men who give it don't stop until the message is received. Because you're the only person in fifty years who's asked the right questions in the wrong town, and I figure either you finish this or they bury you alongside the rest of it, and I'm too old and too tired to watch that happen. And—" he paused, and the gentleness in his own voice surprised him, "—because when you played me that lullaby question on your show, I hummed along before I knew I was doing it. My mother used to sing it. Half this valley's mothers used to sing it. But Ruth Calloway *donated* the songbook it comes from to the library, in 1979. I checked, after your episode. Walked down there myself and pulled the accession card with my own two eyes."

Vesper stared at him. "You checked."

"I'm old, not slow." He allowed himself a small, dry smile—the first one all evening. "There's a difference, and I expect you know that too."

She laughed—a short, unsteady sound, half sob—and pressed her palms flat against her knees. "If you're right, Mr. Greer—if Ruth Calloway is Elena Voss—then she's the witness. She's the living witness to a murder, hidden in the courthouse basement for forty years, and the trial starts in three weeks, and I just—" She stopped. Started again. "I just had tea with her. Last week. She asked me where I grew up."

"How'd that feel?" Hollis asked quietly.

"Like—" Vesper frowned, hunting for the word. "Like coming home to a house I'd never been inside."

Hollis nodded slowly, and something in his chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with his heart and everything to do with secrets, which are heavier than coffins and a sight harder to lower politely into the ground.

"Well," he said, reaching for the last oatmeal raisin cookie and finding, to his mild disappointment, that she'd eaten it, "then I'd say you'd best get yourself back down to that courthouse basement, Miss Delacroix. And I'd say you'd best bring the lullaby with you."

* * *

After she left, Hollis sat on the porch until full dark, listening to the horseshoe chime finally stir—one soft, reluctant clang, like a bell that had forgotten its job.

He should feel lighter. Everyone said confession lightened a man, and the preachers had promised it his whole life. Instead he felt like a man who had set down a stone only to discover what the stone had been holding down.

Because he'd left one thing out. One small thing, said to a frightened girl in a records office in 1976, that he'd kept back tonight the way you keep back the last match in a wet forest.

When Elena Voss had squared that deed against the edge of the desk and handed it to him, she'd looked at him with those level, knowing eyes and said, *You were there.* And then, quieter, almost to herself, almost kindly:

*"So was I. Somebody has to be."*

And Hollis Greer, sitting alone in the dark with the truth finally out of his pocket and the night wind finally moving, understood with terrible clarity that the woman in the courthouse basement had known this day was coming for fifty years.

She hadn't been hiding from it.

She'd been waiting for someone to come find her.
