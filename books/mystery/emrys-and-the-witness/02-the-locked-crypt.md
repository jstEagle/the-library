## 2. The Locked Crypt

The chapter house smelled of beeswax and old decisions. Noor Underhill stood at the edge of the octagonal room with his petition folded into quarters in his jacket pocket, feeling the paper there like a splinter. Around the walls the medieval seats rose in their stone niches, each one carved with a throne canopy above and misericords beneath — a mermaid combing her hair, a wyvern swallowing its own tail — the small irreverences of men who had spent their lives deciding serious things and needed somewhere to rest their hands.

Dean Halloway sat in his chair at the north point, a thin man made thinner by the morning light, his fingers laced over a copy of the agenda he did not appear to be reading. Two canons flanked him, the Reverend Marcus Idloes, who was kind and half-deaf, and the Reverend Prudence Achebe, who was neither. The lay members had been excused for this item. That, Noor noted, was itself a decision.

"Mr. Underhill," Halloway said. "You wished to address the chapter."

"I did." Noor unfolded the petition and read from it, though he had written it himself and knew every clause. He had drafted it the way he catalogued everything — precisely, without adjectives, in the cathedral's house style. *Application for the supervised opening of the undercroft crypt, sealed by order of Chapter, 1987, for the purposes of archival survey and condition assessment of interred materials.* He had even attached the relevant minute numbers. He had attached, God help him, a risk assessment.

"Archival survey," Halloway repeated.

"Condition assessment," Noor said. "The crypt has been sealed for thirty-eight years. The last inventory predates modern conservation standards. There are lead coffins down there, Dean, and lead coffins in a damp environment — I'd like to know what the tide has been doing to them."

"The tide," Halloway said, "is not our concern."

It was such an odd sentence that Noor looked up from the page. The Dean had gone very still, the way a heron goes still, and Noor understood suddenly that the stillness was not calm. St. Aldhelm's stood at the city's edge with its feet practically in the estuary; the whole east end shuddered at spring tides; the crypt beneath the Lady Chapel flooded twice a year and always had, and everyone knew it, and everyone had always said so in the mild way English people discuss weather that is killing them. *The tide is not our concern* was the kind of thing a man said when the tide was precisely his concern, when he thought about it at night.

"The 1987 minute," Noor said carefully, "gives no reason for the sealing."

"The minute gives no reason," Halloway agreed, "because none was needed. Chapters seal things. It is what chapters do. It is a very old building, Mr. Underhill, and it is full of very old doors, and not every door needs to be opened simply because we have a key."

"We don't have a key," Noor said. "That's rather the point. The key is unaccounted for."

The silence that followed had texture. Noor heard the cathedral itself in it — the tick of the heating pipes, a jackdaw somewhere in the tower louvres, the distant administrative murmur of the verger's office. Canon Idloes leaned toward his neighbour and said, in the carrying whisper of the very deaf, "What's this about a key?" and Canon Achebe said, "Nothing, Marcus," in a voice that closed the subject like a drawer.

"Your petition is noted," Halloway said. "And refused."

"Dean —"

"Refused." Halloway rose, gathering his agenda, and for a moment he was close enough that Noor could see the fine tremor in the man's hands, could see that he had not slept — the skin under his eyes had the bruised translucence of tracing paper. "You've been with us four years, Mr. Underhill, and you are the best archivist this cathedral has had in a century, and I say that with real gratitude. But I am asking you — as your dean, and I hope as something more like a friend — to let the crypt stay shut. Some things are sealed for the building's sake. Some for the living's."

"And some," Noor said, "for the dead's."

Halloway stopped. For one unguarded second something moved behind his face — not anger, Noor thought. Recognition. As if Noor had quoted something, some sentence the Dean had heard before, in another voice, another century of his life. Then it was gone, shuttered, and Halloway said only, "Chapter is concluded," and left through the cloister door with his agenda rolled in his fist like a weapon he had decided not to use.

* * *

Noor did not go back to the archive. He went, instead, the long way — out into the close, where the elms were dropping their first yellow leaves across the grass, and around the east end to the place where the sea wall met the cathedral's foundations. The Lady Chapel's buttresses marched down toward the mud like the legs of some wading animal. Below them, half-hidden by a tangle of ivy and a Victorian iron railing, was the crypt's outer stair: a door of black oak banded with iron, and set into the iron, a plate where a lock had once been. The plate was there. The lock was not. Someone had removed it, long enough ago that the screw holes had rusted into soft red blooms — but the plate remained, and bolted to the stone beside it, a small brass box the size of a cigarette packet, green with verdigris. An emergency keyholder's box. He had walked past it a hundred times and never once wondered what it held.

He tried the box. It was locked too, of course, but the hinge was on the wrong side for its age; someone had replaced it. Within living memory, then, someone had stood at this door and cared about who could open it.

Noor stood back and looked up at the chapel wall, at the gargoyles worn faceless, and thought about his mother. It was a habit he was trying to be careful with — grief was a tide too, and he had learned in the last week that it did not respect the flood defences. Marion Underhill had been dead eleven years. She had brought him to this city when he was nine; she had worked, of all things, in the cathedral shop for a winter, selling postcards of the very door he was now staring at. He had a memory — he was almost sure it was a memory and not a construction — of her standing on this exact stretch of path, smoking, which she never did at home, watching the water come in over the mud with an expression he had been too young to read and had spent eleven years not thinking about.

*Some for the dead's,* he had said to the Dean, and had not known until the words were out that he was talking about her.

He went back inside. He went back, finally, to the archive.

* * *

The archive occupied the undercroft beneath the north transept: two long rooms of steel racking under a quadripartite vault, lit by fluorescent tubes and one high slit window through which the estuary light came at certain hours, thin and greenish, like something seen underwater. Noor had arranged it himself, had spent four years imposing order on four centuries of chaos, and he knew its contents the way other men knew their own handwriting.

The visitor ledgers were on bay twelve. They ran from 1893 to the present — not visitors to the cathedral, which kept its own book at the welcome desk, but visitors to the *records*: researchers, genealogists, clergy, the occasional ecclesiastical lawyer, each signing in and out, each leaving behind the small honest evidence of a name, a date, an affiliation. Noor pulled the volume for 1984–86, because 1984 was the year before the sealing and he was, he told himself, simply being thorough — establishing the context of the 1987 minute, nothing more.

The ledger fell open almost at the place. That was the first strange thing. The binding was sound; there was no reason for it to open at March 1985 rather than January. But it did, and there, in a column of signatures in the careful copperplate of a Canon's curate and the blunt capitals of a police inspector conducting what the adjacent column called *enquiries*, he found a name he had not expected to find in this book at all.

**M. Underhill.** 14 March 1985. Purpose of visit, in her own small slanted hand: *family history.*

His mother had never once, in his entire childhood, expressed the slightest interest in family history. She had answered his school-project questions about grandparents with a shrug and a change of subject so deft he had mistaken it for absence of knowledge rather than refusal of it.

He turned the page. She had signed out the same day — the columns were strict about that — but the out-signature was different. The in-signature was fluid, unhesitating. The out-signature had been written slowly, each letter separately considered, by a hand that was shaking. He had catalogued enough signatures to know the difference between haste and fear, and this was fear.

He sat down on the low stool beside bay twelve and did not move for a long time. The fluorescent tube above him buzzed at a frequency that had stopped registering years ago and now, suddenly, was the loudest thing in the world.

Then, because he was his mother's son and order was the only prayer he knew, he began to work. He pulled the ledger forward under the light and examined the page as an object. And there — in the gutter of the binding, half-hidden where the page curved into the spine — he found the scratch.

It was not ink. It was a mark cut into the paper with something sharp, a pin or a nib, the kind of blind annotation a person makes when they are pretending to write and are actually waiting. He angled the page. Letters, tiny, ragged: three of them, then a date.

*E.W. — 21.iii.85.*

Elias Wren. Twenty-first of March, 1985 — the same day his mother had signed the ledger with a shaking hand. And beneath the initials, pressed so lightly it was almost not there, a fourth mark that might have been a cross or might have been the beginning of a word someone had thought better of.

Noor photographed the page with his phone, front and raking-light, the way he would document any fragile item. His hands were steady. That surprised him. Inside, something was not steady at all; inside, the archive — his archive, his ordered, catalogued, dust-sheeted world — had quietly changed category. It was not a library any longer. It was a scene, and he was standing in the middle of it, and the evidence was in his mother's handwriting.

He thought of the letter that had arrived a week ago, postmarked the day after Elias Wren's funeral. *He did not die. He was killed. Your mother saw.* He had filed it, accession number 2025/114, in the correct folder with the correct provenance note, because that was who he was. Now he went and took it out again and read it a third time under the slit window's underwater light, and this time he did not read it as an archivist assessing a document. He read it as a son.

*Your mother saw,* the letter said. And his mother had come to this archive in March 1985 and signed her name and scratched a dead man's initials into the gutter of a ledger, and thirty-eight years later a dean who had not slept was telling him that some doors were sealed for the living's sake.

Noor put the letter back. He locked bay twelve. He stood a moment in the green light with his palm flat against the cold of the racking, and made himself a promise in the form of a catalogue entry, silent, precise, house style: *Item, one truth, location unknown, condition — buried. Action: excavation.*

Outside, far off, the estuary was turning. He could feel it in the floor, the faint tidal shudder that ran through the cathedral's stones twice a day, the building breathing with the water. Low tide would come at 4:17 that afternoon. He did not yet know why that mattered. He only knew that he would be at the sea wall when it did — and that the mud below the Lady Chapel, the mud his mother had once watched with a cigarette burning down between her fingers, was going to give something back.
