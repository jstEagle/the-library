## 4. Mother's Handwriting

The boxes had waited eleven years in the loft of the archivist's cottage, and Noor Underhill had waited almost as long to open them.

Not out of neglect — he told himself that often, climbing the ladder with a lamp in one hand, telling himself it was simply that there was never time, that cataloguing was sacred work and grief was not. But the truth was simpler and less flattering: he could not bear to touch her handwriting. His mother's hand had been a small, slanted thing, all its letters leaning east like grass in wind, and every time he saw it on an old shopping list or the flyleaf of a paperback he felt the specific vertigo of hearing a dead person's voice in an empty room.

Now the vertigo was already upon him, had been since the letter arrived postmarked yesterday from a man buried last week. Canon Elias Wren's confession sat in his archive drawer, filed under accession number 2024/031, claiming murder and naming Miriam Underhill as witness — his mother, dead these eleven years, named by a dead man as though she were merely waiting somewhere for the accusation to reach her.

So he climbed into the loft at nine o'clock on a Thursday night, when the cathedral bells had gone quiet and the tide was drawing back from the sea wall with that long, wet sigh he had heard all his life without ever listening to it.

* * *

Her effects were four boxes, cardboard gone soft at the corners, sealed with tape that had yellowed to the color of old ivory. He cut the first one open with the bone-handled knife he used for string, and the smell rose up to meet him — camphor and paper dust and something faintly sweet, like dried flowers kept too long in a closed room. Lavender. She had tucked lavender into everything. He hadn't remembered that until the smell remembered it for him.

He worked the way he always worked, because method was the only prayer he trusted. Inventory first. A cardigan, moth-eaten at the cuffs. Photographs in a biscuit tin. A sewing box with no sewing in it — instead, keys, odd buttons, a child's marble, a brass thimble worn thin at the crown. Her Bible, its spine broken at the Psalms. And beneath the Bible, wrapped in a tea towel gone stiff with age, a bundle of letters tied with green ribbon.

He knew before he untied the knot that he did not want to know what was inside. His hands untied it anyway. Hands are traitors that way; they go on doing what they have always done while the rest of you stands very still and listens to the water rising.

There were seven letters. Six of them were in her hand — drafts, unsent, written on the backs of parish circulars and the margins of hymn sheets, the paper scrounged the way paper is scrounged by people who cannot afford to buy any for the things that matter most. The seventh was different. Thicker stock. A printed letterhead he recognized instantly, because he walked past it every day of his working life: *The Dean and Chapter of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral.*

It was dated the fourteenth of March, 1987 — thirty-seven years ago, three years before Noor was born. It began *Dear Miss Okonkwo,* which stopped him cold, because his mother had been Miriam Underhill all his life, married name, gravestone name, the name carved in the same stone as his father's. But the letter's salutation did not hesitate. Whoever wrote it had known her before she was anyone's wife.

*Dear Miss Okonkwo,*

*Further to our conversation of Tuesday last, the Chapter confirms the following terms. In consideration of your signed undertaking to make no statement, public or private, concerning events within the cathedral precincts on the night of 9 February 1987, and in particular concerning the undercroft of the north transept (hereinafter "the crypt"), the Chapter will remit to you the sum of two hundred pounds per annum for so long as you shall remain resident in this parish, such payments to be made quarterly and in cash, and to cease upon any breach of the undertaking herein described.*

*We trust this arrangement will allow you to put the matter behind you, as we are all eager to do.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*H. Halloway, Secretary to the Dean and Chapter*

Noor read it three times. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below him, in the walls of the cottage, a mouse moved through the dark with the confidence of a creature that has lived in a house longer than its owner.

Halloway. Not Dean Halloway, not yet — a secretary then, a young man with a signature already practiced, already smooth. The same Halloway who, six days ago, had stood in front of Noor across a desk of polished oak and refused, with hands that would not keep still, to open the crypt. *Some doors are closed for reasons older than curiosity, Mr. Underhill.* He had said it gently. He had said it like a man reciting words someone else had taught him.

Noor set the letter down on his knee and looked at it for a long time without reading it again, letting the shape of it settle into him the way a stone settles into mud. Two hundred pounds a year. Paid quarterly. In cash. To a young woman who had seen something in the crypt on a February night and agreed to forget it.

His mother had taken the money. That was the first fact, and it arrived without mercy. He searched the drafts for some other reading — coercion, fear, a threat implied between the lines — and found them all, and found also that none of them changed the arithmetic. She had signed. She had stayed in the parish. She had raised her son forty yards from the door of the thing she had promised never to speak of, and she had banked her silence quarter by quarter like a woman putting coins in a jar.

* * *

The drafts were worse. The drafts were where she talked.

They were not letters to anyone in particular — or rather, they were letters to everyone she could not tell, addressed variously to *you*, to *E.*, once to *Father*, and twice, unbearably, to *my son, if he ever reads this*. Noor made himself read those last ones standing up, because sitting down felt too much like eavesdropping on a confession, and then he made himself sit down anyway, because a man who catalogs confessions for a living does not get to flinch from his mother's.

*I saw what I saw and no power on earth can unsee it,* she wrote in the earliest draft, the ink faded to the brown of weak tea. *They talk about the tide as if the tide were a thief acting alone. The tide took nothing the tide wasn't given.*

Another, undated but younger in the hand: *Mr. W came to see me today. He is kind, or he performs kindness very well, and either way he brought flowers, which I could not look at. He says the family has suffered enough. He means his family. He has never once asked about mine.*

And another, later, the hand shakier: *Thirty years now. I have paid my silence like rent and the house is still not mine. Some nights I stand at the sea wall and think about how easy it would be to let the water finish the job it started. Then I remember I have a boy who thinks I am a good woman, and I go home and cook his supper and let him go on thinking it.*

Noor read that line — *let him go on thinking it* — and had to put the pages face-down on the floorboards and breathe for a while at the rafters, where a spider's web hung silvered in the lamplight, patient as a net.

Because here was the thing he could not argue with, the thing that turned the betrayal over and showed him its tender underside: she had kept the drafts. She had burned nothing. A woman paid to forget had spent thirty-seven years writing down, in secret, on scraps, everything she refused to forget — and then she had hidden the record inside a tea towel in a loft above her sleeping son, close enough to find if he ever went looking, far enough away that he might never have to.

That was not the act of a coward. That was the act of an archivist. He recognized the instinct because it was his own; he had inherited it the way he'd inherited her lean toward the light when she read.

But the tenderness did not cancel the stranger. He kept turning back to the question that had no bottom: what had she seen? The drafts circled it and never landed. *I saw what I saw.* *The undercroft.* *The ninth of February.* Once, terribly: *She was so young. Younger than me. And the water was so black, and nobody screamed, which is the part I cannot explain to myself even now. Nobody screamed.*

A girl. A girl in the crypt, in the black water, and no one screaming — as if everyone present had already agreed, before the fact, that no sound would be made. As if the silence had been arranged.

And then the thought arrived, the one he had been walking around all evening the way you walk around a hole in the floor, and it arrived with the gentleness of a hand on the shoulder: *she died on the ninth of February.*

Eleven years ago. A fall on the sea wall steps, the coroner said. Wet stone, poor light, a woman of sixty-four with a heart that had carried its burdens a long time. Accidental death, misadventure, file closed. Noor had signed the forms himself with a pen that kept slipping in his sweating hand, and he had believed it because believing it was the only way to keep living in the cottage and working in the cathedral and walking past the sea wall every morning without stopping.

But the ninth of February was the anniversary. The date the crypt had its event. The date the money was, perhaps, counted out. And his mother — who had spent half her life standing at the sea wall thinking about the water, who had written *the tide took nothing the tide wasn't given* — had died on those same steps, on that same date, in the same black weather.

Coincidence was possible. Coincidence was even common; he knew the statistics better than most men, having catalogued enough parish registers to watch chance at work for centuries. But coincidence did not explain why, going through the fourth box now with hands that had begun to shake, he found at the very bottom, folded into a copy of her will, a single sheet in her hand, dated three days before her death:

*If anything happens to me, it will be called an accident. Please believe it was not. I was going to stop taking the money. I told E. so on Sunday. He was very kind about it. He was very kind about everything.*

*E.* Elias. Canon Elias Wren, whose funeral he had attended twelve days ago, whose confession had arrived postmarked yesterday naming her as witness — as if Wren had known, all along, exactly where the thread ran, and had waited until his own death to pull it.

Noor sat in the lamplight among his mother's scattered life, the drafts around him like shed skins, and understood that the woman he had mourned for eleven years had never existed — and that the woman who had existed was braver and more frightened and more compromised than any stranger he could have invented, and that he loved her more, not less, for the ledger of her sins.

Then he noticed what he should have noticed an hour ago: the seventh letter, the Chapter's letter, bore a filing mark in its top corner. A small stamped number, archival red, half worn away.

He had seen that numbering system before. It was the cathedral's own. Which meant this letter had once lived in the St. Aldhelm's archive — his archive — and had been removed, quietly, sometime in the last thirty-seven years, by someone with access.

Someone who worked there. Someone who had been erasing the paper trail one document at a time.

Noor looked at the red stamp, and beyond it, through the little loft window, at the cathedral rising black against the grey night sky, its crypt lying sealed beneath the north transept like a held breath.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would check the accession ledgers. Whatever number this letter had once carried, its removal would have left a gap — and gaps, in a well-kept archive, are the loudest things there are.
