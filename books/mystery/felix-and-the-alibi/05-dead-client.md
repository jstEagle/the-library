## 5. Dead Client

The call came at 7:42 in the morning, which was wrong on two counts.

First, nothing good ever called Meridian Cabs before eight. The night shift knew this the way sailors know weather — not as superstition but as accumulated evidence. Morning calls were complaints, cancellations, the occasional drunk who'd slept through his stop and woken up furious in a stranger's cul-de-sac. Nobody called at 7:42 with anything you wanted to hear.

Second, it came on the landline. The old copper line that rang the depot's back office, the one that had been disconnected from the booking system years ago and kept alive only because Marit refused to let them kill it. "Emergency services still use it," she'd said, though nobody had verified that in a decade.

Felix had stayed past shift end. They told themselves it was paperwork — the prepaid account needed reconciling, the odd booking from Tuesday night sitting in the system like a splinter. In truth they hadn't gone home. Home was a studio apartment above a laundromat where the dryers shook the floor at all hours and the walls were thin enough to hear the neighbor's television laugh at jokes Felix never caught. The depot at dawn, empty except for the hum of the radio base and the smell of burnt coffee, was quieter than home. That said something Felix preferred not to examine.

So Felix was there when the landline rang, and picked it up on the second ring out of pure reflex.

"Meridian Cabs."

"Is this the dispatcher who took a booking on Tuesday evening? Account number ending four-four-one-seven."

The voice was male, flat, official in the way that made Felix's spine straighten without permission. Not a customer. Not a cop, either — not yet. Something adjacent.

"This is Felix Eriksen, I took the booking. Who's asking?"

"Bergen Police District. We're trying to reach the account holder, an Astrid Holm. She listed Meridian as her contact for a standing arrangement." A pause, paper shuffling. "You have no other contact information for her?"

Felix looked at the booking screen. Cash in advance, routed through a third-party payment processor, instructions delivered by phone. No name attached until now. *Astrid Holm.* The name landed strangely, like a key cut for a different lock.

"No," Felix said. "She booked prepaid. Anonymous arrangement. We get those."

Another pause. Longer this time. When the voice came back it had changed register — softer, the way people soften their voices when they're about to hand you something heavy and don't want to drop it on your foot.

"Ms. Holm was found deceased this morning. In the harbor, near the old fish market pier. I'm sorry to inform you this way, but she listed your company, so we're obligated to notify."

The harbor. The word went into Felix like cold water goes into a shoe — slowly at first, then all at once.

"How?" Felix heard themselves ask. Their own voice sounded far away, dispatcher-flat, the professional calm they wore like a uniform. "How did she die?"

"Drowning. Appears accidental. She was seventy-one years old; there may have been a medical event. There'll be an autopsy, of course." The pause again. "Did Ms. Holm seem unwell when she made her booking? Distressed in any way?"

*There was a tremor in her voice,* Felix thought. *Like a woman holding a glass of water very carefully so it doesn't spill.*

"She seemed fine," Felix said, and was surprised to hear the lie come out smooth. "Standard booking. Nothing unusual."

They talked another ninety seconds — would police need the booking records, yes, someone would come by, could Felix confirm the depot address, yes. Then the line went dead and Felix sat in the grey morning light of the back office with the receiver still warm against their ear, listening to the dial tone like it might say something else.

Astrid Holm. Seventy-one years old. Drowned in the harbor.

The same harbor whose black water Felix had watched from the breakwater two nights ago, thinking about a woman's careful, trembling voice asking them to verify that a murder would not happen.

* * *

Felix did not remember deciding to play the recording. They simply found themselves doing it — pulling up the archived call audio on the dispatch terminal, headphones on, the office door pulled shut even though the day crew wouldn't arrive for another hour.

Tuesday, 11:47 p.m. Forty-one seconds.

*"I need a car company that works nights. Someone discreet."*

Felix listened to it once straight through. Then again. Then, on the fourth pass, they stopped trying to hear the words and started trying to hear the woman.

It wasn't just the tremor. Tremors could be cold, fear, age, too much coffee. It was the architecture underneath the tremor — the way Astrid Holm spoke in complete, considered sentences, each word selected and placed like stones across a stream. This was a person who had rehearsed. Who had maybe written it down and memorized it and then thrown the paper away. And beneath the rehearsal, when she said *before it happens* — there. A catch, small as a hairline crack in porcelain. Not hesitation about the words. Hesitation about the truth of them. The sound of someone saying a thing they desperately wanted to be wrong.

And now she was dead.

Felix took off the headphones and sat with the silence, which was not silent at all. The radio base murmured its carrier tone. Somewhere out front, a gull screamed at the dumpsters. The dryers next door hadn't started yet; the laundromat's first customers wouldn't come till nine.

Here was the thing Felix kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth:

Seventy-one-year-old women drowned in harbors sometimes. It happened. Cold water, dark pier, a stumble, a heart that had been quietly failing for years. The statistics were real. Felix knew statistics better than most people — ten years of night dispatch taught you exactly how often ordinary catastrophe dressed itself as accident.

But Felix also knew what they knew, which was this: a frightened woman had paid cash for a strange, specific service — *proof that a murder scheduled for Saturday has not yet happened* — and three days later she was floating in the harbor, and the death was already being filed under *accident* with the tidy efficiency of a form filled out in advance.

Guilt arrived then, and it arrived wearing Felix's own face.

Because here was the ledger Felix ran against themselves, item by item: They had taken the money and filed the job and felt, if anything, a flicker of amusement. A mystery client. A story for the break room. They had not asked her name. They had not asked how to reach her. They had not said, *you sound frightened — are you all right?* Ten years of listening to the city's last calls, and when a real last call came through their own line, Felix had treated it like a fare.

*"Someone has to verify it hasn't happened yet."*

Verify. She'd used the word twice. As if verification were the whole point — as if what she needed wasn't protection but witness. Someone outside the family, outside the circle, someone with no stake, holding a record that said: *on this date, she was still alive.*

And instead the record now said: *three days later, she wasn't.*

Felix pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until the dark bloomed orange. The guilt had a specific flavor, and Felix recognized it from the worst nights of their career — the night a driver had crashed on the Eidet road and Felix, reviewing the logs afterward, realized they'd had thirty seconds of static-filled warning they'd dismissed as interference. Thirty seconds. They'd carried that thirty seconds for six years like a stone sewn into their coat.

This was heavier. This wasn't thirty seconds. This was forty-one seconds of a dead woman's voice, and Felix had spent them thinking about dinner.

* * *

By nine, the day crew had drifted in, and Felix performed normalcy the way they did everything else — competently, invisibly.

Petra from accounts was at the counter arguing cheerfully with the invoice software. Old Halvard was hosing down cab fourteen, spraying the winter's salt off the wheel wells like he was baptizing it. The depot had its morning rhythm, and Felix moved inside it, answering questions, initialing forms, laughing at Halvard's joke about the seagulls having a union — all of it running on rails while behind their eyes a single image repeated: black water, a pier, a woman's careful voice saying *before it happens.*

Twice, Felix almost told Petra. Both times the words got as far as the back of the throat and stopped. Because telling Petra meant explaining the booking, and explaining the booking meant playing the recording, and playing the recording meant handing the strangeness over to daylight people who would file it under *weird customer* and move on by lunch. And Felix couldn't bear that. Couldn't bear the dead woman becoming an anecdote.

Marit arrived at ten. Felix watched her come through the door — sixty-eight years old, silver braid, the rolling gait of a woman who'd driven cabs for forty years and whose knees had filed formal complaints decades ago. Marit hung up her coat, poured her coffee — half coffee, half milk, the ratio fixed since the Nixon administration — and only then looked at Felix properly.

"You didn't go home," she said.

"I had paperwork."

"Mm." Marit drank her coffee and studied Felix over the rim with pale blue eyes that had missed nothing in forty years of reading faces through a windshield. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The one you get when the radio's been quiet too long and you're waiting for it not to be." She set the mug down. "What happened?"

And Felix — who had lied smoothly to a police officer two hours earlier, who had decided not to tell Petra, who had built an entire adult life on the principle that information shared is information lost — heard themselves say:

"We had a client. Tuesday night. Prepaid, anonymous. Strange job." A breath. "The police called this morning. She's dead, Marit. Drowned in the harbor."

Something crossed Marit's face. Felix would spend a long time afterward trying to name it, and would eventually settle on the truth: it wasn't shock. Shock arrives like a slap. What crossed Marit's face was slower and older — a recognition, a door opening onto a room she'd already been in.

For perhaps two seconds. Then it was gone, packed away with the speed of long practice, and Marit's face was just Marit's face, concerned and warm.

"Poor woman," she said. "That's terrible. Was it — how?"

"Drowning. They're calling it an accident."

"Well." Marit turned to the coffee pot and refilled her cup she didn't need refilled. "The harbor takes people. Always has. You knew her?"

"No. Never met her." Felix hesitated. "Just her voice."

Marit stood with her back half-turned, and Felix watched her shoulders — watched them come down from whatever height they'd risen to, deliberately, like someone lowering a window shade.

"These anonymous bookings," Marit said to the coffee pot. "Best thing the company ever did was start taking them, and the worst. Half the city's secrets ride in our back seats and never say a word." She turned around, and her smile was steady and kind and reached all the way to her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Don't carry it, Felix. Whatever it was. People die, and it isn't ours. That's the first rule of the night shift — we take people where they're going. We don't carry what they leave behind."

*We don't carry what they leave behind.* Marit's oldest rule. Felix had heard it a hundred times, had repeated it to new drivers, had believed it the way you believe load-bearing walls.

Standing there in the coffee smell and the morning light, watching Marit's steady smile, Felix understood for the first time that the rule was not comfort. It was instruction. It was a wall, and walls are built by people who know exactly what's on the other side.

* * *

The rest of the day, Felix worked, and the working had the quality of a man walking on ice he knows is cracking — each motion placed carefully, attention split between the surface and the sound underneath.

At noon, Felix pulled the payment records for account 4417. The processor's trail was clean, anonymized, exactly as advertised — but the deposit had originated from a bank branch on Strandgaten, downtown, and the transaction timestamp was Monday at 2:14 p.m. Astrid Holm had walked into a bank on a Monday afternoon and paid for a murder-watch on a Saturday dinner, and by Thursday morning she was in the water. Five days. She'd bought herself five days of hope, or five days of insurance, and whichever it was, it hadn't cleared.

At two, Felix searched the name. *Astrid Holm, Bergen.* Nothing current — no obituary yet, no news item, the machinery of public death not yet finished grinding. But an old municipal record surfaced, a business registration from 1998: *Holm Secretarial Services,* dissolved 2003. Before that, employment records were sealed, but the address history put her, in the early nineties, in an apartment on Kirkeveien.

Kirkeveien. The Voss house was on Kirkeveien. Felix had looked it up Wednesday night, after the booking, tracing the address of the anniversary dinner on a map the way you'd trace a scar.

A secretary who lived around the corner from the Voss estate, twenty-five years ago, who grew into a retired woman who paid cash to a taxi company to watch a family celebrate.

Felix sat back from the terminal. Around them the depot clattered on — phones, radios, Halvard swearing at the hose reel — and none of it touched the cold center of the room Felix occupied alone.

The recording was still on the terminal. Felix copied it twice: once to the encrypted drive, once to a USB stick that went into their jacket pocket, against the chest, where they could feel it like a second heartbeat. They couldn't have said why they hid it from the police. They told themselves it was caution — you don't hand your only evidence to the first officer who asks, not when the death is already stamped *accident* and everyone involved moves in circles where accidents can be arranged. That was true. But underneath the caution was something less defensible and more honest: the recording was theirs. The woman had chosen Meridian. Had chosen, God help them, *Felix* — the invisible man on the night desk, the one nobody noticed, which was presumably the point. She had handed her forty-one seconds to the least important person in the building, and Felix was not going to let those seconds be misfiled, minimized, or drowned in the same harbor she had been.

At 4:50 p.m., the landline rang again.

Felix let it ring twice, watching it, the way you watch a dog you suspect of biting. Then they picked it up.

"Mr. Eriksen. Detective Ivar Brandt, Bergen Police. We spoke briefly this morning — actually, my colleague spoke. I'm handling the Holm matter now." The voice was unhurried, gravelled, the voice of a man who had learned that silence pulls more confessions than questions. "I'll be stopping by the depot tomorrow morning. Routine follow-up. I'd like to see your booking records, and I'd like to hear, in your own words, about the conversation you had with Ms. Holm."

"There wasn't much of a conversation," Felix said. "She booked a car. That's all."

"Mm," said Brandt, in a tone that suggested he had already read the booking notes, already listened to whatever the payment processor would give him, and had already formed — correctly — the opinion that Felix Eriksen was a man choosing his words like steps across ice. "Tomorrow, then. Nine o'clock. Don't stay up late."

He hung up. Felix set the receiver down gently, precisely, the way you set down something you're afraid might go off.

Through the front window, the afternoon was dying into the long blue dusk of early spring. Out on the road, headlights were coming on one by one, and beyond the rooftops, somewhere north and east, the harbor lay black and patient, keeping what it had taken.

Felix touched the USB stick through the fabric of their jacket.

*Don't stay up late,* the detective had said. Friendly. Almost kind.

Which was exactly what a man would say, Felix thought, if he already knew you weren't going to sleep at all.
