## 7. Marit's Silence

The depot at four in the morning had a smell Felix had never managed to describe to anyone who didn't work there — burnt coffee, diesel, the sweet chemical bite of the disinfectant the cleaning crew used on the back seats, and underneath it all, the faint mineral ghost of rain carried in on every driver's coat. It was the smell of the hours when the city pretended to be empty. Felix loved it the way you love a face you've seen ten thousand times: not because it was beautiful, but because it was yours.

Marit was already in when Felix came down the stairs from the dispatch loft. That was wrong, for a start. Marit Solheim did not do early shifts anymore. She was sixty-eight, semi-retired, and her contract — negotiated with the stubbornness of a woman who had driven cabs through two recessions and one flood — was three nights a week, eleven to seven, and not one minute before eleven. Yet there she was at 4:07 a.m., sitting at the break room table with a cup of coffee she clearly wasn't drinking, staring at the vending machine as if it had said something to her.

"You're here early," Felix said, hanging their coat on the hook by the door. The hook was theirs. It had been theirs for nine years. Marit had put it there herself, with a drill, on Felix's first week, and had said, *Everyone gets one hook. That's how you know you belong somewhere.*

"Couldn't sleep." Marit's voice was gravel over ice. She turned the coffee cup a quarter rotation on the table. Turned it back.

Felix got their own coffee — the good stuff from the tin in the cupboard, not the vending machine sludge — and sat down across from her. The break room was small, four chairs and a table that had survived longer than most marriages, a corkboard dense with twenty years of photos: drivers at company parties, the crew at Bjørn's wedding, Marit holding a fish the size of a toddler on some lake up north. Felix had looked at that corkboard a thousand times. Tonight, for the first time, they looked at it and saw a wall of evidence. A wall of years. A wall of everything Marit had never said.

"You heard," Felix said.

"Heard what?" Too fast. Too flat.

"About the client. Astrid Holm. The one who prepaid the account. She's dead, Marit. Drowned in her own bathtub, and the police came to ask *me* questions about it, and I've been sitting in my flat for two days replaying her voice on my phone like a madman, and I keep coming back to one thing." Felix set their cup down. Kept their voice level, the dispatcher's voice, the one that could talk a panicking driver through a heart attack on the ring road. "The booking wasn't about a future murder. It was about the Voss anniversary dinner. Thirty years. And I keep thinking — thirty years ago is 1994. And 1994 is a year this company does not like to talk about."

The refrigerator cycled on. Somewhere in the garage, a car door thumped. Marit turned the cup again.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"Then this will be easy." Felix took out their phone, laid it on the table between them like a card played face-up. "There's an obituary from November 1994. A cab driver died that month. Hit-and-run. His name was Tomas Lindqvist, and he was twenty-four years old, and he worked nights, and the case was closed in nine days, which is not how hit-and-runs get closed in this city unless somebody very high up wanted it closed." Felix paused. "He drove for Meridian, Marit."

Marit looked at the phone. She did not pick it up. Her hands were on the table, and Felix watched them — those hands that had taught Felix the radio codes, that had wrapped a blanket around Felix's shoulders the night of the tunnel fire when Felix came back to the depot shaking and couldn't say why, that had pressed a twenty into Felix's palm every Christmas for nine years with the words *buy something useless, you're too practical* — those hands had gone very still.

"I remember him," Marit said finally. "Nice boy. Fast driver. It was a long time ago."

"It was a long time ago," Felix agreed. "So it shouldn't be hard to talk about. What happened that night?"

"Nothing happened that night. A boy got killed on the E18 by some drunk who was never found, and it was a tragedy, and we put flowers on the break room table, and life went on. That's what happens. Life goes on." She said it like a rehearsed statement. Like a deposition. Like someone who had been asked this question before and had learned the answer so well it had worn smooth as a river stone.

Felix leaned back. "Who was driving that night?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Marit's face changed — not dramatically, nothing so useful as a flinch. It was subtler than that, a draining, the color leaving her cheeks in the order Felix imagined blood leaves a body: first the warmth around the mouth, then the cheeks, then everything down to the jaw. She was pale in the fluorescent light, pale as the coffee she hadn't touched, and for one long second she looked at Felix with an expression Felix had heard a thousand times on the phone but had never once seen on the face of someone they loved. It was the expression of a person listening to a sound in the dark and trying to decide if they'd imagined it.

"I was off," she said. "I was off that night. I don't remember."

"You just said you remember him."

"I remember *him*. I don't remember the night. It was thirty years ago, Felix. I'm sixty-eight. I don't remember last Tuesday." She stood up, chair legs scraping, and carried her untouched coffee to the sink and poured it out, and the sound of it going down the drain was somehow the loudest thing Felix had heard all week. "Why are you asking me this?"

"Because you went white."

"I went white because it's four in the morning and I'm an old woman being interrogated in a break room by someone I—" She stopped. Started again, softer. "By someone I helped raise, more or less."

The word *interrogated* sat between them. Felix hadn't raised their voice. Hadn't leaned in. Hadn't done anything but ask a question in a flat, professional tone, and Marit had called it an interrogation, which meant she had heard it as one, which meant somewhere inside her the question had already been asked, many times, by someone with less patience.

"Marit." Felix kept the voice gentle now. "Astrid Holm called this company. She prepaid cash — cash, which nobody does — and she asked for verification that a murder wouldn't happen at the Voss dinner. Then she turned up dead. And the Voss dinner is in three weeks, and it's the thirtieth anniversary of something that is definitely not a wedding anniversary, because I checked, and Henrik and Ingrid Voss were married in June. The dinner is in November. November 1994. The month Tomas Lindqvist died." Felix let that breathe. "You knew him. You knew all of them. I've seen the photo, Marit."

Marit's back was to Felix, her hands braced on the sink's edge. "What photo?"

"The one in Astrid's apartment. Five young people on a dock, summer, somebody's boat. Ingrid Voss. Her husband. Dahl. A man I now know is Doctor Reinholt." Felix watched the shoulders. "And you."

The silence went on so long that Felix counted the refrigerator's hum through two full cycles.

Then Marit laughed. It was a terrible sound, a laugh with nothing in it, a laugh like a door closing. "That could be anyone. It was a hundred years ago. Everyone had a boat."

"Marit."

"*Drop it.*"

The words cracked out of her like a whip, and then she turned around, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid, all three at once, and Felix had never seen fear on Marit Solheim's face. Not during the flood, when she'd waded into the depot's flooded lower garage in chest-deep water to move the archive boxes up two flights of stairs by hand. Not when Bjørn's heart had stopped in the parking lot and Marit had done compressions for eleven minutes until the ambulance came, her face set like concrete. Not once in nine years. Felix had privately believed the woman was incapable of fear, had built a small shrine to that belief in the back of their mind, one of the load-bearing walls of their whole life.

And now here it was. Fear. On her face. Because of a question Felix had asked.

"You listen to me," Marit said, and her voice had dropped low and urgent, the voice she used on drivers who were about to do something stupid. "You drop this. You drop it tonight. You give the police whatever you have to give them and you forget the name Voss, and you forget the name Holm, and you forget you ever heard of a driver named Lindqvist. You do not go looking. You do not go to the archive. You do not—" She caught herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd said one word too many.

"The archive," Felix repeated quietly.

Marit closed her eyes. "That's not what I meant."

"That's exactly what you meant. You just told me not to go somewhere I hadn't mentioned."

The break room clock ticked. 4:23. Outside, the first grey of false dawn hadn't even started; the windows were black mirrors showing them their own faces, Marit's stricken and Felix's — Felix didn't know what their own face looked like. They imagined it looked the way it had at nine years old, standing in a relative's hallway, realizing the adults had been lying about something and would never admit it.

"Kid." Marit opened her eyes. Something in her had folded; the fury was gone and what was left was worse, a kind of pleading exhaustion. She came back to the table and sat down, and her hands were shaking, actually shaking, and she wrapped them around the empty cup as if to prove to herself they could still hold something. "I'm going to tell you the only thing I can tell you, and then I'm going to ask you for something, and you're going to hate me, and I'm going to deserve it."

Felix said nothing.

"Some things are finished," Marit said. "Not solved. Not fixed. *Finished.* There's a difference. Some things got buried so deep and so long ago that digging them up doesn't get you justice — it just gets you a hole, and then it gets you in it. The people involved are old, or dead, or powerful, and usually all three. And the people who ask questions—" She stopped. Her thumb moved over the cup's rim, around and around. "The girl who asked the questions is in the harbor morgue right now, Felix. That's what asking questions gets."

"You know who she was."

"I know she's dead, and I know she called this company, and I know that if she'd called anyone else she might still be alive, and I know that every single hour since I heard about it I have been waiting for someone to come through that door and ask me exactly what you just asked me." She looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, the tears held off by what Felix recognized as pure discipline, dispatcher's discipline, the discipline of people whose job is to keep talking while everything inside is screaming. "And I prayed it wouldn't be you."

"Why not you?" Felix asked, and their voice came out smaller than they wanted. "Why couldn't it be you? You're the one who told me the night shift is about answering. *Every call gets answered.* That's the first rule. You made me memorize it."

"That's the rule for calls," Marit said. "This isn't a call. This is a grave, and you're standing on the edge with a shovel, and I'm telling you — as the person who taught you everything you know about surviving nights — put the shovel down."

Felix sat with it. The betrayal was a physical thing, a weight behind the sternum, cold and specific. Not because Marit wouldn't answer. Because Marit *could* — Felix could see it in every line of her, in the rehearsed smoothness of her denials, in the fear that had arrived precisely on cue at the words *November 1994* — and she was choosing the grave over the call. The family that had built Felix's hooks and filled Felix's Christmas palms and taught Felix how to be a person who belonged somewhere was standing in front of them, guarding a locked room, and the lock was thirty years old and someone had drowned behind it.

"I found the obituary," Felix said at last. "I'm going to keep reading."

"Then you'll do it alone," Marit said. She stood. Her legs were steady now; whatever the fear had taken out of her, she'd put it away somewhere, and that frightened Felix more than the shaking had. She took her coat off the hook — her hook, the first hook, drilled into the wall in a kinder decade — and put it on with slow, deliberate movements, and at the door she stopped without turning around.

"I drove nights for forty-one years," she said. "You know what I learned? The fares you remember aren't the ones you drove. They're the ones you couldn't. The ones where you got there too late, or you took the wrong road, or you looked in the mirror one second too long and by the time you looked back they were gone. You carry those. You carry them your whole life, and you tell yourself you did what you could, and some nights you even believe it." Her hand rested on the door handle. "I'm asking you to believe me when I say I did what I could. That's all I can ask. And I'm telling you, Felix — the last person who didn't believe that, who kept digging, who wanted the whole truth with a capital T—" The handle clicked. "She didn't get to finish her fare. Don't be her."

The door shut behind her. The break room was very quiet.

Felix sat for a long time, watching the coffee go cold, listening to the building settle around them. Through the window, the black glass showed the corkboard's reflection — decades of parties, weddings, fish, birthdays, the whole bright archive of a found family — floating ghostlike over the empty table where Marit had sat.

*She didn't deny it,* Felix thought. That was the thing that kept circling back, the splinter under the skin. In nine years, Felix had heard Marit deny everything with total confidence — denied owing Halvard fifty kroner, denied snoring, denied crying at Bjørn's wedding while openly crying at Bjørn's wedding. Marit denied things the way she drove: fast, certain, no hesitation. Tonight she hadn't denied a single thing. She had only said *drop it*, and *don't go to the archive* — a place Felix hadn't named — and *I did what I could*, which was not the sentence of an innocent woman. It was the sentence of a witness. Or something worse.

And she'd said one more thing, maybe without knowing it. *The ones you couldn't.* The fares you couldn't drive.

Felix pulled out the phone and opened the note where they'd been keeping everything — the booking details, the callback number, the disconnected payphone outside St. Hallvard's, the obituary, the date of the dinner. At the bottom, under a line they'd written two nights ago in a different mood, they added a new entry, and their thumb was steady the way it got steady on bad calls, the way Marit had taught them.

*Marit knows. Marit was there. Marit is protecting someone — or protecting me, or protecting herself, and I can't tell which, and she won't tell me, which means I have to find out without her.*

*The archive. Basement level. Dispatch logs, 1994.*

*She told me not to go.*

Felix finished the coffee, stood, and took their coat off their hook — *their* hook, the one that meant they belonged — and went downstairs to the basement, where the company kept everything it had never thrown away.

The light at the bottom of the stairs was on a pull chain, and it took three tries before it caught.
