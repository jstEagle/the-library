## 22. Dahl's Offer

The summons came the way bad weather comes to the coast — not announced so much as felt, a pressure behind the ears, a change in the taste of the air.

Felix was at the depot counter at four in the afternoon, an hour that belonged to neither shift, when the black Mercedes glided past the window and stopped in front of the bay doors. It sat there idling for a full minute, exhaust curling in the cold, before the rear door opened and a man stepped out who did not need an introduction. Felix had seen his face on the financial pages for thirty years: Henrik Dahl, silver-haired, silver-tongued, built like a man carved by someone who'd been paid extra for the jawline.

He wore a charcoal overcoat that probably cost more than the depot's annual insurance premium, and he looked around the office — the peeling laminate, the coffee ring on the counter, Marit's ancient radio murmuring weather reports to no one — with the expression of a man cataloguing everything he intended to forget.

"Mr. Eriksen," he said. Not a question. He knew. Of course he knew. "I wonder if we might speak privately."

"The break room's private enough," Felix said. "It's got a door."

Something flickered across Dahl's face — amusement, maybe, or its expensive imitation. "Charming. Very well."

Felix led him back past the lockers, past the corkboard where the night crew pinned photos of their dogs and their kids and one laminated menu from the kebab place on Søndre Gate, into the little room with two plastic chairs and a kettle that took nine minutes to boil if you were lucky. Dahl looked at the chairs as though they might be contagious, then sat anyway, folding himself down with the care of a man entering unfamiliar terrain.

"Tea?" Felix offered. "We've got Earl Grey and something Marit bought in 2019 that claims to be lemon."

"I'll stand." Dahl remained standing. Of course he did. "I'll be brief, Mr. Eriksen. I know you value your time, and I certainly value mine."

*He knows my name,* Felix thought again, and this time let the thought settle fully. *He knows my schedule. He knows which hour I'm alone.*

"You've been asking questions," Dahl said. "About 1994. About people connected to the Voss family. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise — I have people whose entire function is knowing things like that."

"And what do your people say?"

"They say you're thorough. They say you're discreet — or you would be, if someone paid you to be." Dahl reached into his coat and produced a slim envelope, cream-colored, heavy stock, and laid it on the table between them with two fingers, the way a dealer lays down a card he already knows wins. "That's a retainer. There's a figure inside that will buy you out of whatever arrangement you imagine you have with this investigation, such as it is. Consultation fees. Discretion. And a second payment on delivery of any documents belonging to Astrid Holm."

The envelope sat there. Cream against grey laminate. Felix didn't touch it.

"Astrid Holm is dead," Felix said.

"Yes. A tragedy. A drowning." Dahl's voice was smooth as ballast water. "Which is precisely why these matters should rest with the police now, don't you think? Let the professionals grieve her properly. You're a dispatcher, Mr. Eriksen. A good one, I'm told. The night city runs on people like you. That's not nothing. That's actually quite a lot, when you consider it."

*And that,* Felix thought, *is the most honest thing you've said yet.* Because it was true — the night city did run on dispatchers, on the invisible ones, the voices in the wire, and men like Henrik Dahl had spent forty years never once thinking about them until they needed one erased from a story.

"What exactly am I being paid to do?" Felix asked. "Walk away? Or forget?"

"There's no meaningful difference."

"No," Felix agreed. "There isn't."

Dahl studied them then, really studied them, and Felix watched the mask adjust — the warmth dialing down, the arithmetic coming up underneath. This was the face behind the gala photographs, the charity dinners, the endowed hospital wing. This was the face that had been thirty years old once, standing in some bright room while a young driver's name was sanded off the record.

"You strike me as an intelligent person," Dahl said quietly. "So let me skip the part where I pretend this is negotiable and tell you how it actually works. That envelope contains enough money to change your life. Not decorate it — *change* it. New apartment. No more night shifts. Whatever it is you think you'll find at the end of this, it isn't worth what it will cost you. People who ask questions about 1994 have historically fared poorly. One drowned last week. Another went off the road on the E6 Tuesday night — did you hear about that? Terrible stretch of road in the rain."

Felix's ribs ached where the seatbelt had caught them, a dull purple bloom they hadn't told anyone but Nora about. They kept their face still. Ten years of night shifts taught you that — stillness under pressure, voice level, hands quiet.

"That was me," Felix said.

For the first time, something moved behind Dahl's eyes that wasn't performance. A flicker of surprise, quickly filed away. "Then you understand better than most that I'm not threatening you. I'm describing a pattern. There's a difference, and intelligent people appreciate the difference."

"Do they."

"They do." He tapped the envelope once with one finger. "Take it. Take Astrid Holm's papers to the incinerator at any facility you like — I'll even recommend one, they're very reliable. Then take a holiday somewhere with unreliable phone service. When you come back, this will all be settled history, and you'll have learned the single most valuable lesson wealth can teach: that almost everything has a price, and the things that don't are the things that get people hurt."

The kettle clicked off in the corner. Neither of them moved toward it.

Felix thought about the money. That was the honest thing, the thing they'd admit later, lying awake at three a.m.: they thought about it. Not greed — something older and hungrier than greed. The arithmetic of a life. Ten years of nights, a flat above a laundromat where the dryer vibration came through the floor like a second heartbeat, a bank account that flinched every time the rent cleared. The number in that envelope could buy silence, yes — but it could also buy *out*. A morning shift somewhere. A window that faced east. The luxury of being ordinary without paying for it in exhaustion.

Temptation wasn't a devil with a pitchfork. Temptation was a cream envelope on a plastic table, and the sudden vertigo of imagining a life where nobody ever needed you to be brave.

"Tomas Lindqvist was twenty-four," Felix said.

Dahl's expression didn't change, but the temperature of the room did.

"He drove cab three years. His mother lived in Lillestrøm until she died in 2009, still waiting for the case to be solved. There was a girl too — pulled out of the harbor that October, cremated before anyone even tried to learn her name. Two people. Erased. And you're sitting in my break room telling me the lesson wealth teaches is that everything has a price." Felix stood, slowly, and slid the envelope back across the table with one finger. "Here's what I learned driving nights, Mr. Dahl. Everybody talks eventually. Drunks, griefers, cheating husbands, cops off duty — everybody, sooner or later, says the true thing into the dark because the dark feels safe. Thirty years is a long time to hold a true thing. Somebody's been talking. That's why you're here. That's why the envelope exists. You're not buying my silence — you're trying to find the leak."

Dahl rose too, unhurried, buttoning his coat. Up close, Felix could see the age in him now, the fine tremor in the hand that smoothed the lapel — a man running out of decades faster than he ran out of lawyers.

"You're making a mistake," Dahl said. Without heat. Almost gently. A diagnosis, not a threat.

"Probably," Felix said. "I've made worse. But they were mine."

At the door, Dahl paused. He didn't turn all the way around — just his head, profiled against the fluorescent light, and for one unguarded second Felix saw something that looked almost like envy, or its ghost.

"When I was your age," Dahl said, "I would have refused too. That's the tragedy of it. We all start as the kind of person who refuses." He straightened his collar. "The offer stands until Friday. After that, the price changes, and I won't be the one setting it."

Then he was gone, the Mercedes pulling away through the wet street, and Felix stood in the break room with the kettle ticking as it cooled and the cream envelope lying on the table like something dead.

* * *

They didn't throw it away. That surprised them, afterward — they'd expected to bin it immediately, dramatically, the way you do in the stories. Instead Felix carried it to the counter and turned it over in their hands for a long time.

Under the flap, no seal. Inside: a cashier's check for a sum with five digits before the decimal, drawn on a bank whose name Felix recognized from the Voss shipping letterhead. No note. Men like Dahl didn't put things in writing; the check itself was the message. *This is what a person costs. This is what you cost.*

Felix photographed it, both sides, timestamped, and then — after a long moment — slipped it into the same drawer where Astrid's copied pages lived, beneath the takeout menus and the spare fuses. Evidence, maybe. Or a mirror. Something to look at on the nights when the doubt got loud.

Marit appeared at six, stamping slush off her boots, and stopped short when she saw Felix's face. She was carrying two coffees from the gas station across the road, and she set one down on the counter without a word, the way she'd done a thousand times, the small liturgy of the night shift.

"Henrik Dahl was here," Felix said.

Marit went very still. Not pale this time — past pale, into something greyer, older. "What did he want?"

"To buy me. Check's in the drawer if you want to look. Five figures." Felix laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "Ten years of listening to this city's worst hours, and apparently that's my market rate."

"Felix." Marit's voice dropped low, urgent, and she glanced at the empty office before leaning across the counter. "Listen to me. Henrik Dahl doesn't make offers twice. If he came here himself — *himself*, not a lawyer, not a fixer — then he's frightened. Frightened men with money don't negotiate. They escalate. Whatever you've found, whoever you've talked to—" Her hand closed over Felix's wrist, grip surprisingly strong. "You need to give it to the detective. All of it. Tonight. Before Friday."

"I gave Brandt the boathouse pages days ago."

"Then what does Dahl think you still have?"

The question landed like a dropped wrench. Felix turned it over. *Astrid's files.* The copies in the drawer. The notes. The photograph of five young people laughing in the sun outside Voss House, 1994, faces half-turned toward a camera held by someone who'd be dead within the month.

"He thinks I have everything," Felix said slowly. "Astrid's originals. Her final notes. The page that was torn out of—"

The bell over the depot door jangled. Both of them flinched like teenagers.

Nora Voss stood in the doorway, rain-dark hair plastered to her forehead, holding a paper bag that smelled of cinnamon, and she took one look at the two of them frozen over the counter and said, "Okay. What happened?"

"Dahl," Marit said.

Nora set the bag down very carefully, as if her hands needed a task. "My grandmother's oldest friend just made a house call to a taxi depot. Felix — that's not an offer. That's a reconnaissance mission. He wanted to see where you sleep, figuratively speaking. Where you keep things. How easy you'd be to reach." She looked at the drawer. "Please tell me you didn't keep anything important here."

Felix opened the drawer. The menus. The fuses. The cream envelope.

The folder of Astrid's copied pages was gone.

* * *

They tore the office apart in ninety seconds flat — every drawer, every locker, the false bottom of the old filing cabinet that everyone pretended not to know about. Nothing. The folder had been there since Tuesday. Felix had photographed everything first — every page, every note, backed up twice, once to a drive taped under the counter and once mailed to themselves at the depot PO box — but the knowledge landed cold anyway: someone had walked into Meridian Cabs, in daylight, between four and six, and taken what they wanted.

"The Mercedes," Marit said grimly. "He wasn't alone. Driver stayed outside."

"Or the cleaner came back," Nora said. "Grandmother's house staff come and go. Nobody questions a face that belongs."

Felix stood in the middle of the ransacked office, ribs throbbing, and felt the fear finally arrive — not the sharp flare of the sedan on the E6, but a slow, settling cold, the kind that gets into foundations. Dahl had sat in the break room drinking nothing, smiling his carved smile, while someone emptied the drawers ten feet away. The whole visit had been theater staged around a theft. *Keep the dispatcher talking. Keep them flattered, threatened, human. Buy us the twenty minutes we need.*

But underneath the cold, something else was burning, steady and clarifying. Because Dahl had miscalculated — the way powerful men always eventually did. He'd assumed the copies were the treasure. He didn't know about the drive under the counter. He didn't know about the PO box. And he didn't know — couldn't know, because Felix had only understood it that afternoon, watching the check lie there like a corpse — that the price had already gone up.

Friday, Dahl had said. After that, the price changes.

Felix picked up the phone and dialed the number Detective Brandt had scrawled on the back of a receipt weeks ago, and while it rang, they looked at Marit and Nora, at the wrecked office, at the cinnamon bag going cold on the counter, and heard the line click open.

"Brandt."

"It's Felix Eriksen. Dahl was just here. He offered me money for Astrid's files — and while he kept me talking, someone cleaned out my desk." A breath. "Detective, I think we've been reading this backwards. They're not trying to bury 1994 anymore. They're cleaning house. And Friday's the deadline."

A pause on the line, long enough to hear rain against the depot windows.

"Stay where you are," Brandt said. "I'm coming to you — and Eriksen? Don't answer the door for anyone else tonight. Anyone."

Outside, far down the wet street, headlights swung slowly past the depot. Slowed. Held.

And moved on.
