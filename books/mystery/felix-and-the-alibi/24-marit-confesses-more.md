## 24. Marit Confesses More

The dream always began the same way: rain on the windshield, the wipers losing their argument with it, and a woman in the back seat who would not stop shivering.

Marit woke at 4:17 in the morning, as she had for thirty years, and lay in her narrow bed above the garage she no longer used, listening to the radiator tick like a meter running. Outside, Oslo was doing its gray pre-dawn breathing. Somewhere across town, Felix Eriksen was probably awake too — that kid never slept right, she'd known it from the first week, the way they sat at the dispatch console with their shoulders up around their ears like someone expecting a blow.

*It wasn't supposed to be her.*

She said it aloud sometimes now, into the dark of her bedroom, testing whether the words had gotten any lighter with repetition. They hadn't. They were stones. She'd been carrying them so long she'd forgotten they weren't part of her body.

Marit got up, put on the kettle she wouldn't drink from, and stood at the window watching the streetlamp flicker the way it did when the wind came off the fjord. And because sleep was finished with her, she let herself remember — properly this time, not the edited version she'd told Felix on the loading dock two weeks ago, the careful half-truth she'd constructed out of gauze and guilt.

*I drove the cab that night,* she'd said. *But I swear I never saw a body. I was paid to forget.*

All true. None of it the truth.

---

October 1994. She was twenty-six years old, three years into the job, already the dispatcher everyone trusted with the strange fares — the ones that needed discretion, the doctors' wives, the politicians' mistresses, the men who didn't want receipts. Meridian Cabs had a reputation in those days: quiet wheels. What happened in the back seat stayed in the back seat. Old man Meridian himself had built the company on it, and the Voss account was the crown jewel of the whole operation. Thirty cars' worth of contract. Salaries. Christmas bonuses. The sense, which Marit had then and would carry until very recently, that she belonged somewhere.

The call came in at 11:40 on a Saturday night, and it came from Ingrid Voss directly — not through the secretary, not through the house line, but on the private number old Meridian gave to maybe four people alive. Marit took it herself because the night dispatcher had stepped out for cigarettes and she was the only one at the board.

"Voss House," the voice said. Calm. Too calm, the way water is calm over something deep. "One car. The driver is not to come up the drive. Wait at the gate."

"I'll send someone right away—"

"You will drive," Ingrid Voss said, and there was something underneath the calm then, a hairline crack, "because you are the only one there, and I am asking for *you*, Marit. You've driven me before. You don't ask questions."

She hadn't asked questions. That was the sin, she understood now — not what she saw or didn't see, but the smooth professional silence she'd worn like a uniform. Ten minutes later she was idling at the bottom of the Voss drive in the drizzle, heater ticking, watching the gate lamps smear yellow through wet glass.

Ingrid Voss came down the drive on foot.

That detail had haunted Marit for three decades and she had never once examined it properly, because examining it meant asking why a woman with six cars in her garage and a husband's staff tripping over themselves upstairs would walk a quarter mile down a gravel drive alone at midnight. Her hair was plastered to her skull. Her evening dress — Marit remembered it as dark green silk, expensive, ruined — was soaked through completely, clinging to her like a second skin, and her shoes were gone. Barefoot on gravel. In October. Walking with the terrible mechanical steadiness of someone who has decided that if they stop moving they will come apart.

Marit got out and opened the rear door, and the smell hit her — cold water, mud, and under it something else, faint but unmistakable, the green-copper smell of the harbor at low tide.

"Drive," Ingrid said.

"Mrs. Voss, you're freezing, let me get you a blanket—"

"*Drive.*"

Marit drove. She turned the heat to maximum, pulled the wool blanket from behind the passenger seat — every Meridian car had one, company policy, drivers froze waiting on fares — and passed it back without looking in the mirror, because even then, even at twenty-six, some animal instinct told her not to look in the mirror.

For ten minutes, nothing. Rain. Wipers. The city sliding past in smears of sodium orange. Then, from the back seat, in a voice Marit had never heard from Ingrid Voss before or since — small, unarmored, almost young —

"It wasn't supposed to be her."

Marit's hands went tight on the wheel. She watched the road. She said nothing, because she was a Meridian driver, and Meridian drivers didn't ask questions.

"She was supposed to leave," Ingrid went on, as if the silence were an invitation rather than a wall. "I told her — I gave her money, I gave her a ticket, I told her if she ever came near this family again—" A breath that shuddered going in and came out level. Controlled. Even drowning, the woman controlled herself. "There was a party. Everyone saw us all together, all evening, that was the point of it, Henrik wanted the photographs, the *alibi*—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "We were all downstairs being seen. All of us. Every single one."

*Every single one.* Marit would spend thirty years turning that phrase over, and only now, in the dark of her kitchen with the kettle cooling untouched, did she finally let it mean what it meant. Not just Ingrid. Not just Henrik. *All of them.* Dahl. Reinholt. The photographs. The alibi built in advance, deliberately, by five people who knew what the evening was actually for.

And herself. The sixth. The wheel-man. Except she hadn't known, had she? That was the thing she'd clung to, the plank she'd floated on through every sleepless 4 a.m.: *I didn't know. I was just the cab.*

"Where am I taking you, Mrs. Voss?" she'd asked, because the route Ingrid had murmured made no sense — not Voss House, not the hospital, nowhere logical. Just streets. Wandering.

"Anywhere," Ingrid said. "Not home yet." And then, after a mile of rain: "You didn't see me tonight, Marit. There will be an envelope at the depot on Monday. Henrik handles these things. If anyone ever asks — and no one will, because no one knows — you were on the airport run."

"I don't understand what—"

"You don't need to understand. That's what you're being paid for. It's what your company is *for*." And here Ingrid Voss had leaned forward, close enough that Marit could see her face fully in the passing light for the first time — and what Marit saw was not a monster. That was the worst of it, the thing she could never explain to Felix, to Brandt, to anyone who wanted the story simple. What she saw was a woman with terror sitting behind her eyes like a passenger, a woman who had done something irreversible and was discovering, minute by minute, that she could not undo it and could not carry it either. "You seem like a girl who understands how the world works," Ingrid said softly. "The world works because people like me pay people like you to not understand it. Take the envelope, Marit. Live your life. Forget my face."

Then she'd sat back, and Marit had driven her in circles for forty more minutes while the woman in the back seat stared at the rain, and finally dropped her at the service entrance of Voss House where a man — Sander, though Marit didn't know his name then — waited with a towel and took her inside without a word.

Monday, there was an envelope at the depot. Six months' salary. Old Meridian called her into his office, patted her hand, and said, "Good work Saturday night, girl. Discreet. That's why you'll go far here." And she had gone far. Twenty-nine years far. Supervisor, then operations manager, then the gray-haired institution everyone called Aunt Marit, keeper of the night shift, maker of the good coffee, guardian of the found family she'd assembled out of lonely people — Felix among them — because she had never been able to fill the hole the first family left.

Because that was the truth under the truth, wasn't it. The crew, the rituals, the Sunday dinners, the way she memorized everyone's birthdays and bad days and mother's phone numbers — she'd built it all as penance, or maybe as camouflage, or maybe simply because a woman who cannot forgive herself will spend her life trying to earn forgiveness from strangers.

---

The kettle had gone cold. Marit poured it out anyway, ritual without meaning, and stood in her kitchen with her hands flat on the counter.

Here was the rest of it. The part she'd kept back from Felix on the loading dock, the part that had been rising in her throat for two weeks like floodwater.

She *had* seen something that night. Not a body — God, she'd sworn that honestly, she never saw a body — but something worse in its way, something that had made the cover-up make sense to her frightened twenty-six-year-old self, something she'd buried so deep she'd needed Astrid Holm's death to dig it back up.

At the third red light, somewhere past the shipyards, Ingrid Voss had taken off one soaked glove to press her hands against her face — and in the wash of the traffic signal, Marit had seen them in the mirror. Scratches. Long ones, four parallel lines down the left cheek and jaw, shallow, already scabbing. Defensive wounds. The kind a person gets when someone else is fighting for their life.

And Ingrid had caught her looking. In the mirror. Held her own gaze there, unblinking, scratched face and drowned eyes, and said, in that same terrible calm:

"The airport run, Marit. Say it."

"The airport run," Marit had said.

Thirty years, and she could still feel the shape of those words in her mouth.

She'd told herself a thousand stories about those scratches. The girl had fought; Ingrid had struck her; it had gone wrong; it was half-accident, panic, a rich woman's self-defense curdled into murder. She'd told herself Ingrid acted alone. She'd told herself Henrik ordered it. She'd told herself everyone at that party was guilty and no one was, that wealth was the real killer, that she was a minor player, a steering wheel, a pair of hands.

But there was one more thing. One last stone at the bottom of the bag, and it was this: two weeks after that night, a young dispatcher named Tomas Lindqvist — a boy Marit had trained herself, a boy who reminded everyone of a young Felix Eriksen, bright and curious and constitutionally incapable of not asking questions — had come to her at the change of shift, pale, holding a page torn from the dispatch log.

"Marit," he'd said. "The log for the fourteenth. Someone cut it out. Three hours, gone. I checked the backup book in Meridian's office — it's razored too. Who does that? Who cuts out three hours?"

And Marit, who knew exactly who, who knew exactly which three hours, had looked this boy in his earnest face and said, "Paperwork errors happen, Tomas. Drop it."

He hadn't dropped it. Nine days later he was dead under the wheels of a car nobody found, and the case closed itself like a well-oiled door, and Marit had stood at his funeral next to old Meridian and understood — with total, sickening clarity — that she was looking at the reason. That her silence had pointed him at the gap, and the gap had killed him. If she had lied better — told him the pages were lost in a flood, told him anything — he might have lived. If she had told the truth, he might have lived. Instead she'd given him exactly enough to be dangerous and nothing enough to be safe.

She had carried Tomas for thirty years alongside the woman in the mirror. Two bodies, one grave each, both dug by her silence.

---

Felix knocked on her door at seven in the morning, which meant Felix hadn't slept either, which meant the kid had found something new, because that was how Felix worked — obsession as insomnia, insomnia as method.

Marit opened the door before the second knock. She'd made coffee she had no intention of drinking and set out two cups anyway, because whatever was coming, it was coming today; she could feel it the way you feel weather in old bones. The envelope from Ingrid Voss's dinner invitation was probably sitting in Felix's jacket pocket right now. Cream cardstock. *Thirty Years of Keeping Faith.* Marit had gotten one too. She'd burned hers in the sink.

"Felix," she said, before the kid could speak, before the questions could start, because she was done making people dig for it. She'd spent a lifetime being paid to not understand, and she was resigning. "Sit down. I'm going to tell you about the scratches."

Felix sat. Their face did the thing it always did — the careful blankness, the dispatcher's mask, feelings processed off-screen — but their hands, wrapped around the cup she'd given them, were shaking fine as wire.

And Marit told it. All of it, in order, starting with the bare feet on the gravel and ending with Tomas's pale face asking about the missing hours. She watched Felix take each piece the way you watch someone swallow stones. When she got to the mirror — *the airport run, say it* — Felix closed their eyes briefly, and when they opened them there was something in them Marit recognized from her own bathroom mirror at 4 a.m., and it broke her heart, because the kid was too young for that look, everyone was too young for that look.

"There was a girl," Felix said quietly. Not a question. "Before Tomas. The one Ingrid was talking about. *Her.*"

"Yes."

"And the scratches mean she fought. Which means Ingrid's confession — accident, panic —"

"— might be true," Marit said, "and might be the last lie of a woman who's lied beautifully for thirty years. I can't tell you which, Felix. That's the hell of it. I only know what I saw, and what I saw was a guilty woman, and guilty isn't the same as murderous." She wrapped her hands around her own cup just to hold something. "But there's one more thing I haven't said, and you're not going to like it."

Felix waited.

"Ingrid asked for *me* that night. By name. Out of every driver in the company." Marit made herself say it plainly, no softening. "Old Meridian gave her the private number, and she chose me — the one driver who'd already proven discreet. And thirty years later, Astrid Holm books her investigation through Meridian, anonymous, prepaid, addressed to whoever picks up the night line. And it lands on *you*." She met Felix's eyes. "Astrid was Ingrid's secretary, Felix. She knew the history. She knew the account. I think that booking was never anonymous at all. I think it was addressed — to this company, to this lineage, to *us*. To me, maybe. Or to whoever inherited my seat." Her voice cracked, finally, thirty years late. "I think we were chosen, Felix. Both times. And I don't think Ingrid Voss hired Astrid to prove her innocence."

The kitchen was very quiet. Downstairs, the first early cabs were coughing awake in the depot lot, radios crackling to life, the day shift arriving with its ordinary noise.

"What do you think she hired her to do?" Felix asked.

Marit looked out the window, at the gray light coming up over the rooftops, and said the thing she'd been carrying since the night she learned Astrid Holm was dead:

"I think a dying woman hired someone to find her guilt — and then made sure the only person who could finish the job is the granddaughter who hasn't spoken to her in six years. Because Nora is the only one Ingrid can't protect, and the only one she can't warn." She turned back. "Check Ingrid's desk, Felix. Whatever Astrid found, wherever she hid it — Ingrid will have kept it close. She's been keeping it close for thirty years. It's the only thing she owns that matters."

Felix was already standing, coat half on, that dispatcher urgency in every line of them — and Marit caught their sleeve at the door, one last time.

"When you talk to Nora," she said, "tell her the woman in the back seat of my cab was crying. Whatever else her grandmother is, tell her that. She deserves one true thing about her blood."

Felix nodded once and was gone, footsteps ringing down the stairs toward the depot, toward the phones, toward whatever was waiting — and Marit stood alone in her kitchen with two full cups of coffee and, for the first time in thirty years, nothing left to hide.
