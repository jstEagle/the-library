## 29. Sander

The boathouse smelled of tar and forty winters.

Sander Vik had kept it the way he kept everything — swept, oiled, locked. He came down every Thursday to check the hull of the old runabout that hadn't touched water since before Nora was born, because a man needed reasons to move his hands or his hands started remembering things on their own. Today he found the hasp scratched, the padlock hanging wrong, and he stood in the doorway for a long time with the fog pressing at his back like something patient.

The floorboard under the workbench had been lifted and set back down by someone who thought they were careful. They weren't. The dust told him everything: small hands, careful hands, gloved. The pages were gone. Tomas Lindqvist's pages, thirty years in the dark, gone.

And Astrid's notebook — the one she'd pressed into his chest three weeks ago with fingers so cold he'd thought, absurdly, *she's already dead* — that was gone too.

Sander sat down slowly on the upturned dinghy, his knees cracking like kindling, and looked out through the open doors at the gray water where it had all begun. He was seventy-one years old. He had been silent for thirty years, and silence had been the easiest thing he'd ever done, easier than breathing, because breathing you had to think about sometimes.

He'd known this day would come. He just hadn't known which door it would come through.

* * *

He didn't have long to wait.

He heard the bicycle first — the depot bike, he knew its squeak the way he knew the creak of the third stair at Voss House — and then footsteps on the gravel path, hesitant, stopping twice before they reached the boathouse. The dispatcher. Eriksen. The one Ingrid had invited to dinner with her own fountain pen, the one whose name she'd written like a signature on a confession.

Sander didn't turn around. "You took them."

Felix Eriksen stopped in the doorway, silhouetted against the fog, rain beading on their jacket. "I need to know what happened here."

"You broke into an old man's boathouse."

"I broke into a crime scene." Felix's voice didn't shake. Sander gave them credit for that. Most people who came down this path shook. "Tomas Lindqvist's logbook pages. Astrid Holm's notes. Thirty years of evidence someone hid in a boat shed, and I'm supposed to feel bad about the lock?"

Sander turned then. He studied the dispatcher the way he studied weather coming off the water — for what it carried, not what it showed. Young. Tired. A bruise yellowing along their jaw from the road, he'd heard about the bike. And underneath all of it, that thing he recognized because he'd once been young himself, once, briefly, before 1994: the look of a person who had heard something they couldn't unhear and now needed the rest of it, needed it like air.

"Sit down," Sander said, "before you fall down," and nodded at the bench by the wall.

Felix sat. Didn't argue. That, too, told him something.

"I'm not going to tell you anything," Sander said. "That's what I've decided, every year, for thirty years. I decide it again each spring when the ice goes out. It's a ritual, like oiling the hull." He folded his hands over his knee. "And every year it gets easier to keep, and every year it costs more."

"Then don't tell me," Felix said quietly. "Tell her."

The word landed in Sander's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the echo.

"Lene Bakke," Felix said. "She was twenty-three. She waited tables at the anniversary party in '94. She went into the harbor off the north dock and the police report says accidental drowning, and she was cremated unclaimed in January '95 because nobody — nobody — came forward. Not one person. Her mother died two years before her. There was no one left to say her name, so the city said *Jane Doe* and burned her." Felix's voice stayed level but their hands had closed around their own knee, knuckles white. "You've said her name, haven't you. Once a year. Like your ritual."

The fog moved in the doorway. Somewhere out on the water, a buoy clanked its slow idiot bell.

"Twice a year," Sander said finally. "Her birthday too."

* * *

He told it the way he'd rehearsed telling it for three decades, alone, to the walls of the gatehouse, to the ceiling of his bed, to God when God still answered — though God had stopped answering somewhere around the same time, and Sander suspected the two facts were related.

"It was the thirtieth anniversary of the wedding," he said. "Everyone calls it that now, the family anniversary, but that night — that night there was another party. Smaller. After. The real one, the one that wasn't for the newspapers. Mr. Voss liked his after-parties. Dahl organized them. There was champagne that cost more than my father earned in a year, and there were people there who shouldn't have been, and Lene Bakke was one of them."

"She worked the dinner service," Felix said. "Then stayed."

"She was invited to stay." Sander's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "By Mr. Voss. You understand what I'm telling you."

Felix understood. Sander watched them understand — watched the dispatcher's face do the arithmetic of a young woman, a rich old man, a private party, and a harbor fifty meters from the terrace lights.

"I was the caretaker. Twenty-eight years old, ten years in the job. My father had held it before me. You don't ask questions in that house; asking questions is how you stop being employed. But I couldn't sleep that night — too much noise, too much laughter with an edge on it — so I walked down to check the dock lines because the wind was coming up. North wind, hard off the fjord. I remember the wind better than I remember most of my life."

He stopped. This was the part. It was always the part. For thirty years he had arrived at this part and turned back, and tonight the road through it lay open and lit and he could see all the way to the end of it, and the end of it was a name spoken aloud in front of a witness.

"Ingrid came down the dock path," he said. "Not Mr. Voss. Not Dahl. Ingrid. She was barefoot. She'd left her shoes somewhere on the terrace, and she was walking fast, and she was carrying a bottle of champagne by the neck like a club, and I got the impression — I've had thirty years to get impressions — that she'd followed them down. Her husband and the girl. Followed them down to the water."

Felix had gone very still. The kind of stillness Sander had seen in deer at the tree line.

"There was a boathouse then, the old one, bigger. Lights inside. Voices. Then just one voice — hers, the girl's — high, frightened, saying *no, please, I have to go, my shift* — and then Ingrid went past me. She didn't see me. I was behind the line shack. She went onto the dock and into the boathouse light and I heard her say — I will hear it until I die — she said, *'You little thief, get away from my husband,'* and then there was a sound. A crack. Like an oar against a hull. And the girl screamed, short, and then there was splashing, and Ingrid was standing at the dock edge with the bottle still in her hand, looking down at the water, and the water was black and the wind was pushing it, and the girl didn't come up where you'd expect her to come up."

Sander's hands had begun to shake. He let them. He was done hiding them; he'd hidden them since he was twenty-eight.

"The current takes things north along that shore," he said. "Anyone who grew up here knows it. She didn't come up. And Ingrid Voss stood on that dock for — I don't know, a minute, two — and then she turned around and walked back up the path, barefoot, calm as Sunday, and she passed within four meters of where I was hiding, and I saw her face."

"What did you see?" Felix asked. Barely a whisper.

"Not rage." Sander looked out at the water. "That's what nobody understands. Not rage. Horror. She looked like a woman who'd picked up a bottle meaning to break it on the dock boards and break *something*, scare somebody, make a point — and the world had tilted and given her the wrong result. She looked like she was waiting to wake up. And then she walked upstairs and became Mrs. Voss again, and the next morning there were men from Dahl's office all over the property, and the story was that a guest had left early, and nine days later Tomas Lindqvist was dead under the wheels of a car on the E18, and everyone understood — everyone who mattered — that the harbor had swallowed the whole night."

* * *

Felix spoke after a long silence. "Why didn't you go to the police? You saw her. You heard—"

"Go to the police." Sander laughed, and it was an ugly sound, rusted. "Go to the police with what? That I hid behind a shack and watched? That the richest woman in the city killed a waitress and I did nothing? Against Dahl's lawyers, against Reinholt signing whatever paper needed signing, against a police commissioner who fished with Mr. Voss every June?" He shook his head. "And there was the other thing. The money."

Felix's head came up.

"A week after. Envelope in my pay packet. Five years' wages. No note. Every year after, same week, another envelope. Ingrid never said a word to me about it, not once, not ever, in thirty years. We passed each other in the corridors of that house ten thousand times and neither of us ever mentioned the dock." His voice dropped. "You know what that is? That's not payment. Payment ends. That's a chain. She bought my silence annually the way you'd feed a meter, and every envelope said the same thing: *I know you saw. You know I know.* We were married to that night, her and me, longer than she was married to him."

"And Marit," Felix said. "Marit drove her home that night. Soaked through."

Something flickered across Sander's face — surprise, then not-surprise. "The dispatcher. Yes. Yes, she did. Ingrid came up from the water and walked straight to the drive, and Marit's cab was there — Meridian, always Meridian in those days, the only firm that would run the hill road at midnight — and Ingrid got in soaked to the skin and rode home and paid cash and never called that company again if she could help it." He looked at Felix steadily. "But you know that already. You've talked to her."

"She swears she never saw a body. Never knew anyone died."

"I believe her." Sander said it simply. "Ingrid would have made sure Marit saw nothing. One witness was enough. Two was a risk." He paused. "But Marit knows something else. Something she may not even know she knows. Ask her what Ingrid whispered in the cab. Ask her exactly."

Felix wrote nothing down. Sander noticed that — noticed the dispatcher memorizing instead, the way night people do, holding words in the dark where paper can't betray them.

* * *

The rain thickened outside, ticking on the boathouse roof, and Sander got up stiffly and poured coffee from the dented thermos he kept on the shelf — a habit from decades of cold mornings — and handed Felix a tin cup without being asked. The dispatcher wrapped both hands around it like it was the first warm thing in days.

"Astrid," Felix said. "Tell me about Astrid."

Sander sat back down. This part was newer; it hurt in a different place.

"She came to me in September. Three weeks ago. Knocked on the gatehouse door at seven in the morning, which told me everything, because nobody honest knocks on a caretaker's door at seven in the morning." He blew on his coffee. "She'd been Ingrid's secretary for eleven years. She'd found something in the household papers — I don't know what, a ledger, letters, I never learned — and she'd spent months pulling threads, quiet as a mouse in a wall. And she'd figured out that I was the loose thread. Thirty years of envelopes, she'd found those too. Ingrid's generosity has a pattern, and patterns are how accountants see ghosts."

"What did she want?"

"She wanted to know if I'd testify." Sander set the cup down. "She stood in my doorway with the fog coming in behind her and she said, *'Mr. Vik, I know what you saw. I know why you take the money. I'm not asking you to forgive yourself. I'm asking you to help me finish it.'*" His voice caught on the memory, roughened. "And I said no. I said I was too old, that it was finished, that the girl was ash and the witnesses were dying and what good — and she looked at me the way you're looking at me now, and she said the worst thing anyone's ever said to me. She said, *'It isn't finished. It's just quiet.'*"

The buoy bell clanked. Rain ran off the boathouse roof in silver threads.

"Then she said something that scared her while she said it. I watched it scare her — watched the color leave her face mid-sentence, like she'd walked into cold water. She said, *'Mrs. Voss hired me to investigate the death. She hired me to prove it didn't happen. And Mr. Vik, I have come to believe she knows that I know. I think that's why she hired me. I think she wanted to find out how close I'd get.'*" Sander's hands flattened on his knees. "She said, *'If anything happens to me, it wasn't an accident, and it wasn't suicide, whatever they write. Come forward. Promise me.'*"

"And you promised."

"I promised." Sander closed his eyes. "And three weeks later she was floating in the harbor like the other one, and the police wrote *accidental drowning* like it was a form they fill out in triplicate, and I sat in my gatehouse with my promise in my throat and did what I have always done." He opened his eyes and looked at Felix with something raw and level in them. "Nothing. I did nothing. Until you pulled up my floorboards."

* * *

Felix set down the cup. "The notebook. Her final notes. The last page named her killer — someone tore it out. Whoever tore it had access to her apartment and to this boathouse. That's a short list."

"Two names," Sander said. "I've done the same sum. It's either Ingrid herself — and she doesn't climb ladders anymore, her hips won't allow it, and she hasn't left the house unaccompanied in two years — or it's someone acting for her." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone quieter, heavier, the voice of a man setting down something he'd carried too far. "Or it's me. If I wanted to end this the clean way — the way I should have ended it thirty years ago — I'd have taken those pages and burned them and let the dead stay dead. Maybe I should have. Maybe you're sitting in a murderer's boathouse right now, drinking his coffee."

"Are you?"

"No." Sander almost smiled. "I'm sitting in a coward's boathouse. Different animal. A coward keeps the evidence because burning it means deciding, and I haven't decided anything since 1994." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and fixed Felix with a look. "But I'll tell you what I've decided tonight, since we're being honest with each other, which is a novelty I'd like to savor. I'll testify. Monday, Tuesday, whenever Brandt wants me. I'll stand in a courtroom and say Ingrid Voss's name and Lene Bakke's name in the same breath and let God sort out the order. Astrid asked me for a promise and I failed it once. I don't intend to fail it twice."

Felix exhaled — a long, shaking breath, the breath of someone who'd carried a weight into this room and was only now discovering how heavy it had been. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Sander raised a hand. "There's one more thing. The reason Astrid came to *you*. To Meridian. She told me that too, that morning in my doorway, before she lost her color. She said she'd traced the whole thing back to the beginning — to the cab that drove Ingrid home — and she'd found out which dispatcher had tried, back in '94, to get the police to look at a hit-and-run on the E18. A young dispatcher who filed a report nobody read. Who kept calling. Who wouldn't let it go until someone above made them stop." Sander watched Felix's face as the recognition landed. "She said she wanted to hire the one company in this city that had once tried to do the right thing. She said she asked specifically for that dispatcher."

The rain filled the silence.

"But that dispatcher was gone by then," Sander said softly. "Retired that spring. So the booking fell to whoever inherited the night desk." He looked at Felix — at the pale face, the white knuckles, the dawning, terrible understanding. "Astrid Holm didn't choose you, Eriksen. She chose a ghost. You're holding a dead woman's errand, and the woman who gave it to you thought she was handing it to someone else entirely."

Felix stood very slowly, the tin cup forgotten, staring out at the black water as if the answer were floating there.

"Marit," they whispered. "It was meant for Marit."

* * *

Outside, at the top of the gravel path, headlights swung through the fog — slow, deliberate, the grey sedan easing to a stop where the lane met the shore road, engine idling, wipers keeping their patient time.

Sander saw it first. He'd spent thirty years learning what watching meant.

"Eriksen," he said quietly. "I think your ride home just arrived early."
