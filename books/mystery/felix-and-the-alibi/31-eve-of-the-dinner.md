## 31. Eve of the Dinner

The invitation had said six o'clock for drinks, eight for dinner, and Felix arrived at five-forty because a decade of dispatching had taught them that the difference between early and on time was the difference between watching a thing happen and being caught inside it.

Voss House sat at the end of a gravel drive long enough to have its own weather. It wasn't a mansion in the way Felix had imagined — no columns, no fountains — just a low, wide building of grey stone and black timber, built like something that intended to outlast everyone who lived in it. The windows were lit amber against the blue-grey dusk, and through them Felix could see movement: staff crossing rooms with armloads of linen, a man on a ladder adjusting a chandelier, a woman carrying a tray of glasses with the concentration of someone defusing ordnance.

Felix parked the borrowed depot car at the edge of the lot, next to a catering van from a company whose name Felix didn't recognize, and sat for a moment with both hands on the wheel.

*Some fares are one-way.*

The note hadn't come yet. That would come later — Felix knew that now, the way you know the shape of a night before it happens, the way Marit could look at a Friday shift roster and tell you which call would go bad. But the feeling of it was already here, settled into Felix's chest like cold air into a parked cab.

They got out. The gravel crunched. Somewhere behind the house, water moved — the fjord, close enough to hear.

* * *

Inside, the smell was beeswax and cut flowers and, underneath it, something older: wood polish, coal smoke ghosting out of disused fireplaces, the particular staleness of rooms that are only opened for occasions. A housekeeper with forearms like a stevedore directed Felix down a corridor toward the drawing room, where Ingrid Voss's assistant — a young man named Petter whom Felix had spoken to twice on the phone — met them with a clipboard and a smile that didn't reach his eyes.

"Ms. Eriksen. Mrs. Voss asked that you arrive early." He said it as though this were an honor. "She wants you to see the table."

"I'd like that," Felix said, which was true in the way that wanting to look at a wound is true.

Petter led them through rooms that got progressively more formal. Felix catalogued exits without meaning to — service door, garden door, the main stair, a back stair behind a baize-covered door that a maid slipped through with a stack of napkins. Old habit. New necessity.

The dining room opened up all at once, and Felix stopped walking.

The table ran nearly the length of the room, dressed in white linen so heavily starched it looked poured rather than laid. Twenty-two place settings. Crystal that caught the light of two tall windows and threw it around in small, cold fragments. Down the center ran a runner of dark green — Voss green, Felix was learning — scattered with white roses so fresh they still smelled of cold water.

And along one side of the room, on a trolley draped in velvet, lay the silver.

A woman was polishing it. She stood with her back half-turned, working a piece — a serving fork, by the size of it — with a cloth and a paste that smelled faintly of ammonia. She worked slowly. Methodically. There was something about the rhythm of it that made Felix think of the depot at four in the morning, of Marit wiping down the radio console before handover, of the small rituals people build to keep their hands busy while their minds go somewhere they can't say out loud.

"Thirty years," Petter said beside Felix, with the flat pride of a man reciting from a program. "Mrs. Voss insists on the same pieces every year. They were her mother-in-law's."

Felix watched the woman polish. Fork, bowl of the spoon, the knife's spine. Each piece lifted, turned, set down gleaming.

"You do this every year?" Felix asked.

"The silver? Yes. Marta's done it since—" Petter paused, recalculated. "A long time."

Marta, hearing her name, glanced over. Her face was seamed and unbothered, the face of someone who had polished silver through whatever this family had done for thirty years and expected to polish it through thirty more. She nodded at Felix — the nod one working person gives another, an assessment passed in half a second — and went back to the fork.

Felix thought: *she knows.* Not the details, maybe. But houses like this don't keep secrets from the people who clean them. Houses leak downward. The staff always knew things the family didn't know they knew.

"Where would you like me?" Felix asked.

Petter consulted the clipboard. "You're seated at the far end. Place nineteen." He said it without inflection, but Felix heard what he meant: *the end of the table, away from the family, where we can see you.*

"Nineteen," Felix repeated. "Near the kitchen door?"

Something flickered across Petter's face — surprise, quickly filed away. "Yes. Is that a problem?"

"No," Felix said. "It's my favorite seat."

* * *

Ingrid Voss found Felix in the drawing room twenty minutes later.

Felix heard her before seeing her — not footsteps; she wore soft shoes — but the way conversation died ahead of her, moving through the room like a wake through water. Staff straightened. Petter's clipboard rose slightly, defensively. And then she was there, smaller than Felix remembered from the photographs, upright in a way that looked less like posture than architecture, in a dress the color of deep water.

"Ms. Eriksen." Her voice was dry and precise. "You came early."

"You invited me to watch," Felix said. "I wanted to see the whole thing get built."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that the answer had been the correct one, or perhaps simply an interesting one.

"Walk with me."

They walked. Through the drawing room, past a case of ship models — hulls no bigger than bread loaves, each with a brass plaque — and into a conservatory where the evening light came down green through old glass. Ingrid stopped at a table beneath a fig tree grown enormous in its pot, its trunk braided like rope.

"Do you know why I hold this dinner?" she asked.

"The invitation said an anniversary."

"It says a great many things." She touched one of the fig leaves, absently, the way you'd touch a scar. "Thirty years ago, my husband and I gave a party in this house. Everyone who mattered was here. And the next morning, one of our guests was dead, and the newspapers called it a tragedy, and we called it an accident, and we all went on." She turned to look at Felix, and her eyes were pale and level and utterly without apology. "Every year since, we have gathered to celebrate keeping faith with one another. That is what the toast means. Tomorrow night, when I raise my glass, that is what I will be saying."

"And you want me there to hear it."

"I want you there to understand it." She folded her hands. "You've been asking questions, Ms. Eriksen. You've been very thorough. Detective Brandt thinks you're reckless. Dahl thinks you're for sale. I think—" she paused, and for a moment something almost human crossed the architecture of her face, "—I think you're the first person in thirty years who has actually been listening."

"To what?"

"To the frequency underneath everything." She said it plainly, without drama, and somehow that made it worse. "You're a dispatcher. You spend your life listening to people who need help and can't always say so directly. So am I, in my way. Have been, for fifty years." She stepped closer. The fig tree threw dappled shadow across her face. "Tomorrow night, listen carefully. Everything you need will be said at that table. It always has been. No one ever hears it, because no one is listening for it."

"Is that a confession?"

"It's an invitation." She smiled then, briefly, and it was the coldest thing Felix had seen all week. "Dinner is at eight. Do wear something warm. The house gets drafty after midnight."

She left. Felix stood under the fig tree, heart going hard and slow, and understood that he had just been told — in the language of a woman who never said anything directly — that tomorrow night, everything ended. One way or another.

* * *

Felix spent the hour before dark learning the house.

Not obviously. They walked the grounds with a coffee from the catering urn, admiring the gardens like any guest, and while admiring them counted things. The wall around the property: stone, two meters, ivy on the north side. The gates: wrought iron, electrically operated, controlled from a panel Felix spotted in the gatehouse. Storm forecast for tomorrow — Brandt had mentioned it, frowning at his phone — which meant the gates might be closed early, which meant the house could become a box with twenty-two people in it.

There was a boathouse below the lawn, dark now, its windows reflecting the grey water. Felix didn't go near it. The key Nora had taken from her grandmother's desk was in Felix's pocket, and the pages it had opened were in a police evidence bag in Brandt's safe, but the building itself still pulled at Felix's attention like a missing tooth.

Sander was somewhere on the grounds. Felix felt him the way you feel a watcher — a prickle at the neck, gone when you turn. Once, near the greenhouse, Felix caught sight of him: a big man in a canvas coat, standing among the tomato plants with pruning shears he wasn't using, looking at nothing. He met Felix's eyes for exactly one second. Then he raised one hand — not a wave, more a salute, or a warning — and walked away between the rows.

*He saw her strike the girl,* Felix thought. *He's carried it for thirty years. And Astrid came to him frightened, and now Astrid is in the harbor, and he's standing in a greenhouse holding shears.*

Felix drank the terrible catering coffee and thought about Sander the way a dispatcher thinks about a caller who goes quiet mid-sentence. The quiet ones weren't calm. The quiet ones were deciding.

* * *

By seven the caterers had taken over the kitchen wing, and the house filled with the sounds of a dinner assembling itself: the clatter of trays, the murmur of instructions, a string quartet tuning in the music room with the tentative, apologetic sound of instruments finding each other. Felix watched from the edges, a ghost with a coffee cup, and the crew of Voss House flowed around them without question — because Felix had learned, years ago, how to be furniture, and tonight furniture was the safest thing to be.

Marta was still polishing. Felix drifted back to the dining room and found her at the trolley, working through the last of the knives.

"Long day," Felix said.

"They're all long days before these ones." She didn't look up. "You're the one asking questions. The one from the taxi company."

"I'm the one from the taxi company."

"Mm." She buffed the blade, turned it, held it to the window light. "I've worked here forty-one years. Started when the current Mrs. Voss was still a bride. You want to know something about silver?" She finally looked at Felix, and her eyes were shrewd and tired. "It remembers. Every scratch, every dent. People think polishing takes the past off. It doesn't. It just makes the past shine." She set the knife down and picked up the last fork. "That girl there—" a tilt of her head toward the corridor, toward the whole apparatus of the evening, "—she does this dinner like it's church. Like if everything's perfect enough, God won't look too closely." The fork gleamed. She laid it in its place. "God looks closely anyway. I've seen Him do it."

"Did you know Tomas Lindqvist?" Felix asked quietly.

Marta's hands went still. Just for a moment — a quarter second, the length of a dropped call. Then they resumed, steady as ever.

"I knew a boy who drove a cab," she said. "Nice boy. Bad luck." She began folding the polishing cloth with great care. "Bad luck runs in certain families. Like chin dimples." She tucked the cloth under the trolley's velvet and straightened. "Place nineteen, yes? Second fish course comes from the left, wine from the right. If you drop your napkin, leave it — they'll bring another. And Ms. Eriksen." She paused at the door, her broad back filling the frame. "Eat the soup. Whatever else happens tomorrow, eat the soup. It's the best thing old Henrik makes, and it would be a shame to waste it on a night like this one's going to be."

She left. Felix stood alone in the dining room, surrounded by twenty-two settings and thirty years of shining silver, and felt the truth of it settle over the room like dust: everyone in this house knew something. The staff knew. The family knew. The walls knew. Thirty years of polishing, and the past shone brighter every year.

* * *

The guest room they'd given Felix was on the third floor, east wing — Petter had explained, with careful neutrality, that Mrs. Voss insisted all guests stay overnight, given the storm. *Trapped,* Felix translated. *She wants us all in the box.*

The room was beautiful in the way museum rooms are beautiful: four-poster bed, a writing desk, a window overlooking the darkening lawn and, beyond it, the black seam of the fjord. Felix set the go-bag by the door — dispatcher habit, packed since chapter one of this whole affair: charger, flashlight, the burner phone, a printout of the alibi timeline annotated in three colors of ink. On the desk, Felix spread the timeline out one more time and went through it, because method was the only prayer Felix knew.

Dahl, 1994: charity gala, photographed arriving at 9:40 p.m., claimed he stayed until midnight. Weather archive: sleet beginning 10:15, roads closed by eleven. The gala venue was forty minutes from Voss House. Either Dahl drove home through closing sleet or he didn't stay till midnight. Either way, his alibi had a hole in it shaped like a harbor.

Reinholt, 1994: emergency call at 11:20 p.m., patient records falsified to show a house call in the opposite direction. The pager log — Astrid had found it, Felix had confirmed it — showed Reinholt's pager activated from a tower two kilometers from Voss House.

Marit, 1994: driving a Meridian cab, log pages razored out, paid to forget. She'd driven Ingrid home soaked to the skin, whispering *it wasn't supposed to be her.*

And Ingrid herself, at the center of it, hiring investigators for thirty years like a woman ringing her own doorbell, hoping someone — anyone — would finally make her stop.

Felix sat back. The lamp hummed. Out the window, the first spit of rain ticked against the glass, early scouts of tomorrow's storm.

The burner phone buzzed. Brandt: *Storm hits noon tomorrow. Gates may lock by six. If you want out before the dinner, leave by four. This is me being honest with you, Eriksen. Last time.*

Felix typed back: *I'm staying.*

The reply took a full minute. Then: *I know. Bring me something I can use. And Eriksen — sit with your back to the wall.*

* * *

Felix went down once more, near ten, to find the kitchen and eat Marta's recommended soup. The corridors had emptied; the staff had retreated to their quarters below stairs, and the house creaked and settled around its secrets. In the dining room doorway, Felix paused.

The silver had been laid. All of it, arranged on the sideboard in ranks — forks, knives, spoons, the serving pieces — catching the low light like a frozen shoal of fish. Tomorrow it would be handled by twenty-two hands, would carry food to twenty-two mouths, would shine through the toast and the lies and whatever happened after the lights went out.

Felix thought about what Marta had said. *It remembers.*

So did Felix. That was the whole job, really. Ten years of remembering other people's last calls, and now one week of remembering a stranger's — a drowned woman with a tremor in her voice who had dialed a cab company because thirty years ago a dispatcher had tried to save a boy named Tomas and failed. The call had been meant for Marit. It had landed on Felix instead. Some inheritances you don't choose. You just answer the line.

Felix ate the soup in the vast empty kitchen, sitting on a stool at the steel counter like a stray cat that had wandered in. It was, as promised, extraordinary. Henrik, a huge silent man who was scrubbing pans, slid a piece of bread onto Felix's plate without being asked and without comment, and Felix understood that this too was a message: *some of us see you, stranger. Eat.*

* * *

Back upstairs, the room was wrong.

Felix knew it before the door was fully open — the dispatcher's sixth sense, the sense that reads a room the way a sailor reads sky. Nothing knocked over, nothing broken. Just... air. Air that had been disturbed. The closet door hung a finger's width wider than Felix had left it. The bed's pillow bore a crease that wasn't Felix's.

Felix stood in the doorway a long moment, back to the wall, listening. Rain on the glass. The house settling. Nothing else.

Then Felix saw it, lying on the writing desk, square and white in the lamplight. An envelope, unsealed, unsigned. Inside, a single card of heavy cream stock, and on it, in block capitals pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

**SOME FARES ARE ONE-WAY.**

No threat in it beyond the words themselves. No signature. But Felix turned the card over, and there — faint, in the cream stock's surface, where a hard pen had written on the sheet above it in some earlier draft — the ghost impression of letters. Felix tilted it under the lamp, breathing shallowly, reading the phantom script like static resolving into signal.

Three words. Just enough.

*Boathouse. Midnight.*

Felix looked at the clock. 11:41. Outside, the rain thickened against the window, and down the dark lawn, barely visible through the wet glass, the boathouse waited at the edge of the black water with its one dim window lit — though Felix was almost certain it had been dark an hour ago.

Nineteen minutes. Felix pocketed the card, checked the flashlight, and reached for the door.
