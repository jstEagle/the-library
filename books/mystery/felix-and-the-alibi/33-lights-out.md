## 33. Lights Out

The toast had ended, but the silence it left behind was worse than any speech.

Ingrid Voss stood at the head of the long table with her glass still raised, her arm steady in a way that made Felix's skin prickle. *Thirty years of keeping faith.* The words hung over the candles like smoke that refused to rise. Around the table, forty faces wore the expressions of people who had heard a eulogy and weren't sure for whom.

Felix sat near the end, wedged between a Voss Shipping vice-president who smelled of gin and a woman whose name they'd already forgotten twice. The seat card had said *F. Eriksen — Meridian Cabs* in the same calligraphy as everything else, as if Ingrid wanted the room to know exactly what she'd invited to dinner. A dispatcher at a dynasty's anniversary. Either an insult no one understood or a message only one person was meant to read.

Felix watched Dahl.

He was three seats down on Ingrid's side of the table, silver-haired and sunlamp-tan, laughing at something the mayor's wife had said. But his left hand kept moving — small, economical gestures, the kind of motion a man makes when his body is busy elsewhere. Twice during the toast, his hand had drifted to his jacket pocket, hovered, and returned to his napkin like a bird deciding against landing.

The third time, it didn't come back out empty.

Felix couldn't see what he'd taken. A phone? A note? Something small enough to close a fist around, something he'd been passing or receiving under the tablecloth while forty witnesses looked at the matriarch instead. Felix thought of Astrid Holm's apartment, the clippings arranged with such care, the final page torn away. People in this family didn't pass notes. They passed leverage.

Dahl caught them looking.

It lasted half a second — the old man's eyes sliding across the candlelight, finding Felix's face, and stopping there with the mild surprise of a man noticing a stain on a tablecloth. Then he smiled, raised his glass a centimeter in Felix's direction, and drank.

*Some fares are one-way.* The note under the depot door last night. Felix turned their water glass a quarter-turn on the cloth and made themselves breathe. Panic was a luxury. Panic was what happened to people who hadn't spent ten years listening to a city fall apart one voice at a time and learned to keep the board clear anyway.

Across the table, Nora was watching too. She'd been seated at Ingrid's right hand — a peace offering, or a hostage placement — and her jaw had that set look Felix had learned to read over bad coffee at the depot kitchen: the look of someone holding a key in her mind that she wasn't ready to use. When she felt Felix's gaze, she glanced down the length of the table toward Dahl, then back. Her eyebrows lifted a fraction. *You saw it too.*

Felix gave the smallest nod.

Outside, the storm that had been threatening all evening finally committed. Rain hit the tall windows of the dining room in sheets, and somewhere deep in the house a door boomed shut. The chandeliers flickered — once, twice — and the room's conversation stuttered with them, forty voices dipping and recovering like birds adjusting to a gust.

Ingrid set down her glass. "More wine," she said to no one in particular, and the staff moved.

Felix counted exits without meaning to. It was an occupational habit, the same way they counted cabs on a Friday night. Main doors behind the head of the table, double-leafed, currently attended by two staff members. Service door by the sideboard, where Sander had been standing most of the evening — except Sander wasn't standing there now. Felix scanned the room's edges, the shadows beyond the candlelight, and found nothing. The caretaker had simply dissolved sometime after the fish course, the way he seemed able to dissolve from every room in this house, thirty years of practice at being furniture.

*Gone,* Felix noted, and filed it next to the note and the grey sedan and the torn page, another entry in the ledger of things wrong with tonight.

The chandeliers flickered again. Longer this time. The vice-president beside Felix chuckled nervously and said something about the grid, about storms, about how nothing worked anymore, and Felix didn't answer because they were listening past him — to the hum of the house itself, the electrical heartbeat under the music, and how it had changed pitch.

Then the lights went out.

Not a flicker. Not a brownout. Total, absolute dark, the kind that has weight, that presses on the eyes. Forty gasps went up at once, a single collective inhale, and then the noise of the room inverted — laughter dying mid-syllable, chairs scraping, someone's glass going over with a bright ceramic crack against hardwood.

"Stay calm," Ingrid's voice cut through, level as a dispatch channel. "Someone find the—"

The shot came from the middle of the room.

One report, flat and enormous in the enclosed dark, the sound bouncing off paneling and glass until it seemed to come from everywhere. Felix's body knew what to do before their mind did — ten years of police scanners had trained the reflex — and they were already off their chair and low, pulling the woman beside them down by the sleeve, when the screaming started.

It came in waves. A man shouting *lights, lights, get the lights*, a woman sobbing in short mechanical bursts, the crash of the far doors being thrown open and rain roaring in through the gap. Somewhere close, very close, footsteps moved fast and unhurried across the floor — not running, *walking*, the way you walk when you know the room better than anyone in it — and then the service door banged and the night swallowed whatever came after.

"Flashlights!" That was Brandt's voice, from the far end of the room, cutting clean through the panic. "Everyone stay where you are. Nobody leaves. *Nobody moves.*"

A phone light bloomed. Then another. The room resolved in pieces — white circles of glare sliding over terrified faces, over spilled wine black on white linen, over the upended centerpiece, and finally, near the head of the table, over Arne Dahl.

He was on the floor between his chair and the sideboard, on his back, one hand pressed high against his chest. Blood moved between his fingers in slow pulses, shockingly red, shockingly *alive* in the phone-light. His mouth was working. No sound came out of it. His eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling, on nothing, on the whole thirty years of it, maybe.

"Call an ambulance," Brandt said, already kneeling, jacket off, hands pressing down over Dahl's. "Now. Somebody — *now.*"

"The phones are dead." It was the vice-president, holding his mobile up like evidence. "No signal. The storm took the tower, or—" He stopped, because everyone was looking at Felix.

Because everyone knew. The cab company person. The one who talked to the city for a living.

Felix was already moving. Later they wouldn't remember crossing the room — just the floor under their dress shoes, wet near the broken doors, and the cold air coming in with the rain, and Nora's hand catching their wrist for half a second as they passed, a grip that said *come back*. The landline in the entrance hall was an old rotary-mounted thing, a house that old kept its copper lines the way it kept its secrets, and Felix lifted the receiver with hands that were, to their own distant surprise, perfectly steady.

Dead line. Not even static. Cut, or drowned, or cut and dressed up as drowned.

Of course. Of course. The gates had been closed since the storm picked up — Sander's gates, Sander's lock code, Sander who had walked out of the room at walking pace while forty guests screamed. The phones were down, the cell towers were drowning, the road to town was a river running through forest, and the ambulance, when it came, would come because someone drove through that river to fetch it.

Which meant the timing of everything now belonged to whoever had planned it.

*Don't think like that. Triage first.*

Felix set the receiver down, turned, and found the room looking at them. Forty faces in scattered phone-light, waiting — not for Ingrid, not for Brandt, but for the person standing at the telephone shelf with a plan. It was the strangest moment of Felix Eriksen's invisible life: a decade of being the voice behind the city's worst nights, and here, finally, was the room.

"Meridian Cabs," they heard themselves say, and some of the panic in the room actually eased at the sound of it, the way callers' did, the way Marit had taught them it would. "Here's what we're doing."

* * *

They ran it like a shift.

Brandt took the wounded man — pressure, pulse, the doctor among the guests, Reinholt's ancient colleague with trembling hands who turned out to be steadier than his reputation. Felix took the board. There was no radio, no console, no screen, so they built one out of what the house offered: the guest ledger from the entrance hall propped on a side table, a fountain pen, and Nora, who appeared at their elbow with her phone's dying flashlight cupped in her palm like a votive.

"Landline's dead," Felix told the room at large, loud enough to carry. "Cell signal's gone. So we do this the old way. I need two drivers — anyone comfortable in bad weather."

Three men stepped forward before the sentence finished. One of them, a Voss Shipping man named Keller, had a four-wheel-drive SUV under the porte-cochère.

"You'll drive to the main road junction. There's a twenty-four-hour petrol station at Hovden crossroads — eleven kilometers, the bridge floods before the junction does, so if water's over the marker stones, you turn around and take the forestry track east, it adds ten minutes but it holds. At the station, call emergency services from the payphone inside — it runs on its own line, it'll work when nothing else does. Tell them gunshot wound, male, seventy-plus, chest, Voss estate, and tell them the gate code is—" Felix looked at Nora.

"Four-four-one-seven," Nora said. "Sander changed it in March."

*Sander changed it.* Another entry in the ledger. Felix wrote it down anyway, because a good dispatcher writes everything down; the page is smarter than the panic.

"Tell them the gate code is four-four-one-seven and that Detective Brandt is on site. Then you wait at the station and guide them back. Both vehicles go together. Nobody drives alone tonight."

Keller and the second man went. The front doors opened on a wall of rain and the wind came through like a living thing, scattering the guest ledger's pages, and Felix pinned them down with a crystal ashtray and kept writing.

By the time the doors closed again, the room had organized itself around Felix's voice the way a night shift organizes around a console. The elderly were moved to the library, out of the sightline of the blood. The weeping woman was given tea by a housekeeper whose hands shook but who did not stop pouring. Someone covered Arne Dahl — still breathing, barely, the doctor murmuring that the bullet had missed the heart by what the doctor called, in a voice of awful professional wonder, *a finger's width* — with a tablecloth that had, an hour earlier, held the good silver.

And through all of it, Felix kept the board. Who was where. Who had seen what. Who had left the room, and when, and whether anyone could account for them.

The list of the unaccounted-for was short.

"Sander," Nora said quietly, at Felix's shoulder. She'd stopped shaking somewhere in the last ten minutes; Felix recognized the transformation, had seen it in themselves a hundred times at three a.m., the moment when fear gets converted into function because function is the only thing keeping you upright. "He's not in the house. I checked the coat room, the kitchens, the stair hall. And Felix — the gun cabinet in the study. It's open. Empty rack where the hunting rifle should be."

"He walked out at a walk," Felix said. "During the blackout. He knew the room in the dark."

"You think he shot Dahl?"

"I think Sander has been silent for thirty years, and tonight he decided he was done being silent, and I think—" Felix stopped. Looked down the ruined length of the dining room, at Ingrid Voss standing apart from everyone, untouched by the chaos the way the eye of a storm is untouched, her hands folded, her face unreadable. "I think there's more than one person in this house who's been waiting thirty years for something to break."

Brandt came over, wiping his hands on a napkin that was already more red than cloth. Up close he looked older, and tired, and more alive than Felix had ever seen him. Rain had gotten in through the broken doors and the detective's hair was plastered flat.

"Dahl's alive," Brandt said. "Barely. If the ambulance comes in time. Did you get a call out?"

"Two men driving to Hovden station now. Payphone there is on its own line."

Brandt stared at them for a moment. "You run a taxi office," he said, "not a war room."

"Same thing most nights."

Something almost like a smile crossed the detective's face and died there. Then it was gone, replaced by the flat cop-look, and he lowered his voice. "Eriksen. The lights didn't fail. I found the breaker panel in the service corridor. Main switch thrown, and the housing pried open — someone killed the power deliberately, from inside, minutes before the shot. Which means our shooter planned the dark. Planned it, waited for it, used it." He glanced toward Ingrid, then away. "And your caretaker's been missing since before the lights went. I want the grounds searched. Quietly. He knows this property better than the survey maps."

"And if he comes back?"

Brandt looked at Felix for a long moment, and in the phone-lit gloom his eyes were the color of wet slate. "Then we find out why a man spends thirty years holding a door shut, and what finally made him let go."

* * *

They searched the grounds in relays through the worst of the rain, pairs of guests with flashlights and umbrellas turning inside out, sweeping the terraces, the garages, the boathouse path. Felix went with Nora, because nobody assigned it and nobody stopped it, and because when the world collapses into triage you keep the person next to you whom you trust, and Felix trusted exactly one person in this house who wasn't bleeding.

The boathouse was locked. The padlock hung intact, which meant either innocence or a second key, and neither of them said which they believed.

"He was there that night," Nora said, as they stood under the boathouse eave, rain hammering the tin roof above them like applause for something terrible. "Thirty years ago. Sander. Grandmother's letters never said it straight, but the dates line up — he was already caretaker then. He's been here the whole time, Felix. Watching. Keeping the place. Keeping *her*." She hugged her arms against the cold. "What if he's not hiding? What if he's guarding?"

"Guarding what?"

"Her. From Dahl." Nora's breath fogged. "Dahl had something in his pocket at dinner. You saw it. What if it was the blackmail — what if he was going to use it *tonight*, in front of everyone, and Sander—" She stopped. Shook her head. "I keep building stories. Marit says that's what this family does. Builds stories instead of saying things."

Down the path, a flashlight beam swung wildly and a voice shouted — one of the searchers, young, breathless, his light fixed on the ground near the boathouse steps.

"Over here! There's — someone needs to see this!"

They ran. The beam was aimed at the mud below the steps, churned by the rain into a gray stew, and printed across it, sharp-edged and fresh, were boot prints. Large ones. Leading away from the boathouse, not toward it, and beside them, half-drowned already, a long dark shape that one of the searchers had fished out with a stick and now held up dripping in the flashlight glow.

A rifle scabbard. Empty.

And tucked inside the canvas flap, folded to a size that fit a jacket pocket, a piece of paper gone soft with rain. Nora took it with both hands, careful as a relic, and held the light over it, and Felix read the words upside down before she could turn them around.

It was a page torn from a notebook. Astrid's notebook — Felix knew the paper, knew the faint blue ruling, had handled enough of it in that apartment to know it in the dark. Most of the ink had run, the rain doing in ten minutes what thirty years of money and silence had failed to do. But one line near the top had been written in pen pressed hard enough to survive the water, and it was in Astrid's handwriting, and it said:

*If anything happens to me, it isn't Ingrid who'll pull the trigger. Ask Sander who he's protected all these years — and ask him what Dahl holds over them both.*

Below that, the rest had dissolved into blue-gray ghosts.

Nora looked up. Rain ran off her chin. "She knew," she whispered. "Right up to the end, she *knew*, and she hid the page in the boathouse because she thought—" 

Headlights swept the tree line. Two sets, coming fast up the drive through the rain — the SUV returning, and behind it, blue-white and strobing, the ambulance, twelve minutes out from Hovden station, twelve minutes that Felix had bought with a ledger and a fountain pen and a voice that had learned, over ten years of nights, how to be calm in other people's catastrophes.

But Felix wasn't looking at the ambulance. They were looking past it, up the drive, to where the storm-light showed a figure standing at the edge of the trees — still as a post, soaked through, hands empty and raised slightly from his sides, the universal posture of surrender.

Sander had come back.

And he was walking, at that same unhurried pace, straight toward the house where Ingrid Voss waited with her hands folded — and toward Detective Brandt, who was already striding down the steps into the rain to meet him.
