## 39. New Routes

The new dispatcher's name was Yusuf, and he was twenty-three years old and terrified of the radio.

Felix watched him from the doorway of the dispatch room on his first Monday morning — the boy hunched over the console like it might bite, headset too big for his ears, a laminated cheat sheet of zone codes taped to the desk where he could see it. Outside, October rain came down in long grey sheets, streaking the depot windows, turning the yard into a mirror of sodium light.

"You don't have to talk fast," Felix said, coming up behind him. "That's the first rule nobody teaches you. Everyone thinks dispatching is speed. It's not. It's listening."

Yusuf jumped, then laughed at himself for jumping. "Sorry. I keep waiting for it to get loud."

"It gets loud," Felix said. "Friday nights, it gets loud. But even then, you're not the one driving. You're the one who knows where everyone is. That's the whole job. Be the person who knows."

They had hired him through the usual channels — an ad, three interviews, a background check that came back clean as new snow — but Felix had privately decided, somewhere around the second interview, that what they were really doing was hiring someone to sit in the chair. Not Marit's chair; Marit's chair had been retired with her, wheeled down to the break room and claimed by no one, a small monument with a torn vinyl seat. The dispatch chair itself. The one that faced the city all night and kept its voice.

"Zone four," Yusuf said suddenly, pointing at the board. "Car twelve wants — is that a hospital run?"

"Read it back to him first. Always read it back."

"Meridian to Car Twelve, confirming pickup, Hagegata and — " He stumbled over the cross street, corrected himself, finished. On the radio, Car Twelve's driver — old Petter, who had driven nights since before Felix had learned to drive — grunted an acknowledgment that in Petter's language was practically a sonnet.

"There," Felix said. "You just did the job."

Yusuf grinned, and for a second Felix saw their own younger self in it — the first-year dispatcher who had once sat in that same chair believing the city was a puzzle you could solve if you just listened hard enough. They had been mostly right. The parts they'd been wrong about had taken thirty years and two dead people to surface.

But that was done now. Mostly done. The trial was scheduled for spring; Dahl's lawyers were already talking plea agreements in the newspapers like men discussing weather. Sander had pleaded guilty to manslaughter in September, in a courtroom so quiet you could hear the radiators ticking, and Ingrid Voss had attended every session of her own preliminary hearing sitting perfectly straight in the front row, as though it were a recital she had paid for.

Which, in a way, she had.

"Break room's through there," Felix said. "Coffee's terrible. Nora brings better stuff when she visits, but she hoards it, so don't count on it."

"Nora Voss?" Yusuf said, and then went pink. "Sorry. I read the papers. Everybody read the papers."

"Yeah." Felix looked out at the rain. "Everybody did."

* * *

Nora came on Thursdays.

It had started by accident, three weeks after the dinner — she had appeared at the depot door at eleven at night holding a thermos and wearing the expression of someone who had talked herself into a thing on the drive over and couldn't remember why. She'd said something about not being able to sleep in the house anymore, even though she'd moved most of her things out of Voss House by then, even though the estate was being sold in parcels and the boathouse had been turned over to the police and then, quietly, to no one.

"I can't sleep anywhere," she'd said, standing in the doorway with the rain behind her. "So I thought I'd come bother you instead."

"You brought coffee," Felix had said.

"I brought *good* coffee. There's a difference, and you're about to learn it."

Now it was ritual. Thursdays, ten-thirty, the break room. She'd spread her work across one of the tables — she was doing something with architectural preservation these days, drafting surveys of buildings the city wanted to tear down, arguing with committees by email until three in the morning — and Felix would make the terrible depot coffee anyway, out of spite, and she would drink it and wince and say nothing, because that was part of the ritual too.

Tonight she arrived early, shaking rain off her coat, and dropped onto the bench across from Felix with the sigh of the deeply tired.

"The Halvard Bakery building," she said, by way of greeting. "1897. Gorgeous brickwork. The developer wants a parking structure. I spent six hours today explaining to a man in a very expensive suit why a cornice matters."

"Did he listen?"

"He listened the way my grandmother listened to me." She said it lightly, but Felix heard the weight underneath, the way you always did with Nora — grief worn casually, like a coat she'd gotten used to carrying. "He nodded and did what he wanted."

"How is she?" Felix asked. It was the question they asked each other every Thursday, and the answer was always the same shape.

"Ingrid's in the care facility in Bærum. She likes the garden. She doesn't like the other residents." Nora wrapped both hands around her mug. "She asked about you last week. She wanted to know if you were still dispatching. I told her yes. She said — " Nora paused, choosing the words carefully, setting each one down like glass. "She said, 'Good. Someone should be listening.'"

Felix turned their mug slowly on the table. "That sounds like her."

"She also left you something. In the will — the real one, the one her lawyers found after everything. Not the money. She knew you wouldn't take the money." Nora reached into her bag and set a small flat parcel on the table, wrapped in brown paper, tied with string. "I'm supposed to give it to you 'when the time felt right.' Which apparently means tonight, because I've been carrying it around for two weeks feeling ridiculous."

Felix untied the string. Inside was a photograph, old and soft-cornered: five young people on the steps of Meridian Cabs, 1994, laughing at something outside the frame. Tomas Lindqvist front and center, arm slung around a young Marit. And beside them, half-turned away from the camera, a woman Felix hadn't noticed in Astrid's version of this photo — a girl with dark hair and a crooked smile, caught mid-laugh.

Lene Bakke. The girl in the water. Alive, here, laughing, on the steps of the depot where Felix had spent ten years listening to the city's last calls.

On the back, in Ingrid's small precise hand: *I should have been brave enough to say their names. Do it better than I did.*

Felix sat with it for a long moment. The radiator ticked. Somewhere out in the yard, a car door slammed.

"We're going to put it up," Felix said finally. "In the dispatch room. Both names. Tomas and Lene. Where everyone who sits in that chair has to see them."

Nora reached across the table and squeezed their hand, brief and hard, and neither of them said anything else, because there wasn't anything else that needed saying, and because they had both learned — separately, expensively — the value of silence that holds instead of hides.

* * *

Brandt's emails arrived without salutations or sign-offs, like telegrams from a country with limited ink.

*Forensics closed the boathouse materials. Log pages authenticated. Lindqvist file formally reclassified. Dahl's cooperation deal holding — he's given us Reinholt's prescription records from '94, which puts the doctor at the harbor the night of the Jane Doe retrieval. Case against Ingrid Voss will proceed under guardianship provisions given her condition. Her counsel says she intends to plead guilty to obstruction and accessory charges. First time in my career a client has fired a lawyer for suggesting self-defense.*

Then, a week later:

*Reinholt died Tuesday. Heart, in his sleep, his nurse says. His statement dies with him unless Dahl's testimony covers it. It does. Barely.*

And then, the one Felix read three times:

*Cold case unit pulled dental records on the '94 Jane Doe. Confirmed Bakke. Her sister lives in Tromsø. I called her myself. She said — and I'm quoting because I don't know what else to do with it — "We stopped looking in '95. Thank you for not stopping."*

*You didn't stop either. Don't think I haven't noticed what that cost you.*

*— IB* (the dash and initials appearing only once, in the last email, like a door opened a crack and quickly shut).

Felix printed that one and kept it folded in a drawer of the dispatch desk, along with the cream invitation from the anniversary dinner, the threatening note (*Some fares are one-way*), and the torn edge of Astrid Holm's final page. A museum of the worst autumn of their life, filed between spare fuses and outdated zone maps. Some nights, late, when the radio was quiet, Felix would open the drawer and look at it all and feel — not peace, exactly. Something adjacent to peace. The feeling of a debt acknowledged, if not yet paid.

Because it wasn't paid. Not fully. Sander was inside, Dahl would be inside, Ingrid would die in a care facility under state supervision rather than a prison cell, and none of it returned Tomas Lindqvist to the road or gave Lene Bakke back her thirty years. Justice, Felix had learned, was not a restoration. It was a ledger entry. You wrote the truth down where everyone could see it, and you lived with the balance never coming right.

But you wrote it down. That was the part that mattered. That was the part Marit had finally done, testifying for two days in a witness box with her hands folded and her voice steady, telling the whole story at last — the payment, the silence, the soaked woman in the back seat whispering *it wasn't supposed to be her.* When it was over, Marit had walked out of the courthouse into a wall of cameras and found Felix waiting, and had said only: "Your shift starts in four hours. Try to look awake."

* * *

November came down over the city like a lid.

Felix trained Yusuf through it — the ice protocols, the fog routes, the particular madness of the Christmas-party season when every corporate account in the district called at once. The boy was good. Better than good. He listened the way Felix had tried to teach him, and then some nights he listened in ways Felix hadn't taught, catching things in drivers' voices that Felix had needed years to hear. Once, at two in the morning, he flagged a run to the east side and quietly added a note to the driver: *passenger may be distressed, take the long way, keep her talking.* Later the driver called in and said, "Whoever wrote that note — good eyes." Yusuf glowed for the rest of the shift.

"That's the job," Felix told him. "You just did the invisible part. Nobody will ever know you did it. That's how you know it counted."

The crew had changed shape since September. Petter had retired — actually retired, not the way drivers always threatened to — and shown up at his own farewell party drunk on aquavit and sentimentality, weeping openly about the seats of cars he'd missed. Two new hires, a single mother named Camilla who drove days and studied accounting at night, and a laconic ex-paramedic named Odd who had seen enough emergencies to find dispatching relaxing. The break room wall got a new addition: a corkboard where anyone could pin anything — kids' drawings, football scores, a photo of Petter's boat. Nora contributed a postcard of the Halvard Bakery building with *SAVED (for now)* written across it in red marker.

Small rituals. Rebuilt ones. Felix had spent a decade believing they were beneath notice, the coffee and the corkboard and the Thursday visits, background noise to the real business of surviving the night. Now they understood them differently. They were the load-bearing walls. Take them out and the whole building came down.

One Thursday near the end of November, Nora stayed later than usual. The depot had gone quiet — the lull between the theater crowds and the bar closings — and they sat in the dispatch room together, Nora in the spare chair swiveled toward the window, watching the rain, while Felix worked the board with half an ear.

"My mother called," Nora said eventually. "First time since August. She wants to know if I'll come home for Christmas."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her I'd think about it." She turned the chair a quarter-turn, toward Felix. "I might actually go. Is that strange? After everything?"

"No," Felix said. "That's the point of all of it, isn't it? Getting to go home. However you define home."

"And how do you define it?"

Felix looked around the room — the board, the headset, the photograph going up on the wall tomorrow with two names written beneath it in permanent marker, the boy asleep in the break room chair waiting for his ride, the city glittering wet beyond the glass, forty thousand fares a week, every one of them somebody's whole world for twenty minutes.

"Ask me in a few weeks," Felix said. "There's a date coming up."

Nora knew immediately. He saw it land in her face — December ninth, the anniversary, the date Tomas Lindqvist had picked up his last fare and never clocked out. She reached over and took Felix's hand again, and this time she didn't let go right away.

"Whatever you're planning," she said, "I'm coming with you."

Felix smiled and turned back to the board, where Car Nine was calling in from the docks, and the night rolled on, and the city kept talking, and for the first time in ten years, Felix Eriksen was not afraid of what it might say next.

Almost.

Because that evening, sorting the depot mail, Felix found an envelope with no stamp and no return address, hand-delivered sometime during the day shift. Inside was a single sheet, typed, in the same font as the booking confirmation that had started everything — the one from Astrid Holm, the prepaid fare, the request that had unraveled thirty years of silence.

*Thank you for finishing the route,* it read. *One fare remains outstanding. December 9th. The address is enclosed. Some passengers have waited a long time for someone to pick them up.*

Below it, in handwriting Felix recognized from the photograph's backing — small, precise, deliberate — was an address Felix had never heard of, and a time: *11:47 p.m., the exact minute of his last call.*

Felix stood in the empty depot with the letter in their hands, listening to the rain, and understood that Ingrid Voss had arranged one last thing before the end — and that on December 9th, at 11:47 p.m., someone was going to be waiting for a cab.
