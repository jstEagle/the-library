## 10. Shelf Life

The courthouse smelled like pencil shavings and old carpet, and Lior Reyes sat in the witness box for four hours while a lawyer with kind eyes and a prosecutor's patience asked them to say it all out loud.

They had rehearsed this. Not the words — those came easy, worn smooth by repetition, by the confession written in longhand at the kitchen table above the shop with the statute expiring at midnight and the pen moving anyway. What they'd rehearsed was the moment after. The silence that follows a confession in a room full of strangers. The way forty faces turn toward you at once, and you understand, finally and completely, what it means to be seen.

It hadn't killed them. That was the surprise. That was still the surprise, weeks later, standing behind the counter of Brightwater Books with the morning sun cutting through the front window and six people browsing the shelves before ten a.m.

Six people. Before ten.

The bell over the door chimed — a sound Lior had once considered replacing because it rang too honestly, announcing every arrival, every departure, no privacy anywhere. They kept it now. It felt like a promise kept daily.

"Excuse me," said a woman in a green coat, holding up a battered paperback of *The Hollow Season*, one of Lior's own cases rendered into someone else's bestseller. "Is it true? That you're the detective from the Crane trial? The one who—"

"Yes," Lior said.

The woman blinked. She had clearly prepared for denial, for deflection, for the whole choreography of a person who didn't want to be found. "Oh," she said. "I — I bought two of your mysteries last month. Before I knew. They're better knowing, actually. You can tell where you learned things." She said it like a compliment. It landed like one, which was its own small earthquake.

"Can I sign it for you?"

She nearly dropped the book getting it to the counter.

* * *

The testimony itself had been worse than they expected and easier than they feared, which is to say it was exactly like most true things. Lior had stood in the well of the courtroom and described, in plain language, the crime they'd buried nineteen years ago: the evidence suppressed, the report rewritten, the small administrative murder of a case that would have convicted a man who walked free for another decade. Felix Crane's first life, the one before he died the first time.

The gallery had been quiet. Marsh's lawyer had been loud, objecting on principle, but the principle had rotted through months ago when the forged autopsy surfaced from the archive basement along with everything else. Detective Aldous Marsh sat at the defense table in a suit that no longer fit him right, as if his body had finally caught up to what he'd been carrying. He did not look at Lior during the testimony. He looked at his hands, which were folded neatly, the way he used to fold them across case files when he already knew how a story ended and was waiting for everyone else to catch up.

Lior had thought seeing him would feel like victory. It mostly felt like weather. A cold front moving through and out.

"You understood," the prosecutor asked near the end, "that by confessing, you exposed yourself to prosecution?"

"I understood that the alternative was being an accessory to murder," Lior said. "I'd done the math on that trade before. Nineteen years ago I got it wrong."

"And now?"

"Now I got it right."

The sentence they received was suspended — cooperation, timing, the fact that their confession had cracked open a conspiracy involving a faked death, a forged autopsy, and eleven years of a dead man walking free under three different names. The judge, a dry woman named Okafor who had read the entire file twice, looked at Lior over her glasses and said, "You traded your hiding place for the truth. The court notes the exchange rate was unfavorable to you personally." Then she suspended the sentence, revoked what remained of any official standing Lior might have claimed, and told them to go home.

Home had been the bookshop. Home had been climbing the stairs past the mystery section, past the shelf where Elias Vann had left his marginalia in a copy of *Anatomy of a Cold Case* — the book Lior still couldn't reshelved without touching the spine like a talisman — and putting the kettle on and listening to the building settle around them like an old dog circling before sleep.

That had been five weeks ago. The shop had never been this busy. Not once, not even in the good years, the years before Lior stopped pretending the register mattered more than the stock.

* * *

Elias Vann came in on a Thursday, which had become his day, though he arrived on other days too and pretended it was accidental. He moved slower than before — the knife had gone deep along his ribs, a wound taken turning back toward Lior in the archive basement when Crane lunged, and the doctors said he'd carry the scar the rest of his life. He carried it the way he carried everything: quietly, precisely, with the air of a man who had catalogued it and filed it where it belonged.

He was thinner than the gaunt stranger who had walked in with three months' rent in cash. But the nervousness was gone, burned away somewhere between the basement and the hospital, and what remained was a face that finally matched its own eyes.

"The trial coverage called you 'the bookseller-detective,'" Vann said, setting two paper cups on the counter. He'd started bringing coffee instead of accepting it, some private arithmetic of debt he refused to explain. "One outlet called you 'the reluctant witness.' My favorite was the tabloid. 'SHELF-POLTERGEIST OF JUSTICE.'"

"They were reaching."

"They were reaching, yes. But there's something in the phrase. Something about a person who keeps stories and then becomes one." He lowered himself onto the stool behind the counter — his stool now, unspoken, the way certain objects migrate to certain people — and peeled the lid off his cup. "How does it feel? Being a story?"

Lior considered the question honestly, because Vann had earned honest answers the hard way, with blood and rib cartilage.

"It feels like the shop looks," they said. "Brighter than I'm used to. Slightly out of my control." They nodded at the front window, where a man was photographing the display — the trial books, the newspaper clippings Lior had neither put up nor taken down, arranged by some customer into a small shrine of the case. "For nineteen years I curated my own invisibility. Every interaction was a calculation. Could this person place me? Had my name circulated? I was very good at it. Being unseen was the one case I never closed."

"And now?"

"Now a woman asked me to sign a book about my worst professional failure, and I wrote 'To Margaret — may all your cases close cleanly,' and she cried a little, and I felt—" Lior searched for it. "Lighter. Which is obscene. I should feel flayed. Everyone can see me. The department can see me. Whoever Crane answered to, if anyone, can see me. And instead I keep waiting for the dread and it doesn't come. It's like a phantom limb. I keep reaching for the fear and finding shelf inventory."

Vann smiled into his coffee. "Survivorship has bad marketing. Everyone expects trauma to be the permanent condition. Nobody tells you the strangest part is the ordinary morning after."

"The ordinary morning after," Lior repeated. "That's the title of your memoir, if you ever write one."

"I don't write. I read backwards, remember?" He said it easily now, the old joke between them, the phrase Lior had once used to describe how he reconstructed crimes from archived case notes — reading history in reverse until it confessed. "Besides, if anyone writes our story, it'll be you. You've been drafting it since chapter one. Don't think I didn't notice the notebook."

Lior had not hidden the notebook particularly well. It lived under the register, and it held fragments — the stranger with the envelope of cash, the sealed file bearing a retired badge number, the ten days counted down like a fuse, the trap built from a killer's own methodology. Lior wasn't sure yet whether it was a book or just a way of setting the past down gently enough that it stopped rattling.

"Maybe," they said.

"'Maybe' from you is practically a signed contract."

* * *

The afternoon thinned the way good afternoons do, customers arriving and leaving in gentle tides, and at some point Vann got up to shelve a returns cart because he could never sit still among misfiled books — a records clerk to the marrow, everything in its place, everything accounted for. Lior watched him slide a misplaced volume home and thought about the version of him from the file: the clerk who found discrepancies in a decade-old case, who followed them all the way down to a retired detective hiding behind a counter, who engineered an entire murder-that-wasn't to flush a killer into the light. Bait and prey and architect, all at once. Lior had been furious when they figured it out. The fury had lasted approximately nine days, and then Crane had come up out of the archive dark and Vann had turned back into the knife, and fury had retired permanently in favor of something with no clean name.

Gratitude, maybe. Or the particular bond of two people who had each spent years being someone slightly false, and had met at the exact point where the falseness failed.

"Reyes." Vann paused at the mystery shelf, his hand resting on the spine of *Anatomy of a Cold Case*. "There's mail for you upstairs. Came this morning. I signed for it because the courier looked confused by the name."

"What name?"

"Yours. Your actual one. On official letterhead." He brought the envelope down and set it beside the register without ceremony, but his eyes stayed on Lior's face, doing that thing he did — reading backwards, reading the present tense of a person for signs of what came next.

Lior turned it over. Heavy cream stock. An embossed seal they recognized from another lifetime: the Office of Internal Review, the unit formed after the Crane proceedings to audit every case Marsh had ever touched. Eleven hundred cases, the papers said. Someone had to consult on the old ones. Someone who knew how Marsh thought, how the files were built, how a story could be assembled backward from its ending.

Someone who read forwards now, having spent so long reading backwards.

Lior held the envelope and felt the old reflex rise — the calculation, the exit mapping, the instinct to become smaller. And beneath it, steady as the floorboards, the newer thing: the weight of the suspended sentence, the signed confession in the courthouse record, the name in the newspapers, Margaret's tears over an inscription. All of it visible. All of it survived.

"Well?" Vann said.

Lior slit the envelope open with the letter opener shaped like a dagger — a gift from a customer, obviously — and read the first line. Then the second. Then set it down on the counter between the coffee cups, in full view of the bright window and the bell over the door and anyone who cared to look.

"They want me to come in Monday," Lior said. "Officially. With my name on the door of the meeting room and everything."

Vann raised his cup. "To visibility."

"To shelf life," Lior said, and drank.
