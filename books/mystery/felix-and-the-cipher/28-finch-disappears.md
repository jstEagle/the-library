## 28. Finch Disappears

The call came at seven in the morning, and it wasn't from Finch.

It was from the pastor of the Methodist church, a mild man named Reverend Ellsworth who had spent the last two weeks pretending not to know why Jonah kept asking about hymn numbers from 1988. His voice on the phone was thin with something between apology and fear.

"Mr. Yarrow. I don't know if this is my business to tell you, but Walter didn't come to the archive committee meeting last night. He's never missed one. Not in thirty years." A pause, filled with the sound of a man deciding how much trouble he wanted. "And his car is still in his driveway. But his porch light was on all night, and his newspaper is still in the yard."

Jonah stood in the back room of the shop, coffee going cold in his hand, and felt the particular dread that only comes when a pattern you've been dreading finally completes itself. He had spent forty years learning what people looked like right before they ran. He'd seen it in witnesses, in suspects, once—shamefully—in himself, standing in front of the chief's desk with his resignation letter folded in his pocket like a confession.

"Has anyone knocked?" Jonah asked.

"Mrs. Teller next door did. No answer. She says she saw him Tuesday evening carrying boxes to his car, and then yesterday morning very early, before light, loading more. She thought he was reorganizing. Walter was always reorganizing."

*Boxes.* Jonah closed his eyes. "Reverend, has anyone called the police?"

"I called Chief Okafor's office. They said they'd send someone by." Another pause. "They said that yesterday too."

Of course they had. Okafor was reinstated barely a week, her authority still held together with tape and the state investigator's presence, and every call that touched the Vane matter now went through three layers of people who wanted it to go away. The machine Ashford had built over forty years didn't stop working just because one gear had been replaced. It simply ground slower, waiting for the moment it could seize up again.

"I'll go out there," Jonah said. "If that's all right."

"It's your business more than mine," Ellsworth said quietly, and hung up before Jonah could decide whether that was an accusation.

* * *

Finch's house sat on Larch Street in the kind of neighborhood where the maples had been planted by men who expected to watch them grow for fifty years, and their grandchildren now complained about the roots. It was a small brick house with white trim, tidy the way everything Walter Finch touched was tidy—the house of a man who alphabetized his spices and dated his receipts. Jonah had been inside twice: once years ago, on some civic errand he couldn't even remember, and once eleven days ago, after Finch had confessed through tears in the back of the bookshop that he'd spent decades quietly editing the town's official memory for people whose names he mostly hadn't known until it was too late.

The second visit had ended with Finch handing over a cardboard box of original documents—the minutes of the Ashford Development Company, the correspondence, the machinery of the lie laid bare—and then sitting down heavily in a kitchen chair and saying, "I kept copies, you understand. All these years. In case they ever decided I was worth more dead than useful."

That sentence had been rattling around in Jonah's head since. He'd told himself it was Finch being careful, Finch being the archivist to the bone. Now, standing on the curb looking at the uncollected newspaper lying in dew-soaked grass, he understood it differently. Copies weren't just insurance against murder. Copies were merchandise.

Mrs. Teller was already on her porch, a small woman in a cardigan despite the warming morning, watching him with the frank appetite of someone who had been waiting all day for exactly this visitor. "You're the bookshop man," she said. "You're the one causing all the fuss."

"That's me," Jonah said. "Mrs. Teller—did Mr. Finch say anything to you? Before he left?"

"He didn't *say* he was leaving." She descended one step, lowering her voice as though the house itself might be listening. "But Wednesday night he knocked on my door around nine o'clock, which he never does, and asked if I'd keep an eye on the place. Said he might be going to see his sister in Ohio for a while. Walter doesn't have a sister in Ohio. I've known him thirty-one years. He has a brother in Tucson who doesn't speak to him."

"What did you say?"

"I said that sounded nice." Her mouth went flat. "And then I watched him carry four boxes out to that old Buick of his, and I thought, well, whatever Walter's mixed up in, it finally got big enough to make him run. Because that's what that was, Mr. Yarrow. That wasn't packing for a trip. That was a man taking everything he couldn't stand to lose and getting out from under it."

"Did anyone else come by? Anyone visit him this week?"

She considered, and Jonah watched her weigh gossip against fear and find them nearly equal. "A car Tuesday night. Parked down at the corner with its lights off, maybe an hour. Dark sedan. I mind my own business, so I didn't get the plate." She hesitated again. "There was a man walked past the house twice that evening, slow. Young-ish. Big shoulders. He looked in windows—not sneaky, just looking, like a man checking stock in a store."

Marcus's muscle. Or Reed's. The distinction had stopped mattering weeks ago; the money flowed downhill from the same source either way.

"Thank you," Jonah said. "You've been more help than you know."

"I haven't helped anybody," Mrs. Teller said firmly. "I saw nothing, and if anyone official asks, that's what I'll say. But you—" she pointed a knuckly finger at him, "—you find out what happened to Walter. Whatever he did, he watered my roses when I had my hip surgery, and he never once asked me a question about any of it. There aren't many like that left in this town."

* * *

The back door was unlocked.

Jonah stood in Finch's kitchen with his gloves on—he'd started carrying them again, along with a flashlight and a growing collection of habits he'd sworn off—and took inventory the way he'd once taken inventory of a hundred scenes. The kitchen was clean but not *finished*. A mug sat in the drying rack. A calendar on the wall still showed appointments through the end of the month: archive committee, dentist, a lunch marked *R.—discuss*. Someone had crossed out the archive committee entry with a single hard line.

In the living room, the story sharpened. The bookshelves were half-empty, gaps in the rows like missing teeth, and the gaps were not random. Every folder, every box, every bound volume that could have contained paper had been removed. What remained was decorative: novels, some framed photographs, a ceramic owl. On the desk, the blotter had been lifted and set aside—a detail that made the hair on Jonah's arms rise, because no fleeing man bothers to lift the blotter unless he's retrieving what was hidden beneath it. The drawer of the filing cabinet stood open and scraped clean. Even the hanging folders were gone; he'd taken those too, the empty green skeletons of them, because a folder can carry fingerprints or fibers or a stray page someone forgot.

This was not abduction. Abduction leaves coffee mid-sip and doors ajar and a life interrupted mid-sentence. This was a life *concluded*, deliberately, by the man living it. Walter Finch had not been taken. Walter Finch had executed a withdrawal.

Except.

Jonah stood in the middle of the room and let the wrongness surface, the way he'd taught younger detectives to do—stop thinking, let the scene talk. And the scene said: *the copies are gone.*

Not the originals. He'd given Jonah the originals, the true documents, the ones that could hang everyone including himself. If Finch had simply fled—if he'd panicked, grabbed what mattered, run for Ohio-that-didn't-exist—he would have taken the originals back, or at least told Jonah to return them. Instead he'd left the truth in Jonah's hands and taken only his own duplicates. Duplicates that proved nothing in court, that carried no chain of custody, that were worthless as evidence and priceless as leverage.

Or as payment.

Jonah walked slowly through the rest of the house. The bedroom closet gaped, emptied of the boxes Finch had hoarded for decades. In the bathroom, the medicine cabinet was stripped—even the aspirin gone, which was the detail that finally settled it. Nobody running from killers forgets the aspirin. A man planning to vanish takes the aspirin. A man *negotiating* takes the aspirin. Only a man who wants to look like a victim—or who has been instructed to stage a departure—leaves a scene this carefully ambiguous.

He found two more things. The first was small: on the kitchen table, under the crossed-out calendar, a slip of notepaper in Finch's cramped, precise hand, a single line:

*Some prices are only quoted once.*

No signature. No date. It read like a note to himself, something copied down or composed in the dark hours before leaving, and Jonah turned it over twice looking for more and found nothing. It could have been a fragment of anything. It could have been a message meant to be found. With Finch you could never be sure; the man had spent his whole career hiding meaning inside ordinary paper.

The second thing was in the garage, and it stopped Jonah cold.

The Buick was there.

Mrs. Teller had said his car was in the driveway, and it was—but it was the wrong car, or rather, it was the Buick stripped of purpose: keys on the workbench, doors unlocked, glovebox open and empty. And beside it, where Finch's lawnmower had stood on Jonah's last visit, was nothing. The mower was gone. So was the snow shovel, the coil of garden hose, the toolbox. The garage had been picked over for anything convertible to cash.

A man paid handsomely to disappear doesn't sell his lawnmower first.

Unless the payment wasn't handsome. Unless the payment was merely *enough*—enough to settle whatever debts had been quietly accumulated against him, enough to start over smaller and farther away, enough to buy the one thing Ashford's powerful families had always purchased cheaply from men like Walter Finch: his absence.

Jonah leaned against the workbench and stared at the bare concrete floor and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he had misread Finch from the beginning. Not entirely—the terror had been real, the tears in the bookshop had been real, the thirty years of guilt had been real. But terror and greed are not opposites. They are neighbors. They share a wall, and in the small hours a man can hear both of them talking, and sometimes the louder voice wins.

Someone had come to Finch—probably the man with the broad shoulders, probably Tuesday night, probably with an offer structured exactly the way such offers are always structured: here is what happens to you if you stay, and here is what happens to you if you go. And somewhere in that conversation, the price of Jonah's trust had been quoted, and Finch had discovered that his loyalty, like everything else in Ashford, had a market value.

*Allies are currency here,* Jonah thought, walking back through the empty house. *Spent freely. By everyone. Including me.* Because he had spent Finch too—had taken the man's confession and his documents and his trembling courage and deployed them like assets, without ever asking what it would cost Finch to be the kind of man who confessed. He had treated the historian as a source. The town had treated him as a tool. And in the end, Finch had done what every currency eventually does: he'd converted himself into something spendable and left the economy behind.

At the door, Jonah paused. On the hall table, neatly squared to the edge, lay the spare key to the house, and beneath it, weighted by a stone from the reservoir shore—Jonah recognized the pale granite, the same stone that had capped the tin at the mill ruins—a folded piece of paper.

His name was written on the outside. Just *Jonah*, in Finch's hand. First name, no title, no surname. Whatever else Walter Finch had sold, he had apparently wanted one honest thing delivered.

Jonah put on a fresh glove and opened it.

*They know about the organ.*

Four words. Jonah read them three times, and each time the floor seemed to tilt a little further. Marsh's final letter, decoded only days ago, had named the hymn-number chamber inside the church organ as the resting place of her master copy—the complete record, the document that ended everything. He had told no one except Okafor, and Okafor had told the state investigator, and the state investigator was supposed to be clean. Or he had been overheard. Or Finch had known all along, because Finch knew everything that moved through this town's paper and most of what moved through its silences, and when the price was finally quoted, he had paid with the only coin he had left: information.

And yet he'd left the warning. Sold the secret and mailed the receipt. Jonah couldn't decide whether that was conscience or cruelty—whether Finch was apologizing or simply making sure Jonah would know, forever, exactly what his trust had bought.

Outside, the morning had brightened into a hard, indifferent blue. Down the street, a dark sedan idled at the corner with its lights off, and Jonah stood very still on Finch's porch and looked at it, and it looked back—or rather, the shape behind the windshield did, motionless, patient, doing nothing illegal at all. After a long moment it pulled away, unhurried, and turned toward the center of town.

Toward the church.

Jonah was moving before the thought finished forming, down the steps and across the wet grass to his own car, fumbling the keys, the slip of paper crushed in his fist like a heart he was trying to keep beating. *They know about the organ.* The master copy was still there—it had to still be there, nobody had reached it yet, Finch's note was the proof that they were on their way and not that they'd arrived—

He was halfway to Larch Street's end when his phone buzzed against the wheel. Okafor.

"Jonah." Her voice was flat and fast in a way he'd learned to dread. "Where are you?"

"Finch's house. He's gone. Took everything, left the country, left a note—"

"Forget Finch." A breath, and beneath it, faintly, sirens—not near her, near *somewhere*. "There's smoke over on Chestnut. The Methodist church is on fire."
