## 31. Threats Converge

The message came at 9:47 in the morning, typed on a card of heavy cream stock that had no business being hand-delivered to a failing bookshop on Marsh Street.

*Your mother and I should talk,* it said. *Before things get expensive.*

No signature. No return address. The courier—a kid on a bicycle who couldn't have been more than nineteen—was already halfway down the block when Jonah stepped out onto the sidewalk holding the card like something dead.

He stood there a long moment, the gray light of an October morning pooling around him, the smell of wet leaves and diesel from the delivery trucks on Route 9. *Your mother.* As if Cordelia Vane were a shared acquaintance. As if Marcus Vane hadn't sat across from him three weeks ago with a checkbook and a smile, probing gently at the edges of what Jonah knew, the way a man tests ice before committing his weight.

Jonah turned the card over. Blank. He read the front again. *Before things get expensive.*

It wasn't subtle. It wasn't meant to be. Subtlety was for men who still believed the town would protect them. Marcus Vane had learned—perhaps from his mother's careful silence after Jonah's visit, perhaps from the network of eyes that Ashford's old families kept the way other towns kept weather stations—that Jonah Yarrow had gone to Wrenfield House and sat with Cordelia for forty minutes, and that whatever passed between them had made her cancel two charity appearances and take to her rooms.

Jonah went back inside, set the card on the counter beside the register, and looked at his shop.

Yarrow's Books had been dying for years, but it died beautifully, the way old men do—with dignity and clutter and a certain stubborn grace. Forty feet deep and twenty wide, shelves rising to the pressed-tin ceiling, the aisles narrow enough that two browsers had to negotiate passage like ships in a canal. The front half was trade paperbacks and dog-eared mysteries, the midlist of another era, priced to move and mostly not moving. The back half—the last twelve feet behind the bead curtain Dana Okafor used to joke was the only thing in town older than the cemetery—was the rare-book room: climate-controlled by a window unit that wheezed like a consumptive, locked with a key Jonah wore on a chain under his shirt, and containing the only inventory he'd ever truly cared about. First editions. Signed association copies. A run of nineteenth-century county atlases with hand-colored maps. The small, patient wealth of a lifetime spent trading up one book at a time.

And on the shelf behind the register, spine out where any customer could reach it, the first edition of *Felix and the Cipher*—the annotated one, the one with E.V.'s marginalia climbing the margins like ivy. Jonah had photographed every page weeks ago, all two hundred and sixteen of them, high-resolution images stored on his laptop, a flash drive in his coat pocket, and a cloud account he'd set up with his nephew's help and never quite trusted. But the book itself—the physical object, the thing that had started everything—lived here, because some part of him still believed that evidence belonged in daylight, among ordinary books, where secrets went to become history instead of weapons.

He looked at the card again. Then he called Dana Okafor.

"It's a threat," she said, when he'd read it to her twice. "It's also not actionable. No explicit threat, no signature, delivered anonymously. I can log it. That's about it." A pause. He could hear her office around her—the radio, someone laughing too loudly, the institutional noise of a police department trying very hard to sound normal while its chief waited out a suspension she was technically still serving. "Jonah. How did Marcus find out about your mother?"

"Same way everyone in this town finds out everything. Someone talked."

"Cordelia wouldn't."

"No," Jonah agreed. "But houses like Wrenfield have ears. Staff. Gardeners. The woman who comes to do the silver." He rubbed his eyes. "Dana, I need you to hear me. If something happens to the shop—"

"Nothing is going to happen to the shop."

"Promise me you'll treat it as a crime scene. Not an accident. Not faulty wiring. A crime scene."

The pause this time was longer. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into the register he remembered from stakeouts, from the bad nights, from the case that had ended his career. "You've got the photographs?"

"Every page. Three copies."

"Then whatever happens to the paper, you haven't lost anything that matters." She said it gently, and he understood she thought she was comforting him. "Keep your phone charged. Keep the outside light on. And Jonah—for God's sake, don't be a hero. You're sixty-eight years old."

"I'm aware," he said. "I feel it every morning."

He hung up and spent the afternoon doing the thing he'd been avoiding for a month: preparing for loss. He moved the most valuable volumes from the rare-book room into the fireproof document safe he'd bought secondhand from a defunct law office—too small for more than a dozen books, but enough for the irreplaceable ones. He backed up his laptop again, then put the flash drive in his truck's glove box, which was stupid, and then took it out and put it in his coat pocket, which was marginally less stupid. He wrote down the insurance policy number and taped it inside the register drawer. He ate a sandwich standing up at the counter, looking at his shop the way a man looks at a room he knows he's leaving.

At six he flipped the sign to CLOSED, checked the back door lock twice, and stood a moment in the middle of the floor, listening to the building breathe. Old shops creak and settle as the temperature drops; Yarrow's Books had a whole vocabulary of complaint, groans from the joists, the tick of the tin ceiling cooling. He knew them all. He'd know, he told himself, if someone were in here. He'd know.

He drove home to the apartment above the hardware store, ate soup, and did not sleep.

* * *

The call came at 2:14 in the morning.

Not from Dana. From Earl Brandt, who ran the laundromat two doors down and lived above it, and whose voice on the phone was stripped down to its simplest components: "Jonah. Fire. Your shop. It's—it's bad, Jonah."

Jonah was dressed before the sentence ended. He drove the nine blocks with his headlights off for the first stretch, which was insane, which was the kind of thing a younger man would have done out of urgency and an older man out of pure animal refusal to wait, and when he turned onto Marsh Street he saw the light before he saw the fire.

It was coming through the front windows—not leaping, not roaring yet, but glowing, a deep orange respiration behind the glass, the display window he'd dressed every season for eleven years now filled with moving shadow. Smoke poured along the eaves in a flat black sheet. Two figures were already there—Earl with a garden hose doing nothing, and Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery in a bathrobe, both of them shouting at him as he got out of the truck, and the heat reached him across the street like a hand laid flat against his chest.

"Jonah, don't—" Earl grabbed his arm. "Fire department's coming, they're coming, you can't go in there—"

"The back room," Jonah said. He heard his own voice and didn't recognize it. It was calm. It was terribly calm. "Earl, let go of my arm."

"You'll die, man, you will die—"

The front door was hot. He knew it would be hot and it was anyway, the brass handle searing his palm even through the moment of contact, and he went around the side instead, down the alley between his building and the dry cleaner's, where the night air was still clean and cold and smelled of garbage and rain. The back door opened onto the stockroom, and the stockroom, when he stepped inside, was full of smoke at head height, a gray lake of it, and beneath the lake the world was lit wrong—a flickering amber glow pulsing from the direction of the front of the store, from somewhere near the biography section, and the sound. He hadn't understood fire had a sound until that moment. Not crackle. A kind of breathing. A vast, satisfied inhalation, drawing the air out of the room toward itself.

He dropped low, below the smoke, and crawled.

The aisle between the biographies and the poetry was an oven. Heat came off the shelves on both sides in waves he could feel through his jacket, and when he glanced left he saw why: someone had done this properly. Not a match tossed carelessly. Accelerant, poured along the base of the shelving in a deliberate line, burning with a steady orange hunger, eating upward through the spines. He watched a biography of Eleanor Roosevelt curl and blacken and come apart, and felt something in his chest tear loose—not grief exactly, or not only grief, but the specific pain of watching a body of work die. Those books had survived their authors. Some had survived their authors' grandchildren. Now they were becoming heat and light for a rich man's convenience.

*The bead curtain.* He crawled past it, into the back twelve feet, and pulled the door shut behind him.

The rare-book room was cooler. The little window unit had died years ago in everything but name, but the interior wall and the closed door bought it time, and in the red emergency-light glow leaking under the doorframe Jonah moved fast, on his knees, pulling books from the shelves by the armload. The safe held twelve. He filled it in ninety seconds—Vane's *Felix and the Cipher* went in first, then the signed Cather, the Melville letters, the atlas with the hand-tinted map of the reservoir valley, the things that could not be re-bought at any price because they weren't commodities, they were witnesses. He spun the dial. His hands were shaking so badly it took three tries.

Smoke was under the door now, threading along the floor like something alive and curious.

That was when he saw the cipher book's empty space on the shelf behind the register—he'd carried it into the safe, thank God, thank God—and beside the shelf, on the floor, something that stopped him colder than the fire.

A book of matches. The long kitchen kind, white cardboard, lying where it had been dropped just inside the front door. And next to it, placed deliberately, face-up, weighted with a stone from the shop's own windowsill garden: a photograph.

He picked it up with a sleeve over his hand. It was a print, glossy, recent—a picture of Jonah himself, taken from across the street, standing in front of Wrenfield House. Standing on Cordelia Vane's porch. Dated, in ballpoint on the back, with the date of his visit.

*A message,* he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter. *They want me to know this isn't random. They want me to know it's arithmetic.*

The smoke alarm finally woke, shrieking, absurdly late, and the sound broke whatever trance had held him kneeling there. Because the truth—the truth he would spend the rest of his life turning over—was that he had maybe thirty seconds left and he spent them badly. He looked at the poetry section. At the fiction walls. At the children's corner with its small chairs and the hand-lettered sign READING IS A DOOR. Everything was already lost; he knew that the way you know a person is dead before the doctor says it. And still his hands closed on the nearest stack—six or seven paperbacks, nothing, worthless—and he was three steps toward the back door when the window above the biography section burst inward with a soft glassy exhalation and the fire stood up.

It stood up the whole height of the room, a column of orange and black, and the heat hit him like a physical blow, and Jonah Yarrow, sixty-eight years old, ran.

* * *

He made it four steps into the alley before his legs quit arguing and simply obeyed, and then he was out, in the cold, on the wet asphalt, coughing so hard he folded at the waist. Behind him the back windows of his shop began to glow. Somewhere a siren found its voice, still distant. Earl was shouting his name. Mrs. Okonkwo had her hands on his face, turning it, checking him, saying something about his eyebrows, about his hands, and he realized his palms were burned where he'd gripped the safe door and the flash drive case, and that he was clutching seven worthless paperbacks against his chest like a rescued child.

"They're gone," he said. His voice came out as gravel. "All of it. It's all—"

He stopped. Because it wasn't. Twelve books in a safe. Two hundred and sixteen photographs in three places. A ledger recovered from a reservoir. Originals in Walter Finch's keeping—or wherever Finch had gone. Minutes, letters, hymn numbers, a coroner's page with a ghost signature underneath.

Marcus Vane had sent men to burn down a building. He had not touched—could not touch—the actual record. You cannot burn a photograph of a page. You cannot burn a memory of a hymn number. The old man in the cottage by the river had built his life on paper and shadows, and paper, Jonah thought, watching his life's work breathe its last through the breaking windows, paper survives whoever thinks they've destroyed it. That was the whole secret of archives. That was the whole secret, maybe, of everything.

Dana Okafor arrived without a uniform, in her personal car, and pushed through the gathering crowd with her badge out and her face like a closed fist. She found him sitting on the dry cleaner's loading dock, an oxygen mask someone had given him hanging loose around his neck, watching the fire department drown the skeleton of his shop.

"Talk to me," she said.

"He left a photograph." He handed it to her, sleeve-wrapped. "On the floor. Inside. They wanted me to find it—they put it under a rock from my own windowsill. It's me at Cordelia's house, dated." He watched her look at it, watched her jaw tighten. "This wasn't vandalism, Dana. This was a receipt. This was Marcus telling me the meter's running."

"Arson with a message." She said it quietly, for him alone. The state fire marshal's people would arrive within hours; the local department would investigate; the investigation would stall; everyone in Ashford knew the choreography by heart. "You okay? Actually okay?"

Jonah considered the question honestly, which was more than he usually managed. His hands hurt. His lungs felt scoured. Eleven years of inventory was dying behind yellow tape, and with it the careful invisibility he'd built his retirement out of—because everyone on this street, everyone in this crowd, everyone by tomorrow morning in the diner and the church basement and the council chamber would know that Jonah Yarrow's shop hadn't burned by accident, and a man whose shop burns for reasons is a man the whole town looks at differently. He was visible now. Utterly, permanently visible. Marcus Vane had seen to that.

"No," he said. "But I'm clear. For the first time since the coffin came up out of the ground, I'm completely clear." He looked at her. "He thinks he took my evidence tonight. He burned a building full of paper because he doesn't understand that the paper was never the point. Rosalind Marsh understood it. She hid her master copy where the whole congregation sings from it every Sunday. Edmund Vane hid his confession inside a novel. These people hide things in plain sight, Dana, and they assume everyone else is too frightened or too stupid to look."

"And you're going to look."

"I'm going to look." He stood, swaying slightly, and steadied himself against the dock's edge. Across the street, the roof of Yarrow's Books sagged, sighed, and let go in a gout of sparks that rose straight up into the dark like something released. Jonah watched them climb. "Get me everything on the two men Marcus uses for dirty work. And Dana—first light tomorrow, I want to be at the Methodist church. There's a chamber inside that organ console where the hymn-number mechanism lives. Marsh dated her last letter the week she drowned. She told us where everything is. We just never finished reading her."

Okafor put the photograph in an evidence bag, looked once more at the burning shell of the shop, and said, "They picked the wrong bookseller."

"They picked the wrong retired detective," Jonah said. "The bookseller was just the cover."

Above the ruins, the sparks kept rising, and he stood in the cold and counted them like votes.
