## 4. Ada

Mrs. Pell announced her with the particular ceremony of a woman who has decided that if a disaster must enter the house, it should at least enter through the correct door.

"Miss Corliss will see you now," she said, and then, in a lower register, as though passing along intelligence rather than hospitality: "She's in the morning room. She's always in the morning room before noon. After noon she's in the garden, and you do *not* follow her into the garden."

"Noted," said Bram.

"And Miss Corliss doesn't care for—"

"Pell." The voice came from within, low and unhurried, the way water is unhurried right up until it takes the ground out from under you. "You're frightening the poor woman."

"I'm *preparing* her," said Mrs. Pell, with dignity, and withdrew down the corridor like a tide going out.

Bram stepped through the doorway and understood, all at once, why the housekeeper had been so anxious about the introduction.

The morning room was small and very bright — the one room in Marlowe House that seemed to have been exempted from the general embargo on light. Every window stood open to the sea. The dust sheets had been folded here, not merely pushed aside but folded, with corners squared, and stacked on a chair as if even they had been given permission to rest. On the mantel sat a clock, and it was running. Bram heard it tick for three full seconds before she registered how strange that was, given every other clock in the house had stopped at some private hour of its own despairing.

And in the middle of the room, in a wicker chair angled precisely toward the water, sat Ada Corliss.

Bram had prepared herself for grief. It was the reasonable expectation: a woman in her sixties, sister drowned thirty years past, household embalmed around the loss. She had prepared for the red-rimmed eyes, the careful voice, the way grieving people hold their teacups like something that might break.

What she got instead was elegance so complete it read almost as armor. Ada wore dove-grey silk and a string of pearls the color of old moonlight, and her hair was silver and immaculate, and she was — there was no other word for it — *serene*. Not calm the way a lake is calm. Calm the way a lake is calm when you know, looking at it, exactly how deep it goes.

"You'll be Miss Iversen," said Ada. "The lawyer. Sit down, please. You look as though you've been fighting the hedge on your way up from the gate, and I find it's easier to be intimidating when one isn't dripping."

"I wasn't aware I was here to intimidate anyone."

"No one ever is." Ada gestured to the tea service already laid out on the table between them — two cups, which meant Mrs. Pell had known Bram would get this far. "Milk?"

"Please."

"Sugar?"

"No, thank you."

"Mm. Felix took sugar." Ada poured with a steady hand. "I'm making conversation, Miss Iversen. You'll forgive me. I find that when a person arrives asking questions about my sister, it helps to establish first whether they can be talked to at all."

Bram accepted the cup. "And can I be talked to?"

"We'll see. You haven't flinched yet, which puts you ahead of the vicar." Ada settled back and looked at her over the rim of her own cup, and her eyes were pale grey and quite, quite steady. "Now. You've come about Lila."

It was not a question, so Bram didn't treat it as one. "I've come about a document," she said. "A will. It names a death that hasn't happened yet — scheduled, if the language is to be believed, for the anniversary of your sister's drowning. At this house."

"Yes," said Ada.

Bram waited. The clock ticked. Somewhere below, the sea did what the sea does, patiently, over and over, like a witness who never gets tired of giving the same testimony.

"That's it?" Bram said finally. "*Yes?*"

"What would you prefer? Shock? Outrage? A fainting couch is available; Pell keeps it aired." Ada set down her cup. "Miss Iversen, I have spent thirty years being asked about my sister by people who wanted me to perform grief in whatever key they found comfortable. The constable wants me stoic. The vicar wants me resigned. The occasional journalist — we had one in '89, dreadful man — wanted me mad. I have run out of performances. What remains is the truth, or as much of it as I intend to hand out today, and you may take it or leave it."

"Which is?"

"Lila drowned on the fourteenth of September, thirty years ago. The coroner called it accidental. Everyone in this town will tell you the case was closed before the flowers on her grave had wilted. And everyone in this town," Ada went on, in the same mild voice, "will say it while looking at their shoes."

"Inspector Hale told me essentially that yesterday."

"Hale." Something moved behind the serenity — not a smile, exactly. An echo of one. "Hale was a constable then, you know. Young. Eager. He carried the lantern down to the cove the night they dragged the water. Did he show you his hands?"

Bram blinked. "His hands?"

"He used to shake, afterward. He may have trained himself out of it by now. Or he may simply keep them in his pockets where the town can't see them." Ada turned her face toward the window, toward the grey line of the sea. "Anniversaries matter in this house, Miss Iversen. That is the only thing I will tell you outright today, so I suggest you write it down."

Bram did not write it down. She was too busy watching Ada's profile against the light, cataloguing the things that were wrong with the picture. The serenity was real — that was the unsettling part. It wasn't a mask over turmoil; it was more like stillness over depth, the composure of someone who made peace with something long ago and had never once checked whether the peace was legitimate.

But her thumb, resting on the arm of the wicker chair, pressed a small crescent into the rattan. Had been pressing it, by the look of the weave, for years.

"Tell me about Felix," Bram said.

There it was. Bram had been circling toward it since she sat down, and she watched the name land — watched it land *softly*, which was worse than watching it land hard. Ada did not stiffen. She did not go white as Mrs. Pell had. She closed her eyes for a moment, briefly, the way one does when a piece of music one loved comes on unexpectedly in a shop.

"Felix," she said. "Yes. I wondered how long you'd take."

"You know him."

"I knew him when we were young enough that knowing someone meant something different than it means now." Ada opened her eyes. "He was — " She stopped. Started again, choosing the words like stones for a crossing. "Felix Ashcombe was the boy who taught my sister to row. He was the boy who was supposed to be on the water with her that night, and wasn't. He is the reason the word *anniversary* has teeth in this house. And he has been dying, I understand, for some little while now, which I imagine is what has finally brought all of this to your desk after thirty years of everyone agreeing, very loudly, that there was nothing on it."

Bram kept her face professional, which took effort, because inside she was doing rapid, unflattering arithmetic. *Everyone* in this house knew Felix. The housekeeper went white at his name. The client had lied to her — or at least edited her — about his connection to Marlowe House, presenting himself as a stranger with a strange document when he was in fact a returning ghost.

"You asked whether I'd flinched yet," Bram said. "I'd like to revise my answer. My client represented himself to me as unconnected to this family. He is not. That's material misrepresentation, and it gives me grounds to return his fee and walk away."

"It gives you grounds," Ada agreed pleasantly. "And yet here you sit, drinking tea I made Pell put out before you arrived, because I knew you'd come and I knew you'd stay. Shall I tell you how I knew?"

"Please."

"Because Felix never does anything halfway, and he would never have hired a lawyer who could be walked off a case by a locked drawer or an unfriendly constable. He'd have found you specifically. He'd have paid you triple." Ada's eyes came around and fixed on Bram's, and for just a moment the serenity thinned, and underneath it Bram saw something that made her breath catch — not menace, nothing so simple. Longing. Terrible, disciplined longing, like a fire banked so thoroughly it had forgotten it was allowed to be warm. "Did he pay you triple, Miss Iversen?"

Bram said nothing.

Ada smiled — a real smile, brief and sad and unexpectedly lovely. "There's your answer. He always did believe in buying the best and then daring it to disappoint him." She rose, smoothly, and went to the window. Below, the garden fell away in terraces toward the cove, and the cove held the sea the way a cupped hand holds rain. "Do you see the boathouse?"

"The one with the green roof, half-hidden in the trees."

"Lila's boat is still in it. Her actual boat. Nobody has moved it in thirty years. Pell dusts the cover." Ada's voice stayed level, conversational, describing a garden feature. "Every year on the fourteenth of September, this household observes certain — customs. Candles. A minute of darkness at midnight. You'll think it morbid. The vicar thinks it morbid. But grief is a tenant, Miss Iversen, not a guest, and it pays its rent in ritual. We pay it. It stays quiet. Most years."

"*Most* years?"

"This year Felix has written to say he's coming." Ada said it the way one reports weather. "Dying men are permitted their pilgrimages. I have not yet decided whether to receive him."

"May I ask what you'll decide?"

"You may ask." She turned back from the window, and the serenity was fully restored, seamless as the pearls at her throat. "Whether I answer is another matter entirely. Now — I imagine you have a list of questions as long as your arm, and I am going to disappoint you with nearly all of them, so let us be efficient. Ask me the three that matter most. Choose carefully. I answer honestly, but only exactly."

Bram considered. This was, she reflected, the most dangerous kind of witness: one who told the truth, because the truth could be shaped. Lies could be caught. Precision had to be *outmaneuvered*.

She chose.

"One: did Lila Corliss drown accidentally?"

"Accidentally," said Ada, "is a coroner's word. It describes a mechanism, not a story. Lila went into the water at night, alone, in a boat that was later found adrift and empty. Whether that constitutes an accident depends entirely on what you believe she was doing on that water, and who, if anyone, believed it too. So: no answer, but I've told you where the answer lives."

"Two: do you believe Felix intends harm — to you, or anyone — when he comes here?"

Ada laughed. It startled them both, apparently; she pressed her fingertips to her lips for a moment. "Forgive me. Harm. Felix couldn't harm a fly; he apologizes to furniture he bumps into. No, Miss Iversen. Whatever Felix is coming here to do, it is the opposite of harm, and that is precisely what frightens me about it."

"Three." Bram set down her cup. "Who razored the pages out of your sister's diary?"

The room went very quiet. Even the clock seemed to consider its position.

Ada looked at her for a long moment, and Bram watched the serenity do its work — watched it absorb the blow, distribute the weight, hold. But her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the pearls at her throat, and her fingers had closed around them the way a drowning woman closes around a rope.

"Now that," said Ada softly, "is a very good question. Which tells me you've already been to the boathouse, which tells me Pell gave you the key, which tells me Pell has finally begun to crack after thirty years, which tells me—" She stopped. Drew a breath. Let it out. "Which tells me everything is moving, and I am no longer steering."

"Then answer it."

"No," said Ada.

Just that. No elaboration, no deflection, no performance of regret. *No*, delivered with the same courtesy she'd used to offer milk.

"Was it you?" Bram pressed.

"You've had your three answers, Miss Iversen. Well — two answers and a map." Ada released the pearls, smoothed her skirt, and became once more the hostess, the serene woman in the bright room, and Bram understood suddenly that the brightness itself was curated, that this room was the one place in Marlowe House where Ada permitted herself to be seen, and that everything outside these windows — the dust sheets, the stopped clocks, the portrait facing the wall — was Ada's grief arranged into architecture. "Pell will show you out. Do give my regards to Inspector Hale. And Miss Iversen—"

Bram paused at the door.

"—when Felix arrives, and he will arrive, because he has never once in his life done the sensible thing — watch his hands, not his face. Faces lie beautifully at our age. Hands only ever tell the truth they've been holding."

* * *

Mrs. Pell walked her down the front steps in silence, which lasted until the gravel, at which point the housekeeper evidently judged them beyond the reach of the windows.

"Well?" she demanded.

"She answered two of my questions and drew me a map with the third."

"That's her all over." Mrs. Pell glanced back at the bright facade of the morning room, and her expression was complicated — loyalty and fear and something older than either, braided together. "Thirty years I've served that woman. Thirty years, and I've watched her keep this house like a widow keeps a grave, neat and shut and *quiet*. And I'll tell you something for free, since you're the first person in three decades to ask a question in this house that anybody wanted answered." She lowered her voice further, though there was no one for half a mile in any direction but gulls. "That serenity of hers? It's not peace. Peace forgives. What she's got is a held breath. Thirty years of held breath, Miss Iversen, and I don't mind saying it to you straight: I don't know what happens to a house when a woman like that finally lets it out. I don't think *she* knows."

"Any advice?"

"Aye." Mrs. Pell squinted out toward the cove, where the boathouse crouched under its green roof among the trees, patient as a secret. "Whatever Felix Ashcombe is bringing to this house on the fourteenth — and I've seen the letter, so don't pretend otherwise — be standing next to her when it opens. Not across from her. *Next* to her." She turned back, and her eyes were wet and furious and resolute all at once. "Because I've a feeling that when this family finally says what it's been swallowing since before you were practicing law, miss, there won't be a soul in Marlowe House steady enough to catch her. Except maybe you. And maybe not even you."

She pressed something into Bram's hand — quick, furtive, done before Bram could refuse it.

A second key. Older than the first, its brass worn smooth, tied with a length of faded ribbon.

"That's not the boathouse," Mrs. Pell said. "That's the writing desk in Lila's old room. The one nobody's opened since." She was already retreating up the steps, wiping her hands on her apron as though the key had left a residue. "I never gave you anything. I'm going to go polish something until I feel better about myself."

Bram stood alone on the gravel drive, the sea wind pulling at her coat, and turned the key over in her palm.

Behind her, in the bright morning room, the clock went on ticking — the only honest timekeeper in a house full of stopped ones — and out past the terraces, down where the water met the stones, the cove waited with the particular patience of a place that had been keeping a secret for thirty years and saw no reason whatsoever to hurry.
