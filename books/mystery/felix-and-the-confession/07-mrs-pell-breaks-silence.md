## 7. Mrs. Pell Breaks Silence

Sleep had abandoned Mrs. Pell sometime around the war — the second one — and never fully moved back in. For thirty years she had made an arrangement with her insomnia: she would not fight it, and it would confine itself to the small hours between two and four, when even the sea seemed to hold its breath. In exchange, she permitted herself tea at three in the morning and the last of the shortbread, and considered the bargain fair.

But tonight the insomnia broke its terms. It was barely eleven when Mrs. Pell found herself sitting upright in her narrow bed above the kitchen, listening to Marlowe House settle around her like an old dog turning in its sleep. The rain had stopped. The clocks were all still wrong — they had been wrong since 1961, stopped at twenty minutes past midnight, and no one wound them, because winding them would mean deciding what time it was again, and this house had decided long ago that it preferred not to know.

She thought about the lawyer. Miss Iversen. The one with the sharp grey eyes who asked questions the way other people laid cards down — flat, deliberate, face up.

*Did you work here when Lila Corliss drowned?*

Mrs. Pell had answered, *Everyone here worked here then,* which was true, and *It was a long time ago,* which was also true, and neither of which was an answer, and Miss Iversen had known it. She'd written something in her notebook anyway. That was the thing about lawyers. They wrote down your non-answers and filed them where non-answers went to ripen.

Mrs. Pell got up. There was no point lying in bed pretending. She put on her dressing gown — wool, dove-grey, mended at both elbows because a housekeeper of her standards did not replace a garment for so trivial a thing as wear — and went downstairs by memory, past the dust-sheeted furniture that rose around her like patient ghosts.

The kitchen was the only room in Marlowe House that lived. It was warm, it was honest, it smelled of bread and brass polish, and it contained nothing that had been covered in a sheet since the year of the drowning. Mrs. Pell lit the burner under the kettle and stood with her hands folded while the flame ticked.

That was when she heard footsteps on the back stairs.

She did not startle. Thirty years in this house had taught her the difference between the sounds that belonged to it and the sounds that did not, and these footsteps — hesitant, bare-footed, pausing twice — belonged to their guest. Miss Iversen, unable to sleep too. Well. Misery loved company, and Mrs. Pell had always kept a well-stocked larder of it.

"Kettle's on," she said, before the lawyer reached the bottom step. "There's no use creeping about. I hear everything in this house. It's the floors. They gossip."

Bram Iversen came into the kitchen looking, Mrs. Pell noted with some satisfaction, considerably less like a card-sharp than she had at breakfast. Her hair was down. She was wearing a man's jumper far too big for her, which suggested either sentimentality or poor packing, and Mrs. Pell filed both possibilities away.

"I heard you moving," Bram said. "I thought I'd see if you were awake."

"You heard me moving because you were listening for me. Which is a different thing." Mrs. Pell took down two cups. "Sit. The chair by the stove. The other one wobbles, and I can't abide watching people negotiate with furniture."

Bram sat. The kettle began to murmur. For a moment the two women regarded each other across the warm little room, each understanding perfectly well why the other was there, and neither willing to say it first.

It was Bram who broke, though not entirely. "You went white," she said. "When I mentioned Felix's name. At the door, the first morning. You looked like someone had read your post aloud in church."

Mrs. Pell poured the tea. This gave her hands something lawful to do. "Felix Ashcombe," she said, as if testing whether the name still bore weight. "He used to come round the back way, summers. Lila would leave the scullery door unlatched for him." She set the cup down in front of Bram. "Ada never knew he used the back door. Ada believed he came to the front like a gentleman, and old Mr. Corliss believed Felix Ashcombe was beneath his daughters' notice altogether. Everyone in this house believed something convenient. That's rather the house specialty."

"And you?"

Mrs. Pell sat down opposite her. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs, a clock that hadn't run in decades nevertheless seemed to wait.

"I believed what I saw," she said. "Which is a dangerous habit in this house, and one I gave up for Lent in 1962 and never picked back up."

"What did you see?"

And there it was — the question, laid flat, face up, just like her others. Mrs. Pell wrapped her hands around her own cup and felt the heat go into her palms and thought: *thirty years.* Thirty years of carrying a thing the size of a boat and the weight of a girl, tucked behind the ribs where nobody checks. Thirty years of polishing silver for a family that paid her well and trusted her completely and deserved, in every particular except one, exactly that trust.

"You'll think me a foolish woman," she said.

"I think most people are braver than they get credit for," said Bram. "I'm waiting for the evidence."

Despite herself, despite everything, Mrs. Pell laughed — a short, surprised sound, like a cork coming out. "Oh, you're a piece of work, Miss Iversen. Felix said you would be."

"Felix talked to you? Recently?"

"He wrote. Once a year, every year, on the same date. A letter I never answered." Mrs. Pell stared into her tea. "Fifty-one letters. I've kept them all, which will tell you something, though I couldn't tell you what. A woman doesn't keep fifty-one letters from a man she's determined to forget. She keeps them the way you keep a splinter. Not because you want it. Because removing it is worse."

"The same date," Bram said softly. "The anniversary."

"There's only the one date that matters in this house." Mrs. Pell heard her own voice go strange and quiet. "Anniversaries matter here. Ada says so."

"I know. She told me."

"Of course she did." Something in Mrs. Pell's chest shifted — a stone rolling downhill after thirty years of standing still. "Miss Iversen. I am going to tell you a thing now, and once I've told it, we'll both wish I hadn't, and there's no taking it back, so let us be clear-eyed about that."

Bram set down her cup. She didn't lean forward — Mrs. Pell noticed that; lawyers leaned forward on the television, but the good ones knew leaning forward frightened people off — she simply waited, the way one waits for weather.

"That night," Mrs. Pell said, "I couldn't sleep either. Same as tonight. Same as every night since, if you must know, though I've gotten very good at not knowing it. I went down to lock up, and I saw lights at the boathouse, and I went out to have a look. It was coming on to rain. I remember thinking Lila would be out in it — she swam at night that summer, wild thing, like an otter, like something the sea had lent us and meant to take back—"

Her voice caught. She pressed on.

"I got as far as the boathouse window. There's a window on the landward side, low, at knee height. You could see the whole dock through it if you crouched. So I crouched. And I saw—" 

*Tell her,* said fifty-one unopened letters' worth of conscience. *Tell her and be done. Let it be someone else's to carry. You're seventy-three years old and your knees are shot and you cannot carry it into whatever comes next.*

"I saw the boat," Mrs. Pell said. "Out on the water. And I saw a lantern on the dock. And I saw—" 

Ada's name rose in her throat.

It rose the way it always rose, complete and terrible, three syllables wearing a white summer dress, and Mrs. Pell shut her mouth on it the way she had shut her mouth on it for thirty years, because — because. Because Ada had been nineteen. Because Ada had been grieving before anyone else had anything to grieve over. Because a housekeeper who tells what she sees through windows destroys houses, and Mrs. Pell had spent her whole life keeping houses standing, and a body does not unlearn its trade.

Because, if she was honest — and sleeplessness makes a woman honest whether she likes it or not — because telling would not bring Lila back, and might break the one Corliss left alive, and Mrs. Pell loved Ada Corliss with the fierce, unsentimental love of a woman who had bathed her scrapes and bandaged her heart and watched her grow from a screaming infant into a serene, unreachable lady made mostly of grief and good posture.

"I saw enough," Mrs. Pell finished. "And I went back inside, and by morning Lila was dead, and the constable came, and it was an accident, everyone agreed, and I agreed too. Loudest of anyone." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as wire. "I'm very good at agreeing. It's how houses stay quiet."

Bram was watching her with those grey eyes. "Who was on the dock, Mrs. Pell?"

"You know, I believe I'll have that shortbread now," said Mrs. Pell, rising, "if you'll excuse me."

"Mrs. Pell."

"Was there anything else you wanted? There's a spare blanket in the linen press, second floor, if your room runs cold. It does. They all do. This house holds its heat the way it holds everything else."

"Who was on the dock?"

Mrs. Pell stood at the counter with her back turned, cutting shortbread with the good knife, and the knife trembled very slightly against the tin, and she hated it, and she steadied it.

"If I tell you," she said, "you will write it in your little notebook, and it will ripen, and one day it will come up out of the ground like something planted, and it will not be my hand that planted it but it will be my mouth that fed it, and Ada—" 

She stopped. Of course she stopped. She stopped at Ada's name the way water stops at a sill.

"Ada what?" Bram asked gently.

"Nothing." Mrs. Pell cut the shortbread. Cut it neatly, evenly, into pieces fit for a funeral. "Ada is my responsibility. She has been since the day she was born, and more especially since the day her sister wasn't. Whatever happened at that window, whatever I saw or half-saw or have spent thirty years persuading myself I didn't see — it ends with me. I am the wall of this house, Miss Iversen. Walls don't testify."

"They do," Bram said. "Eventually. When the pressure's right."

"Well." Mrs. Pell turned around and set the plate between them with a decisive click. "Then let us hope the pressure stays civil."

They ate shortbread in silence for a while. The stove ticked. Outside, the tide was turning, and the sound of it came up the cliff path like slow breathing.

It was Mrs. Pell who ended the silence, because it occurred to her — standing there with crumbs on her dressing gown and thirty years of silence going soft in her chest like old fruit — that there was a difference between speaking and handing something over. She could not say the name. But perhaps she did not have to. Perhaps there were other ways a wall could open.

"Wait here," she said.

She went to the dresser, the big oak one against the north wall, and knelt before the lowest drawer — the one she had never once opened in front of anyone in thirty years, the one containing, officially, table linens gone grey with disuse. She moved the linens aside. Beneath them, wrapped in a tea towel gone soft as chamois, was a key.

Brass. Old. Cold as a held breath.

She had taken it the week after the drowning, when the household went mad with grief and the locks were changed and old Mr. Corliss ordered the boathouse sealed — *sealed, Pell, and I'll know if anyone goes in* — and she, folding away Lila's things for a family that could not bear to look at them, had simply slipped it into her apron pocket the way one pockets a button that has come off a beloved coat. Meaning to return it. Never returning it. Carrying it through three decades of days until it wore smooth as a pebble in her fist on bad nights, of which there had been many.

She came back to the table and set it down in front of Bram without ceremony.

"What is it?" Bram asked.

"A key."

"I can see that."

"It's to the boathouse. The landward door, the little one, under the ivy. The main doors were chained after — after. But the little door, Mr. Corliss forgot it existed, being as he'd forgotten most things by then that didn't concern his own grief." Mrs. Pell sat down heavily. "There are things in that boathouse that were never given back. Things of Lila's. The police took them, and then the police gave them to Mr. Corliss, and Mr. Corliss put them where he put everything he couldn't look at, and then he died, and then the sealing, and then the years." She folded her hands. "Whatever you're looking for, Miss Iversen — whatever Felix sent you to find — it isn't in the ledgers or the parish records or Inspector Hale's tidy little file. It's behind that door. It has been behind that door for thirty years."

Bram picked up the key. Turned it over. "Why give me this if you won't tell me what you saw?"

"Because I'm a coward," said Mrs. Pell comfortably, "but I'm not a fool. Those aren't the same thing, whatever the church says. A coward tells the truth by proxy." She met the lawyer's eyes at last, straight on, and found — to her surprise — that it didn't burn. "You'll go out there tomorrow. You'll find whatever's waiting. And when you come back — and you will come back, because you strike me as a woman who finishes things, God help you — you'll sit in this kitchen and you'll tell me what you found, and I'll decide then whether I can afford to have seen it."

"And if what I find needs saying? Out loud? By you?"

Mrs. Pell looked at the stove, at the kettle beginning its low song, at the doorway beyond which Marlowe House lay sheeted and silent, its clocks stopped, its portrait turned to the wall, its lady sleeping badly upstairs behind a door Mrs. Pell had closed every night for thirty years.

"Then you'd best hope," she said quietly, "that what's behind that door is gentler than what's behind mine."

Bram rose, key in hand. At the doorway she paused. "Thank you, Mrs. Pell. For the tea. And the shortbread."

"For the key," said Mrs. Pell. "Don't thank me for the key. Thanking a woman for a key is thanking her for the lock it opens, and neither of us knows yet what's locked in there."

She listened to the lawyer climb the back stairs — slower now, thoughtful, the key presumably in her pocket, already ripening — and then Mrs. Pell stood alone in her warm kitchen with the plate of funeral shortbread and the truth she had almost spoken sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

From the drawer of the dresser, she took the fifty-first letter, the one that had arrived that spring, the one whose final line she knew by heart though she had read it only once:

*Tell her, or I will.*

Mrs. Pell put the letter back. She washed the teacups. She banked the stove.

And upstairs in the linen press, behind the spare blankets, she checked — as she did every night, and would check again tomorrow, and the day after — that the second key, the one to Ada's writing desk, was still exactly where she had hidden it.

Just in case. Just in case the day ever came when a wall was asked to open from the inside.
