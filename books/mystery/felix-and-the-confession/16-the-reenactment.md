## 16. The Reenactment

I have attended wills executed, deeds signed, and one memorable divorce finalized in the parking lot of a Denny's, but I had never attended a ritual before. I didn't know what to wear to one. In the end I chose the same gray suit I wear to probate hearings, on the theory that if the evening went badly, at least I'd be dressed for the paperwork.

Mrs. Pell had laid out instructions that afternoon, delivered in the flat voice of a woman reciting lines she'd been made to memorize so long ago the memorization had worn grooves in her.

"Candles at the dock at eleven forty-five. Nobody speaks after the boat goes out. One minute of darkness. Then we come back up to the house and have tea, and it's over for another year." She paused, wringing the dish towel she was holding as though it owed her money. "You don't have to come, miss. Guests never come. But you're not a guest, are you."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. She set a candle in my hands — a plain white pillar, the wax gone soft and yellowed with age, as if it had been waiting thirty years for someone to finally light it.

"Ada likes everyone to carry one down," Mrs. Pell said. "It's the walking down that matters, she says. Lila walked down to the dock in the dark, so we walk down in the dark too, but with candles, so we can see. So we don't lose anybody else."

*So we don't lose anybody else.* I turned the phrase over all through the long grey afternoon while the rain came off the sea in slow, deliberate curtains, and by the time the light failed I had stopped pretending to myself that I was here as an observer.

I was here as a witness. It was, after all, what I'd been paid for.

* * *

At eleven forty, Ada Corliss appeared at the top of the stairs dressed in white — not a gown, nothing theatrical, just a plain white dress of a cut thirty years out of fashion, and her silver hair pinned up the way a girl of nineteen might have worn it. She looked, I thought, less like a woman playing a part than like a woman who had finally, after decades of understudying, been given the role she was born for.

She handed out candles with the serenity of a woman distributing hymnals. When she reached me, she held my eye a moment longer than the others.

"You'll stand at the end of the dock," she said. "Where the light won't reach you. Witnesses should be able to see without being seen. That's what Felix always said, when we were young. He wanted to be a writer, you know. He said every story needs someone standing just outside the light."

"Did he send you?" I asked — the question she had asked me on my first day, returned like a letter that had taken a month to arrive.

Ada smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever been on the receiving end of, and I once sat across from a widow while she read her husband's second, secret will aloud.

"He didn't have to send me," she said. "I've been waiting for him for fifty years. One doesn't send for a thing like that. One simply hopes the post is not lost." She touched the flame of her candle to mine, and my wick caught, and she moved on down the line of servants without another word.

We walked down to the water in a slow procession — Mrs. Pell, two aging gardeners whose names I never learned, the young cook who had been born long after the drowning and performed her part in the ceremony with the solemn confusion of a child in a nativity play. The rain had stopped. The clouds had torn open just enough to let down a thin, indifferent moon, and the cove lay black and glassy beneath it, holding the sky the way a held breath holds a scream.

The dock was old cedar, silvered and soft-footed, and at its end sat the rowboat — the same boat, Mrs. Pell had once told me, or a boat kept in the same condition, oars shipped, a white dress folded on the stern seat. Ada's own hands had folded it, I had no doubt. She did everything herself. That was the terrible economy of the ritual: one woman, keeping one night alive, alone, for thirty years.

The candles were set in a row along the dock's rail. Eleven of them. I counted. I count everything; it's a lawyer's tic, like straightening pens.

Ada stood at the water's edge and spoke the only words the ritual permitted before the darkness — I'd gotten them out of Mrs. Pell that afternoon, and hearing them now, in Ada's voice, out loud, in the open air, was a different thing entirely from hearing them in a kitchen over cold tea.

"Lila," she said, to the water, the way you'd speak to someone standing just past the far shore. "It's your sister. We remember. We're sorry. Come home when you're ready."

Then she pinched out the candles, one by one, beginning at the seaward end, and the darkness came up the dock toward us like a tide.

* * *

I want to be honest about what that minute of darkness was like, because everything that came after depends on it.

It was not peaceful. It was not reverent. It was the longest sixty seconds of my professional life, and I have sat through depositions. The sea made small sounds against the pilings — patient, conversational sounds, as though the water were discussing us among itself. Somewhere behind me on the shingle, a stone shifted under a foot that was not part of the household. I knew the household's footsteps by now; I'd had three weeks to learn them. Mrs. Pell breathed the way she did everything, dutifully and slightly too fast. The gardeners were perfectly still, the way men are still when they have done a thing many times and wish never to have to do it again.

And someone else was there. Someone who had arrived after the candles went out, or during, moving on the shingle with the careful, dragging gait of a man whose body had become a burden he was tired of carrying.

The minute ended. Ada struck a match and relit the seaward candle first, as she always did — I knew that too — and the small flame came up and showed us the dock, and the boat, and the white dress, and the water, all exactly as they had been.

Except that standing at the landward end of the dock, holding a candle of his own — an unlit one, because of course he'd arrived after the lighting — was Felix.

* * *

He was thinner than he had been in my office, if such a thing were possible, and he was wearing a good coat over a body that had stopped caring about good coats. He had walked, I would learn later, nearly a mile down from the road, because the taxi driver had refused the last stretch — everyone in that county refused the last stretch, I was beginning to understand; the whole peninsula had an arrangement with the dark. He stood leaning on a cane I had never seen before, and he looked, in the candlelight, like a man who had spent fifty years rehearsing a speech and had finally decided there would be no more rehearsals.

"Felix," Ada said. Just the name. Fifty years in two syllables.

"Hello, Ada." His voice was rough, but steady. Steadier than mine would have been. "You look well. The dress suits you. It always suited you better than it suited her, if you want to know — she hated white, she only wore it that night because your father told her to."

Mrs. Pell made a small sound, like a stitch tearing.

"Don't," Ada said. Not loudly. "Felix. Whatever you've come to do, don't. We do it this way. We do it *quietly*. That's the whole of it. That's how she's kept — quietly. You don't get to come here after thirty years and—"

"After thirty years?" Felix laughed, and it turned into coughing, and he rode the cough out with his eyes closed, and when he opened them they were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "Ada. Ada, my darling, my darling girl. She has been kept in the dark for thirty years. That's not keeping someone. That's *burying* them and calling it keeping."

"Mr. Iversen." Ada's voice came to me across the dock, level and cold, the voice she'd use on a tradesman. "You are a witness to nothing that happens here. I'd remind you of your professional obligations."

And there it was — the trap I had been walking toward for three weeks, laid out so neatly I almost admired it. Because she was right. I was retained counsel. Everything I witnessed tonight was arguably privileged, sealed, mine to carry and no one else's. She wasn't threatening me. She was reminding me, gently, of the walls of the building I stood in.

I have thought about that moment a great deal since. About how she said it. Not *leave*, not *stop him*. Just: you are a witness to nothing. As if what she wanted, more than anything, was for it to be true — for the night to fold itself back up like it did every year, for the candles to burn down, for the tea to be poured, for the whole machinery of silence to turn over one more revolution and carry them all safely into morning.

"Felix," she said again, softer now, and here was the thing I was not supposed to see, the thing the candlelight showed me anyway: her hands were shaking. "Please. Go home. Go back to your hospice. Let me have my sister the way I've had her. You can't give her back to me, so don't take away the only way I have of—"

"She's not yours, Ada." He said it gently. That was the worst of it — how gently he said it. "She was never yours to keep. She was hers. And she had things to say, at the end, and they were razored out of her diary by a girl who loved her and was terrified of her, and I know it was you, and I have never once blamed you, and I am going to say them anyway, tonight, at the water, because I am dying, and I will not let the water have the last word."

The candles guttered. All of them, at once, though there was no wind — I checked, afterward, I asked Mrs. Pell, I asked the gardeners; there was no wind. The flames lay down flat and stood back up, and in that stutter of light and dark I saw the shingle behind us, and the shape of a man standing at the tree line with his hat in his hands, and I knew the set of those shoulders from a photograph on a constable's office wall.

Inspector Hale had come to watch. Or to stop it. I genuinely believe he had not yet decided which, and I have often wondered whether he ever decided at all, or whether he simply stood in the dark for forty years of his life doing both at once.

"Ada," Felix said, and set his candle down on the rail, unlit, beside the eleven lit ones. Twelve candles. I counted. I count everything. "I hired a lawyer. Did she tell you? Best in the county. Never closed a case she couldn't close. I paid her to come here and prove that a murder hadn't happened yet — that's what the will said, that's the joke of it, the will named its own victim, and everyone who read it thought they understood." He looked at me, at the end of the dock, at the edge of the light, exactly where Ada had placed me, exactly where he had always said a witness belonged. "But that's not what I paid her for. I paid her to be here tonight. Because a confession needs a witness, and I am too much of a coward to confess to a priest, and too much of a coward to confess to you, Ada — I have been a coward on this water for fifty years — but I am not so much of a coward that I'll die with it. So."

He put one hand on the gunwale of the rowboat, on the boat that had gone out with two people's weight in it thirty years ago and come back with one, and he drew a breath that seemed to come up from somewhere below the dock, below the water, below the drowned girl at the bottom of all of us.

"On the fourteenth of August, thirty years ago," Felix said, "I rowed out to the point to wait for Lila Corliss, because she had written to me that she was coming, that she had the diary and the train money and her father's rage, and that if I loved her I would be there at midnight and not a moment later—"

"*Stop.*" Ada's voice cracked across the cove like the ice on a pond, and Mrs. Pell was crying now, silently, the way she did everything, and the candles guttered again, and out on the black water something moved — a swell where no swell should be, a long slow lifting, as if the cove had turned over in its sleep. "Felix, *please* — if you say it here, it can never be unsaid, and I have built thirty years on it never being said, I have built a *house* on it—"

"I know," he said. And his hand found hers in the dark, or she found his; from where I stood I couldn't tell, and I have decided never to ask. "I know exactly what you've built. I helped you lay the first stone. That's why it has to be me."

And out of the shadows at the tree line, in a voice worn down to gravel and shame, Inspector Hale said: "Let him say it, Miss Corliss. For God's sake. I've carried my share of it for thirty years. Let the man put his down."

The candles stood up straight. The water went flat and listening.

Felix looked out past the dock, past the boat, past all of us, at the place where the black water met the blacker sky, and began again.
