## 3. A Town That Forgets on Purpose

Hollowmere has two seasons: tourist season and forgetting season. The tourists come in July for the harbor and the saltwater taffy and the quaint notion that a town with one traffic light must be honest. The rest of the year, we forget.

I walked Main Street on Tuesday morning with my collar up against a wind that smelled of low tide and old decisions, and I did what I always do when something itches at me: I catalogued. It's not a skill I chose. My memory files itself—faces, dates, the exact number of steps from the post office to the bakery (forty-one, forty-two if Mrs. Aldous is sweeping her step and you have to go around her)—and so walking down Hollowmere's main street is less like strolling through a town and more like walking through a filing cabinet that someone has been quietly rifling through for ten years.

Take the boathouse, first thing you see if you come in from the coast road. Voss property, technically, though the estate hasn't used it since—well. Since. The padlock on its doors is new-ish; I remember when it was installed, eight years ago, a brass number three lock bought from Fenwick's Hardware on a Thursday afternoon by a man whose name I've never learned but whose face I could pick out of a police lineup in four countries. Before the padlock there was a chain. Before the chain there was nothing, because nobody needed to keep anyone out of a boathouse in a town where everyone knew everyone's business.

Now there's a padlock, and a sign that says *NO TRESPASSING — HOLLOWMERE HARBOUR AUTHORITY*, which is a lie twice over, because the Harbour Authority has no jurisdiction over private property and also because the sign was printed by the same shop that prints the church raffle posters. I know the font. I know the paper stock. I know that whoever commissioned it wanted it to look official without actually being official, which in Hollowmere is what we call governance.

Then the dock. The dock was repainted the summer after Marguerite Voss drowned. Not repaired—repainted. There was nothing structurally wrong with it. A dock doesn't need fresh gray paint ten weeks after a woman dies at the end of it unless someone wants the wood to stop telling stories. And paint does tell stories, if you know how to listen. I stood at the rail this morning and looked at the boards and remembered—the memory arriving uninvited, as it does—that the original boards had a stain near the mooring cleat. Dark. The kind of dark that rain doesn't explain. I was fourteen when I arrived in this town and twenty-four when she died, and I have never once been able to look at that cleat without seeing the stain that isn't there anymore.

The constable retired early. That's the third item in the catalogue. Constable Hale gave up his badge at fifty-three, which in Hollowmere years is roughly the retirement age of a healthy oak tree. He took up tomatoes. Everyone said how nice, how he'd earned it, how the force would miss him. Nobody asked why a man who loved the job—I remember him loving the job, I remember him walking his beat like a man in love with the very idea of order—nobody asked why he left the year after the drowning. In Hollowmere we don't ask questions that have answers. We ask questions like *lovely weather* and *how's the family*, questions engineered to receive answers engineered to close the subject.

I passed the bakery, where Agnes's bread is sold under another woman's name because Agnes only bakes for the house now. I passed Fenwick's, where the padlock was purchased. I passed the tavern where Marcus Voss drinks with the enthusiasm of a man trying to drown something that floats. And everywhere I went, people nodded to me, smiled at me, called me by name—and every smile had the same architecture. Warmth on the surface, a locked door underneath.

That's the thing about belonging somewhere by adoption rather than birth. You learn to read the difference between being welcomed and being included. This town welcomed me the day the Vosses took me in. They have never entirely included me, because inclusion requires telling you what everyone has agreed not to know, and I came here too late for the agreement and too permanently for them to pretend I don't exist.

*

I arrived at Voss House on a rainy evening in October, fourteen years old, holding a suitcase containing four shirts, a photograph of my parents, and a hardcover dictionary I had already read cover to cover. Orphaned twice over—my mother when I was six, my father when I was thirteen—and shuttled through two foster placements that failed for reasons the paperwork described as "fit" and my own memory describes with considerably more precision.

Marguerite Voss met me at the door. I remember everything about that meeting, because I remember everything about every meeting, which is both my gift and my affliction. She wore green. She looked at me the way a woman looks at furniture she's agreed to store for a friend—assessing, slightly weary, already calculating where I'd fit. Behind her stood the twins, Delphine and Marcus, fifteen and identical in the way that only fraternal twins can be identical: same height, same coloring, opposite in every way that mattered. Delphine looked at me like a problem she intended to solve. Marcus looked at me like a friend he hadn't met yet.

And behind all three of them, half in shadow in the hallway, stood a small round woman with flour on her apron and eyes that saw straight through the suitcase and the dictionary and the careful blankness I'd learned to wear like a coat.

"You're thin," Agnes Whitlock said. It was the first thing she ever said to me. "When did you last eat?"

"I ate," I said, because I was fourteen and proud and lying.

She turned to Marguerite and said, "He's lying," and then she took my suitcase out of my hand and led me to the kitchen, and that was that. I belonged to Voss House from that moment—not to Marguerite, who paid for me the way one pays for a roof repair, necessary and unglamorous, but to the kitchen, and to Agnes, and eventually, in ways none of us planned, to the twins.

Agnes fed me that night until I couldn't eat another bite, and then she sat across from me while I ate anyway, and she never once asked me about my parents. Everyone else asked. Strangers at the funeral home asked. The caseworker asked. The whole world seemed organized around the question of my grief, wanting to inspect it, weigh it, confirm it matched the shipping label.

Agnes just fed me. Ten years later I understand that this was her entire philosophy of love: don't interrogate the wound, feed the body, and let the wound heal or don't on its own schedule. She taught me to knead bread that first winter, my hands inside hers, saying, "You don't have to talk. The dough listens better than people anyway."

I have never told anyone this, but I think that was the moment I stopped being an orphan. Not legally—legally I remained a ward, a case number, a line item in Marguerite's charitable deductions. But in the place where it counts, in the kitchen-smelling interior country where a boy decides who his people are, I was claimed that night. Claimed by a housekeeper with flour on her hands, in a house owned by a woman who would be dead within a decade, in a town that would spend those ten years agreeing not to wonder why.

*

Back on Main Street, the morning was getting on. Mrs. Aldous was sweeping her step—step forty-two—and she nodded at me with the particular brightness people use when they hope you won't stop.

"Morning, Casper."

"Mrs. Aldous." I stopped anyway. I'm not proud of it, but grief makes some of us rude. "Lovely weather."

"Lovely," she agreed, and swept harder, and I moved on before either of us had to say anything true.

Outside the post office, Tom Bracken was coiling rope outside his chandlery, and I watched him watch me approach, and I watched him decide to be busy with the rope. Tom was on the dock the morning they found her. I know this because I was on the dock too—I'd gone down at dawn because sleep and I were barely speaking that autumn—and I remember the crowd assembling in the exact order it assembled: Tom first, then the Ellery brothers, then word spreading inland like spilled milk finding every crack in a floor. Tom had been white as the foam on the pilings. He'd said, "Poor woman," and then he'd said nothing else for eleven days. I counted. Eleven days of silence from a man who normally narrated the tide like a sports commentator.

"Poor woman," Tom might have meant. Or Tom might have meant *poor everyone*, or *poor us*, or *I saw something and I will carry it to my grave because carrying things to graves is what this town does instead of burying them*. With Tom, as with everyone here, the sentence you hear is the smallest possible subset of the sentence available.

I crossed to the harbor wall and stood where I'd stood that dawn ten years ago, and let the memory play, because refusing to watch it has never once worked.

Gray water. Gulls going mad over something past the moorings. The police boat, slow and official. And Marguerite Voss coming ashore under a tarp, and the whole town lining the seawall like mourners at a parade, and every face doing the same arithmetic: *tragedy, terrible, unthinkable, and thank God it wasn't me.*

I remember thinking, even then, even at twenty-four, even gutted by it because she had taken me in and fed me in her distant, transactional way: *the tide is wrong.* I didn't know why I thought it. I filed it away the way I file everything—involuntarily, completely, against my will—and there it has sat for ten years, a loose thread in a sweater everyone insisted was fine.

Here's what nobody tells you about a photographic memory: it isn't a camera. A camera is neutral. What I have is more like a curse of total witness. Everything stays. Every kindness, every cruelty, every stain on a dock board, every eleven-day silence, every face at every funeral arranged in perfect permanent rows. Most people get to forget on purpose. That's the mercy built into ordinary minds—the mind edits, softens, loses the phone numbers and the insults and the exact color of the water. Mine keeps everything at full resolution, forever, and the only choice I've ever had is what to do with the archive.

Agnes understood that about me before I did. Second winter at Voss House, I recited the twins' school reports back to them verbatim, including Delphine's humiliating C in music, and Delphine cried, and Agnes found me in the pantry afterward and didn't scold me. She said, "You can't help what stays in your head, love. But you choose what comes out of your mouth. That's the whole of wisdom, right there. Memory's only a gift if you decide what it's for."

I was fifteen. I thought she was talking about tact.

I am thirty-four now, standing on a harbor wall with two letters in my breast pocket—one confessing to murder, one offering money for silence—and I am beginning to suspect she was talking about something much larger than tact.

*

By noon I'd completed the circuit. The catalogue read as follows:

Item: one boathouse, sealed, sign fraudulent, lock purchased at Fenwick's, purpose unstated.

Item: one dock, repainted prematurely, stain removed, story sanded smooth.

Item: one constable, retired early, currently growing tomatoes with the intensity of a man doing penance.

Item: one chandlery owner with an eleven-day silence. One baker's step swept with excessive attention. One main street where forty-one steps between landmarks can become forty-two if a broom is in the way, and where every resident can tell you the price of mackerel but not one will tell you what they saw the week Marguerite Voss went into the water.

Item: one investigator, adopted, fed, raised, beloved in the specific hollow way this town loves its own—which is to say, warmly, publicly, and strictly within the terms of the agreement.

Because that's what it is. I finally named it this morning, standing at the rail of the repainted dock. An agreement. Unwritten, unsigned, renewed annually at the memorial service with flowers and lowered voices. *We do not ask. We do not compare notes. We repaint, we retire, we lock the boathouse, and we call it healing.*

And into this carefully maintained amnesia, someone has posted two letters. Someone who knows enough to confess and enough to pay. Someone who knows me well enough to know that Casper Okonkwo does not forget on purpose, has never forgotten on purpose, and cannot be bought off with money any more than he can be bought off with bread—though God knows the bread has come closer.

I turned away from the harbor and started up the hill toward Voss House, because there was one more item to add to the catalogue, and it wasn't a place or a silence or a lock. It was a pair of hands. Hands that kneaded dough and pulled splinters from children's fingers and once, I realized with a coldness that started in my stomach and spread outward like the tide, held a woman underwater—or so a stranger's letter claims, in handwriting I haven't yet managed to identify, though I have spent two nights trying.

The insurance pretext was already composed in my head. Estate review, policy updates, the dull liturgy of my profession. Agnes would feed me. Agnes always feeds me. And I would sit in her kitchen, in the room where I was saved, and I would watch her hands, and I would try to make the two pictures agree: the hands that raised me, and the hands the letter describes.

They are the same hands. That's the horror of it. Whatever the truth turns out to be, they are the same hands, and only one of us gets to decide what they were for.

The gate to Voss House clicked shut behind me, and from the kitchen window, Agnes saw me coming and waved, and began, I knew, to put the kettle on.
