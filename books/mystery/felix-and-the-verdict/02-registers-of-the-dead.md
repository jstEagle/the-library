## 2. Registers of the Dead

The money sat in my desk drawer all night, and I confess I dreamed about it.

Not the amount — though three times my usual fee was not nothing, and "not nothing" for an archivist who charges by the hour and is rarely hired at all — but the way Elias Crane had counted it out. Not in crisp notes, the way a man pays when he wants to impress. In soft, creased bills that had clearly lived a long time folded inside a sock or a Bible, each one smoothed flat against his knee before he laid it down, as if he were apologizing to it for spending it.

I dreamed of the bills smoothing themselves, one after another, into a paper trail. When I woke at six with the cathedral bells going overhead like a thought I couldn't shake, I knew I'd already decided.

This annoyed me. I had decided nothing. I had told him no, more or less. I had said, *I don't do people, Mr. Crane; I do paper,* and he had looked at me with those wet, hopeful eyes and said, *Then find me the paper that says she's alive.*

Which was, I'll admit, a very good line. Men who tremble are not usually clever, and men who are clever are not usually sincere, and Elias Crane had managed both, which made him either the most honest client I would ever have or the most dangerous. There was no third option. There never is.

So: breakfast (toast, eaten standing over the sink like a woman with somewhere to be), keys, torch, and down into the archive before the vergers arrived to make the building holy again. The archive does its best work unholy — in the dark, before anyone's prayed anything, when the records are still just records and not yet relics.

* * *

The burial registers live in the iron cabinet against the north wall, because paper fears fire more than it fears God, and rightly so. I pulled the volume for the relevant year — three years back now, the year Vivian Crane died, if she died — and set it on the reading table under the green lamp.

Here is what nobody tells you about death registers: they are the most honest books in any cathedral. Baptismal registers lie cheerfully — every child recorded there is beloved, wanted, born into hope, even when the truth was otherwise. Marriage registers perform. But a burial register has no audience. Nobody reads them for pleasure. They record only what could not be argued with: a name, a date, a grave. Even God, I suspect, doesn't fact-check them. He was there.

Vivian Crane's entry was where it should be. October, three years past. *Vivian Alice Crane, organist of this cathedral, aged 34 years, died 14th October, buried 21st October, consecrated ground, plot 447, east section.*

And beneath it, as certifying officer: *Ambrose J. Locke, Sub-Dean.*

I sat with that for a while.

Father Locke signs everything. That is not an accusation; it is a description. He signs funeral certificates and flower-fund receipts and the guest book at the deanery with the same expansive, confident hand, a hand that believes in itself, loops and flourishes like a man waving from a train. I have catalogued four hundred of his signatures in my time here. I know his pen pressure the way some women know their husbands' snoring.

His signature on Vivian Crane's certificate was perfect. That was the first wrong thing.

Because here was the second wrong thing: the rest of the entry wasn't his.

Look — I don't expect laypeople to understand this, and I don't blame them. But a register entry is written by one hand, start to finish, in one sitting, with one pen. Ink changes shade only if you stop for tea, and even then the difference is subtle, a deepening, not a transformation. What you do not get, in an honest book, is an entry that begins in one handwriting and ends in another.

The first line — her name, her office, her age — was written in a small, careful, upright script. Slightly cramped. The letters leaned on each other like tired commuters. It was not Father Locke's hand, and it was not the registrar's usual hand either, which is round and lazy and dots its i's like it's throwing them away.

Then, mid-entry — between *aged 34 years* and *died 14th October* — the writing changed. It opened up. It grew confident. The loops lengthened, the slant relaxed, and by the time the pen reached *Ambrose J. Locke* it was, letter for letter, stroke for stroke, Father Locke's signature entire.

Except it wasn't, because Father Locke hadn't written it. Someone had *become* him partway through a sentence.

I have seen this before.

Three years ago — no. Longer. Five years ago, before I understood what this archive was for, before I understood what *I* was for, there had been a case. A legacy question, a disputed pew endowment, nothing dramatic. But in working through the old fabric accounts I found a receipt whose signature shifted the same way — mid-word, almost, a chrysalis of ink, one hand hatching into another. I had traced it then to a clerk in the diocesan office, a small grey man who wept when I showed him, and who gave up a name in exchange for my silence. A name I wrote on a card, filed under F, for Forgeries, because I am a coward about filing things under names.

I got up, took down box F, and found the card.

*Harrow.* R. Harrow, solicitor, Chancery Lane chambers, retained by the cathedral restoration trust.

I stood very still in the cold, holding a five-year-old index card, and felt the particular sensation I have never learned to describe to anyone, because there has never been anyone to describe it to. It starts in the sternum. It is part thrill and part dread and part something warmer than either, and it means: *the pattern is real. You saw truly, once, and here it is again, larger.* It is the feeling of being right, which for a woman like me is the closest thing to being loved.

I put the card back. I washed my hands, though there was nothing on them. Old habit. Paper leaves residue of a kind.

* * *

Plot 447, east section, was easy to find. The east section is the clergy-and-dignitaries quarter, all good marble and confident angels, and 447 sat in the shadow of a decomposed archdeacon whose stone face had weathered into an expression of permanent mild disappointment.

The Crane stone was new-ish granite, well cut:

*VIVIAN ALICE CRANE, 1979–2016*
*"She made the stones sing."*

Very nice. Expensive. Paid for, I'd have bet my pension, out of trust funds administered by R. Harrow, solicitor.

I crouched and did what I always do, which is look at what isn't there. No lichen on the north face — the stone was too new, fine. But also: no rain-staining below the carved letters, no settling tilt, and the turf over the grave was level, dense, unbroken. Three-year-old graves settle. The earth remembers being disturbed and takes years to forget it. This earth had forgotten completely, or had never been asked.

I pressed my palm flat against the grass. Cold, even for October. Deeper cold than surface soil holds.

Underneath the turf, faint but unmistakable to anyone who has spent thirty years reading floors the way other people read faces: the seam of a slab. Not soil. Stone, turfed over.

You do not bury a body under a stone slab and then turf it neatly. Turf is for hiding. Slabs are for returning to.

I stood up, brushed off my knees, and became aware — the way one does, late, that one has been watched for some time — of a smell. Woodsmoke and engine oil and, beneath it, the particular sweetness of a man who sleeps outdoors and has made his peace with it.

"Don't mind me," said a voice from behind the archdeacon. "Just keeping the dead company. They're better listeners than the living."

Denzil emerged around the monument, pushing a supermarket trolley loaded with tarps, a coil of copper wire, and what I sincerely hoped was a tuning fork. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that had opinions and eyes that missed nothing, which is a combination people underestimate until it's too late. He had tuned the cathedral organ for twenty years before the chapter let him go, and now he slept in the crypt, unofficially, and everyone official pretended not to know, which is the arrangement cathedrals do best.

"You're the archivist," he said. "Marguerite. Pemberton." He pronounced my name carefully, like a chord he wanted to get right. "You come down here sometimes. Tuesdays. You check the cistern water level and you think nobody sees."

"I check for damp," I said.

"You check for damp," he agreed gravely. "Nobody checks for damp on Tuesdays." He looked at the grave, then at me, and his voice dropped into something quieter. "You're asking questions about her, aren't you. The organist."

I should have said nothing. My whole life is built on saying nothing. But the feeling in my sternum was still burning, and Denzil was looking at me with the expression of a man who has been waiting years for someone to ask.

"What makes you say that?" I said.

"Because you touched the ground." He nodded at my hand. "People who visit graves pray. People who touch the ground are investigating." He scratched his beard. "And because someone plays her organ at midnight. Every week. Her own music — stuff she never played for the chapter, she used to hum it in the loft when she thought the building was empty. I heard her humming it through the floor for two years." He looked toward the tower, where the morning light hadn't reached yet. "Dead women don't practice scales, Miss Pemberton."

No, I thought. They don't.

I thanked him — stiffly, I'm sure; gratitude sits awkwardly on me, like a coat borrowed from a taller woman — and walked back across the grass with the index-card name turning over in my mind like a coin. *Harrow.* And above it now, in the same careful upright hand that had begun Vivian Crane's register entry: a second question I hadn't yet asked aloud.

Who wrote the first half of that entry? Because whoever forged Locke's signature had needed a genuine sample to copy from — and the neat, cramped, tired-commuter hand that opened the entry belonged to someone who had written Vivian Crane's death details *accurately*. From knowledge.

Someone had written down how and when she died. And then someone else had picked up the pen and buried her.

I locked the archive behind me, and for the first time in years I left the light on in the window — a small, stupid, human signal, visible from the close, telling anyone who cared to look: *someone is in here. Someone is watching the paper.*

Elias Crane had paid me triple to prove his sister was alive.

It was beginning to look as though the paper agreed with him — and that the men who wrote it disagreed most urgently of all.
