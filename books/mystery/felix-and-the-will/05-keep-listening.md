## 5. Keep Listening

The second letter came on a Friday, which Xanthe would later decide was deliberate. Fridays were the nights the depot filled with noise — drivers arguing over shift swaps, the coffee machine dying its weekly death, someone's radio bleeding weather reports through three walls. A letter that wanted to be noticed would have come on a Tuesday, quiet as the first one had. This one came buried in the noise, folded inside a stack of vendor invoices that nobody read until Monday.

Xanthe found it because Xanthe always found things. That was the job, or the sickness behind the job. Every fare was a case: pickup here, drop-off there, a route that either made sense or didn't. Most people looked at a dispatch log and saw numbers. Xanthe saw testimony.

The envelope had no stamp. Hand-delivered, then. Someone had walked it into the office and slipped it into the invoice pile sometime between eleven and midnight, when the front desk emptied out for the driver changeover. The handwriting on the outside was block capitals, careful and sexless: **X. OYELARAN, DISPATCH**. No return address. No postmark to trace. Just the name, spelled correctly, which bothered Xanthe more than it should have. Half the company still got the surname wrong.

Inside was a single sheet of paper, torn from a legal pad, and on it, in the same block capitals, one line:

**PAGE 114.**

The number was circled twice. The ink was blue, ballpoint, pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath it. Whoever wrote it had been angry, or afraid, or both. Fear and fury leave similar signatures in handwriting — too much pressure, not enough patience.

Xanthe sat with it for a long time while the depot roared around them. *More if you keep listening*, the first note had said. And now this. Not a threat. An instruction. Someone out there wasn't just watching Xanthe listen. They were grading the homework.

* * *

The old paper ledgers lived in the archive room in the basement, down a stairwell that smelled of cold concrete and mouse droppings and the ghost of forty years of cigarette smoke soaked into the walls back when smoking at your post was legal and universal. The room itself was a grid of gray steel shelving, each shelf sagging under the weight of bound volumes in cracked leatherette covers, organized by year, then by month, then by nothing anyone could explain. The company had gone digital in 2009 and never thrown anything away, because throwing things away required a budget line, and this company had never met a budget line it liked.

Page 114 belonged to a ledger from six years back. October. Xanthe pulled the volume off the shelf, set it on the scarred wooden table under the flickering fluorescent tube, and opened it with the care of a surgeon and the reluctance of a man opening a bill.

The pages were ruled columns: date, vehicle number, driver ID, pickup zone, drop-off zone, fare, tip, dispatcher initials. Thousands of entries in the cramped hands of a dozen different dispatchers across a decade. Most of it was noise. Fares to the airport. Fares home from the hospital. Drunks delivered gently to their doors at 2 a.m., their tips recorded in quarters.

But Xanthe wasn't looking for most of it.

They'd spent two weeks copying anomalous entries into a notebook — the identical fares that repeated like a stutter, the tips that exceeded the fare by amounts no drunk ever paid, the routes that looped, pointlessly, past the industrial park where Ada Voss had once worked her courier rounds before going independent. Xanthe hadn't known what the pattern meant yet. They'd only known it was there, the way you know someone is standing behind you in a dark room without hearing a footstep.

Page 114 held fourteen entries. Xanthe read them once, twice, and then went very still.

Seven of them were flagged. Not flagged officially — nothing so helpful as a red mark or an asterisk. Flagged in the way Xanthe had learned to see them: the fare of $23.40 repeating across different nights, different vehicles, different drivers; the tips logged at exactly double the fare, down to the cent, which no human being did, because human beings tipped in fives and tens and whatever crumpled bills they could find in the dark; the drop-offs clustered along a stretch of Harrow Street that had no businesses, no residences worth visiting, nothing but a shuttered print shop and a loading dock that hadn't taken deliveries since the nineties.

Seven entries on one page. Xanthe's notebook held copies of maybe thirty such entries scattered across four months of ledgers. Thirty felt like a curiosity. Seven on a single page felt like a sentence.

* * *

Saturday night, the depot thinned out around 1 a.m., and Xanthe stayed. This was normal — Xanthe always stayed, chasing the last stragglers' paperwork, reconciling the till, doing the small invisible labor that kept the night side running and kept management grateful enough not to ask questions. What wasn't normal was what Xanthe did after: pulled every ledger volume from October of that year through March of the next, and started cross-referencing.

The method was simple and brutal. For each flagged entry — each impossible tip, each stuttering fare, each dead-end drop-off — Xanthe recorded the dispatcher initials in the final column. Dispatchers signed their work. It was accountability theater, mostly; nobody ever audited the initials, and half the drivers used whatever letters were convenient when they logged themselves. But the system existed, and systems leave fingerprints even when the people using them think they're wiping.

Xanthe built a tally on the back page of the notebook. Initials down the left margin, tick marks beside them.

By 3 a.m., the tally looked like this:

Most dispatchers' initials appeared once, twice, three times across thirty-odd flagged entries. Statistically meaningless. Anybody could have logged a fare on a weird night.

One set of initials appeared twenty-six times.

**D.K.**

Xanthe stared at it. Twenty-six out of thirty-four. If the flagged entries were random noise, the distribution should have been flat. Instead it was a spike, a mountain range with one peak. Whatever these entries recorded — and Xanthe still didn't know what they recorded, only that they weren't fares — they had passed through one pair of hands more often than chance could survive.

D.K. D.K. D.K.

Xanthe knew the initials the way everyone at the company knew them, the way you know the names carved into a building's cornerstone. There was a plaque in the lobby. There had been a retirement party two years ago, catered, held at a steakhouse downtown, attended by men in good coats who gave speeches about service and loyalty. Denny Kroll. Night supervisor for nineteen years. The man who had trained half the current staff, including, in a distant, impersonal way, Xanthe herself — a two-day orientation session eight years ago where Kroll had explained the ledger system with the pride of a man showing off his own kitchen.

*"Paper doesn't lie,"* Kroll had said, tapping the bound volume. *"Computers lie all day long. Paper remembers."*

Xanthe had thought, at the time, that it was just something old men said.

Now it sounded like a confession.

* * *

Sunday, Xanthe did something they had never done in nine years of night shifts: called in favors during daylight. The personnel office was closed weekends, but Marta in payroll owed Xanthe for covering three Christmases running, and Marta could pull a file faster than any system, legal or otherwise.

"Denny Kroll?" she said over the phone, her voice pitched low the way people do when they're gossiping and know it. "Retired, what, two years ago now? Best pension in company history, if you ask me. He went out at the top of the scale. There were rumors."

"What kind of rumors?"

"The kind that get you invited to fewer parties." A pause, papers shuffling somewhere far away. "Why do you want him? You doing some kind of anniversary thing?"

"Something like that."

"Well, he didn't go far. Bought a place out past the reservoir. Big house, big boat, big everything. Guy drove a cab company desk for twenty years and retired like he'd sold software." She laughed, but it was a thin laugh. "Anyway. Why?"

"Curiosity."

"Xanthe." Marta's voice dropped further. "You're the least curious person I know. That's why everybody likes you. Don't start now."

Xanthe hung up and sat in the break room with the vending machine humming against the silence. *The least curious person I know.* It was meant as a compliment. It was also, Xanthe understood with sudden clarity, a description of camouflage. Nine years of being nobody, of taking every shift, of asking nothing and noticing everything — it had worked. It had kept Xanthe safe and invisible and employed.

And now somebody had put a name on an envelope anyway.

* * *

Monday night, Xanthe went back to page 114 and read it differently. Not as a pattern anymore. As a record.

Fourteen entries. Seven flagged. All seven dispatched by D.K., all seven between the hours of 11 p.m. and 4 a.m., all seven on nights when the weather was bad — Xanthe checked, pulling the old forecasts from the newspaper morgue at the library on her dinner break, because bad weather meant fewer witnesses on the street and slower police response and cabs that could move through the city unremarked. Rain, sleet, fog. Every flagged entry sat inside a curtain of bad weather like a jewel in a setting.

And the vehicles. Xanthe had noted the vehicle numbers weeks ago without understanding them. Now, cross-referencing against maintenance logs — public records, filed with the city's livery board, boring beyond words and therefore unread by anyone — a second pattern surfaced. Every vehicle in a flagged entry had gone into the shop within seventy-two hours afterward. Bodywork. Repainting. Interior detailing. One car, unit 44, had been in the bay for six days for what the log described as "rear panel replacement."

Unit 44. October 14th. The night Ada Voss died on Corbett Avenue, three miles from where Marcus Teale's cab was logged, struck by a vehicle that left behind a headlight fragment the police report described as consistent with "a late-model fleet sedan."

Marcus Teale drove unit 31.

Xanthe closed the notebook and pressed both palms flat against the table until the wood bit into the skin. The archive room was silent except for the drip of a pipe somewhere and the fluorescent light's insect whine. Somewhere overhead, the depot carried on — phones ringing, radios crackling, the whole machinery of ordinary nights grinding forward as if the paper beneath the building weren't testifying.

The first letter had confessed to killing Ada Voss. The details hadn't matched the logs; Xanthe had proven that much already. Marcus Teale was buying a verdict with his guilty plea, or someone was buying it for him. And now the money trail — because that's what the tips were, Xanthe was almost sure, payments dressed up as gratuities, a cleaner's wage hidden in plain sight in columns no auditor ever read — now the money trail ran through the initials of a man who had retired rich two years ago and lived past the reservoir with a boat.

*More if you keep listening.*

Xanthe took out the second letter again and studied it under the bad light. Block capitals. Blue ballpoint. Heavy pressure. And then, for the first time, Xanthe turned it over — really turned it over, holding it up to the fluorescent tube, tilting it against the glare.

There, faint as breath on glass: an impression in the paper. Something had been written on the sheet above it, on the pad, and torn away — but the pen had pressed hard, and paper keeps secrets the way Kroll said it did. Xanthe angled the page, squinting, tracing the ghost-grooves with a fingertip.

Three characters. Maybe four. The first was unmistakable, a capital letter with a sharp diagonal spine.

An *N*. Or possibly a *K*.

Below it, half-formed, the curve of another letter — an *S*, or a *V*, or nothing at all.

Xanthe stood in the basement for a long moment, the letter trembling slightly in a hand that hadn't trembled in nine years. Then they copied the impression into the notebook, sealed the letter in a plastic sleeve, and climbed the stairs toward the noise of the living depot, carrying the first real thread of the case like a lit fuse.

Above them, a phone rang. Above them, someone answered, and gave a pickup address, and sent a cab out into the rain.

Above them, the night went on keeping its books.
