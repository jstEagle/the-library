## 2. What the Tide Keeps

Greywater Bay announced itself the way a liar does — with too much charm.

The road in from the highway curved along a ridge of black spruce, and then the trees simply gave up and there it was: a bowl of grey water held between two arms of granite, a town of salt-bleached shingles stacked along the shore like driftwood someone had bothered to arrange. Fishing boats knocked gently against the pilings of a long municipal dock. Gulls hung motionless over the harbor as if pinned there.

I pulled my rental onto the shoulder and did what I always do before entering a story. I said the dead boy's name out loud.

"Daniel Voss."

Seven years old when he walked out of his foster home on a Tuesday in October and never came back. Seven years since his foster brother, Cole Merrick, seventeen years old, confessed to drowning him in the shallows and then walked into the bay himself before anyone could ask a second question. Case closed. File sealed. Town healed.

Except for one letter, postmarked the day after Cole's funeral, sitting in a fireproof box in my apartment four hundred miles south, insisting that none of that was true.

I put the car in gear. The sign at the town line read WELCOME TO GREYWATER BAY — POP. 2,140, and beneath it, smaller, newer, a brass plaque bolted by some civic committee: *A Community That Remembers.*

Everyone remembers, I thought. The question is what they've agreed to remember.

* * *

The Greywater Bay Public Library occupied a converted cannery at the end of Water Street, all exposed beams and donated furniture, and the woman behind the desk had the rehearsed smile of someone who'd answered the same questions for seven years.

"Anniversary piece," I told her, handing over a business card with a name I use for exactly this purpose. "Sable Eastwick, freelance. Coastal Review. Seven years since the Merrick confession — my editor wants a piece on how a town moves forward after something like that."

"And you picked us." Her name tag said DELIA. She turned the card over as if checking it for a trap. "We get a lot of press people around the anniversary. Or we used to. They mostly stopped coming."

"Why's that?"

"They kept asking the same questions," she said, "and we kept giving the same answers, and eventually even they got bored." She smiled again, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges. "There's a memorial bench down by the seawall. Daniel's mother — foster mother, Mrs. Voss — she tends the flowers there herself. You should talk to her. Everyone does."

"I'd like that. And the harbormaster? I understand Aldous Crane was central to the search."

Something moved behind Delia's eyes — not hostility, nothing so useful as hostility. It was more like a door closing quietly in a house where you hadn't realized there were doors.

"Aldous is the best thing this town has," she said. "You'll want to be careful how you frame him. He doesn't like being framed."

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what journalists do when they want a town to talk: I bought things. Coffee at the Saltbox Diner, where the waitress told me about the herring season without being asked. Bait at the tackle shop on Front Street, where the owner told me about the herring season again, word for word, as if they'd rehearsed it together. Diesel at the marina, where a man with forearms like mooring rope looked at my out-of-state plates and said, "You'll be here about the boy, then," and walked away before I could answer.

By four o'clock I had a notebook full of weather, fish prices, and grief — grief delivered in identical cadences, grief with its rough edges sanded off, grief that had been through so many retellings it had become a kind of civic uniform. Everyone wore it. Everyone wore it the same way.

And underneath it, everywhere, the same small reflex. Mention Crane, and people's eyes went to the middle distance. Mention the night Daniel disappeared, and their hands found something to do — wipe a counter, straighten a shelf, check a phone that wasn't ringing.

A locked room, I wrote in the margin of my notebook. But locked rooms have keys, and somebody in this town is holding one.

I found the memorial bench at dusk. It sat on the seawall facing the flats — a wide, terrible expanse of tidal mud that at low tide ran nearly half a mile out from shore, ribbed and glistening, cut with channels that filled and emptied with the moon's indifferent schedule. A child could walk out there. A child could walk out there and the water would come back for him like it always does, patient and total.

Someone had planted white asters in a stone-bordered bed beside the bench. They were freshly deadheaded. Kneeling among them with a pair of kitchen scissors was a woman in her sixties, silver hair clipped short, cardigan buttoned against the wind.

"Mrs. Voss?" I said.

She didn't startle. She finished the stem she was cutting, stood, and looked at me with an expression I recognized from a hundred interviews — the flat, pre-loaded patience of a person who has answered everything already.

"You're the writer," she said. "Delia called ahead. You're doing the anniversary piece."

"If you'd rather not—"

"No." She set the scissors down on the bench, precisely, parallel to the slats. "I'd rather you write it correctly. Cole Merrick killed my Danny. Whatever else anyone tells you — whatever theories, whatever rumors — that part is true, and I will say it to your face, and I will say it to any editor who calls me. Are we clear?"

"We're clear," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because the letter in my fireproof box said otherwise, and I had come four hundred miles because of it.

She studied me for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then her face softened into something almost kind.

"You're younger than the others," she said. "The ones who came before. They came looking for a monster. You look like you're looking for something else."

"What do you mean?"

"I don't know yet." She picked up her scissors and a watering can that had been hidden behind the asters. "But when you find it, you'll wish you hadn't. They all did." She paused at the edge of the seawall path. "Talk to Aldous. If you're going to write about this town, you should meet the man who holds it together."

* * *

I met Aldous Crane the next morning, though technically he met me first.

I was standing on the public dock taking photographs of the flats — establishing shots, the kind of thing an anniversary piece actually needs — when a voice behind me said, "Careful on those boards. Third plank from the ramp's been soft since the spring."

He was sixty-something, built like the breakwater he maintained, with a full white beard and the deep tan of a man whose office is outdoors. Flannel shirt, canvas jacket, a knit cap pushed back off his forehead. He looked less like a harbormaster than like a painting of one, the kind a chamber of commerce commissions.

"Sable Eastwick," I said, offering my hand. "Coastal Review. Anniversary piece."

"Crane." His grip was dry and strong and lasted exactly the right length of time. "Delia mentioned you. The piece on moving forward." He nodded toward the flats. "That's where they found the truck, you know. Cole's truck. Out past the channel marker, stuck in the mud like it grew there. Whole town walked out on the flats that morning. Two hundred people, maybe more, picking through the mud in a line." He shook his head slowly. "You don't forget a sight like that."

"Was that when they knew?"

"That was when we knew." He said it simply, without performance, and that simplicity was itself a kind of performance — the practiced ease of a man who has told this story until it sits in his mouth like a smooth stone. "Cole confessed the next day. Wrote it all out, signed it. Bad business. Bad, sad business." He looked at me, and his eyes were pale blue and perfectly steady. "Your editor wants healing, give them healing. This town earned it."

"I'd love to interview you properly."

"Anytime." He smiled, and the smile reached all the way up, and I understood immediately why this town loved him, why Delia closed her door for him, why Mrs. Voss sent me to him like a tribute. "Come by the harbormaster's office tomorrow. I'll show you the search maps. And Eastwick—" he said it lightly, turning away, already leaving, "—the folks here have been through enough anniversaries. Write it gentle. For them."

He walked down the dock with the unhurried gait of a man who owns every board of it.

* * *

It happened that evening, at the Saltbox Diner, and it took less than ninety seconds.

I'd claimed the corner booth to transcribe notes, and I'd made the mistake of working from a photocopy — the anonymous letter, flattened by my copier into grey type on cheap paper, tucked into my notebook as reference. The waitress refilled my coffee. The fry cook argued with the radio. Ordinary sounds.

Then a shadow fell across the table, and a woman's hand — quick, certain, trembling only at the very end — snatched the photocopy off my open notebook.

She was perhaps fifty-five, compact, wearing the kind of cardigan that has been mended rather than replaced. Her hair was pinned up carelessly, strands escaping at her temples, and her face had the particular bloodless quality of someone operating far outside her own nervous system.

"That's mine," I started. "That's—"

She didn't look at me. She looked at the diner — at the cook, at the window, at the street beyond the window — and then she walked, fast but not running, to the woodstove in the corner, opened its door, and fed my letter into the fire.

I was on my feet by the second step. Too late. The paper caught, curled, went orange, and was gone. She closed the stove door with a click of finality that I felt in my teeth.

When she finally turned to me, her eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, and she spoke so quietly that only I could hear it under the fryer's hiss.

"Don't come back here with that. Don't carry it. Don't leave it anywhere." Her gaze flicked once toward the window, toward the dark street, toward something I couldn't see. "They know what paper looks like. They know what ink smells like. You want to keep something safe in this town, you keep it in your head or you keep it nowhere."

"Who are you?" I whispered.

Her jaw worked. For one moment — one naked, unguarded moment — she looked at me the way a drowning person looks at a rope, and I thought, *this is the key, this is the locked room opening.*

Then the bell above the diner door rang, and a man in a canvas jacket stepped in out of the cold, stamping his boots, calling a greeting to the cook, and the woman flinched like a struck animal.

"He's just checking," she breathed. "He checks everywhere. Go back to your hotel. Burn your notes. Leave while the tide's out."

She retreated to the kitchen, and the man in the canvas jacket ordered a coffee to go, and looked at me exactly once — a long, mild, cataloguing look — before leaving.

I sat back down. My coffee had gone cold. Through the window I watched the taillights of his truck roll down Water Street and turn, unhurried, toward the harbor.

In the woodstove, the last of my letter settled into ash — the letter that named Aldous Crane, that said *he made Cole say it*, that had been mailed the day after Cole Merrick's funeral by someone who was still, evidently, alive.

Still alive. Still here. Still afraid enough to burn her own words twice.

I paid my bill, walked out into the salt wind, and stood on the seawall looking at the flats, black now under a rising moon, waiting for the tide to come in and cover everything it always covers.

Somewhere in this town was a woman who had just told me, in nine sentences, more than seven years of official record ever had.

All I had to do was find her before Crane did.
