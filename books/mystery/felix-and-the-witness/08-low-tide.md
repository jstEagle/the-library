## 8. Low Tide

The note came folded inside a hymnal Sable had bought at the church rummage sale three days earlier, a book they'd never opened, wedged under the door of the rental like a communion wafer slipped to a heretic. The paper was the same cream stock as the first letter. The handwriting was the same careful, schoolteacher's cursive.

*The marsh past the old cannery. Tomorrow. Low tide is at 5:40. Come alone. Come early. He watches the water when it gives things back.*

No signature. None needed. Sable had spent seven years learning to read the silences in other people's stories, and this one had a name written all through it — Maren Quill, the innkeeper who refilled their coffee without being asked and never once met their eyes.

They told no one. That was the whole point of coming alone, and besides, there was no one left to tell. Danny Okafor, the teenager who'd helped them pull property records, hadn't answered his phone in two days, and his mother had looked through Sable like weather when they knocked. The ransacked rental had taught its lesson well: everything Sable knew lived now only in their head and in a single flash drive sewn into the lining of their jacket, because Sable Eastwick did not make the same mistake twice.

At 4:15 in the morning, Greywater Bay was a held breath. Fog sat on the harbor in low gray banks, and the sodium lamps along the pier made halos that dissolved ten feet out. Sable walked the length of Front Street with their hood up, past Crane's dark office above the chandlery, past the memorial bench with Elian Marsh's name on it, worn smooth by seven years of hands that needed somewhere to rest their grief. At the cannery — a rusted skeleton of a building leaning toward the water like an old man listening for a train — Sable turned onto the mudflat path and let the fog take them.

* * *

Maren Quill was already there.

She stood at the edge of the exposed flats where the tide had retreated almost a quarter mile, leaving behind a landscape that looked like the surface of another planet — ribbed sand, tidal pools holding the last of the night sky, channels cutting silver threads through the muck. She wore rubber boots and a coat too heavy for the season, and she did not turn around when Sable's footsteps crunched the barnacle-crusted ground.

"You came," she said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped." Her voice was exactly what Sable had imagined from the letters: measured, precise, the voice of a woman who had spent thirty years choosing every word twice. "I wasn't sure you would. Smart people run."

"I'm not smart people. I'm a podcaster."

That earned something — not quite a laugh, but the ghost of one. Maren turned then, and up close her face was older than her file photo by decades, carved by salt and by whatever it costs to keep a secret that size. Her eyes went over Sable's shoulder, scanning the fog behind them, then back.

"The tide's still going out. We have maybe forty minutes before it turns." She started walking, boots sucking at the mud, out onto the flats proper. "Walk where I walk. There are channels that'll take you to your knees, and if you go down out here, nobody finds you until the water does."

Sable followed. The fog closed behind them like a door.

"Seven years ago," Maren said, not slowing, "I stood about where we're standing and watched Aldous Crane's truck parked on the flats at eleven o'clock at night. And in the cab of that truck, alive, was a boy who was supposed to be asleep in his bed on Harrow Street." She stopped at a channel's edge and pointed across it, to a place where the mud rose into a low hummock furred with samphire. "There."

Sable's mouth had gone dry. "You saw him. You saw Elian alive after the time the state says he was already dead."

"I saw him. And I said nothing, because three years earlier Aldous Crane had taken everything I had — my job, my name, my marriage — for saying less than this." She looked at Sable, and her eyes were terrible with it, the arithmetic she'd done and redone for seven years. "One child against my whole life. I told myself it wasn't a choice. It was a choice. I've been paying interest on it ever since."

"Why now? Why me?"

"Because you asked questions nobody else asked. Because you didn't leave when he scared you. And because—" Maren's voice caught, steadied. "Because the bay is giving things back. Look."

She led Sable down into the channel, along its firm bottomed edge, to the base of the hummock. Winter storms had scoured the flats all season; the erosion line ran six inches deep along the bank, and there, half-exposed in the collapsed sediment, was a shape that did not belong to any natural order of things.

Metal. A lockbox, olive-drab, army surplus, its hasp furred with corrosion but intact. Around it, pressed flat by seven years of mud, were smaller shapes: a canvas shoe gone the color of bone. A lunchbox, tin, dented. Something small and plastic that Sable refused, in that first second, to let themselves identify.

A backpack. Child-sized. Blue.

Sable crouched, and their hands were shaking so badly they had to press them flat against their thighs. "This is—"

"Elian's. His foster mother identified the backpack in the case file. They never found it. The confession ended the search." Maren knelt beside the box, working at the hasp with a pocketknife, her movements quick and practiced, as though she'd rehearsed this for years in her head. "I found it three weeks ago at a minus tide. I couldn't open it alone. I couldn't tell anyone alone. Do you understand what I'm saying, Mr.—Ms.—"

"Sable. Either works."

"Sable." She got the knife tip under the hasp. "I am asking you to be my witness. Whatever happens next, I need someone standing next to me who isn't from here. Someone whose word carries past the county line."

The hasp gave with a crack that sounded obscene in all that silence.

Inside: a ledger. A cheap composition book, its cover swollen with damp but its pages legible, kept dry by some miracle of the seal or by Crane's own carelessness about anything buried deeper than his conscience. Maren lifted it out with both hands, and Sable leaned close as she turned pages in the gray pre-dawn light.

Names. Dates. Amounts. *R. Pell — $400/mo — quiet re: dock fire.* *T. Vasquez — $250 — '86, ferry schedule.* *D. Hollis (deputy) — $600/mo — ongoing.* Page after page, decades of them, a town's silence itemized in a harbormaster's cramped hand. And near the end, dated four days after Elian Marsh disappeared:

*M. Quill — handled. No further payments required.*

"He wrote you down," Sable said quietly. "'Handled.'"

"I know." Maren's jaw was tight. "I know exactly what I am in that book. Turn to the last page."

Sable took the ledger. The final entry was recent — within the year. *D.O. — $1,000 — family situation resolved.* D.O. Danny Okafor. Sable felt the cold move through them like the tide itself.

"We need to go," they said. "Now. This can't stay here, and neither can we—"

Headlights.

Far off across the flats, where the path met the shore road, twin beams swept through the fog and cut engines. Two doors slammed. Voices, low, carrying badly over the mud — or carrying perfectly; impossible to tell.

"They watch the tides," Maren breathed. "I told you. He watches when the water gives things back." She grabbed Sable's arm. "The channel. Downstream. There's a culvert under the shore road, big enough to crawl. It comes up behind the boatyard."

"And the box? The ledger?"

Maren hesitated one full second — Sable watched her do the arithmetic again, seven years of it compressed into a heartbeat — and then she shoved the ledger into Sable's jacket, zipped it, and pressed the corroded lockbox lid shut over the backpack and the small plastic thing.

"It stays. Buried evidence, documented, photographed—" she was already pulling her phone, snapping pictures with hands that barely shook, "—chain of custody starts now. If we're caught with it, it disappears and so do we. If we're caught without it, it's still here, and the state police can dig it up in front of cameras."

Flashlights bloomed across the flats. Three beams now. Maybe four. They moved with purpose, fanning out, men who knew this ground.

"Go," Maren whispered.

They went — down into the channel, boots plunging through ankle-deep brine, the ledger a hard rectangle against Sable's ribs like a second heart. Behind them a voice called out across the mud, amplified by fog, unhurried and horribly familiar:

"Maren! I know those boots, honey. Tide'll be turning soon. Water waits for nobody."

Crane himself. Of course himself.

Maren didn't answer. She pulled Sable left, off the channel, toward a dark seam in the embankment where a concrete pipe exhaled the smell of old harbor. She went first, on hands and knees, and Sable followed into the black, the ledger clutched against their chest, the sound of boots on mud growing louder behind them and the sound of water ahead — the culvert running shallow, thank God, running shallow —

Halfway through, in the absolute dark, Maren's hand found Sable's and gripped it. Not for direction. For courage. Or to give it.

Neither of them spoke. They crawled together through the throat of the town that had swallowed a child and a teacher and a truth, toward the thin gray light at the other end, and behind them, faint but distinct, Sable heard the lockbox hasp click shut under someone else's hands.

Let him have the empty box, Sable thought. Let him think the tide keeps his secrets.

They emerged behind the boatyard as the first true light came up, muddy, soaked, breathing hard. Maren stood, wiped her face with a sleeve, and looked at Sable with an expression that had crossed some line and could not cross back.

"There's a town meeting Thursday," she said. "Storm or no storm. He's calling it himself — to run you out." A grim almost-smile. "He thinks numbers will save him. He's always thought that."

"What are you going to do?"

Maren Quill looked out at the harbor, at the water just beginning its long crawl back up the flats, covering everything again.

"I'm going to speak," she said.
