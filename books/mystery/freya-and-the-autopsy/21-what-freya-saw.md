## 21. What Freya Saw

The east wing had been dying for years before anyone set fire to it.

Freya Lindqvist knew that the way she knew the backs of her own hands—by touch, in the dark, without thinking. She was twenty-three and had cleaned every room in the Hotel Vesper for four years, but it was the east wing she loved best, or loved most honestly: the rooms nobody booked anymore, where the radiators knocked like prisoners and the wallpaper peeled in long curls that looked, if you squinted in the dusk, like maps of countries that no longer existed. She liked the silence of it. She liked being somewhere the hotel forgot.

She should not have been there that night. She knew that too.

But Marta had gone home sick with a fever, and someone had to strip Room 311 before the morning inspection, and Freya had stayed late—too late, past the hour when Mrs. Ansel locked the service corridor and stopped counting heads—and by the time she finished, the front lifts were shut down and the only way back toward the staff stair was through the east wing's spine, that long carpeted throat of a hallway where the emergency lights glowed amber as candle flame.

She was halfway down it when she heard voices from the study annex—the small office off the landing that old Mr. Vesper used for signing things, away from the grand study downstairs where he received people he wanted to impress. The annex door stood open an inch. The light inside was green-gold, lamplight through whisky glass, and the voices were low, which meant they were serious. Freya had learned early at the Vesper that loud voices were theater. It was the quiet ones you had to fear.

She meant to walk past. That was the whole of her intention—one more corridor crossed, one more night survived, head down, rag over her shoulder, invisible. Four years of practice pressed her shoulders into that shape.

Then she heard Cordelia say their father's name, and something in the way she said it—not *Father* but *Edmund*, flat and cold as a coin on a table—stopped Freya's feet as surely as a hand on her chest.

She stepped backward into the alcove of the linen cupboard. The door there hung on a broken hinge and never quite closed; she'd reported it twice and been ignored twice, and now she blessed the hotel's neglect. Through the gap she could see a slice of the annex: the desk, the lamp, the edge of a chair. And she could hear everything.

* * *

"You're asking me to steal from myself," Cordelia said.

"I'm asking you to be patient," Edmund Vesper answered, and even muffled by oak and distance, his voice carried the particular weariness of a man who has explained the same thing many times to someone who never listens. "Your mother's trust dissolves next year. When it does, everything reverts to the estate—to me, and then, God willing, to all three of you equally."

"Equally." Cordelia laughed, and it was not a laugh at all; it was a knife being drawn slowly so the sound would be remembered. "Alistair gets the hotel because Alistair has never wanted anything else. Julian gets whatever Julian wheedles out of you this quarter. And I get a third of a rotting building and a third of its debts. Mama's money came from *her* family, Papa. Her father built those shipping lines while yours was renting out rooms to traveling salesmen. That legacy is mine. You've been sitting on it for thirty years like a hen on a stone."

"Cordelia—"

"I'm not asking your permission." Papers rustled. "I'm telling you what's already done. Harrowgate Holdings. Three transfers this year, quarterly, routed through the solicitor in Leeds. Your signature. Clean, consistent, dated properly. No one will ever look twice."

Silence then—a long one, the kind that has weight. Freya found she was holding her breath, her cleaning cart abandoned halfway down the corridor behind her like a confession of her own. She should go back for it. She should go back for it right now, wheels squeaking, announce herself with the ordinary noise of ordinary work, let them know a maid was in the corridor so that nothing she overheard could ever matter.

She didn't move. Later, all the rest of her life, she would try to explain to herself why she didn't move, and the closest she ever came was this: it was the first time in four years that the Hotel Vesper had shown her its true face, and she could not look away.

"You forged my name," Edmund said at last. He didn't sound angry. That was the terrible thing, the thing Freya turned over for years afterward—he sounded *tired*. Sounding tired, from Edmund Vesper, was worse than shouting. Shouting was for strangers.

"I improved it," Cordelia said. "You hold your pen too high. Anyone who knows your hand would spot the real one."

"And what happens," Edmund said, very quietly now, "when someone finds out?"

"Who would find out? The accountants see what the accountants are given. Alistair sees ledgers, not signatures. Julian can't read a balance sheet sober." A pause, and when Cordelia spoke again her voice had changed—softer, sweeter, and somehow more frightening than the knife-laugh, because sweetness from Cordelia Vesper was a tool, and tools get used on things. "Papa. Sit down. Have your drink. We're family. Families keep each other's secrets—that's the whole point of one."

Freya heard the creak of the leather chair taking his weight. Heard the clink of decanter against glass. And then Edmund Vesper said, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy and deciding to leave it on the floor:

"The girl."

"What girl?"

"There was a girl. In the corridor. I saw her shadow pass the frosted panel when you were talking about Leeds." The ice shifted in his glass. "Housekeeping. Dark hair. Small."

Freya's heart did not stop—it kept beating, brutally, faithfully—but everything around it went white and silent, the way a room goes when a window breaks in winter. She was already moving, silent on stocking feet, pulling the cart into the alcove with her, pressing herself into the dark between shelved linens that smelled of starch and dust and other people's sleep. Her hands were shaking. She made fists of them until they stopped.

Through the cupboard gap, still visible, still audible: Cordelia rising. The click of her heels—two steps, three—toward the door.

"It's midnight," Cordelia said. "No one cleans at midnight."

"Marta went home ill. They send the quiet one to finish." Edmund again, unhurried. "I make it my business to know the staff. All of them. Even the ones who think they're furniture."

"Then we'll speak to her."

"No." And here Edmund's voice changed, took on a tone Freya had never heard him use with guests—with anyone—and would spend twelve years trying to forget. Not anger. *Arrangement.* The voice of a man moving a piece on a board. "Leave her to me. You've done enough tonight. Go home, Cordelia. Take the papers with you and burn the drafts. I'll deal with the girl."

Heels retreating. The annex door opening fully now, spilling lamplight into the corridor, and Freya flattened herself behind a stack of folded sheets as Cordelia Vesper passed within six feet of her hiding place. Up close, in the amber light, she was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful—perfect surface, and underneath it, depth, and cold, and the certainty that anything falling through would not come up again. She paused at the corner. For one absurd moment Freya thought: *she knows, she's going to turn, she's going to look directly at the cupboard.*

Cordelia only checked her reflection in the dark window, adjusted an earring, and walked on toward the front stairs, carrying her father's stolen name in a leather folder under her arm.

* * *

What happened next, Freya would replay ten thousand times, and every time it would end the same way, because it ended the same way in life: she waited too long.

She told herself she was waiting for them to be gone—all of them, Edmund included, the whole household settled behind doors. She told herself she'd slip out through the service stair, report nothing, know nothing, be no one. She was good at that. She'd been practicing invisibility since she was nineteen and arrived with one suitcase and a mother's warning: *In places like that, girl, the ones who see too much don't get paid more. They just stop getting paid.*

But the annex light stayed on. And after ten minutes, Edmund Vesper's voice came again—not raised, never raised—speaking to someone on the telephone, and though Freya caught only fragments through the oak, the fragments arranged themselves into a shape she understood immediately, completely, the way animals understand weather.

"...yes, the same arrangement... no, tomorrow is fine, tomorrow is better... the east wing, obviously, it wants doing anyway, it's been on the insurance schedule for two years... no witnesses, that's rather the point..."

A pause. Then, dry as ash:

"She's a chambermaid from Hull with no family and a room over the garage. People like that don't have accidents, Mr.—they have *disappearances*. There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork. Make sure there's none."

Freya sat down on the floor of the linen cupboard because her legs would no longer negotiate with her.

The east wing. Tomorrow. *It wants doing anyway.*

Her mind moved fast and cold, faster than fear, cataloguing the way it always had—she was the one who noticed things, that was her whole self, the girl who knew which guests lied about their luggage and which maids stole and which of the Vespers' smiles were load-bearing. And what she noticed now was this: the fire was scheduled. Insured. Arranged. And she was the loose thread Edmund Vesper intended to pull out of the seam.

She could run. Tonight. Out the service door, past the garage, into the city with one suitcase and four years' wages half-paid and nowhere to go. But running was a confession, and Edmund would find her anyway—men like him had solicitors in Leeds and arrangements with men whose names were cut off mid-syllable. Or—

Or she could stay. Be seen in the morning, cheerful, ordinary, dusting rooms in the west wing as if the east wing were nothing to her. Let the fire come and go. Let it burn empty rooms.

Except it wouldn't be empty, would it. Because she'd be in it, or near it, or *found* in it afterward, and the paperwork would say what Edmund needed it to say. A chambermaid asleep where she shouldn't have been. A tragedy. Flowers in the lobby. Mrs. Ansel crying. A grave in Hull with her mother weeping over it.

Unless the paperwork said something else.

The thought arrived whole, fully formed, terrifying, and hers: *Let them burn the wing. Let them think they burned me. And let me take the proof with me.*

Because she had proof. She hadn't known she was collecting it until this moment, but she had been—for weeks, months, the way she collected everything, unconsciously, compulsively. The odd memo she'd vacuumed around on Edmund's desk about Harrowgate Holdings. The courier envelope from Leeds she'd signed for and logged, date and sender, in the concierge's duplicate book because Mrs. Ansel made everyone log everything. The draft letter she'd found crumpled in Cordelia's wastebasket last month—crumpled but legible, in a hand almost-but-not-quite Edmund Vesper's—and had smoothed out and read and, God help her, *kept*, folded into the lining of her workbag, because some instinct older than caution had whispered that a girl alone in a grand hotel ought to carry evidence the way other girls carried pepper spray.

Tomorrow the wing would burn. Tonight, while the house slept, she could go to the annex desk, photograph nothing—she had no camera—but *take*: the transfer schedules, the duplicate book page, the crumpled draft already in her bag. Put it all somewhere fire couldn't follow. Somewhere Edmund Vesper would never look, because to him she was furniture, and furniture doesn't hide things.

And then, when the smoke cleared and the world believed Freya Lindqvist dead—*then* she would decide what a ghost could do with the truth.

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the only plan. She was proud of how calm she felt holding it.

She was still proud, still calm, still moving quietly along the corridor toward the annex with the cupboard key she'd lifted from housekeeping in her fist—when she smelled the smoke.

* * *

It was early. That was her first coherent thought, absurdly practical: *it's early, the fire isn't until tomorrow, this isn't it.* But smoke doesn't keep appointments, and neither, apparently, do men named Mr.-something, and the smell was coming from below—from the east wing's ground floor, from the old ballroom storage where the paint cans and solvents lived, where a fire would climb the service shafts like ivy and reach the upper floors in minutes.

The alarm didn't ring. Of course it didn't ring. She understood that with a clarity that would haunt her longer than the burns: someone had isolated the east wing's alarm zone. The fire was meant to eat quietly, discovered late, blamed on wiring, and any body found in it would be found well-done and unarguable.

Freya ran.

Not toward the stairs—toward her room over the garage, no time, no time—she ran for the service door at the corridor's end, the one Tomas unlocked at dawn and locked at midnight, and it was locked, and she hit it with her shoulder and screamed once, briefly, into the wood, and then pressed her sleeve to her mouth and turned back into the smoke because there was one other way: the laundry chute housing, the narrow maintenance ladder behind it, down three floors to the basement and out through the coal door.

The smoke found her on the second flight. It came down the shaft ahead of her, warm and greasy, tasting of solvent and burnt varnish, and the world shrank to a brown lit corridor of screaming air. She remembers—in the way she remembers everything, cursed to remember everything—the wallpaper curling off the walls as she passed, peeling like the maps of vanished countries, and thinking, with a lucidity that felt like madness: *the hotel is undressing itself.*

The heat took her hands at the basement door. The iron handle had been hot for some time, and she gripped it anyway because the alternative was staying, and her palms seared and stuck and tore, and she would carry those scars across both hands for the rest of her life, would learn to sign her name left-handed, to pay cash with her left, to keep her right hand folded in public like a lady at prayer. At the time she felt almost nothing—adrenaline is a merciful accountant—and she was through, and down, and out through the coal door into the alley where the night was shockingly cold and clean, and she lay on the wet cobbles behind the bins and watched the east wing windows fill with orange light, one after another, like the hotel lighting candles for her.

From the alley she could see the front of the building. Saw the staff spill out onto the steps, counted heads out of habit—Mrs. Ansel in her coat over her nightdress, the barman, the porter, young kitchen boys—and heard Mrs. Ansel say her name, once, twice, and then say it differently, the way you say a name when it has become a list item: *Lindqvist, Freya, chambermaid, east wing, unaccounted for.*

She watched Tomas come out last. Watched him look up at the burning windows, and then—this was the detail, this was the detail she would need someday, the one she banked like the crumpled letter—watched him look quickly, guiltily, at the service door, and then at the alley, and then away, smoothing his face into grief before anyone could read it. Tomas had seen something. Tomas would say he hadn't. She filed him away with the rest.

By dawn the east wing was a black ribcage. By noon there were inspectors and a tent and men in suits, and Edmund Vesper stood on the steps in his good coat looking appropriately devastated, and Cordelia touched his arm, and Alistair stared at the ruin with an expression Freya, watching from a rooftop two streets away with blistered hands and a workbag full of paper, could not yet read—and would not understand for twelve years.

They found no body. She'd made sure of that, insofar as she could: her room over the garage held her coat, her shoes, her mother's photograph, everything with her name on it, fed generously to the flames' path. Let them dig. Let them wonder. A missing girl in a fire is a tragedy; a missing girl with no body is a question, and questions have a longer shelf life than grief.

Hull got its grave anyway—empty, but grassed over, with a stone that said her name and dates, and her mother's tears watered it for years, and every one of those tears was a debt Freya wrote into a ledger she kept in her head, in the same neat columns Mrs. Ansel had taught her. Edmund Vesper owed her a mother's grief. Cordelia owed her a life. The hotel owed her a name.

She traveled north first, then coastwise, and finally to Harrowgate, because of all the places connected to the Vespers, the one they'd sold and forgotten—the little cottage on the gray headland—was the one place no one would think to look for a dead maid. She paid cash. She learned to use her left hand. She waited.

Twelve years is a long time to wait. Long enough for the letters to yellow and the duplicate-book page to fade at the folds. Long enough to learn that Edmund Vesper had known all along she was alive—had found the cottage, had sent the tall man in the gray coat once a month, not mercy exactly, but maintenance: a ghost kept comfortable is a ghost kept quiet. Long enough to understand that the truth was a debt the whole family was paying interest on, in cash, in silence, in lies.

And long enough to learn, last spring, that Edmund Vesper himself had finally decided the interest payments were over. *She stays dead or we all do,* he'd written, and Freya, reading those words in circumstances she would not explain to anyone for a long time, understood that the ledger was about to be called in.

So when Edmund Vesper died—died suddenly, conveniently, of a heart attack no coroner thought to question—Freya Lindqvist did the one thing a ghost can do.

She wrote it all down. Every name, every date, every payment, every lie. She addressed it to the Hotel Vesper, because the truth belonged in the building where it was buried. And she carried it to the post office in Harrowgate and asked the clerk, politely, with her scarred right hand tucked out of sight, to postmark it for the day after the funeral—

—because she knew, better than anyone alive, that the living only listen to the dead once the mourning is over.

She dropped the letter into the slot and heard it land, soft as ash, at the bottom of the box.

Then she went home to wait, and to count, and to see who would come looking for a girl who had been dead for twelve years—and whether, when they found her, they would come as witnesses, or as gravediggers finishing the job.
