## 27. Room 412

The page was in the hotel.

Freya had said it the way she said everything—flatly, without ceremony, as though she were reporting a stain on a carpet. *It's where no one would look. It's where I looked every day for two years and no one ever asked me why.* And then she had given Mirabel the only clue she would give: a room number.

Four hundred and twelve.

Mirabel stood at the end of the fourth-floor corridor at half past one in the morning, key ring silent in their pocket because they'd learned decades ago how to carry keys without letting them speak. The corridor stretched away under its dim sconces, the wallpaper's faded trellis pattern climbing toward the ceiling like ivy that had given up halfway. Room 412 sat at the far corner, facing the courtyard, and its occupant was Mr. Aldous Wren, retired actuary, resident of eleven years, currently asleep—or so the house phone's silence suggested—and, more importantly, currently scheduled to leave on Thursday for his daughter's house in Brighton, curtains to be laundered by Mrs. Ansel's rotation on Friday.

Which meant tonight was the only night.

Mirabel had spent three days confirming what Freya would not explain outright. The east wing fire of 2011 had gutted rooms 401 through 409; 412 had survived, scorched only along its window frame, and had been rehung with fresh curtains from the same bolt of fabric used throughout the floor. Freya had worked that floor. Freya had hung those curtains herself—Mrs. Ansel remembered it, remembered the girl insisting on doing the top floors alone because she liked the quiet up there. A girl who knew she was going to have to disappear, sewing a torn page of a fire report into a hem where it would hang in plain sight for twelve years, washed and pressed and sun-bleached, waiting for someone to ask the right question.

*No one would look,* Freya had said. *Because no one looks at curtains. They look through them.*

Mirabel let themselves into 412 with the master key and closed the door without a sound.

The room smelled of Mr. Wren: pipe tobacco long banned and smoked anyway on the fire escape, wool, eucalyptus balm. The bed was an island of neatness. On the nightstand, a glass of water, a worn paperback with a receipt for a bookmark, a hearing aid in its charging cradle like a small sleeping animal. Mirabel catalogued all of it out of habit—the concierge's curse, noticing everything, filing it nowhere—and then turned to the windows.

The curtains were heavy things, velvet the color of dried blood when the light hit them right, olive in shadow. Twelve years of laundering should have destroyed any stitching that wasn't meant to be found, but Freya had been clever even then; the seam was done in thread matched to the lining, invisible unless you knew to feel for it. Mirabel ran their fingers along the bottom hem of the left panel, inch by inch, feeling the weave, feeling the weight of the fabric settle differently—

There. A ridge. A rectangle the size of a postcard, sewn flat against the hem, wrapped in something plastic that crackled faintly under the fingertips like frost.

Mirabel took out the penknife they'd carried since their first year at the Vesper, back when an older concierge named Halloran had told them, *A blade is a tool before it is anything else, and if you remember that, you'll never have to learn it the harder way.* They picked the seam open stitch by stitch, working by feel more than sight, and drew out a small package: a freezer bag, sealed with tape gone amber with age, and inside it a single sheet of paper, folded twice, soft as cloth from its years of hanging in the dark behind the velvet.

The missing page of the fire report.

Mirabel did not unfold it immediately. They stood in the dark of Room 412 holding twelve years of buried truth in both hands, and for a moment the room seemed to tilt—not literally, nothing so dramatic, but the way a corridor tilts when you realize you've been walking down it your whole life in the wrong direction. Edmund Vesper's signature was on this paper. The word *accelerant* was on this paper, in an inspector's careful hand, followed by whatever conclusion someone had paid to make vanish. This page was the difference between a tragedy and a crime. Between Freya Lindqvist, deceased, and Freya Lindqvist, attempted murder victim.

Between everyone in this hotel who had stayed comfortable, and everyone who had burned.

Mirabel unfolded it under the weak glow of the desk lamp, angling the shade away from the window, and read.

The inspector's name was R. Calloway. The notes were terse, professional, increasingly uneasy: *residue consistent with accelerant at base of stairwell door — origin point inconsistent with electrical fault cited in preliminary — request follow-up sampling.* And beneath that, stamped diagonally in blue ink that had faded to the color of a bruise: *CASE CLOSED BY ORDER OF E. VESPER, OWNER. NO FURTHER INVESTIGATION AUTHORIZED.*

Edmund had signed the closure himself. Not Cordelia. Not an anonymous string-puller. Edmund—who had, according to Alistair's broken confession, helped Freya vanish after the fire—had first signed the report that declared her death an accident worth no further inquiry. Which meant Edmund had known, before the fire or during it, exactly what needed burying. Which meant his later protection of Freya was not mercy. It was containment. He hadn't saved her from the fire. He'd saved her from being found, because a living witness was dangerous only until she was managed, and Edmund Vesper had managed people his whole life the way he managed the hotel: firmly, quietly, and always to his own advantage.

Mirabel photographed the page—all four quadrants, flash off, exposure held long—and folded it back along its old creases. Then they stopped.

Because there was a second thing in the freezer bag. Tucked behind the report, thin as a moth wing: a photograph. Small, glossy, curling at the corners. A young woman in a chambermaid's uniform stood at the end of this very corridor, fourth floor, laughing at something outside the frame, her sleeves rolled, her hair pinned carelessly. She looked seventeen, maybe eighteen. Alive in a way that made Mirabel's chest hurt, because Mirabel had met her three days ago in a safehouse above a laundromat and the woman there had been carved down to essentials—scarred hands, watchful eyes, a voice like a door closing. This girl in the photograph didn't know yet. This girl still laughed in corridors.

On the back, in pencil, in a young hand: *F.L., June 2011. Last good summer.*

Mirabel put the photograph back with the page, sealed the bag, and was reaching for their jacket's inner pocket—the one where the letter's photographs lived, the one that had become, over these strange weeks, a kind of portable conscience—when the door handle turned.

Not knocked. Turned.

Mirabel killed the lamp and stepped back against the wall between the wardrobe and the window in one motion, heart going hard and fast, the penknife open again in their hand. The lock had been set. Mirabel had set it. But this was the Hotel Vesper, and half the locks in the building could be opened with a credit card and patience, and Julian Vesper had grown up in this building learning exactly which ones.

The door eased open. A shape slipped through—tall, expensively rumpled, smelling of the bar downstairs even at this hour—and pulled the door shut behind him before he switched on his phone's torch.

The light swept the room and landed on Mirabel like a spotlight.

"Christ," Julian whispered, and nearly dropped the phone. "Christ, Mirabel, you scared the—I thought—" He pressed a hand to his chest, laughed shakily, without humor. "I thought you'd be the other one."

"The other one," Mirabel said quietly. Not a question. An invitation.

Julian's laugh died. He looked terrible in the torchlight—handsome the way a photograph of a handsome man is handsome, all the life drained out of it. His collar was open, his eyes were rimmed red, and his hands wouldn't stay still. "Vane," he said. "Mr. Vane. He came to my room an hour ago. Sat down uninvited, poured himself a drink from my bottle, and told me—" Julian swallowed. "He told me he knows about my debts. All of them. He had figures, Mirabel. Figures I don't even have."

"And?"

"And he offered to clear them." Julian's voice cracked on the word *clear*. "Every pound. Gone by Monday. In exchange for one small thing." He was shaking now, visibly, the charm that was his native language failing him entirely. "He wants to know who I've been talking to. Whether I've seen the ledgers. Whether anyone's asked me about the fire." He paused, and his eyes, when they came up, were the worst thing in the room—worse than fear, worse than greed. Shame. "And whether the concierge has been asking questions about Room 412."

The corridor's silence pressed against the door. Somewhere below, the boiler ticked through its nightly cycle, the sound the hotel made instead of breathing.

"He knows you're here," Mirabel said. "Or he guessed."

"He knows. He told me the room number before I told him. That's how I—" Julian gestured helplessly at the space between them. "That's how I knew to come. I've been standing in the hall for twenty minutes trying to decide whether to warn you or sell you." He laughed again, horribly. "That's the honest version. You deserve the honest version. Everyone in this family lies so beautifully that honesty feels like a foreign country."

Mirabel slid the penknife closed and put it away, deliberately, letting him see it happen. "You're here to warn me."

"I'm here to make a deal." The words came out of him in a rush, as if they'd been building pressure for days. "I'll testify. About that night. About what I saw leaving your father's study—" He caught himself, corrected. *Leaving Edmund's study.* The slip was interesting; Mirabel filed it. "I saw someone, Mirabel. The night Edmund died. I never told anyone because I told myself it was nothing, it was shadows, it was the brandy—but it wasn't nothing and we both know whose side the shadows were on. I'll go to the police. I'll sign whatever you want. I'll burn Cordelia's whole elegant world down if that's what it takes." He stepped closer, and now his voice dropped to the register he probably used with the men he owed money to, the register of a man laying his last card face-up. "In exchange, you clear my debts. Or get Alistair to. Or tell me how to disappear the way she disappeared, because God knows this family has the method down to an art."

There it was. The price. There was always a price; Mirabel had learned that at this very front desk, watching guests negotiate with themselves in the lobby mirror before working up the courage to ask for the room where they'd conduct their affairs. Everyone had a number. Tomas's had been a pension and a blind eye. Alistair's had been twelve years of guilt amortized into monthly payments. Even Freya's price had been a name erased and a life shrunk to the size of a hiding place.

Everyone had a price, and Julian was asking Mirabel to pay his.

"No," Mirabel said.

Julian blinked. "No?"

"No." Mirabel moved away from the wall, folded the freezer bag into their inner pocket, and buttoned the jacket over it with unhurried fingers. "I'm not clearing your debts, Julian. I'm not buying your testimony, and I'm not selling mine. If you testify, you testify because it's true and because it's yours to tell. That's the only testimony worth anything, and the only kind that will hold when Vane's lawyers start pulling at it. The moment your statement has a price tag, it's worthless—to the police, to the probate judge, to Freya." They let that name land, watched him flinch. "Yes. She's alive. Yes, I've met her. No, I won't tell you where, because an hour ago you were standing in that hallway deciding whether to sell me, and people who deliberate about things like that don't get addresses."

"That's—" Julian's jaw worked. "That's not fair. I came to you."

"You came to me because Vane frightened you and you wanted to be bought by the kinder party. I understand it. I might even forgive it." Mirabel crossed to the door and listened at it—nothing, only the boiler's tick and the building settling around its old bones. "But I watched this hotel run for thirty years on purchased silences, and I can tell you exactly what they buy. They buy twelve years. They buy a girl sewn into a curtain. They buy a funeral where nobody cries honestly." They turned and looked at him, and for once did not manage their face at all. "I'm done paying. If you want to be free of your debts, testify and take the ruin that comes with telling the truth—it's cheaper than the alternative, I promise you. Vane doesn't clear debts. He transfers them. Ask yourself who holds the paper on you, Julian. Ask yourself who introduced you to the men you owe."

Julian went pale in a new way. "Cordelia introduced me to—"

"Don't say it in this room." Mirabel opened the door a hand's width, checked the corridor, empty, the sconces burning their patient orange. "Go back to your room. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow morning, if you still want to testify, come to the front desk at nine and ask me—in front of witnesses—for the address of a good solicitor. That's your opening. That's the only deal on the table."

"And if Vane comes back to me?"

"Then you'll know what Freya has known for twelve years." Mirabel held the door for him, and their voice was almost gentle. "What it costs to be a witness. Go."

Julian went—paused at the threshold as if to say something, some last appeal or apology, then thought better of it, and his footsteps receded down the corridor, uneven, a man walking away from the last exit he'd believed in.

Mirabel closed the door, leaned their forehead briefly against the cool wood, and allowed themselves ten seconds of stillness. The photograph of the laughing girl pressed against their ribs. The page of the fire report lay over it like a bandage over a wound.

Then they heard it, faint through the door: the lift, at the far end of the corridor, arriving without being called.

And voices. Two of them. One low and smooth, unmistakable even muffled—Vane, and he was not whispering. The other voice answered, and Mirabel's blood went cold, because it was not Julian's, and it was not a man's at all.

It was a woman's voice, cultured and calm, giving instructions in the tone of someone rearranging flowers.

Mirabel pressed their ear to the wood and caught only fragments through the old door—*Friday*, certainly. *Before the reading*. And once, clearly, terribly, a short laugh, and the words:

*"Then sew her into something."*
