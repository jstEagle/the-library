## 32. Fire Again

The east wing still smelled of old smoke.

Twelve years, and the smell had never left—baked into the plaster, into the joists, into the bones of the building itself. Freya stood in the doorway of what had once been Room 409 and breathed it in like a language she'd been fluent in once and never quite forgotten. Char and wet ash and something sweeter underneath, the ghost of burned curtains, burned ledgers, burned paper that had been a girl's whole life.

She had died here. That was the official version, anyway. Freya Lindqvist, chambermaid, perished in the electrical fire of March 2011, no body recovered, services private. She had read her own obituary in a laundromat in Harrowgate with hands too scarred to hold the newspaper steady, and she had laughed until she cried, because what else was there to do?

Now she was back. And this time, she was not the one who would burn.

* * *

They had taken her at dusk, on the walk from the safehouse to the corner shop. She had known they were watching for two days—the gray sedan that never parked twice in the same spot, the man who bought cigarettes he didn't smoke outside the laundromat. She could have run. She had gotten very good at running. But running was how you stayed dead, and Freya Lindqvist had decided, somewhere between the grave visit and the lavender, that she was finished being dead.

So when Vane stepped out of the sedan's shadow with his soft voice and his patient hands, she let him take her arm as if she were a lady being escorted, and she said, "You'll want the east wing. It's where these things are done."

He had blinked. Just once. Men like Vane did their work by believing it was work—clean, procedural, nothing personal—and she had just made it personal, and he hadn't liked it.

"Miss Lindqvist," he'd said.

"Freya," she corrected. "You've been paid to erase me for twelve years. The least you can do is use my name."

Now she sat on an upturned crate in the gutted ballroom of the east wing, wrists bound loosely in front of her—he'd used zip ties, but he'd done it while she was looking at her shoes, which told her he was careful but not cruel, or careful until instructed otherwise—and watched him prepare.

That was the word for it. *Prepare.* He moved through the ruined room with the unhurried competence of a man setting a table. A jerry can, red plastic, dented at one corner. A coil of extension cord trailing from a portable generator he'd carried in himself, its little engine silent for now. A bottle of accelerant that he decanted with the tenderness of a sommelier. He laid everything out on a sheet of plastic tarp, each item squared to the next, and Freya understood that she was watching a ritual older than any of them: fire, cover story, paperwork. The same liturgy Edmund Vesper had performed in this room twelve years ago, when he'd poured the accelerant himself because some things a man would not delegate.

"You're left-handed," Vane observed, not looking up. "You keep favoring your right in front of me. Why?"

"Because the right hand is what people look at," Freya said. "Scars make people curious. Curiosity makes questions. Questions get women like me dug up." She flexed the ruined fingers of her right hand, the skin pulled smooth and shiny over knuckles that no longer fully closed. "You'd know all about questions, Mr. Vane. You ask them for a living."

"I ask them so I don't have to answer them."

"And tonight? Who's paying for tonight?"

Vane folded the empty jerry can flat with one efficient press of his boot. He was a tall man gone slightly stooped, gray at the temples, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten by everyone who saw it. He had probably been handsome once, in the way that furniture is handsome.

"You know I won't tell you that."

"I know exactly who," Freya said. "I've always known. I just like hearing the shape of the silence when you don't say it."

Something flickered across his face—not guilt; she doubted he kept any in stock—but recognition. She had seen that look before, on Tomas the night porter, on Mrs. Ansel, on every servant of the Hotel Vesper who had ever looked at her and chosen not to see. The look of a person standing next to a truth and stepping around it.

"The fire in 2011," Vane said, crouching to test the generator's fuel line. "Electrical, according to the report."

"The report lost a page."

"So I'm told." He glanced up at her then, and for a moment the professional blankness thinned, and underneath it was something almost like respect. "You've been ready for this night for a long time, haven't you."

Freya looked around the ballroom. Moonlight came through the boarded windows in pale slats, striping the debris floor. The chandelier lay in the corner where it had fallen, a carcass of crystal and blackened brass. Somewhere under the rubble was the scorch mark where a filing cabinet full of forged signatures had become a column of flame, and where a nineteen-year-old girl had run through smoke so thick it tasted like swallowing coins.

"Yes," she said simply. "Since the night I ran out that door."

* * *

She had planned it in the bath, of all places, three nights ago in the safehouse above the laundromat, Mirabel Coldharbour's phone number written in grease pencil on the inside of her forearm because paper could be taken from you but skin took effort.

*If they come for me,* she had told Mirabel, *they'll take me to the east wing. It's symmetry. People like Cordelia think in symmetry—they think it's poetry. Let them think it.*

*And if I'm wrong about that,* Mirabel had said, *if they just drive you out to the marshes—*

*Then I'm dead and you'll know I was wrong, and you can feel bad about it at my second funeral. I've had one already. They're overrated.*

Mirabel had not laughed. Mirabel had that quality Freya both resented and relied upon: the concierge took everything seriously, especially jokes, because jokes were where people hid the things they meant. *Tell me the plan again,* Mirabel had said. *All of it. Slowly.*

So Freya told it slowly. The sprinkler riser in the east wing had never been decommissioned after the fire—it was one of the hotel's thousand small negligences, a system patched and permitted and forgotten, still charged, still fed by the mains, because inspecting it would have meant admitting the wing should have been demolished years ago. Freya knew this because she had spent twelve years learning buildings the way other people learned languages, and because she had walked the east wing's service corridors a hundred times in her dreams and twice in person last month, wearing a cleaner's tabard and carrying a mop nobody ever asked her to use.

The riser valve was behind a panel in the corridor outside the old linen room. The fire alarm pull-station—the original one, yellowed plastic, glass intact beneath its dust—was forty feet further down, by the service stair. The panel covering the valve was held by two screws. She had loosened them herself, weeks ago, and left them sitting in their holes like teeth waiting to be pulled.

And the generator Vane had brought, the one humming now in the corner, feeding power to whatever he intended to ignite first—that generator drew from the east wing's only live circuit. Which meant the sprinkler system's pump, on the same legacy feed, would have power even if Cordelia's people thought they'd cut the building off.

Vane had checked the water main at the street, Freya was nearly certain. He was thorough. But he had checked the *street* main, and the street main was fine, and the irony of it was going to be exquisite: the hotel's decay, its refusal to modernize, its stubborn rotting infrastructure—all the things that had made the Vesper a mausoleum—were going to save her life.

*You're smiling,* Vane said.

Freya realized she was. "Am I?"

"People smile before the end sometimes. Relief, usually. Or madness." He straightened, wiped his hands on a cloth, and looked at her with genuine, almost gentle curiosity. "Which is yours?"

"Neither." She tested the zip tie against her wrist bone—loose, as she'd maneuvered it during the ride over, loose enough. "Mine's arithmetic. Do you want to hear it?"

"I imagine you'll tell me regardless."

"There are four hundred and eleven rooms in the Hotel Vesper," Freya said. "Room 412 doesn't exist anymore—it was sealed after the fire, part of the east wing closure. But the numbering was never changed. So when someone says *room 412*, staff know what it means. It means the room that isn't there. The room that burned." She watched his face carefully. "Your employer thinks she's having me killed in a ruin. But I'm not in a ruin, Mr. Vane. I'm in a hotel. And hotels have staff."

For the first time, Vane went still in a way that wasn't professional.

"What did you do?"

* * *

What she had done was this: she had called Mirabel from the corner shop's phone, thirty seconds before letting Vane take her arm, and said six words. *East wing. Midnight. Bring everyone. Call police.* Then she had hung up, because more words were risk, and six was enough. Six was enough because Mirabel Coldharbour had spent twenty years reading guests, and a concierge who could parse a guest's meaning from the way they asked for extra towels could certainly parse *bring everyone.*

Bring everyone meant Tomas, who still had keys to doors that officially had no keys. Meant Mrs. Ansel, whose master override opened any panel in the building built before 2015. Meant the barman, Deniz, who had arms like dock rope and had once, according to legend, removed a drunk Russian oligarch from the premises through the front doors without touching the doors. Meant the night kitchen boys, the day porter, the two housekeepers who had worked the east wing before the fire and still flinched at its name.

It meant the invisible people. The ones who noticed everything and said nothing, until the night they didn't.

* * *

"Now," Vane said, checking his watch. "I'm sorry about this. Genuinely. You seem like a woman who's suffered enough."

"That's very kind," Freya said. "Do you say that to all of them?"

"I've never had a repeat customer."

He picked up the accelerant. And this was the moment, Freya knew—the moment she had rehearsed in baths and bus stations and the blue hour before dawn for twelve years. He would pour. He would step back. He would reach for the cord connected to the generator, or a lighter, or however he preferred his beginnings, and in that window between preparation and ignition there would be perhaps ninety seconds, maybe less, and every plan she had ever made lived inside that window like a seed inside winter.

He uncapped the bottle.

Freya stood up. The zip tie snapped against her wrist—she'd been working the frayed edge against the crate's rough nail head since they arrived, and it parted with a sound like a small sigh. Vane turned, startled, hand going to his coat—

—and Freya was already moving, not toward the door, not away from him, but *past* him, bare feet silent on the debris, toward the corridor and the loosened screws and the panel she had dreamed about for twelve years.

"Stop," Vane said, and there was something new in his voice now, something that had finally stopped being procedural.

She wrenched the panel open. The valve was there, cold iron, exactly where her memory had kept it polished and gleaming all these years. She seized the wheel with both hands—the scarred right and the strong left together, both of them, for the first time in public, for the first time in front of anyone—and turned.

The wheel resisted. Twelve years of disuse, mineral scale, rust. Her scarred skin screamed. She screamed with it, out loud, and kept turning, and felt something in her right hand shift that might have been scar tissue tearing and did not care, because the wheel gave—a quarter turn, a half—and then the pipes above the ceiling woke up.

It began as a shudder in the walls. Then a knocking, arrhythmic, like a fist. Then, above the ballroom, with a sound like the building clearing its throat, the sprinkler heads opened.

Water hit the plastic tarp first, sluicing the accelerant off in reeking sheets. Water hit the generator, which coughed, sparked, and died. Water hit Vane, drenching him in two seconds flat, plastering his forgettable hair to his forgettable forehead, and he stood there blinking like a man ambushed by weather.

Freya was already running for the alarm station.

Her hand closed around the pull lever—the old one, the original, the one she had run past in the opposite direction twelve years ago while the world behind her turned orange—and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy: *This time I pull it toward me.*

Glass cracked. The lever dropped.

The fire alarm of the Hotel Vesper's east wing sounded for the first time since March 2011, and it sounded like vengeance played on a cheap electric bell—harsh, ugly, relentless, filling every corridor and stairwell and sealed room with a noise that said *now, now, now.*

* * *

Chaos is a precise thing when you've built it yourself.

Vane recovered faster than she expected—she'd give him that. He came through the doorway with water streaming off him and something glinting in his fist, and Freya dodged left into the linen room, into the dark, into territory she knew better than he ever would. His blade caught her shoulder as she passed—not deep, a hot wire of pain, her blood warm under the sprinkler rain—and then she was through the old linen shelves, tipping the last one down behind her, and the room became a maze and the maze was hers.

"Miss Lindqvist!" Vane's voice, controlled again, which frightened her more than shouting. "This changes nothing. There are exits I know and you don't."

"There's a door you don't know," she called back, pressing her palm against her shoulder, backing deeper into the dark. "There's always a door we know and you don't. That's the whole history of this hotel."

Somewhere below, boots on the service stairs. Many boots. Voices—Tomas's gravel baritone, unmistakable, shouting something about the north door; Mrs. Ansel's sharper register cutting through it; Deniz's laugh, wild and delighted, the laugh of a man who had waited years for permission. Flashlight beams swung crazily through the sprinkler haze. And beneath it all, faint but growing, a sound Freya had never expected to hear aimed in her favor: sirens. Real ones. Mirabel had actually called the police. The concierge who had spent a career noticing everything and saying nothing had picked up a telephone and *said something.*

Vane hesitated in the linen room doorway, blade low, water dripping from his chin, calculating. She watched him do it—watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes, exits versus voices versus the growing thunder of the stairwell—and she understood with perfect sympathy that he was deciding whether she was worth dying for.

"She'll disown you," Freya said quietly. "When this goes wrong. Whoever she is. Women like her always do. I was proof of concept, Mr. Vane. So were you."

His jaw tightened. In the strobing flashlight glow, with the sprinklers falling like silver rain and the alarm shrieking its twelve-years-late warning, he looked suddenly, terribly tired—a man who had erased people for a living discovering what it cost to be unable to erase a single night.

Then the lights came on.

Every light in the east wing, banked breakers thrown by hands that had kept this building's secrets for decades, and the linen room blazed white, and Vane was exposed in it like a specimen, and behind him, filling the doorway, was Deniz the barman with two kitchen boys and a length of pipe, and beyond them Tomas, ancient and furious, holding a fire axe he must have taken off the wall by the old stairwell, and beyond *them*, pushing through, soaked and breathing hard and searching the chaos with those concierge's eyes until they found hers—

Mirabel.

"You're bleeding," Mirabel said. It was not a question and not an accusation. It was inventory. It was the first line of a report that would be exact.

"He grazed me," Freya said. Her voice shook. She let it. "I'm fine. I'm—" She laughed, and it came out cracked and wet and real. "I'm alive. Do you understand? Twelve years, and I'm standing in the fire, and I'm *alive.*"

The police arrived then, four of them, guns drawn, shouting commands that bounced off wet walls, and Vane—surrounded, drenched, outmatched, and, Freya suspected, relieved in some chamber of himself he'd never admit to—set the knife down on a fallen shelf and raised his hands with the weary courtesy of a man surrendering to a schedule change rather than a defeat.

As they cuffed him, he turned his head and found Freya through the crowd. Not hatred. Something worse: acknowledgment. *You were ready,* his expression said. *Nobody's ever been ready.*

"Mr. Vane," Freya said clearly, so the officers would hear, so the record would hold. "You'll want to call your employer. Tell her the east wing is on fire again. Tell her the ghost did it." She smiled with all her scarred, terrible history showing. "And tell her I kept the page."

Vane went still. Through the din of the alarm and the orders and the water still raining from the dead system's last breaths, his eyes moved—from Freya, to Mirabel, to the sealed door at the end of the corridor marked with a faded stenciled number, **412**—and Freya watched him understand, all at once, that the thing his employer feared most had not been hidden in a vault or buried in a marsh.

It had been hanging in the hotel all along, in plain sight, gathering dust in the curtains.

And somewhere upstairs, in a suite that smelled of lilies and control, Cordelia Vesper was about to receive a phone call that would end her.
