## 4. The Heir Apparent

Beatrix Crane knew the Meridian's rhythms the way a priest knows the liturgy. Tuesday brought the dry cleaning of the Vanterpool account and the florist's delivery for the lobby vases—white lilies, never roses, because roses shed. Wednesday brought the linen truck at seven-forty and the accountant's courier at noon. Thursday was quiet, which is why the black car idling outside the awning on a Thursday morning registered in his mind before it had fully stopped.

He watched from behind the desk as the driver opened the rear door, and a man unfolded himself onto the pavement with the unhurried grace of someone who had never once worried about weather.

Julian Ashcombe.

Beatrix had not seen him in eleven years—not since the funeral, where Julian had worn grief like an ill-fitting coat, checking his watch during the eulogy. He was broader now, silver at the temples in a way that looked purchased rather than earned, and he moved through the revolving door into the lobby as though the hotel were a room in his own house. Which, technically, it was.

"Mr. Ashcombe," Beatrix said, before the man could announce himself. "We weren't expecting you."

The smile that followed was warm, easy, and entirely without content. "Nobody ever is, Mr.—Crane, isn't it? You've been here forever. My grandmother thought the world of you." He set a leather bag on the marble counter with a soft, expensive thump. "I'd like a room. Fourth floor, if you have it."

Something turned over quietly in Beatrix's chest. He kept his hands moving—register, key drawer, the small choreography of welcome. "The fourth floor is largely closed. We keep two rooms serviced for occasional use."

"Then I'm occasional." Julian leaned in slightly, lowering his voice into a register of conspiracy. "Sentimental reasons. My grandmother's floor. I want to feel close to her."

*Her floor*, Beatrix thought, *where she died, and where something else happened thirty years ago that no one will name.* He slid a brass key across the counter. Room 407. Not 412. The distance between those numbers was eleven doors and one sealed staircase, and Beatrix intended to keep every inch of it.

"Wonderful," he said. "Breakfast is served until ten."

* * *

Julian took the stairs up, declining the lift, counting doors.

He had done his homework the way he did everything: thoroughly, remotely, and without anyone noticing until it was too late. Three months of letters to himself, unsigned, arriving like weather. A confession written by a dead woman's hand—*my hand*, he corrected himself, *my property, my inheritance twice over*—and a second letter in his own careful script promising more to follow. Both mailed to the concierge, because the concierge was the only person in the building who noticed things, and because fear worked best when it arrived at the exact address of a man's pride.

The old man had looked. Of course he had. Julian had counted on it. The letter under his door this morning—*You looked. Now you owe me silence*—had been delivered by a courier at six a.m., timed so Crane would find it after a sleepless night of wondering.

Now Julian walked the fourth-floor corridor, trailing his fingers along the wallpaper. Faded stripes, water stains blooming near the ceiling like brown flowers. It smelled of dust and old carpet and, beneath it, something faintly sweet—the ghost of some perfume that had soaked into the plaster decades ago and refused to leave.

412 stood at the corridor's end. The door was painted shut over the original paint, a padlock hasp screwed in above the handle. He tried the handle anyway. Solid.

His father had carried a rolled carpet down the back stairs on a night in November when Julian was nine years old and pretending to be asleep. He had heard the carpet thump against each step—heavy, awkward, dragged—and he had heard his father breathing through his teeth, and in the morning the fourth floor was closed and nobody ever said why, and his mother said *your grandfather fell, darling, these old houses are dangerous,* and Julian, being nine, believed her for exactly as long as it took him to grow up and understand what a rolled carpet weighs.

Freya had kept the confession locked away all these years. He'd found it three months ago in her solicitor's archive, misfiled among correspondence, dated three days before her death and never sent. A dying woman's tidy accounting: the affair, the pregnancy, the fall, the cover-up she had orchestrated to protect the maid who fled. And threaded through it, damning as wire: the true terms of her will.

That will could not be allowed to surface. The forged one—his father's masterpiece, notarized by a solicitor since dead—gave Julian everything. The confession, if it reached a lawyer, would unravel thirty years of ownership in a season.

So: silence. Purchased not with money but with leverage. The concierge would keep quiet or become complicit; either way, useful. And there was one other holder of secrets in this town, an old porter drinking his pension in a pub by the rail yard, who had been squeezed gently for months and would squeeze again if needed.

Julian reached the end of the corridor and stood before 412's sealed door. He pressed his palm flat against the wood, feeling for warmth, for anything.

"Grandmother," he murmured. "You should have burned it."

Behind him, a floorboard creaked.

* * *

Beatrix had followed him up. Not obviously—he had business on the fourth floor, or could invent it within four seconds—but the truth was simpler: he wanted to see what Julian Ashcombe did when he thought no one was watching. People were most honest in the belief of their solitude.

What he saw was a man laying his hand on the door of 412 like a pilgrim at a tomb, and speaking to it.

"—should have burned it."

Beatrix withdrew around the corner, footsteps silent out of twenty years' habit, and descended the service stair with his mind working fast and cold.

Julian knew about the room. Knew enough to grieve it, or to fear it. And he had arrived unannounced, asking for the fourth floor, on the same week two impossible letters had landed on the front desk like stones dropped down a well.

Coincidence was a currency Beatrix rarely accepted. He checked its weight now and found it counterfeit.

At the desk, he pulled the guest ledger toward himself—an old habit, the ledger being both record and refuge—and wrote Julian Ashcombe's entry in his precise hand. Then, beneath it, in the margin where no auditor would look, a smaller notation:

*Arrived day after second letter. Requested 4th flr. Spoke to 412.*

He closed the book. Somewhere upstairs, a shower ran in 407, and Beatrix stood in the quiet lobby listening to the pipes, thinking about Marek, about Ilse Reyes, about a woman named Freya who had loved badly and ruled absolutely and left behind her a hotel full of locked doors.

One of them, he suspected, had just checked in.

* * *

Dinner that evening was Julian's idea, which surprised no one and reassured everyone, which was precisely its purpose.

He sat at the corner table in the dining room—the one with the view of the whole lobby, Beatrix noted, the seat of a man who liked to watch—and ordered for the table even though he dined alone. He tipped the waitress in cash, remembered her name from the introduction, asked after her daughter's university applications. By nine o'clock the kitchen staff were saying *he's not so bad, really, nothing like they say about the family.* By nine-thirty he had learned the names of three housekeepers, the head of maintenance, and a young handyman named Tomas Reyes, whose handshake he held half a second too long while studying his face with an expression he believed was friendly curiosity.

Beatrix watched all of it from the desk, and understood that he was watching a man conduct an audit. Julian wasn't charming the staff. He was inventorying them—who was loyal, who was lonely, who could be bought, who might talk.

When the dining room emptied, Julian strolled to the front desk with two fingers of brandy in a glass he had carried out against policy, and set it down beside the ledger.

"A nightcap, Mr. Crane? No? Suit yourself." He drank, and looked around the lobby with proprietary fondness. "God, it hasn't changed. She wouldn't allow it, would she? Not one lampshade." He smiled. "You knew her better than anyone, I imagine. Thirty years at this desk."

"Twenty-six," Beatrix said. "I knew her professionally."

"Professionally." Julian tasted the word. "My grandmother collected people like you. Quiet ones. She used to say the staff saw everything and said nothing, and that was the entire art of hospitality." He set down the glass. "Tell me, do you remember much about '94? I was a child, but I have the sense the hotel went through something that year. Some sadness. Rooms closed, staff leaving."

"Weather was bad that autumn," Beatrix said. "Business was slow. Several staff moved on. Hotels turn over."

"Mm." Julian studied him for a long moment, and the charm thinned like ice over deep water. "And room 412? When was that taken off the books?"

"The year the fourth floor was consolidated. Structural concerns."

"Structural." He laughed softly, genuinely amused. "You're very good, Mr. Crane. My grandmother said you never gave away more than a room key." He pushed off from the desk and gathered his glass. "Well. I'll be here a week or two. Sorting out the estate. There are papers, you know—old papers, family papers. If anything of hers surfaces in this building, any correspondence, any documents tucked away in odd drawers—I'd consider it a personal kindness if you'd bring them to me directly. Rather than, say, the solicitors. Families should handle their own ghosts, don't you think?"

"I'll bear it in mind," Beatrix said.

"Of course you will." Julian raised the glass in a small salute and crossed the lobby toward the lift. At the door of it he paused, turned, and added pleasantly: "Oh—one more thing. The night porter. Marek, the old fellow. Is he still alive? I have such fond memories of him. I thought I might look him up while I'm in town."

The lift doors closed on Beatrix's face, which had not changed at all, which never changed at all, and which was, at that moment, the only thing in the building keeping him upright.

* * *

After midnight, alone, Beatrix allowed himself the luxury of fear for exactly four minutes. He timed it by the lobby clock, because discipline was the spine of everything he was.

Marek. Julian had said the name the way a man lays a card face-down on a table—showing nothing, telling everything. Either he was going to visit the old porter, or he was reminding Beatrix that he knew exactly where the bodies were buried, including the ones still walking around.

Beatrix took out the two letters and read them again by the green-shaded lamp. The first, Freya's confession, in that slanted, imperious hand he remembered from a hundred memos: *Do not let them make me a saint. What happened in 412 was mine to answer for...* The second, the threat, machine-neat: *More to follow.*

Two different hands. Two different authors. One dead, one living, and the living one now drinking brandy in room 407 and asking after old men by the rail yard.

He thought of Marek's hands, huge and cracked, dealing cards in the porter's room in the old days, always letting the young ones win. He thought of the rolled carpet Marek had never mentioned, and of what a man carries for thirty years when he carries that.

Then Beatrix did something he had not done in twenty-six years at the front desk. He picked up the telephone, consulted a number written in the back of his own address book in fading ink, and dialed the pub by the rail yard.

"Marek," he said, when the voice came, gravelly and wary. "It's Beatrix Crane. From the Meridian." A long silence on the line. "An old friend of the family is in town. I think you should come see me tomorrow. Before he comes to see you."

Another silence, longer, and then, very quietly, the sound of a glass being set down on wood.

"He's there already, isn't he," Marek said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Tomorrow, Marek. Please."

He hung up and sat in the green lamplight, listening to the hotel breathe around him—the boiler's murmur, the tick of the clock, the faint creak of the fourth floor settling under the weight of its history. Tomorrow he would learn what Marek knew. Tonight he wrote one last line in the ledger's margin, beneath Julian's name:

*He knows about Marek. Move first.*

Outside, rain began against the awning, soft as footsteps overhead, on the floor where a sealed door waited at the end of a corridor, patient as the dead.
