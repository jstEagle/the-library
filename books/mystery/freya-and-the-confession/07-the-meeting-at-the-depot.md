## 7. The Meeting at the Depot

She came out of the dark the way fog comes off a river—gradually, and then all at once.

I'd been leaning against the chain-link fence behind the old Greyhound depot for two hours, long enough for my coffee to go cold and my feet to go numb, watching the chalk mark I'd left on the third pillar from the door. The spiral with the crossbar. Freya's symbol, copied from the margins of her diary, the one she'd drawn beside the entry that said *if they answer, it's safe.*

At 4:17 a.m., a voice behind me said, "Don't turn around."

I turned around. Habit. Also, I wanted to see if she matched the driver's license photo I'd studied until I could draw it from memory. She did, and she didn't. Four years had thinned her face and grayed it, put a permanent squint in her eyes like she was reading fine print in bad light. She was holding a pistol low against her thigh, grip forward, the way people hold them who've practiced and hoped never to use it.

"You were supposed to stay facing the fence," she said.

"I'm told I have a problem with authority."

"August Lockridge." She said it like she was checking a receipt. "The dispatcher who buys dead women's diaries."

"Forty dollars. I overpaid, frankly. Half the pages are water damage."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An ancestor of one. "You left the reply mark."

"You left the question mark first."

We stood there in the cold, two people conducting an arms inspection by other means. Her eyes went over my jacket, my hands, the space behind me. Mine went over hers. The pistol was a small-caliber revolver, older than both of us put together, probably. The coat she wore was two sizes too big, and I'd have bet my pension it was chosen for running, not for style.

"There's a diner two blocks east," I said. "Opens at five. Booths in the back, sight lines to both doors."

"I know the diner. I've eaten there eleven times in four years. Never twice in the same week." She lowered the gun, but didn't holster it—just let it hang there like a punctuation mark she wasn't finished with. "Why are you doing this, Lockridge? And don't say justice. Men who say justice usually mean something smaller."

"Because everyone told me not to."

That got the almost-smile again. "That's the first honest answer I've had since 2019. Fine. The diner. You walk first. If anyone follows you in, I was never here, and you never saw me, and you'll want to believe that very sincerely."

* * *

The booth in the back of the Starlite Diner had a tear in the vinyl shaped, if you squinted, like the state of Ohio. Freya sat facing the door with her back to the wall, which told me she'd done this before, more than once. The waitress brought coffee without being asked. Freya wrapped both hands around the cup like it was something she'd been dreaming about.

"Ask," she said.

"Voss. Start with Voss."

She looked at the coffee for a long moment. Then she started, and her voice changed—flattened, professional, the voice of a woman reading back a record.

"Nine years I transcribed for that courtroom. You know what a stenographer actually is, Lockridge? We're the only people in the building who hear everything. Lawyers perform for the judge. Judges perform for the appellate record. But the machine doesn't care who's watching. I typed it all. The asides. The off-record conferences. The things said when the tape was off and my machine was still running." She took a sip. "Voss liked to go off the record. He liked it the way some men like a second drink."

"What was he saying off the record?"

"Sealed sessions. Family court mostly, some probate—places where the public never looks and the press never goes. Corliss Development had cases in front of him maybe thirty times over six years. Land assemblages, zoning variances, a conservatorship or two where elderly property owners suddenly had new guardians who signed everything over. Every sealed session, Corliss's people walked out with exactly what they came for, and Voss walked out with—" she made a vague gesture, "—consideration. Not cash. Cash leaves fingerprints. It went through a foundation, through a church renovation fund, through fees paid to an 'expert witness' who didn't exist. I transcribed the shell companies' names because they got read into the record. I transcribed everything. That was my job. Nobody thought about the fact that my job made me the evidence."

"And you kept copies."

"I kept copies of everything for nine years. It started as insurance and turned into a hobby and turned into a religion." Her hands had gone tight around the cup. "Then, in the spring of 2019, Corliss filed to assemble the riverfront parcels. The marina district. There was a holdout—an old man, a widow's estate, a church. The sealed session where Voss fixed that one, I transcribed it on a Tuesday and I couldn't sleep on a Tuesday night, and by Thursday I'd made a mistake. I asked a question. Not to Voss. To the clerk of court, about whether sealed transcripts were ever audited. That's all. One question."

"And someone heard it."

"The clerk mentioned it to the bailiff. The bailiff mentioned it to Corliss's fixer, because the fixer had half the courthouse on a coffee-and-favors retainer. By Friday there was a man standing outside my building, and by the following Monday there was a man outside my building who wasn't even pretending to read a newspaper." She set the cup down. Her hands were very steady now, which frightened me more than shaking would have. "Corliss sent a message through my landlord. A nice message. A message about my sister in Tucson. Her street address, her work schedule, the make of her car. That was the message."

"So you ran."

"I ran the way you run when the building's already on fire. Except I couldn't just run, because running leaves a trail, and a trail leads to Tucson. I needed to be dead. Dead people don't have sisters who can be visited." She looked out the window at the empty street. "And that's where your mentor comes in."

* * *

I'd known it was coming. I'd seen Ray's name countersigning the destruction of that transcript, and I'd spent the drive over turning it over like a stone, looking for the soft underside. Hearing her say it was different.

"Ray Okonkwo was working a case in my building," Freya said. "He noticed the man watching my building, because Ray notices things—it's why you trusted him to teach you, I'd guess. He came to me, not the other way around. He'd been trying to build something on Voss for two years and getting stonewalled at every level, because Voss had friends in the prosecutor's office and the police brass. Ray said, and I quote, 'You can't beat them inside the system. The system is how they beat you.' So we did it outside."

"The drowning."

"The marina, October 2019. Ray knew the recovery protocol cold—he'd worked enough water deaths. A body was pulled from the river that night, a Jane Doe, no ID, been in the water a while. Ray made sure the description matched me. Height, hair color, the coat I'd left folded on the seawall with my purse. The ME was stretched thin, overworked, inclined to accept an ID from a veteran detective. The dental records—" she paused. "The dental records were Ray's masterstroke. He knew which records the office would pull, and he made sure the file they pulled had been swapped. I'd had a filling done two years earlier. The chart in the system said otherwise."

"Backdated report. I found that already."

"Ray's work. He's thorough. It's his best quality and it's the one that's going to kill him someday." She turned the cup a slow half-circle on the Formica. "I got on a bus that night with a new name and a bag of clothes, and I've been a ghost for four years. Waitressing, housekeeping, night stock at a warehouse in a town I won't name. Every few months I come back through, because you can't run forever on fear, and because I wanted to know if anyone ever asked about me. Nobody did. Until a cab dispatcher started asking questions the whole city had agreed to stop asking."

"Why now? Why come back at all? You could have stayed a ghost."

"Because the ghost has an expiration date." She leaned forward, and for the first time I saw the thing under the wariness—not fear exactly, more like a banked fire, something that had been burning low and steady for four years and was now being fed. "The crimes in those transcripts—there's a statute. Fraud, obstruction, the corruption charges that would actually stick. Nine days from tomorrow, at midnight, it all expires. And do you know what Voss is doing on the fifteenth?"

"I read the paper."

"They're elevating him. State supreme court. Lifetime appointment, federal confirmation, the whole parade. After that he's untouchable in a way that makes the statute look generous. He goes from a man who could be prosecuted to a man who'd be a constitutional crisis to prosecute." She laughed, short and bitter. "He's being promoted for the same crimes I drowned for."

"The transcripts. You said you kept copies."

"Copies. And copies can be called forgeries—Voss's lawyers will say I typed them myself last month, and they'll be right that it's possible, and a jury doesn't care that it isn't likely. What can't be called a forgery is the original. The certified originals of the sealed sessions are in the courthouse vault, in the annex basement, under seal. Voss has spent four years trying to get his hands on them—there were two 'routine audits' and one small fire in a records room that didn't reach the vault. He can't destroy them without a paper trail, so he's going to outlive them. Statute runs out, he's elevated, and those transcripts become historical curiosities." She looked at me straight on. "They're the only thing on earth that can stop him, and they're sitting in a basement nine days from becoming landfill."

"So you need someone to get into the vault."

"I need someone who knows the building, the schedules, the shift changes. I need someone the courthouse hasn't learned to watch for. And I need someone who's already proven he doesn't take no from powerful men for an answer." She slid something across the table. A key, old brass, taped to a card with a locker number written on it. "Bus station, locker forty-one. Everything I have. Nine years of copies, my notes, the names of every shell company. If something happens to me, it's yours. That's not trust, Lockridge. That's a dead man's switch, and you're the dead man."

"Charming."

"I'm told I'm charming under pressure." She stood, dropped a five under her cup, and pulled the big coat around herself. At the edge of the booth she stopped. "One more thing. Your mentor. Ray."

"What about him?"

"When you see him next—and you will—he's going to tell you to stop. He's going to look you in the eye and tell you the case is closed and I'm dead and some doors stay shut. And you're going to want to believe him, because he raised you, and because he's a good man." Her eyes were steady and sad. "He is a good man. That's the whole tragedy of it. He helped me live, and then he helped them bury me, and he's been carrying both halves for four years. Don't let his guilt make your decision for you."

She walked out the front door into the gray light, and I watched through the window as she crossed the street without looking at the traffic, the way people walk who've stopped believing the world will make room for them.

I sat with the cold coffee and the key and did the math. Nine days. A vault. A mentor on the wrong side of a sealed door.

I flagged the waitress for a refill. I had a feeling I wasn't going to sleep again until this was over.
