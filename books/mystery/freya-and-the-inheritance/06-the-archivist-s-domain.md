## 6. The Archivist's Domain

Marta Voss had been cataloguing the Vane papers for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once lost her place.

This was not a boast. It was a discipline, the way some people never missed morning prayers or the first light of dawn on water. The archive lived in the east wing of Vane House, in what had once been a conservatory — Freya's grandmother had grown orchids there, back when the family still believed in growing things — and Marta had converted it shelf by shelf, case by case, into a room where paper could breathe and be understood. The humidity was controlled. The light was filtered through linen blinds so that no document ever suffered the indignity of direct sun. There was a ladder on brass rails that sang a single low note when you pushed it, and Marta had come to think of that note as the room greeting her.

She arrived at six each morning, before the household stirred, and made tea in a small electric kettle kept behind the map drawers because the kitchen was too far and the kitchen had too many ears.

Today the kettle clicked off and she poured, and she stood for a moment in the grey-blue early light with both hands around the cup, listening to the house. Gulls outside. The sea doing its patient work against the rocks below the cliff garden. Somewhere upstairs, Aldous was already awake — she could tell by the particular quality of his footsteps, quick and guilty, like a man crossing a floor he owed money on. Corinne would not appear until nine, pink-eyed and apologetic about existing. And the investigator —

Marta sipped her tea.

The investigator had arrived yesterday, and Marta had watched them come up the drive from the archery-room window, and she had felt something she could only describe, privately, in the privacy of her own careful heart, as *recognition*.

Not surprise. She was never surprised; surprise was for amateurs who hadn't read the file. But recognition, yes — the way an archivist recognizes a volume that has been mis-shelved for decades and has finally, finally come home to its proper place on the shelf.

Lena Coldharbour. Insurance investigator. Thirty-four years old. Photographic memory, though the official assessments called it "exceptional recall" because the assessors were cowards. Raised in foster care after the grandmother died. No siblings. No partner currently. A flat in a city Marta considered architecturally forgettable. A habit of eating the same lunch four days out of five.

And, of course: born in March, 1990, to a mother whose name appeared in exactly one place in the entire Vane archive, and even there only as an initial, in a ledger entry Marta herself had adjusted one rainy afternoon in 1998, with Freya standing behind her saying nothing at all.

*The child is handled,* Freya had said afterward, which was not agreement, but which was also not objection, and in those days Marta had learned to read Freya's silences the way other people read handwriting.

She set down her cup and went to work.

* * *

The morning's task was the correspondence of Edmund Vane, Freya's father, 1961 through 1974 — thirteen boxes, acid-free folders, each letter sleeved and numbered in Marta's small, upright hand. She worked with white cotton gloves until ten, then without, because she believed a person should touch paper honestly at least part of the day, the way a doctor should sometimes use their bare hands. Paper told you things through the skin. It told you if it had been folded in anger (creases too sharp) or read in bed (softening along the thumb-line) or wept upon (a faint tide-mark, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent thirty years learning the language).

Edmund had been a dull man who wrote dull letters, which made him easy. Dull men did not hide things well; they hid things obviously, in locked drawers, as if secrecy were furniture. She catalogued forty-one letters from his mistress in Leeds, noted them, cross-referenced them, and placed them in the sequence where they belonged — between the letters about drainage and the letters about the horse — because history was not improved by drama. History was improved by *order*.

It was nearly eleven when she came to the folder marked E.V. / 1971 / PERSONAL, and here she paused, gloves off, tea gone cold beside her, and permitted herself the small pleasure of review.

Three pages had been removed from this folder.

She knew this the way she knew the room's humidity: completely, and without needing to check. She had removed them herself, fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday, in the hour between Freya's last breakfast and the finding of Freya's body. Three pages in Freya's later hand — shaky, accusatory, unwell — pages that named names and described doses and ended mid-sentence in a way that no reader should ever be allowed to see, because an unfinished sentence is an invitation, and Marta did not run an invitation house.

The removal had taken ninety seconds. The renumbering had taken twenty minutes. The satisfaction had lasted considerably longer.

Now she stood over the gap in the sequence — folders 12, 13, 14, then 15, with nothing missing to any eye but hers — and felt the particular warmth she always felt after a correction. People imagined that destroying history was violence. People were sentimental. Destroying history was *editing*, and editing was love. Every family was a manuscript, and every manuscript had passages that wounded it if left in — the embarrassing chapter, the inconsistent character, the child who complicated the plot. To leave such passages in place was not honesty. It was negligence. It was letting the story bleed.

Freya had understood this, near the end. That was the tragedy of the final year — not the illness, not the small daily poisonings, which had been almost gentle, almost administrative — but that Freya had begun to *revise*. She had started keeping her own archive. A shadow catalogue. Letters copied in secret, records requested under false pretenses, a whole parallel edition of the family's history assembled in defiance of the authorized text.

Marta had found most of it. Most.

She smoothed the empty space in the folder with two fingers, the way one smooths a bedsheet, and moved on.

* * *

At noon, Lena Coldharbour came to the conservatory door.

Marta heard them coming — they walked quietly, but everyone walked audibly if you'd trained yourself for thirty years on floorboards — and she arranged herself at the map drawer with a folio open, in the attitude of a woman interrupted rather than a woman expecting. This was not deception, precisely. It was staging. All presentation was staging; the only question was whether you admitted it.

"Ms. Coldharbour." She turned, and gave the smile she used for appraisals — warm at the corners, neutral at the center. "You're up early for someone on holiday."

"I'm not on holiday."

"No," Marta agreed pleasantly. "You're not. You've been awake since five-forty. You re-read the claims file twice and then sat by your window watching the gulls for eleven minutes, which I suspect is the closest thing you have to a holiday." She let that sit, gently, like a card laid face-up. "Tea?"

Something flickered across the investigator's face — not alarm, exactly. Recalculation. Marta found it delightful. Most people, when shown they were known, either flinched or blustered. Lena Coldharbour did neither; they simply *re-indexed*, visibly, the way a library does when a new acquisition arrives.

"How do you know my schedule?" Lena asked.

"I know everyone's schedule. It's my job. An archive isn't paper, Ms. Coldharbour — paper is just what the archive leaves behind. The archive is the *pattern*. Who writes to whom. Who lies on Tuesdays. Who takes sugar in their tea and pretends they don't." She poured a second cup without asking, because asking was a delay and the tea was at the correct temperature now. "Aldous lies on Tuesdays, incidentally. Has done for years. Something to do with his father's disapproval, long dead, still governing. Corinne doesn't lie at all, which is why nobody listens to her, which is its own kind of tragedy. And Freya—" Marta paused, and here she allowed the tenderness into her voice, real tenderness, the kind she kept for documents and dead women, "—Freya lied only about kindnesses. Everything else, she wrote down."

Lena took the cup. Their fingers did not tremble, but they held the saucer a half-second longer than necessary, and Marta noted it, filed it, loved it a little. *Subject accepts offered objects readily. Trust extended cautiously, then fully. Watch the trust.*

"You knew her well," Lena said.

"Thirty-one years. I was hired in—" and here Marta recited it with the ease of a birthdate, "—March, nineteen eighty-nine, after the unpleasantness at the National Register. You'll have read about that, of course. In your research."

"I've read about it."

"And what did you conclude?"

"That the provenance records were falsified. That documents were altered to inflate value and bury origins. That you were cleared of personal responsibility." Lena looked at her over the rim of the cup. "Cleared isn't innocent."

"No," Marta said, delighted again, genuinely. "It isn't. But it is *employed*, and I have been employed here ever since, and in thirty-one years not one document in this house has been lost, mislaid, damaged, or discovered in a condition inconsistent with its true history." She smiled. "That is more than the National Register can say."

They stood a moment in the filtered light. On the table between them lay the open folio — Edmund's drainage letters, harmless, deliberately so — and beyond it the shelves rose in their quiet thousands, every box numbered, every number true, except where truth required assistance.

"Why keep it?" Lena asked suddenly. "All of it. Bills, receipts, ration books, letters about horses. Why does any of it matter?"

Marta considered giving the polite answer. Then she considered giving the true one, and decided — why not? The investigator was going to be interesting. One might as well begin as one meant to continue.

"Because a family is a story," she said, "and stories rot if nobody tends them. Left alone, memory turns to mush — everyone remembers what flatters them, forgets what doesn't, and within two generations the whole thing is sentimental soup. Someone has to decide what the record *is*. Someone has to choose." She touched the edge of the folio, straightening it a millimeter. "I chose. For thirty-one years I have chosen, and the result is that the Vane family has a history instead of a rumor. That is not a small gift, Ms. Coldharbour. Most families never receive it. Most families don't deserve it."

"And who decides whether they deserve it?"

"I do," said Marta, pleasantly, and refilled their cup before they could answer.

* * *

In the afternoon, the investigator asked to see the photograph.

Marta had expected this — had, in fact, prepared for it, which is to say she had taken the framed photograph from the morning room and hung it in the archive's reading alcove two days ago, angled to catch the good light, positioned so that anyone viewing it must stand exactly where Marta wanted them, close enough to be observed, far enough to feel alone.

The photograph itself was a handsome thing: Vane House, summer, 1998, the whole surviving family arranged on the front steps in their terrible clothes. Edmund squinting. His sister's widow clutching her pearls. Freya in the center, fifty-eight years old and already looking like the last of a species. And at the edge of the group, slightly apart, a blur where a person had been — a softness in the emulsion, a smudge the size of a child, airbrushed with the crude optimism of the pre-digital age.

Lena stood before it for a long time. Long enough that Marta, watching from the doorway with a duster she had no intention of using, saw the change come over them — the stillness deepening, the head tilting, the eyes moving in that peculiar locked pattern she had read about in the assessment files and had never until now seen live. Memorizing. Taking the image in whole, the way other people took in weather.

*Check the record against the document,* Marta thought, with something dangerously close to affection. *Go on. Compare us.*

"The blur," Lena said at last. "There was someone here."

"There was a flaw in the print," Marta said. "Common in that era's processing. Freya kept it anyway — sentiment. She was sentimental about flaws."

"It's not a flaw." Lena's voice was very even. "Flaws are random. This is shaped. Shoulders. A height consistent with a child of eight or nine. Whoever edited it wasn't skilled, but they were thorough about the face."

Marta crossed the room slowly, unhurried, and stood beside them, and looked at the blur with the fondness one reserves for one's own early work — technically imperfect, morally sound.

"Families edit photographs," she said. "It's practically a tradition. The Victorians painted over the mad. We airbrush the inconvenient. Would you like to know what I think, Ms. Coldharbour? I think you are looking at that smudge the way other people look at mirrors." She turned her head, and let her gaze rest on the investigator's face — the strong jaw, the watchful eyes, the face that appeared in no Vane portrait and yet belonged, unmistakably, to the architecture of this family, the same brow as Edmund's mother, the same set of the mouth as old Aunt Verity in the 1952 portrait upstairs. "And I think you find something there you recognize."

Lena said nothing. But their breathing changed — one breath, slightly shorter than the ones before it — and Marta heard it the way she heard everything, and stored it, and treasured it.

*Subject suspects. Subject cannot yet prove. Subject's memory is the only remaining copy of certain texts.* 

*Which means, of course, that subject is now the most valuable document in the house.*

* * *

That evening, after the household had eaten its tense, over-salted dinner and withdrawn to separate silences, Marta made her rounds.

She checked the locks. She checked the windows. She checked, with a fingertip, the three gaps in the correspondence shelves, the hollow places where pages had been lifted out and burned in the cliff-garden incinerator on a windless night, and she found them all still sealed, still secret, still hers.

Then she stood a while in the dark conservatory, among the sleeping papers, and allowed herself the evening's one indulgence: imagining the finished archive. The day, years hence, when the Vane story would be complete — every loose thread resolved, every inconvenient character edited to their proper weight, every page in its place, and nothing anywhere in the world contradicting it. Not in a storage unit. Not in a solicitor's safe. Not in a hotel room where an insurance investigator slept with a claims file under their arm like a breviary.

Nothing anywhere, except in one head.

And heads, Marta thought fondly, switching off the lamp, were the one format she had not yet learned to edit.

But she was a patient woman. Thirty-one years patient.

She would learn.
