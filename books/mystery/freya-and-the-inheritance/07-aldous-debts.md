## 7. Aldous Debts

Aldous Vane found me on the second morning of my stay at Vane House, standing in the doorway of the guest room I'd been given—the one with the view of the sea and the view of the sea's other opinion, which was to say fog. He was holding two cups of tea like a man who had been told tea was how one handled things and had decided to commit to the strategy fully.

"Mr.—sorry. Ms. Sorry. Lena." He said the name the way one handles a piece of heirloom glassware that might, at any moment, be revealed to be a fake. "I didn't know how you took it. Milk? Sugar? I brought both options and also a third cup, in case."

"In case of what?"

"I genuinely don't know anymore." He set all three cups down on the writing desk with the care of a man defusing something. He was taller than he'd seemed at our first meeting, and thinner, in the way of someone who had recently begun forgetting meals for reasons that had nothing to do with appetite. His shirt was ironed but his collar wasn't, which told me he ironed his own shirts and had stopped bothering with the details around the throat. Around the throat was where the rope goes, I thought, and then thought, *that's grim, Coldharbour*, and then thought, *no, it's accurate, keep it.*

"Thank you for the tea," I said, and took the milk one, because choosing something was kinder than making him stand there while I deliberated.

He watched me drink it like a man watching a jury.

"I wanted," he began, and stopped. He tried the window instead, pulling the curtain aside an inch, looking at fog, letting the curtain fall. "I wanted to say, before the assessment goes any further, that I know how this looks."

"How what looks?"

"You. Here. Me. Talking to you." He laughed, a short unhappy sound. "Everything, really. Everything looks like something."

I have interviewed hundreds of claimants, beneficiaries, and persons of interest, and I have developed a private taxonomy of the ways people begin a conversation they're afraid of. There are the openers—*I'll be honest with you*, which means they won't; *I don't know why I'm telling you this*, which means they've rehearsed it; and the rarest and most dangerous, which is what Aldous did next, which is simply to sit down uninvited, heavily, like a building settling, and say nothing at all until the silence does the confessing for them.

He sat on the edge of the chaise. The chaise was antique and disapproved.

"My aunt is dead," he said. "Freya. And I loved her. I want that on the record, whatever the record turns out to be."

"Noted," I said, and meant it, which surprised me a little. Grief, when it's real, has a texture. His was real. It was also, I suspected, sitting in the same room as something else, the way two strangers share a train compartment and pretend to read.

"The thing is," he said, and here his hands started in, worrying at each other, "the thing is that money is—there are complications. On my end. Unrelated complications that are nonetheless—" He gave up on the sentence and tried a fresh one. "I'm in debt, Ms. Coldharbour. Lena. A considerable amount. To people who do not send letters. They send men."

I set down the tea. "How considerable?"

He told me a number. I won't repeat it here, partly because it's not mine to repeat, and partly because my memory doesn't work that way—numbers stick whether I want them to or not, and I've learned to be selective about what I feed it. Let's say it was the kind of number that has a comma in a place commas shouldn't be. The kind of number that arrives with men attached.

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you're the investigator. Because the estate can't settle while the claim is open. Because—" His voice did something I hadn't heard it do before, which was to drop all its polish at once, like a coat falling off a hook. "Because I'm asking you to hurry. That's it. That's the whole ugly speech. I'm asking you to hurry up and decide that my aunt died of natural causes so that I can pay these people before they decide I'm more valuable as an example than as a client."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below us, Marta was doing something in the archive—I could hear the small, metronomic sounds of her work, drawer, paper, drawer—and the house seemed to hold its breath around that rhythm, the way a household does around whatever its true clock is.

"Mr. Vane," I said. "Aldous. I don't decide how your aunt died. I assess the claim. And if I find evidence that she didn't die of natural causes, I don't sit on it to accommodate anyone's creditors."

"I know." He was looking at his hands. "I know that. I'm not asking you to lie. I'm asking you to be quick."

"There's a difference between quick and clean, and I only know how to do one of them."

He looked up at that, and for a second something moved across his face that wasn't desperation. It was almost relief—the look of a man who has spent weeks surrounded by people who say *whatever you need* and has just heard someone say *no, but honestly*, and found it restful.

"You're not what I expected," he said.

"Nobody ever is. It's the whole trouble with expectations."

* * *

He didn't leave. That was the thing about Aldous—he didn't leave rooms. He circled them. He picked up a porcelain shepherdess from the mantel and put it down. He looked at the fog again. And then, with the air of a man stepping off a ledge because standing on it had become worse than falling, he said:

"There were letters."

I kept my face still. My face is very good at still; it's the one part of my memory I can actually control.

"Letters," I said.

"After Freya died. Two of them. Corinne found them in the hall—no, that's not—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "God. I'm terrible at this. *I* found them. In the morning post, two days after. One was—" He swallowed. "One was a confession. To killing her. No name on it. And the other one said—" his mouth worked— "it said the inheritance wasn't finished."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a room does when a fact you've been carrying privately turns out to have been public all along. I had received those same letters. I had told no one. And here was the nephew, telling me, unprompted, in a room that I now understood might contain listening of one kind or another—though I want to be clear that at this stage I suspected the house itself more than I suspected any person in it.

"Did you tell the police?" I asked.

"I didn't tell *anyone*." The words came out fast and low. "Because I sat here—actually in this room—and I thought: whoever wrote that confession wanted it found. And if I hand it to the police, then I'm the nephew with gambling debts whose aunt just died. I'm the story. I'm the *obvious*." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "I've read enough crime novels. The obvious gets arrested and the actual killer gets a nice paragraph at the end about how no one ever suspected them."

"And so you hid them."

"I hid them." He said it like a man laying a card face up. "In my workshop. And then I lay awake every night since thinking about the fact that I hid them, and what that makes me, and whether the person who wrote them knows I have them, and whether that's why the second letter said what it said."

"Which is?"

"*The inheritance is not finished.*" He repeated it flatly, without the theatrical dread he'd used a moment ago, and the flatness was worse. "I keep turning it over. Finished how? Finished like a building? Finished like a—" He stopped. He looked at me, and I watched him arrive at a thought he clearly didn't enjoy. "Why are you here, Ms. Coldharbour? Really. An insurance claim for a woman who died in her sleep doesn't need an investigator on site for a week. Devi said the policy required it, but Devi says things in a way that makes you not want to ask the second question."

"Devi is thorough," I said, which was true and also a door I closed gently rather than opening.

He nodded slowly. He was, whatever else he was, not stupid. Desperation had made him stupid in the specific way that debt makes people stupid—tunnel vision, the world narrowed to a number—but underneath it there was a working mind, and I remember thinking, with a small cold feeling I didn't yet have a name for: *this man would make a very poor murderer and a very good decoy.*

I didn't know yet why that thought had come to me. I let it sit.

* * *

We went down to lunch. Corinne had laid the table herself, which I gathered was unusual—normally, I'd been told, the housekeeper did it, but the housekeeper had been given the week off, an arrangement Marta had described as "Freya's standing instruction for assessment periods," and which I was beginning to suspect was Marta's standing arrangement for having the house to certain people at certain times.

Corinne had made soup. It was good soup, and she watched us eat it with the anxious attention of someone who has recently learned that the people around her are capable of things, and has started reading everything—soup, weather, silence—as a possible message.

"Aldous," she said, after a while, "was telling me you're from the north side of town. The harbor end."

"I grew up there," I said. "Off Rope Walk."

"Rope Walk!" She smiled, and the smile had a strange flicker in it, like a lamp with a loose connection. "My grandmother lived off Rope Walk. Before she married. She used to say you could tell the season by the smell."

"You can," I said. "Tar in summer. Diesel and salt in winter."

"Yes!" And for a moment she was just a woman talking about her grandmother, and the relief in it was so plain it hurt to look at. Then Aldous's knee bumped hers under the table—accidentally, I think, or accidentally on purpose, a reminder—and the lamp flickered and steadied into politeness again.

Marta came in while we were eating. She didn't eat with us; she never ate with us. She came to the sideboard, collected a folder that had been left there, and paused.

"Mr. Vane," she said pleasantly. "You were in the blue room this morning."

It wasn't a question. Aldous's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"I was showing Lena—" he began.

"The blue room's contents are catalogued," Marta said, still pleasant, still mild, in the tone of someone remarking on the weather. "I only mention it because the shepherdess from the mantel was moved two inches to the left. I've put her back." She smiled at all of us, at the soup, at the fog beyond the window, and left.

The silence she left behind had a texture too. It was the texture of a household in which a woman two inches of porcelain was an event worth noting aloud.

Corinne stared at her bowl. Aldous stared at the doorway. And I sat there with my spoon in my hand and thought: *she catalogs the rooms the way she catalogs the papers. She catalogs the people. She probably has a drawer for me.*

I found, to my own surprise, that I wasn't frightened. I was *furious*, in a slow, cold, extremely organized way, and I made a private note of that too, because fury was new for me and I wanted to remember what it felt like from the inside.

* * *

That afternoon I found Aldous in his workshop, a converted boathouse down the lawn where he made—of all things—model ships. Beautiful ones. There was a half-rigged brig on the bench with sails so fine they seemed spun from breath, and I stood looking at it for longer than was professionally necessary.

"You made this?"

"Badly," he said. "It's the only thing I've ever done slowly. Everything else in my life I've rushed." He didn't look at me. He was sanding something small. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it. If I'd been a slow person, I'd never have walked into that card room in the first place."

"Where are the letters, Aldous?"

His hands went still. The sandpaper whispered to a stop.

"You said your workshop," I said. "You told me yourself, this morning. I'm not reading anything out of you. I'm asking you to give them to me—not to the police, not yet—to me. I'm the one person in this house whose job is looking at documents and noticing what's wrong with them."

"And then what?" He turned. His eyes were red-rimmed and honest and terrified. "Then you find out Freya was murdered, and the claim collapses, and the men come back, and I'm the nephew with debts and hidden letters and a workshop full of boats, and every newspaper in the country prints my face next to the word *suspect*."

"Yes," I said. "That might happen. Or I find out the letters are a performance, and the claim stands, and you pay your debts, and in five years you're sanding boats and telling this story at dinner parties. I can't promise you which. But I can promise you that the version where you sit on them ends worse. Letters like that don't stay hidden. Whoever wrote them *wants* them found. You're not hiding them, Aldous. You're keeping them warm."

He looked at the little brig for a long moment. Then he went to the tool cabinet, moved a false back off a drawer with the practiced motion of a man who had done it many times and hated that he'd needed to, and took out two envelopes. He held them out. His hand was shaking.

"I didn't kill her," he said. "I need you to know that. I loved her. She was the only one who never—" He stopped. "She was kind to me. Once. In a way nobody else in that house has ever been."

"Once?"

"When I was a boy." His jaw worked. "There was a summer—I don't remember which—she took me out on the water and taught me the knots. Real knots. She said, *a knot should come apart exactly when you want it to and no other time.*" He laughed wetly. "I've been thinking about that all week. About how I've never once come apart when I wanted to."

I took the envelopes. I didn't open them there; some things you do in a room with a locked door and good light. But I looked at the handwriting on the outside—block capitals, careful, unhurried—and something in my chest went tight, because the handwriting was *wrong* in a way I couldn't yet name, the way the photograph had been wrong, the way the whole house was wrong, everything in it a half-inch to the left of true.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me." He was already turning back to the bench, back to the boat, back to the only slow thing he had. "Just be quick. Please. Be quick."

I walked back up the lawn through the fog with the letters in my coat, and I did what I always do with facts that frighten me: I laid them out in order and looked at them.

A man in debt, who knew of the letters, who hid them, who begged me for speed. A motive with a comma in the wrong place. A confession in the post with no name on it. By any method I'd been trained in—by the methods I used on strangers every week, quickly, cleanly, and without losing sleep—Aldous Vane was the answer. He was the obvious.

And the obvious had been sitting in my chest all afternoon like a stone I'd swallowed, and it would not go down, and I finally understood why. It wasn't sympathy. Well—it was sympathy, some, for a man who rigged sails finer than breath and couldn't rig his own life. But underneath the sympathy was something colder and more useful:

The confession had been *mailed*. Two days after the death, to two different addresses, in careful block capitals, by someone unhurried.

And Aldous Vane, by his own account, could not do anything slowly except make boats.

I reached the terrace steps and stopped. Because there was a third thing, wasn't there, that I'd been not-thinking since breakfast. The letters had come to Aldous in the morning post at Vane House.

They had come to me, at my hotel, addressed in the same careful hand—to an investigator whose name should never have appeared in this family's business at all.

Whoever wrote them hadn't been confessing.

They'd been *casting*.

And somewhere behind me, in the warm lamplit heart of the house, I heard the small metronomic sounds of the archive—drawer, paper, drawer—going on and on, patient as tide, as though someone in there had all the time in the world, and was using mine.
