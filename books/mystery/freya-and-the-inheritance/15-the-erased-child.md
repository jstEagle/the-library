## 15. The Erased Child

The letters were sorted by decade, which told Lena something about Freya before a single word was read.

Nobody sorts grief by decade. Grief is a shoebox, a drawer, a plastic bag from a pharmacy that outlived the prescription. Decades mean a person sat down—more than once, deliberately, with tea going cold beside them—and made decisions about their own past. Filed it. Curated it.

Lena knew what that looked like from the inside. They did it for a living.

The storage unit smelled of cedar and old glue, and in the corner where the ransackers had torn through boxes looking for the ledger, Freya's correspondence had been left scattered like a hand of cards nobody wanted. Lena had spent the previous evening reconstructing the poison ledger from memory, writing out all forty-three entries in their own careful hand while a police constable named Okafor watched with the expression of a man being shown a magic trick he'd rather not believe in. Now, in the flat grey light of a coastal morning, they sat on an upturned crate with the letters spread across their knees, reading a dead woman's mail in roughly chronological order, and the world began, very quietly, to rearrange itself.

* * *

The earliest letter was dated March 1997, addressed to a Dr. Halloran in Edinburgh, and it was about the weather.

Not really about the weather. Lena had learned long ago that people who write careful letters are never really about the weather. *The garden has come through the winter better than I deserved,* Freya wrote, *though the roses my mother planted have given up entirely, which I confess suits me. I have never liked being reminded that she decided what grew here.*

Lena smiled despite themself. There it was, thirty years early: the same dry, unyielding voice that had written a ledger of her own poisoning in a hand that never wavered. Freya Vane had gone to her death the way she apparently went through her whole life—annotating.

The next letters were family correspondence, and this was where it got strange, because Lena recognized the names. Aldous's father, Gerald. A cousin named Beatrice who appeared in no file Lena had ever seen, and whose letters stopped in 2003 with a final note from Freya that read, in full: *I am sorry we could not save you from them. I will remember you properly.*

*Remember you properly.*

The phrase landed in Lena's chest like a key turning in a lock they'd forgotten they owned. They set the letter down. Picked it up again. Read it three times.

Outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something, and the sea did its patient gray work against the rocks below the cliff, and Lena Coldharbour—who could recite every page of the ledger, every line of the doctored photograph's frame, every word of three anonymous letters—sat very still and felt the first vertigo of a memory that was not their own pressing up against the edges of their mind.

* * *

It was the 1998 letters that undid them.

There were eleven, spanning February to October of that year, and Freya had kept both sides of the correspondence, which meant Lena got to watch an argument unfold in real time across the gulf of twenty-seven years. The other party was Gerald—Aldous's father, the brittle nephew's brittle father—and the subject was a photograph.

*I have your note about the portrait,* Freya wrote in February, and her handwriting, for the first time, looked hurried. *I want to be very clear about what I am being asked to agree to. You are asking me to remove a child from a photograph. Not to lose the photograph. Not to misplace it. To take a brush to a little girl's face and pretend she was never standing on that platform with the rest of us.*

Lena's hands had gone cold. They kept reading.

*She is four years old. She waved at the camera. Gerald, I was there—I held her hand while your mother fussed with her collar. I remember the weight of her. I remember that she had her father's stubborn chin and her mother's laugh, which she had already inherited at four, God help her. And now her mother is dead and her father is being disappeared into a "position abroad" and you want the family album to read as though none of it happened.*

*You are not asking me to protect the family name. The family name is not a person. You are asking me to protect the family's comfort, which is a different animal entirely and a far less deserving one.*

There was no reply from Gerald in the file—of course there wasn't; Freya kept her own side and let the silences speak—but his position was clear from her next letter, dated three weeks later.

*So it is to be "the arrangement." A nice clean word. The child goes to strangers, the father goes abroad, the photograph goes to the archivist's table, and in five years no one will be able to prove any of us ever had a heart between us.*

The archivist's table.

Lena read that phrase four times. Marta had been with the family since—Lena had traced it themselves, in chapter and verse—thirty years. Since before 1998. Marta's hands had been on that photograph. Marta's brush, or Marta's instruction, or Marta's quiet professional agreement that a story with an inconvenient child in it was a story improved by editing.

*You were always part of this story,* the third letter had said. *Stop pretending otherwise.*

Lena had read that as a threat. Sitting on an upturned crate in a ransacked storage unit, they began to understand it might have been—whichever hand had written it—a statement of fact.

* * *

There was one more letter from 1998, dated the 14th of October, and it was different from all the others. Shorter. Written in ink rather than Freya's usual pencil, as though she'd wanted it to be permanent.

*To whoever finds this, if anyone ever does:*

*Today I held my grandniece's hand on a train platform for the last time. She is four. She does not know she is being erased; she thinks she is going on a journey. Children are always going on journeys. That is the cruelest thing about what we do to them—they trust the platform.*

*Her name is Lena. She was born in March, out of wedlock, to my grandniece Sable, who died in June, and whose family has decided that grief is best managed by amputation. I signed the papers because I was outvoted, and I will regret the word "outvoted" for the rest of my life, because it implies I fought. I did not fight hard enough. I let them call it an arrangement.*

*I gave her a brass key and told her to remember me properly. She will not know what it means for years, if ever. Perhaps she will think it was a dream. Perhaps that is a mercy. But in case it is not—in case she is the kind of person who keeps things, who files things, who remembers—then let this letter be the proof she will need:*

*She was not given up. She was taken. There is a difference, and the difference is a whole family's cowardice, and I was part of it, and I am sorry.*

*The photograph on my wall is a lie. I keep it because I cannot bear to lose the last true image underneath the paint, and because one day I intend to have it restored, and to find her, and to say all of this to her face. I am sixty-eight years old and I have finally learned that the family name is worth exactly what the family is willing to be. We were not willing to be much.*

*Remember me properly, Lena. That is all I ever asked. It is more than I deserved.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Lena knew the hand from the ledger, from the margins of the estate files, from forty-three entries documenting eighteen months of slow murder, written in ink that never wavered.

They sat with the letter in their lap for a long time.

* * *

The strange thing—the thing Lena would try to explain later, to Devi, over tea that went cold in solidarity—was that the revelation didn't arrive as a shock. It arrived as a correction. Like a number in a ledger that had been wrong for thirty years, and some deep clerical part of them exhaled: *ah. There it is. The accounts balance after all.*

The train platform was real. The brass key was real. The woman with the dry voice and the steady hand had been real, and she had been Freya Vane, and she had held four-year-old Lena's hand and asked to be remembered, and then the family had amputated the memory, and Lena had spent their whole life being a person with no past because a family full of adults had looked at a grieving four-year-old and decided the family name weighed more.

And underneath that—underneath the grief, which was enormous and would need its own filing system—there was something else. Something warmer and more terrible.

Freya had known. Freya had known Lena's name before the third letter ever arrived, before the will, before the insurance claim. Freya had spent twenty-seven years keeping a lie on her wall because the truth was underneath it, waiting, and she had spent those same years writing letters to a grandniece she expected never to read them, and she had died—been murdered, slowly, by the person trusted to keep the family's records—with Lena's name in her will and Lena's key in her plan.

*I will remember you properly.*

She had. That was the unbearable part. Freya Vane had remembered Lena properly for twenty-seven years, alone, in a house full of people who orbited a curated version of events, and she had built her whole final act—key, storage unit, ledger, will—around the hope that the erased child would turn out to be the kind of person who keeps things.

Lena laughed. It came out wrong, wet and startled, and they wiped their face with the heel of their hand and laughed again, properly this time, alone in a cedar-smelling storage unit with the gulls arguing overhead.

"Well," they said to the empty air, to Freya, to the four-year-old on the platform. "You got your wish. I keep things."

* * *

They spent the afternoon with the letters, and the warmth of the morning's revelation cooled into something more precise.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't sit still: Freya had discovered the falsified photograph. She said so, in her own hand, in 1998. She had known for twenty-seven years that Marta—or Marta's table, at minimum—had edited a child out of the family record. And yet Marta had remained. Had been trusted. Had been given the archive, the house's memory, the run of the place.

Either Freya had forgiven an unforgivable act, or Freya had kept her enemy close, or—

Lena laid the letters out on the crate floor in date order and looked at them the way they looked at a suspicious claim. Not at what was written. At what was *missing*.

There were no letters from Freya to Marta. Not one, in thirty years of meticulous record-keeping, in a collection that preserved even a 1997 complaint about roses. A woman who kept both sides of every correspondence had kept nothing from the most important relationship in her final years.

Which meant one of two things. Either Freya had destroyed those letters herself—

—or someone else had gotten to them first.

Lena sat back on the crate and looked at the ransacked corner of the unit, the boxes torn open, the careful decade-sorting violated. The thieves had come for the ledger. The police said so; Okafor said so; the kicked-in door said so. But thieves ransack. Thieves take valuables and leave the rest in drifts.

This had been different. Lena's memory, unbidden and exact, replayed the scene: the boxes torn open, yes—but selectively. The correspondence from the 1970s, untouched. The 1980s, untouched. The 1990s, rifled. And in the 1990s box, gaps. Gaps with the particular shape of removed letters, the way a hand leaves a shadow on a page it has lifted out.

Someone hadn't ransacked the unit. Someone had *audited* it.

And if letters between Freya and Marta had existed—if Freya had spent her final years documenting not just her poisoning but her archivist's thirty-year career of quiet erasures—then somewhere in this coastal town, or in that house on the cliff, there was a missing correspondence that named Marta not as a murderer of one old woman but as the editor of an entire family.

Lena gathered the letters, squared their edges, and slid them into a portfolio with hands that were almost steady.

The storage unit's corrugated door rattled in the wind off the sea. Somewhere up the coast, in a house where everyone orbited a curated version of events, Corinne was alone with her knowledge and her terror—and Lena had a sudden, cold, photographic certainty that the household's true architecture did not tolerate loose pages for long.

They should call her. They would call her.

The phone was in their coat, in the other room, and by the time Lena reached it, it was already ringing—Corinne's number on the screen, an hour of voicemail silence waiting on the other end, and a message that would change the shape of everything.
