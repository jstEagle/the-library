## 22. Remember Me Properly

The house smelled of rosemary and scorched butter, which Lena had learned was the smell of Corinne attempting to cook in a kitchen that had been designed by someone who hated cooking. Lena had designed it. Or rather, Freya had designed it, in 1974, and Lena had simply failed to change it, and now they were all living inside a dead woman's opinions about counter height.

"You're burning it again," Aldous called from the doorway. He was holding a bottle of sparkling water the way he used to hold whiskey—two hands, reverently, like something that might save him or kill him. Nine months sober now. He still announced it sometimes, unprompted, the way other people announced the weather.

"I am *charring it intentionally*," Corinne said. "It's called flavor development. Marta taught me."

The name dropped into the room the way a stone drops into a well. Everyone heard it land. Nobody flinched the way they used to.

It had been Corinne's idea, actually—a joke at first, in the hospital corridor where she'd spent two days recovering from her own kidnapping, hollow-eyed and furious and more alive than Lena had ever seen her. *We should keep her recipes,* she'd said. *The poisoner's recipes. Cook them badly. Take them back.* And so the rosemary chicken was Marta's recipe, executed with love and incompetence by a woman Marta had once locked in a cellar, and there was something in that which Lena thought Freya would have understood. The archive doesn't get to decide what a thing means. The living do.

"Devi's on the porch," Aldous said. "She's been out there for ten minutes. I think she's staging an intervention about the tea."

"She's been staging that intervention since March."

"She brought her own kettle, Lena. She brought her own *kettle*."

"I heard that," Devi said from the doorway, and there she was, solicitor, keeper of wills, a small precise woman in a cardigan the color of a filing cabinet, carrying—as advertised—a kettle. "And I'll stage it as long as it takes. Your kitchen contains four teas and all of them are crimes. There is a box labeled *Mystery Tea* in your cupboard. Mystery to whom, Lena? Who is the tea a mystery to?"

"To whoever opens the cupboard," Lena said. "It's an interactive experience."

"It's a *liability*." Devi set her kettle down on the counter with the click of a gavel. "I have known you for a year and a half. In that time I have watched you solve a murder, donate a fortune, and nearly burn down your own kitchen making toast. I have earned the right to bring my own kettle."

Corinne laughed—the real laugh now, the one that had come back slowly, like a bird returning to a garden after a bad winter, testing the branches. Lena catalogued it without meaning to, the way they catalogued everything: the exact pitch of it, the way Aldous's shoulders came down half an inch when he heard it. Nine months ago this woman had been a footnote in someone else's archive. A nervous wife. A liability. Marta had kept a file on her two inches thick.

Lena had burned the file. Corinne had asked to watch.

* * *

Dinner was, by any professional standard, a disaster.

The chicken was dry. Devi and Aldous got into a forty-minute argument about whether the memorial dinner should continue next year—Devi said tradition was a load-bearing wall, Aldous said the load-bearing wall had been used to trap a murderer and maybe they should consider drywall—and Corinne kept narrating the meal like a nature documentary. *"Here we see the investigator, attempting to carve. Note the hesitation. The chicken fights back."*

Lena let it happen around them and thought: *this is what it's supposed to feel like.*

That was the strange thing. Lena had spent forty years believing that other people's noise was a tax on their attention, a cost of being near the living, something to be endured and then recovered from in a quiet room with a case file. Their whole life had been organized around the management of other people's noise. And now they were sitting in the loudest room they'd been in since the anniversary dinner—since the other dinner, the trap, the toast, Marta's face going smooth and white as a page—and the noise felt like warmth on skin that had forgotten what warmth was for.

"You're doing the thing," Corinne said.

"What thing?"

"The staring-into-the-middle-distance thing. The remembering thing."

"I'm always remembering something."

"I know." Corinne reached over and put a hand on Lena's wrist. "But you're *here* while you do it. That's new. Keep that one."

Aldous raised his sparkling water. "To Freya," he said. "Who left me nothing, which was the most honest inheritance I've ever received, and probably saved my life."

"To Freya," Corinne said, "who hired a monster and then spent her last year building the trap that caught it."

"To Freya," Devi said, "who wrote a will with two options because she understood that her niece—" she nodded at Lena, "—would need a door, not a destination."

Lena looked at their glass. "To Freya," they said, "who told me to remember her properly. I'm still working out what properly means. I think it means this. I think it means *bad chicken with people who stay.*"

Nobody cried, which was its own kind of achievement. Corinne's eyes shone and Aldous pretended not to notice and Devi refilled glasses with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had spent thirty years watching families grieve and had developed opinions about how it should be done.

* * *

Afterward, Lena walked the house.

They did this most nights. Not the haunted pacing of the first months—that had been different, an investigator sweeping for threats, cataloguing exits, flinching at the sound of the sea. This was something else. This was a person learning the geography of a place they'd decided to keep.

The study first, always. The fireplace had been rebuilt—new stone, the old hearth scraped clean of its history—and above it hung the photograph.

The original. The 1998 one, restored by a conservator in Edinburgh who had wept, actually wept, when she lifted the airbrush layer and found the child underneath. A girl of six, standing at the edge of the frame, half-turned toward the camera, one hand raised in a wave that no one in the family had ever acknowledged. Lena had stood in front of the restoration for an hour the day it came back, memorizing it, which was redundant—they had never once needed to look at it twice—but which felt necessary anyway. Some things you don't keep in the head. Some things you hang on the wall where the bloodline can see them.

The girl in the photograph was Lena. The wave had been for Freya, standing behind the camera. *Remember me properly,* Freya had said on the platform eleven years later, pressing the brass key into a child's hand, and for thirty years Lena had filed it as a dream, a glitch, a piece of noise in the archive of their own childhood. Now it hung framed in the entryway too—a small photograph of a train platform, a woman in a green coat, a child with a key in her fist. The conservator had found it in the true archive, in an envelope marked *for L.C., if she comes.*

*If she comes.* Not *if she comes back.* Freya had known Lena had never once been allowed to belong. She had only hoped Lena would arrive.

Lena touched the frame the way they did every night, briefly, a ritual of one second, and moved on.

The library next, where the donated archive lived in duplicate—Devi had insisted on copies, Devi insisted on everything, it was her love language—and where the empty shelves stood in a row like extracted teeth. The Vane papers were at the public trust now, in Exeter, catalogued by people who had never met the family, who had no stake in which version survived, who answered to the public record and nothing else. Lena had visited in the spring. A graduate student had been reading Freya's poison ledger with white gloves and an expression of scholarly horror, and had looked up at Lena and said, "You're the niece, aren't you? The one she hid?" and Lena had said, "The one she *saved*," and the student had written it down, and that was the whole ceremony. History, held by strangers, honestly. It was more than most families ever got.

Marta was in a secure facility now, awaiting trial, and the reports that filtered out were strange and quiet. She had asked for paper. She had asked for a great deal of paper. She was writing, the guards said, constantly—letters, mostly, to no one, an archive of a life that had ended the moment she lunged for the fireplace and found the study door locked from outside. Corinne had locked it. Corinne, who had spent a year being edited out of her own marriage, had stood in the corridor with her hand on the bolt and her heart going like a hammer and had held it shut, and when Lena had found her afterward she'd said, "She kept everything. So I kept *her*," and laughed, and then cried, and then laughed again, and Lena had understood that this was what survival looked like when it finally arrived: not dignified. Just true.

Lena did not hate Marta. They had expected to, had braced for it the way you brace for a dental procedure, and it had never come. What they felt instead was something more unsettling: recognition. Two people with the same affliction—memory that would not release its grip—and two opposite answers to it. Marta had decided that memory was a burden only she was strong enough to carry, and had made herself its warden, and had killed to keep the collection complete. Lena had decided, eventually, slowly, with help, that memory was only unbearable if you carried it alone.

That was the whole difference. That was the entire gulf between the archive and the dinner table.

* * *

The last thing, before bed, was the letter.

Lena had started it in the winter and finished it in the spring and rewritten it in the summer, and tonight they took it out again—the actual paper, because some things should be paper—and read it in the kitchen by the light over the stove while the house ticked and settled around them.

*To whoever comes next:*

*You will find this, if you find it at all, because you went looking. That's how it worked for me. A woman I never knew to call family spent thirty years leaving a trail for me—brass keys, hidden archives, a photograph with my face put back in—and I spent thirty years not knowing I was being looked for. We were both stubborn. We found each other anyway, mostly on time.*

*I used to think my memory was a warehouse. Everything filed, nothing felt. I was good at it. I made a living out of it. And then I learned that the same memory could be a table—something you set for other people, something you lay out and say, sit, eat, tell me what happened to you. The difference wasn't the memory. The difference was whether anyone was allowed to sit down.*

*So: sit down. Whoever you are. If you're a Coldharbour or a Vane or nobody at all, if you're the erased child of some other family's tidy story—there is a chair. The chicken will be dry. The tea will be a crime. The solicitor will bring her own kettle and be right about everything, which is insufferable, and the recovering gambler will be the most honest person in the room, and the woman who was kept in a file will laugh louder than all of us. It isn't a bloodline. It's a table. Those are different things. I checked. It took me forty years to check, so take my word for it.*

*Remember me properly, someone once told me. I think she meant: don't let anyone else write the version. I think she also meant: pass it on.*

*The kettle is on the third hook. The key is under the blue pot, out of habit, though there's nothing left to lock. Come in.*

Lena read it twice. Then they folded it, put it in an envelope, and wrote the address they had been writing for months, the one that wasn't an address at all:

*To whoever comes next.*

No street. No town. No postcode. Devi had argued that an unaddressed letter was legally meaningless, and Lena had agreed, and had mailed it anyway, because it was not a legal document. It was a message in a bottle, and the sea was the future, and the point had never been delivery. The point was that somewhere in the house, in a drawer, in the record, there existed a sentence that said *you are expected.*

They left it on the hall table, weighted under the brass key—the original, the platform key, which opened nothing now except the memory of a woman in a green coat—and went to the window.

Outside, the sea was doing its ancient, unbothered work. Aldous's light was on in the guest cottage—he'd stayed the night, he stayed most nights now, sober and fumbling and slowly becoming someone his aunt might have tolerated. Corinne had gone home to the village but had texted a photograph of the leftovers with the caption *the chicken haunts me still,* and Lena had laughed alone in the kitchen, an undignified snort, and had not catalogued it, had just let it happen.

And then—unhurried, without warning, the way the best ones came now—a memory arrived.

Not the platform. Not the ledger. Not the smooth white face of a woman explaining that stories need editors. Just this: the kitchen, tonight, an hour ago. Corinne's laugh. Aldous's shoulders coming down. Devi's kettle clicking down on the hob like a small gavel calling the family to order. The dry chicken. The argument about tradition. All of it, held whole, in perfect detail, and for the first time in forty years of perfect detail, it didn't hurt.

It was just a good evening. Filed nowhere. Kept everywhere. Belonging to no archive but the one that mattered, the one that sat at the table.

Lena stood at the window until the moon came up over the water, and then they turned off the lights, one room at a time, the way you close a book you intend to open again tomorrow.

Down in the hall, in the dark, the letter waited under the brass key—addressed to no one, promised to everyone, patient as the sea.

*Come in,* it said.

And somewhere, someday, someone would.
