## 3. Entries Past the End

The Grand Meridian kept its secrets in layers, like sediment. On the surface: brass and linen, the smell of beeswax polished into banisters by men who had been dead longer than Zephyrine had been alive. Beneath that: the ledgers, the rosters, the quiet choreography of who was sleeping in whose bed under whose name. And beneath all of that—deeper than the wine cellar, deeper than the old chapel nobody used anymore—the drawer.

Zephyrine climbed the back stairs to their apartment above the carriage house with the diary inside their coat, pressed flat against their ribs like a second heartbeat. They had locked Room 411 behind them at eleven forty, logged the departure of Guest 411 as *vacated, no forwarding*, and stood for a long moment in the corridor afterward listening to the radiator tick, feeling the way a man feels when he has picked up something on a beach that turns out to be live ordnance.

Upstairs, they did not turn on the overhead light. Habit. The apartment faced the harbor road, and the harbor road carried headlights belonging to people whose comings and goings Zephyrine had spent five years cataloguing without ever once being asked to. Lamplight only. The green-shaded lamp on the desk, the one with the frayed cord, the one that made a small warm island in a dark room.

They set the diary down and did not touch it for a full minute.

This was discipline, not sentiment. Zephyrine Delacroix believed in procedure the way other people believed in prayer. There was a right way to open a guest's abandoned property: gloves, inventory, log entry, sealed envelope, safe. There was a wrong way, and the wrong way began with curiosity and ended with Cormac Bell asking why the concierge's fingerprints were on a dead girl's private papers. So Zephyrine sat with their hands folded and let the wanting pass through them like weather, and then, because they were honest with themselves if no one else, put on a pair of white cotton gloves from the housekeeping supply and opened the book anyway.

*Freya Okonkwo* was embossed in gold on the lower right corner of the cover, the letters worn nearly smooth, as though a thumb had worried them for years. The leather was oxblood, cracked along the spine, soft as an ear. It smelled of cedar and salt and something faintly chemical—preservative, maybe, or the ghost of whatever bag it had traveled in.

The first entry was dated the fourteenth of March, 2017.

Zephyrine's breath went out slowly. Fourteen months before she vanished. Two years before the parish buried its grief and paved a memorial garden over it, the little stone bench by the sea wall with FREYA carved in letters that were already going soft with lichen. Zephyrine had walked past that bench four hundred times. Had watched tourists sit on it eating chips, watched gulls defile it, watched Rector Okonkwo stand beside it every anniversary with his hands folded and his face arranged into the particular architecture of a man carrying something heavier than sorrow.

The handwriting, in those early pages, was round and confident, slanting slightly left, the hand of a girl who wrote fast because her thoughts came fast. She wrote about organ practice. About her father's Sunday sermons and how he rehearsed them in the kitchen, pacing between the stove and the table with his glasses pushed up into his hair. About a boy named Tomas who worked the crab boats and had a laugh like a dropped bucket. About boredom, mostly. The exquisite, suffocating boredom of a clever nineteen-year-old in a parish where the most exciting event of the season was the blessing of the fishing fleet.

Zephyrine read the way they did everything else—thoroughly, sideways, noting what was *not* said. And what was not said, page after page, was anything about the harbor's business after dark. Not yet.

Then, in September of 2018, the tone shifted the way weather shifts: not all at once, but you could feel the pressure drop.

*I saw Mr. Bell's car at the boathouse again. Third time this month. Papa says the Bell family has always been generous to Saint-Aubin and I should mind my own business, but Papa says that in the voice he uses when he doesn't want me to ask twice.*

Zephyrine turned the page. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the tide was working at the pilings, patient as a dentist.

The entries grew shorter. The handwriting grew smaller, tighter, as though she were trying to fit more truth into less space. She wrote about numbers she'd seen—figures copied out of a ledger left open on Bell's desk while she waited to discuss the church fundraiser. She wrote about a fisherman named Aldous Wren, whom everyone liked, who "asked too many questions at the Anchor" and then stopped coming to the Anchor. She wrote, in November:

*Aldous's boat went down off the point. Everyone says weather. The weather that night was nothing. I checked the shipping forecast twice. Papa held a memorial service and I sang the descant and I wanted to scream the whole time. If I say what I know, who listens? The sheriff is Bell's brother-in-law. The mayor plays cards with Bell every Thursday. This town is a closed fist and I am inside it.*

Zephyrine set the diary face-down on the desk and stood up and walked to the window, because their hands were shaking and they would not permit that to be true.

Below, the harbor lay black and silver, the masts of the leisure boats nodding like a congregation agreeing with a sermon. The Meridian's sign glowed its patient rose-gold. Everything was exactly as it had been five minutes ago. Nothing was exactly as it had been five minutes ago.

Because here was the thing—the thing that made the floor feel slightly tilted, that made Zephyrine grip the windowsill like a passenger on a listing ship: Freya Okonkwo had disappeared on the ninth of June, 2019. That date was carved into the parish's memory the way it was carved into the bench. The search parties. The divers off the point. The candlelight vigil where three hundred people sang *Abide With Me* and Cormac Bell wept openly, beautifully, into a handkerchief monogrammed with his initials. The memorial garden. The closed file. The settled thing, the finished thing, the grief the town had paid for in full and receipted.

And the diary in Zephyrine's hands continued past that date.

Past it by eighteen months.

Zephyrine returned to the desk and found the June entries with fingers that felt borrowed. The last entry before the ninth of June was frantic, half a page, the handwriting breaking apart like ice in spring:

*He knows I have copies. I don't know how he knows. Tomorrow night I'm going to leave them somewhere he'll never look and then I'm going to fix this. If anything happens to me, it wasn't the water.*

*It was never the water.*

The next dated entry was the eleventh of June. Two days after the disappearance. Written in the same hand—but changed. Smaller now. Compressed. A hand writing in fear, pressing hard enough to dent three pages beneath.

*I'm alive. I'm alive and I can never go home. Details later, no time. He thinks I drowned. Let him think it. Let the whole town think it. Grief makes people stupid and stupid people don't dig.*

Zephyrine sat down very slowly, the way one sits when standing has become unreliable.

They read until three in the morning.

The eighteen months unspooled like a rope paying out over a rail. Freya had gone to ground in ways that were almost funny in their modesty—a room above a launderette in Falmouth for six weeks, then a hostel, then a position cleaning holiday lets under a name she never recorded. She wrote about missing her father with a ferocity that made Zephyrine's throat ache. She wrote about watching, from a distance, the news coverage of her own memorial service, seeing Bell's face on the television among the mourners, and writing beneath it a single line: *He cried better than anyone. I almost applauded.*

But she came back. That was the part that stopped Zephyrine's heart and held it stopped. In October of 2019 she wrote that she couldn't do it anymore, the running, the erasure—and that she had realized something: *the best place to hide is inside the story everyone already believes.* She came back to Saint-Aubin before Christmas. She wrote about dyeing her hair. About changing the way she walked, the way she held her chin. About applying, through a music agency on the mainland, for the post of parish organist—"they wanted someone with no local history, someone anonymous, someone who wouldn't gossip. I was perfect."

She wrote about playing for her father's services while he searched her face for a daughter he believed was at the bottom of the sea, and finding—or choosing to find—nothing there. She wrote about the scar on her left palm, got the night she ran, climbing the sea wall in the dark, and about wearing gloves so no one would ever see it and remember.

*Papa knows,* she wrote in February 2020, the letters very small. *I told him everything. He wanted to go to the police and I made him swear not to. The sheriff would have me in the harbor within a week and my father in the dock beside me for obstruction. So now Papa lives with a ghost in his own church and preaches resurrection every Sunday and cannot say a word. God forgive me. God forgive all of us. But Bell still doesn't know I'm here, and as long as he doesn't, I'm the only witness he has.*

The final entry was dated December of 2020. After that, blank pages—dozens of them, ruled and waiting, the way a life waits when it has been interrupted.

*Something has changed. Bell has started asking questions about the organist. Small ones. Where she trained. Whether anyone's met her family. Nadia thinks I'm being paranoid but Nadia isn't the one he smiles at across the market square. If I stop writing in this book, it means I've hidden it. Look where the music sleeps.*

Zephyrine closed the diary.

The lamp hummed. The tide worked the pilings. Down the hill, the memorial bench stood in the dark with its soft-carved lie, and three hundred people's candles had guttered years ago, and the whole parish slept on top of a falsehood the way the town slept on its harbor—comfortably, generationally, without ever wondering what was underneath.

Zephyrine understood, with a vertigo that was almost physical, that they were holding the evidence. Not a clue. Not a thread. The evidence itself: a dead girl's own hand proving she had never died, proving the town's grief was theater, proving that Cormac Bell had wept at the funeral of a woman he believed he had murdered—and that she had been sitting thirty feet from him every Sunday since, playing hymns.

And proving something worse, something Zephyrine felt settle into their chest like cold water filling a boot: that whoever Guest 411 was, whoever had carried this book across the Meridian's threshold and left it behind, that person knew what it was. You did not abandon this diary by accident. You either lost it in terror, or you planted it like a seed.

Zephyrine took a fresh index card from the drawer—the real drawer, the secret one, the one that lived behind the false back of the stationery cabinet—and wrote in their small, precise, concierge's hand:

*Okonkwo, F. — ALIVE. Diary confirms. Last entry Dec 2020. "Look where the music sleeps."*

They stared at the card for a long time. Then they did something they had never done in five years of cataloguing the parish's sins: they hesitated over where to file it. Every card had a place. This one belonged nowhere, because nothing in the alphabet was built to hold a living dead girl.

In the end Zephyrine slipped it into their breast pocket, over the heart, and turned off the lamp.

Outside, far off toward the church, a single note sounded on the organ—one low pedal tone, sustained, deliberate, unmistakable even at this distance, even at this hour. Someone was practicing past midnight. Someone was awake.

Or someone was answering.

* * *

Zephyrine did not sleep. They sat by the window instead, watching the parish resolve out of darkness as the sky went the color of dishwater, and by six they had made two decisions, both of which frightened them more than the diary had.

First: they would return the diary to Room 411 before the morning shift arrived. Whatever game Guest 411 was playing, Zephyrine refused to be caught holding the piece.

Second: tomorrow was Sunday. And for the first time in five years of efficient, invisible, unnoticed Sundays, Zephyrine Delacroix was going to attend the ten o'clock service at Saint-Aubin Parish Church—not for the sermon, not for the singing, but to watch the hands of the woman at the organ.

To see whether she wore gloves.

They dressed in their best grey suit, the one they saved for funerals, which seemed suddenly, horribly appropriate, and went downstairs to return a dead girl's diary to a living girl's empty room.

The book weighed almost nothing.

It weighed everything.
