## 17. The Sheriff's Long Shadow

The payphone outside the bait shop on Route 9 had not been used, by the look of it, since the Clinton administration. Its receiver was furred with salt air and its coin slot was rusted shut, but when Zephyrine lifted it to their ear there was still a dial tone—thin and patient, like a held breath that had been holding for twenty years.

They fed in quarters from a paper cup. Their hands were steady. They had made themselves steady on purpose, standing in front of the motel mirror that morning practicing stillness the way other people practiced speeches. *You are a concerned citizen. You are no one. You are a voice on a wire.*

The number they dialed was one Nadia had scrawled on the back of a gas station receipt two days before she vanished: *Lt. Amara Osei, State Bureau of Investigation — Financial Crimes. Tell her Cormac Bell's name and watch her sit up.* Zephyrine had copied it onto an index card, then burned the receipt in the sink of their apartment and watched the ash curl like a small black prayer.

It rang four times.

"Bureau of Investigation, Osei."

A woman's voice. Flat, tired, professional—the voice of someone who answered phones the way lighthouse keepers watched horizons, expecting nothing and missing nothing.

Zephyrine spoke carefully. They had written nothing down; the words lived only in the muscle of their throat. "I'm calling about the Okonkwo case. Freya Okonkwo. Saint-Aubin parish. Reported drowned in October 2019."

A pause. Not long enough to be suspicion. Just long enough to be attention.

"Go on."

"Her body was never found. The parish declared her dead anyway. The rector signed the papers. And I'm telling you that Cormac Bell—" the name came out of them like a stone dropped into deep water, "—the manager of the Grand Meridian Hotel, was connected to her disappearance. There are ledgers. Harbor accounts routed through hotel books going back at least a decade. A fisherman named Émile Toussaint who drowned in March of 2020, three weeks after he told people he was getting out of something. His boat was found swamped off Gull Rock with the bilge pump disconnected."

Another silence. Longer this time. Zephyrine could hear, beneath the woman's breathing, the faint percussion of a keyboard—someone typing as they listened.

"Who am I speaking with?"

"A concerned citizen."

"Concerned citizens usually call the sheriff."

Zephyrine looked out through the phone booth's grimy glass at the flat gray water beyond the dunes. A heron stood in the shallows with the stillness of a thing pretending to be a stick. "Sheriff Dane Bell is Cormac Bell's brother-in-law," they said. "I'd have thought you knew that."

"I know it." The typing stopped. When Lieutenant Osei spoke again, her voice had shed some of its procedural shell. "I also know the Bureau has a file on Saint-Aubin harbor that's been gathering dust since before the pandemic. Every time we open it, something happens. Witnesses recant. Documents go missing from evidence lockers. One of my predecessors retired early and bought a house on a lake." She let that sit. "So let me ask you plainly, citizen. Do you have physical evidence? Paper?"

"I have photographs of ledgers," Zephyrine said. It was true. It was also not all of it, and the part they withheld sat in their chest like swallowed glass. "And I know where more can be found."

"Then here's what happens next, so you're not surprised by it. I open a preliminary inquiry. Standard protocol for a tip involving a sitting sheriff requires me to notify the local jurisdiction within seventy-two hours—a courtesy notification, nothing more. Which means Sheriff Bell will know someone called about his brother-in-law inside of three days." Her voice was dry as old rope. "I'm telling you this because I want you to understand the arithmetic of this parish. The law is a road here, and every road has tolls. If you have evidence, get it somewhere the tolls can't reach. A newspaper. A federal office. Get it out of the county."

"And the inquiry?"

"The inquiry is real," Osei said. "Whether it survives the trip is another question. Thank you for calling."

The line went dead with a click that sounded, in the empty booth, almost like a door closing.

Zephyrine hung up the receiver and stood a moment in the salt-smelling dark of the booth, watching the heron. Then they took out a handkerchief and wiped everything they had touched—the receiver, the coin slot, the folding door—and walked back to their car without looking at the road, looking instead at the water, where the tide was going out and exposing, inch by inch, all the things the harbor preferred to keep covered.

* * *

Seventy-two hours. Osei had said it like a warning label, and Zephyrine had counted the hours the way a patient counts the hours before surgery.

Day one, they did nothing. That was deliberate too. They worked the desk at the Grand Meridian with their usual seamless grace, checking in a wedding party from Baton Rouge, resolving a dispute over a broken air conditioner, smiling at Cormac Bell across the lobby's marble expanse while the diaries' absence sat between them like a third person neither would acknowledge. Bell had the diaries now; Zephyrine had the copies, mailed in brown paper to three addresses—a journalist in the state capital, the Bureau's internal affairs office, and a post office box in New Orleans belonging, according to Nadia's research, to a retired federal prosecutor who had once made a career of eating small-town empires. The letters were already gone. Whatever happened next, the truth was loose in the world, traveling at the speed of the postal service.

But knowing that did not stop Zephyrine from watching the sheriff.

That was the strange compulsion of it. They wanted to see the machinery work. They wanted, with a hunger that embarrassed them, to believe that the phone call had set something honest in motion—that somewhere in the parish, unseen, the gears of justice were beginning to turn with clean oil and good intentions.

Sheriff Dane Bell was easy to find because he made himself easy to find. He came into the Grand Meridian every morning at nine-fifteen for coffee he did not drink, taking the same corner table in the Palm Court with its view of both entrances, and he stayed forty minutes doing a crossword he never finished. He was a broad, sunburned man with his brother-in-law's heavy jaw and none of Cormac's silk; where Cormac moved like poured cream, Dane moved like furniture being rearranged. He tipped his hat to the staff. He remembered names. He had, five years ago, personally delivered the news of Freya Okonkwo's drowning to the rector's door, and Zephyrine had watched him do it through the lobby window, hat in hand, grief arranged on his face like flowers at a funeral he had ordered himself.

On day two, at nine-forty, a gray sedan with state plates pulled into the hotel's circular drive.

Zephyrine saw it from behind the front desk and felt their heart perform a single hard knock against their ribs. A woman got out—tall, close-cropped hair, a blazer that had seen weather—and stood a moment looking up at the hotel's façade with the expression of a person reading a document. Lieutenant Osei. She had come herself. Hope rose in Zephyrine like water finding a crack.

Osei did not come inside. She walked the drive slowly, once around, looking at the boathouse path, the harbor stairs, the service entrance where the tunnels began. Then she drove to the sheriff's office on Cane Street, and Zephyrine, on their lunch break, walked past it twice.

Through the office's tall windows they saw it happen. Saw Osei at the dispatch counter, talking to a deputy. Saw Sheriff Bell come out of his inner room with his hat already on, which meant he had heard the car, or been told, or simply known the way animals know weather. Saw the two of them speak in the doorway—saw Bell laugh, actually laugh, a big warm sound Zephyrine could not hear but could read perfectly in the set of his shoulders—and saw him clap Osei on the arm like an old friend, and gesture her inside, and close the door.

The blinds came down.

Zephyrine stood on the sidewalk in the flat white heat of noon, in front of the parish's war memorial with its list of names from wars that had at least ended, and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, exactly what they were watching. Not an investigation. A courtesy. The toll being collected.

* * *

Day three, Zephyrine called the Bureau again from the same rusted booth, and this time Osei picked up on the second ring, as if she had been waiting.

"It's done," Zephyrine said. No preamble. They had learned that much about her.

"This morning," she said. "Notification logged at eight-twelve a.m. Sheriff Bell acknowledged receipt at eight-fifteen." A pause. "Three minutes. In my experience, a sheriff who's surprised by a notification takes longer than three minutes to acknowledge it. He didn't even print it first."

"What does that mean?"

"It means your sheriff already knew the inquiry was coming before my paperwork reached him." Her voice was level, but underneath it Zephyrine heard something that sounded like disgust wearing professionalism as a coat. "I'll tell you what else it means, and then I'm going to say some things I probably shouldn't say on an open line. The preliminary file I opened has been flagged. *Flagged*, citizen—not by me. Someone above my rank reassigned it to a joint review with the parish district attorney's office. The DA is Cormac Bell's golf partner. I have watched this happen four times in six years, and each time I have filled out the forms complaining about it, and each time the forms have gone into a drawer in a building in the capital where drawers go to die." She exhaled, long and slow. "You asked me once if the inquiry was real. It was real. So is the tide. Neither one cares what it covers."

Zephyrine leaned their forehead against the cool metal wall of the booth. Outside, evening was coming down over the marshes, bruising the sky purple along the treeline. Somewhere a boat engine coughed and caught.

"So that's it," they said. "That's the whole of it. We tell the truth and it dies in an inbox."

"Not the whole of it." Osei's voice sharpened. "Listen to me, whoever you are, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to hang up and pretend this conversation was about fishing licenses. The system I serve has doors, and most of them in this parish are locked from the inside. But the system is bigger than the parish. There are people in the capital who hate Cormac Bell's kind of quiet—not for virtue, mostly, but because his kind of quiet makes their jobs harder, and spite is the most reliable fuel in government. Your letters, if they landed where you sent them, will reach those people. But letters alone won't hold. Letters get filed. What holds is a witness. What holds is a face, a voice, a living person standing in a courtroom saying *I saw it* while the cameras run." She paused. "You said you know where more evidence can be found. Find the person it happened to. Dead men make bad witnesses, but a woman the parish buried alive—that's not a witness, that's a resurrection. Nobody files a resurrection."

The line clicked. Dial tone.

Zephyrine stood in the darkening booth with the receiver against their ear long after she was gone, listening to the hum of the wire like it was a seashell holding the sound of the whole indifferent sea.

*She's alive.* Osei didn't know it—couldn't—but she had just handed Zephyrine the sentence they had been circling for days without daring to write down. Everything came back to the organ loft. To the woman with gloved hands thirty feet from the pulpit. To the choice Zephyrine had been carrying like a stone in each pocket: the law, or Freya. Justice, or the fragile life of the only person in Saint-Aubin who had ever truly escaped.

And now the law had shown them its face. Three minutes. Blinds drawn. A file flagged into a drawer where drawers go to die.

Zephyrine hung up the phone. Drove home through the blue dusk with the windows down, smelling the incoming tide, thinking about the hymnal's penciled warning—*Stop before they stop you*—and understanding it now not as fear but as expertise. Freya had tested these waters years ago. Freya had learned what Zephyrine had learned in seventy-two hours flat: that in Saint-Aubin, justice was not a door left locked. It was a door painted on a wall, and the whole town had agreed to walk through it, one after another, year after year, and call the bruise on their foreheads progress.

There was one door left open. It was narrow, and it was dangerous, and it went through the organ loft at midnight.

Zephyrine parked outside their apartment and sat in the car a moment, watching the lit window of the unit across the courtyard where old Mrs. Adeyemi watered her ferns, ordinary and unafraid. Tomorrow was Sunday. Tomorrow there would be a service, a congregation, a hymn.

Tomorrow, Zephyrine decided, they would stay for the whole thing. And afterward, when the nave emptied and the last of the daylight drained through the stained glass, they would climb the stairs to the loft and refuse, this time, to leave until the gloves came off.

The tide was coming in. Somewhere out past Gull Rock, under all that returning water, the truth waited with the patience of the drowned—and it was done waiting politely.
