## 18. The Bell Tolls Wrong

The tunnels beneath the Grand Meridian smelled of salt and old iron, of decades of damp rising through limestone the way memory rises through a body. I had walked them a thousand times in five years—delivering forgotten umbrellas, chasing runaway steam, learning the hotel's skeleton the way other people learned scripture. Tonight the skeleton felt like it was walking beside me.

I had been summoned by a note slipped under my apartment door at eleven o'clock. No signature. Just a time and a place written in a hand too neat for Cormac Bell: *Tunnel C, midnight. Bring what belongs to the hotel.*

*What belongs to the hotel.* As if the diary had ever been his. As if anything in this parish belonged to anyone but the families who had owned it since before the harbor silted.

I went anyway. Not because I was brave—I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps for concierges and liars—but because Nadia had been gone four days, and because the single index card on her pillow had been card number 217 in my own private alphabet: *Okonkwo, F. — organ loft, gloves, left hand.* Someone had been inside my drawer. Inside my head. And the only way to find out how deep they'd reached was to walk into the dark and let them show me.

The tunnel lamps were on timers, half of them dead. My footsteps came back to me doubled, tripled, a congregation of one. Somewhere ahead water dripped with the patience of a thing that had all eternity and no conscience.

He was waiting under the third working lamp, sitting on an overturned crate as though he'd been there since the hotel poured its foundations. Cormac Bell wore his gray suit the way he wore everything—like it had been tailored by the building itself. His hands were folded. His face was pleasant. That was always the worst part about Bell; he looked like a man who remembered your mother's name and asked after her garden.

"Zephyrine." He said it warmly. "You look tired."

"I keep irregular hours." My voice came out steadier than my hands felt. I kept them in my coat pockets, where secrets live.

"We all do, lately." He gestured to the crate opposite him. An invitation. I didn't take it. Standing seemed important; sitting felt like signing something. Bell shrugged and continued as if we were both comfortable. "Do you know how old these tunnels are? Older than the hotel. The rum-runners used them in the twenties. Then the Okonkwo girl's grandfather used them for—well. Different cargo, same current." He smiled at some private joke the harbor had told him. "Everything in Saint-Aubin flows through here eventually. Money. Grief. People who ask questions."

"Is that why you asked me down? To give me the history?"

"No." He unfolded his hands, and the warmth drained out of his voice like tide off a flat. "I asked you down because a friend of mine in the sheriff's office tells me an anonymous tip reached a state investigator last week. Very detailed. Ledgers, dates, a drowned fisherman named Aubert. And because that same investigator's supervisor received a package yesterday that I'm told contains photographs." He tilted his head. "Photographs of things that belong to this hotel."

The dripping filled the silence. I counted the drops the way I counted everything—seven per minute, steady as a pulse—and made myself meet his eyes.

"If you already know," I said, "why am I here?"

"Because knowing and having are different animals, Zeph—" he caught himself on the familiarity, corrected it with a thin smile, "—Ms. Delacroix. I know you're clever. Clever people make copies. Copies can be found. But the originals—the originals have a sentimental value. A evidentiary value. And I'm a sentimental man."

"You're a murderer."

The word fell between us and neither of us flinched, which told me everything about how far past pretending we both were. Bell sighed, genuinely regretful, a man disappointed by a hotel review.

"Aubert's death was an accident of enthusiasm," he said. "Mine, not his. He wanted out, and out wasn't something the operation offered. Freya Okonkwo understood that better than anyone—that's why she's not dead, whatever the headstone says." He watched me absorb it. He *wanted* me to absorb it. "Yes. I know you know. You've known for weeks, and you've done nothing with it except photocopy paper and mail envelopes, and do you know why nothing has happened? Because paper is slow, Ms. Delacroix. Paper goes through inboxes, and inboxes go through brothers-in-law, and brothers-in-law go through me. By the time your photographs reach anyone with authority, there will be no operation, no witnesses, and no parish worth saving. There will just be a hotel, and a concierge position, open."

"There it is," I said quietly. "The offer."

"The offer." He stood, brushing nothing from his trousers. "Lifetime post. Your salary doubled. Your little drawer of index cards becomes an institution—you'd be the keeper of this town's whole confession, untouchable, permanent. Every secret in Saint-Aubin flowing through you, protected. Isn't that what you've always wanted? To be noticed?" His voice softened into something almost kind, which was the cruelest register he owned. "Five years watching everyone and being seen by no one. I see you, Zephyrine. I've always seen you. That's what frightens you—not me. Being *known*."

And God forgive me, for one full second, it worked. The tunnel lamp hummed and the water dripped and I stood in the wet dark being *seen* by the most dangerous man in the parish, and some starved animal in my chest turned toward the light of it like a plant toward a window. Five years of invisible competence. Five years of holding everyone's sins and being handed nothing back but tips and thanks and the occasional complaint about slow elevators.

Then I thought of Nadia's empty room. Of the index card on the pillow, placed there like a signature on a painting—*we were here, we read everything, we own the alphabet now.* Of Freya's gloved hands on the organ keys, playing hymns over the grave the town dug for her while she was still breathing.

"You're right," I said. "That's exactly what I wanted. Which is how I know it's a lie. People who get everything they want from Cormac Bell end up like Aubert. Or like Nadia."

Something moved behind his eyes—satisfaction, maybe, or its cousin. "Nadia," he repeated, tasting the name. "Lovely woman. Terrible driver, apparently. Left her car at the ferry lot and just... went. People do that. They get frightened, they run, they stop writing." He smiled, and the smile did the work his words wouldn't. "Of course, she might surface. If certain items were returned to their rightful owner, I imagine she'd hear the weather changed and come home. People always come home when it's safe." He spread his hands. "So. The diaries. Both of them. Tonight, and none of this ever touches you. Refuse, and—" he glanced up at the tunnel ceiling, toward the lobby, toward the church two streets over whose bells I could almost hear, "—well. You've seen how things toll wrong in this parish when they're not maintained."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit.

I gave him the diaries.

Not because I was broken. Not because I believed his promises—a man who drowns fishermen does not honor contracts. I gave them to him because of arithmetic. Because Nadia had been gone four days and every hour past the fourth made the odds worse. Because Bell had shown me his hand: he feared the *originals*, which meant the originals mattered, which meant whoever held them held leverage, and leverage in Bell's possession was a gun pointed at everyone I loved, while leverage in mine was a target painted on my back alone. Better one back than many. Better a trade than a grave.

So I reached inside my coat and brought out the bundle wrapped in a linen napkin from Room 204—Freya's first diary, its cracked leather spine warm from riding against my ribs, and the second diary from beneath the chapel floor, still smelling of earth and candle smoke. I had carried them everywhere for six days. Slept with them. Read them until I knew Freya's handwriting better than my own. Handing them over was like handing over a lung.

Bell took them with reverence, actually reverent, turning the pages with clean dry fingers. "Both halves," he murmured. "The whole confession. You have no idea what a relief this is, Ms. Delacroix. For everyone."

"I want proof she's alive," I said. "Before I walk out of this tunnel. One sign. A word from her. Anything."

He closed the diaries and looked at me with something that in a better man would have been pity. "You're in no position to want things."

"Then consider it professional courtesy. From one keeper of secrets to another."

For a long moment the only sound was the drip, seven per minute, and I thought: *he'll kill me here, under the hotel, and in the morning Cormac Bell will attend my memorial service and say something gracious about attention to detail.* But then he tucked the diaries inside his coat and said, lightly:

"She plays the eleven o'clock service Sunday. Same as always. Gloves and all." He stepped past me, close enough that I could smell his cologne fighting the tunnel's rot. "Come listen, if you like. It's a lovely piece. Bach. She chose it herself." At the mouth of the passage he paused, silhouetted against the distant yellow of the service stairwell. "Oh—and Zephyrine. Give my regards to the rector. Tell him the maintenance issue is resolved."

His footsteps faded. The lamp above me buzzed, flickered, and steadied.

I stood alone in Tunnel C for a long time, doing the arithmetic again, the part I hadn't shown him. Because Bell had taken the diaries, yes—but I had let him believe the photographs were all he needed to fear, and men like Cormac Bell always believe the paper is the danger. He never once asked about the envelope I'd mailed Tuesday morning, the one addressed not to the investigator's supervisor but to a journalist in the state capital, the one containing not photographs but a letter, signed, dated, and sworn, telling a story that began with a drowned fisherman and ended with a missing woman who played the organ at eleven o'clock every Sunday.

Paper is slow, Bell had said. He was right. But Sunday was three days away, and bells, whatever else you say about them, are fast.

I climbed the service stairs into the cold ordinary light of the hotel corridor, and I began, at last, to write the plan in the drawer.
