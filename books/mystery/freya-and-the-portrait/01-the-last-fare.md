## 1. The Last Fare

The radio crackled at 2:47 a.m., and Mirabel Lockridge knew before she keyed the mic that the man on the other end was going to cry.

She'd learned to hear it coming years ago — the particular texture of a pause that wasn't hesitation but gathering. Unit 44 had been quiet for eleven minutes, long enough for the driver, a kid named Tomas on his third week, to finish his fare and pull over somewhere dark. When he came back on the air, his voice would have that thin, stretched quality, like wire about to snap.

"Dispatch, this is 44." Yep. There it was. "You, uh. You copy?"

"Copy, 44. Go ahead."

A breath. In the background, wind against an open window and the distant wash of traffic on the parkway. "I just dropped a guy off at St. Vincent's. Hospice wing. He — we talked the whole ride, you know? He told me about his daughter. Showed me pictures on his phone. And then when I stopped he shook my hand like we were — like we were friends or something, and he said, *take care of yourself, son.*" Another breath, shakier. "And I just sat there after he got out and I thought, that's what people say when they're not coming back. That's a goodbye sentence. And I don't know why that's messing me up so bad, but—"

"Tomas." She kept her voice level, warm in the way she'd practiced until it stopped being practice. "Pull over if you haven't. Take five. You did right by him. Sometimes the last kind thing a person hears is from a stranger, and you were that stranger tonight. That's not nothing."

Silence on the line. Then, thick: "Thanks, Dispatch."

"44, clear for five minutes. Log it as a comfort stop."

"Clear for five. Thanks, M — Dispatch."

Mirabel set the mic down and looked out the dispatch window at the lot below, where the taxis sat in their rows under sodium lights, beaded with the first cold rain of October. Eleven years on nights. She could read a driver's whole biography in the shape of his silences. She could talk a panicking rookie through a diabetic passenger, talk a drunk down from a fight, talk a grieving widow through the ride home from the funeral home without either of them acknowledging what the ride was. What she could not do, she was beginning to suspect, was feel anything at regular size.

The griefs of strangers came through her headset every night, and she handled them like change from a fare — counted out, correct, gone.

Her desk was a fortress of habit: two monitors, a mug of coffee gone the color of creek water, a paper log she kept even though the digital system made it redundant, because the paper log was hers, and because writing things down by hand was the closest thing she had to prayer. The wall above her held a corkboard of pinned maps, annotated in her small square handwriting — road closures, detours, the bar district's closing times, the hospital's ambulance bay hours. She knew this city between midnight and six better than she knew her own face.

At 3:15, the phone rang. Not the radio. The landline, the old direct line that only the company office used during business hours, which meant nobody should be using it now.

"Dispatch."

"Mirabel?" A woman's voice, older, careful. It took her a second to place it — Marion, from the office, who processed the paperwork and remembered everyone's birthdays and whom Mirabel had spoken to perhaps forty times in a decade, always about shift swaps.

"It's three in the morning, Marion."

"I know. I know it is. I've been sitting here trying to figure out how late is too late, and then I figured there isn't a good time for this, so." A breath. "Mirabel, honey, I saw it in the obituaries tonight when I was doing the accounts. I checked twice because I couldn't — well. It's your aunt. Freya Lockridge. It says she passed on Tuesday. At home."

The coffee mug was halfway to her mouth. Mirabel set it back down with a small click, precise, deliberate, the way she did everything.

"Okay," she said.

"I'm so sorry. Were you two close?"

Were you two close. The question hung there, absurd and enormous. Freya Lockridge lived four hours north, in a lake town Mirabel hadn't seen since she was nineteen. They spoke maybe twice a year — a card at Christmas, always the same heavy cream stock, always signed *Freya* in handwriting like copperplate engraving, no love, no x's, just the name, as if signing a legal document. Once, when Mirabel was small, Freya had visited for a weekend and taught her to skip stones, saying almost nothing the entire afternoon, and it had been the best day of that summer. Her mother, Ruth, had watched from the porch with a glass in her hand and an expression Mirabel had been too young to name and had spent thirty years declining to name since.

"No," Mirabel said. "Not close. But thank you for calling, Marion. Really."

"There's something else. There was an envelope here for you — came with the office mail yesterday. Heavy paper. Your name and the dispatch address. I didn't open it, obviously, but given the news, I thought—" Marion hesitated. "It might be from the family. About services."

"Services."

"The obituary says the funeral is Saturday. Up there. Harrow Lake, it looks like. I can read you the arrangements if you want them."

Harrow Lake. The word went through Mirabel like a fishhook finding old scar tissue — a tug from somewhere deep, meaningless on the surface, impossible to ignore. She hadn't heard that name spoken aloud in twenty years. She'd never known why her mother went quiet whenever it came up on the news, some drowning or other, some lake town in trouble. Ruth Lockridge had died nine years ago, liver done at fifty-four, and had taken her silences with her into the ground, all of them, every one.

"Saturday," Mirabel repeated. "Yeah. Read me the rest."

Marion read them: the funeral home, the church, the hour. Then: "Do you want me to put the envelope in your cubby? You can get it Friday when you come in for payroll."

"You could open it," Mirabel heard herself say. "If it's about the funeral, I'd rather know now than wait two days."

A pause on the line. Marion was a rule-follower; Mirabel could hear her weighing it. "It's addressed to you, honey. I shouldn't."

"Right. Of course. Cubby's fine."

They hung up, and Mirabel sat in the blue glow of her monitors while the rain thickened against the window, and tried to locate herself inside the news. Aunt Freya was dead. Freya, who sent cards in engraved script. Freya, who had once skipped stones with a silent child on a gray northern beach. Freya, who had fought with Ruth at their grandmother's funeral in a whisper-fight Mirabel had caught only fragments of — *you don't get to decide that* — before both women turned smiling faces toward the mourners like lamps snapping on.

Freya was dead, and Mirabel felt — what?

She ran an inventory, the way she'd inventory a driver's status. Sad? The word sat there and refused to attach to anything. Numb, certainly; numb was her native weather. Curious, yes, uncomfortably so — curiosity about a death felt like a moral failure, so she shelved it. Something else underneath, some low vibration she couldn't tune. Like a frequency bleeding through from a channel she didn't subscribe to.

At 4:30, unit 27 called in a fare dispute; she resolved it. At 5:10, a nurse needed a standing ride from the hospital and Mirabel sent the car and listened to the driver, old Sal, hum to himself the whole way, off-key, unbothered, and envied him with her whole flat heart. At 5:55 the sky over the east side went the color of dishwater, and the night shift began handing itself over to the day.

* * *

She slept until one in the afternoon, dreamless, the sleep of the professionally exhausted, and woke to rain still ticking on the fire escape and her phone showing two notifications. One was a voicemail from a number she didn't recognize, left at 9:02 a.m. The other was a text from Marion: *Envelope is in your cubby. Also a lawyer called the office asking how to reach next of kin for F. Lockridge. I gave him nothing. Thought you'd want to know.*

Mirabel played the voicemail. A man's voice, smooth and unhurried, with the rounded vowels of someone who had spent decades being listened to: *"Good morning. This message is for Mirabel Lockridge. My name is Douglas Devane, calling on behalf of the family of Freya Lockridge. We understand you may not have been aware of the arrangements, and we wanted to make certain you received an invitation. The service is Saturday at eleven, St. Brigid's in Harrow Lake. Freya spoke of you, Ms. Lockridge, more than you might expect. We do hope you'll come. Please call if I can answer any questions."*

He left the number twice, unhurried, courteous, complete.

Mirabel sat on the edge of her bed with the phone in her hand and the rain on the glass and thought: *who are you?* Not Devane himself — though the name snagged somewhere too, another hook in old wood — but the voice. The warmth of it. Family lawyers didn't call dispatch offices at dawn to personally invite estranged nieces. Estranged nieces got the obituary, or nothing. Someone had told this man her name. Someone had told him where she worked.

Or — she made herself consider it — Freya had. *Freya spoke of you.* Past tense, chosen carefully by a careful man. Maybe her aunt, in her last months, in her last weeks, had gone looking down the family tree and found only Mirabel at the end of the branch, and had reached out the way dying people do, belatedly, awkwardly, through intermediaries.

Maybe. The word did a lot of work. It always had, in this family.

She got up, showered, dressed in her off-duty uniform of jeans and a gray sweater, and drove to the office in daylight, which felt transgressive, like wearing pajamas to church. The building was different in daylight — smaller, shabbier, the dispatch room just a room. Her cubby held a single envelope.

Heavy cream stock. She knew it before she touched it. The same stock as twenty years of Christmas cards.

Her name was typed. Not handwritten — typed, centered near the top of the envelope, in a clean serif font. No return address. Postmark smudged but legible: the local sorting facility, dated Wednesday. The day after Freya died.

Mirabel stood in the empty office holding the envelope and did not open it. This was the part she would think about later, in the bad hours — that she stood there for a full minute, turning it over, feeling its single sheet of weight, and genuinely considered dropping it in the trash bin by the copier unread. Because envelopes like this were never good. Because the last time family news had found her at work, it had been Ruth, face-down in a motel bathtub two towns over, identified by a maid. Because she was forty-one years old and had built her entire life on the principle that other people's emergencies could be managed if you kept your hands steady and your voice level, and this envelope was addressed to her, by name, and there was no headset protocol for it.

She opened it in her car, in the parking lot, with the wipers going.

One page. Typed. She read it standing up, and the parking lot went strange around her, the rain suddenly very loud on the roof.

*Ms. Lockridge,* it began. *By the time you read this, Freya Lockridge will be dead, and I will be the reason. I am not writing to confess to the police. I am writing because you deserve to know what your aunt died for, and what your mother carried, and what happened at Harrow Lake in the summer of 1994. Three teenagers stood on a dock and let a boy drown. Your mother was one of them. Freya was another. Ask about the third. Ask about D.*

There was more — dates, a reference to records, a promise that *the proof exists and always has* — but Mirabel read those lines four times without absorbing them, because the fourth line kept restarting in her head like a stuck record. *Your mother was one of them.*

Ruth. Ruth, who cried at commercials. Ruth, who wouldn't let Mirabel take swimming lessons and never explained why. Ruth, who drank herself to death in a town a hundred miles from anywhere, whose last coherent words to her daughter, in the hospital, had been a slurred and desperate *don't go up there, promise me, whatever happens, don't go up there* — and Mirabel had assumed she meant heaven, or God, or nothing, the way drunks meant nothing.

Don't go up there.

Harrow Lake was north. Four hours north. Where Freya had lived alone for thirty years in a shuttered cottage on the estate side of the water, keeping to herself, sending cream-colored cards, skipping stones with a child on a gray beach and saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing at all.

Mirabel became aware, distantly, that she was shaking. Not crying — shaking, the way machinery shakes, fine and constant, a bearing going out somewhere deep. She folded the letter along its original creases with hands that wanted to crumple it, and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where she could feel it like a second heartbeat.

Through the windshield, the rain was easing. Across the lot, one of the day-shift cabs pulled out with a fare, taillights blooming red in the wet, and Mirabel watched it go and thought about Tomas, and the hospice man, and *take care of yourself, son* — goodbye sentences, sentences people say when they're not coming back.

Freya had mailed this the day after she died. Which meant that before she died — days before, maybe hours — she had known this letter was coming. Had written it, or approved it, or arranged it. Had sent it to the one person in the family young enough to care and stubborn enough to act.

*Ask about the third. Ask about D.*

Mirabel started the car. She had a funeral to attend on Saturday, four hours north, at a lake her mother had forbidden her with her dying breath.

She was going to ask.
