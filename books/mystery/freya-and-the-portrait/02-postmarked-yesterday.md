## 2. Postmarked Yesterday

The funeral was held on a Wednesday, which struck Mirabel as wrong. Freya Lockridge had been a Sunday kind of woman — the sort who made you drive out to her cottage for pancakes and then interrogated you about your life over the griddle until you confessed something true. A Wednesday funeral felt like a clerical error, like the universe had filed her under the wrong day and couldn't be bothered to correct it.

Mirabel arrived twelve minutes late because a jackknifed semi had closed Route 9 and she'd spent twenty minutes rerouting three of her own drivers around it by habit before remembering she wasn't working. She was still in her dispatch headset when she parked. She sat in the taxi company's loaner car — her own had died in January — and pulled the headset off slowly, like peeling away a bandage from skin that hadn't healed underneath.

The church was small and white-clapboarded, the kind with a steeple that leaned two degrees east and had leaned that way since before anyone alive could remember. Mirabel had never been inside it. She'd grown up ninety minutes south of Harrow Lake, but her mother had kept the family at arm's length the way you keep a stove at arm's length after it's burned you once, and so Mirabel's childhood had contained no aunts, no cousins, no white churches with leaning steeples. Just Ruth Lockridge and her wine glasses rinsed clean and hidden under the sink, and long silences that Mirabel had learned to fill with radio chatter.

She slipped into the back pew. The organ was playing something mournful and slightly off-key, or maybe the organist was grieving too.

The front rows were full of people she didn't know, which shouldn't have surprised her and did anyway. Freya had been seventy-one. There should have been decades of accumulated people — students she'd taught, neighbors she'd fed, friends from whatever life she'd built up here in the lake country. Instead the pews held perhaps forty souls, most of them gray, several of them wearing the particular expression of people doing arithmetic.

Mirabel recognized the expression. She heard it every night on the radio, in the pauses between addresses: *what do I get out of this.*

* * *

The service was brief. A pastor who clearly hadn't known Freya read a psalm and said she was "a woman of strong convictions," which was the kind of sentence that could mean anything from *she believed in charity* to *she died holding evidence against someone powerful.* The eulogy was delivered by a man in his sixties with silver hair combed back from a tanned forehead and a voice like warm honey poured over gravel. He spoke about Freya's generosity, her fierce intelligence, her love of the lake.

"I had the privilege of knowing Freya for most of my life," he said, and his eyes went bright and wet at exactly the right moment. "She was the last thread connecting us to a summer none of us will ever forget."

Something in the phrasing snagged on Mirabel like a fishhook. *A summer none of us will ever forget.* Not *the summers we shared.* Not *our childhood.* A single summer, held up like a specimen under glass.

The pastor identified him afterward as Judge Douglas Devane, retired, "a dear family friend." Devane worked the receiving line like a politician, gripping hands, bowing his head, letting the widow-adjacent women pat his forearm. When Mirabel passed him — she'd hung back until the line thinned, intending simply to sign the book and leave — he took her hand in both of his and held it a beat too long.

"You must be Mirabel," he said. "Freya spoke of you."

"She did?" Mirabel hadn't spoken to Freya in eleven years. The last conversation had ended with Freya saying, *You sound just like your mother used to, before,* and hanging up, and Mirabel deciding not to call back.

"Oh, yes." Devane smiled. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes, which were pale blue and very steady. "She was proud of you. The work you do — listening to people. That's a rare gift." He squeezed her hand once more and released it. "Your mother would have been proud too."

He moved on down the line before Mirabel could answer, and she stood there with her hand still tingling, trying to work out why the compliment had felt like being measured for something.

* * *

The reception was held in the church basement, where folding tables bore platters of ham sandwiches and a lemonade pitcher sweating rings onto the tablecloth. This was where the circling began.

It started subtly. A woman named Carol — second cousin, she announced within four seconds of introducing herself — asked Mirabel whether Freya had ever mentioned "arrangements." A heavyset man with a golf tan asked if Mirabel was "the executor or just next of kin," as though those were the only two categories of person a funeral could contain. An elderly couple cornered her by the lemonade and spent ten minutes establishing that they had visited Freya *every* Christmas, *every one*, including the last one, had she mentioned that?

Mirabel answered in her dispatcher voice — calm, neutral, professionally warm — and watched them realize, one by one, that she knew nothing, controlled nothing, and was therefore worthless. They peeled away from her like leaves off a wet windshield. By the end of the hour she was standing alone by the sandwich platters, watching Carol and the golf-tan man conduct a huddled conference near the coffee urn, their heads bent together, their glances flicking toward the door whenever anyone new arrived.

*They're not grieving,* she thought. *They're auditing.*

And the terrible thing, the thing that lodged under her ribs like a swallowed stone, was that she felt nothing about it. No anger, no shame. Just the flat professional recognition of a pattern, the same part of her brain that could hear a caller's address before they finished giving it. She had spent so many years feeling other people's emergencies on behalf of strangers that when an emergency of her own finally arrived, she could only process it as data.

*Freya is dead. You are at her funeral. You feel nothing.*

Except that wasn't quite true either. Because twice during the reception — once when the pastor said Freya's name and once when someone laughed near the kitchen doorway with a certain rusty, honking quality that sounded nothing like Freya and somehow everything like her — Mirabel's chest had done something strange and painful, a kind of structural failure, brief and total, like ice cracking underfoot. And both times she had breathed through it the way she talked panicked callers through CPR, counting the rhythm, keeping her voice level, waiting for the paramedics of routine to arrive.

She left before the sandwiches ran out.

* * *

Freya's lawyer turned out to operate out of a converted house on Elm Street with a brass plaque and a waiting room that smelled of carpet shampoo from 1998. His name was Gerald Pruitt, and he looked at Mirabel over his reading glasses with the weary air of a man who had seen this exact scene many times and found it disappointing each time.

"The will is straightforward," he said. "The cottage and its contents go to you. The savings — modest, I'll say — are divided among four charities. There's no contested estate here, Ms. Lockridge, whatever your relatives may be hoping."

"Carol thinks there might be."

"Carol thinks a lot of things." He slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. "You're the sole heir. Congratulations, or condolences. In my experience the two arrive together." He tapped the paper. "There's also this. Freya instructed me to give it to you personally, not mail it. She was specific about that. She said—" he consulted a note card, frowning, "—she said, *'Tell her to read it alone, and tell her not to throw it away, even though she'll want to.'*"

"What is it?"

"That's between you and Freya." He sat back. "Though I'll say this much, since you've got the look of someone who won't sleep tonight otherwise. She brought it in eight months ago. Sealed. Told me to hold it until — and I quote — 'it's needed.' I assumed it was sentimental. A letter she'd written and thought better of sending." He removed his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I've been practicing law thirty-four years, Ms. Lockridge. I know what a confession looks like. That envelope had the weight of one."

Mirabel took the envelope. It was ordinary — manila, typed label, her name and address printed in a courier font. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady. Twenty years of night shifts had taught them that steadiness was not the same thing as calm; it was just what hands did when the mind went somewhere else to work.

"Thank you," she said, and walked out into the gray afternoon.

* * *

She didn't open it in the parking lot. She didn't open it in the loaner car outside the church, where she sat for forty minutes while rain began stippling the windshield. She drove back to her apartment — a third-floor walkup above a shuttered laundromat, chosen precisely because nobody would ever visit — and set the envelope on the kitchen table, and made coffee she didn't drink, and stood looking at it the way you look at a door you suspect has something behind it.

Pruitt was wrong, she told herself. Old people wrote letters. Old people sealed things with lawyers. It was probably a recipe. Freya's dill bread, or instructions about the furnace, or — this was the thought that almost made her smile — a list of the relatives worth trusting, which would be a very short list, possibly blank.

But Pruitt had practiced law for thirty-four years, and he knew the weight of a confession, and he had still handed it over with both hands, the way you hand someone something breakable.

Mirabel picked up the letter, carried it to the trash can beside the counter, and held it over the mouth of the bin.

The lid was open. Inside she could see yesterday's grounds, a teabag, the crumpled wrapper from the sandwich she'd bought at a gas station on the drive up. Ordinary trash. The letter would fit right in. Nobody would ever know. She could go back to her shifts and her routes and her callers' emergencies, and Freya could stay dead in the simple, uncomplicated way that people were supposed to stay dead, and the stone under her ribs could remain exactly the size it was instead of growing.

*Tell her not to throw it away, even though she'll want to.*

Eleven years of silence, and Freya had still known her well enough to predict this exact moment, standing at this exact trash can, in some future she wouldn't live to see. That was either love or surveillance, and with Freya it had always been hard to tell the difference.

Mirabel lowered the letter onto the table instead.

She slit it open with a paring knife. Two sheets, typewritten — actually typewritten, the letters unevenly inked, one *e* riding higher than its neighbors. No signature. No salutation. It began without preamble, mid-thought, like a transmission already in progress:

*Mirabel Lockridge. If you are reading this, then Freya gave it to you, which means she trusted you with it, which means I can trust you with it too, because we wanted the same thing even if we never met.*

*I killed Freya Lockridge.*

The kitchen went very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere below, a car passed through standing water.

*I am dying. The doctors give me months, and the doctors are optimists. I am not writing this to bargain or to explain myself into forgiveness. I am writing it because the truth should not die when I do, and because you deserve to know what happened to your aunt, and why, and what your mother had to do with it.*

Mirabel's eyes stopped on the line. Stopped the way a car stops when the road ahead simply isn't there anymore.

*Thirty years ago, in the summer of 1994, a boy drowned at Harrow Lake, at the old Devane estate. He was fifteen years old. His name was Danny. Three teenagers were on the dock when it happened, and none of them called for help, and all of them helped bury what really occurred. One of them was Freya. One of them was Douglas Devane, who is now a judge, and who has spent thirty years making sure the truth stayed buried deeper than the boy did.*

*And one of them was Ruth Lockridge. Your mother.*

The word *mother* seemed to sit on the page longer than the others, the way certain words do, the ink going dense and dark.

Mirabel sat down. She didn't remember deciding to sit down; her legs had simply reported for duty elsewhere. She read the rest of the letter twice, three times, the dispatcher part of her mind transcribing automatically while the rest of her stood in the wreckage:

*Freya came to me last spring. After thirty years she had started pulling threads — don't ask me how, she never told me, only that she'd found something at the lake, something physical, something that would hang him. She wanted a witness. I was the witness. I was the caretaker's son; I saw things that summer a boy shouldn't have seen, and I kept my mouth shut for thirty years because Douglas Devane's father paid my father to keep it shut, and fear is inherited like eye color.*

*Freya was going to go public. She told me she'd mailed copies of what she found to two people she trusted. Then she stopped answering my calls. Then I heard she was dead, and everyone said her heart, an old woman's heart, and maybe that's even true. But I know what Douglas Devane does to problems, and Freya Lockridge had become a problem.*

*I did not kill her. I need you to believe that, though I understand why you might not. But I know who did, or I know enough that they'll come for me too, and when they do, everything I have will be in a place Freya knew about. Find it. Finish what she started. Your mother tried to save that boy. Whatever else is true about Ruth Lockridge — and I know what became of her, and I'm sorry — she tried, and they made her pay for trying for the rest of her life.*

*Don't go to the police. Not yet. You'll understand why soon enough.*

*Burn this after you've read it. Or don't. Freya said you wouldn't.*

There was no signature. At the bottom of the second page, alone, was a single typed line that might have been an afterthought or the whole point:

*Postmarked the day after she died. Ask yourself why.*

Mirabel set the pages down on the table with great care, aligning their edges, the way she organized incident logs at the end of a shift. Her hands were still steady. Her heart was going like a struck bell somewhere far away, in someone else's chest.

Outside, the rain had thickened. Down in the street, headlights swept the ceiling of the laundromat below, slow, deliberate, and paused — she was almost sure they paused — before moving on.

Her mother's name. Typed on a dead woman's stationery by a stranger's machine. *Ruth Lockridge.* Ruth, who had died six years ago with a liver full of scars and a phone that never rang, whom Mirabel had buried with the same numb competence she was wearing now, and whom she had spent six years not thinking about in the dedicated, exhausting way that some people spend their whole lives not thinking about one specific thing.

*Tried to save that boy.*

Mirabel stood, walked to the window, and looked down at the wet street. Empty. Just rain, and the neon buzz of the CLOSED sign across the way, and the ordinary dark of an ordinary town where nothing had ever happened.

Behind her, on the table, the letter lay in the light of the kitchen lamp like something washed up on a shore.

She needed to know what was in her mother's boxes. The ones she'd hauled from the old apartment and never opened, sealed with packing tape in the back of her closet, six years now, gathering dust and patience.

The tape was going to come off tonight.
