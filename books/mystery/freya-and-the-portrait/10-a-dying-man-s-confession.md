## 10. A Dying Man's Confession

The note came on a Tuesday, folded inside a taxi company envelope with no return address, slipped under the door of Mirabel's apartment sometime between her shift ending at six and her waking at noon. She knew because she'd stepped over it going to bed, too exhausted to register anything but the shape of paper on linoleum, and found it again at noon, when the light through the blinds had turned it into something that looked almost deliberate — an arrow pointing at her feet.

*St. Agnes Hospice, Room 14. Ask for Eddie Marsh. Come alone. Come before Friday.*

No signature. The handwriting was shaky in a way that wasn't age so much as effort, each letter fought for and won, like someone writing against a tide.

Mirabel stood in her kitchen holding it for a long time, coffee going cold beside her. Since the letter had arrived — since *Freya*, typed out in confession, naming her mother among three teenagers who'd watched a boy drown at Harrow Lake — she had been living in a state of suspended dread. Someone had killed her aunt. Someone had confessed to it and then, apparently, not meant it, or meant it differently than it read, because the police had nothing, the fire marshal called Freya's cottage an electrical fault, and Douglas Devane smiled his judge's smile at anyone who asked questions.

And now this. A name she didn't know. An invitation that felt like either a trap or a gift, and no way to tell which until she was standing inside it.

She thought about calling Detective Okafor, who had taken her statement after the funeral and clearly thought grief was making her imaginative. She thought about Devane, about the black sedan that had followed her home from the lake two weeks ago, tail lights dissolving into rain. She thought about the phone call to her dispatch line from a number tied to Freya's cottage — silence, breathing, a click — and how she still hadn't told anyone about that, because saying it aloud would make it real.

*Come alone.*

Whoever wrote the note knew she would understand why. That was its own kind of message.

* * *

St. Agnes sat on the east side of the city where the neighborhoods got quieter and the trees got older, a low brick building that might once have been a convent, surrounded by a garden gone brown and architectural for winter. Inside it smelled of lilies and disinfectant, the particular perfume of places where people were kept comfortable on their way out. A volunteer at the front desk looked up with the gentle, practiced warmth of hospice staff everywhere.

"I'm here to see Eddie Marsh. Room fourteen."

"Are you family?"

Mirabel hesitated. "I'm the reason he asked."

The volunteer studied her a moment, then picked up a phone, murmured, listened, and nodded. "Go on back. He's been awake since four waiting for you. He'll be glad you came."

Room fourteen was at the end of the hall, past a solarium where an old woman slept in a wheelchair facing a window full of gray light. The door was half open. Mirabel knocked anyway.

"Come in." The voice was thin but steady, a voice used to being obeyed by its own body if nothing else. "Shut the door, please. There's a chair."

Eddie Marsh was smaller than she expected, folded into the bed like a letter into an envelope, hands resting on the blanket with the stillness of a man who had stopped pretending they did what he wanted. His face was gaunt, yellowed at the edges, but his eyes were sharp and dark and fixed on her with an intensity that made her think of a lamp turned all the way up in an empty room.

"You're Ruth's girl," he said. "You have her chin."

Mirabel didn't sit. "You wrote me the letter."

"I wrote you *a* letter." He coughed, wet and long, and waited it out with the patience of long practice. "Sit down, Miss Lockridge. I've got maybe a week of talking left in me and I'd rather not spend it watching you stand there deciding whether to run."

She sat. The chair was vinyl and cold.

"Eddie Marsh," he said, as though introducing himself at a church social. "My father kept the grounds at Harrow Lake for the Devane family, thirty-one years. I grew up in the caretaker's cottage. I was sixteen the summer Danny Aldous drowned." He watched her absorb the name. "Yes. That Danny. Vera Aldous's boy. You've talked to Vera, I hear. Good. Saves me the part where I explain what kind of people we're dealing with."

"You wrote that you killed my aunt," Mirabel said. "You wrote it in a letter and mailed it to me the day after her funeral."

"I did write it. And I mailed it. And every word of the crime itself is true — what happened at the lake, who was there, who paid, who buried it." His eyes didn't waver. "But I didn't kill Freya Lockridge, and I need you to believe that before I say another word, because everything else depends on it."

"Then why sign your name to a murder?"

"Because a confession gets opened." He said it simply, without self-pity. "I've been dying since March. Pancreatic. They gave me till Christmas and I intend to disappoint them by less than a month. I spent thirty years telling myself that what I saw as a boy wasn't mine to carry, that I was a kid, that saying anything would only hurt my father's pension and get us both thrown off the estate. And then Freya started coming around last spring, asking questions, showing me things. Photographs. A photograph of four kids on a dock, one face scratched out. She knew more than I did by then. She'd been digging for years, quiet as a mole, and she was close — closer than I ever got — and then in October she stopped answering my letters." His jaw tightened. "Two weeks later she was dead. Electrical fire, they said. And I understood that whatever window I had to put the truth somewhere it couldn't be burned or buried was closing with me inside it."

"So you confessed to a murder you didn't commit."

"I confessed to the only murder I *did* witness," Eddie said. "Danny Aldous, August 1994. I wrote 'I killed him' because in every way that matters, we all did — everyone who saw and said nothing, everyone who took the money, everyone who built a life on top of that boy like he was a foundation stone. I put it in your hands because you're Ruth's daughter and Freya trusted you, and because a dead man's letter can't be subpoenaed but it also can't be ignored forever." He paused, breath rasping. "I'm sorry for the way I did it. Fear opens doors faster than truth does. I needed you scared enough to keep reading."

Mirabel's hands were flat on her knees. She noticed she was gripping them hard enough to blanch the knuckles, and made herself stop. "Freya was investigating. Before she died."

"For years. Her brother — your grandfather — never believed the drowning verdict. But Freya got serious about five years ago, after her mother died and she found some papers. She came to see me last April. Sat right where you're sitting." Something moved across his face, brief and unguarded. "She brought me a copy of a photograph. Four teenagers on the dock at Harrow Lake, summer of '94. Your mother. Freya herself. Douglas Devane. And a fourth figure scratched out with a pin. She asked me who the fourth one was."

"And you told her?"

"I told her it was Ruth." He said it gently, watching Mirabel take the blow. "I know what you've concluded. I know what Devane wants you to conclude. But I was there that night, Miss Lockridge. I was seventeen and I was up in the boathouse loft with a radio and a bag of chips, doing what teenage boys do, which is hide. I heard the whole thing. The screaming, the car, the argument on the dock afterward. Your mother was the one begging them to call for help. She was fifteen years old and she was on her knees on those boards begging Douglas Devane to let her dial out, and he held her arms while Freya — your Freya, the girl she was — covered her mouth." His voice roughened. "They were children doing a terrible child's thing, terrified out of their minds. But Ruth wasn't driving the car. Ruth didn't hold Danny under. Ruth spent the next thirty years paying for it in the only currency she had left."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the hall a monitor beeped twice and stopped.

"The car," Mirabel said. "Whose car?"

"Douglas drove. It was his father's Mercedes, and his father — Judge Harlan Devane, may he rot wherever he is — arranged everything after. The coroner, the verdict, the cash. Ruth got an envelope too. Everyone always assumes she took it willingly." Eddie shook his head slowly. "Harlan Devane sat in our kitchen and told my father that if the envelope wasn't accepted, the family would be charged as accessories, and that he'd make sure my father never worked again. My father took the money and drank over it for twenty years. Ruth just drank. Different kitchens, same poison."

Mirabel found her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Then who killed my aunt? If not you."

"That," Eddie said, "is the question I've been turning over since October, and I think I finally have the shape of it." He lifted one hand — it trembled with the effort — and pointed at the nightstand drawer. "In there. A key. Freya mailed it to me three weeks before she died, along with a note that said, *'If anything happens to me, give this to Ruth's daughter. Not the police. Ruth's daughter.'* No explanation. Freya never explained; she just aimed you at things and let you find out why."

He watched Mirabel open the drawer. The key was small, brass, old-fashioned, with a paper tag tied to it in Freya's upright handwriting: a string of numbers and the words *Harrow Street Station — lockers.*

"She rented a locker at the train station?" Mirabel said.

"Or she wanted whoever found it to think so. Or the numbers mean something else entirely. Freya liked layers." Eddie settled back against his pillows, exhaling. "Here's what I know, and then I want you to take the key and go, because I've been talking twenty minutes and it costs me. Douglas Devane killed your aunt. I can't prove it — yet — but I know it the way you know weather coming. Freya had something on him. Something physical, something a coroner once touched and lied about, because she told me in September: *'When I show them the oar, Eddie, even his own family won't be able to save him.'* Two weeks later she was ash. He burned the evidence and called it wiring."

"But the oar—"

"Is maybe not the only copy of what she had." His mouth curved, dry as paper. "Freya was thorough. She was also careful in ways I'm only beginning to map. Which brings me to the other thing." He reached to the nightstand himself now, slow as a crane, and produced a folded page, handing it to her. "This came to the hospice addressed to me, Tuesday last. No postmark I could read. Read it."

Mirabel unfolded it. Typed, same as the first letter, same worn typewriter font:

*Eddie — Stop sending the girl pieces. She'll assemble them wrong without me. Tell her the calls are not what she thinks. — F.*

His name at the top. And the signature: *F.*

The room tilted slightly. Mirabel heard her own pulse. "Freya sent this. After she died."

"After she died," Eddie agreed. "Which is impossible. Unless—" He let it hang, letting her arrive there herself, because he was a dying man and had learned to ration revelations like cigarettes.

"Unless the fire marshal was wrong," Mirabel said slowly. "Unless nobody died in that cottage."

"Or unless someone wants us to think so. Or unless Freya had a friend typing for her. I don't know. What I know is that somebody has been watching you, watching me, and steering us both — and that whoever it is knows things only Freya knew, and signs her initial." He closed his eyes briefly, opening them with visible effort. "Take the key. Take the note. Don't come back here — if Devane learns I've talked, he doesn't need to kill me, he just needs to lean on this place and I'll die confused and sedated and my account will die with me. Everything I've told you tonight, I've also written down. It's with the key. Freya's instructions, remember. *Ruth's daughter.*"

Mirabel stood. Her legs felt strange, half hers. At the door she stopped.

"Eddie. The night Danny drowned. Did my mother ever try to tell anyone? Later, I mean. As an adult."

He was quiet a moment. "Once. About ten years ago she called the house line asking for my father. I answered. She asked if he still had the envelope — the one Harlan gave him. She said she wanted to buy it back. She sounded—" his voice caught, steadied, "—she sounded like someone trying to return a stolen thing she'd been forced to keep. My father was already gone by then. I told her that. She hung up without saying goodbye." He looked at her. "Your mother spent her whole life trying to give that night back, Miss Lockridge. Nobody would take it."

Mirabel walked out past the sleeping woman in the solarium, past the lilies and the disinfectant, into the gray afternoon. In her pocket, the brass key pressed its teeth against her palm like a small animal, alive and wanting something.

Behind her, on the second floor, a phone rang in an empty office, rang, and stopped — and if she had stayed, she would have heard the nurse answer it, and hear a woman's voice, calm and familiar in a way she couldn't place, ask only one question:

*"Did she take the key?"*
