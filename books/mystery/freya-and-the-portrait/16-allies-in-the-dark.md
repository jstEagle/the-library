## 16. Allies in the Dark

The hospice sat on a hill above the river, a low brick building that had once been somebody's country house and now smelled of antiseptic and overcooked carrots. Mirabel parked her taxi in the visitor lot at four in the afternoon, engine ticking down into silence, and sat for a long moment with her hands on the wheel.

She had not told anyone where she was going. Not the police — she'd stopped trusting institutions somewhere around the moment she'd learned the coroner was family. Not Eddie's hospice staff, who would have no reason to care. And certainly not Douglas Devane, whose voice she could still hear from two nights ago, smooth as poured cream: *Your mother took the money, Miss Lockridge. Ask yourself what you're holding out for.*

What she was holding out for was sitting up there in a narrow bed with a plastic wristband and maybe three months left on his clock. Eddie Marsh. The caretaker's son. The man who had written a confession for a murder he hadn't committed because the truth needed a body to live in.

She went inside.

* * *

The nurse at the desk looked up from a crossword puzzle. "Visiting?"

"Edward Marsh."

"You family?"

Mirabel considered the question. "I'm the one he wrote to."

Something flickered across the nurse's face — recognition, or maybe just the particular weariness of someone who worked around dying people long enough to know that visitors came in stranger varieties than kin. She picked up an internal phone, murmured, hung up. "Room 9. He's having a good day. Take it while it lasts."

Room 9 was at the end of the hall, its window facing west, the river running gold below. Eddie Marsh was propped against pillows, a thin man in his mid-forties wearing a cardigan that had been chosen by someone else, his skin the color of old candle wax. His eyes, though, were sharp. They found her the moment she appeared in the doorway and did not let go.

"You came," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "I half thought you wouldn't. I half hoped."

"Why would you hope that?"

"Because everyone who gets close to this ends up worse off." He gestured at the chair beside the bed. "Sit down, Miss Lockridge. You look like a woman who doesn't waste time, so I won't either. Did you bring the letter?"

"I memorized it. I don't carry it anymore." She sat. Up close she could see the tremor in his hands, the way his chest worked harder than it should for each breath. Whatever was eating him was doing it efficiently. "You wrote me a confession to killing my aunt. Then you told me you didn't do it. You'll understand if I want to know which version is true."

"Both." He laughed, and it turned into a cough that bent him forward until she instinctively reached for the water glass on the side table. He drank, waved thanks, settled back. "Both versions are true. That's the whole rotten shape of it. I didn't kill Freya Lockridge. But I'm the reason she's dead, same as if I'd held the pillow myself."

"Explain that."

He looked out the window at the river for a while. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped, gone quieter, as if the walls might be listening. Maybe they were. She'd learned in the last month that they usually were.

"I've been watching that house since I was nine years old," he said. "My dad was the caretaker for the Devanes. We lived in the cottage past the boathouse. I knew every path on that property before I knew my times tables. And in the summer of '94, I saw three kids come off that dock looking like ghosts, and I saw Judge Devane — old Devane, the father — pay my father six months' salary to forget what we'd both seen. Danny Okafor went under that water and nobody called anybody. Nobody dove in. They stood there and watched him drown, and then they drove away, and the most powerful man in the county made sure the lake stayed quiet."

"You were a child. You couldn't have stopped it."

"I could have told someone." His jaw tightened. "That's what I've had thirty years to chew on. A nine-year-old can tell a teacher. Can tell a priest. I kept my mouth shut because my father told me to and because I liked the ten dollars old Devane slipped me every Christmas like a bribe wrapped in tinsel. So no, Miss Lockridge, I couldn't have saved Danny Okafor. But I could have made sure the world knew what happened, and I chose not to, year after year after year. That's my crime. Freya died carrying the weight of mine."

Mirabel felt something shift in her chest — not forgiveness, not yet, but the beginning of understanding. Her mother had stood on that dock too. Had begged them, according to the flashback her own mind kept replaying from fragments of letters and photographs, to call for help. And had been silenced. And had taken the money anyway. And had drunk herself into an early grave with the sound of a boy's name ringing in her ears.

"Eddie," she said carefully. "Freya was investigating. I found her clippings, her notes. Someone killed her for it. If you didn't—"

"I know who did." He said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Or I know enough. And about two weeks before she died, Freya mailed me a package. I want to show you something, but first I need to tell you why I finally broke my silence after thirty years, because you deserve to understand that I'm not doing this out of courage. I'm doing it out of cowardice. The last kind there is."

"The kind that waits until it's safe."

"The kind that waits until it's *dying*." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, self-lacerating. "Pancreatic. They gave me till spring. I figured, what's Devane going to do to me? Fire my dead father? Sue my estate? There's a freedom in a terminal diagnosis that I never found in a church. So I wrote your letter. I mailed it the day after the funeral because I wanted it to find you raw. I wanted you angry enough to dig."

"It worked."

"I know. I've been following what you've been digging up." He raised an eyebrow. "Small town, Miss Lockridge. My niece works at the pharmacy in Harrow Lake. She says a woman's been asking questions about the summer of '94. Says the judge has been asking questions about *her*."

A cold thread went down Mirabel's spine. The tail lights on the road back from the lake. The call to her dispatch line from a number tied to Freya's cottage — breathing, then silence, then nothing. She'd assumed Devane. Now she wasn't sure what she assumed.

"He knows about you?" she asked.

"He knows someone's pulling threads. He doesn't know it's me yet — or he didn't as of last week. But here's the thing you need to understand about Douglas Devane." Eddie leaned forward, wincing, and gripped the bed rail. "Everyone thinks he's the respectable one, the one who got out clean, the judge with the good suit. But Douglas isn't the rot in that family. He's just the branch it grew on. His father ran that cover-up like a military operation. Bought the coroner. Bought the sheriff. Bought my father. And Douglas — Douglas was seventeen and he drove the car. He was the one behind the wheel when they left Harrow Lake with Danny Okafor's body floating behind them. Everything he's built since, every gavel he's ever swung, is standing on top of that boy's drowned lungs. Men like that don't confess. They *prune*."

* * *

He made her wait while a nurse came in with medication, small white paper cup, cheerful patter about dinner choices. Mirabel used the time to study the room: a framed photo of a younger Eddie in front of the Harrow Lake boathouse, arm slung around a heavyset man she took for his father; a get-well card signed by what looked like an entire church congregation; a Bible on the nightstand with a receipt for a bookmark, still unread, pages crisp.

When the nurse left, Eddie pulled open the drawer of the nightstand with trembling fingers and took out a padded envelope, soft with handling, postmarked three weeks before Freya's death.

"She sent this to me," he said. "No note. Just this."

Inside was a brass key, old-fashioned, heavy, and a slip of paper with eleven digits written in a careful hand — numbers that meant nothing to Mirabel until Eddie turned the paper over, and there, in the same handwriting, were two words:

*First National. Trust me.*

"A safe deposit box," Mirabel breathed.

"That's what I figure. I've been sitting on this thing for weeks trying to decide what to do with it. Part of me wanted to flush it down the toilet and die in peace. Part of me wanted to march into that bank and open it myself." He pressed the key into her palm and closed her fingers around it, and his hand was cold and light as a bird's. "But it's not mine. It was never meant for me. Look at the envelope again."

She looked. Her own name and address were written on a label stuck over the original — but beneath the label's corner, the original ink showed through: *E. Marsh.* Freya had addressed it to him, then relabeled it? No —

"No," Eddie said, reading her face. "She addressed it to me. But the label's in *my* handwriting. I wrote your name on it after she died. Because I panicked. Because I opened my door one morning and found a copy of the local paper on my step with the page about Danny Okafor's drowning circled in red, and I knew Devane's people had found out Freya had talked to me. I couldn't keep it here. So I mailed it to the only person I knew was already hunting." He exhaled, a long rattling breath. "And then I called you to the hospice and waited to see if you'd come. If you came, you were serious. If you were serious, you deserved the key."

Mirabel turned the key over in her palm. Brass, worn smooth at the bow, a number stamped near the teeth: *114.*

"Freya trusted you," she said.

"Freya pitied me. There's a difference, but I'll take it." He lay back against the pillows, exhausted by the effort, and for a moment he looked every one of his forty-six years and then some. "There's more. I finished it last night. Took me three tries — my hands don't work right anymore — but it's done."

He nodded toward the Bible. Mirabel lifted it and felt the wrongness of the weight immediately. Between the pages, occupying the space where scripture should have been, was a bound stack of lined paper, handwritten, front and back, thirty years of held breath released in ballpoint blue.

"My account," Eddie said. "Everything. The dock, the night it happened, what I saw, what my father was paid, the Christmas envelopes, the red-circled newspaper, all of it. Signed and dated. It's not evidence a court will love — hearsay stacked on hearsay, a dying man's word — but it's the truth, and it's yours now. Do whatever you think is right with it. That's the deal. That's always been the deal with this thing. Somebody has to choose."

Mirabel held the manuscript against her chest. It was warm from the Bible's place on the sunlit stand, absurdly, and she felt that warmth travel through her like a current.

"Why me?" she asked. "You could have gone to the state police. The attorney general. Anyone but a dispatcher with no standing."

"Because you're Ruth's daughter." He said it simply. "Because Freya spent her last months telling anyone who'd listen that the Lockridge girl had her mother's spine and none of her fear. Because—" he paused, and his eyes went briefly, unbearably bright, "—because I heard your voice. On the radio. Three years ago, my cab from the hospital, the driver's dispatch crackling away, and this calm voice saying *take the river road, there's an accident on Main* — and I swear to God, Miss Lockridge, it was the first time in thirty years I heard somebody steering strangers safely through the dark, and I thought: *that's what should have happened at the lake. Somebody should have been on the line. Somebody should have sent help.*"

The room was very quiet. Down the hall, a television murmured. The river ran gold to copper below the window.

"So here's where we are," Eddie said, rallying, gripping the bed rail again. "You've got my account. You've got the key. Box 114 at First National, and I'd bet my remaining lung capacity it holds whatever Freya found that got her killed. And Douglas Devane thinks he's talking to a scared little dispatcher he can buy or bury." He managed the terrible smile again. "Two damaged people, one dying, one hunted, choosing truth over safety. It's not much of an alliance. It's probably a stupid one. But it's ours."

Mirabel stood. At the door she stopped, the key in one pocket, the manuscript zipped inside her jacket against her ribs.

"Eddie. The calls I've been getting — on my dispatch line. From a number tied to Freya's cottage. Breathing. Silence. Before she died, did she ever mention—"

"Phones?" He frowned, thinking, and then shook his head slowly. "No. But she did say something odd, once, near the end. I asked her why she didn't just go to the police with everything, and she said—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it, "—she said, *'The police are his, the courts are his, but the airwaves, Eddie — the airwaves belong to whoever's listening.'*" He opened his eyes. "I thought it was grief talking. Freya's sister was a dispatcher too, you know. Your mother. Did you know that? Ruth worked nights, same as you. Same chair, different decade."

Mirabel hadn't known. The knowledge landed in her like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom.

She thanked him. She meant it for more than the key.

Outside, dusk had come down over the river, and the parking lot lights buzzed on one by one. As she crossed to her taxi, she checked the street out of habit — and froze.

Across the road, idling with its headlights off, sat a dark sedan. Not moving. Not leaving. Just waiting, patient as a spider, and even at this distance she could make out the silhouette of a man in the driver's seat, and the small orange bloom of a cigarette lighter flaring once in the dark.

They knew she'd been here. Which meant they knew about Eddie.

Mirabel got in her cab, started the engine, and pulled out onto the river road at exactly the speed limit, hands steady, mind already mapping three routes home — while behind her, unhurried, the sedan's headlights came on and followed.
