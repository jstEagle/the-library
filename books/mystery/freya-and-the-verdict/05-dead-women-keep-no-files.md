## 5. Dead Women Keep No Files

The county records database opened at six a.m., and Lena was already at her desk when it did, coffee untouched, the window behind her still the color of wet slate. She had not slept well. She rarely slept well, but last night the insomnia had a shape to it—a name, four syllables, sitting on her chest like a cat that refused to be moved.

*Freya Lindqvist.*

She typed it into the vital records search the way she did everything: precisely, without flourish, as though the keyboard might notice hesitation and report it.

The birth certificate surfaced in under a second. Freya Astrid Lindqvist, born March 2, 1961, St. Anthony's Hospital, Port Merrow. Father: Henrik Lindqvist, fisherman. Mother: Astrid Lindqvist, née Halvorsen. The document was scanned in that flat gray of microfilm converted badly to digital, the ink of the registrar's signature faded to a ghost's handwriting. Lena zoomed in until the pixels broke apart. A real certificate. A real girl, born in a real hospital on a cold morning fifty-nine years before anyone thought to make her dead.

Lena sat back. This was the part of the work she loved—the part no one ever saw, the quiet archaeology of paper. Every life left sediment. You just had to know where to dig.

She dug.

* * *

By nine, the shape of a woman had assembled itself out of fragments on Lena's second monitor, and Lena found herself unreasonably reluctant to look away from it.

Nursing license, issued 1983, renewed faithfully every two years for three decades. Specialization in geriatric care—a detail that snagged something in Lena's chest and refused to let go. Employment history: St. Anthony's General, eight years. Then, in 1991, a gap. Then private practice—unlisted employer, which in nursing usually meant one thing: someone rich enough to hire care instead of renting it.

Lena pulled the probate file again, laid Marguerite Voss's timeline beside Freya Lindqvist's, and watched them braid together like rope.

Marguerite Voss, born 1948. Diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis at thirty-four, per a medical exhibit attached to an old property dispute Lena had requested from the clerk on a hunch. A woman in chronic pain for forty years would need a nurse. A nurse who specialized in geriatric and chronic care. A nurse whose employment went private in exactly the year Marguerite's health began its long decline.

They had known each other. That much was arithmetic, not speculation.

And then—

Lena scrolled forward through the license renewals. 2017. 2019. The 2019 renewal was stamped *lapsed — non-renewal*. After that, nothing. No tax filings. No property. No utility hookups, no voter registration, no traffic citations, not even a library card renewal. Freya Astrid Lindqvist, who had left a thirty-year trail of bureaucratic breadcrumbs across three counties, simply stopped dropping them in the spring of 2019.

The same season Marguerite Voss walked into the sea—or was said to have—and came out the other side as a death certificate filed by a family that had never produced a body Lena could find reference to. Cremation, per the file Edmund Voss's lawyer had sent over. Ashes scattered at the family's "private memorial rock." How convenient, Lena thought, that grief required no grave to visit.

She printed nothing. She wrote nothing down. Years of habit had taught her that the most dangerous documents were the ones that existed; she kept her best work in the locked room behind her eyes, and she kept that room dark.

But she was aware, as she sat there in the gray morning light with two dead women braided together on her screen, that the room was getting crowded. And that for the first time in her professional life, she wasn't sure whether she was building a case or dismantling one.

* * *

The Port Merrow Public Library occupied a Carnegie-era stone building that smelled of cold radiators and older paper, and it was, Lena reflected as she climbed the steps, the kind of place where a person could disappear in plain sight forever. Nobody watched you here. Nobody remembered you unless you wanted them to.

She had dressed for invisibility: charcoal coat, no jewelry, hair pinned back. Her profession had taught her that the best way to learn what people knew was to give them nothing to react to. Ask about records. Be boring. Boring people got answers; interesting people got managed.

The reference desk was manned by a woman in her sixties with reading glasses pushed up into silver curls and a cardigan the color of weak tea. Her nameplate said MRS. OKONKWO, but everyone in town, judging by the framed photos on the wall behind her, called her Dot.

"Probate records?" Dot repeated, when Lena asked. "County archive has those, hon, but they're slow as Christmas. What are you looking for, if you don't mind my asking? Sometimes I can save you the trip."

Lena had prepared a cover story—an estate matter, a routine heir search. It was even mostly true. But something about the library's hush, the rain starting against the tall windows, loosened the lie halfway up her throat.

"A person," Lena said. "Freya Lindqvist. Born here in '61. I need to find her or find out what happened to her."

Dot's hands, which had been stamping returns, went still.

It lasted maybe two seconds. Then the stamp came down on a paperback romance with a soft *thump*, and Dot smiled the smile librarians keep holstered for patrons who ask about local history. "Lindqvist. Old Port Merrow name. Fishermen, mostly. There were Lindqvists out past the cannery row." She tilted her head. "You family?"

"Distant," Lena said, which was technically true of everyone.

"Mm." Dot removed her glasses, polished them on her cardigan with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman buying time, and put them back on. "Well. There was a Freya. Nurse. Sweet woman—used to bring the bookmobile out to the shrimpers' families when their people were sick, back before the county cut the route. Everybody knew Freya." She paused. "Everybody also knows she passed. Few years back now."

"Passed," Lena repeated carefully. "Do you know where she's buried? Or which funeral home handled it?"

"You'd think I would." Dot's voice stayed pleasant, but something underneath it had gone careful, the way ice goes careful under a boot. "Funny thing about this town, hon. We remember everybody's funerals. We argue about the flowers. But ask me who buried Freya Lindqvist and I couldn't tell you. Ask anybody." She leaned slightly over the desk, and her voice dropped into the register people use for things they've decided are true but have never been permitted to say aloud. "No service. No notice in the *Gazette*—and we print notices for dogs here, hon, we printed one for my sister's parakeet. Nothing. One day she was here and then folks just started saying she'd died, the way you say a road's closed. Nobody remembers the sign going up."

Lena kept her face still. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a key finding its first tumbler.

"When did people start saying it?" she asked.

Dot considered. "Spring of '19. Same time—" and there she stopped herself, lips pressing together, eyes flicking once toward the newspaper archive in the corner, bound volumes of the *Port Merrow Gazette* stacked like headstones.

"Same time as what?" Lena asked softly.

Dot looked at her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Lena's face made her decide something. "Same time the Voss woman drowned," she said. "Marguerite Voss. Big house up on the bluff. And I'll tell you something else for free, since you've got that look about you." She glanced toward the door, then lowered her voice further. "There's a woman volunteers at the county archive. Tuesdays and Fridays. Quiet little thing, gray cardigan, glasses. Been there—I want to say five years now? Nobody knows where she came from. She's good with the old files, better than good. Knows where everything is. Knows things she shouldn't, if you follow me." Dot straightened, resumed stamping, voice returning to its public register. "If you're hunting a ghost, hon, that's the one to ask. Ghosts keep company with ghosts."

"What's her name?" Lena asked.

"Wren," Dot said. "Just Wren. Like the bird." The stamp came down. "Small gray bird. You'd never notice her in a crowd." Another *thump*. "Which is, I expect, the whole point."

* * *

Lena did not go straight to the courthouse. She walked.

Port Merrow in the rain was a town pretending to be asleep: shuttered cannery, bait shop with a hand-lettered CLOSED FOR SEASON sign that had been closed for seasons, the bluff above the harbor where the Voss house sat like a crown on a head that had forgotten it was wearing one. Lena stood at the seawall for a while, watching water the color of gunmetal fold itself against the rocks, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing all morning.

Two women had vanished in the same season. One officially dead, one unofficially so. One wealthy, one not. One with a family that mourned twice and lied beautifully, and one with no family at all—no one, apparently, to insist on a funeral, a notice, a stone.

And a will, flawless and impossible, that named the vanished nurse as its witness. A will that could only be executed if Freya Lindqvist walked into a courtroom and authenticated it. Which meant either that Marguerite Voss had drafted a document designed to fail—

—or that she had drafted one designed to *summon*.

Lena's breath fogged and tore away in the wind. She thought about the codicil's strange language, the thirty-day clock, the beneficiary described only as "the party the witness shall name." She thought about Edmund Voss's lawyer threatening disbarment before Lena had done anything worth disbarment for. People threatened hardest when they had the most to lose.

Someone had built a trap. The question that made her stomach tighten was the oldest question in her profession: *for whom?*

She turned from the water and walked up toward the courthouse, and it occurred to her, climbing the hill with rain soaking through her coat hem, that she had spent her whole life being the person nobody noticed—the group-home kid who learned early that visibility was danger, the associate who made partner by making herself furniture, the executor who executed flawlessly because flawless left no fingerprints. She had built a career out of being forgettable.

And now a librarian she'd known for eleven minutes had looked at her and seen right through it, and told her exactly where the other invisible woman worked.

Tuesdays and Fridays, Dot had said.

Lena checked her watch. It was Friday.

* * *

The county archive lived in the courthouse basement, down a stairwell that smelled of limestone and damp, behind a door with a brass plate worn nearly illegible: RECORDS & PRESERVATION. Lena stood outside it longer than she needed to, telling herself she was composing her approach, knowing she was doing no such thing. Her heart was behaving unprofessionally.

Through the door's wired-glass window she could see fluorescent light, steel shelving marching away into shadow, and at a worktable near the far wall, a figure bent over a folio with white cotton gloves on her hands, turning pages with the tenderness people reserve for the wounded.

Gray cardigan. Glasses. Small, still, easy to overlook—which was, of course, why Lena could not stop looking.

Lena pushed the door open. The hinges didn't squeak; someone oiled them. Someone who knew every sound this basement made.

"Help you?" The archivist looked up, and her voice was mild, pleasant, professionally neutral—and her eyes, behind the smudged lenses, did something complicated in the half-second before they settled into blankness. They went, very briefly, to Lena's hands. To her briefcase. As if checking it for police.

"I'm looking for probate records," Lena said. "Estate of Marguerite Voss. Filed materials, 2019 and after."

The archivist did not flinch. That was the remarkable thing—she did not flinch at all, and the effort of not flinching was visible the way a held breath is visible. "Voss," she repeated. "I can pull the index. Sign in, please." She slid a ledger across the table, and her gloved hand was perfectly steady.

Lena signed. *L. Nakamura.* The archivist read the name upside down, the way people who work with ledgers always can, and something passed behind her eyes like a cloud crossing a small gray sky.

"Wait here," the archivist said, and disappeared into the stacks.

Lena waited. She listened to the small sounds of the basement: the hum of dehumidifiers, rain finding the building somewhere above, the whisper of acid-free folders. On the worktable beside her lay the folio the archivist had been restoring—deeds, by the look of it, old ones, 1970s-era, their margins annotated in pencil by some earlier surveyor. Lena did not touch it. But she read the parcel numbers, and she memorized them, because parcel numbers along the north bluff in the 1970s were exactly what a forged deed would need to be forged *from*.

The archivist returned with a single folder and set it down between them like a card played face-down. "Everything the county holds on the Voss estate," she said. "Death certificate's certified copy. Original filings are sealed pending litigation." A pause, precise as a metronome. "Is there anything else?"

Lena looked at her. At the gray cardigan, the gloves, the name tag that said only WREN in block letters, no surname, though every other staff tag in this building, Lena happened to know, carried both names and a title. At the woman who had heard the name *Marguerite Voss* and gone still as a held breath, and who knew which filings were sealed in a case that hadn't been filed yet.

"No," Lena said quietly. "That's everything. Thank you, Ms.—"

"Just Wren."

"Just Wren," Lena agreed. She gathered the folder, turned toward the door, and stopped. Then, as casually as filing a form, she slipped her business card inside the folder's front cover, deep enough that only someone re-shelving it—only *her*—would find it. The card was plain: her name, her office number, and beneath them, handwritten in pencil in her small exact hand, a single line:

*I believe you're alive. I'm not the danger. Thirty days.*

She didn't look back to see if the archivist watched her go. She climbed the stairs into the gray afternoon feeling, for the first time since the will had landed on her desk, something that was not dread.

Hope, she thought, testing the word like a loose tooth. So that's what that feels like.

Behind her, below the courthouse, in the hum and hush of the record room, a woman in a gray cardigan stood holding a business card with both gloved hands, as if it were something that could burn her—or save her—and outside, the rain kept falling on a town full of beautiful liars, while somewhere up on the bluff, in a house with one portrait missing from its wall, a telephone began to ring.
