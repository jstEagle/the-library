## 7. Second Body, Same Closet

The call came at 4:47 in the morning, and Lena knew before she answered.

Not because of the hour—though the hour was telling. Lawyers who were merely witnesses did not get calls before dawn. But because of the voice on the other end: Detective Okafor's, stripped of its careful professional neutrality, roughened by something that might have been anger or might have been pity.

"Nakamura. Where is your car?"

Lena sat up in bed, the sheets pooling cold around her waist. "The municipal lot on Harbor Street. Level two, near the stairwell. Why?"

A pause long enough to hear Okafor breathing. "Don't come down here."

"I'm coming down there."

"Lena." The first name, uninvited. "Don't come down here."

She came anyway. She took a rideshare because her hands were not steady enough for the wheel, and she spent the entire twelve-minute ride rehearsing explanations she didn't have, cataloguing the last time she'd touched the car—the parking stub still in her coat pocket, the half-empty coffee cup she'd meant to throw away, the dry cleaning she'd picked up Tuesday and never carried inside. Her mind, that meticulous machine, kept offering up details like a clerk presenting evidence against its own owner.

Level two smelled of salt and diesel from the harbor below. The crime scene tape moved in the wind like something alive. And there, under the portable lights, sat her car—a three-year-old gray sedan so aggressively ordinary it had once been a point of pride—and beside it, on the stained concrete, lay a man she had never seen before, dressed head to toe in clothes she had worn herself.

The charcoal overcoat. The gray scarf with the loose thread at the fringe. Even the shoes—her oxblood derbies, size eight, absurd on a dead man's feet, laced tight as if someone had wanted them to fit.

"His own clothes were folded in the trunk," Okafor said quietly, appearing at her elbow. "Neatly. Like laundry."

Lena looked at the man's face. Younger than the first one. Soft-jawed, sun-starved, a faded tattoo of an anchor disappearing under the collar of *her* shirt. She felt nothing, and the nothing frightened her more than horror would have. This was a stranger wearing her life like a costume, and all she could think was: *the scarf is going to be ruined; they'll never get the blood out.*

That was the moment she understood she was in trouble deeper than suspicion. Not because of what she felt—but because of what she couldn't.

* * *

Okafor drove her back to the station himself, waving off the uniformed officer who'd been assigned to her. In the car, he didn't turn on the radio. He drove the way people drive when they're buying time.

"The ME puts him at twenty-six, twenty-seven," he said. "No ID on the body. His prints aren't in any system we can reach tonight. Cause of death looks like drowning again—same as the first one. Same as—" He stopped himself.

"Same staging," Lena said.

"Same staging." He turned onto the empty boulevard, headlights carving a tunnel through fog. "Here's what I know, Nakamura. Somebody got into your car without breaking anything. Somebody had access to your wardrobe—your actual wardrobe, not approximations. Monogrammed cufflinks the first time. Now the coat, the scarf, the shoes. These aren't things you lose. These are things someone takes, or things someone is given access to."

"My apartment wasn't touched. I checked after the first body. Nothing missing."

"Then where?"

And there it was—the question she'd been circling for six days, the one she'd been afraid to say aloud because saying it made it real. "My dry cleaner," Lena said slowly. "On Calloway Street. I dropped off three suits and the overcoat the week before the first body. When I picked everything up, the ticket said four items. I remember thinking the count was wrong, but the coat was there, so I assumed I'd miscounted." She heard her own voice go flat, clinical, the voice she used in depositions. "Someone has been collecting my clothing. Item by item. Building a wardrobe."

Okafor pulled the car over. He put it in park and turned to look at her, and in the dark his face was very tired.

"You understand what this looks like," he said. "Two bodies. Both dressed in your clothes. Both found within a mile of places connected to you. Your car. Your building. Your office. A prosecutor could walk a jury down that hallway blindfolded."

"I understand what it looks like. I don't understand what it *is.*"

"No?" He almost smiled, and there was no warmth in it. "I've been asking myself that since the first night. Here's my best guess. Someone isn't trying to frame you for murder. Framing is crude—you plant one knife, one gun, you're done. This is different. This is *narrative.* Someone is writing a story where you're either a killer or a target, and they're feeding it to us chapter by chapter. They want you watched. They want Internal Affairs crawling through your files. They want your life opened up like a probate estate and every drawer inventoried." He paused. "They want you visible, Nakamura. And I keep wondering why a person would want that."

Lena stared out at the fog. Visible. The word landed somewhere old and tender, a bruise she'd carried since childhood, since the group home where being noticed was the same as being chosen—for punishment, for scrutiny, for the social worker's clipboard. She had built her whole adult life on the principle that the safest place in any room was the corner no one looked at. Someone had just dragged her into the center of it.

"What do I do?" she asked, and hated how small it sounded.

"Get a lawyer," Okafor said. "Not yourself. An actual one. And Lena—" he started the car again, "—as of six a.m., I can't talk to you anymore. IA opened a file this morning. I'm officially conflicted out. Everything I do for you from here on is me burning my own career, and I need you to understand that I know that, and I'm doing it anyway, and I don't fully know why."

* * *

Internal Affairs arrived at nine o'clock sharp, which told Lena they'd been waiting for daylight rather than cause.

There were two of them: a tall woman named Reyes with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair, and a younger man, Vasquez, who carried a laptop like a weapon and never quite met anyone's eyes. They took over the conference room at Okafor's precinct—or rather, they requisitioned it, and Okafor stood in the hallway watching them carry in boxes labeled with case numbers that hadn't existed yesterday.

Reyes interviewed Lena first. She was polite in the way surgeons are polite, everything behind the eyes already mapped and measured.

"Walk me through your relationship with Marguerite Voss."

"Professional. I was retained to execute a will."

"A will for a woman dead three years."

"That's the question the file raises, yes."

"And the beneficiary of that will is unnamed. The authentication witness is a dead woman. And now two dead men are turning up in your clothing." Reyes removed her glasses, cleaned them with slow deliberation. "You have to admit, Ms. Nakamura, from where I sit, the file writes itself."

"Files write themselves all the time," Lena said. "That's why we read them carefully."

Something flickered across Reyes's face—not amusement, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of one careful person acknowledging another across a table that might become an interrogation room.

Vasquez took her phone, her laptop, her client files—all of them, including the Voss probate documents, which she surrendered with a copyist's precision, having photographed every page days ago. She watched them seal the originals in evidence bags and thought about Freya Lindqvist, dead four years, waiting somewhere in the silence between records. Thought about the codicil's strange conditions. Thought about how whoever was dressing corpses in her clothes must know about the will, must know about the thirty-day clock, must want that clock stopped by any means necessary.

"They're not trying to convict me," she said suddenly.

Reyes looked up.

"They're trying to delay me. Every body, every headline, every IA file—it's friction. It's sand in the gears. Someone doesn't care whether I go to prison. They care whether I finish executing that will before day thirty." Lena stood, straightening her borrowed jacket—one of the few items of clothing she now trusted, bought cash that morning off a rack. "You can follow me, charge me, or fire me. But if I were you, I'd ask who benefits when a probate deadline lapses."

Reyes said nothing. But she wrote something down, and she wrote it quickly.

* * *

That evening, Lena sat in her apartment with the lights off, watching the street through the gap in the curtains.

Her apartment had been searched—she was nearly certain—while she'd been at the station. Nothing taken, nothing broken, but the books on the second shelf sat a quarter inch out of alignment, and the bathroom towel hung with its fold facing the wrong way. Small things. Things only she would notice. Whoever it was wanted her to notice. That was part of the story too: *we are in your walls, we are in your closet, we know the shape of your shoulders.*

She thought about the young man on level two. Anchor tattoo. Sun-starved skin. Somewhere, presumably, a mother. She had called the medical examiner's office and asked to be notified when identification came through, and the clerk had paused, confused—*you're the suspect, ma'am*—and Lena had said, evenly, *I'm the executor,* and hung up before her voice could crack.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number. She almost didn't answer.

A voice, distorted, pitched low—through an app, a filter, something cheap and effective. "Day twenty-two, counselor."

Lena went very still. "Who is this?"

"You've been busy. Courthouses. Archives. Asking librarians about dead women." A soft sound that might have been laughter. "You should see what happens to people who dig up Voss ground. We showed you twice. We can show you a third time, and next time the clothes won't be empty."

The line went dead.

Lena lowered the phone. Her hands were shaking, and she let them shake, alone in the dark, because no one was watching and shaking was free. Then she walked to her desk, took out the photographs of the Voss will—every page—and laid them flat beneath the lamp.

Twenty-two days. Eight left, if the bodies kept coming. Eight days to find a witness the world insisted was dead, while the world worked overtime to make sure she joined her.

She looked at the photograph of the signature page, at the witness line where *Freya Lindqvist* was written in a hand so calm and certain it seemed to dare the paper to argue.

"You're alive," Lena said to the empty room. "Marguerite faked a death and you inherited the trick. Dead women keep no files—but living ones keep *secrets*, and secrets leave postmarks."

She reached for the letters she hadn't yet found, for the trail she hadn't yet followed, and began—at midnight, hunted, in a room someone else had already visited—to plan how a dead woman moves through the world.

Because whoever was dressing corpses in her clothes had made one mistake. They had taught her, body by body, exactly how much she stood to lose.

And Lena Nakamura had never once lost a case she refused to abandon.
