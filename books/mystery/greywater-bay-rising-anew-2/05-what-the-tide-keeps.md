## 5. What the Tide Keeps

The call came at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, which was how I knew it was bad. Good news in the hospitality business keeps banker's hours; catastrophe prefers to catch you before coffee.

I was in the back office reconciling the weekend's folios when Ambrose appeared in the doorway. He'd retired from the door eleven years ago but still came by most mornings, ostensibly to "inspect the brass," actually because a man who spent forty years opening doors for strangers doesn't know how to stop watching them arrive. He looked, for the first time since I'd known him, genuinely old.

"Sable." He said my name like it was a diagnosis. "They've found Dr. Okonkwo."

Adaeze Okonkwo had been Greywater Bay's coroner for thirty-one years before retiring to a bungalow on Pelican Row with a garden she talked about the way other people talk about grandchildren. She had signed more death certificates in this town than anyone alive could count, and I had known her since I was eight years old, when she'd knelt down in a hospital corridor, looked me in the eye—something almost nobody did back then—and said, *You're allowed to be frightened, little one. Just don't let it make you small.*

She had signed one certificate that mattered more than all the others.

"Drowned," Ambrose said. "In her own bathtub."

* * *

Detective Voss was already there when I arrived at Pelican Row, standing in Okonkwo's kitchen with her hands in her coat pockets like a woman trying very hard not to touch anything. The house smelled of bergamot and wet wool. On the counter sat a teacup with a lipstick mark on the rim, half a scone on a plate beside it—a breakfast interrupted, or perhaps never finished, or perhaps arranged. I've spent thirty years reading rooms for a living, and this room read wrong. Too tidy for a struggle. Too staged for an accident.

"Concierge Coldharbour." Voss didn't turn around. "You get around."

"You called the hotel. Front desk logged it. I read the log."

"Of course you did." She turned then, and up close her eyes were the color of bay water in February—grey going on green going on something colder. "Care to tell me why your hotel's keycard was on the bathroom floor?"

The floor dropped out from under me, the way it does in dreams where you keep walking and the stairs simply aren't there anymore.

"That's not possible," I said. "Our cards are chipped and logged. Every door, every elevator, every service corridor. If one of ours opened anything in this house, it's in the system."

"It is in the system." She pulled out her phone, showed me a screenshot. Card 4117. Assigned to: Housekeeping, sub-master, issued March 2019. Last recorded use: none. A ghost card. A card that existed in our inventory and had never once touched a reader—which meant either it had been cloned, or someone with access to the engineering office had rewritten its chip.

Dev could do that in nine minutes. Dev had taught me to solder when I was twelve, hunched over a workbench that smelled of flux and machine oil, telling me that everything broken is just something waiting to be understood. I thought about his hands, steady and kind, and felt sick at how easily kindness can be made to look like evidence.

"Anyone could have taken it," I said. "Housekeeping keeps forty of those in circulation."

"Anyone," Voss agreed pleasantly. "That's the trouble with your hotel, Coldharbour. Anyone could have done anything. It's remarkable how often *anyone* turns out to be on your payroll."

"I want to see the bathroom."

"No."

"I knew her." My voice came out flatter than I intended, and I watched that register with her—the flatness, the control, another data point in whatever file she was building on me. "Thirty years ago she certified a drowning in this town, and now she's drowned herself, and you're telling me no?"

Voss studied me for a long moment. Outside, gulls were arguing over something on the roof, their voices thin and vicious.

"You knew her how?" she asked.

"As the coroner. Everyone knew Dr. Okonkwo."

"Everyone," she repeated, and wrote something down. I have never wanted to snatch a notebook out of a detective's hands so badly in my life.

* * *

Marisol found me that afternoon in the linen room, which is where I go to think, because the smell of starch and lavender has always been the closest thing I have to a mother's kitchen. She came in without knocking, which Marisol never does, and shut the door behind her, which Marisol absolutely never does.

"They're saying it was her heart," she said. "The radio. Her heart gave out in the tub. Seventy-four years old, poor woman."

"The radio also says the Grand Hotel is the crown jewel of the Greywater waterfront," I said, folding a bath sheet into thirds. "Radios say what's comfortable."

Marisol took the towel out of my hands and set it down, and held my forearms, her grip warm and iron underneath. She was smaller than me, had always been smaller than me, and yet I had never once in my life stood over her.

"Mija." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "You went to the house."

"Ambrose told me. I went."

"Why?"

Because Adaeze Okonkwo once told a frightened child it was allowed to be afraid. Because she signed a piece of paper in August 1994 that said Tomas Reyes died of accidental drowning, and I needed to know if her hands had shaken when she signed it. Because every person who touched that summer is dying or lying or both, and I am running out of people to ask.

"Because I knew her," I said, which was true, and insufficient, and we both knew it.

Marisol's thumbs pressed into my wrists, right over the pulse. For a moment she just breathed, and I matched her without meaning to, the way you learn to as a child in a house full of adults holding their breath.

"There are things," she said finally, "that were buried for good reasons. Not easy reasons. Good ones. And the ground is giving them back whether we consent or not, and I need you—" her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in fifty years of mortar— "I need you to be careful. Not brave. Careful. There's a difference, and brave people end up in bathtubs."

"Is that what happened to her? Was she brave?"

Something moved behind Marisol's eyes, quick and dark, a fish under ice. "I don't know," she said. "God forgive me, I don't know, and some mornings that's worse than knowing."

Then she kissed my forehead, the way she used to when I had fevers, and left me alone with the lavender and the starch and the terrible arithmetic of it all.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world stops making sense: I went to the registry.

Not the guest registry—the staff one, the old leather-bound volumes Dev kept in the engineering office alongside spare fuses and a bottle of rye he believed nobody knew about. Employment records, shift logs, the sedimentary layers of three decades of quiet lives. If Adaeze Okonkwo had been silenced, then someone was working down a list, and lists have logic, and logic leaves fingerprints.

I found what I was looking for in the volume covering 1994, in the section where departing staff were noted. Tomas Reyes, bellhop, employed June 1992. Departure date: August 14, 1994. Reason: *resigned — relocated south.* Written in a hand I recognized instantly, because I have seen it on twenty years of commendations and charity galas and Christmas cards addressed to the whole staff in that same confident, generous loop.

Edmund Hale's handwriting. Hale, who had been manager then, who was manager still in every way that mattered, though the title had passed on and his body was failing him now, room 1201, visiting Tuesdays and Fridays, beloved by everyone including, God help me, me.

Beneath the resignation entry, in different ink, added later, someone had written a single word: *witnessed.* No initials. No date. Just that one word, sitting there like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool.

And below that, in pencil, faint enough that I had to tilt the page against the light: *A.O. knows.*

Adaeze Okonkwo knew. Knew what? That Reyes hadn't resigned? That the drowning she certified wasn't accidental? That the body in the bay last week, wearing my clothes, was a man who had supposedly walked away thirty years ago?

I photographed the page with hands that were not quite steady, then closed the ledger and sat in the engineering office among Dev's orderly tools and listened to the building breathe around me—the tick of pipes, the hum of the boiler, the thousand small sounds of a hotel pretending nothing is wrong.

Six weeks. That's what Voss had let slip during my second interrogation, thinking I didn't notice, or maybe wanting me to notice: the statute clock on whatever ancient charge might still be brought. Six weeks for the truth to matter legally, after which it would only matter morally, and morality has never once cleared a docket.

Someone was erasing witnesses. First Reyes himself, dredged up out of the bay after thirty years like the tide returning what it borrowed. Now Okonkwo, in her own bathtub, with a keycard from my hotel placed beside her like a signature—or a frame.

Which meant the list had a shape, and I could read it. Coroner. Witness. Whoever else had touched that summer. Marisol, who had been a young housekeeper in 1994 and cried for a week without telling anyone why. Ambrose, who worked the door that August and remembered every face that came and went, because remembering faces was his whole religion. Dev, who maintained the sub-basement pumps and knew every inch of the flooded dark below us.

And me. Eight years old, standing in the sub-basement stairwell, seeing something I had spent thirty years refusing to look at directly.

My phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, and I stared at it for a long time before I picked it up, because some part of me already knew.

It was four words long:

*You were always observant.*

No name. No threat. None needed. Just my childhood nickname's crueler cousin, aimed at the thing I'd built my whole life around—the watching, the noticing, the careful invisibility—and turned into a promise.

Outside the window, the fog was coming off the bay in slow grey sheets, swallowing the pier, the boats, the far shore, everything. In six weeks the statute would close like water over a drowning man. And somewhere in this hotel, in one of these rooms, someone who loved this place as much as I did was deciding who got to survive knowing what it had done.

I put the phone face-down on Dev's workbench, next to the soldering iron, and began, very quietly, to make a list of my own.
