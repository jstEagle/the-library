## 10. Greywater Bay Rising Anew

The fog came back on reopening day, which everyone agreed was either an omen or a homecoming, and in Greywater Bay nobody has ever been able to tell the difference.

I stood at the front desk of the Grand Hotel at six in the morning, wearing a uniform that fit me the way it was supposed to—crisp, unremarkable, mine by choice rather than by theft—and watched the tide do its slow, patient work against the seawall. Thirty years I had made myself a ghost in this building. It is a strange thing, being resurrected in the same place you were buried.

The lobby smelled of lemon polish and fresh paint, because Marisol had insisted we repaint the lobby before reopening, and Dev had insisted on rewiring half the third floor while he was at it, and Ambrose had insisted on standing in the doorway supervising them both with the gravity of a man who had been holding that door open for forty-one years and did not intend to break the streak now.

"You're hovering," Marisol said, coming around the desk with a rag and the expression she used on stains that refused to cooperate.

"I'm observing."

"You're hovering. Sit down. You look like a man waiting for the police."

"I was waiting for the police for most of last month. It's habit now."

She flicked the rag at me, a gesture of pure affection, and went back to her polishing. Her hands had shaken for the first week after Hale's collapse. I had watched her try to hide it—wrapping them around teacups, pressing them flat against her apron, folding them in her lap like contraband. They were steady this morning. Mostly.

* * *

Detective Imara Voss arrived at nine, out of uniform in a way that was somehow more official than her uniform—a grey coat, no badge visible, hands in her pockets. She did not come to the desk. She stood at the edge of the lobby, under the chandelier Dev had finally gotten all twelve bulbs working in, and waited for me to come to her. It was, I suspected, a courtesy. Or a test. With Voss it was difficult to distinguish.

"Detective," I said.

"Concierge." She glanced around the lobby, at the fresh paint, at Ambrose by the door, at the small crowd of returning guests beginning to trickle in with their luggage and their suspicions. "It looks good. Alive."

"We did our best. The dead leave a lot of dust."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. Voss did not smile on duty, and I had the sense she was never entirely off duty, but she came closer than usual. "The file's closed. Officially, it was closed by Hale's death. Unofficially—" she paused, choosing her words the way she chose everything, deliberately, "—unofficially, there are some loose threads I've decided not to pull. A retired housekeeper's statement that doesn't quite match a payroll record from 1994. A death certificate that was signed by a woman who, tragically, can no longer clarify her findings. You understand."

"I understand nothing," I said, "and I never have. That's always been my story."

"Your story," she said, "has improved considerably. I read the transcript of the penthouse recording eleven times. Hale confessing like a man unburdening himself at last—did you know he'd been waiting for someone to ask? Thirty years, waiting for someone to ask." She shook her head slowly. "I've spent my career thinking evil was patient. Turns out mostly it's just tired, and proud, and grateful for an audience."

"And the others? Marisol, Dev, Ambrose. The ones who buried the truth to protect each other."

Voss looked at me for a long moment. The fog pressed against the tall windows behind her, turning the lobby into a snow globe of grey.

"The statute expired on a Tuesday," she said. "Three weeks ago. Whatever they did or didn't do in 1994, the law lost its appetite for it a long time ago. What's left is between them and the bay." She turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth, Coldharbour—I never thought you killed anyone. I thought you were hiding from something. I was right. I just had the direction wrong. People hide from the future. You were hiding from the past."

"And now?"

"Now you're standing in the lobby," she said, "in plain sight. So I'd say you stopped." She pulled a card from her pocket and set it on the marble desk, face up. Her number. No note, no explanation. "In case the bay keeps anything else."

* * *

The reopening was small, as reopenings go. A ribbon Ambrose refused to let anyone cut with anything but the original ceremonial scissors, which he had kept in his locker since 1987. A handful of guests. A reporter from the *Greywater Bay Courier* who asked Marisol if the hotel could ever recover from its association with tragedy, and Marisol, who has raised three children and one concierge and buried one secret, looked the woman dead in the eye and said, "Honey, this hotel has been associated with tragedy since 1921. It's called character."

Dev had hung a new bell by the front door—polished brass, loud as a church. He'd found it in the sub-basement, of all places, in a crate marked 1994, and he'd spent a whole evening polishing it before he could bring himself to mount it. I didn't ask him what he was polishing away. Some things you clean, and some things you just make shine.

The sub-basement itself had been drained and sealed. Voss's people had gone through it in the first week, cataloguing what the tide and the decades had kept: Tomas Reyes's effects, a ledger in Hale's handwriting, and the small camp of a man who had survived down there for days on rainwater and hope before Hale found him. I went down once, before the seal went on. I stood where the eight-year-old version of me had stood, at the top of the stairs, watching a shape in the dark that I had spent my whole life telling myself was a trick of the water.

It wasn't a trick. It never was. I had seen a living man, and I had run, because I was a child and children run, and the shame of that had shaped every careful, invisible day of my life since. Ambrose found me down there, standing in the dark with my flashlight off.

"You were eight," he said, as if I'd asked.

"I should have told someone."

"You were eight," he said again, gentler. "And the people you would have told were busy deciding what kind of lie would keep the rest of us alive. You did the only thing a child can do, Sable. You survived it. Took you thirty years to finish the job, but here you are."

* * *

Evening came, and the guests settled, and the lobby went quiet the way old hotels do—creaking into themselves, settling their bones. Marisol locked the office. Dev killed the work lights. Ambrose, who technically no longer worked here, who had retired twice and unretired once, stood by the door out of pure habit and looked at me across the lobby with an expression I had never been able to read until recently, when I finally understood it was the same expression he'd worn the day I arrived at the hotel as a foster placement in 1994: a man deciding to keep something.

"Registry's open," he said. "New book. Marisol got it from the stationer on Harrow Street. First entry's yours, if you want it."

I looked at the ledger lying on the desk. Green leather, gilt edges, a ribbon bookmark. The kind of book that expects things of you.

For thirty years I had signed *S. Coldharbour*—initial and surname, the smallest possible footprint, a name worn like a disguise. Before that, in the children's home records, I had been a case number. The hotel had given me *Sable* the way it had given me everything: quietly, without paperwork, through the accumulated love of people who had no legal claim on me and never once mentioned it.

I picked up the pen. My hand did not shake. That surprised me more than anything else that had happened that autumn.

*Sable Marisol Coldharbour,* I wrote. Full name. Middle name and all, the one Marisol had given me over a birthday cake in the staff kitchen in 1996, half a joke, entirely serious, never once written down until now. I looked at the ink drying on the page and felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched—some small, permanent fist that had been holding itself closed since a foggy morning in 1994.

Marisol came up behind me and read it over my shoulder. She didn't say anything. She put her hand on the back of my neck, the way she did when I was small and feverish, and left it there for a moment, and then went to turn off the last of the lights.

Dev paused at the stairwell. "You know the tide'll come in tonight," he said. "Big one. They said on the radio."

"The tide always comes in," I said. "That's rather the point of it."

Ambrose held the door for the last guest, and then, for the first time in forty-one years, closed it himself and turned the lock, and stood there a moment with his hand on the brass, listening to the building breathe.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the bay in long grey ribbons, and beneath it the water rose and fell, rose and fell, keeping what it keeps and giving back what it's ready to give.

I turned the registry to a fresh page, in case anyone else needed a place to sign.
