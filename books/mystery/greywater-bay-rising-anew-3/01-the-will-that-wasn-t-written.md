## 1. The Will That Wasn't Written

The fog came in off Greywater Bay like a creditor — patient, inevitable, and unwilling to be turned away at the door. I watched it swallow the last mile of coast road from the window of the bus, which was less a bus than a van with delusions, and I thought, not for the first time in my thirty-eight years, that estates are never really about property. They're about what people can't bring themselves to say while they're alive.

My name is August Underhill. I probate wills. It sounds like a small life and mostly it is: stamping documents, soothing heirs, translating grief into legalese. But every so often a file crosses my desk that hums. This one had been humming for two weeks before I ever set foot in the bay.

Elias Vann, fisherman, sixty-one years old, drowned on the night of October 3rd. His boat was found adrift off the point with his boots still in it, which is the kind of detail that makes coroners comfortable and makes me itch. Boots in the boat means he went over deliberately or carelessly; either way, accidental, case closed, everyone home by supper.

Except his will.

I'd read it four times before I arrived, once aloud to my empty office just to hear how insane it sounded. The document was dated October 6th. Three days after Elias Vann went into the water. It named an executor who had predeceased him by eleven years. And it bore the signatures of two witnesses — Maren Vann, his daughter, and one Aldous Crane, harbormaster — both of whom, when contacted by phone, had told me in flat, unbothered voices that they had never signed any such thing and, further, that they had no memory of ever meeting Elias Vann at all.

His daughter. Claiming she'd never met her father.

You can see why I took the ferry instead of flying. I wanted time to think, and there's no thinking to be done at altitude.

* * *

Greywater Bay announced itself as a smell before it became a place: diesel and brine and something older underneath, something like the inside of a locked room. The fog had thinned to a gauze by the time I stepped onto the dock, but it hung around anyway, the way relatives do after a funeral, reluctant to commit to leaving.

The town was built the way towns are when they've stopped expecting anything good to happen: low, weathered, hunched against the water. A cannery that hadn't canned anything since the nineties. A church with a steeple leaning like a man listening hard. And everywhere, the bay itself, grey as its name promised, flat and patient, holding whatever it held.

The law office of Hester Quill occupied the ground floor of a building whose upper floors had been empty long enough to develop opinions. Hester herself was seventy if she was a day, small and dry as a salted cod, and she looked me over through bifocals with the frank appraisal of someone who has buried most of her contemporaries and expects to bury the rest.

"Underhill," she said. "City lawyer."

"August Underhill. Estate practice."

"I know what you practice. I asked the bar association about you." She said this the way other people say *nice weather*. "You closed the Halloran matter without a single appeal. That's rare."

"It's mostly luck."

"No." She unlocked a drawer and produced a folder thick enough to stop a bullet. "Luck is when nobody's lying. Sit down, Mr. Underhill. You're going to want to sit down for this one, though I expect you already know that, or you wouldn't have come all this way for a two-page will."

The office smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the sea, which gets into everything here, I would learn. She laid the will on the desk between us like a card she didn't want to play but couldn't afford to fold.

"You witnessed the filing?" I asked.

"I witnessed the finding. Fisherman's boy found it taped inside the icehouse door, wrapped in oilcloth, three days after they pulled Elias out of the bay. Dry as a sermon. Somebody put it there after the drowning, Mr. Underhill, and somebody made sure it would survive the weather."

"And the signatures?"

"Maren Vann signed it. I've known that girl since she was born — I know her hand like my own. That's her signature, sure as sunrise. And that's Aldous Crane's beneath it, and Aldous has signed a hundred harbor affidavits in front of me." She folded her hands. "They both swear they never signed it. They both swear they never even sat down with Elias to discuss it. Maren says she hasn't spoken to her father in nine years."

"Do you believe them?"

Hester Quill looked at me for a long moment, and behind the bifocals her eyes were the color of the bay and about as warm.

"Mr. Underhill, in this town we don't believe things. We *know* them, or we keep our mouths shut until knowing becomes safe. What I know is that Elias Vann was a careful man with a cruel sense of humor, and if he wrote a will dated after his own death, then he did it on purpose, and he did it expecting you." She pushed the folder across. "Read it again. Slowly. Then go talk to the daughter. And do try not to drown."

* * *

I read it again in my rented room above the Salt Line Tavern, with the window open to the sound of water slapping pilings in the dark.

*Last Will and Testament of Elias Vann*, dated the sixth day of October — three days after the date of death recorded by the coroner, a fact the document seemed almost proud of, as if daring anyone to notice. The estate: the boat, the house on Pilchard Row, the fishing license, four thousand dollars in a credit union, and something listed only as *"the contents of locker seven, Greywater Bay storage yard"* with no inventory attached.

Everything — everything — was left to a single beneficiary: *the estate of Daniel Okafor, deceased 1994.*

I sat with that name for a long time. Daniel Okafor. I knew it. Not from this file — from another one, years ago, back when I was young and still believed closing a case meant finishing it. A deckhand, nineteen years old, drowned off a working boat in the summer of '94. Ruled accidental. File closed. I'd been barely out of law school; I'd reviewed the paperwork as a junior associate doing due diligence on some insurance matter, and I remembered thinking even then that the file was too clean, too tidy, a story with no loose threads, and loose threads are what real accidents are made of.

And now a dead fisherman in a town I'd never visited had left everything he owned to a boy who'd been dead thirty years, via a will dated after his own death, witnessed by two people who denied knowing him.

There were, legally speaking, perhaps six ways to attack such a document, and I lay awake cataloguing them like a miser counting coins. Lack of capacity — except the handwriting was steady, the language precise, the structure exactly what you'd expect from a man who'd drafted and redrafted for months. Improper execution — except the signatures were genuine, per Hester. Fraud — by whom? A dead man? Forgery — to what end? The beneficiaries named had nothing to gain; Okafor's estate, if it even existed anymore, would dissolve into some distant kin or the state.

A will is a voice. That's the first thing they teach you, though they don't say it that way. Every clause is a sentence the testator chose to say and couldn't say out loud. And this voice was saying: *look closer. Look at the date. Look at the name. Look at locker seven.*

It wasn't a will. It was bait.

Somewhere below my window, the tide turned, and the pilings groaned like old men shifting in their sleep. I thought about Elias Vann going into the water with his boots left neatly in the boat, and I thought: a careful man with a cruel sense of humor doesn't leave his boots behind by accident. He leaves them because he knows someone will find the boat, find the boots, find the tidy story, and close the file. And then he leaves a will that reopens it, dated from beyond the grave, addressed — in every way that matters — to whoever is stubborn enough to come asking.

To me, as it happened. Though I didn't yet know why me. I wouldn't know that until the letter came.

But that's ahead of the story.

What I knew that first night was smaller and colder: that I had been summoned, however the summons was dressed up, by a dead man who had planned his own drowning the way other men plan retirements. That the two witnesses were lying, and lying badly, and lying *in unison*, which takes rehearsal. And that somewhere in this fog-bound town was a locker — number seven — that a careful man had filled with something worth killing for, or worth dying for, or possibly both.

I fell asleep with the file open on my chest and dreamed of tide charts, which should have told me something, because I hadn't seen a tide chart in thirty years. Not since my mother used to mark them up at the kitchen table, red pen in hand, humming.

In the morning, I would call on Maren Vann.

She answered the door before I knocked.
