## 9. Threats, Politely Worded

The envelope came on the third morning, riding in among the bills and a postcard from a listener in Duluth who wrote *you have a voice like a locked door, and I mean that as a compliment.*

Ada stood at the kitchen counter of her aunt's boarding house with coffee going cold in one hand and the envelope in the other. It was heavy card stock, cream-colored, expensive in the way that expensive things are expensive—quietly, without needing to announce it. Her name was typed, not handwritten. No return address, but there didn't need to be one. She knew the firm's letterhead before she slid the flap open. Whitcombe & Fenn. The kind of firm whose name appeared on courthouse plaques and yacht club donor walls, the kind that had probably drafted half the bylaws Greywater Bay ran on.

*Dear Ms. Coldharbour,*

*This firm represents the interests of several parties who prefer, for the present, to remain unnamed. We are aware of your recent activities in our community, including but not limited to interviews conducted under false pretenses, inquiries into sealed and adjudicated matters, and the solicitation of town residents regarding events resolved to the satisfaction of this jurisdiction more than two decades ago.*

*We must advise you that continued conduct of this nature may expose you to civil liability, including but not limited to claims of defamation, tortious interference, and harassment. We respectfully request that you cease all such activities immediately.*

*We trust you will give this matter the serious consideration it deserves.*

*Sincerely,*

*Gerald Fenn*

Ada read it twice. Then a third time, standing very still, the way she'd learned to stand when something wanted to knock her over. It was beautifully written. That was the first thing she noticed—the craftsmanship of it. Every clause load-bearing. *Sealed and adjudicated matters.* Maren's drowning had never been adjudicated; it had been waved through, a coroner's verdict signed off between a long weekend and a fishing derby. But *sealed* was doing quiet work in that sentence, wasn't it? Someone had gone back and made sure the file was closed properly, retroactively tidy, like straightening a picture frame after a murder.

And *several parties who prefer to remain unnamed.* Not a client. Clients. Plural, coordinated, frightened enough of a woman with a microphone to spend real money on real paper.

She set the letter down on the counter and looked at it the way she'd taught herself to look at evidence: not as a threat but as a map. A cease-and-desist was a confession wearing a suit. Nobody sent lawyers after a woman chasing ghosts unless the ghosts were getting warm.

"You're up early."

Vera came into the kitchen in her quilted robe, gray hair pinned up crooked, moving toward the coffee pot with the economy of a woman who had been making coffee in this kitchen for forty years. Ada slid the letter beneath a dish towel without deciding to—it was reflex, the old instinct, hide the wound before anyone can see it bleed.

"Lawyer's hours," Ada said.

"Lawyers keep early hours now?" Vera poured her coffee, and her eyes flicked—just barely—to the towel, to the corner of cream stationery showing beneath it, and away again. "In my experience they bill you for every hour God sends and sleep like babies besides."

"Maybe this one's an optimist."

Vera hummed something noncommittal and carried her cup to the window that looked out over the bay. The fog was doing its morning work, erasing the waterline, the chapel's drowned spire, everything past the end of the street. Ada watched her aunt's back and thought about the word *unnamed,* and about how Vera had gone quiet at dinner last night when Julian Vane's marina came up on the news, and how some silences had shapes, if you looked at them long enough.

"I'll be out most of the day," Ada said.

"Mm." Vera sipped. "Mind the fog on the coast road. It doesn't lift till noon, and it doesn't care what you're driving."

It was as close to tenderness as the house offered before eight a.m., and Ada took it, because she'd learned to take whatever was given.

* * *

She spent the morning being polite right back.

If Gerald Fenn wanted to play at formality, she'd return his serve. She typed a reply on her laptop at the library—her aunt's Wi-Fi went mysteriously dead whenever she used it for uploads, another small courtesy—and printed it at ten cents a page. The librarian, a stooped man named Osgood who'd been shelving books since before Maren died, watched her print with the frank curiosity of someone who had nothing left to pretend about.

*Dear Mr. Fenn,*

*Thank you for your correspondence. I note that your clients prefer to remain unnamed, which is their right. I would remind you that journalism, including podcast journalism, is protected activity in this state, and that truth is an absolute defense to defamation. Should any of your unnamed clients wish to discuss the facts of July 1999 or October of this year, my door—and my recording equipment—are open.*

*I would also note, for the record, that I have retained counsel of my own. She is excellent, she works pro bono for journalists, and she finds cease-and-desist letters sent to grieving sisters to be excellent material for counterclaims of intimidation. Feel free to share that with your clients. All of them.*

*Warm regards,*

*Ada Coldharbour*

She didn't actually have a lawyer. Yet. But Ruth Vane's niece had married a media attorney in Seattle, and Ada had already emailed her, and the woman had replied within the hour with a single line: *Greywater Bay? Oh, honey. Send me everything.* That was as good as a retainer until proven otherwise.

She mailed the letter, bought stamps she didn't need, and let herself feel, for exactly one block of walking, the clean hot satisfaction of having answered power with paperwork. Then she got back to her car—the rental, because her own car was still in Seattle with a keyed gash down both doors that the body shop quoted eleven hundred dollars to fix—and found the note.

It was under the wiper blade.

Not mailed. Not slipped under a door. Hand-delivered, while she'd been inside the post office for perhaps six minutes, in a town where everyone knew everyone and strangers were spotted the way gulls spot fish. The paper was cheap, torn from a legal pad, the handwriting slanted and hurried, ballpoint pressed hard enough to emboss the lines into the page beneath:

*Maren talked too. Look how that ended.*

Ada stood on the sidewalk with the note in her hand and the fog pressing in from the harbor, and for a moment—one long, cold, tidal moment—she was nineteen again, standing on these same streets, learning that her sister was dead.

The town moved around her. A woman pushed a stroller. Two men argued cheerfully about crab quotas outside the bait shop. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. Life, indifferent, enormous, going on around the small frozen figure of a woman holding four words that had been written by someone who knew exactly where she'd be and when.

*Maren talked too.*

Not *your sister asked questions.* Not *people who dig here get hurt.* The phrasing was specific. Intimate. *Maren talked too* meant the writer knew what Maren had talked about—or believed she had. It meant the writer connected Maren's death to her speaking, which was not the official story, which had never been the official story, which only three people alive could plausibly connect: whoever killed her, whoever helped bury it, and whoever had listened at the time and said nothing since.

Ada folded the note along its existing creases and put it in her coat pocket, over her heart, where she kept the things that hurt.

Then she walked back to the boarding house, made herself a sandwich she ate without tasting, sat down at the kitchen table, and did what she always did with fear. She worked.

* * *

Here was the thing nobody understood about Ada Coldharbour, the thing the podcast listeners thought they understood and got wrong: she was not brave. Brave people felt fear and walked through it, glowing. Ada simply refused to let fear be seen. It was a discipline older than the podcast, older than Seattle, older than the night she'd packed a duffel bag at nineteen and sworn she'd never come back. When the shaking started—and it was starting now, her hands unsteady over the notebook, the pen skipping—she treated it like weather. You didn't argue with weather. You dressed for it and went out anyway.

She opened the notebook to a fresh page and wrote the note out verbatim, timing herself against the original, checking the letterforms. Then she made lists.

Who delivered it: someone local, someone mobile, someone who knew her schedule well enough to strike during a six-minute window. That narrowed it brutally. Either she was being watched continuously—which meant resources, which meant money, which pointed uphill toward Fenn's unnamed clients—or someone close to her orbit was reporting her movements. The boarding house had three other lodgers: a retired ferry mechanic named Dolph who slept till noon, a birdwatching couple who'd left for a week on the Olympic Peninsula, and a silent young man in room four who paid cash and kept odd hours. Or it was Vera. Or it was someone watching the house from outside, and there were plenty of windows facing the street, and plenty of reasons a neighbor might glance out and reach for a phone.

What it meant: escalation. The lawyer's letter was the institutional response—impersonal, deniable, designed to frighten with process. The note was personal. The note was a hand, not a firm. Institutions threatened you when they wanted you to stop. Individuals threatened you when they were afraid you wouldn't.

Which meant the strategy was working, and which meant the danger was real, and both of those things could be true at once, and usually were.

She photographed the note against a neutral background, front and back, with a scale reference—a coin, per protocol. She logged the time of discovery, the weather, the location, the estimated window of delivery. She photographed the lawyer's letter too, all three pages, though it was only one. Then she scanned both into the encrypted folder she called COLD FILE / GREYWATER, alongside the locket photographs, the tide tables, the interview audio from Ruth Vane's interrupted confession, and the growing dossier on the founding families that was beginning to look less like research and more like an indictment.

Her phone buzzed. Rook.

*Heard you got mail. Both kinds.*

She stared at the screen. He knew. Of course he knew—Fenn would have copied the police chief, or the chief heard it at the diner, or the whole town simply breathed it in off the fog the way it breathed in everything. In Greywater Bay, information wasn't transmitted. It seeped.

*How?* she typed.

*Fenn brags. Also the postmistress is my informant network. You want coffee later or are you busy being threatened?*

There it was—that dry, sideways humor he deployed like a shield, and beneath it, unmistakable even in text, the question he was really asking: *are you okay.* She considered lying. She considered the performance of fine that she'd been giving the entire world since she was nineteen years old, the flawless production, twenty years running, no intermission.

*Busy,* she typed. *But bring the coffee to me. I'm not driving anywhere today.*

A pause, three dots appearing and disappearing.

*That bad?*

*That instructive,* she answered, and put the phone face-down before he could see her smile, which was thin and sharp and entirely for herself.

Because here was the other thing, the thing she'd admit to no one, maybe not even to herself in the dark: some part of her had exhaled when she read the note. Not relief—nothing so clean. But recognition. For twenty years she had operated in the dark, solving deaths no one cared about, whispering justice into earbuds, invisible, effective, alone. And the loneliness of that work was a specific loneliness, the loneliness of knowing things no one else knew and being unable to say so without ending the invisibility that kept the work possible. The threats were proof she'd stopped being invisible. The threats were the sound of the case waking up. If they were frightened enough to send lawyers and leave notes, then somewhere in this fog-bound town, behind a named door, someone was afraid of a dead girl's sister, and fear made people careless, and carelessness was where the truth lived.

She just had to survive being right.

* * *

Rook arrived at four, in his department SUV, carrying two paper cups and a paper bag that smelled like the fry shack on the wharf. He'd changed out of his uniform shirt into a flannel that made him look less like a cop and more like what he probably was underneath—somebody's brother, somebody's ex, a man who'd ended up in Greywater Bay the way driftwood ends up on a beach, deposited by forces he didn't fully explain.

"Fenn's letter," he said, handing her a cup. "I pulled the firm's client list. It's public, mostly. Harrow Shipping. Vane Marina Group. Crane's chambers use them for court filings. Take those three names, add a few smaller accounts, and you've got basically the entire municipal power structure sharing one law firm."

"One firm, many clients." Ada took her cup. "Conflicts of interest don't seem to trouble them."

"In a town this size, conflict of interest isn't a bug. It's the org chart." He dropped into the chair across from her, and his eyes went, briefly, to the dish towel on the counter. Still covering the letter. Old habits. "You want to show me the other thing? The one that didn't come from a lawyer?"

She held his gaze for a moment. Weighing. This was the part she hated—not the fear itself but the decision of whether to spend it, whether to show anyone the soft underside of the operation. Showing weakness to a cop assigned to stop her was operational risk. Showing weakness to Elias Rook, specifically, sitting in her aunt's kitchen smelling of salt and fried food, was something else entirely, something she didn't have a category for yet.

She took the note from her pocket and laid it on the table between them, unfolded flat, and watched his face as he read it. He was good—she'd give him that. His expression barely moved. But his jaw tightened, a small mechanical event, and his thumb pressed white against the edge of the table, and when he looked up his eyes weren't dry-humored anymore.

"When?" he said.

"Between 11:40 and 11:46 this morning. Post office parking lot."

"Anyone approach the car?"

"It's a rental with out-of-state plates in a town of four thousand people. Everyone approaches the car. That's rather the point."

He almost smiled at that, then didn't. He pulled out his phone, photographed the note from several angles, then set the phone down. "You know what this is?"

"A threat."

"It's a confession of knowledge." He tapped the table beside the note without touching it. "*Maren talked too.* Whoever wrote this knows why she died. The official story is accident. This says otherwise, in handwriting, on paper, from somebody who was either there or talked to someone who was. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Somebody just handed you corroboration because they panicked."

"I know." Ada wrapped both hands around her cup. "That's why I'm not scared."

"That's a lie."

"It's a working posture." She met his eyes and let him see a fraction of what was underneath—the nineteen-year-old on the sidewalk, the shaking hands, the sandwich she hadn't tasted. Just a fraction. An installment payment on trust, not the whole loan. "Elias. I've done this before. Three times. Three 'accidents' that weren't, three families that got answers, three times I received letters worse than this one and kept working. I know what fear costs. I pay it in private and I don't itemize."

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the fog was finally lifting off the bay, and the light came into the kitchen low and gold, catching the steam off their cups.

"What happened to them?" he asked. "The three. Your solved cases."

"They got the truth. That's all I ever promised anybody. Not arrests, not convictions. Truth." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "One of them cried on the phone for an hour. One thanked me and never spoke to me again. One sent me a Christmas card every year until she died. That's the job. You hand people the worst day of their lives, reframed, and then you go home alone."

"And the people who caused the accidents? What happened to them?"

Ada looked at him across the table, at this man sent to stop her who kept showing up with coffee instead, and told him a piece of the truth because he'd earned a piece. "Sometimes nothing. Sometimes the truth does its slow work and the wheels grind. Once—" she paused, choosing, "—once, the man who did it read my episode, drove to the widow's house, and confessed to her directly. I never went public. He did. The truth found its own exit. I just opened the door."

"And this time?"

"This time the door's already open, and something on the other side is looking back at me." She nodded at the note. "They're not trying to scare me off, Elias. Read it again. *Maren talked too. Look how that ended.* That's not deterrence. That's a warning shot with a message attached: we did it once, we'll do it again. Which means they think I can be handled the same way she was. Which means—" her voice stayed level, she made sure of it, level and cool as deep water, "—they don't know me at all."

Rook studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to settle something in him, or unsettle it; with him it was hard to tell the difference.

"There's a unit in Seattle that handles threats against journalists," he said. "I can make a call."

"Don't."

"Ada—"

"If you call Seattle, this becomes a federal file, and a federal file becomes a reason to seize my research as evidence, and my research sits in an evidence locker until the trial that will never happen because Judge Crane recuses himself and his friends appoint someone reasonable." She shook her head. "No. These stay local. These stay mine. You want to help me? Find out who has access to legal-pad paper and a grudge. Start with the marina office. Start with whoever Fenn's firm hired for 'field services'—they always hire someone local for the ugly work, keeps the partners' hands clean."

He wrote it down, which pleased her more than she expected—this detective taking notes in her aunt's kitchen, officially off-duty, unofficially hers.

At the door, leaving, he paused with his hand on the frame. "You eat anything today besides that sandwich?"

"That's a strange question for a detective to ask."

"It's a strange case. Everybody in it keeps pretending they're fine." His eyes moved over her face, cataloguing, the way he probably catalogued crime scenes—methodically, gently, missing nothing. "For the record, Coldharbour, I don't think you're fine. I think you're the toughest person in this town and also the most alone, and I think those two things are connected, and I think whoever wrote that note made the second-biggest mistake of their life."

"And the biggest?"

"Killing Maren Joubert and thinking nobody would come." He said it plainly, no drama, and left before she could answer, because he was a man who knew when to exit a scene.

Ada stood in the doorway and watched his taillights dissolve into the fog coming back off the water. Then she went inside, locked the door, checked the locks, checked them again, and sat down at the kitchen table with the note spread out in front of her under the lamp.

Fear she refused to show anyone. Fine. But alone, in the yellow circle of lamplight, with the town breathing outside and the tide coming in somewhere past the fog, she allowed herself thirty seconds of it—thirty seconds of shaking hands and shallow breath and the animal certainty that she was being hunted by people who had done it before. Thirty seconds, timed on her watch, the way she timed everything.

Then she wiped her eyes, squared the note beside the lawyer's letter, and opened her notebook to a fresh page.

At the top she wrote: *WHO KNEW WHAT MAREN TALKED ABOUT?*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, the question that had been forming all day, the one the note had handed her whether it meant to or not: *AND WHO ELSE DID THEY TELL IT TO?*

Because *Maren talked too* implied a list. A roster of talkers. And if Maren was on it—if Maren had been silenced for speaking—then somewhere in this town there might be others who'd spoken and survived, or spoken and been paid, or were still speaking, quietly, in kitchens, waiting twenty years for someone to ask.

Tomorrow she'd ask. Tomorrow she'd drive out to the Vane place again, uninvited, undeterred, and sit across from Ruth Vane with her recorder running, and see whether the old woman's courage had grown or shrunk since her son cut the interview short.

But tonight, before bed, she did one more thing. She took the note to the window, held it up to the lamp, and angled it flat—and there, faintly, embossed into the next sheet down when the writer had pressed too hard, a ghost of indentation. Words from whatever had been written on the page above. She couldn't read them all. But three letters at the start of one line were legible, deep and deliberate, and they spelled out the beginning of a name.

Three letters. *Jul.*
