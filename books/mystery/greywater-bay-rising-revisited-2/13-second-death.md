## 13. Second Death

The call came at 6:14 in the morning, and Elias Rook knew before he answered that it was going to be bad.

Not because of the hour—bad news kept its own schedule in a town like Greywater Bay, where the fog rolled in off the water and swallowed everything past the breakwater until mid-morning, and where men who'd spent their lives on the bay had a habit of dying in it. He knew because of the voice on the other end. Deputy Okafor was twenty-six years old, steady as a metronome, and she sounded like someone reading from a script she hadn't rehearsed.

"Detective. We've got a body on the water. Tom Pell. His boat's drifting about two hundred yards off the old cannery pier."

Rook sat up in the rented room above the hardware store, his second week in Greywater Bay, the mattress still smelling of someone else's cigarettes. "Pell. The archivist?"

"You know him?"

"I know *of* him." Rook was already pulling on his boots. "Everybody knows of everybody here. That's the problem."

He drove out with his headlights cutting cones through the fog, wipers going even though it wasn't raining—the mist was thick enough to warrant them—and thought about Tom Pell the way you think about a man you've only met once across a counter. Sixty-something. Cardigan in all weather. Hands stained with the particular rust-brown of old paper and older ink. Pell had worked the cannery records room for thirty years, cataloguing manifests and payrolls for a company that had stopped existing before Rook was born, and he did it, by all accounts, because somebody had to.

And because, Rook now understood, sitting up straighter behind the wheel, Tom Pell had recently been talking to Ada Coldharbour.

The boat came into view the way things come into view in fog: first a shadow, then a shape, then the whole sad fact of it. A nineteen-foot skiff, white paint gone chalky, bobbing in the flat gray swell maybe fifty yards off the cannery pier. Two deputies in a Zodiac alongside it. One of them looked up as Rook's cruiser crunched onto the gravel lot, and the look on the man's face told him everything before anyone said a word.

The ramp down to the float was slick with weed. Rook took it slow, feeling the bay move under him, that faint breathing motion the water made even when there was no wind. The Zodiac bumped against the skiff's hull. Tom Pell lay in the bottom of the boat on his side, one arm folded under his head like a man napping on a park bench, his other hand resting open on the thwart. His face was gray in the way faces get when the blood has stopped bothering to argue. There was no wound. No blood. No sign of struggle.

Just a man dead in a boat, alone, in the fog.

"Heart attack," Deputy Marsh said, crouched at the gunwale. "Looks like. Doc Ellery's coming out, but—" He shrugged, and the shrug said the rest: *What else would it be? Old guy, bad heart, cold morning. Sad, but simple.*

Rook didn't answer right away. He leaned over the gunwale and looked, really looked, the way he'd been trained to look in a city where nothing was ever simple and everyone had a reason to lie. Pell's boots were dry. That was the first thing. A man who'd gone out on the water at dawn in October fog would have wet boots unless he'd never left the cabin—or unless he'd been put in the boat after it was already floating. The skiff's outboard was tilted down, fuel cap secure, tank three-quarters full. There was a thermos wedged against the seat, upright, unspilled. If Pell had died mid-crisis, clutching his chest, thrashing—something would have spilled. Something always spilled.

"Who found him?" Rook asked.

"Harbormaster. Routine run at six. Boat was just... out there."

"Anybody see him launch?"

Marsh shook his head. "Fog was heavy. Nobody's out much this time of year anyway."

*Of course they aren't,* Rook thought. *Nobody sees anything in Greywater Bay. That's practically the town motto.*

He photographed the boat from every angle, though he knew the photographs were theater. By noon this would be an accident. By evening, a sad story in the *Greywater Ledger* under a headline about a beloved local archivist. By the weekend, Tom Pell would be a paragraph in the eulogy section between a church bake sale notice and an ad for winterized engine oil. Rook had seen small towns do this work before. They were efficient at it. Grief moved through fast and paved everything flat behind it.

But the second letter had promised otherwise.

He'd read it four times since Ada Coldharbour had shown it to him over bad coffee at the diner on the edge of town—*the next one soon*—and he'd filed it, officially, as a crank letter, a cruelty, the kind of thing that crawled out of the woodwork whenever a true-crime podcast pointed its microphone at a quiet place. Unofficially, he'd done what he always did with things that bothered him: turned them over in the dark, again and again, looking for the seam.

*The next one soon.*

Tom Pell had contacted Ada ten days ago. Rook knew because Ada had told him—laid it out with that flat, careful precision she used when she was handing him something she wanted him to take seriously without admitting she wanted anything at all. Pell had called her. Said there were records from the summer Maren Joubert drowned that didn't add up. Said the cannery's shipping manifests for July of that year had pages missing—not torn out, which would have been obvious, but renumbered, which took patience and access and a reason. He'd offered to pull the originals.

Ten days later, Tom Pell was dead in his own boat with dry boots and an unspilled thermos.

Rook stood in the rocking skiff and felt the cold come up through his soles, and understood, with a clarity that tasted like copper, that he was standing inside the second letter. The promise had been kept. Kept neatly, quietly, in a way that required no explanation and would invite none. A heart attack. In a town full of heart attacks. On a morning too foggy for witnesses.

The only person in Greywater Bay who would look at this scene and see murder instead of misfortune was currently holed up at her aunt's boarding house with a keyed car and a stack of cease-and-desist letters, and the department's official position—one Rook's captain had delivered with a smile that didn't reach his eyes—was that Ada Coldharbour was a nuisance, an agitator, a woman who profited off other people's grief, and that Detective Rook's job was to keep an eye on her before she stirred up trouble the town couldn't afford.

*Trouble the town can't afford.* Rook had heard that phrase twice in two weeks, once from his captain and once, verbatim, from a lawyer named Whitcombe who smelled like money and wore his authority like cologne. It was starting to sound less like a concern and more like a budget line.

* * *

By eight o'clock the fog had burned back to the tree line and the cannery pier stood exposed in hard morning light, all rusted pilings and rotted decking, gulls screaming over the fish heads scattered on the float. The medical examiner's people had come and gone. Doc Ellery himself—a retired GP who did the county's autopsies out of a converted garage—had pronounced it, preliminarily, cardiac arrest, consistent with natural causes, pending review of Pell's history. Pell had a history. Hypertension, a stent placed six years ago, a brother who'd dropped dead at fifty-eight. The narrative wrote itself. It always did, here.

Rook walked the pier while the harbor crew towed the skiff in. At the far end, where the boards gave way to bare stringers, someone had carved initials into a piling decades ago—*T.P. + L.M.*, wrapped in a lopsided heart. He ran his thumb over the grooves. Linda Marsh, probably, or Linda Mercer—he'd have to check—but the point was that Tom Pell had been a boy here once, in love, immortal, carving his name into wood that would outlast him. Everyone in this town had been young once, and everyone seemed determined to pretend it hadn't happened.

His phone buzzed. Dispatch. Then, a moment later, a text from Ada Coldharbour, which was itself becoming a thing he had to manage—how quickly she'd gotten his number, how naturally the messages arrived, how little he minded.

**ADA:** I heard about Tom Pell. Is it true?

He stared at the screen for a long moment. There was a version of this where he said *it's an apparent heart attack, pending autopsy, please don't make noise*, the version where he did his job the way the job was designed. And there was another version.

**ROOK:** Found in his boat off the cannery pier. No signs of trauma. ME says looks natural.

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

**ADA:** Did he have his glasses on?

Rook frowned. He pulled up the scene photos, thumbed through them, and felt the hair rise on his forearms. Pell wasn't wearing his glasses. And Rook had seen the man's file photo from his fishing license—wire-rimmed, thick-lensed, the kind of prescription a man couldn't cross a room without. More than that: in the photographs of the boat's cabin, tucked into the chart holder beside the wheel, sat a pair of wire-rimmed glasses, folded neatly, waiting.

A man rowing or motoring out at dawn in fog doesn't take his glasses off and fold them. A man having a heart attack doesn't stop to do it either.

**ROOK:** Where are you?

**ADA:** The boarding house. Why?

**ROOK:** Stay there. Don't go to the pier. Don't post anything yet. I'm coming to you.

He pocketed the phone and stood a moment longer at the end of the pier, looking out at the flat bright water where a skiff had drifted all night carrying a dead man, and let himself say the thing internally that he could not yet say out loud: *They killed him. Whoever sent those letters killed him, and they did it the day after he offered to pull the manifests, and everyone in this town is going to nod sadly and call it a heart attack, and I am the only cop here who thinks that's a problem.*

Except not the only one. That was the trouble. That was exactly the trouble.

* * *

The boarding house sat on Cormorant Street with its paint peeling in long gray curls, and Aunt Vera answered the door before Rook knocked twice, as if she'd been watching him come up the walk. She was a small woman with iron-gray hair pinned back and eyes that assessed him the way dockworkers assess weather.

"She's in the kitchen," Vera said. "I'll be in the parlor."

The kitchen smelled of coffee and, underneath it, something medicinal—Vera's arthritis liniment, maybe, or just the house's own old smell. Ada sat at the table with her laptop closed and her hands wrapped around a mug she wasn't drinking, and when she looked up at Rook, he saw something in her face he hadn't seen before. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman watching a pattern complete itself.

"Tell me," she said.

So he told her. The boat, the position of the body, the dry boots, the thermos, the glasses folded in the chart holder. He watched her take each detail the way a locksmith takes the dimensions of a key. When he got to the glasses, she closed her eyes briefly, and when she opened them they were wet, but her voice stayed level.

"He called me Tuesday," she said. "Nine days ago. He was nervous—I could hear it. He kept his voice down like someone might be listening, and I remember thinking, *Tom, nobody's listening, it's a phone call in 2024.* But he lowered his voice anyway. Twenty years of working in that records room for those families—you don't shake that. He said the manifests for July, the year my sister died, had been renumbered. He said a man doesn't renumber pages unless he's hiding a gap. And he said—" she paused, and Rook watched her choose whether to give him the last of it, and watched her decide to, because that was who she was, all evidence to the contrary; she kept giving pieces of herself away to people who hadn't earned them— "he said, *Miss Coldharbour, your sister's name is in that gap. I'm sure of it. She signed the cannery books that summer. Part-time, nights. And her signature stops three weeks before she did.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard ticked as the house cooled.

"Her signature stops three weeks before she did," Rook repeated.

"Maren worked nights at the cannery office. Nobody ever mentioned it. Not in any report, not in any article, not in anything. The police file has her as a waitress at the Anchor Inn, full stop. But Tom found her name in the payroll ledgers. Which means she was somewhere, doing something, three weeks before she drowned, that somebody spent real effort making disappear." Ada turned the mug a quarter turn on the table. "And now the man who found that is dead in his boat, and in twelve hours this town will have decided he died of a bad heart, and anyone who suggests otherwise will be told they're grieving, or hysterical, or chasing podcast numbers."

"That's why I told you not to post."

"And that's why you're here instead of at the station writing your report." She said it without heat, almost gently, and it landed harder than an accusation would have. Rook looked at her and she held his gaze, and something passed between them that neither of them named—the understanding that he had already crossed some line, that showing up here with the details of an open scene was not what a detective assigned to *monitor* a podcaster did, and that they both knew it, and that knowing it changed nothing.

"The ME's preliminary is natural causes," Rook said. "Ellery's thorough, but he's also seventy-three and does forty autopsies a year for a county that doesn't like surprises. Toxicology will take six weeks. By then—" 

"By then the ground's paved." She nodded slowly. "I know how it works. I've spent nine years documenting how it works. Small towns don't cover things up, Detective. They just decline to look. There's a difference, and the difference is that nobody has to be guilty of anything." Her jaw tightened. "Except somebody is. Somebody put Tom Pell in that boat."

"Or he went out on the water because he liked the water, and his heart quit. People do die."

"People do." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, and he saw they were trembling very slightly, and saw her will them still. "Elias. I've solved three deaths that three jurisdictions called accidents. I know what coincidence smells like, and I know what staging smells like, and I know that the second letter said *the next one soon* and nine days later a man who contacted me about my sister's case is dead in a way that requires no investigation. I am not asking you to believe me. I'm telling you what I know, and I'm telling you that I'm going to find out what Tom Pell found, and whoever killed him knows that too, which means I'm probably next on the list, and I need you to tell me honestly—are you here because you want to catch them, or are you here because your captain wants a leash on me?"

It was the question he'd been avoiding for two weeks, and she asked it the way she did everything—directly, without armor, offering him the truth of her own fear as collateral. Rook thought about his captain's smile. About *trouble the town can't afford*. About the renumbered pages and the folded glasses and the dry boots, and about a girl named Maren whose signature stopped three weeks before she did.

"I was sent here to stop you," he said. "That was the assignment. Watch the podcaster, keep the peace, make sure nothing catches fire." He held her eyes. "I'm telling you the manifest pages are missing, the glasses were folded, and I drove straight here from the scene instead of writing it up. You tell me what that makes me."

For a moment she didn't answer, and the silence between them had a different weight than any silence they'd shared before—less like a truce, more like a door opening.

Then her phone buzzed on the table. She glanced down, and Rook watched the color leave her face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"What is it?" he said.

She turned the phone so he could see. An email, sent forty minutes ago, from an address that had been created yesterday. No subject line. The body was a single sentence:

*Tom talked too. Now you have his files. Better hurry—they won't stay lost forever.*

Attached was a photograph: a cardboard records box, lid half off, folders visible, sitting on a wooden floor in a room Rook didn't recognize. And in the corner of the frame, half-cut by the edge of the shot, a wall calendar from a year that ended in a number that made both of them go still.

The year Maren drowned.

Ada looked up at him, and her voice, when it came, was barely above the hum of the refrigerator.

"Somebody wants me to find them," she said. "And I think I just figured out who."
