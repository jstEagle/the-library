## 21. Ruth Vane Dies

The call came at 6:47 in the morning, and Ada knew before she answered.

She knew the way you know a storm is coming—not from any single sign but from the pressure in your ears, the way the gulls go quiet, the way your body remembers what your mind refuses to calculate. The phone buzzed against the nightstand of her aunt's spare room and she lay in the grey half-light watching it shudder across the wood, and she thought: *not yet, not yet, I only had one more day with her.*

Then she picked it up, because that was the whole of who she was—the woman who always picked up.

"Ms. Coldharbour." Detective Rook's voice, stripped down to its studs, no warmth left in it to soften what came next. "Ruth Vane was found dead this morning. Her housekeeper discovered her at the bottom of the back stairs around five-thirty."

The room tilted. Ada gripped the edge of the mattress and made herself breathe.

"I was supposed to see her today," she said. "Eleven o'clock. She called me Tuesday night. She said she'd finished something she wanted me to have—in person, not by mail. She said—" Ada stopped. Her voice had gone somewhere thin and strange. "She said she was tired of being careful."

On the other end of the line, Rook said nothing for three full seconds. In those seconds Ada heard everything: the wind through his open window, the distant clang of a buoy bell, and beneath it all the sound of a man deciding how much truth he could afford a woman like her.

"Fall down the stairs," he said finally. "That's the preliminary finding. Seventy-eight years old, bad hip, house on a hill. It happens."

"It happens," Ada repeated.

"Ada." His voice dropped, and something in it—something urgent and carefully banked—made her sit up straighter. "I'm telling you this before it hits the scanner because I'd rather you hear it from me. And I'm telling you as a courtesy, not an invitation. Do you understand the difference?"

"No," she said honestly. "But thank you for calling anyway."

She hung up and sat in the grey light with her hands flat on her knees, and the guilt arrived exactly on schedule, heavy and familiar as tide.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not stop performing: Ruth Vane had been silent for twenty years. Silent through the drowning verdict, silent through the funeral where she'd stood at the edge of the crowd in a black coat, silent through two decades of Sunday services and marina galas and the slow rot of a town that knew what it knew and called it weather. Then Ada Coldharbour came home with a microphone and a grudge, and within six weeks Ruth Vane had written one letter, nearly spoken into a recorder, mailed a partial confession—and died on her stairs the night before she could finish the work.

*You did this,* said the stone in her chest. *You knocked on her door. You put your questions in her mouth like lit matches.*

And underneath the stone, quieter, colder, another voice—the one that had kept her alive through three solved cases and twenty years of solitude—answered: *Or someone else did. Someone who watched her mailbox and her kitchen window and waited until the old woman's courage finally outweighed her fear, and then made sure the fear won permanently.*

Both things were true. That was the cruelty of it. Both things were true at once, and no amount of distance or discipline would separate them.

Ada got dressed in the dark.

* * *

By eight o'clock the Vane house on Beacon Hill had already grown its crowd.

They gathered the way gulls gather over fish guts—quickly, loudly, without shame. Two cruisers angled in the drive. A coroner's van idled with its hazards on. Neighbors stood along the hedgerow in bathrobes and rain jackets, murmuring into their collars, and every single one of them turned to watch when Ada parked at the curb and got out.

She felt their eyes move over her like hands. She kept walking.

The Vane house was the oldest thing on the hill after the lighthouse—a tall, narrow Victorian the color of driftwood, built by Julian's great-grandfather back when the Vane fortune was still measured in fish rather than slips and fuel docks. Ada had been inside once, years ago, some charity thing Maren had dragged her to. She remembered a staircase. She was thinking about that staircase now, standing at the police tape with her press badge in her pocket and no legitimate reason on earth to be there.

"Ms. Coldharbour."

Officer Denny Marsh—young, broad, the son of a crab boat captain Ada had gone to school with—stepped into her path with the apologetic rigidity of a man following orders he didn't enjoy. "I can't let you past the tape."

"I'm not asking to." She held up her phone. "I'm asking you to tell Detective Rook I'm here, and that I'll wait in my car as long as it takes, and that I'm not leaving Greywater Bay until I understand what happened to that woman."

Denny's jaw worked. Behind him, up the walk, the front door stood open and Ada could see into the hall—could see, just barely, the foot of the back stairs and a rectangle of pale floor where something had been laid and then taken away.

"She was going to talk to me today," Ada said, softer now. "You can tell him that too."

Denny looked at her for a long moment, and something human moved behind his professional blankness. He'd been maybe ten when Maren drowned. Everyone in this town had been somebody's age when Maren drowned.

"I'll pass it along," he said.

She went back to the car. She sat with the windows fogging and watched the house, and at 9:20 Rook came out, descended the steps, and crossed the lawn to her window without breaking stride, rapping twice on the glass. She rolled it down. The morning air came in cold and salted.

"You're making my job harder," he said.

"I know."

"The medical examiner is leaning natural. Cardiac event on the stairs, blunt trauma consistent with a fall. There's no sign of forced entry, no sign of struggle, nothing disturbed." He recited it flatly, a man reading coordinates off a chart. "Her son arrived last night. Julian flew in from Portland around nine. Housekeeper says he went straight to bed."

"And you believe all of it."

Rook looked back at the house. Upstairs, a curtain shifted in a second-floor window and settled again. "I believe seventy-eight-year-old women fall down stairs," he said. "I also believe that in the last month, three people connected to Maren Joubert's death have either talked to you, tried to talk to you, or died. Pell. Now Ruth. And I believe whoever sent you those letters keeps their promises." He turned back to her, and this close she could see the sleeplessness ground into him, the bruised skin under his eyes. "Which means the next name on that list might be yours, and I am professionally obligated to prevent that, and personally—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. "Did she say anything to you? On the phone Tuesday. Anything at all."

Ada told him the truth. All of it. The call at 8:40, the old woman's voice papery and resolute, the sentence Ada had replayed forty times since dawn: *I've finished it, dear. Twenty years is long enough to be a coward. Come tomorrow and I'll give you the rest, and then you can do whatever you like with it—I'm too old to care anymore.*

"She sounded free," Ada said. "That's what I keep coming back to. She sounded like someone who'd put down something heavy."

Rook absorbed that. Somewhere behind them, the coroner's van pulled away, empty, its work done at the house. "If there's a letter," he said slowly, "it didn't leave with her body. We searched. No papers on her, nothing in the obvious places—desk, safe, bedside table. Julian's requested copies of everything she owned, which tells you the family's already circling."

"Then it's somewhere he wouldn't think to look," Ada said. "Somewhere she hid things her whole life. Rook—she was married to a man who ran smuggling routes through a chapel tunnel. She raised a son in a house full of secrets. Women like Ruth don't keep important paper in desks."

He studied her. Rain had started again, fine as spray, beading on his shoulders. "Where, then?"

"I don't know yet," Ada admitted. "But I knew her for six weeks, and I know what she reached for when she was frightened. When her son interrupted our first interview, she picked up a hymnal off the side table and held it in her lap for the rest of the conversation. Turned the pages without reading them. Like a hand on a rail."

Something flickered across Rook's face—recognition, and the war that came with it. She watched him weigh it: the letter of his orders against the shape of the truth pressing up under them like a body under ice.

"If you set one foot inside that tape," he said at last, quietly, "I will arrest you. Do you hear me? I will cuff you in front of all these neighbors and I will enjoy it, because it will be easier than what I want to do instead."

"What do you want to do instead?"

He didn't answer. He walked back up the lawn through the fine rain, and Ada sat in her car and understood that she had just been given something that wasn't permission but functioned exactly like it.

* * *

The church of St. Brendan-by-the-Sea sat at the bottom of Beacon Hill, squat and granite-grey, its shingles furred with lichen. Ada had avoided it for nineteen years—it was where Maren's funeral had been held, where the whole town had filed past the closed casket because the water had kept most of her daughter for eleven days—but grief makes strange doors, and by noon she was pushing through the side entrance into air that smelled of candle wax and cold stone.

The nave was empty. Wednesday midday, no service for hours. Ada moved down the aisle between the pews, her footsteps swallowed by the carpet, past the memorial plaques bolted to the walls—HARROW, VANE, CRANE, generation after generation of founding-family brass, donors to God the way they'd donated to everything else, purchasing absolution by the square foot.

The pew racks held hymnals by the dozen. Navy cloth, gold-stamped, identical. Ada lifted one out at random and felt the absurdity of it wash over her: a woman searching a church for a dead friend's secret, working from a gesture she'd glimpsed once across a sitting room.

But Ruth Vane had been coming to this church for fifty years. If she had a habit, it lived here.

Ada started at the front pew—the Harrow pew, according to the little brass plate—and worked backward. She checked each hymnal quickly at first, riffling pages, feeling for loose paper, then more slowly as the minutes stacked up and nothing surfaced. Psalms. Advent carols. The doxology. Nothing. Halfway down the aisle her hands began to ache with the cold and her patience began to fray, and the stone in her chest grew heavier with every empty binding—*this is what you get, this is the price, you came too late by one day, one day*—

The fourth pew from the back had a kneeler with a split cushion, mended clumsily with duct tape, and Ada paused, because the mending looked recent and the tape was the cheap silver kind sold at the hardware store on Cannery Row, and something about that small domestic repair in this fortress of old money snagged her attention. She knelt. The rack beneath held four hymnals. The third one was different—its spine cracked wider than the others, its cover worn smooth at the corners the way a book gets when the same hands hold it week after week, year after year.

She opened it to the ribbon marker.

Psalm 130. *Out of the depths I cry to thee, O Lord.* Of course. Ada almost laughed, and the laugh came out closer to a sob. Of course Ruth Vane had marked the depths.

The pages felt wrong—too thick, slightly swollen, as if the book had once gotten wet and dried unevenly. Ada turned them gently, one by one, and between page 84 and page 85, sewn into the gutter itself with three careful stitches of blue thread, she found the paper.

Not one sheet. Several, folded together into a packet soft with handling, the creases gone furry at the edges. Ruth had stitched it into the hymnal the way sailors' wives once stitched letters into coats—built to survive searching, built to survive time.

Ada's hands were shaking so badly she could barely unfold it.

The top sheet was dated three weeks ago. Ruth's handwriting, upright and unhurried, the penmanship of a girl educated by nuns:

*Maren Joubert came to me the night she died. I never told anyone this, not even Aldous when he asked me point-blank, and God knows I lied to a judge with a straight face, which I expect has cost me something. But I am seventy-eight years old and I have buried a husband and outlived my usefulness to everyone who ever needed me quiet, and I find I would like, before the end, to be the kind of woman Maren deserved.*

*I am writing down everything. What she saw. What she said to me. What she demanded, because make no mistake—that girl demanded, she had iron in her the way her sister does. And what I saw with my own eyes from the gallery window of the yacht club, the night of the Founders' Dinner, while our husbands toasted the harbor below.*

Ada looked up. The nave stretched away from her, dim and still, the votive candles guttering in their red glass. Somewhere overhead a rafter ticked as the building warmed.

Below the fold, the handwriting continued—pages of it, dense, dated, detailed. Names. Dates. Amounts.

Ada folded the packet closed along its original creases, slid it inside her coat against her ribs, and returned the hymnal to the rack, ribbon marker and all, stitches and all, exactly as a careful woman had left it.

Then she knelt there a moment longer in the empty church, one hand pressed flat over the paper warming against her heart, and gave the only prayer she had left: *Hold on, Ruth. You wrote it down. Whatever they took from you—they were one day too late.*

Outside, the rain had stopped. The fog was lifting off the bay in long white banners, and far out at the waterline, exposed by the retreating tide, the drowned chapel showed its black ribs to the sky.

Ada walked to her car, started the engine, and did not go home. She drove toward the flats, because Ruth's letter began with a date—the night of the Founders' Dinner, twenty years ago tonight—and Ada intended to read every word of it standing on the exact shore where her sister had made her stand.
