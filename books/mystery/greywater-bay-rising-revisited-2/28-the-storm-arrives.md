## 28. The Storm Arrives

The storm had a name before it had a face—*Odile*, the weather people called her, as if naming a hurricane made it a woman you could reason with. Ada watched the radar loop on Aunt Vera's ancient television, the spiral of red and orange crawling toward Greywater Bay like a fist slowly closing.

"They're saying landfall sometime after midnight," Vera said from the kitchen doorway. She'd been packing all afternoon with the grim efficiency of a woman who had lived through a dozen of these—the photo albums in plastic bins, the good silver wrapped in dish towels, the boarding house's registry tucked under her arm like a Bible. "Governor declared the emergency at noon. They're evacuating the low streets."

"I'm not leaving."

Vera set down the bin. For a moment she looked every one of her seventy-three years, her face drawn tight around eyes that had seen too much of this town's history to pretend surprise at anything. "Ada."

"Julian signed that letter for a reason. Friday. High tide." Ada turned back to the television, where a meteorologist with wind-ruffled hair gestured at a map of the coastline. "He wants an audience. A storm is an audience of one—he thinks it'll just be me and him and the water. If I'm not here, he waits. And if he waits, he finds another way, and next time there's no letter, no date, no warning. Next time it's Ruth's stairs again, or Tom Pell's boat, except this time it's me, or you, or Rook."

"And if you're here?" Vera's voice cracked on the last word. "If you're standing where he wants you when that water comes through?"

Ada didn't answer, because they both knew the answer. The chapel sat at the tide line. High tide during a hurricane surge would put six feet of greywater through those broken windows, across that flooded floor, up over the altar where Maren had stood twenty years ago arguing for her life.

"He killed my sister," Ada said quietly. "He killed Tom Pell. He killed Ruth Vane, who was ninety years old and trying to tell the truth with whatever strength she had left. I'm not giving him the satisfaction of running."

Vera crossed the room and took Ada's face in her hands, her palms dry and warm and trembling slightly. "Your mother used to say the same thing. *I'm not giving them the satisfaction.* Look how that ended for her—drinking herself to death in a house full of casseroles nobody ate." She kissed Ada's forehead, hard, almost angry. "Boarding up the back windows. You'll help me. Then we'll talk about Friday."

They worked through the afternoon in a silence punctuated by hammer blows and the rising moan of wind off the water. By four o'clock the light had gone strange and greenish, the color of old glass, and the gulls had vanished from the sky entirely. By five, the first rain came sideways.

* * *

Rook stood in the station house and listened to his captain tell him exactly what he'd known he would be told.

"—nonessential personnel evacuated by eighteen hundred hours. That includes you, Detective. The state police are handling emergency response until the storm passes. Your only remaining task is the Coldharbour woman."

"The Coldharbour woman has a name."

His captain—Brennan, twenty-six years in Greywater Bay, a man whose face had settled into permanent skepticism like concrete poured wrong—leaned back in his chair. "Her name is a problem, is what she is. She's got half the country listening to her podcast, she's accusing Julian Vane of three murders on air, and now she's refusing evacuation orders. If she drowns in this storm, we've got a national story about a whistleblower the town let die. So you're going to her aunt's boarding house, you're going to put her in a car, and you're going to drive her inland yourself. Understood?"

"Understood," Rook said.

It was even true, in its way. He intended to go to the boarding house. He intended to find Ada. What happened after that was between him and his conscience, and his conscience had been losing arguments all week.

Because that morning, before dawn, he'd copied the entire case file on Maren Coldharbour's drowning. Every page. The original autopsy notes that contradicted the final report. The witness statements that were never followed up. The photograph of the locket found on the tidal flats—logged, photographed, and then missing from evidence, its chain of custody ending in a signature that read *A. Crane*. He'd fed the pages into a scanner in the empty records room while the rain started against the windows, and when he finished, he'd sat in the dark with his badge in his hand and understood that he was no longer investigating a case. He was choosing a side.

"You still here?" Brennan said.

"Just thinking about duty," Rook said, and left.

Outside, Greywater Bay was coming apart by degrees. The marina district had flooded at noon; now the water was climbing Front Street in slow brown increments, swallowing parking meters, lapping at the doors of the Harrow Shipping offices where the lights still burned behind sandbagged windows. Someone had nailed plywood over the window of the bait shop, and someone else had spray-painted across it in dripping black letters: **WE KNOW**.

Rook drove past the shuttered cannery rows, past the church where sandbags lined the steps like a congregation kneeling, past the Yacht Club where a handful of trucks remained in the lot—men securing their boats, their property, their empire, riding out the storm in a building built to survive anything. He thought about the stationery. *Greywater Yacht Club*, embossed in silver, the paper Ada had matched to the confession letter. Comfortable men wrote comfortable letters. It had taken him thirty years as a cop to understand that evil rarely looked like evil. It looked like a man tying down a boat.

His phone buzzed at a stoplight. Dispatch: all units recalled. Then, a second message, from Brennan directly: *Coldharbour first. Everything else after.*

Everything else after. As if the murders were paperwork. As if Tom Pell's body wasn't still in the county morgue waiting for a forensic pathologist who might never come, because the roads were flooding and the world had decided Greywater Bay's dead could wait.

Rook turned the wipers to maximum and pointed the car toward Vera Joubert's boarding house on the hill.

* * *

The power died at 9:47 p.m.

Ada heard it happen rather than saw it—a deep mechanical sigh from somewhere beneath the town, and then the television went dark and the refrigerator stopped humming and the whole house exhaled into candlelight. Vera moved through the rooms lighting oil lamps with the calm of long practice, and Ada stood at the front window watching the bay disappear.

That was the thing no one told you about hurricanes: the ocean didn't look violent so much as *wrong*. The water had risen into the streets below the hill, and the surface lay flat and gleaming under the strobe of the emergency lights downtown, moving with a terrible patience, like something taking its time deciding what to take.

Her phone buzzed. Rook.

*Where are you.*

*Where do you think,* she typed back.

*I'm coming to get you. Evacuation order. Captain's orders.* A pause, then three dots appearing and disappearing twice before the message arrived: *Please be there. Please answer the door.*

She stared at that last line. In two months of circling each other—interrogations disguised as conversations, evidence passed like contraband, coffee gone cold between them at the diner on Route 1—he had never once asked her for anything. He had never once let the professional mask slip far enough to show the man underneath, the one who covered for her at the Harrow estate, who burned his own credibility without hesitation, who copied classified files knowing it would end him.

*Come get me,* she typed. *But I'm not leaving the bay, Elias. Not before Friday.*

She watched the message send. Watched the dots appear. Watched them vanish.

Then the wind hit.

Not gradually—the way storms were supposed to build—but all at once, a wall of sound that struck the house like a freight train derailing. The old windows flexed inward in their frames. Somewhere upstairs, something fell and rolled. Vera appeared in the hallway holding a lamp, her hair loose, her voice pitched to carry over the roar: "Interior bathroom! Now! Both of us!"

They gathered in the downstairs bathroom with mattresses dragged off beds, flashlights, water, the radio. For two hours they sat pressed together on the floor listening to the house take hits—shingles tearing away with sounds like screams, the back porch furniture detonating against something, a tree coming down across the street with a crack that Ada felt in her teeth. The radio reported storm surge estimates of nine feet, then eleven. It reported a section of the seawall failing near the drowned chapel flats. It reported, in a bored announcer's voice, that all coastal roads were impassable.

At some point Vera slept, or pretended to, her head against the wall, her hands folded like a woman at a funeral. Ada sat in the flashlight beam and thought about Maren.

Twenty years ago tonight, there had been a storm too—not a hurricane, just a hard autumn blow—and Maren had walked out of the chapel into weather like this, or been carried out of it, or been brought to the water's edge by a boy from a family that owned everything including the truth. Had she been afraid? Had she argued? Ruth's letter said she'd demanded a cut or exposure. Braver than anyone knew. Braver than Ada had ever been, at nineteen, packing a car and fleeing inland while her sister stayed and fought alone.

"I'm here now," Ada said aloud, to no one, to the dark, to the dead. "You hear me? I'm here."

The wind answered. It sounded almost like an answer.

* * *

Rook lost the road at the bottom of Harbor Hill.

One moment the pavement was there, gleaming under his headlights, and the next it was gone—drowned under a foot of churning greywater that pushed his sedan sideways toward the drainage ditch. He reversed, wheels spinning, and took the hill from the north side instead, the long way, past the cemetery where the oldest stones leaned like tired sentries.

The radio in his dash spat static and fragments: *...surge... twelve feet... all units shelter in place... repeat, all units...*

All units. Everyone but him. He'd logged out at the station at ten o'clock, taken a personal vehicle—the department SUV would have GPS—and driven into the worst storm to hit Greywater Bay in forty years to reach a woman who had told him, explicitly, that she would not leave.

He could have laughed at himself. Thirty-one years old, eight years on the job, a career already bleeding out on the floor of the records room, and here he was doing the one thing they trained you never to do: making it personal.

The boarding house materialized out of the rain like a memory. Dark, shuttered, the porch swing torn loose and hanging by one chain, thrashing against the railing. Rook killed the headlights and sat for a moment, engine running, watching the windows. One candle flickered behind the glass of what he knew was the front parlor.

He got out into a world made entirely of noise and water. The wind took his door and nearly tore it from his hand; the rain hit horizontally, stinging, blinding. He bent into it and fought his way up the walk, grabbing the railing, hauling himself onto the porch, and hammered on the door with the flat of his fist.

Nothing. He hammered again, longer, harder, screaming her name into the wind with no idea whether sound could even travel through this.

Then the lock turned, and the door opened six inches against the chain, and a flashlight beam hit him in the face.

"Elias?" Ada's voice, incredulous, nearly torn away. "Are you insane?"

"Probably." He put his eye to the gap. Behind her, candlelight. Behind that, Vera's face, pale and furious. "Open the door, Ada."

"You shouldn't be on the roads—"

"The roads are underwater. There are no roads. Open the door."

A pause. Then the chain scraped, and the door swung wide enough for him to shoulder through, and suddenly he was inside—soaked to the skin, gasping, alive—in a dim hallway that smelled of lamp oil and old wood, with Ada Coldharbour staring at him like he was either a miracle or a hallucination.

"You defied a direct order," she said.

"I misheard it." He pushed wet hair out of his eyes. "There was a lot of static."

Behind her, Vera threw a towel at his chest with the precision of long practice. "Bathroom," she said. "Both of you. And if the roof goes, you get under something solid, and if the water comes, you go *up*, not out. Am I understood?"

"Yes, ma'am," Rook said.

Ada led him to the parlor, where the candles had been clustered away from the rattling windows, and stood with her arms crossed, studying him. Water dripped off him onto the hardwood. Outside, the storm screamed against the house like something trying to get in.

"You came through that," she said. "For me."

"I came through that because you're the most stubborn person I've ever met and someone has to make sure stubbornness doesn't drown." But his voice betrayed him, rougher than he meant it, and she heard it—of course she heard it, she heard everything, that was the whole trouble with her.

"Elias."

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't say it like that. Not yet. Ask me again when the sun comes up, if there's still a town."

She crossed the room then, and took his cold face in her warm hands, the same gesture Vera had used on her hours ago, and kissed him—briefly, fiercely, like sealing a promise neither of them would speak aloud while the world was ending outside.

Then the front window exploded.

Glass burst inward across the parlor floor, driven by a fist of wind and rain that scattered candles and plunged the room into strobing darkness. Vera was shouting. Rook pulled Ada down behind the overturned sofa, covering her head with his arms as debris raked the walls. Through the broken frame, the night showed itself: black water climbing the street, the bay risen into the town, and above it all the sky churning like something boiling.

"We have to move!" Vera screamed, dragging a mattress. "Upstairs! NOW!"

They ran. Rook carried the radio; Ada carried the bag she'd packed without ever admitting she'd packed it—the recorder, the ledger photographs, Ruth's letter in its waterproof sleeve, the original confession with Julian Vane's signature. Her whole case, her whole war, zipped into a bag she held against her chest like a child as they climbed the stairs into the shaking dark.

On the landing, Ada stopped. Below them, through the shattered window, she could see the water reaching the base of Harbor Hill—and beyond it, far out at the drowned flats, a single point of light. Small. Steady. Deliberate.

Someone was at the chapel.

Her phone lit in her hand. Unknown number. A text, arriving through the chaos like a hand reaching out of the dark:

*High tide comes early tonight. Come see what your sister saw. — J.V.*

Ada looked at the light burning out on the water, and the water rising to meet it, and felt the last twenty years collapse into a single moment of perfect, terrible clarity.

He wasn't waiting for Friday anymore.

The storm was his Friday.
