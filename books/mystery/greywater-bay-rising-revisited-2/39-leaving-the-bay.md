## 39. Leaving the Bay

The morning came in clean and cold, the storm scrubbed out of the sky, and Ada stood in her aunt's boarding house kitchen watching Rook try to fold a map.

He was bad at it. Bad at folding, bad at asking for help with it, bad at pretending he wasn't watching her while he failed. The map of Greywater Bay—creased soft as cloth from a decade in his glovebox—kept fighting him, collapsing into a shape like a paper boat, and finally she crossed the kitchen and took it from his hands.

"You're a detective," she said. "You interrogate people for a living."

"Maps don't talk back." He leaned against the counter, arms crossing over that flannel shirt she'd already started associating with bad coffee and worse decisions. "That's why I liked them."

*Liked.* Past tense, deliberate. She smoothed the map flat on the table, weighted its corners with two mugs and the salt cellar, and didn't look up when she said, "You could still take it back. The badge. Vera would give you your room through winter. The town needs someone who isn't bought."

"The town had twenty years of someone who wasn't bought." His voice stayed level, but she heard the thing underneath it—the same note that had been there since he'd handed Captain Mercer his badge and his report in the same manila folder, since the state investigators arrived and started pulling threads that led straight up to Judge Crane's chambers. "They buried him too. Or forgot him. I'm not interested in being a monument, Ada. I'm interested in—" He stopped.

She looked up then. He was watching her the way he'd watched her across the table at the Edge of Town diner, two hours of argument ago now, when she'd slid the locket across the formica and watched a career cop decide, line by line, to believe her.

"Say it," she said.

"I'm interested in what comes next. With you. If you'll have me in the passenger seat instead of the rearview mirror."

The kettle began to shriek behind them. Neither of them moved to it for a long moment, and then they both did, and their hands collided on the handle, and Ada laughed—a real one, cracked open, the first laugh this house had heard from her since she was nineteen years old—and thought: *this is what Maren never got. This ordinary, stupid, kettle-screaming morning.*

She poured two cups anyway. They'd need them. There was a car to pack.

* * *

Packing took longer than it should have, because the boarding house kept giving things back.

Vera appeared in the doorway of Ada's old room holding a cardboard box with *MAREN* written on the side in a teenager's handwriting, the letters fat and certain. "These were yours. Both of yours. I couldn't—" Her aunt's voice snagged. Twenty years of paid silence, and now silence was the one thing Vera Joubert couldn't afford anymore; the women of Greywater Bay were talking in kitchens all up and down Cannery Row, one testimony at a time, and Vera had given hers three days ago with her chin up and her hands shaking. "I couldn't look at them. I'm sorry, Ada. I'm sorry for all of it."

Ada took the box. Inside: a swim ribbon gone brittle, a cassette tape labeled *MIX — DON'T TOUCH ADA*, a paperback with a tide chart folded inside it, and at the bottom, wrapped in a dish towel, the rusted locket from the tidal flats, photographed and logged and returned by an investigator who no longer needed it. Maren's initial scratched inside the lid. Ada closed her hand around it and felt the metal warm against her palm.

"It goes where I go," she said. Not to Vera. To the room. To the bay visible past the window, gray-green and glittering under the washed sky, looking innocent as it always had.

Rook carried the boxes down. That was the other thing she was learning about him—he didn't ask which things mattered. He just watched her face and picked up the ones that made it change.

Her car sat in the gravel drive, the keyed door panel finally sanded and painted by a neighbor who wouldn't leave his name on the milk bottles he'd also left. The trunk swallowed boxes: recording equipment, case files copied in Rook's own hand on his own long nights, the ledger photographs, Ruth Vane's letter in its plastic sleeve. The whole machinery of The Cold File, going wherever she went.

"Anywhere," Rook said, shutting the trunk. He rested his forearms on the lid and looked out at the water. "That's really the plan? You can record from anywhere?"

"Everywhere." She came to stand beside him, shoulder against his arm. "That was always the point of the show, even before I knew it. A file doesn't need an address. There are a hundred Greywater Bays, Elias. Towns where the founding families own the judge and the judge owns the truth. I spent ten years solving them from a thousand miles away because I couldn't come near this one. Now—" She turned the locket over in her fingers. "Now I know what it costs to come home. And I know it's worth paying. For every one of them."

"And me?" He said it lightly. It wasn't light.

"You're the first person who ever chose me with his whole life on the table." She met his eyes. "I don't intend to waste that."

* * *

Vera made them lunch they didn't have time to eat, which was how love worked in the Joubert family—too much food, offered too late, eaten standing up in the kitchen anyway. Cod chowder, Maren's favorite, and nobody said so and everybody knew it.

At the door, Vera held Ada's face in both hands. Her aunt's skin smelled of salt and dish soap, the smell of every childhood summer Ada had fled and every one she'd secretly loved.

"I'll keep the room made up," Vera said. "In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case coming back ever feels like coming home instead of coming back." Vera's thumb brushed Ada's cheekbone, once. "Your sister used to say the bay rises. That one day it would give everything back. I thought she was being a child." Her voice went rough. "She was being a prophet. Go on, now. Before I make fools of us both."

Ada hugged her hard enough to hurt, and let go, and got in the car.

* * *

They didn't leave immediately. Rook drove, because Ada's hands wanted to be free, and she held the recorder in her lap—not running, just resting there, an old habit, a talisman—and they took the coast road slow, past the cannery rows with their new scaffolding and their FOR LEASE signs coming down one by one, past the marina where Julian Vane's boats sat impounded under yellow tape, past the courthouse where the state's investigators worked behind lit windows on a Sunday.

At the turnoff for the tidal flats, Ada said, "Stop."

He stopped. Of course he stopped.

She walked down alone to the waterline, boots sinking in the wet sand, and stood where the drowned chapel's roofline broke the surface at low tide, black and patient as a whale's spine. Somewhere under that water was the tunnel. Somewhere under that water was the shape of her sister's last night, walked again by Ada herself with salt in her lungs and Rook's hands hauling her back into the air.

She didn't say anything out loud. She didn't need to. She pressed the locket to her sternum, felt it through her coat, and turned back toward the car, toward the man waiting beside it with the passenger door already open.

"Ready?" he asked.

"No," she said honestly. "But I'm going."

"That's the only kind of ready there is." He shut her door gently and came around to drive.

* * *

The bay fell away behind them in the mirrors—first the chapel spire, then the harbor lights, then the headland, and then just fog and water and the long gray road south. Ada watched until there was nothing left to watch, and then she turned forward, and something in her chest unclenched that had been clenched for twenty years.

Rook drove with one hand on the wheel. After a few miles, the other found hers on the seat between them, and neither of them made anything of it, which was how they made everything of it.

"Where first?" he asked.

"There's a woman in Astoria whose brother drowned in 2011. Ruled accidental." She pulled out her notebook, pages soft with handling, and read by the passing light. "Two witnesses changed their stories within a week of each other. Both of them worked for the same family. Sound familiar?"

"Terribly." He smiled at the windshield. "And after Astoria?"

"After Astoria, everywhere. Until there aren't any more." She clicked the recorder on, just to hear the small mechanical heartbeat of it, the sound of her whole working life. "Testing. One, two. The Cold File will continue."

The road unspooled ahead of them, south along the water, toward every town that had ever buried a girl and called it weather.

Ada Coldharbour pressed record, and drove on.
