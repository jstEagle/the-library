## 1. The Guest Who Never Left

The coffin came up out of the ground at nine-forty in the morning, and Noor Halloran was polishing the front desk when it happened.

She heard it before she saw it — the diesel grumble of the small excavator the heritage committee had rented from the mainland, backing down the hill road toward the old cemetery above the harbor. Greywater Bay's sesquicentennial was eleven days away, and the town had spent the better part of a year preparing for it: bunting ordered from a catalog, a commemorative ale brewed at the Anchor & Anvil, and the centerpiece of it all, the ceremonial reinterment of the founder himself, Edmund Voss, whose remains would be lifted from the neglected family plot and laid to rest in a marble vault facing the water, where tourists could appreciate him.

Noor had watched the preparations the way she watched everything — from behind the desk, at a slight angle, with a cloth in her hand. Fifteen years at the Greywater Bay Hotel had taught her that the best view of any event was the one nobody thought to guard.

She set down the cloth when the first phone call came.

"Is it true they're doing it today?" Mrs. Calloway from the gift shop. "The dig?"

"Nine o'clock, I believe," Noor said. "The committee wanted low tide for the equipment."

"And they're moving him to the vault on the point. For the ceremony."

"That's my understanding."

"Well." A pause with weight in it. "It'll be good to have him home."

Noor hung up and looked out the lobby's tall windows at the hill, where the excavator's arm rose and fell like a heron fishing. The morning was the kind Greywater Bay did best in early autumn — pale gold light, water like hammered tin, the smell of salt and cold coming off the flats. She had lived through thirty of these autumns, fifteen of them at this desk, and she knew the town's rhythms the way she knew the register: which guests came back, which never did, which names repeated in ways that meant something.

Edmund Voss had been dead for a hundred and thirty years. Whatever they lifted out of that ground today belonged to history, and history, Noor had always believed, was the one guest who never complained about the room.

She was wrong about that by ten o'clock.

* * *

The call from the cemetery came to the hotel because of course it did. The heritage committee had no office; its chairwoman, Prudence Voss-Ashby — Edmund's great-great-granddaughter, a retired schoolteacher with a voice like a struck bell — conducted all official business from the lobby's leather armchair by the fire, where she'd been holding meetings since before Noor's time.

"Noor, dear," Prudence said, crossing the lobby at a pace Noor had never seen her manage, "I need the telephone list for the mainland. The coroner's office. And someone should call the Anchor — they'll want to know the ceremony is —" She stopped. Her hands, which were always folded, were not folded.

"Prudence," Noor said. "What's happened?"

"Nothing has *happened*," Prudence said, in the tone of a woman rehearsing. "There's been a — an irregularity. With the coffin. The seal was compromised, the wood's rotted through, it's entirely a question of drainage, the plot sits low and the clay holds water —"

"Prudence."

"The coffin is empty, Noor."

The lobby was very quiet. The grandfather clock in the corner — a Voss piece, like half the furniture in the building — ticked with the patience of a thing that had outlived everyone's secrets once already.

"Empty," Noor said.

"No remains. No — nothing. Just water at the bottom, and —" Prudence's voice caught on something and swallowed it. "Some papers. A book of some kind. The coroner's man took it. He said it looked like a ledger."

Noor felt it then — not fear, exactly, and not surprise. Something older and colder than either. The feeling of a name surfacing in a register where you were certain no name had been written.

"A ledger," she repeated.

"Waterlogged, he said. Barely legible. He's sending it to the mainland for — conservation, or whatever they call it." Prudence had recovered her posture, if not her color. "It's nothing. It's a curiosity. The papers will love it — *founder's coffin held no founder* — and then it will pass, the way everything passes. But the ceremony, Noor. The ceremony must proceed. We'll need a new coffin, a sealed one, and the committee will need the hotel's ballroom Thursday for an emergency meeting, and I'll need you to say nothing to anyone until we've spoken to the family."

"Of course," Noor said. "Nothing to anyone."

It was what she said to every guest who asked her to hold a key, a letter, a confession at the desk at two in the morning. It was the first sentence of her profession and the last one too: *the desk remembers so you don't have to.*

Prudence hurried out. Noor watched her go up the hill road, small and upright and moving too fast, toward a hole in the ground that had just swallowed a hundred and thirty years of certainty.

Then she went to the safe.

* * *

The hotel safe lived behind the front desk, in a closet that also held the vacuum, the lost-and-found, and forty years of registers shelved in date order. The safe itself was for the things that couldn't be shelved: passports, cash deposits, and — once, in her second year, when she was still learning the job from Ruthie — a ledger.

She had never forgotten it, because Ruthie had made a small ceremony of it, which Ruthie never did.

"Guest folios go here," Ruthie had said, kneeling at the open safe, her gray braid coming loose over her shoulder. "Deposits here. And this —" she lifted a ledger from the back of the shelf, bound in oxblood leather with brass corners gone green at the edges, "— goes nowhere. Back of the shelf, spine in, and you don't ask."

"What is it?"

"House book," Ruthie said. "Old accounts. It's not yours and it's not mine, and the day it's yours, you'll wish it still weren't."

Noor had been twenty-six, newly orphaned for the second time in her life — first her parents, then the aunt who'd raised her — and freshly arrived in a town where she knew no one, and Ruthie had taken her in the way the hotel took in stray furniture: without discussion, permanently. She had not asked again. She had shelved the ledger spine-in, and in fifteen years she had opened the safe perhaps four hundred times without once letting her eyes rest on that oxblood spine.

But she had seen it. She had seen it the way a concierge sees everything — peripherally, permanently, without meaning to.

Now she knelt at the safe with her heart going hard and steady, and moved the folios aside, and there it was. Oxblood leather. Brass corners. Water stain along the lower third, the kind of tide line a book gets from lying in standing water for a long, long time.

She had not touched it in fifteen years. She touched it now.

The stain was fresh. The leather was still dark with it, cold to her fingers, smelling of clay and iron and the particular mineral sweetness of grave water. And the brass corner — the lower right one — was bent inward, as if the book had been dropped, or wedged, or used to hold something down.

Noor sat back on her heels in the closet among the vacuum bags and the forty years of registers, and made herself think like a professional, because thinking like anything else was not survivable.

The ledger in the safe had been there since before her time. Ruthie had shelved it. Ruthie had gone quiet about the years before her — Noor knew that now, though she hadn't known it then; she'd only known that Ruthie, the most generous gossip on the coast, went silent as a closed door whenever anyone mentioned the mid-nineties, and that she'd died four years ago taking the silence with her.

The ledger in the grave was waterlogged. The ledger in the safe was waterlogged.

Either there had been two, which was possible — a house this old kept many books — or there was one, and it had left the safe, and Noor did not believe it had walked.

She put it back. Spine in. Back of the shelf. She closed the safe and spun the dial and stood up and smoothed her apron, and when the next guest came through the door — a birdwatcher from Vancouver, early for the festival, wanting a room with a water view — she greeted him with a voice so even and so warm that he complimented her on it to the manager later that afternoon.

*The desk remembers so you don't have to.* She held to that all day. She held to it through the emergency committee meeting in the ballroom, where Prudence announced a "conservation delay" in terms so smooth that Noor, pouring coffee at the sideboard, admired it the way you admire a well-made bed: you know the work that's hidden in it. She held to it through the phone call from the *Coast Chronicle*, through the visit from two members of the Voss family who asked, separately and with identical casualness, whether the hotel kept any records from the founding era, and whether the safe in the lobby was the original.

She held to it until ten minutes past closing, when the lobby was empty and the lamps were low and she was alone with the registers.

Then she took down the volume from the year before her hiring — the last register Ruthie had kept — and set it on the desk under the lamp, and opened it to the autumn pages.

* * *

Noor's relationship with the registers was not sentimental. It was professional, which is deeper. Fifteen years of check-ins and check-outs, of room numbers and license plates and the small marginal notes concierges make — *arrived agitated, requested no calls; departed without settling bar tab, see folio; asked twice about the lighthouse road, unaccompanied* — had taught her that a register was not a record of guests. It was a record of a town. People came here to be away from whoever they were elsewhere, and what they were when they thought no one was writing it down was the truest thing Noor ever read.

Ruthie's hand was different from hers — rounder, older, the letters leaning like things grown in wind. Noor turned the pages slowly. October. The storm month. The pages from the last week of October, the year before she came, carried a smudge of something brown across the lower corners, and the ink had run in one place as if the book itself had once been wet.

She had noticed that smudge before, years ago, and filed it under *old book, old water.* Tonight she looked at it and thought: *grave water.* And made herself stop thinking it.

Four check-ins in that week shared something she had never had reason to notice. The handwriting was different — four different hands, four different names, the kind of names people invent when they're booking rooms they don't want remembered: a *Mr. Gray*, a *Mrs. Salt*, a *C. Marsh*, a *T. Byrne*. But the room numbers ran consecutively — 211, 212, 213, 214 — and the folios, when she pulled them from the archive boxes, had all been settled the same way: cash, exact, no bar charges, no calls, no meals ordered in the dining room. Four guests who had come to a hotel in storm season and wanted, in effect, not to be there at all.

Noor sat with that for a long time. The clock ticked. Somewhere out on the flats the tide was turning, the way it did twice a day, indifferent, correct.

She should close the register. She should put the folios back. She should go home to her apartment over the boathouse and sleep, and tomorrow the coroner's ledger would go to the mainland, and in eleven days a new sealed coffin would go into a marble vault, and the town would have its ceremony, and whatever Edmund Voss's empty grave meant would be conserved, or delayed, or buried a second time — this time under marble, which held water even worse than clay.

That was the professional path. She had walked it for fifteen years. *The desk remembers so you don't have to* — and the corollary, the one Ruthie had never said aloud but had taught her every day: *and what the desk remembers, the desk keeps.*

Noor took out a sheet of hotel stationery. She told herself it was for a note to the manager about the ballroom booking. Her hand wrote something else.

*211 — Gray — cash, exact. 212 — Salt — cash, exact. 213 — Marsh. 214 — Byrne.*

Four names. Four rooms. One week, thirty years ago, in a register kept by a woman who went silent, about a ledger that sat in a safe she was told never to ask about, in a grave that had just been opened in front of the whole town and found holding nothing but water and accounts.

She folded the page once and put it in her apron pocket, which was where she put things that weren't yet decided.

The lamp hummed. Out the tall windows, the hill cemetery was a black shape against the blacker water, and somewhere down the hill road, a car engine started and faded toward the point, where the Voss family's houses kept their lights on later than anyone else's.

Noor closed the register. She shelved it in its place, in date order, in the long row of years — and her hand stopped, just briefly, on the oxblood spine visible through the safe's open door.

Spine in. Back of the shelf.

*You don't ask,* Ruthie had said. *The day it's yours, you'll wish it still weren't.*

Noor Halloran had spent fifteen years keeping other people's secrets, and she had believed — right up until this morning — that she was good at knowing the difference between a secret and a sin. She folded her apron over the page in her pocket like a hand closing over a wound.

In eleven days, they were going to bury an empty coffin and call it a founder.

And tomorrow, she thought, turning off the lamp, tomorrow she was going to find out whose handwriting was on that fourth folio — because she had recognized it. She had recognized it the way you recognize a voice in a dark room: late, and completely.

It belonged to a man who knitted every Thursday in her lobby, who had taught her the cable stitch the first winter she came to town, who called her *niña* and brought her ginger candies and had never once, in fifteen years, asked her for anything.

Tomás.

The clock ticked on, patient as a gravedigger. Noor stood in the dark lobby of the Greywater Bay Hotel, holding a page she should not have taken, and understood that the guest who had never left had finally, after thirty years, come down to the desk to check in.

She just didn't yet know which of her friends had carried him there.
