## 2. Fifteen Years of Check-Ins

The registers lived in the archive room behind the linen closet on the second floor, and Noor Halloran had kept them the way other people kept faith.

Fifteen years of them, bound in grey cloth, spines labeled in her own hand: *GBH — Guest Register — 2010–11*, and so on back to the year she'd been hired. Before that, the handwriting changed. Ruthie's hand — small, slanted, impatient, as if the pen were always trying to get somewhere faster than the words could carry it. Ruthie had run the front desk of the Greywater Bay Hotel for thirty-one years before handing Noor the keys like a relay baton and retiring to a bungalow on the north bluffs where she grew orchids nobody ever saw.

Noor unlocked the archive room at six in the morning, before the sesquicentennial chaos swallowed the day. The hotel was full. Camera crews had been arriving since Tuesday for the exhumation coverage, and the lobby smelled of coffee and cable-news impatience. She wanted an hour with the books before anyone needed anything from her, which was, she understood, the closest thing to prayer her life still contained.

She pulled the 1993–94 register down first. Not because she'd planned to. Because her hands did it while her mind was still replaying yesterday: the coffin coming up out of the founder's plot, the crowd hushed as church, the coroner's gloved hands lifting the lid, and the long terrible pause before someone said, quietly, the way you say something in a hospital corridor, *it's empty.*

Empty except for the ledger. Water-swollen, black-bound, brass corners gone green. And Noor, standing at the edge of the gathered town with her lanyard and her professional composure, had felt her stomach drop through the floorboards of herself — because she knew that binding. She had shelved its twin in the hotel safe eleven years ago, when Ruthie handed it to her without explanation and said only, *keep it dry, keep it quiet.*

* * *

Ruthie taught her everything worth knowing about the desk.

"Anyone can check a guest in," Ruthie told her, Noor's first week, tapping the ledger with one lacquered nail. "The job is noticing. The man who signs *J. Smith* but hesitates over his own address. The woman who books two nights and packs for ten. You notice, and then—" here Ruthie would pause, and her eyes went flat and bright at once, like water over stone— "you decide what to do with it."

"What do I do with it?" Noor had asked, twenty-six years old and newly orphaned by a car accident that took both parents in one wet November, newly arrived in Greywater Bay because it was the farthest coastal town from her old life that still had a hotel needing staff.

"You decide," Ruthie said, "what belongs in the book."

Noor hadn't understood then. She understood now, standing in the cold archive room with the 1994 register open on her knees, that Ruthie had meant it literally. That some things never made it into the book at all.

* * *

The register itself was a beautiful object, if you loved such things. Guests signed in their own hands — fountain pen fading into ballpoint across the decades, one flamboyant signature from a touring cellist that took up three lines. Dates, room numbers, rates. In the margins, Ruthie's annotations in pencil, a private shorthand Noor had learned to read like weather: a small circle meant a repeat guest; a dash meant paid cash, no card trail; a tiny *w* meant watch this one, don't ask why.

October 1994. Noor ran her finger down the page and felt the hair rise on her arms.

Four check-ins, the same week — the week Edmund Voss vanished, though the register didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it until spring when the town gave up pretending he'd gone traveling. Four check-ins under names that made the back of Noor's neck prickle, because she had spent fifteen years learning every founding family of this town the way you learn the faces in your own photo album.

*Mrs. E. Crane*, Room 210. There was no Crane family. But Delphine Ashford's mother had been born a Creighton, and Creighton had a cousin line called Crane, and Delphine had married into money that liked discretion.

*T. Alvarez*, Room 214. Tomás Reyes-Alvarez. Old Tomás, who came to knitting circle every Thursday and brought lemon bars and never talked about anything before 2001.

*M. Whitlock*, Room 208. Marta Whitlock née Sørensen, who had taught Noor to knit, who called her *girl* in Danish-inflected affection, who had held Noor's hand at her mother's funeral when no one else in this town knew her well enough to.

And the fourth — the fourth stopped her breath.

*C. Voss*, Room 212. Signed in a hand she recognized instantly, because Judge Cassian Voss still signed documents with that same narrow, vertical pressure, that same little flourish on the V like a bird taking off.

Four rooms. One week. October 9th through the 14th, 1994 — the nine days of the Great Storm, when the ferries stopped and the phone lines came down and Greywater Bay was cut off from the world like a snow globe shaken and set on a high shelf.

All four accounts settled by a single payment. Ruthie's pencil notation in the margin, so faint Noor had to tilt the page toward the window light: *one acct. — see Oct. folio — C.V.*

One account. Cassian Voss.

* * *

"Noor?" Marta's voice floated up the stairwell. "You up there? The television people want early checkout, all of them, they're going to the cemetery."

"Tell them I'll be down in five," Noor called, and heard Marta's footsteps retreat, unhurried, the tread of a woman who had walked these halls for forty years.

Noor looked down at the register. At *M. Whitlock* in Room 208, written in Marta's own looping hand, younger but unmistakable — the same way she dotted her i's with a small circle even now, even on the thank-you cards she left at the desk.

Her first instinct was the concierge's instinct, drilled into her by Ruthie: protect the guest. A name in a book is a confidence. You don't discuss who slept where. It was practically liturgy.

Her second instinct was newer, and colder: *why are four of my friends in this book the same week the founder disappeared?*

She photographed nothing. She wrote nothing down. She simply stood there in the archive room among the linens and the ledgers and felt the floor of her life tilt one degree, the way a ship tilts when something heavy shifts below decks.

Then she tore out the page.

Not neatly. She wet her thumb and forefinger and worked the page loose along the binding gutter the way you'd ease out a splinter, and it came free with a sound like a small tear in fabric, and she folded it into quarters and put it inside her uniform pocket, against her hip, where it felt warm immediately and stayed warm, like a living thing.

She had never stolen so much as a pen from this hotel. She stood very still and listened to herself not regret it.

* * *

That afternoon the exhumation story broke wide. EMPTY COFFIN OF FOUNDER STUNS GREYWATER BAY, said the *Sentinel*, and the national feeds picked it up by evening, and the lobby became a tide pool of reporters asking the same questions in different accents. Noor answered all of them with the same serene nothing: the hotel had no comment, the hotel was honored to host the ceremony, yes, the sesquicentennial would proceed as planned.

At seven, Detective Ivo Marsh came in. New face, new coat, new-town carefulness. He asked for a glass of water and stood drinking it slowly, looking at the guest register on the counter the way a man looks at a locked door he intends to open eventually.

"Fifteen years at this desk?" he asked.

"Fifteen in June."

"That's a long time to be watching people arrive and leave."

"It's a long time to be watching people pretend they're doing either," Noor said, and he almost smiled.

He left a card. She put it in the drawer under the counter, next to the spare keys, and thought about the page warming against her hip all through her shift, and went home to her apartment above the chandlery and did not sleep.

* * *

In the morning she drove out to the north bluffs, to Ruthie's bungalow, though Ruthie had been dead fourteen months and the bungalow belonged now to a nephew in Tacoma who came twice a year to mow.

She let herself in with the key Ruthie had given her years ago — *in case of orchids,* Ruthie had said, which meant *in case of anything.* The house smelled of dust and dried lavender. On the kitchen table, under a ceramic bowl, exactly where Ruthie always kept things she wasn't finished with, Noor found a single sheet of hotel stationery, yellowed, with Ruthie's slanted hand across it:

*If anyone asks about '94, the book is wrong. I corrected it. Don't look for what I corrected. Some guests stay forever whether we like it or not.*

No date. No signature beyond the hand itself, which was signature enough.

Noor sat down at her dead mentor's kitchen table and read the note eleven times. *The book is wrong. I corrected it.* Meaning Ruthie had altered the register. Meaning the four names — or some of them — might have been entered after the fact, backdated, a record manufactured rather than kept. Meaning Ruthie had known, and had chosen falsification over truth, and had spent the rest of her life growing orchids nobody saw and saying nothing about the middle decade of her career, going silent whenever the nineties came up at staff reunions, changing the subject with the smoothness of a woman closing a door on a room with the lights already off.

*I corrected it.* Corrected toward what? Toward the lie, or away from it? If the entries were false, what was true? Had her friends stayed at the hotel that storm week, or had someone wanted it to look that way — an alibi woven into wool, thread by thread, in a concierge's careful hand?

Or worse: had the entries been true, and the correction something else entirely?

Noor folded Ruthie's note into her pocket beside the torn register page. Two documents now. Two pieces of a past that had no business being in her possession, weighing almost nothing, weighing everything.

* * *

Thursday night was knitting circle, as it had been every Thursday for twelve years. Marta's house, the good lamp, the bad sherry, Tomás's lemon bars, Delphine's stories about her son in Vancouver. Noor sat in her usual chair with a half-finished sock and said, casually, into a lull, "Strange business at the cemetery this week. Empty coffin. Can you imagine?"

The needles kept clicking. All of them. Three women and a man, four sets of needles, and not one of them missed a stitch, and Noor — who had been trained to notice hesitation, to hear the half-second gap where a true answer lives — registered the absence of surprise the way you register a missing picture on a wall.

"Moriah says the ground sank," Delphine offered. "Sinkhole. All that rain last month."

"Grave robbers, more likely," said Tomás. "Looking for the founder's ring. They say he was buried with it."

"Mm," said Marta, and turned her heel, and asked Noor to pass the sherry, and her eyes when they met Noor's were perfectly, professionally calm — the calm of the front desk, the calm Noor herself wore every day, and seeing her own composure reflected back at her from the face of her oldest friend in this town was like looking into a mirror and finding someone else already standing there.

They knew. Or they suspected. Or — and this was the possibility that followed her home through the fog — they had known for thirty years, and the knitting circle, the Thursday lamp, the lemon bars, the whole gentle architecture of her found family had been built, without her knowledge, on top of a grave that wasn't empty and a coffin that was.

Back at the hotel, past midnight, Noor unlocked the safe with her own key and took out the black ledger with the green-brass corners, the twin of the one the coroner had lifted from Edmund Voss's coffin. She set it beside the torn register page and Ruthie's note, three objects arranged on her desk like an accusation.

She opened the ledger to October 1994.

The entries matched the register exactly — same dates, same rooms, same initials, same single settling account marked *C.V.* — except in one place. Where the register said *M. Whitlock, Rm 208, Oct 9–14*, the ledger said something else, in a cramped hand she didn't recognize:

*Rm 208 — W. — paid in full — do not enter again.*

W.

Noor sat in the dark of the manager's office with the ledger open and the harbor lights blurred through the rain-streaked window, and she understood that she had just crossed a line whose location she couldn't quite see behind her, only the fact of having crossed it. She had stolen a page. She had withheld evidence, probably; obstructed, certainly; lied by omission to a police detective with kind, patient eyes. And tomorrow morning the lobby would fill again, and she would smile, and check people in, and decide — as Ruthie had taught her — what belongs in the book.

Outside, the tide was going out, exposing the flats, the old pilings, the things the bay usually kept covered.

Somewhere in this town, she thought, there was a third thing hidden — because towns like this never hid just one thing; they hid in threes, the way liars hide behind two lies — and she was going to find it, God help her, before anyone found her.
