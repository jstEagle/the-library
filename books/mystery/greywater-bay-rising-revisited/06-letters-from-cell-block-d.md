## 6. Letters from Cell Block D

*March 12*

Adaeze,

They let me have paper again. You'd think after thirty years they'd trust a man with a pen, but the rules here change like the tide — in and out, in and out, and nobody at Greywater Bay ever asked the tide for its reasons.

I write to you because I always wrote to you. The chaplain says I should write to someone living. I told him you're the only one who ever read me properly. He didn't understand. Chaplains mean well. Most people here mean well. That's the strange thing about a prison — it's full of men who did one wrong thing and guards who do small kindnesses all day. The world outside thinks it's the other way around.

I'm not going to tell you again that I'm innocent. I stopped doing that years ago, when I understood what the word was costing me. Innocence is a luxury item, Adaeze. Like fresh peaches. Like a room with a window facing water. You can want it, but wanting it every day wears grooves in a man. So now I just say: I didn't do it. Plain as weather. And then I get on with my day, which is mending chairs in the woodshop and reading whatever Ruth from the library cart brings me, which is usually mysteries. There's an irony there the universe seems to enjoy.

The new warden wants us writing about our "formative experiences." Therapy through correspondence, he calls it. So fine. Here is my formative experience:

October 1994. The storm came up from the southeast like it had a grudge. Nine days the town was cut off — no ferries, no phones, lines down everywhere. I was working the hotel that week, doing repairs. Edmund Voss kept a list of things he wanted fixed before winter and he liked to watch you do them, standing there with his hands behind his back like a man inspecting troops. He paid cash. He paid late. He asked questions that weren't questions — *your brother's papers came through yet? Your family settled in alright? Funny thing, fires, how they start.* That kind of talk. I was twenty-six and I thought if I just kept my head down and my work good, he'd run out of things to hold over us.

He never ran out. Men like him don't. Holding things over people wasn't his hobby, it was his income.

You know the rest, or you knew it once. They found him gone — his rooms empty, his boat missing, then not missing, then him, or most of what a storm leaves of a man. And me with motive written all over me: the fire, our brother, the money Voss swore he'd never owed anyone. Three witnesses said they saw me near the boathouse that night. Three witnesses, all connected to the hotel, all certain. Certainty like that doesn't grow wild, Adaeze. Somebody plants it.

I sat in the courtroom and watched them say it and what struck me — what still strikes me — is that none of them looked like liars. That's the trick of it. Liars in the pictures have shifty eyes. Real ones have kind faces and steady hands and they believe their own story by the time they tell it, because they've told it to themselves first, over and over, until the weave is tight. I could see the weave if I looked hard. But juries don't look hard. Juries look at faces.

I'll write more soon. Save your strength, wherever you are. I'm saving mine.

Your brother,
Wren

* * *

*April 3*

Adaeze,

Something happened. I keep starting this letter and stopping, so I'll just say it plain: a woman came to visit me two weeks ago and gave a name that isn't hers.

I know it isn't hers because I've had thirty years to learn how names sit on people. A name you've worn your whole life fits like an old coat. Hers fit like a borrowed one — she checked the visitor form twice before signing, the way you check a door you've already locked. Nora something. Nora Bell. She said she was doing research on coastal towns, some heritage project, and that my case came up in old newspapers.

Her hands gave her away. She held her purse like it was evidence. And when I talked — I don't talk much at visits, I've learned visitors want either a monster or a miracle and I'm neither — she listened the way nobody has listened since you died. Not waiting for her turn. Listening like each word cost her something.

I told her the trial story, the short version. The three witnesses. Marta Ellison, who ran the front desk mornings; Delphine Roux, who did the books; Tomás Ferreira, maintenance, my own supervisor, the man who taught me which pipes froze first. All three said they saw me near the boathouse between eleven and midnight. All three used the same phrase — *he was carrying something toward the water.* Same words. I counted, in the courtroom, sitting there with my lawyer who was older than the courthouse and tired past caring. Three independent witnesses, same sentence. Nobody noticed but me and God, and God wasn't called to testify.

Nora — whoever she is — went white when I said that part. Not shocked-white. Recognizing-white. Like a woman finding her own handwriting in someone else's letter.

Here's what I can't stop turning over: why come at all? Thirty years. Everyone who cared is dead or scattered. My appeals ran out when Clinton was president. If she wanted a story for her heritage project, I'm a bad ending — no twist, no reveal, just a man who mends chairs. But she cried in the parking lot. I saw her through the glass, before the guard walked me back. She stood by her car a long time.

Maybe guilt sent her. Maybe curiosity. Maybe she's one of theirs — a daughter, a niece, checking that the lock still holds. I've been wrong about people before. It's how I got here.

But Adaeze, I'll tell you something I haven't written down anywhere. When she said *tell me about your brother*, I almost believed the world had sent someone to fix it. Thirty years and one stranger with a false name, and hope came walking back in wearing a friendly face like it never left. I hated her a little for that. Then I hated myself more, because underneath the hating I was glad. Glad like a cold man glad of a fire he knows will go out.

If she comes back, I'll tell her everything. What else do I have left to lose? Only the habit of silence, and that's been eating me alive anyway.

Your brother,
Wren

* * *

*April 20*

Adaeze,

She came back. Noor. That's her real name — she told me herself this time, second visit, standing at the glass with her coat still buttoned like she wasn't sure she'd be allowed to stay long enough to take it off. Noor Halloran. Concierge at the hotel. Fifteen years behind the same desk where Marta Ellison used to sit.

I laughed when she said it. I couldn't help it. Of course. Of course the one person who comes asking is from the hotel. The building itself reaching out its long arm across three decades. She flinched at my laughing and I apologized, but I'll be honest with you, since you're the only one I'm honest with: part of me enjoyed it. Let the hotel send somebody to look at what it made.

Except she isn't the hotel. That's the thing I keep getting wrong about her. She talks like the place — careful, polished, everything in its right drawer — but underneath she's shaking. Second visit, I told her about the night Voss died. How he summoned me up to his rooms at ten o'clock, said the radiator in 214 was knocking, and kept me there an hour talking in circles while the storm got worse, saying things like *a man's debts outlive him, Wren, remember that,* and *when I'm gone, the ledger balances itself.* I thought he was drunk. He wasn't drunk. He was frightened. I know that now. Frightened men talk in circles; guilty men talk in straight lines.

Then he sent me down to fetch a part from the boathouse workshop, and that's the last honest memory I have of that night — me, wet to the knees, hunting a valve in the dark while the wind tried to take the roof off. Whatever happened after, I wasn't there for it. But three good people said otherwise, and their saying it was worth more than my being somewhere.

Noor wrote it all down in a little book she thought I couldn't see. Her hand stopped when I described Voss's voice. Stopped dead, pen lifted, like the words were too heavy to set down.

I asked her, point blank: *Do you think I did it?* And she did the concierge thing, the professional thing, the answer-that-isn't-an-answer. *I think the truth matters,* she said. And I said, *That's not what I asked.* And she looked at me for a long moment through the glass and said, quiet as a confession, *No. I don't think you did it.*

First person in thirty years to say it to my face. First person who mattered.

So now I'm sitting here in Cell Block D with a feeling I don't have furniture for. Because if she believes me, she'll go digging. And if she digs, she'll find what's buried, and what's buried won't stay arranged. Those four — Marta, Delphine, Tomás, and whichever Voss stands behind them pulling strings — they built their lives on top of that night like a house on a grave. I know what happens to houses like that when the ground shifts. I've watched this whole town do it for thirty years: pave it, plant it, hold a festival on it every summer.

And here's the part I need you to hear, Adaeze, because you always said I was too soft for my own good: I don't want revenge. I checked that desire at the door years ago, the way you check a weapon. Revenge is a luxury too. What I want is smaller and harder. I want the record corrected. I want one line in some file somewhere that says *this man was wrongly convicted* instead of *appeal denied.* I want to walk out of here and stand on the tide flats and not have anyone cross the street.

But I've seen what truth does to towns like Greywater Bay. It doesn't free them. It splits them. And she'll be standing in the crack when it opens, that woman with the borrowed name and the shaking hands, and everyone she loves will be on one side of it and she'll be on the other.

I should warn her. Next visit, I'll warn her: knowing is a door that only swings one way.

Or maybe I won't. Maybe I'm selfish enough, after thirty years, to let her open it.

Your brother,
Wren

* * *

*May 8*

Adaeze,

Short one tonight. The guard says lights out early, some inspection tomorrow.

She came a third time. Didn't sign in under any name at all — the officer just waved her through, said *your regular visitor,* like I was a man who had those. Imagine that. Thirty years and I have a regular visitor.

We didn't talk about the trial. We talked about you. She asked what you were like and I told her about the garden you kept behind the rooming house, tomatoes in fog country, stubborn as scripture. I told her how you wrote to me every week for the first five years and then the letters slowed and then a woman from social services wrote to tell me you were gone, in a paragraph, like you were a change of address. Noor cried. I didn't. I'm past crying about you; I keep you somewhere the weather can't reach.

Then, at the end, she leaned close to the glass and said: *There's a ledger. It survived. I've seen it.*

And Adaeze — I felt the floor of this whole long sentence shift, like ice going out in spring. Because I know what ledger she means. Voss kept two. The pretty one for the bank and the true one for himself, and the true one had our brother's name in it, and the fire, and the sums he claimed we owed him for his silence about a fire he likely set himself. I told the detectives about it in '94. They wrote it down and it never appeared again, the way things from that week never appeared again.

*It was in the coffin,* she said. *Where his body should have been.*

I sat with that a long time. Somewhere in Greywater Bay there's a hole in the ground where a founder should be, and instead of a body there's a book of debts, and the whole town is planning a party to bury an empty box and call it history.

I told her one more thing before they took me back. I said: *Whatever you're going to do, do it knowing they'll offer you a place among them. That's how it works. They don't threaten people like you. They adopt them.* She went pale and asked how I knew, and I said, *Because that's how it worked on us. First they offered my father work and my mother silence. The offer comes wrapped in kindness. That's how you know it's an offer.*

She left without saying goodbye. Just stood, and pressed her hand flat against the glass for a second — I put mine up to match it, don't ask me why, thirty years of glass between me and the world and I put my hand up like a schoolboy — and then she was gone down the corridor, fast, like a woman running from a fire she'd just decided to walk into.

Lights are going out. One more thing, sister, and then I'll let you sleep:

For thirty years I kept my innocence folded away like a suit too good for everyday wear. This month I've taken it out twice. It still fits. That frightens me more than anything they could do to me, because a man who starts hoping in here is a man they can hurt all over again.

But if she reads that ledger aloud somewhere — if one person in that town hears my brother's name spoken as a debt instead of an accident — then even if it costs me another thirty years, it will have been worth the postage.

Tomorrow the inspection. Tonight, the tide turns somewhere without me, the way it always does.

Your brother,
Wren
