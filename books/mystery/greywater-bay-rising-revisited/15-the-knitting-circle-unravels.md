## 15. The Knitting Circle Unravels

Marta had been knitting the same grey scarf for three weeks, which was how Noor knew something was wrong with her, and how Marta knew that Noor knew, and how the two of them had arrived at a silence so complete it had its own weather.

The scarf was for Wren Okafor.

She hadn't planned it that way. She'd bought the wool in October — good wool, merino, the kind she never bought herself because she was a cotton-and-acrylic woman, practical, unpretending — and only when she'd cast on the first row did she understand what her hands were doing. Grey like the bay in November. Grey like the inside of a cell. She'd been knitting him into warmth for twenty years without ever once letting herself think his name at the needles, and now the name came every time, row after row, *Wren, Wren, Wren*, like a stitch pattern she couldn't drop.

* * *

It had started the winter after the trial, when the verdict came down and the town exhaled and Marta had gone home and been sick for an hour and then washed her face and made dinner for her husband as if the world were still shaped the way it had been shaped that morning.

The money was Delphine's idea first, though Delphine would deny it to her grave, and probably would, given where things were heading. Not money exactly — Delphine had said *someone should see to the sister*, meaning Adaeze, meaning the woman who'd stood up in that courtroom every day with her spine straight as a mast while her brother was taken apart by three witnesses who all used the word *certainly* in their statements, certainly he was there, certainly she saw him, certainly, certainly, certainly. Marta had been one of the certain ones. That was the part no amount of yarn could absorb.

So Marta had started small. An envelope of cash left in the collection plate at St. Brendan's, earmarked by a note in her left-handed print: *for the Okafor family.* When the priest asked around, nobody looked at Marta, because Marta was the one who ran the coat drive and sat with the dying and taught the confirmation class, and conscience wears a uniform; nobody suspects the uniform.

When Adaeze died — and God, that was its own wound, because Marta had liked her, had genuinely liked her, had sat beside her at the harvest supper in '96 and held her hand and felt the calluses from hotel work and thought *I did this, I did this to this woman's whole life* — when Adaeze died, the envelopes kept going. There was a niece then. A grandniece now, the girl checking into Room 214 under a name that wasn't hers, and Marta knew about that too, knew it the way you know a storm is coming, by the pressure in your teeth.

Twenty years. She'd done the arithmetic once, drunk on communion wine at Easter, sitting in her truck in the church lot. Twenty years of envelopes at roughly forty dollars a week, adjusted upward whenever the cannery laid people off, adjusted downward the year her roof needed replacing. It came to some number that meant nothing and everything. It was not enough. It had never once been enough. It was not even the wrong currency for what she owed. But it was the only motion her hands could make, besides knitting, besides praying, besides showing up every Thursday at the hotel lobby with her basket of wool and her smile calibrated to *nothing has ever happened here*.

* * *

Thursday came, and with it the circle, and with it Noor.

Noor had changed. Marta saw it the way you see weather change over water — nothing you can point to, everything you can feel. She still poured the coffee. She still complimented Delphine's lacework, which deserved it, and teased Tomás about his tension being too tight, which was true. But there was a new stillness underneath, the stillness of a person counting.

They were six weeks out from the sesquicentennial. The reburial was announced — a new coffin, sealed, a ceremony, Cassian Voss presiding in his judge's robes like the town's own priest of forgetting. Delphine had brought a newspaper clipping about it and read it aloud in a voice pitched carefully at *isn't this nice*, and Tomás had nodded along, and Marta had watched Noor watch all of them, and understood that the counting had reached some total.

Afterward, when Delphine had gone and Tomás had shuffled out into the rain with his cane tapping the mat twice — his old signal, *all clear, all clear*, from thirty years ago, from the night they'd carried the tarpaulin — Marta stayed. She began packing her wool with exaggerated slowness, and Noor began stacking chairs with exaggerated patience, and the lobby emptied around them until it was just the two of them and the rain against the tall windows and the front desk standing between them like an altar.

"Marta." Noor didn't come around the desk. She stayed behind it, which was itself a message. "Can I ask you something? Just you."

"You can ask me anything. You always could."

"The envelopes. At St. Brendan's. Forty dollars a week, give or take, for twenty years." Noor's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. Gentle was for people you loved. "Left-handed print. For the Okafor family."

The floor did not open. The rain did not stop. Marta set down her basket very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Who told you?"

"Nobody told me. Father Ambrose mentioned the fund at a council meeting — anonymous benefactor, twenty years, remarkable constancy. And I keep records, Marta. You know that. I cross-reference everything. It's my whole life." She almost smiled. "I checked the dates against the trial. The envelopes started eleven days after the verdict."

Eleven days. Marta remembered those eleven days. She'd spent them telling herself that Wren Okafor had gotten a fair trial, that juries knew things, that certainty was certainty. On day twelve she'd bought the wool.

"It's charity," Marta said. "People leave anonymous gifts all the time."

"No," Noor said, still gently, still behind the desk. "Charity doesn't start eleven days after a verdict. Charity doesn't adjust for cannery layoffs. I've read the parish ledger, Marta. Whoever gives that money isn't giving. They're paying."

The word landed between them and lay there.

"And if I am?" Marta heard her own voice go thin. "If I'm paying? What is it to you, Noor Halloran? You weren't here. You didn't—"

"I wasn't here," Noor agreed. "That's the whole point of me. I'm the only person in this town who wasn't here, which means I'm the only person who can see it. Four alibis, Marta. Word for word alike. *I was home with a candle lit because of the storm.* All four of you. Same sentence. Do you know what we call that at the front desk? We call it a scripted guest. Somebody coached. And I've spent fifteen years loving four people who were coached, and I found out last month, and I have been carrying it alone ever since, and I am so tired, Marta. I am so tired of carrying it."

Marta's hands found each other and held on. "Then don't carry it alone. That's all I'm asking. That's why I stayed." She came closer, close enough to see the exhaustion living in Noor's face like a tenant. "Give it to me. Whatever you found — the ledger, the page, whatever Ruthie left you — give it to me and let it end with me. I'll take it to my grave the way Ruthie took hers to hers. Nobody else has to break. Wren gets his envelopes. The town keeps its—"

"Its what? Its lie?" Noor's voice cracked and reassembled. "There's a man in Cell Block D who has stopped saying he's innocent, Marta. Did you know that? I visited him. He's calm. He's *gracious*. He thanked me for coming. Twenty-nine years, and he's made peace with it, because hope was too expensive. And you want to buy his silence with forty dollars a week and a grey scarf you can't finish."

Marta flinched as if struck. "How do you know about the scarf?"

"Because I notice everything. You taught half this town that about me, and now you hate it." Noor came around the desk at last, slowly, and stood within arm's reach, and Marta saw that she was shaking. "I love you. Do you understand that? You were the first person in this town who treated me like I belonged. When my mother died and I came here with two suitcases and nothing, you brought me soup and taught me the cable stitch and never once asked me a question I couldn't answer. You are the closest thing I have to family. And I am going to burn your life down, and I need you to hear that I love you while I do it, because otherwise neither of us will survive it."

"There's another way." Marta grabbed her hands. Hers were cold; Noor's were burning. "There has to be another way. Quietly. Get him a lawyer, get the conviction reviewed — quietly, Noor, without the ceremony, without reading names aloud in front of three hundred people, without Delphine ending her life in a courtroom and Tomás ending his in a cell at his age, his *heart*, Noor—"

"Without the truth, you mean."

"Without the *ruin*." Marta's voice broke free of her at last, twenty years of it coming up all at once. "You think I haven't imagined confessing? Every single Thursday, sitting in this lobby, watching you pour coffee, I imagine standing up and saying it. I have rehearsed it, Noor. I know the words. *We hid the body and we let an innocent man hang for it.* I've said it a thousand times in the dark. But saying it out loud doesn't just land on us. It lands on the girl in 214, who is the last Okafor there is, and it lands on Tomás's grandchildren, and it lands on this town, which will eat itself, because that's what towns do, they don't absorb, they *eat*. Mercy sometimes looks like silence. I decided that twenty years ago and I have bled for that decision every week since, and I am asking you — I am *begging* you — to find the version of this where somebody survives."

Noor was quiet a long moment. Rain on glass. The register lying closed on the desk between them, fifteen years of arrivals and departures, everyone accounted for except the ones who mattered.

"No," she said finally. "There isn't a quiet version. I looked. I've been looking for a month, Marta, every night, and there isn't one. Either the truth comes out whole or it stays buried, and if it stays buried, they seal it Friday week in a coffin with Cassian Voss's name on it, and Wren Okafor dies in Cell Block D a guilty man, and you knit scarves until your hands give out, and I check people in forever and know what I chose." She squeezed Marta's hands once, hard, and let go. "I won't let that happen. I'm sorry. I love you. I'm going to do it anyway."

"When?" Marta's voice was small. "At least tell me when."

Noor picked up her keys from the desk. At the door she paused, backlit by the grey light, and for one moment she looked seventeen years old again, the orphan girl with the suitcases.

"Soon," she said. "Before the reburial. I promise you that much — you'll get to say goodbye to the town before it says goodbye to you."

And then she was gone, and Marta stood alone in the lobby with her unfinished scarf, and understood that the envelope she would leave Sunday would be the last one, and that mercy and truth had finally met in this room, face to face, and only one of them was walking out alive.

She sat down in the best chair by the window, the one with the good light, and took up the needles, and knitted.

Row after row after row. *Wren, Wren, Wren.*

Outside, through the rain, she watched a car she didn't recognize park across the street, and a man get out who moved like a policeman even before he showed his badge to anyone, and she thought, with a strange floating calm:

*He's early. Or we're late.*

Either way, the unraveling had begun.
