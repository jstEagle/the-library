## 4. A Witness, Presumed

The payroll discrepancy had been filed under Q for Quill, cross-referenced under P for Pemberton's Own Damned Curiosity, which is not a letter of the alphabet but has always been the largest section of my ledger.

It was a small thing, the kind of small thing I collect the way other people collect spoons. Seven years ago—June, the week after Mara Quill drowned—a final paycheck issued in her name. Cashier's check, drawn on the hotel account, endorsed and cashed at the First Marlowe Savings on a Tuesday morning. The signature on the endorsement was hers. The problem was that dead girls, as a rule, do not stand in line at banks on Tuesdays. They do not sign their names with a confident rightward slant. And they certainly do not, according to the teller's note I'd coaxed out of old Mr. Fenwick over two cups of coffee and one strategic compliment about his garden, ask for the money *in fifties*.

Dead girls also do not wear sunglasses indoors, but that was speculation, and I keep speculation in a separate drawer.

I sat with the file for a long time that night, the lobby clock ticking toward three, the radiator doing its usual impression of a man being strangled politely. Two hours north. That was what Fenwick had said, unprompted, when I'd asked whether he remembered anything else about the woman. *She had salt in her hair. Said she was headed up the coast.* People say things like that all the time; it means nothing and everywhere. But I am a night concierge, and my entire profession is treating nothing as a clue until it confesses.

So: two hours north. The coast. A town where a woman who wanted to be no one could become someone else with seven hundred dollars in fifties and a talent, apparently, for signing her own name while legally deceased.

I told myself I was going up to check on the linen supplier. I even wrote it in the schedule book, because if I was going to lie, I was going to lie in my own handwriting, where I could keep an eye on it.

* * *

Gull Harbor smells like money that went to sea and never came back. It's the kind of town where the shingles are silvered by weather rather than paint, where the church and the bait shop share a parking lot, and where a stranger is noticed within four minutes of arriving. I know this because I arrived at eleven in the morning and by eleven-oh-four a man mending a net outside the chandlery had looked up twice, memorized me, and gone back to his work with the deliberate calm of a man who would be describing me to someone by supper.

I had dressed down for the occasion, which for me means a cardigan without buttons and shoes I have owned since before Theo was born. I carried a canvas tote from a pharmacy two towns over, so I'd look like errands, not questions. This is tradecraft, of a sort. My tradecraft has always been stationery-based.

The harbor office kept the mooring registrations. The woman behind the counter had a face like a closed ledger herself, and I respected her immediately.

"Help you?"

"I'm looking for a relative," I said. "My aunt. She came through here years ago. I've only recently—" I let my voice do the thing voices do when they're carrying something fragile. "Family's been estranged."

"What's the name?"

"Quill," I said. "Mara Quill."

And there it was. Not recognition—something before recognition. The flicker of a person deciding how much they know. Her eyes went to the window behind me, to the street, then back.

"Don't know it," she said.

"Of course not," I said pleasantly. "Would you know it if I asked the harbormaster directly? He seems the thorough type."

Her jaw tightened. "You'll want Mrs. Aldous, then. She does the rentals on Pelican Lane. Keeps her own books." A pause, and then, quieter, almost unwillingly: "Back cottage. The blue door. She doesn't like people knocking before noon."

It was 11:52. I bought a postcard of the harbor to justify my presence and waited eight minutes on a bench like a woman admiring the view, which, to be fair, I partly was. Somewhere behind those silvered shingles, a girl who had been drowned on paper was possibly buying groceries under another name, and I found my hands were not entirely steady. Ten years of cataloguing other people's secrets, and I had forgotten what it felt like to be close to one that mattered to me.

Mrs. Aldous turned out to be eighty if she was a day, with reading glasses on a chain and the unblinking directness of someone who has outlived everyone who ever lied to her.

"Pelican Lane rentals," I said. "I'm looking for a tenant who may have come around seven years ago. Quiet woman. Paid careful attention to paperwork."

"Seven years ago," Mrs. Aldous repeated. "That would be the summer the Hendersons' boy nearly drowned off the jetty. Terrible summer. Why?"

"The name might have been different. She'd have paid in cash, early, maybe several months at once."

Mrs. Aldous looked at me for a long moment. Then she took off her glasses, which I understood to be a formal gesture, the ceremonial removal preceding truth.

"You're the second one," she said.

The word landed in my chest like a dropped key. "The second?"

"Came through in the spring. Man. City coat, wrong shoes for the dock. Asked near enough the same questions you're asking. About a quiet woman, cash rent, careful with papers." She put her glasses back on, and the ceremony was over. "I told him the same thing I'll tell you. There was a woman rented the back cottage on Pelican Lane from June of that year. Called herself Ruth Merrow. Kept to herself. Walked the tideline every morning rain or shine. Paid six months ahead, every time, in cash, in an envelope left in my mailbox, never late once in seven years." She paused. "Until this spring. When she stopped."

"Stopped paying?"

"Stopped *being*. Cottage's been empty since April. Left it clean as a whistle. No forwarding address. Just a note saying thank you and that she'd gotten what she needed." Mrs. Aldous's eyes, magnified behind the lenses, were very sharp. "Now. You want to tell me why two strangers in one year are asking after a ghost?"

I wanted to tell her many things. I told her none of them. "Did the man leave a name?"

"He did not. But he left mud on my step, city mud, gray clay, the kind we don't have here. And he drove a dark car with dealer plates." She leaned forward. "Dealer plates, dear. That's a man who borrows cars the way other men borrow umbrellas. You mind yourself."

I thanked her. I bought nothing. I walked back along the tideline, past the jetty where the Henderson boy had nearly drowned in the terrible summer, and I stood looking at the water for longer than a woman checking on linen suppliers should, thinking: *she got away. She actually got away. Seven years of mornings on this beach, alive.*

And then: *someone found her anyway. Someone in a borrowed car with city mud on his shoes got here first, in the spring, and by April she was gone.*

The question that followed me back down the coast highway was not *where did she go*. It was *what made her run again*—and whether what frightened her out of Gull Harbor was the same thing now following me.

* * *

I noticed the tire forty minutes into the drive home, at the rest stop past the county line, when the steering began to pull left with the gentle insistence of a cat wanting attention.

I pulled over. The rear driver's-side tire was not flat. It was *wounded*—a clean slash, blade-work, not blowout, done neatly enough that it hadn't failed all at once but was dying by degrees, letting me get most of the way home before it gave out entirely. Whoever did it hadn't wanted me stranded on the coast road at midnight. They'd wanted me to make it almost home. To feel the pull. To stop, and kneel, and understand.

Message received.

I changed the tire myself, because I am a night concierge and there is no one else at three in the afternoon on a county road, and because my hands needed something to do besides shake. The spare was soft—I'd been meaning to deal with that since the Carter wedding—and I drove the last twenty miles at forty-five with the hazards on, feeling every mile like a hand resting lightly between my shoulder blades.

Here is the thing about fear. I have watched it move through the Marlowe Grand for ten years, through guests and staff and the occasional politician, and I have learned its grammar. Fear makes people loud or silent, fast or frozen, but it always, always makes them smaller. It contracts them toward whatever they're trying to protect.

Kneeling on the gravel shoulder with my ruined tire, I waited to contract. I waited to think about resigning, about burning the ledgers, about the long safe sleep of a woman who never asks about ghosts.

Instead I thought about Mara Quill walking the tideline every morning for seven years, rain or shine, sending cash in envelopes, keeping her head down and her name borrowed and her life small—because staying small was the price of staying alive. And someone had come and taken even that from her.

And I thought about a cashier's check signed by a dead woman, filed under Q, waiting for me all these years like a fuse I'd mistaken for a bookmark.

By the time the Marlowe Grand's neon came into view, pink and shabby and faithful against the dusk, the fear had stopped contracting and started concentrating. It had narrowed itself down to a single point, the way light narrows through a lens, and at the center of it was not terror.

It was arithmetic. Someone had slashed my tire within an hour of my leaving Gull Harbor. Which meant they knew where I'd been. Which meant they were watching me closely enough to follow me north—or closely enough to already know what I'd find there.

Which meant the next move wasn't mine to make quietly anymore.

I parked in my spot behind the hotel. I gathered my tote, my postcard of Gull Harbor, my notes written in pencil on the backs of receipts. And as I crossed the service lot, I saw the light on in the bell desk window—Theo's shift, though Theo was supposed to be off tonight—and I understood, with the particular dread of a woman whose sanctuary has already been breached once, that the question of who is watching whom in this hotel was about to get much more complicated.

Theo was standing at the desk. And he was holding my second ledger—the one I keep hidden—in both hands, like an offering, or a confession.
