## 3. What the Crypt Keeps

The crypt of St. Ambrose Cathedral sat directly beneath the crossing—the point where nave and transept made their stone cross—and Hazel had spent twenty years walking over it without once thinking of what lay below.

That was the point of a good archive, she'd always believed: it taught you where not to look. The cathedral's records were arranged so that attention flowed upward and outward—toward the stained glass, the endowments, the choir school, the names carved into the donor wall in the narthex. Nothing in the cataloging system invited a reader downward. There was no folder marked *Crypt*. There didn't need to be. A sealed room generated no paperwork, and no paperwork meant no entry point for curiosity.

But now there were two letters filed under G, and one of them said *she has been down there since 1979*, and so on Wednesday morning, at 7:40, before the vergers arrived and before Mrs. Okafor's cleaning cart began its slow pilgrimage up the north aisle, Hazel stood in the ambulatory with a ring of keys she wasn't supposed to question and looked at the door to the crypt for the first time as an archivist rather than as a woman passing through.

It was an unremarkable door. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing that bothered her. Everything else in St. Ambrose announced itself—the 1893 doors of the west entrance with their ironwork hinges like fern fronds, the sacristy door with its hand-carved pelican feeding her young from her own breast. The crypt door was plain oak, painted institutional green sometime in the last century, fitted into a stone frame worn smooth at the edges. It looked like a door to a boiler room. It looked like a door someone had wanted people to stop seeing.

Hazel crouched. Her knees cracked—a sound absurdly loud in the empty church—and she examined the threshold.

There it was. The seal.

Bishop Hollis had ordered the crypt sealed in November 1979; every account agreed on this, though none of them said why. The sealing had been done properly, liturgically, with wax and ribbon, because St. Ambrose did nothing halfway when it came to ceremony. A lead disc the size of a saucer was set into the wood beside the lock plate, and across it ran a band of red wax stamped with the diocesan arms—a mitre above crossed keys. The wax had darkened to the color of dried blood, which Hazel supposed was fitting, though she tried not to think about why.

She took out her phone and photographed the seal from four angles. Then she put on the white cotton gloves she kept in her cardigan pocket out of habit—gloves for handling documents, not doors, but the gesture steadied her—and leaned close.

The wax was unbroken.

She checked twice, running her gloved thumb along its edge, looking for the hairline seam that would betray a cut-and-reweld job. Nothing. The stamp impression was crisp, uncracked, continuous. Whatever else might be true, no one had opened this door by breaking the bishop's seal.

Which should have been reassuring. It was not, because Hazel had spent Tuesday night doing something she almost never did: reading against the grain of her own filing system.

* * *

An archive is honest only if you know how to make it talk. Most people assumed records simply *contained* information, the way a jar contains jam. Hazel knew better. Records contained absences, and absences had shapes. You learned to read the hole, not just the paper around it.

So on Tuesday night, after closing, she had pulled the facilities files—not the curated ones in the reading room but the working files, the ones nobody consulted because they smelled of dust and administrative boredom. Maintenance logs. Contractor invoices. Work orders, some of them forty years old, written on carbon-copy triplicate in the fading purple ink of a typewriter ribbon stretched past its dignity.

She had gone through them the way she went through everything: chronologically, completely, without skipping. It took three hours. She found roof repairs from 1974, boiler replacements from 1988, a long and oddly passionate correspondence about guttering from the mid-nineties. She found the invoice for the original crypt lock, installed in 1961, brass, from Whitlock & Sons Ironmongers, £14 6s.

And then she found the second invoice.

March 1981. Whitlock & Sons again. *Supply and fit new mortice lock, undercroft door.* £22 10s. Billed to the Office of the Provost. Signed off by a name she recognized with a small cold jolt: *A. Merrick, sexton.*

Someone had replaced the crypt lock eighteen months after the sealing.

Hazel had sat back in her chair and stared at the invoice for a long time. The reading room clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door closed. And she felt the specific vertigo she imagined astronomers felt when they noticed a star moving wrong—not dramatic, not loud, just a single data point refusing to fit, dragging the whole constellation out of true.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she could not stop turning over.

If you sealed a room and never intended to open it again, why replace its lock?

A broken lock on a sealed door was not a problem. A sealed door needed no lock at all—it needed the seal, the wax, the ceremony, the weight of the diocese saying *no*. If the lock had rusted or failed, the sensible response—the response Hazel herself would have taken, would have *filed*—was to let it fail. To let the door become truly shut. Instead, someone in March 1981 had spent parish money on making sure the crypt door locked securely. Had made the room more closed than the closure required.

Unless, of course, they'd needed to open it. Unless they'd needed a lock they controlled, a key that lived outside the official key register, a way in and out that left the bishop's wax untouched.

You didn't have to break a seal if you had your own key.

Hazel had gone home and slept badly, dreaming of green paint and purple ink, and woken at five with the certainty that she needed to see the door itself, in person, before anyone else was in the building.

* * *

Now she was here, and the seal was intact, and the invoice sat in her mind like a splinter.

She straightened up and examined the lock plate. Brass, newer than everything around it, though "newer" meant 1981, which meant it was still older than she was. Four screws held it to the oak. She tilted her phone's flashlight across the surface and saw the scratches she'd expected without knowing she'd expected them: fine crescents around the keyhole, the kind of wear a lock develops from use. Not decades of disuse. Use.

Her heart was doing something quiet and arrhythmic. She made herself breathe the way she did before difficult conversations—four counts in, six counts out—and turned to the doorframe.

Stone tells stories if you let it. The frame's lower corners were scuffed in a way consistent with things being carried through—a stretcher, a coffin bier, furniture. But the scuffs were old, worn smooth, probably from the era when the crypt had actually been used for burials, before the municipal cemetery opened in the fifties. She photographed them anyway. Then she crouched again and looked at the floor of the ambulatory in front of the door, the flagstones, the grout lines.

There. A faint arc of lighter stone, maybe two feet long, curving away from the threshold toward the pillar. As if something heavy had been dragged—or wheeled—across the flags often enough to polish them. A hand truck. A trolley. Something with wheels, coming and going, coming and going.

Or coming and going once, very heavily, and cleaned up badly.

Hazel knelt there longer than she meant to. The cathedral woke around her in its small morning ways: pipes ticking, a pigeon resettling in the clerestory, the first sounds of Mrs. Okafor's cart two aisles away, the squeak of a wheel she had heard ten thousand times and never once listened to. She thought about Dolores Okafor, who had been cleaning this church since before Hazel was born, whose memory reached back through all of it like a root through soil. Dolores would have been here in 1981. Dolores would have seen the locksmith come, seen the sexton sign for it, swept the brass filings off these very flags.

*Dolores remembers rain,* whispered some part of her mind, unbidden, and she didn't know yet why that phrase had come, or where it belonged, only that it arrived with the weight of something true.

"Hazel?"

She flinched hard enough to bang her elbow on the door. Mrs. Okafor stood at the mouth of the ambulatory with her cart, a small woman in a floral overall, her gray braids pinned up, regarding Hazel with the mild, bottomless patience of someone who had watched every soul in this parish grow up, sin, repent, and repeat.

"Good morning, Mrs. Okafor."

"You're on the floor." It wasn't a question. Dolores Okafor had never asked a question in her life when a statement would do.

"I'm checking the door hardware. Routine condition survey." The lie came out smooth, which frightened her more than the lie itself. Twenty years of invisibility apparently included training in invisibility's grammar.

"Mm," said Mrs. Okafor. She looked at the crypt door, then at Hazel, then at the scuffed flagstones, and something moved behind her eyes—something old and careful, a drawer being pushed shut. "That door's been trouble longer than you've been alive, child."

"It's been sealed since 1979," Hazel said. "Hasn't it?"

Mrs. Okafor's hands folded themselves on the cart handle, one over the other, deliberate as a woman arranging flowers. "Sealed," she repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. They sealed it. I remember the day. Cold that week. Everyone stood in the crossing and sang the hymn and Bishop Hollis read the words, and afterward there was sherry in the vestry and Mr. Voss senior made his little speech about putting the past to rest." She smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Putting the past to rest. That's what he called it."

"And after that? Did anyone ever go down there?"

The pause lasted exactly one second too long. Hazel counted it the way she counted everything—involuntarily, precisely—and knew she was watching a decision happen behind another human face.

"Doors get opened," Mrs. Okafor said at last. "Doors are for opening. That's all I know about doors." She gripped the cart. "You'll want to speak to Father Anselm about the crypt. He kept all those papers."

"Father Anselm died fourteen months ago, Mrs. Okafor."

"Yes," said Dolores Okafor. "He did." And she pushed her cart past Hazel, up the aisle, wheel squeaking, leaving behind her the smell of beeswax and something else—fear, maybe, or its cousin, guilt—so faint Hazel might have invented it.

* * *

Back in the archive, Hazel did what she always did with a disturbance in the record: she built a timeline. A single sheet of acid-free paper, pencil only, columns ruled with the straightedge she kept for exactly this purpose.

**Nov 1979** — Crypt sealed by order of Bishop Hollis. No stated reason in any file she could find. Ceremony attended by town leadership, including the Voss family. (Note: *which* Voss family? Check.)

**Mar 1981** — New mortice lock fitted to crypt door. Invoice signed by sexton A. Merrick. No corresponding work order. No entry in the key register. (She had checked the key register three times now, running her finger down the columns: *nave, sacristy, tower, vestry, organ loft, undercroft stores* — the crypt appeared nowhere. A lock existed. Its key did not.)

**1979–present** — Seal intact. Officially, the room behind the green door had not admitted air, light, or a living person in forty-four years.

Except the lock said otherwise. The polished flagstones said otherwise. Mrs. Okafor's one-second silence said otherwise.

And—one more thing, the thing she had saved for last, the thing that had been sitting in her peripheral vision all morning like a guest she didn't want to acknowledge—the letters said otherwise. *She has been down there since 1979.* Present tense, in its way. A body that stayed. A body someone might have visited.

Hazel put down her pencil and pressed her palms flat against the desk until the trembling stopped.

Here was the crack, and she could feel it running through everything she had trusted for twenty years. Not the confession—that was still, in her careful private taxonomy of evidence, an *allegation*, possibly a cruelty, possibly a hoax by someone who knew Anselm's hand. But the lock was not an allegation. The lock was a £22 10s invoice in purple ink, signed by a dead sexton, and it proved one simple, terrible thing:

The official story required the crypt to be untouched. The physical record showed someone had made sure they could touch it whenever they wished. Both facts could not stand. One of them was a wall, and one of them was a door, and Hazel Galloway had built her entire life on the belief that walls, given enough patient attention, eventually confessed to being doors.

She thought of the second letter. *Find the ledger hiding in daylight.* She thought of Anselm's handwriting, and how she had traced the *f*s last night until 2 a.m., comparing them against sermon drafts, telling herself the resemblance meant nothing, meaning it less each hour.

And she thought—reluctantly, the thought arriving like cold water finding a seam—about the timing. Sealed November 1979. Lock replaced March 1981. Sixteen months. Long enough for grief to settle. Long enough for the town to stop watching the green door. Long enough for whoever needed access to be certain no one would ask questions.

Long enough, perhaps, for a baby to be born. For papers to be signed. For a girl with no last name of her own to be given one, along with a life filed neatly under G.

Hazel looked at her timeline, at the neat pencil columns, at the gap between November 1979 and March 1981 that she had drawn a small bracket around and labeled, in her own tidy hand: *what happened here?*

Then she opened the drawer where she kept the restricted-access files—the ones requiring the provost's countersignature, the ones she had never once requested in twenty years because requesting them would mean announcing that Hazel Galloway had become a person who asked questions—and she wrote on a request slip, in handwriting as steady as she could force it:

*Adoption registers, diocesan series, 1979–1980.*

The slip trembled slightly in the tray. Outside, the cathedral bells began the quarter-hour, and beneath her chair, beneath the floor, beneath forty-four years of wax and paint and polite silence, something waited in the dark with its own version of events.
