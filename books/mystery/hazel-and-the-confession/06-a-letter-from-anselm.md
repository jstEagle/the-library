## 6. A Letter from Anselm

The archive at St. Ambrose kept its coldest hours between four and six in the morning, when the boiler cycled down and the stone walls gave back the night's chill like a held breath. Hazel had learned this over twenty years of early arrivals, and she had come to love the hour—the cathedral above her still dark, the town still dreaming, and she alone with the shelves in the blue-gray light, doing the only work she had ever been any good at.

She was not alone now. She could feel it.

The two letters sat on the reading table in front of her, squared to its edges out of habit, though nothing about them felt square or settled anymore. She had not slept. She had made coffee at midnight and forgotten to drink it; it sat beside her now, cold and oily, a small monument to distraction. Twice she had picked up the phone to call someone—no one—and twice she had set it back down. There was no one to call. That fact, ordinarily a kind of clean emptiness she'd built her life around, now felt less like emptiness and more like a wall with no doors.

*One confesses. One promises.* She turned the first letter over again, as if the answer might be printed on the back. *A death in the crypt, 1979. A ledger hiding in daylight.*

And the hand. God, the hand.

She had told herself all night that grief was playing tricks on her—that after eleven months without Father Anselm, any slanted, patient script would look like his. But she was an archivist. She did not deal in impressions. She dealt in evidence.

So at five past four, she had done what an archivist does.

* * *

The sermon files lived in cabinet nine, drawers labeled by year, each homily filed in Anselm's own meticulous order—draft, revision, final—because he had been, in his way, as much an archivist as she was. *We are all custodians of something, Miss Galloway,* he used to say. *Some of us just get better filing cabinets than others.*

He had said things like that all the time. Warm little jokes wrapped around cold little truths. She had loved him for it. She had loved him for twenty years, since the day a sixteen-year-old ward of the diocese with a head full of alphabetical systems had been given a job dusting the archive and found, for the first time in her life, a room where everything knew its place—including, eventually, her.

Now she pulled the sermons from November 1979 forward, because if the confession was true, Anselm had been alive and writing in 1979, and men do not change their hands between thirty and eighty so much as they wear them down.

She spread six pages across the table under the lamp: the Founders' Day sermon of 1979, drafted and final. The Advent sermon of that same year. And beside them, the two anonymous letters.

Then she began, methodically, to compare.

The evidence came in layers, the way it always did.

First, the gross features. Anselm's hand was a cursive that had begun as seminary copperplate and spent sixty years being hurried—a rightward lean, tall ascenders, descenders that looped open instead of closing. The letters matched. Not approximately. Exactly. The *f* in *forgive* crossed itself with the same impatient double-stroke she had seen ten thousand times in his marginalia. The *y* in *yesterday* dropped below the line and curled back like a hook catching on something.

Second, the idiosyncrasies—the details no forger thinks to steal because no one outside the archive ever sees them. Anselm dotted his *i*'s not with points but with short horizontal dashes, a habit she had once teased him about and he had defended as "more efficient." Every *i* in both letters wore its little dash. He broke his *t*'s at the crossbar rather than lifting the pen. Both letters did the same. And there was one more thing, the thing that had woken her at three in the morning with her heart going like a trapped bird: when Anselm wrote the word *truth*, he always—always, in every sermon draft, every note, every Christmas card he'd sent her—crossed the *t*'s with a single unbroken stroke that ran through both letters like a wire.

Both letters used the word *truth*. Both crossed it the same way.

Hazel sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere far above her, the great building ticked and settled, indifferent.

Third layer: the paper. She took up her loupe and examined the stock of both letters against the archive's reference samples. Laid paper, watermarked with a fleur-de-lis she recognized instantly—Crozier & Sons, the stationer on Mercer Street, which had supplied the diocese office for forty years until it closed in 2019. Anselm had bought his last box of it in bulk, she remembered now, because he hated changing brands. The letters were written on dead stationery from a dead shop, by a hand that matched a dead man's sermons stroke for stroke.

But it was the fourth layer that undid her—not the physical evidence but the textual kind, the kind she was best at. Because she had read every word Anselm ever preached, some of them dozens of times, and the letters were full of him. Not quotations exactly. Echoes.

The confession said: *What is buried is not forgiven; it is only buried.*

In the Advent sermon of 1986, Anselm had preached: *We mistake concealment for mercy. What we hide does not heal. It waits.*

The second letter said: *The ledger hides in daylight, where all our best lies live.*

Easter, 1994: *Sin in a town like ours does not skulk in alleys. It sits in the front pew. It signs the guest book. It keeps the books.*

He had been preaching around it for forty years. Circling it, year after year, Sunday after Sunday, dropping these small dark stones into the well of ordinary parish life and listening—for what? For a splash? For anyone to notice? No one had noticed. No one but the girl who catalogued his sermons and read them the way other people read novels, and even she had heard only morality, never memoir.

Until now.

Hazel laid her palm flat on the confession letter, carefully, the way you'd touch something sleeping. It was his. There was no honest doubt left. Father Anselm—her mentor, her employer, the closest thing to family the world had ever offered her—had written a confession to a killing and mailed it to her from beyond the grave.

* * *

The postmark told the rest of the story, or most of it.

She had photographed it the first night and enlarged it since, working with a magnifier and the archive's light table. The cancellation stamp was smudged but legible: posted in Ashford—not this Ashford parish but the regional sorting depot forty miles away—dated fourteen months ago. Anselm had died fifteen months ago, in the hospice in Corvelden, with Hazel holding his hand through the last three days of it.

Fourteen months ago, he had been alive. Weak, she knew—she had visited every week—but alive, and lucid in the mornings, and mobile enough that a taxi to the sorting depot was not impossible. He had written the letters, sealed them, stamped them, and then—this was the part that made her chest ache in a way she couldn't name—he had arranged for someone to hold them. A delayed delivery. She thought of the solicitor in Corvelden who had handled his estate, the dry little man who had handed her a box of "personal effects" containing nothing but his breviary and a tin of pencils. Had the letters gone through him? Through the hospice chaplain? It didn't matter, not really. What mattered was the timing, and the timing was a sentence all its own:

*He wanted me to have these only after enough time had passed that they could not be traced to his deathbed. After the trail went cold. After I had grieved.*

Or—she turned it over, and over—after he was certain she would be alone with them. Alone, and unable to ask him anything.

She got up and walked the length of the archive aisle, because sitting still had become unbearable. Her footsteps sounded wrong in the empty stacks, too loud, like someone else's.

Why?

That was the question underneath all the others, the one the handwriting analysis couldn't touch. Why write a confession at all, after decades of silence? Why entrust it to her? And why—this was the splinter, the one she kept pressing on—why wait? Not just why mail them late, but why had he never, in twenty years of shared mornings and shared coffee and shared custody of the town's entire documented memory, why had he never once said to her, *Hazel, there's something you should know*?

He had trusted her with everything except the truth.

She stopped at cabinet twelve—the restricted materials, the drawer she herself maintained but rarely opened—and stood there without touching it. Twenty years. He had watched her build a whole self inside these walls. He had taught her conservation technique and provenance research and how to steam a warped page flat without tearing it. He had eaten birthday cake with her, alone, in this room, because there was no one else. On the tenth anniversary of her employment he had given her a bookmark with a quote from Jerome: *The office of an archivist is to forget no wrong done to truth.* She had laughed and pinned it above her desk, and he had watched her pin it, and said nothing, absolutely nothing, while knowing—knowing—that somewhere in his keeping lay the largest wrong ever done to truth in this town's history.

Had he been protecting her? That was the charitable reading, and part of her reached for it desperately. The confession concerned 1979. She had been born in 1979. Coincidence was possible. Coincidence was even common—archivists know better than anyone that dates rhyme by accident all the time. But the letters hadn't mentioned her, hadn't explained why *she* of all people should receive them, and their silence on that point rang louder than speech. Unless the reason was so obvious he hadn't needed to say it. Unless the reason was her.

*You are the only person I know who will not look away,* he might have reasoned. Or: *You are the only person with no family to protect.* 

Or—or—the thought arrived cold and unwelcome—*you are the only person with a stake in it.*

She pressed her knuckles against her mouth. Betrayal and grief braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which strand was which. She missed him. That was the obscene part. Eleven months dead and she missed him *while being angry at him*, missed his voice saying *Miss Galloway* with that dry warmth, missed the way he'd hum when he shelved. And underneath the missing, a colder question was forming, patient as frost:

If he hid this from her for twenty years, what else had he hidden?

* * *

She worked until the boiler kicked on and the radiators began their morning knock, and by then she had built a timeline on a legal pad, because timelines were how she thought.

1979: a death in the crypt, per the confession. November, per the ledger entries she'd found—those impossible round sums recurring every third of November, which she had circled in red pencil and which now looked less like accounting errors and more like anniversaries.

1979: her own birth, October, per the only document she possessed of her origins—a baptismal certificate with her mother's name inked in a hand she now, staring at it across the table, suddenly wanted very badly to compare against the letters.

Ruth Galloway. The name she'd carried all her life without knowing why it fit her like a borrowed coat.

Anselm had signed that certificate. She had known that for years—it was one of the few facts about her infancy the diocese had deigned to preserve. He had baptized her. He had named her, in effect; *Galloway* was Ruth's surname, passed down like an heirloom nobody discussed.

And in 1981, per the maintenance records she'd pulled two nights ago, the crypt lock had been quietly replaced—two years after the official sealing, a detail filed by no one, authorized by no one she could find. Someone had gone back inside. Someone with access. Someone the diocese trusted completely.

Her pencil hovered over the pad. She wrote, in her own small precise hand, a question she had never before allowed herself to write:

*Who signed my adoption papers?*

Because here was the thing she had learned in twenty years of archives, the lesson that governed everything: documents do not appear by accident. They are created by someone, for a reason, at a moment of choice. Her file existed. Somewhere in the diocesan records—in the very restricted materials she was sworn to maintain and forbidden, by an instruction she had never questioned until this week, to examine—there was a folder with her name on it, and a signature chain, and a story.

Anselm had kept her close for twenty years. He had trained her, employed her, protected her, lied to her, and finally confessed to her—all, apparently, from beyond a locked door she had walked past every day of her adult life.

The clock above the reading table showed seven-forty. Above her, faintly, the cathedral bells began the quarter-hour, and she heard the first car doors in the parking lot, the muffled voices of the sacristans arriving. The day was starting whether she understood anything or not.

Hazel gathered the letters, the sermons, the legal pad, and locked them in the bottom drawer of her own desk—the one drawer in the archive that belonged to her alone. Then she stood in front of cabinet twelve, the restricted materials, and listened to her own heartbeat.

Twenty years of obedience said *not your key to turn.*

Two letters in a dead man's hand said otherwise.

She reached for the ring of keys at her belt and found her fingers were shaking—and then stopped, entirely, because among the brass tags she had polished and sorted a hundred times, one tag caught the lamplight, and she read it, and the floor seemed to tilt gently beneath her.

The tag on the restricted-cabinet key was not original equipment. She knew every key in this building's history; she had catalogued them. This tag was newer, added within the last two years, engraved in a hand she now knew better than her own.

It read: **G — WHEN SHE IS READY.**

Father Anselm had left her a key. Filed, in his fashion, in plain sight.

Hazel closed her hand around it, cold brass warming against her palm, and understood that whatever was in that drawer had been waiting for her longer than she had been waiting for it—and that opening it would tell her not only who killed a man in 1979, but who she was.

She slid the key into the lock.
