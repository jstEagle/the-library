## 7. The Galloway Question

The diocesan records office kept its secrets the way a riverbed keeps stones—plainly, patiently, under a thin and ordinary surface of water.

Hazel had worked in that building for twenty years and had walked past the locked gray cabinet on the second floor perhaps ten thousand times without once seeing it. That was the trick of archives, she knew better than anyone: nothing hides so well as the thing filed in plain sight. The cabinet stood between the baptismal registers and the marriage dispensations, four drawers of manila folders labeled ADOPTIONS — CLOSED CASES in faded block letters, and it was as invisible to her as her own reflection in a window she'd stopped looking into years ago.

She had never been allowed to touch it.

That was the phrase she'd carried since she was nine years old, when Sister Bernadette at St. Vincent's Home had told her, with a gentleness that made it worse, *Your papers are with the diocese, dear. They're not for you to see.* Not sealed by court order. Not lost in a fire. Simply *not for you to see* — as though Hazel herself were a document someone else had classified.

She stood before the cabinet now on a Thursday morning in October, the two letters from Anselm's hand folded in her cardigan pocket like coals, and understood that she was about to break a rule she had spent her whole life keeping.

The archive was empty. Mrs. Okafor's Tuesday visit had ended; the cleaning crew wouldn't come until evening. Hazel had left the crypt door report open on her desk downstairs — the unbroken seal, the replaced lock, the year 1981 — a small fire smoldering on paper while she climbed these stairs.

Her key ring held forty-one keys. She knew each one by weight. The one for the gray cabinet she did not know, because it had never been on her ring, but Anselm had kept a spare set in his desk drawer, and Anselm's desk was hers now, inherited along with everything else he'd never told her.

The third key from the left turned with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Inside, the folders were arranged by year, then alphabetically. Hazel found herself cataloguing them automatically, the archivist's reflex running beneath her fear like a subfloor: 1962 through 1994, maybe three hundred files, thin ones from the fifties growing thicker through the seventies, the paper stock changing color decade by decade. Someone had maintained this collection carefully. Someone had believed it mattered.

She pulled the drawer for 1979.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that, and was grateful for it, and distrusted it all at once. Twenty years of handling fragile things had taught her body a calm her mind could not always justify.

GALLOWAY appeared between GALLAGHER and GALLO, a folder no thicker than a hymnal. Her own name on a tab, typed in the same elite typewriter font she'd seen on ten thousand other documents in this building — and yet seeing it here, in this drawer, made the name look foreign, like a word in a language she'd only ever read, never spoken.

Hazel Quentin Galloway.

*Quentin.* She had asked about it exactly once, at fourteen, and been told it was "a family name." Whose family, no one would say. She had invented, over the years, an entire ghost-uncle Quentin to house the name — a man who sailed, who read Latin, who had died young and gallantly somewhere warm. Children do that. They build furniture for the empty rooms adults leave behind.

She carried the folder to the reading table by the window, laid it flat, and sat with her hands on it for a long moment, the way she might rest her hands on a box she suspected contained something alive.

Then she opened it.

* * *

The first page was a standard relinquishment form, dated November 6th, 1979 — three days after the crypt had been sealed, she registered distantly, three days after the date that recurred in the ledgers like a heartbeat. The mother's name was printed in the same typewriter font:

RUTH QUENTIN GALLOWAY.

Hazel read the name four times. It did not become real on any of the readings. It sat on the page the way a photograph of water sits on a page — recognizable, and utterly dry.

*Ruth.* Her mother had a name. Of course she had a name; everyone had a name; Hazel had spent twenty years filing other people's names into boxes. But the name had always been theoretical, a grammatical necessity — *your mother*, subject of sentences Hazel declined to finish. Now it was Ruth, with a Q in the middle, sharing her daughter's initials like a hand-me-down coat.

H.G. R.G. She turned the initials over in her mind and felt the floor of her identity tilt a degree, the way a ship lists when cargo shifts below decks.

The form recorded the father as DECEASED. No name given. A single line in the remarks section, initialed by someone whose signature she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water down the spine:

*A.W.A.* — Anselm William Ambrose. Father Anselm had signed as witness. Had signed, she saw as she turned pages, nearly every document in the folder. He had been there at the beginning of her life, standing in the margins, initialing the edges of her existence, and she had never known.

The next page stopped her breath.

It was a memo, on diocesan letterhead, dated November 9th, 1979. TO: Office of the Chancellor. FROM: Rev. A. W. Ambrose. RE: Placement of infant, Galloway matter. Most of the language was the dry administrative code of adoptions — *suitable placement secured, foster arrangement pending permanent resolution* — but the final paragraph was not code at all, or rather it was code of a different kind, and Hazel, who had spent six weeks decoding ledger entries, read it the way a locksmith reads a lock:

*"In light of recent events within the parish, I recommend this file be closed under seal of the Ordinary and remain so indefinitely. The child's welfare is best served by silence. Some questions, once answered, cannot be unanswered; some doors, once opened, cannot be quietly resealed. I ask that this recommendation be honored for as long as I am able to honor it myself."*

Sealed by the Ordinary. By order of the bishop's office.

The same office, she thought, the same chain of authority, that had closed the crypt case in 1979. The same chancery stamp, the same signatory — she had photographed those documents last week, spread across her desk like evidence, and the signature on the closure order matched the authorization line on this memo as neatly as a key matches its ward.

Her origin and the crime were not merely entangled. They were *filed together*. Somewhere in the cathedral's memory, the story of Hazel Galloway and the story of the dead man in the crypt were cross-referenced, shelved side by side, dusted by the same indifferent maintenance of records.

She was not an orphan who had stumbled onto a mystery.

She was the mystery's appendix.

* * *

She sat at the reading table for a long time after that, the folder open before her, the light moving slowly across the floor as the morning aged, and let the vertigo have her.

This was what no one warned you about, she thought. Not grief — she knew grief's architecture, had lived in it since childhood, knew its load-bearing walls. This was something else. This was discovering that the building you'd lived in your whole life had a thirteenth floor. That the rooms you'd furnished so carefully — orphan, archivist, faithful servant of St. Ambrose, content in her ordered solitude — had been built over a void, and the void had a name, and the name was Ruth.

Twenty years she had tended this archive. Twenty years of knowing where every document slept, of believing that knowledge was a form of safety, that if she could just catalogue the world thoroughly enough nothing in it could ambush her. And all the while the most important file in the building had been three floors above her head, locked in a cabinet she passed daily, bearing her own name, guarded by the one rule she had never dreamed of breaking because breaking it meant admitting she wanted what was inside.

*Not for you to see.*

Sister Bernadette had been wrong, or kind, or both. It had been for her to see all along. It had simply not been permitted — by Anselm, by the chancery, by whatever machinery of protection and shame had decided that a baby was safer as a blank page than as a chapter.

And Anselm — she pressed her fingertips against her eyes — Anselm, who had taught her everything, who had hired her at nineteen with a kindness she'd mistaken for charity, who had written her two letters timed to arrive after his death like delayed charges set on a fuse — Anselm had stood in this room, or one like it, and recommended her own erasure. *Some questions, once answered, cannot be unanswered.* He had written that about her. About whether she should know her mother's name.

He had chosen silence for her. And then, twenty years later, dying, he had chosen truth — but only the truth that served his purpose, delivered on his schedule, in his handwriting, addressed to her like a summons. Never asking. Never warning. Never saying, on any of the thousand ordinary evenings they'd shared over tea in the archive kitchen: *Hazel, the dead man in our crypt is written into your blood.*

Betrayal braided itself into her grief so tightly she couldn't tell which strand she was pulling.

* * *

There was more in the folder. There was always more; archives reward the patient. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand — a practical gesture, not a weeping one; she had never learned to cry decorously — and kept turning pages.

A foster placement record: St. Vincent's Home for Children, effective December 1979. Signed by Anselm again. A note in his actual handwriting this time, not the typewriter — his small, precise script, the script she knew from twenty years of margin notes and shelf labels, and seeing it here, in a folder about her, made her chest ache in a way she hadn't anticipated:

*"Child is quiet, observant, fond of arranging objects by size. Will make an excellent keeper of records someday, God willing."*

He had known even then. Or he had decided even then. Either way, she had been groomed for this archive the way a key is cut for a lock — and the lock, she now understood, was not the cabinet. The lock was the truth itself, and she had been shaped, patiently, over decades, into the instrument that would one day open it.

The last document was the thinnest. A single sheet, undated, listing the contents of the file for a future auditor: relinquishment form, placement record, chancery memo, medical summary. And then a final line, added in different ink, different decade — the pen strokes heavier, the hand older but unmistakable:

*"One item removed from file [date illegible]: personal effects of R.Q.G., retained separately. See donor records, Founders' Trust."*

Founders' Trust. One of the three founding families whose money flowed through the ledgers every November third. Hazel stared at the words until they blurred.

Her mother's belongings — whatever remained of Ruth Quentin Galloway's short life — had not been destroyed, had not been scattered, had not been given to the child who might have wanted them. They had been *retained*. Filed away by the same families who paid the diocese every autumn to keep a crypt shut. Kept the way one keeps leverage, or evidence, or a hostage.

Someone still had her mother's things.

Hazel closed the folder. Her hands were no longer steady; she noticed that too, and this time did not distrust it. She returned it to the drawer, alphabetized between Gallagher and Gallo, and locked the cabinet, and stood in the empty office listening to her own breathing.

Below her, through the floor, she could feel the cathedral — the great stone body of it, the nave where she'd sat every Sunday of her adult life, the crypt beneath the east transept where a dead man waited with her blood in his veins or hers in his, she couldn't yet say which, grandfather or father, the genealogy forked like a lightning scar. Above her, the gray cabinet. Around her, on every wall, the orderly shelves of a town's agreed-upon past.

She had come up these stairs carrying two letters. She went back down carrying a third question, larger than both: *If my file was sealed to protect me, who decided what I needed protecting from — and are they still deciding?*

At her desk, the crypt door report waited where she'd left it. Beside it, on the blotter, lay something that had not been there when she went upstairs.

A plain white envelope, unsealed, unsigned. Inside, a single index card in block capitals that were not Anselm's hand — younger, harder, pressed deep enough to emboss the card:

*SOME FILES STAY CLOSED. ASK YOURSELF WHO BENEFITS WHEN YOU OPEN THEM. THEN ASK WHAT HAPPENS TO ARCHIVISTS WHO FORGET THEIR PLACE.*

Hazel read it twice. Then, with hands that had finally found their old steadiness — the steadiness of a woman who has spent twenty years learning that fear is just information arriving early — she took out a fresh folder, wrote a label in her precise archival hand, and slid the card inside.

She filed it under V.

For Voss, she told herself. Though she didn't yet know why she was so sure.

For now, it was enough to know that whoever had written it had been in her office while she sat upstairs, holding her own name, learning who she was — and that they had let her learn it anyway.

Which meant they weren't afraid of what she knew.

They were measuring how much she'd risk to learn the rest.
