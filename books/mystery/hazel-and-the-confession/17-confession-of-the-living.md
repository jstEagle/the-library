## 17. Confession of the Living

The pain woke him at two in the morning, the way it always did now—patient, familiar, almost courteous. Samuel Voss lay in the dark of his bedroom and let it wash through him like a tide he'd stopped fighting months ago. The morphine took the edge off by day. At night, the cancer introduced itself properly, made its rounds, reminded him whose house this was.

He got up because lying still had become its own kind of lie.

In the kitchen he poured himself three fingers of the good bourbon Edmund had given him for his sixtieth birthday—the last gift between them that hadn't been a transaction—and stood at the window looking out at the town. St. Ambrose's spire caught the streetlight at its tip, a thin bright needle stitched into the dark. Forty-six years he'd looked at that spire. Delivered babies in the shadow of it. Signed death certificates beneath it. Buried half the town with one hand while the other hand kept the secret that held the whole place up like a keystone nobody knew was there.

The archivist woman had been asking questions for eleven days now. He knew because Dolores Okafor had called him on the fourth day, her voice thin as tissue paper: *She came to see me, Sammy. She's asking about the rain.* And he'd known then that Anselm's letters had finally landed where the old priest had always intended them to land—in the hands of Ruth's daughter, whether anyone had told Hazel Galloway that yet or not.

Edmund had called too, of course. Edmund called every day now, smooth as varnish, asking after his health, offering to drive him to appointments, saying things like *we should get ahead of this* and *the family name* in the same breath, as if they were the same thing. As if they had ever been the same thing.

Samuel drank the bourbon and thought about the file in his desk drawer. He'd written it in October, when the diagnosis went from bad to terminal, in handwriting that no longer resembled his own. *To whom it may concern,* it began, and he'd hated those words immediately, because they were coward's words, and he had been a coward for forty-six years and did not intend to die as one.

But intention, he'd learned, was a currency that depreciated. Every morning he meant to deliver it. Every evening he hadn't.

Then tonight, driving home from the hospital past the cathedral, he'd seen a light burning in the archive wing at ten o'clock. One small yellow window, high up, like an eye that refused to close. And he'd understood, with the strange clarity the dying are granted, that if he didn't go to her, she would come to him—and she would come to him as a stranger, and he would meet her as one, and there would be no time left to be anything else.

* * *

Hazel Galloway opened the door before he knocked.

Later he would think about that—how she must have heard his car, or seen his headlights sweep the archive windows, or simply known, the way blood knows blood, that someone was coming for her in the dark. She stood in the doorway in a cardigan over her nightclothes, gray-streaked hair pinned up crooked, and behind her the archive glowed warm and orderly, every shelf squared, every folder flush. A life spent making things line up.

"Dr. Voss," she said. Not a question. Not warmth either. Just the name, set down carefully, like something that might go off.

"Miss Galloway." His voice came out rusted. "May I come in? It won't take long. I don't—" He stopped, corrected himself. "It will take exactly as long as it takes. But I'm short on time. Mine, I mean."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softening, but assessment. He recognized it; he'd seen it in the faces of families deciding whether to believe a prognosis. She stepped back and let him in.

* * *

She didn't offer him a chair at first. She stood on the far side of her reading table with the lamp between them, arms folded, and he understood that the furniture in this room answered to her, and he would sit when she decided he could sit.

"You've been sending me notes," she said.

"No."

"The board chairman has been sending me notes."

"Yes." He said it flatly, and watched her flinch at how easily he confirmed it. "Edmund. Since the fourth day, I'd guess. He found out about your questions from Dolores, same as I did. He's been writing threats in block capitals for forty years—he used to leave them in my mail slot too, back when silence needed maintaining. He thinks fear is a tool. He's never learned it's a confession."

"You're telling me this," Hazel said slowly, "because you want me to trust you."

"I'm telling you this because I'm dying, and dead men have no use for trust. Only for truth, and only if someone will carry it." He set the envelope on the table between them—thick, sealed, his name written across it in the ruined hand. "I wrote this in October. I've driven past this building eleven nights running trying to find the courage to bring it in."

"And what stopped you?"

"Fear," he said. "The family kind. You'll understand in about four minutes."

She looked at the envelope for a long moment. Then she pulled out the chair opposite hers and said, "Sit down, Dr. Voss," and there was something in her voice—an archivist's cadence, deposition-taking, record-keeping—that told him she already knew this was going to be testimony.

He sat. The chair was hard. Everything was hard now except the truth, which had gotten strangely light.

* * *

"Tell me what you know so far," he said. "So I don't insult you."

"I know the crypt was sealed in November 1979 and the official record is false. I know payments moved from three founding families to the diocese every November third for forty-six years. I know Father Anselm wrote me letters before he died and timed their delivery. I know my adoption was signed by him nine days after the sealing, and that my mother, Ruth Galloway, died the same week. I know the man in the crypt is family to me. What I don't know is which family. Or which brother still breathes."

Samuel closed his eyes. She had done in eleven days what forty-six years of his medicine, his charity, his good works had not managed: she had laid the whole skeleton on the table, articulated, labeled, waiting only for flesh.

"My brother," he said. "Thomas Voss. Your grandfather."

Her breath caught—a small sound, quickly mastered, but he heard it. He kept his eyes closed. It was easier to say it to the dark.

"Ruth was Thomas's daughter. Her mother—my sister Margaret—died young, and Thomas raised Ruth alone. In 1979 there was a quarrel over Ruth's inheritance. Thomas wanted to hold her share in trust until she married a man he approved of. Ruth wanted her money and her freedom, and she'd found both in a boy named—well. That part isn't mine to tell. On the second of November, the night before the Founders' Day service, Thomas went down into the crypt to move the family papers ahead of the sealing. Ruth followed him. So did I."

"Why you?"

"Because Ruth telephoned me. She said, *Uncle Sam, come quick, Papa's locked himself down there with the deeds and he's been drinking.* Simple as that. A niece asking her uncle for help with her stubborn father." He opened his eyes and found her face across the lamplight, pale and absolutely still. "I went down. We argued, Thomas and I. About Ruth, about money, about the Voss name and who had the right to spend it. He swung at me first. I swear to God, Miss Galloway, he swung first—but I was fifty years younger and stronger than I ever wanted to be, and I hit him back once, open-handed, meaning to stagger him. He fell against the crypt steps. His head. The sound—" His voice failed, gathered itself. "I've heard that sound every night for forty-six years. Morphine doesn't touch it."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the building a pipe ticked, cooling.

"He wasn't dead," Samuel said. "Not at first. I'm a doctor; I knew the injury was grave, and I knew—if I called it in honestly—that Ruth's child would be born into scandal. An illegitimate pregnancy, a dead grandfather, a manslaughter charge against a founding-family doctor. Ruth was five months pregnant. She was standing on the stairs holding her belly, and she looked at me, and I looked at Anselm—who had come down when he heard the shouting—and the three of us made a decision in about ninety seconds that we spent the rest of our lives paying for."

"We let him die," he said. "We could have tried. There was a chance—small, maybe, but a chance—and we weighed it against a baby's future and a town's peace, and we chose wrong. Anselm arranged everything. The sealing. The records. The story that Thomas had gone abroad. He signed your adoption papers nine days later because Ruth begged him to—she couldn't raise you under that weight, she said, and she was right, God forgive her, and then her heart gave out that winter and Anselm was left holding all of us. He carried it thirty more years. He was a better man than me. He at least found the courage to confess before he died. I only found the courage to confess *after*."

* * *

Hazel had not moved. Samuel realized he'd expected weeping, or fury, or the chair thrown back. Instead she sat with her hands flat on the table, the posture of a woman cataloguing, and when she spoke her voice was level in a way that frightened him more than rage would have.

"The notes in my filing trays. The fire threat Edmund implied. The early retirement offer. All Edmund."

"All Edmund. He learned the truth within the year—Anselm told him, God knows why, maybe to share the weight—and Edmund's answer was to build the payment schedule. Three families, annual installments, expiring with the statute. He calls it stewardship. He believes, sincerely, that he saved this town." Samuel laughed, and it turned to coughing halfway out, dry and terrible. "He sent you threats because he thinks you're a burglar breaking into the house of everyone's respectability. He doesn't know what you actually are."

"What am I?"

He looked at her then—really looked—at the gray in her hair, the order of her hands, the stubborn architecture of her face, and saw in it a ghost he'd delivered screaming into the world on a March night in 1980 while her mother's coffin waited in the churchyard above.

"You're Thomas Voss's granddaughter," he said. "And you're mine, too—my grand-niece, my sister's blood, the last living piece of the family Edmund and I broke. Ruth named you Hazel before she died. Did you know that? She chose it herself. Nobody gave you that name. Your mother did."

For the first time, Hazel Quentin Galloway's composure cracked—not into tears but into something worse, a trembling around the mouth, the look of a woman watching forty years of orphanhood rearrange itself into genealogy. He reached across the table and set the envelope beside her hand.

"Everything's in there. Names, dates, the quarrel, the fall, the decision, the payments. Signed and witnessed—I made Dolores witness it in September; she's known since the rain, she's carried it longer than any of us. When the statute expires, publish it. Don't wait for permission. Edmund will fight you with lawyers and sermons and the full weight of the Voss name, and he will win for a while, because men like Edmund always win for a while." He pushed himself up from the chair, joints announcing every year of his betrayal. "But you're an archivist, Miss Galloway. You know better than anyone that paper outlives power. File it under whatever letter keeps it safe."

At the door he stopped, hand on the frame, and looked back at her one last time.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I know it's worthless. I know sorry is the smallest coin in circulation. But it's all I have, and I'd rather leave it with you than with God."

He walked out into the cold, and behind him the archive light burned on, and he understood that he had just done the only thing in forty-six years that might outlive him.

* * *

Hazel sat alone for a long time with the envelope unopened, listening to the pipes tick and the dark press against the windows.

Then she slit it open, and the first thing she saw was a photograph clipped to the first page—a young woman on the cathedral steps, 1979, laughing, one hand resting on a belly just beginning to show.

Ruth. Her mother. Holding *her*.

And beneath the photograph, in Samuel Voss's failing hand, a single line that stopped her breath:

*There is a second copy of the ledger. Anselm hid it where only family would look. Ask yourself what a Voss would never throw away.*

Outside, dawn was still hours away, and somewhere across town a light was burning in Edmund Voss's study, and Hazel understood that whatever came next, it would come fast.
