## 21. The Reading

The town hall had not been full since the flood of '96, and even then people had come to argue about sandbags. Tonight they came because Hazel Galloway had sent a letter to every household in Ashcombe, printed on archive paper, signed with her own name: *I have found what St. Ambrose buried. Come and hear it, or spend the rest of your lives pretending you didn't.*

She stood at the podium in a gray cardigan, the same one she wore to work, and behind her on an easel she'd propped a framed photograph of a ledger page. She had considered slideshows, projectors, some modern apparatus. But she was an archivist. She believed in paper. Paper could be held. Paper could be checked.

The room smelled of wet wool and old varnish. Three hundred chairs, and people standing along the walls. Dolores Okafor sat in the front row with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral she had helped plan. Edmund Voss sat two rows back, flanked by his lawyer, his silver hair immaculate, his face arranged into an expression of patient grievance — the look of a man who intended to be seen as the victim of an ungrateful employee. Samuel Voss was not there. Everyone noticed. Everyone said nothing.

Hazel set a single sheet of paper on the podium. Anselm's second confession, recovered from the crypt, photocopied for the record, the original sealed in evidence bags she had purchased herself from the hardware store on Miller Street because she no longer trusted anyone official.

"My name is Hazel Quentin Galloway," she said, and heard her voice come out steadier than she felt. "For twenty years I have kept this town's records. Tonight I'm going to read you one of them."

* * *

From the back of the hall, where he had come late to avoid the cameras, Father Marek watched the archivist's hands. They did not shake. He had known Hazel for six years, since his transfer from Bratislava, and he had never once seen her hands shake — not when the roof leaked over the rare manuscripts, not when the diocesan auditor accused her of misfiling a bequest. But he had learned, in six years, that stillness in Hazel Galloway was not calm. It was discipline. Somewhere beneath that gray cardigan was a woman holding herself together with both hands.

He had begged her, three days ago in the sacristy, to let the dead stay dead. *The cathedral will not survive it,* he'd said. *The town will not survive it.* And she had looked at him with those pale, patient eyes and said, "Father, it survived on a lie for forty-five years. Let's see how it does with the truth."

Now he listened to her read Anselm's words — Anselm, who had baptized him into this parish's secrets before Marek ever arrived — and understood that neither of them had been right about what survival meant.

* * *

*"On the third of November, 1979,"* Hazel read, *"I helped carry a dead man down seventeen steps and seal him behind a door I locked myself. His name was Thomas Voss. He was killed by his brother-in-law, Samuel Voss, in a quarrel over money and a child. I have kept this confession for twenty years because a girl needed a childhood more than a town needed a trial. That girl is reading this now. I am sorry, Hazel. I should have given you the truth and the childhood both. I did not know how. I chose, as everyone in this room has chosen at least once, the smaller cowardice."*

A sound went through the crowd like wind through wheat. Not gasping — something lower, more collective. Recognition. Half the people in that hall had knelt at Anselm's funeral. Half of them had eaten dinner at a Voss table, or taken a Voss scholarship, or named a boat after a Voss grandfather. The founding families were not history in Ashcombe. They were the water supply.

Hazel turned the page.

"The ledger," she said. "You've all received copies in your mailboxes this week. For forty-five years, on every third of November, payments moved from three accounts — Voss Holdings, the Calloway Trust, and the estate of Margaret Ashcombe-Reeve — into a diocesan maintenance fund designated for 'crypt preservation.' The crypt, as you know, has been sealed since 1979. The final payment cleared last month. The statute of limitations expired eleven days ago." She paused. "Whoever arranged this schedule wanted the truth to become legally safe before it became publicly safe. It never did. So it becomes public tonight."

Edmund Voss rose. The lawyer's hand caught his sleeve; he shook it off.

"This is a slander," he said, and his voice carried beautifully — a man who had given a hundred toasts in this very hall. "This woman was dismissed from her post—"

"You tried to dismiss me," Hazel said mildly. "The board declined to ratify it. You can check the minutes. Page forty-one. I filed them myself."

Someone laughed. It broke something open. The laughter spread, nervous and then less nervous, and Edmund Voss stood in the middle of it with his jaw working, and Dolores Okafor thought: *there it is. There's the boy from the rain.*

* * *

Dolores had rehearsed nothing. At eighty-one she had stopped rehearsing things; it was one of the few advantages of age. When Hazel called her name — "Mrs. Okafor has agreed to say a few words" — she rose from the front row with the help of the armrests and made her way to the podium, and the hall went quiet for her the way it goes quiet for the very old and the very young, out of some animal courtesy.

"I was thirty-six years old," she said. "Founders' Day service, 1979. It rained so hard the gutters jumped their banks, and I know it rained because my hat was ruined and I complained about it for a week. But the next morning, the *Ashcombe Courier* printed a photograph of that service under a blue sky. Sunshine. Everybody smiling on the cathedral steps, dry as bones." She looked out at them. "And nobody said a word. Not me, not anybody. Because the picture was nicer than the rain."

She gripped the podium edge.

"That's what we did. All of us. We let them print sunshine. I saw young Mr. Voss there that night — Edmund, and his father's brother Samuel — carrying something heavy between them toward the crypt door, and Ruth Galloway walking behind them like a ghost, and I told myself I'd imagined it. I told myself that story for forty-five years until it got so worn out it fell apart in my hands." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I'm done pretending it didn't rain."

She came down slowly. No one applauded. Applause would have been wrong, and everyone knew it, and the silence itself felt like the first honest thing the town had done in decades.

* * *

Samuel Voss had intended to stay home. He had gotten as far as his armchair with the television off, listening to the rain — actual rain, the first of the season — when he found himself putting on his coat.

He stood at the back of the hall now, half-hidden by the coat rack, leaning on his cane. The cancer had taken thirty pounds off him since summer; nobody recognized him at first, and that was a mercy. He listened to Hazel read the ledger entries, year after year after year, his own signature authorizing each one in the early decades before the lawyers took over, and he felt something he had not expected to feel, which was gratitude. She was doing it cleanly. No theatrics. Just the record, laid end to end, the machinery of his whole life exposed gear by gear.

When she finished, she said, "Dr. Voss asked to speak tonight. He's here. Dr. Voss?"

The room turned. He felt three hundred pairs of eyes find the dying man by the coat rack, and he walked forward on the cane, one deliberate step at a time, and the aisle opened for him the way aisles do.

At the podium he didn't need the microphone. He'd spent fifty years making himself heard over crying children.

"My brother Thomas is dead because of me," he said. "I struck him once, in anger, in the crypt below St. Ambrose, on the third of November, 1979, and he fell against the steps, and he died. I have said that sentence perhaps four times in my life, always to people who already knew. This is the first time I've said it to strangers." He looked down at his hands, the surgeon's hands, steady even now, which seemed like a joke God was playing. "I was spared a trial by a calendar. Nobody in this room owes me forgiveness, and I'm not asking for it. I'm telling you what happened because Hazel Galloway risked everything she had to make lying impossible, and the least I owe her is company in the truth."

He looked up and found Hazel at the side of the stage, watching him with her archivist's face.

"Miss Galloway," he said. "Your mother loved you before she ever met you. Whatever else is true tonight, that's true too."

Then he stepped back from the podium, and the hall stayed silent, and he understood that the silence was the verdict, and that he would live — however long that was — inside it.

* * *

Edmund Voss did not speak again. He left during the questions, escorted by his lawyer past a wall of faces that no longer parted for him, and outside, a reporter from the regional paper asked him for comment and he said, "No comment," which the next morning's headline rendered, accurately, as the only thing he had left to say.

Inside, the questions came like weather. A Calloway cousin demanded to know if the trust documents were public. They were. A woman whose father had worked the Voss mills asked whether restitution would be pursued. Hazel said the statute foreclosed prosecution but not civil action, and that she had retained copies of everything, and the woman nodded slowly, filing it away, and Hazel saw the future assembling itself in the room — lawsuits, audits, a historical commission, someone's thesis, someone's novel — and felt no triumph. Only the strange lightness of a weight transferred. It was theirs now. All of it. She had only carried it to the door.

Near midnight, Father Marek found her packing papers into the same canvas bag she'd used for twenty years.

"The cathedral will survive," he said. It wasn't a question anymore; he'd surrendered the question somewhere around hour two. "I keep thinking it won't, and then I keep being wrong."

"It'll survive," Hazel said. "Cathedrals are good at surviving. It's the stories we tell about them that need replacing." She zipped the bag. "I'm reopening the archive Monday. Full recataloguing. Everything unsealed, everything cross-referenced. It'll take years."

Marek almost smiled. "Of course it will. What else would you do?"

She looked out at the emptying hall — Dolores being walked carefully to a car by neighbors, the folding chairs clacking shut, the janitor sweeping around people instead of waiting for them to leave, because even the janitor seemed to understand that nobody wanted this night to end too quickly, that ending it meant going home to the new version of Ashcombe.

"Hazel," Marek said quietly. "Your mother's grave. St. Ambrose churchyard, east row. Have you—"

"I know," she said. "I found it Tuesday. I've been bringing flowers." She paused. "I put some on Thomas Voss's marker too. He waited forty-five years under my floor. It seemed polite."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The streets shone under the streetlamps, washed clean, and Hazel walked home through the shine with her canvas bag over her shoulder, past houses where lights were still on, where she could see, through windows, families sitting up late at kitchen tables with photocopies of a ledger spread between them, talking — arguing, grieving, but talking — and she understood that this was what mending looked like at the beginning: not quiet, not whole, just honest, and lit.

In her bag, at the bottom, beneath the confession and the photographs and the minutes of page forty-one, lay a blank folder she had bought that afternoon. Heavy stock. Archive grade.

On the tab, in her small precise hand, she had written a single letter:

**G.**

Tomorrow she would fill it.
