## 7. Ashes and Alibis

The hospital in Corvane was forty minutes from Ashford Hollow, and I drove them all in nineteen.

I told myself it was because every minute mattered — that if Ruth Okafor was going to talk again, she'd talk to me first, before the sheriff's office got a deputy stationed outside her door with a clipboard and a smile. I told myself it was journalism. Deadlines. Instinct.

It wasn't. It was guilt with its foot on the accelerator.

I'd been the one who knocked on Ruth's door. I'd been the one who sat at her kitchen table drinking bad coffee while she told me about Elias Hazel and the child and the massacre at the Kettle Creek homestead, her hands wrapped around her mug like it was the last warm thing in the world. And twelve hours later her house was a black skeleton against the dawn sky, and she was in an ambulance with smoke inhalation, and the only thing left standing on her property was the mailbox, which had bent but not burned, as if even it had decided to keep its mouth shut.

The nurse at the desk gave me the room number without checking anything, which should have been my first warning. In my experience, hospitals guard privacy like banks guard vaults — unless someone upstairs has decided you're allowed through.

* * *

Ruth looked smaller than she had in her kitchen. Smaller and older, the way people do when something has been subtracted from them overnight. Her hair was washed — someone had washed it — and there were oxygen prongs in her nose and a bruise the shape of a thumbprint fading along her jaw, which nobody had mentioned in any report I'd heard.

She saw me in the doorway and her whole body did a thing I'll never forget. It didn't flinch. It went still. Total, deliberate stillness, the stillness of an animal that has calculated that movement is more dangerous than motionlessness.

"Wren." My name came out flat as a coin dropped on a counter.

"Ruth. God. I'm so glad you're okay. They said you got out through the back—"

"Through the kitchen window." She said it like she was reading a weather report. "I heard glass break downstairs and I went out the window. That's what happened."

"That's not—" I stopped. Because here's the thing about twenty years of interviewing people: eventually you stop hearing words and start hearing architecture. The load-bearing walls underneath. And everything Ruth said next, I could hear the scaffolding behind it. Rehearsed. Fed. Approved.

"I shouldn't have talked to you," she said. "I was confused. Grieving my mother, and I got carried away. Old stories get bigger every time you tell them. There's no pact. There's no ledger. Elias Hazel is exactly where they buried him, or where they didn't, and either way it isn't a story."

"Ruth—"

"It isn't a story, Wren." And there — just for half a second — her eyes flicked past my shoulder to the door, to the hallway beyond it, and back. "I'm sorry I wasted your time."

I pulled the visitor's chair closer. She watched me do it the way you watch someone handle a live wire.

"The responding fire crew found an accelerant pattern in the front hall," I said quietly. "I've seen the photos. Pour lines, Ruth. Someone poured something down your hallway while you slept upstairs."

"I knocked over a lamp." Her voice didn't rise at all. "Old wiring. These houses are a hundred years old. You know that."

"I know that you had a bruise on your face when they brought you in, and it isn't from falling out a window onto grass."

For a moment — one long, terrible moment — I thought she was going to crack. Her chin trembled. Her hand moved on the blanket toward mine and then stopped, mid-air, like a bird hitting glass.

"You seem like a nice person," she said, "and I think maybe you actually believe all this. That's the saddest part. Go home, Wren. Write about the harvest festival. Write about anything. Please."

The *please* broke something in me. Not because it was pleading — because it was precise. It was the please of a woman who knew exactly what she was asking me to abandon and exactly what would happen to both of us if I didn't.

I stood up. At the door I turned back, and I said the thing I probably shouldn't have said, the thing that made everything after it worse:

"They can burn your house, Ruth. They can't burn the recording. I already made a copy. It's somewhere they'll never find it."

I meant it as comfort. Watch her face as I said it and tell me what it was instead. Terror. Pure, distilled terror, rising off her like heat off asphalt — because I hadn't protected her. I'd signed for her. A copy meant leverage, and leverage meant she would never be allowed to simply recant and be believed. She'd always be the woman who might still have the tape.

I walked out of that room understanding, with total clarity, that I had made things harder for the only person who'd helped me. It's a specific kind of sickness, learning that your courage is a currency other people pay for.

* * *

The official report came out three days later, and it was a masterpiece of the genre.

Cause of fire: accidental. Origin: electrical fault in aging wiring near the front hall. Accelerant detected: none. (None — though two separate volunteer firefighters, men I'd known since they were paperboys, told me off the record that the dog had gone crazy over the hallway floor before anyone said the word accelerant.) Smoke inhalation: minor. Bruising: consistent with escape through window. Cause of ignition: undetermined but non-suspicious.

Non-suspicious. Two words doing the work of a shovel.

I requested the full incident file through a records request, because that's what you do when you're a journalist, and what came back taught me more than the report itself. Not what was in it. Who wrote it.

Deputy First Class Garrett Voss. Responding officer of record.

I sat in my basement archive with the file open on one screen and the digitized founding charter on the other, and I ran the name through the marginalia ledger I'd spent weeks decoding. Initials, dates, payments, spanning a hundred and fifty years. Every founding family accounted for, generation after generation, their contributions recorded in that cramped antique shorthand like entries in a church donation book.

G.V., 1998. G.V., 2004. G.V., 2011. Same hand, same initials, same family line running unbroken back to a man named Garret Voss who had signed the original charter in 1847 with ink that had cost him, by the exchange rate of the era, roughly the price of a good horse.

The deputy who investigated the arson attempt on Ruth Okafor's house belonged to a family that had been paying into the silence since before the town had streetlights.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part where a lesser storyteller would say *and then everything made sense.* It didn't make sense. It made a different kind of sense, a sick, structural sense, the way you understand a building is rotten not from one beam but from the pattern of the cracks. One corrupt deputy is a scandal. A deputy whose great-great-grandfather's initials sit four lines down from Elias Hazel's in a coded payment ledger, investigating a fire set to silence the last person talking about that ledger — that's not corruption. That's maintenance.

This was never a secret being kept by people. It was a secret being kept by an institution. The law itself was a subscriber.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world tilts: I worked.

I built a timeline. Ruth's interview, timestamped 6:14 p.m. Fire dispatch call, logged 1:52 a.m. — a gap of under eight hours, most of them dark. Sheriff Dane Mercer's press statement calling it "a tragic accident, and frankly a distraction from the heritage ceremony our town deserves," delivered at 9 a.m. sharp, polished enough that he'd either written it in advance or had help.

And then there was the detail I couldn't shake. The dispatch log showed the fire reported by a passerby at 1:52 a.m., but Deputy Voss arrived on scene at 1:54. Two minutes. From wherever he was, to a house at the far end of Hollow Road, in under two minutes, on a night when the roads were damp.

Either Garrett Voss had broken the laws of physics, or he'd already been close. Very close. Sitting-there-watching close.

I called him. Of course I called him. Twenty years of podcasting teaches you that the phone call itself is evidence — how fast they answer, how smooth the first sentence is, where the pauses fall.

He answered on the second ring. "Voss."

"Deputy, this is Wren Dravenmoor. I'm doing a follow-up piece on the Okafor fire."

A pause. Short. Controlled. "That matter's closed. Accidental electrical. It's all in the report."

"It is. I read it twice. Deputy, where were you coming from when you got the call? Dispatch shows you on scene in under two minutes."

Another pause, longer this time. When he spoke again his voice had changed — cooled, smoothed out, gone rehearsed in the same way Mercer's press statement was rehearsed, in the same way Ruth's recantation was rehearsed. This town, apparently, trained everyone in the same elocution.

"I was on patrol on Hollow Road. Routine."

"Hollow Road runs eight miles. Where on it?"

"Dravenmoor." My name in his mouth sounded like a case file. "You've been stirring up a lot of old nonsense lately. The Hazel thing, the ceremony thing. Now you're bothering a burn victim in her hospital bed. I'd think about what you actually know versus what you think you know. Those are different things, and confusing them tends to... escalate situations."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's advice. Free of charge." A beat. "Anything else?"

"No, Deputy. Thank you for your time."

"No trouble." And he hung up — not slammed, hung up, gently, like a man closing a door on a room he intended to come back to.

I sat there in the blue glow of my monitors with goosebumps crawling up my arms. Not because of the threat. Because of the phrasing. *What you actually know versus what you think you know.* He hadn't denied anything. He hadn't claimed ignorance of my investigation. He'd spoken like a man reading from an inventory — my knowledge, itemized, assessed for danger.

They knew what I had. Which meant someone knew what Ruth and I had discussed. Which meant either her house had been listening, or my basement had.

* * *

That night I went back to Ruth's property. The tape across the door had gone gray and slack, and inside, the smell of wet ash still hung in the air like a held breath. I wore gloves and took photographs of everything — the pour lines in the hall floor that the official report said weren't there, the melted lamp that was supposedly the culprit sitting upright and intact on an end table six feet from the origin point, the bedroom window with its clean, unhurried exit path.

In the kitchen, half-buried in collapsed ceiling tile, I found her coffee mug. The one she'd been holding when she told me everything. It had survived. Of course it had — stoneware laughs at fire. I crouched over it for a long time, and I won't pretend I wasn't crying a little, kneeling in the ruins of a stranger's kindness.

Because that's what Ruth had been. A stranger who owed me nothing, who opened her door because her conscience outweighed her fear, and I had repaid it by making her the most dangerous woman in the county.

On my way out, my flashlight caught something wedged in the frame of the back door — the door Ruth had escaped through, or hadn't. A sliver of plastic, snapped off, lodged in the latch plate. I pried it free and turned it under the light.

A broken zip tie. Fresh. The kind used to secure a padlock hasp so it doesn't rattle.

Someone had locked that door behind themselves on their way out. Locked it so that if the woman upstairs woke and ran for the back, she'd hit a sealed door and lose the seconds that mattered.

She'd gotten out the window anyway. Barely. But they'd planned for her not to.

Standing there in the burnt throat of that house, I finally let myself say the sentence I'd been circling for days, out loud, to no one, my voice bouncing off wet char:

"This was attempted murder, and the man who ruled it an accident is on the payroll of the people who ordered it."

And then, quieter, because the second thought was worse than the first:

"So who do I even take it to?"

The state police, who'd take the county's file at face value? The district attorney, whose last campaign had been funded by a fundraiser hosted at the Pike estate? The newspaper, owned by a charter-family widow?

Every door I mentally knocked on opened onto the same faces.

There was exactly one institution left that Ruth's fire hadn't touched, one place where the truth still had any value at all. Not the courts. Not the cops. Not the town.

The audience.

Twenty years, I'd built it — forty thousand listeners who trusted the voice in their ears more than they trusted any badge. I'd always thought of the archive as a product. Standing in the ashes, I understood it was something else entirely. An arsenal. The only one I had.

I drove home with the windows down and the plan forming in the cold air, and I didn't see the car that pulled out of the tree line a quarter mile behind me and followed, patient as a shadow, all the way to the edge of town — where it stopped, because whoever was driving already knew exactly where I lived.

They didn't need to follow me home.

They'd had the key for years.

* * *

Next morning, I started building the broadcast case. Sworn recordings. Scanned documents. Copies in places no subpoena and no arsonist could reach.

And the first thing I did — the very first — was change the lock on my basement door.
