## 14. Running Out of Alphabet

The ledger had a grammar, and once Wren learned it, she couldn't stop reading.

She'd spent three nights at her kitchen table with the founding charter unrolled under a banker's lamp, the marginalia transcribed onto index cards, the index cards arranged into columns on the floor like a crime scene. Initials. Dates. Amounts. The payments ran in cycles — quarterly at first, then annually, then, in the lean decades, whenever someone could scrape together anything at all. A hundred and fifty years of families paying rent on a lie.

But the ledger wasn't just a record of what people gave. It was a record of what they were protecting. And buried in the earliest entries, in Elias Hazel's own cramped hand, was a line Wren had read forty times before its meaning finally detonated:

*E.H. — trust established for M., per agreement. Disbursements to commence upon majority.*

M. The witness child. Not dead. Not smuggled across the country and lost to history. *Held in trust.* Which meant there was paper somewhere — a trust instrument, a beneficiary designation, something with a name on it that had been updated every generation like a relay baton passed hand to hand.

And if the trust was still paying out — and the recent entries suggested it was, small disbursements routed through a land company Wren had never heard of until now — then the heir wasn't a ghost in an archive. The heir was alive. Adult. Local.

Wren got up from the floor, knees cracking, and made coffee she wouldn't drink. Outside her window, Ashford Hollow lay under its usual October fog, streetlights dissolving into soft orange smears, the water tower standing over everything like a headstone. She had lived inside this view for thirty-eight years. It had never looked like a cage before.

She worked backward through the alphabet.

The ledger's later entries used full initials — the families had gotten careless, or confident, or both. Cross-referencing against census records, church registries, and the county's digitized birth index, she built a filter: descendants of signers, born within five years of any major trust restructuring, still resident in the Hollow, under forty. The list started at eleven names. Then seven. Then four.

Then three.

She wrote them on a fresh card and stared at it until the letters swam:

**Dale Pruitt, 34.** Grandson of a signer. Works at his family's feed store. Married, two kids, coaches Little League. A man with too much to lose and no apparent curiosity about anything.

**Cormac Bell, 29.** Descendant of the Bells, who'd signed the second-generation addendum. Left for college, came back, managed the hardware store. Quiet. Watchful. Had once, Wren remembered with a chill, asked her at a barbecue whether her podcast ever covered "old town stuff."

And **Marisol Hale, 27.** The librarian.

Wren laughed out loud, alone in her kitchen, a short ugly sound. Of course. Of course it was Marisol — who had pulled county records for her without being asked, who had flagged the 1874 land transfer Wren would have missed, who had shown up at the archive two days after the exhumation with coffee and a smile and the question, casual as weather: *"Find anything good?"*

No. It couldn't be. Marisol was a Hale, and the Hales weren't a charter family — they were newcomers by Hollow standards, arrived in the 1950s, which was exactly the point, wasn't it? You don't hide the heir among the conspirators. You hide her one layer out, close enough to watch, far enough to plausibly deny. Raised by the very structure that destroyed her family, employed by the institution that kept its records, positioned — deliberately, elegantly — as the helpful hand extended to anyone who dug too deep.

Or she was just a nice young woman who liked books, and Wren was so far down the rabbit hole she was seeing patterns in wallpaper.

That was the thing about claustrophobia. It didn't always come from walls closing in. Sometimes it came from not knowing which walls were real.

* * *

She should have moved faster. She knew that afterward, replaying it, cataloguing the hours she'd lost to caution. But she wanted to be wrong about Marisol, and wanting to be wrong is its own kind of paralysis. So instead of driving to the library, she spent the morning verifying — pulling the trust's land-company filings from the state registry, confirming the registered agent was a law firm whose senior partner was named in the ledger eleven times.

By noon she had it. By noon it was over.

The knock came at 12:40 p.m. Not the doorbell — the knock, heavy fist on wood, the sound authority makes when it isn't asking. Through the front window Wren saw the cruiser angled in her driveway and two deputies flanking the porch, and behind them, unhurried, Sheriff Dane Mercer climbing out of his SUV with his hat in his hands like a man arriving at a funeral he'd helped plan.

She considered, briefly, not answering. Then she remembered the cameras in her basement, the dead-man switch Theo had helped her arm, the fact that everything she owned was already duplicated in three places Mercer didn't know about — and she opened the door.

"Sheriff."

"Ms. Dravenmoor." His voice was gentle, which frightened her more than shouting would have. Dane Mercer had known her since she was nine years old, when he'd carried her out of the creek after she fell through the ice. He'd taught her to drive on the quarry road. He'd stood at her grandmother's funeral with his hand on her shoulder and said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and she'd thought then that silence between them was a kind of love. "I'm sorry to do this."

"Do what?"

He held up the warrant. She read it twice because the first time refused to make sense. *Tampering with a burial site. Destruction of public records. Obstruction.* Dated yesterday. Signed by a judge whose name she recognized from the ledger — not as a signer, but as the son of one.

"You're arresting me," she said, "for the grave I didn't open."

"I'm executing a warrant." He said it evenly, but his eyes went past her shoulder, into the house, doing inventory. She watched him do it. Watching him watch. "The court will want your equipment, Wren. Recorders, drives, files. Everything related to the Hazel investigation."

"The Hazel *ceremony*, you mean. The pageant. The one where the coffin turned out to be full of rocks."

"Wren—"

"Somebody opened that coffin a hundred and fifty years ago, Dane. Was it you? Or did you inherit the job, like the ledger, like the badge, like—" She stopped herself. The deputies were listening. Every word was a stone dropped in water, and she couldn't see how far the ripples reached.

Mercer stepped closer, lowered his voice. Up close she could see what the rehearsed calm was costing him — the fine sweat at his hairline, the way his thumb worried the brim of his hat. "You've been poking something old," he said quietly. "Older than me, older than my father. People are scared, and scared people with money and history do things you don't want them to do. Give me the drives voluntarily. Stay at my sister's place in Cedar Falls for a few weeks. Let this cool off. That's me talking, not the badge."

"That's the ledger talking," she said. "I've read it, Sheriff. Your initials are in it. Your father's. Your grandmother's."

Something moved behind his face — not surprise. Grief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin, resignation. "Get your coat," he said.

They took her equipment in evidence boxes: the recorders, the laptop, the external drives from her desk. They did not find the basement archive, because the warrant listed rooms and Mercer, reading it aloud on her porch, had skipped over the basement — either an oversight or a mercy or a trap, and she no longer had the luxury of distinguishing. They did not find the copies in the storage unit in Cedar Falls, or the encrypted upload cycling through a server in Reykjavik, set to publish if she missed two consecutive check-ins.

They cuffed her in her own driveway, in front of Mrs. Aldous Pike's house across the street, where the curtains twitched and did not open. The younger deputy wouldn't look at her. The older one, a Voss — *Voss,* said the ledger card burning in her memory, *Voss, quarterly, 1911 to present* — handled her with exaggerated gentleness, murmuring, "This isn't right, what they're doing," so quietly she almost missed it, and she understood that even inside the machine, some part of the machine was ashamed.

* * *

The holding cell smelled of bleach and cold coffee. Wren sat on the bench with her back to the wall and counted the ways she'd been foolish, because counting was better than panicking.

Foolish: researching the heir from her home address. Foolisher: letting the verification take a morning. Foolishest: believing, somewhere beneath twenty years of cynicism, that Dane Mercer would come to her house with a warrant and mean only the warrant.

Because here was what she knew now, sitting in the cell while the fluorescent light buzzed its one flat note: the charge didn't matter. Tampering with a burial site was a misdemeanor dressed in felony language; no prosecutor in the county would spend real resources on it. The charge existed for one reason — probable cause, signed and sealed, giving them legal access to her life's work. They hadn't arrested Wren Dravenmoor. They'd arrested the archive, and she came along as packaging.

Which meant they were afraid of what she'd found. Which meant she was close. Which meant—

Which meant the list. Three names, written in her own handwriting, sitting on her kitchen table when they walked in. Dale Pruitt. Cormac Bell. Marisol Hale.

Had they seen it? Had Mercer's eyes done their inventory and landed on the index cards fanned across the floor? She'd left them there — she'd been mid-verification when the knock came, the charter still unrolled, the whole architecture of her deduction laid out like a place setting. If they'd photographed it, if they'd read it, then somewhere in Ashford Hollow, right now, someone was deciding what to do about a librarian.

Wren pressed her palms against her eyes. Think. The check-in protocol: she had until tomorrow night before the Reykjavik switch fired and released the fragment — one episode's worth of the ledger, redacted names included, enough to start fires but not enough to finish them. Theo could delay it if she reached him. Or she could let it burn. She hadn't decided which was rescue and which was ruin.

Footsteps in the corridor. She expected the deputy with the bologna sandwich they'd offered and she'd refused. Instead: Mercer, alone, keys in hand, stopping outside the bars. He looked older than he had on her porch. Older than he had at the creek, at the funeral, at every moment of her life where he'd stood between her and something.

"They're processing your equipment," he said. "It'll take time. Courts are slow."

"Dane."

"Don't." He glanced down the corridor, then closer to the bars. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into a register she'd never heard from him — stripped of office, stripped of rehearsal. "There's a name you haven't found yet. In the trust papers. There's a clause nobody's enforced since 1948, and if you find it before they do, it changes everything. Look for the word *reversion*."

"Why are you telling me this?"

He was quiet long enough that she heard the station around them — a radio crackling, a chair creaking, the ordinary machinery of a small-town afternoon continuing as if the world weren't tilting.

"Because my grandfather paid into that ledger for forty years," he finally said, "and on his deathbed he told me it was the worst thing he ever did, and he made me promise that if it ever came to a choice, I'd stand on the right side of it." His jaw tightened. "I've been waiting twenty years to find out if I keep promises. So have they. Get yourself a lawyer, Wren. One from outside the county."

He turned to go.

"Dane — the list on my table. Three names. Did they see it?"

He stopped. Didn't turn around. And the pause before he answered was longer than any answer he could have given.

"Arraignment's at nine tomorrow," he said, and walked away, and the corridor swallowed him, and Wren sat alone with the buzzing light and the arithmetic of fear: three names, one night, and a town full of people who had just learned exactly how much she knew — and how little time she'd need to know the rest.
