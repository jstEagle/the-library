## 1. The Night Desk

The dispatch office of Calloway County Emergency Services occupied the basement of the old municipal building, a fact that suited August Coldharbour fine. Above him, by day, the county conducted its business in fluorescent-lit rooms where men in good suits said things like *fiscal quarter* and *liability*, and where the missing persons of Calloway County were filed, stamped, and forgotten at a rate of roughly one per week. Below him, in the blue hours when the heating pipes knocked like a polite ghost, August did the work that nobody upstairs wanted done: he listened.

The night shift ran from ten to six. August had held it for eleven years, first as a junior operator, then, after the great budget purge of the recession years, as the only operator — which meant he was also, by default, the supervisor, the records clerk, and the entire after-hours institutional memory of a county of forty thousand souls. He answered the phones. He dispatched the ambulances, the sheriff's deputies, the volunteer fire crews out of Harlow Bend and Tates Mill. He logged every call in a ledger he kept himself, a green clothbound book with columns ruled in his own careful hand, because the county's computer system had been obsolete before he was hired and its replacement was always, perpetually, *two fiscal quarters away*.

He was forty-three years old and built like the furniture in a waiting room — serviceable, unremarkable, designed to bear weight without complaint. He had a face that people forgot while they were still looking at it, which he had learned was not a defect but a tool. People told waiting-room furniture things they would never tell a person.

At 11:47 on a Tuesday night in October, the switchboard lit up.

"Calloway dispatch, this is August."

The voice on the line was old, papery, and frightened in the particular way that old voices get frightened — not loudly, but with a tremor underneath, like water about to go over a dam. "Is this — they said to call this number. The night number. They said the day people don't—"

"They said right," August said. "Take your time. What's your name?"

"Doris Pell. I'm on Kestrel Road. Out past the church." A breath, ragged. "There's a man in my barn. I think he's — I think he's one of them missing ones. From the paper."

August's hand was already moving, pulling the green ledger toward him, flipping to the current week. "Tell me what you're seeing, Miss Doris. Slow as you like."

"He's in my hay. Been there since dark, I think. I went out for eggs this evening and heard him breathing in the loft and near shot him with the twelve-gauge, but he called out — he said his name was Marcus. Marcus Teale." Her voice cracked on the name. "That's the boy from the paper. The one been missing since June. His mama's in my Sunday school class."

August knew the name. Of course he knew it. Marcus Teale, nineteen, vanished June 3rd, last seen walking away from a gas station in Tates Mill with a duffel bag and a bad attitude, according to the deputy who'd taken the report and then taken an early retirement to Florida. The file was one of eleven in the drawer behind August's desk that he thought of, privately, as *the drawer of the unbothered* — cases the county had closed with the notation *no further leads* and which August, on nights when the phones stayed quiet, reopened and worried at like a dog with a bone.

"He say why he ran?" August asked, writing.

"He's hurt, I think. Or sick. He's crying, Mr. Coldharbour. Nineteen years old and crying in an old woman's hay like a baby." A pause, and then, quieter, the question August had learned to hear coming from a mile off, from every caller, every night: "Am I doing right? Calling you? The sheriff's office, they just—"

"They just closed it," August said. "I know. I'm sending an ambulance and a deputy both. You stay on the line with me till they're close. And Miss Doris — don't let him run off before they get there. Talk to him through the barn door if you have to. Tell him his mama's Sunday school class is praying for him. That usually holds a body still better than any lock."

It held. At 12:31 the ambulance from Harlow Bend rolled up Kestrel Road and Marcus Teale, dehydrated, hypothermic, and wanted in three counties for a probation violation he'd been running from out of pure terror, was carried out of Doris Pell's hayloft on a stretcher with his mother's name on his cracked lips. At 12:40, August called the number he'd spent two weeks of quiet nights finding — the cell number of Marcus Teale's mother — and woke her up to tell her that her son was alive, and listened to her make a sound he had no column in the ledger for.

The deputy who rode along, a young man named Pruitt with the county's whole institutional indifference folded into his jaw, looked at August in the barn's doorway and said, "How'd you know to call the mama?"

"I didn't know anything," August said. "I just don't like leaving things unfinished."

Pruitt grunted, the grunt of a man filing something away that he would never quite retrieve, and drove off with the ambulance's taillights bleeding red down Kestrel Road.

* * *

The drawer of the unbothered sat behind August's left shoulder, a two-drawer file cabinet the county had condemned in 2009 and which he had rescued from the dump with a new set of casters and a can of spray paint. Eleven folders, then ten, now — after tonight — nine. He slid the Teale folder out, wrote *LOCATED — Kestrel Rd, 10/14, 0031 hrs* across the top in his small, even script, and paused with the pen hovering.

Eleven years of nights had taught him the shape of the county's forgetting. It wasn't malice, mostly. It was arithmetic. A dispatcher's salary, a deputy's salary, a sheriff's political calendar — none of it had room for a nineteen-year-old who probably ran away on purpose, a fifty-year-old fisherman probably drowned, a church organist probably dead in a river that had already been dragged twice. The county did its sums and the sums came out *unbothered*, and the folders went into the drawer, and the mothers went on putting flyers in grocery store windows until the tape yellowed and someone took the flyers down.

August had never set out to be a solver of missing persons. It had started, as these things do, small — a name in a ledger that nagged at him, a detail in a call that didn't sit right, a night with no radio traffic where the alternative to thinking was thinking about other things. He had a method, if you could call it that: he read everything, twice; he wrote down what didn't match; and he called people at hours when their defenses were asleep. The phones were his instrument. At three in the morning, a person's voice told you what their daytime voice was built to hide. He had found a drowned man's truck by the way a widow said the word *lake*. He had found a runaway girl because her grandmother, half-asleep, had mentioned a bus schedule. The county had never once acknowledged any of it, which was fine. Being unseen was the whole architecture of the night desk. You were a voice, a light on a switchboard, a name people said wrong — *Mr. Coldwater, Mr. Cold Harbor* — and then forgot by morning.

He filed the Teale folder in the drawer marked *CLOSED — COLDHARBOUR*, which was not an official designation and existed nowhere but on the drawer itself, and he was reaching for the log sheet to close out the incident when the switchboard lit again.

"Calloway dispatch, this is August."

Silence. Not dead air — the particular silence of a line that's open, of someone who has dialed and is now standing on the other end of it, breathing.

He let it run five seconds. "You've reached Calloway dispatch. I'm here when you're ready."

Another five seconds. Then, so quietly he almost lost it under the hum of the pipes: "You found him."

The voice was a woman's, young or youngish, and it had a strange doubled quality, as if she were speaking through a cloth or a wall or the memory of her own voice. "Marcus Teale. You found him. Nobody was going to find him."

"Miss Doris found him," August said, in the tone he used for deflecting credit, which was also the tone he used for deflecting attention, which was also, if he was honest, the tone he used for deflecting nearly everything. "I just answered the phone. That's the job. Do you need help tonight? Is someone missing?"

The laugh that came down the line was short and wrong, a laugh with a bruise under it. "Someone's been missing for three years, Mr. Coldharbour. And everybody in this county did the sums and came out *unbothered*."

August's pen stopped.

He had never used that word on a phone. He had never used it anywhere but in his own head, in his own ledger margins, in the private vocabulary of the night. It was not a word that appeared in the newspaper coverage. It was not a word anyone had ever said to him.

"Who is this?" he asked.

"You'll get a package," the voice said. "Probably tomorrow. Maybe the day after. The mail here is slow." A breath. "When it comes, don't take it upstairs. Don't take it to Sheriff Dunmore, and don't take it to Deacon Crane, whatever he says, however kind he is. Especially not Deacon Crane."

"Deacon Crane of—"

"You'll know," she said. "You're the only one who'll know what to do with it. I read about the Teale boy's grandmother's bus schedule. That was you. Three years ago. Nobody ever figured that out but I did." The doubled voice frayed at the edges, and beneath it, for just a moment, August heard something younger and plainer and terrified. "My name is in the first entry. Read the first entry first. And Mr. Coldharbour — the dates. Pay attention to the dates."

The line went to dial tone.

August sat with the handset against his ear long after it was dead, listening to the dial tone the way a man listens to a room he suspects is not empty. Then he pulled the green ledger toward him and wrote, in the log, *1100 hrs — non-emergency call, no assistance required, caller declined to identify*, which was the official language, and beneath it, in the margin, in the small script he used for the things that didn't match: *She knows the word. She knows the bus schedule. She knows Crane.*

He knew the name she hadn't said. Everyone in Calloway County knew it. Hazel Voss, organist at St. Brannoc's Parish Church, thirty-one years old, walked out of choir practice on the fourth of November three years ago and was never seen again. Her car had been found at the boat ramp on Lake Calloway, locked, her purse inside, her shoes neatly placed on the passenger seat. The river and the lake had both been dragged. The county had done its sums — a lonely woman, a troubled history, a sad and tidy suicide — and come out unbothered, and the folder had gone into the drawer behind his left shoulder, and for three years it had sat there being the heaviest thing in the room.

He pulled it now. He'd read it before, more than once, on slow nights — he'd read all of them — but he read it again, every line, twice, the way he did, and it told him what it had always told him: almost nothing, arranged with a neatness that bothered him more than mess would have. Shoes on the seat. Purse in the car. A woman who'd played Sunday services for nine years and never once missed one, not even with the flu, choosing the week of the harvest supper to walk into a lake.

The pipes knocked. The switchboard stayed dark. Outside, past the high basement windows, the town of Calloway slept under its one traffic light, and up on the hill the white steeple of St. Brannoc's stood against the stars like a held breath.

At 5:52 a.m., eight minutes before the day shift arrived to relieve him, August closed the ledger, locked the drawer of the unbothered, and climbed the stairs into the gray October morning — and there, wedged upright against the dispatch office's outer door where no mail carrier had any business leaving it, was a package.

No stamps. No return address. His name on it in a hand that slanted left, pressed hard, the ink gouged deep enough to read with a fingertip: *A. COLDHARBOUR. NIGHT DESK ONLY.*

He stood in the cold with it in both hands and did not open it, because he already knew — the way you know the weight of a thing before you weigh it — what kind of book was inside.

The dates, she'd said. Pay attention to the dates.

He turned it over once, and through the brown paper he could feel the cover's tooled leather, and a title stamped in gold that time had half worn away, and beneath his thumb, faint as a scar, the word *DIARY*.
