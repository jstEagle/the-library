## 5. Entries in Someone Else's Hand

August Coldharbour did not sleep so much as he kept vigil over his own body, letting it lie flat on the narrow bed above the dispatch office while his mind continued working the switchboard of the night. By four in the morning he had given up the pretense entirely and sat at his kitchen table with the Voss diary open under a lamp whose shade he'd angled to throw a hard, raking light across the page.

Raking light was an old dispatcher's trick. Paper remembers pressure even when ink forgets. Run a beam sideways across a page and every groove of the pen rises up like a scar under skin. He had learned it from a missing-girl case in his second year on nights—a girl who'd left a note that read *gone to my aunt's, don't worry*—and the light had shown him the ghost of other words beneath, pressed through from a page torn out above: *he knows about the money*. The aunt had never existed. The man who knew about the money was sitting in her kitchen when August knocked.

He tilted the lamp now and looked at Hazel Voss's handwriting the way you look at a face you suspect of lying to you.

The early entries were what you'd expect from an organist's diary—small, upright, almost fussy script, the letters leaning together like hymnals on a shelf. *Practiced the Duruflé until my fingers ached. Deacon Crane says I rush the doxology. He is right; I always rush toward the good parts.* The ink was brown-black, iron-gall by the look of it, gone slightly rusty at the edges of each stroke. The paper had yellowed evenly. Three years of honest aging.

The entries dated after her disappearance were another animal altogether.

They were neater. That was the first thing. Too neat—the way a room looks too tidy after someone has searched it. The letters stood at attention, uniform in height, spaced with a draftsman's care, and they were written in a blue-black ballpoint that sat *on* the paper rather than sinking into it. Iron-gall pen for three years of a woman's life, then ballpoint on the night she vanished? People changed pens, certainly. People changed everything. But people did not change the *pressure* of their hand, the unconscious rhythm of their loops, the little hesitations where thought caught on feeling.

August fetched a sheet of tracing paper from the drawer where he kept carbon copies of old dispatch logs and laid it over two entries—one from March, before the disappearance, one from November, after. He traced the lowercase *g* from each. The earlier *g* had a closed, looping descender, a tail that curled back on itself like a question mark lying down. The later *g* was open, angular, its tail chopped off mid-curl, as if the writer had learned the letter from a manual rather than a life.

Two hands. One diary.

He sat back and let the lamp hum. Outside, the town did what it always did at this hour: nothing, beautifully. A delivery truck sighed past on the county road. Somewhere a dog made a single remark and thought better of it.

*She didn't write these,* he said aloud, to the kitchen, to no one. *Someone else is writing her.*

And then the worse thought, arriving the way bad thoughts do—not knocking but simply already present: *Someone else wants me to believe she wrote them.*

Because here was the thing he hadn't said to Nell, hadn't written in his ledger, hadn't allowed past the locked room of his own skull: the post-disappearance entries described *him*. Not by name—he was "the night man," "the dispatcher," once, unbearably, "the one who keeps the lights on"—but the details were his details. The third coffee ring on the desk shaped like a crescent moon. The way he read files standing up because sitting still felt like surrender. The green-shaded lamp he'd bought at the estate sale of a telephone company man dead twenty years. Entries describing a room only August and God had ever seen, written in a hand that wasn't Hazel Voss's, dated across the very months the diary had been traveling anonymously toward his desk.

It wasn't a message from a missing woman. It was a lure. And lures are tied by people who know exactly what fish they're fishing for.

*

At nine that morning—his morning being everyone else's midnight—August walked into the county records annex with the diary in a canvas mail bag and a story ready about estate appraisal, which nobody believed and nobody questioned, because August Coldharbour had spent eleven years cultivating the particular invisibility of a man who asks small favors and repays them instantly.

Dolores Pike ran the document examination desk the way lighthouse keepers run lights: alone, faithfully, and slightly resentful of ships. She'd been a forensic examiner for the state lab until her knees and her marriage gave out in the same year, and now she did freelance authentication for probate courts and suspicious nephews. She owed August for the time he'd stayed on the line forty minutes with her sister during a diabetic crisis, talking her calm until the ambulance found the farmhouse off the unmarked road.

"You want me to look at a diary," she said, not looking up from her magnifier.

"I want you to tell me how many people wrote it."

That got her eyes up. She wiped her hands on her cardigan, took the bag, and opened the diary with a cotton glove and the tenderness people reserve for things that might bite.

For an hour August sat in the corner chair among boxes of deceased persons' paperwork and watched Dolores work. She measured letter heights with a clear rule. She held pages to the window against daylight, then to the angled lamp, hunting pressure grooves. She sniffed the ink—actually sniffed it—and made a face at the ballpoint sections. Twice she laughed softly at something Hazel had written decades of Sundays ago, some wry complaint about the choir, and August felt the strange grief of hearing a stranger laugh at a missing woman's jokes.

Finally she pushed the magnifier away and rubbed her eyes.

"Three hands," she said.

August went very still. "I found two."

"Then you weren't looking at the last thirty pages." She turned to the back of the diary, to the final entries, and angled the lamp low and warm instead of raking. "Your first hand—call her Hand A—is your organist. Confident, personal, all the idiosyncrasies in place. Hand B starts the day after she disappears and runs for about a year. Careful, controlled, imitative but not perfect—they're copying her style, and copying is exhausting, so their own habits bleed through. The open-tailed g. A habit of crossing t's left to right instead of both directions."

"And the third?"

Dolores was quiet a moment. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into the register she probably used for telling probate judges bad news.

"Hand C only appears in the last month. And it's not imitation at all. It's shaky, uneven, the letters drift downhill across the line." She tapped the page. "This isn't someone pretending to be Hazel Voss. This is someone writing *as themselves*, badly, in pain or in fear—or writing with a hand that doesn't work right anymore. Tremor, injury, restraint. Take your pick of horrors." She looked up at him. "There's something else. Hand B and Hand C use the same ballpoint. Same batch, by the wear pattern on the casing marks. Whoever wrote those last pages and whoever imitated her for a year were holding the same pen."

"One person," August said. "Or two people who share a desk."

"Or a jailer and a prisoner sharing a cell." Dolores closed the diary gently, as if it might feel it. "August. What is this?"

"A file," he said. "An old one."

She looked at him the way old friends look at men carrying old files, and let it go.

But as he was leaving she called after him, and her voice had the careful flatness of someone handing over a live wire: "The last entry. The shaky one. It's dated six days ago."

Six days ago. While the diary had been sitting on his desk. While he'd been reading it by lamplight, believing himself its audience, its terminus. Someone, somewhere, had been writing the next page while he read the previous ones.

"Read it again," Dolores said. "The last one. All the way through. Slowly."

*

He read it in his truck in the annex parking lot, windows fogged, engine off, the heater ticking down toward cold.

*He lets me have the pen on Sundays. I am writing this small so he won't notice how much I have to say. If anyone finds this—I was wrong about who helped me disappear. The person I trusted to bury Hazel Voss kept Hazel Voss. Tell the night man the loft has a floor. Tell him the arithmetic doesn't add up and never did. Tell him—*

The sentence stopped. Not trailed off—stopped, mid-word, the pen dragged a half-inch down the page in a thin skid mark, as if the writer's hand had been taken from her, or the pen, or both.

August read it four times. On the fourth reading he noticed what exhaustion had hidden: the phrase *tell the night man*. Not *the dispatcher*. Not *the one who keeps the lights on*. Those were Hand B's coinages, the imitator's romantic vocabulary. This was plainer, more desperate, and it meant whoever wrote it knew the diary's destination—knew it would land on a night-shift desk, knew the man there would keep reading.

The diary hadn't been sent to him despite its contents being monitored. It had been sent to him *because* they were. Someone on the inside was using August Coldharbour as a dead-drop, a signal fire, the one pair of eyes outside the walls.

*The loft has a floor.* Organ lofts have floors; that was nothing. Unless it meant the floor was wrong. False. Hiding something. And *the arithmetic doesn't add up*—Deacon Crane's arithmetic, if the premise of the whole rotten parish held true. Money. Fraud. A trial docket he'd seen glimpsed in the newspaper: *State v. Crane, fraud and witness tampering, trial set for the 14th.*

Twelve days away.

August started the truck. His hands were steady, which surprised him, because underneath the steadiness something in his chest had begun to move like water finding a crack.

*

Nell Okafor was waiting outside his office at dusk, which was against their arrangement. Their arrangement was that Nell, a patrol officer with too much conscience for her shift sergeant's comfort, brought him copies of reports the department preferred forgotten, and in exchange he solved what the department preferred unsolved, and neither of them ever appeared together anywhere a camera might remember. But here she was, leaning against his door in civilian clothes, arms crossed, jaw set.

"You didn't call me back," she said.

"I was working."

"You were working *her* file without me." She pushed past him into the office, into the smell of cold coffee and ozone from the switchboard, and put both palms flat on his desk. "August. I pulled the original missing-persons report last night. Hazel Voss, reported by her sister, three years ago. You know what's attached to it?"

"A closure memo."

"A closure memo signed by *Detective R. Mabry*—" she said the name like spitting out a seed, "—who retired to Florida two weeks after signing it, and whose signature, I happen to know from the Farrow case, was being applied to documents by other people in that building during his last year. Somebody closed Hazel Voss's file fast and dirty, August. And somebody in records flagged it restricted within a week. Restricted missing-persons files are for cops' messy divorces and councilmen's nieces. Why does an organist rate restricted?"

August lowered himself into his chair. Through the window behind Nell, the parish's spire stood black against the last orange of the sky, and beneath it the deacon's white clapboard house with its porch light burning, always burning, a beacon of respectability.

"Because she isn't missing," he said. "She's kept."

He told her. About the three hands. About the same pen. About the last entry and its unfinished sentence, and the words *the loft has a floor*. Nell listened the way she did everything—completely, silently, her stillness a form of fury—and when he finished she was quiet long enough that the switchboard clicked twice with calls he let ring out.

"The organ loft," she finally said. "St. Brigid's. That's public ground. We could walk in tomorrow and pull up whatever boards you want."

"And if the floor is a message and not a hiding place?" August asked. "If pulling boards tells Crane we've read the diary? Whoever's writing those entries is alive because no one knows they're writing them. Six days ago the pen got taken away mid-sentence, Nell. We go stomping around that loft, and the next entry might be a period."

"So we do nothing?"

"We do what dispatchers do." He pulled his ledger toward him—the fat green notebook, eleven years of cases, cross-references, timelines. "We listen. Every voice that comes through that parish between now and the trial gets logged. Crane's visitors. The sexton's truck. Who rings the rectory bell after hours. Somebody in that church is Hazel's hand C, and somebody else is hand B, and hands leave prints eventually." He uncapped his pen. "We just have to be the wall they talk to."

Nell stared at him. "You want to stake out a church."

"I want to listen to a parish," August said. "There's a difference. Churches are full of people confessing. They just think they're only talking to God."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't, because her eye had caught the diary where it lay beside the ledger, and she reached for it, and August—without deciding to, faster than deciding—closed his hand over the cover.

"Not yet," he said.

"It's evidence."

"It's a lifeline. Evidence gets logged, logged gets flagged, flagged gets read by whoever restricted that file in the first place." He met her eyes. "Right now the only copy of the truth is in a book that fits in my coat pocket, and I intend to keep it that way until I know which of us it's safe with."

Something moved across Nell's face—hurt, maybe, or the recognition of it—and she nodded slowly and took her hand off the diary.

"Fine. Then log this instead." At the door she paused, backlit by the hallway's failing fluorescent. "I checked the trial docket today. State v. Crane. You know who's listed as the state's material witness—unnamed, sealed, protected?"

August felt the room tilt a degree, the way it did when a caller's voice suddenly matched a face.

"No," he said carefully.

"Neither does the docket. The witness designation was filed by the district attorney's office three years ago, the same week Hazel Voss disappeared. Sealed by judicial order." Nell's voice dropped. "August—the state thinks their star witness is dead. Or the state *is* the reason she's supposed to be. Either way, somebody in the prosecutor's building signed off on a dead woman testifying at a trial twelve days from now."

She left. August sat alone with the switchboard's small green lights, the diary under his palm like a heartbeat, and understood that the parish was only the outermost ring of the thing.

Somewhere in the dark, a phone rang. He let it ring twice, three times, watching it, listening to it, the way you listen to a floor.

Then he picked up.
