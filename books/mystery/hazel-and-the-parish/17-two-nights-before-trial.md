## 17. Two Nights Before Trial

The rain came in off the harbor around nine that evening, a fine gray needle-rain that made the parish's cobblestones shine like the scales of some enormous sleeping animal. August Coldharbour stood at the window of his dispatch office with the lights off and the radio turned low, watching water bead and run down the glass, and he thought: *two nights.*

Two nights until the deacon's fraud trial opened in the county courthouse—Judge Aldous Merrick presiding, eleven counts of misappropriated parish funds, a prosecution that had taken three years to assemble and would take perhaps three weeks to spend. Two nights until Deacon Elias Crane walked up those courthouse steps with his good coat and his better smile and his hands folded like a man arriving early for his own funeral because he wanted to be sure of the seating.

And somewhere between now and then, August had to move Hazel Voss out of the convent on Wren Lane without anyone seeing, or see her delivered to the district attorney's safehouse by morning, or—and this was the thought that kept circling back like a moth against the glass—do neither, and help her vanish so completely that even the diary would have nothing left to say about it.

Behind him, the switchboard sat dark except for its pilot lamp, a small amber eye that never blinked. Forty-one years that board had been lit. His father had worked it before him, and his father's father before that, back when the town was called something else and the parish was just a clearing with a bell. August knew every jack and cord on it the way other men knew their own knuckles. He knew which lines hummed when the weather turned. He knew that line six—the rectory line—had been quiet for three days running, which was itself a kind of noise, a silence shaped exactly like a man waiting.

He had not slept properly since the convent grounds. Since Hazel.

* * *

She had looked smaller than her photograph, but photographs lie in both directions, and what she'd lost in flesh she'd gained in something else—a stillness, an economy of motion, the way she'd held her teacup with both hands as though the cup might be evidence too. They had talked for four hours in the convent's cold parlor while Sister Anselm kept watch at the door and the rain worked at the windows, and by the end of it August understood three things clearly.

First: Hazel had not faked her death alone. The witness-protection arrangement had been real once—real marshals, real paperwork, a real plan to produce her at trial and bury Crane under her testimony. Then the file had gone sideways. Someone inside the arrangement had sold it, or bent it, or simply lost their nerve, and instead of a new life in another state Hazel had gotten three years in a room above the convent laundry, fed and clothed and forbidden to leave, a witness being *preserved* rather than protected, kept fresh like meat in a cellar until someone decided the trial was worth the risk of producing her.

Second: the person who decided that was not Crane. It was worse than Crane. Crane was arithmetic—he stole, he smiled, he calculated odds. But whoever had kept Hazel all this time had done it out of something softer and far more durable than greed. Reverence, maybe. Or love. Or the particular piety of people who believe the parish is a living thing and that any wound to it, however deserved, must be cauterized before it spreads.

Third: Hazel was done waiting. "I've been patient," she'd said, in a voice like a struck bell fading, "and patience is how they kept me. I want to walk into that courtroom on my own feet, Mr. Coldharbour, or I want to walk out of this town on them. I don't much care which anymore. But I'm finished being carried."

August had promised her nothing. That was the discipline of the night desk—you learned early that promises were just lies with a schedule attached. But walking back down Wren Lane through the rain, he had felt the old thing stir in him, the thing that made him open files no one else would touch. Not hope exactly. Something more stubborn than hope.

* * *

At 11:40 the switchboard woke.

Not line six. Line two—the convent's ancient party line, the one Sister Anselm had sworn was disconnected years ago. August slid the jack home with two fingers and said nothing, the way he always said nothing first, letting the caller fill the silence.

For a long moment there was only breathing, and beneath the breathing, faintly, organ music—recorded, he thought, or played from a room away, something in a minor key that rose and fell like tide.

Then a voice. A woman's voice, low and unhurried, with the smoothed-over vowels of someone who had trained herself out of the parish accent and only half succeeded.

"You're the dispatcher." Not a question. "The one who found her."

August's hand was steady on the desk. His pulse was not. "This is the night desk. Who's calling?"

"You know who I am." A pause; the organ music swelled and subsided, as if someone had adjusted a volume knob. "You've been reading her diary. You've been to the loft. You went out to Wren Lane last Tuesday in a cab you paid cash for, and you stayed four hours and seven minutes, and you left through the garden gate because the front door has a bell that rings in two places."

The rain ticked against the window. August counted the seconds, an old habit, letting the numbers steady him. Whoever this was, she had eyes—or ears—in more places than he'd mapped. "If you know all that," he said, "then you know I'm not the one who should be worried."

"Oh, I'm not worried about you, Mr. Coldharbour. I'm worried *for* you. There's a difference." The voice softened, and that softness was somehow the most frightening thing about it. "Elias goes to trial in two days. Everything we did—all of it, the marshals, the money, the girl—we did to protect the parish from what happens when a body like his is opened up in public. You understand that, don't you? It was never cruelty. It was triage."

"Three years in a laundry room," August said. "That's your word for mercy?"

"Three years *alive*," the voice said, and for the first time something moved underneath it, something cracked and human. "Do you know what the alternative was? Do you know what was arranged for her the week after she 'died'? I sat in a car outside a boatyard and I made a phone call and I unmade it. I have been unmaking it every day since. So don't stand there in your little dark room with your little lit board and tell me about cruelty."

August closed his eyes. In the dark behind them he saw the ledger in his lap, the columns of calls he'd traced over the past weeks—the pattern that had led him here, the quiet constellation of numbers that all orbited, eventually, the same small set of houses. "You're the one who sent me the diary," he said.

Silence. Longer this time. When the voice came back it had dropped almost to a whisper, and the whisper was aimed, he realized, not at him but past him—at whoever else might be listening on the line, if anyone was.

"I sent it because it was going to be burned. She wrote it in the margins of hymnals and on the backs of collection envelopes and I collected it page by page for three years like a woman saving string, and when the trial got scheduled I understood it was time for it to find its way out. To someone with no parish, no family, no stake. Someone who works nights and sees everything and belongs to nothing." A breath. "You were the obvious choice, Mr. Coldharbour. I'm sorry for that."

"Don't be sorry yet," August said. "Tell me where she's supposed to go. Tell me who's coming for her."

But the line had already begun its dying fall—that thinning of signal he knew from forty-one years of dead air—and the last thing he heard, barely, threaded through the organ music, was five words:

"*Thursday night. The van comes.*"

Then the dial tone, flat and endless as a frozen lake.

* * *

He did not sleep. He pulled the ledger across the desk and worked by lamplight, and the work confirmed what the voice had let slip. Thursday night. The trial convened Thursday morning; therefore the van would come Wednesday night—tomorrow night—to move Hazel before the courthouse filled with cameras. Move her where? Out of the county, certainly. Permanently, probably. The parish's solution to Hazel Voss had always been the same solution applied again and again: keep her, keep her, keep her—and when keeping became impossible, lose her.

Unless he got there first.

August spread the map of the convent grounds on his desk—the one he'd drawn from memory after his visit, the gate, the wall, the laundry wing with its high north-facing windows, the chapel whose bell rope hung within reach of the yard. He marked the sightlines. He marked the road. And then he sat very still and listened to the building around him, the pipes ticking, the wind worrying the fire escape, the city murmuring its insomniac murmur two floors below, and he asked himself the question he had been refusing to ask since Wren Lane.

*Which do you serve?*

The law wanted Hazel produced, sworn, examined, spent. The law had failed her once already—had let her file rot in some compromised drawer for three years while she folded other women's sheets. And the parish—God, the parish. The parish wanted her erased, gently, reverently, with organ music. Between those two appetites stood a woman who only wanted to choose her own door.

August Coldharbour had spent his whole life behind a switchboard connecting other people's voices, invisible, indispensable, unseen. He knew what it was to be kept. He knew what it was to be a piece of someone else's arrangement.

At 3:15 in the morning, he picked up a pencil and wrote two lines in the margin of the map, in his father's shorthand, the private script of night men:

*Van comes Wed. We leave Tue.*

And beneath it, after a long moment, a third line he crossed out and rewrote twice before leaving it plain:

*I will be seen.*

* * *

Wednesday dawned hard and clear, the rain scrubbed away, the light off the harbor so clean it looked manufactured. A bad day for moving unseen. August spent it doing ordinary things with extraordinary attention. He bought a secondhand panel truck from a lot on Delancey Street, paying cash, telling the dealer he was hauling church furniture—which had the advantage of being true. He drove it out to the convent in the afternoon under a tarpaulin of legitimate errands, and Sister Anselm met him in the garden with her sleeves rolled and her face like a closed book, and said only: "She's packed. She's been packed for a year."

Hazel came out through the laundry door at dusk wearing a coat that had belonged to some sister decades dead, and she stopped when she saw the truck, and she laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering oil. "A furniture van," she said. "Of course. This whole town has been moving me like furniture for three years. At least this time I get to pick the room."

"Not yet," August said. "One more night in place. Tomorrow night we go—but tonight everything has to look exactly as it's looked for three years. Lights on, radio playing, your shadow crossing that window at ten past eight like always. If they check tonight, they find nothing changed."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow they'll come for you at midnight and find a cold cup of tea and a note addressed to the district attorney." He held her gaze. "You'll testify, Hazel. On your feet, in the open, where every camera in the county can see you. That's the only way this ends—not in a van, not in a cellar, not in a grave with your name spelled wrong. In daylight."

Something moved through her face then—fear, yes, but under the fear a kind of terrible relief, the look of a swimmer who has finally been given permission to stop treading water. "And if your daylight doesn't hold?" she asked quietly. "If they reach me first?"

August looked past her, at the convent wall, at the road beyond it where the evening was settling in blue and gold and utterly innocent. Somewhere out there, a van was being fueled. Somewhere a phone was ringing in a house with good curtains, and a voice was saying *tomorrow night*, and plans older and quieter than his were tightening like a noose drawn by loving hands.

"Then they'll have to go through the dispatcher," he said.

He left her at the gate as the bells began for evening service, and he did not look back, because looking back is how watchers learn they've been seen. But halfway down Wren Lane, passing the shuttered front of Voss & Daughters Piano Restorations—dark these three years, its windows papered with auction notices—he stopped.

The paper over the nearest window had been peeled back at one corner. Freshly. And pinned behind the glass, in the dark of the empty shop, a single candle was burning, and beside it, propped where the streetlight could catch it, lay a page torn from a hymnal.

He crossed the street. The handwriting on the page was small and level and unmistakable, the same hand that had written entries dated after a death:

*They know about Tuesday. Change the day. — H*

August stood in the middle of the empty street with the bells tolling overhead and the candle guttering behind the glass, and understood that the clock he'd been racing had just been reset—and that somewhere behind him, unhurried footsteps had begun to follow.
