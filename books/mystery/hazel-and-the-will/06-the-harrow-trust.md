## 6. The Harrow Trust

The Harrow Trust occupied the ground floor of the old Merchant's Bank building on Calloway Street, a space that had been a bank when the town had money worth banking and was now a foundation, which was the same thing with better lighting. Hazel had walked past it a thousand times. She had attended two fundraisers in its marble lobby, back when she still attended things, and had never once thought to ask who funded the restoration of the courthouse clock or the new wing at the elementary school or the annual winter coat drive that kept half the town's children from looking poor in December.

She knew now. She'd spent the previous evening with the trust's filings, which were public, because trusts that want to be admired keep their paperwork where the light can reach them. The Harrow Trust was a charitable remainder trust, established eleven years ago, funded initially by the liquidation of a logging partnership — Wren & Harrow Timber — and administered, per the documents, by Aldous Harrow, sole trustee, sole signatory, sole beneficiary of record upon dissolution.

Tobias Wren had been a name on a partnership. Aldous Harrow was the name on everything else.

Hazel dressed for the interview the way she dressed for difficult families: plainly, without armor, nothing that could be read as either sympathy or accusation. A coroner learns early that you get more from the grieving when you look like a tired woman with a clipboard than when you look like the law. She took the file on Tobias Wren's death — her father's file, the one with the missing page — and she took her own questions, written out that morning in the kitchen in a hand steadier than she felt.

She was not investigating Aldous Harrow. That was what she told herself on the drive in. She was conducting a routine follow-up prompted by a letter of unknown provenance, and a coroner who receives a letter claiming a death was no accident is obligated — obligated — to re-interview the principals. If the principal happened to be a councilman and the town's most visible philanthropist, that was simply the shape of the town.

The shape of the town. She'd been using that phrase her whole life to mean the things nobody rearranges.

* * *

Aldous Harrow received her in a glass-walled office at the back of the trust, a room arranged so that anyone sitting across from him faced the street — faced, Hazel noted, the courthouse. He was seventy, or close to it, tall in the way that men who were tall at twenty remain tall at seventy, silver-haired, with the particular tan of a man who golfs in all weather because the town expects him to be outdoors being seen. He wore a gray sweater over a collared shirt. He looked like a man who had been carved by consensus.

"Miss Crane." He rose and shook her hand with both of his, which was either warmth or control, and Hazel had spent enough years around the dying and the dead to know that it was usually both. "I wondered when someone would come. Sit, please. Coffee? Marla makes it too strong but it's honest."

"No, thank you."

"Then I'll take mine, if you don't mind." He poured from a carafe on the sideboard without asking anyone, which told her the Marla in question had not existed in some time, if she had ever existed at all. "You have your father's way about you. He used to sit exactly like that — spine straight, hands quiet, like a woman waiting for a storm to be over."

"You knew my father well?"

"Everyone knew Ed Crane well. He held this town's sorrows for forty years." Harrow settled back into his chair. "I was sorry when he passed. This town has lost its institutions one by one. First the mill, then the bank, then men like Ed." He said it without self-pity, which was the trick of it. He was the last institution standing, and he wanted you to feel the draft where the others had been.

Hazel opened the file. "I'm here about Tobias Wren."

Something moved behind Harrow's face. Not a flinch. A settling, like a house adjusting to a change in temperature.

"Three years," he said. "Three years, and now there's a letter going around, I hear. Saying his death wasn't an accident." He shook his head slowly. "Tobias. That man could start a rumor by falling down stairs."

"You've heard about the letter."

"Emmett Doyle mentioned it. Funeral home gossip travels faster than the fire department." He sipped his coffee. "Ask me what you came to ask, Miss Crane. I'm an old man with a full calendar and a clear conscience, and both of those things make me patient."

So she asked. She asked him about the partnership, and he told the story the way a man tells a story he has told at dinners for a decade: two young loggers, one with capital and one with nerve, building a company out of second-growth pine and stubbornness. Tobias had the nerve. Aldous had the capital — family money, he admitted it freely, "money I've spent my life trying to deserve." They ran Wren & Harrow for nineteen years. Then the mill economics turned, and Tobias wanted to hold the timberland, and Aldous wanted to convert, and they dissolved the partnership "as friends, if not as partners."

"When was that?"

"Eleven years ago. The land went into the trust. Conservation easements, sustainable harvest — Tobias signed off on the whole structure. His signature is on every page. He was proud of it. He said it was the first thing either of us had built that would outlive us." Harrow's eyes went to the window, to the courthouse across the street. "It did. Just not the way he meant."

"You remained close?"

"We had lunch every other Thursday for thirty years. Then we had lunch every other Thursday for nothing, because he stopped coming." A pause, measured to the half-second. "About a month before he died. I called. He didn't call back. I told myself he was in one of his moods. Tobias had moods the way the river had floods — you waited them out." His voice caught on the word *floods*, and Hazel watched the catch happen and could not decide whether it was grief or craft. That was the horror of the man, she was beginning to understand: every human signal he emitted was also a plausible performance of it, and no instrument she owned could tell the difference.

"Where were you the night he died?"

"Home. Alone. Asleep by ten, as I have been every night since my wife died." He said it without resentment. "I know how that sounds. The whole town knows how that sounds. I've given this answer to your father, to two deputies, and now to you, and it has never once changed, because it has never once been anything but true."

"Did anyone see you?"

"Does the mail count? I bring my own mailbox up the drive in the morning. The carrier waves." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and Hazel hated how much she wanted to believe it. "No, Miss Crane. Nobody saw me. A man my age, asleep in his own house — the most invisible crime there is."

"It's not a crime."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It's an alibi. Or the absence of one. I've had three years to make my peace with the absence."

* * *

She shifted ground. She asked him about the funeral.

"I paid for it," he said, simply. "Tobias had no one. Vivian had been gone twenty years — I won't pretend I understand what happened between brother and sister, and I won't repeat what Tobias said about it, because the dead deserve better than my memory of their anger. Someone had to bury him. I was the someone. I'd known him since we were nineteen years old, cutting pulp on the Miller place." He looked at his hands, which were large and unblemished and folded neatly in his lap. "You spend nineteen years in business with a man, you bury him. That's not generosity. That's arithmetic."

"Did you handle any of his affairs after the funeral? His estate?"

"I paid the funeral bill. That's all I touched." He met her eyes. "The estate went through probate. I have no interest in Tobias's estate, Miss Crane — I never did. He had a house on Darrow Road worth less than the taxes on it, a truck with two hundred thousand miles, and a checking account. The trust doesn't need his leavings. I built the trust out of money I already had." He spread his hands. "You can check all of it. I'd be insulted if you didn't."

*I have checked all of it*, she did not say. *I have checked the deed transfers and the probate stamp and the signature that Vivian Wren says she never wrote, and the chain runs through the county clerk's office and ends, every single time, at a trust with your name on the door.*

What she said was: "You were his beneficiary of record. On the timberland."

"The land was never his, not after the dissolution. He sold his half into the trust. For value. There are appraisals." He recited the numbers without looking at anything, from memory, eleven years cold: acreage, per-acre valuation, total consideration. "Tobias got paid in full. He banked it, and by all accounts he spent it slowly and lived small, which was Tobias all over. The man had money and dressed like a hermit." A small, fond laugh. "People see the trust's name on things and assume I took something. I've stopped being hurt by it. Mostly."

Hazel wrote nothing. She had learned that writing nothing made people fill the silence, and Harrow filled it exactly as she'd predicted a man like him would — generously, with context.

"You have to understand what Tobias was, in the end," he said. "He was a lonely man with a grievance. Toward the end of the partnership he'd become convinced — I say *convinced* because there was never a shred of paper behind it — that the timberland was worth more than the appraisal, that I'd lowballed him, that the whole trust was some scheme to take what was his. It wasn't true. But he believed it, and a man who believes a thing like that doesn't stay for lunch." He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn. "If he wrote angry letters, if he told someone the death wasn't an accident, if he — I don't know, kept a file, made a will with his sister's name on it out of spite — you'll find it, and it'll be the grievance talking. Not the facts. The facts were always on my side of the ledger."

Hazel felt the sentence go past her like a truck at night. *Made a will with his sister's name on it.* She had not mentioned a will. She had not mentioned Vivian. She had said the words *estate* and *probate* and nothing more.

She kept her face the way her father had taught her to keep it at death scenes: interested, unhurried, empty.

"Did Tobias make a will?" she asked, mild as milk.

"I have no idea. I'd assume so. Men like Tobias always had a will — something to leave to somebody, even if the somebody hated them." Harrow smiled again, the consensus smile. "Why? Has one turned up?"

"Not yet."

"Well. If it does, I hope it's kinder to Vivian than Tobias was in life." He stood, signaling the interview's end with the ease of a man who ended interviews for a living. "Miss Crane, I'll tell you what I told your father, three years ago, in that same office of yours. Whatever Tobias's death was, it wasn't mine to answer for. I loved him the way you love a brother you can't live with — which is to say, entirely, and at a distance. If someone killed him, I hope you find them, and I hope they burn. And if the river took him, then the river took him, and no letter three years late changes what the water did."

He walked her to the door himself. In the marble lobby, beneath the restored clock the trust had paid for, he paused with his hand on the glass.

"One more thing," he said, "since you were kind enough not to ask. I heard Vivian's name mentioned around town last week. If she comes back — and she won't, she never comes back — be gentle with her. Whatever Tobias left her, she earned worse." He opened the door. "Good afternoon, Miss Crane. Do give my regards to the records office. I know how thorough you Cranes are."

* * *

Hazel sat in her car in the trust's lot for eleven minutes and did not start the engine.

He had mentioned the will. He had mentioned Vivian. He had mentioned the records office. Three things she had not said, wrapped in three layers of plausible deniability — funeral gossip, town talk, a joke. Any one of them, alone, was nothing. Together they were a man showing her the edges of what he knew, the way a card player shows you the corner of an ace to convince you he's bluffing.

Or they were a man who had genuinely heard everything through funeral home gossip, because in a town this size the gossip *was* the records office, and she was reading menace into the ordinary static of small-town knowledge.

That was the horror of it, she thought. Not that Aldous Harrow might be guilty. That he was the town — the clock, the coats, the morgue's funding line, the funeral of a man with no one. You could not investigate Aldous Harrow without investigating the scaffolding that held up every decent thing in Calloway County, and the scaffolding was made of people who would swear, honestly, that he was the best man they knew. He was guilty or he was innocent, and either way the town would take his side, because the town *was*, in every way that mattered, his side.

Her phone buzzed. A number she didn't know, local prefix.

She answered. A woman's voice, low, pitched to not be overheard, with the flat careful diction of someone who had rehearsed the sentence on the drive over.

"Miss Crane? This is Deputy Vega. Marisol Vega. I pulled Tobias Wren out of the river three years ago." A breath. "There's something I logged wrong. I need to show you before I lose my nerve."

Hazel looked up at the glass office across the lot, where Aldous Harrow stood silhouetted behind the window, watching her with what was, from this distance, indistinguishable from patience.

"I'm listening," she said.
