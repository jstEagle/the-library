## 16. Threats in Alphabetical Order

The door was unlocked when she arrived at 6:40 in the morning, and that was the first wrong thing.

Hazel Crane had locked it herself the night before — she remembered the specific resistance of the deadbolt, the way the old frame had to be nudged left before the bolt would seat. She'd stood on the courthouse steps for a full ten seconds with her hand on the rail, watching the streetlights come on along Delmar Avenue, because Ruth Okafor had told her that week: *You're past the point where you can be careless, Hazel. Careless people become exhibits.*

She had not been careless. The door had been locked.

Now it swung open under her palm without complaint, and the smell hit her first — not violence, nothing so dramatic. Just paper and dust disturbed, the sour note of a room that had been breathed in by someone who didn't belong. The overhead fluorescents were off but the desk lamp was on, angled toward the wall, throwing a long shadow of the chair across the file cabinets like a sundial pointing at six o'clock. Or midnight. She couldn't decide which.

Hazel stood in the doorway for a moment and let the coroner's habit take over: don't touch anything, catalog everything, narrate silently as though a jury were standing behind you.

*Scene secure. No signs of forced entry. Lighting altered. Subject entered with a key or a skill.*

Her office was small — twelve by fourteen, one window overlooking the parking lot, walls lined with the gray steel cabinets that held three decades of the county's dead. It had never been an impressive room. It was less impressive now. Every drawer in the lateral cabinet beside her desk hung open like the mouths of drowned men. Folders lay fanned across the carpet in a pattern that looked random until you noticed it wasn't: they'd been gone through methodically, top to bottom, and replaced loosely, the way someone re-shelves books they don't want a librarian to notice were borrowed.

Whoever had done this hadn't been looking for everything. They'd been looking for something.

Hazel set down her coffee — she'd bought it at the Gas-N-Go out of ritual, and now the cup trembled slightly against its lid — and walked the perimeter without touching. Her blotter was askew. The pen cup had been moved two inches. The framed photo of her father in his coroner's blazer, squinting into sun outside the old morgue, had been turned face-down.

That was a message too. Everything here was a message. That was what frightened her most — not the ransacking itself, which was almost gentle, almost professional, but the *grammar* of it. This wasn't panic. Panic knocks things over. This was editing.

She crouched at the bottom drawer of her desk, the one with the false back her father had installed years ago for — she'd assumed — whiskey during budget season. The photocopy of the forged transfer document, Vivian Wren's copy, the one with the signature Tobias's sister swore she never wrote — gone. The folder labeled *WREN, T.* in her own handwriting — gone. Even the legal pad where she'd been sketching the chain of custody, the clerk's impossible stamp dated two days before the body was found — gone, torn cleanly from the pad rather than taken whole, so the pad would look untouched.

They knew exactly what they wanted. Which meant they knew exactly what she had.

Which meant someone had told them.

Hazel sat down slowly in her chair, which had been swiveled fifteen degrees off true, and stared at the face-down photograph of her father. In the photo he was fifty-one, two years before his heart quit in the parking lot of the VFW, and he was laughing at something off-camera. She turned it upright. His eyes came back to her, cheerful and useless.

"You could have just told me," she said to him. "It would have saved everyone a great deal of trouble."

The room did not answer. Rooms never did. That was the whole tragedy of her profession.

* * *

By seven-fifteen she had called exactly one person, and it wasn't the sheriff.

"Describe the lock," Ruth Okafor said, before Hazel finished her second sentence. There were sounds behind her voice — a kettle, maybe, or the white noise machine she used to cover phone calls she didn't want recorded.

"Unmarked. No scratches around the cylinder."

"So either they had a key or they raked it clean enough that you can't tell. Either way, that's not a kid. That's not even a hired thug. A hired thug kicks the door and takes everything and feels bad about it later." A pause; the kettle clicked off. "What did they take?"

"The photocopy. My notes. The custody timeline."

"What didn't they take?"

Hazel looked at the cabinets, the fanned folders, the careful disorder. "Everything else. It's strange. It looks like a search that wants to be found."

"It doesn't want to be found. It wants to be *understood*. There's a difference." Ruth's voice went flat and quick, the register she used when she was thinking in case law. "A ransacked office says 'we're sloppy criminals.' Your office says 'we know what you have, we've taken the copies you'd need for court, and we want you to lie awake wondering how we knew.' It's litigation by interior decorating. Did they leave anything?"

Hazel had been avoiding the desk. Now she made herself look at it properly. Under the lamp, squared precisely to the edge of the blotter, was a single sheet of paper. Cream stock. Heavy. She recognized the weight of it before she recognized anything else about it, because she'd held sheets exactly like it in Emmett Doyle's back office, sheets from the funeral home's stationery closet, the same paper the confession letters had been typed on.

"No," she said. "Wait. Yes."

"Read it."

She picked it up by the corner, the way she'd pick up evidence, though she already knew better than to hope for prints. Typed, same typewriter font as the letters — the same uneven *e*, the same struck-through look to the *t*. Five words.

**Some files stay closed.**

"That's it," Hazel said. "'Some files stay closed.'"

Silence on the line for three seconds. Then Ruth laughed — one dry syllable, no humor in it at all.

"They're quoting your own case back at you. That's stationery from Doyle's funeral home, isn't it. Same stock as the letters."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've been staring at both letters under a magnifying glass for a week, and because whoever wrote those letters wanted us to trace them to that stationery eventually. Hazel. Listen to me carefully." The white noise behind her cut out; she'd muted it, which meant she was about to say something she meant. "This break-in is not Harrow being clever. This is too neat, too literate, too *theatrical*. Aldous Harrow buries things in trusts and shell filings and friendly judges. He does not leave poetry on desks. Whoever did this wants you scared in a specific direction — toward him, or away from someone else, I can't tell yet. But the person who typed that note has access to a funeral home and a grudge with excellent punctuation."

"Emmett said the envelope was sealed. He said Tobias left instructions—"

"And Emmett also said he mailed two letters and nothing else. People say a lot of things when their livelihood is standing in the room with them." A pause. "Where's the will?"

Hazel closed her eyes. The locker key. The brass key to bus-station locker 44 in Ashford, holding Tobias Wren's handwritten will naming his sister sole heir and accusing Aldous Harrow of the assault — the original, the thing that mattered more than any copy, because copies could be called forgeries and originals could only be authenticated or destroyed.

Last night, after Ruth's warning, after lying awake until two doing arithmetic with days remaining on the statute, Hazel had gotten up and driven to the cemetery in the dark like a woman in a fever dream. She'd knelt at the grave registry — the heavy bound volume kept in the stone alcove by the cemetery gate, listing every plot, every name, every date back to 1911 — and she had opened it to the W family, to Tobias Wren, plot 217, and slid the key into the gutter of the binding where the spine met the pages, wrapped in a strip of archival tissue. Then she'd signed the visitor log with a fake name, because even her paranoia had a sense of humor, and driven home.

Nobody hides anything in a graveyard, she'd reasoned. Nobody reads grave registries. The dead keep better secrets than the living.

"The will is safe," she said. "It's somewhere they'll never think to look."

"Good. Keep it there. And Hazel — do not go to the sheriff. Not yet. Marisol's log is our hammer; we don't swing it until the hearing. If you report this break-in, it goes into a system Harrow's people can read, and worse, it tips them that the key survived. Right now they think they got everything. Let them think it."

"They turned my father's photograph face-down, Ruth."

"I know," Ruth said, quieter. "I know what that means. It means they know about him too. Which means the list of people who knew about your father is shorter than you think, and somebody on it is talking." The kettle sound resumed; she'd unmuted it, conversation over. "Thirty days minus four, counselor. Get the will to my office by Friday. And buy a deadbolt with a camera. You're not paranoid if the memo is real."

The line went dead. Hazel sat in her edited office, holding a threat on funeral-home stationery, and did the thing she always did when the living became unbearable: she thought about the dead.

Tobias Wren, forty-six, drowned, ruled accidental by Edward Crane, coroner, contusion noted and struck through in his own careful hand. Three years quiet. Three years while a trust ate his estate and his sister slept in motels two counties over, afraid of her own hometown. And then, right on schedule, letters began arriving — timed like a man setting a watch — postmarked the day after the funeral's anniversary, then the day of the funeral itself, each one pulling Hazel one step deeper, each one knowing exactly which drawer she kept her conscience in.

*Who mails a confession?* Ruth had asked, and answered it herself: someone directing traffic.

But now, sitting in a room that had been searched by hands that knew her filing system, Hazel asked a harder version: *who benefits from the confession arriving late?* Because that was the thing she kept circling. The instructions had been sealed with Emmett Doyle — *open it if the town stays quiet too long.* Three years was a long time. Long enough for a statute to nearly run. Long enough for a forged transfer to harden into settled fact. If Tobias had wanted justice, why seal it? Why wait?

Unless the waiting wasn't Tobias's idea.

Unless the envelope had been opened early, read early, and mailed late — mailed only when someone realized the clock was about to bury the crime forever, and decided the confession letter was the perfect instrument: enough to start an investigation, not enough to finish one. Enough to make Hazel Crane spend thirty days digging with a shovel someone else controlled.

"Some files stay closed," she read aloud again. Her breath fogged faintly in the cold office; the heat hadn't kicked on yet. "Whose files?"

* * *

At nine o'clock, Deputy Marisol Vega appeared in her doorway without knocking, which was unlike her, and closed the door behind her, which was more unlike her still.

"You heard?" Hazel asked.

"I heard you had a mess." Marisol's eyes went around the room, cataloguing with a cop's economy, and stopped on the note. She read it upside down from across the desk, lips moving slightly. When she looked up, her face had gone the particular shade of gray Hazel had learned to associate with people deciding whether to tell the truth. "Can I say something off the record? As in, so far off the record it never existed?"

"You've been giving me off-the-record things all month, Mari. One more won't sink us."

"Two nights ago I got a call from dispatch. Anonymous noise complaint at the boat launch, eleven-forty PM. I was closest. I went." She pulled a chair over without asking and sat, forearms on her knees, the posture of a woman bracing against weather. "Nothing there. But the gravel by the dock had tire tracks, fresh, backing almost to the water. And there was a burn barrel somebody had dragged out from the storage shed. Still warm. Ashes in it. Paper ash — you could see the edges of the sheets, the way paper curls when it burns slow."

Hazel felt the morning tilt. "You logged it?"

"I started to." Marisol rubbed her jaw. "And then Chief Calloway called my cell before I'd even finished the report. Told me the noise complaint was a clerical error, no unit dispatched, and that I should take some comp time. Hazel, dispatch never sent me. I checked. There was no call in the log. Somebody *made* dispatch call me — sent me out to the launch so I'd find the barrel and be officially aware of it, and then erased the reason I was there."

"Why would anyone—"

"Because they wanted a witness," Marisol said. "Or they wanted to see what I'd do. I don't know which is worse." She reached into her jacket and set a folded page on the desk between them — a photocopy, grainy, of her own incident log, the entry for two nights ago, annotated in her handwriting: *burn barrel, paper ash, tire impressions, photos taken.* "I photographed the tracks before I left. Tread's consistent with the tires on Harrow's Suburban. I'm not saying that's proof. I'm saying it's the same truck I saw three years ago, and somebody burned something at the exact dock where Tobias died, forty-eight hours after you got the second letter."

Hazel looked at the photocopy and understood, with a cold settling clarity, the shape of the last seventy-two hours. The second letter arrives. Within two days, someone is burning documents at the boat launch. Within five, her office is searched by someone who knows her filing system and leaves notes on funeral-home stationery. The machinery wasn't reacting to her investigation.

The machinery was *cleaning up around* it — and leaving her just enough wreckage to follow.

"They're not trying to stop us," she said slowly. "They're choreographing us."

"Then stop dancing," Marisol said. She stood, took her copy back — no, she pushed it across the desk, flat-palmed, final. "Keep it. Off the record, remember. Friday, Ruth gets whatever you've got. And Hazel—" At the door she paused, hand on the knob, and for a moment she looked very young and very tired, a woman carrying three years of a deer-poacher lie plus one fresh one. "The registry book at the cemetery. The visitor log. I run the detail that checks it Tuesdays."

Hazel went still.

"You signed in as 'D. Crane,'" Marisol said. "Your father's initials. Cute. But the night attendant logs camera pulls, and Tuesday's tape shows a county vehicle parked at the gate at 1:20 AM. If I noticed, someone else can notice. Whatever you hid in that book — move it today. Move it somewhere nobody's ever going to walk a dog."

She left. The door clicked shut with the soft, terrible precision of everything else this week.

Hazel sat alone in her edited office, the note before her, the photocopy beside it, the photograph of her father smiling his useless smile. Outside the window, the parking lot filled with the ordinary cars of ordinary people coming to work at the business of the county, and none of them looked at her window, and that absence of attention felt newly sinister, like a held breath.

Thirty days minus four. Twenty-six.

She took out a fresh legal pad — they could steal the old one, but they couldn't steal the habit — and wrote at the top, in block letters:

**WHO KNEW THE OFFICE LAYOUT. WHO KNEW DAD'S PHOTO. WHO HAS DOYLE'S STATIONERY. WHO COULD REACH DISPATCH.**

Four lines. Four questions. And beneath them, unbidden, her pen added a fifth before she could stop it:

**WHO BENEFITS IF I FIND EVERYTHING EXCEPT WHAT CONVICTS ANYONE?**

She stared at that fifth line for a long time. Then she tore the page off, folded it into her coat pocket, picked up her keys, and headed for the cemetery — to move a brass key out of a grave registry before the town's patient, well-organized ghosts read the visitor log and came looking for what the dead had left behind.

Behind her, on the desk, the note waited in the lamplight. *Some files stay closed.*

Hazel Crane intended to open every last one of them — starting with the one sealed inside her own family's name.
