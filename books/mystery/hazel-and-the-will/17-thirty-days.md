## 17. Thirty Days

The petition went in at 9:14 on a Tuesday morning, and Ruth Okafor had learned long ago that the minute mattered. Courts were like rivers — you could drown in them or ride them, but either way you entered at the same bank, and the bank was paper.

She filed it herself. No courier, no e-filing portal with its cheerful little confirmation chime. She drove to the county courthouse in Ashford, forty minutes of two-lane road with the petition box on the passenger seat like a passenger, and she walked it up to the clerk's window in person because she wanted a human being to look her in the eye and stamp it.

The clerk was a young man with a jawline and a name tag that said DEV. He read the caption — *Okafor, on behalf of Vivian Wren, petitioner, v. The Estate of Tobias Wren, the Harrow Family Trust, and Aldous Harrow, respondent* — and his eyebrows did something complicated.

"Fraud petition," he said. "Against the Harrow Trust."

"Against the paper," Ruth said. "The man comes later."

Dev stamped it. The sound was small and final, a period at the end of a three-year sentence. Ruth took her copy, checked the date and time, folded it into her bag, and walked back down the courthouse steps into a morning so clean and cold it felt like the world had been washed for the occasion.

Thirty-one days. That was what was left of the statute when the ink dried. She'd told Hazel thirty-four; the filing had eaten three. The clock was now a countdown, and everyone in the county who needed to know would know by lunch.

She gave them until lunch. She was right.

* * *

Ruth had been disbarred for eleven years, and in that time she had developed a professional relationship with the law the way a divorced couple develops a professional relationship with a house they still co-own: she knew every creak in its floors, every place it leaked, and every room she was no longer allowed into. Practicing law without a license was a crime. Filing a petition pro se on behalf of a sister-in-interest with a power of attorney in her pocket was not. Ruth had structured everything so that Vivian Wren's name sat in the caption and Ruth's name appeared nowhere but the signature block of a limited appearance — a narrow, technical, grudgingly permitted thing that let a non-lawyer stand in a courtroom for one purpose and one purpose only.

She had fought for that allowance years ago, in a different case, in a different county, and lost. Then appealed, and won, on the theory that the right of access to the courts did not evaporate because a person had once been bad at being a lawyer. The appellate judge who wrote the opinion — a dry, ancient man named Calloway — had included a footnote that Ruth had framed and hung above her desk:

*The law is a door, not a club. We do not bar the door because some have used it badly.*

Aldous Harrow's lawyers were going to spend the next thirty-one days trying to convince a judge that the door was, in fact, a club, and that Ruth Okafor was holding it.

She was almost looking forward to it.

* * *

The response came faster than she expected, which told her everything about the quality of counsel Harrow had bought.

Merrick & Sloane, out of the state capital, filed a motion to dismiss and a motion to strike and a motion to quash, all in one document, forty-one pages of it, and a separate motion to disqualify the petitioner's representative that was, Ruth had to admit, beautifully written. She read it twice at her kitchen table with a pot of coffee going cold beside her, and she found herself nodding at the prose the way you nod at a good opponent's serve.

The argument was simple. Ruth Okafor was disbarred. Disbarred attorneys were presumptively unfit to represent interests in court. The petition was a nullity. Strike it, dismiss it, and by the time Vivian Wren retained proper counsel — if she could afford proper counsel, which was the point — the statute would have run, and the whole ugly business would be a matter of historical interest only.

It was elegant. It was also a confession. You didn't spend that kind of money on that kind of motion unless the paper underneath it scared you.

"They're not arguing the will is fake," Hazel said that evening, on the phone. Hazel's voice had a new texture these days, Ruth had noticed — something sanded down, like driftwood. "They're arguing you're not allowed to say it's real."

"That's the tell," Ruth said. "If we were nothing, they'd let us drown quietly. You don't hire the capital firm to swat a fly. You hire the capital firm when the fly has a copy of your client's fingerprints."

"Vivian's recording is in the petition. Marisol's log. The will itself."

"All of it. Exhibit A through C. And the affidavit from the forensic pathologist as Exhibit D, though that one's for the criminal referral, not probate. I wanted it in the record anyway. Let it sit there. Let their lawyers read it and lose sleep."

Hazel was quiet a moment. "Do you think they'll get it suppressed?"

"Everything?" Ruth said. "No. Something? Probably. That's how it works. You throw a whole kitchen at the court and the court hands back the sink. But here's what their motion can't touch —" she tapped the table, a habit Hazel couldn't see but could hear — "the will. The original will, in Tobias Wren's hand, authenticated, from a sealed locker with a documented chain of custody. That document doesn't go away because my name is on a disqualification motion. Worst case, Vivian hires a real lawyer, we lose a week, and I sit in the gallery taking notes like a retired schoolteacher."

"You'd hate that."

"I'd be magnificent at it," Ruth said. "But I don't think it's coming to that. Calloway's footnote is precedent in this district. And the judge they drew —" she paused, savoring it — "is Judge Anneke Brandt."

"Is that good?"

"Brandt clerked for Calloway. She cited his access-to-courts opinion in her first year on the bench. She also, and I say this with professional affection, does not like being told what her courtroom may and may not hear." Ruth let that settle. "The motion to disqualify gets heard Thursday. The suppression motions get rolled into the main hearing. And here's the part you're going to like, Hazel — Brandt set the hearing date herself."

"When?"

"The twenty-ninth." Ruth watched the calendar on her wall, where she'd marked the statute's death in red. "Four days before the claim dies. She gave us the last week of the world."

* * *

Thursday, the disqualification hearing, lasted ninety minutes and felt like a chess game played in a phone booth.

Merrick & Sloane sent a partner named Douglas Fenwick, silver-haired, cuff-linked, with the particular calm of a man who had never once been interrupted. He argued Ruth's disbarment like a man reading an obituary he'd written himself: the facts were not in dispute, the discipline was a matter of public record, and the court should not countenance a nullity of a petition propped up by a woman the bar had already judged.

Ruth stood at her little table — she'd worn her one good suit, the charcoal one, and she noticed Fenwick's eyes flick to it, doing the arithmetic of a disbarred woman's wardrobe — and she kept her answer to one page.

"Mr. Fenwick is right about my record," she said. "I was disbarred. I won't waste the court's time litigating ancient history, and I won't insult this court by pretending the discipline was unjust. It was earned." She heard the gallery shift — Hazel was there, third row, and she heard the small intake of breath that meant Hazel hadn't known Ruth would concede it. "But the question before this court is not whether I was a bad lawyer eleven years ago. It is whether Vivian Wren gets to have her brother's will heard before the law's own deadline erases it. Judge Calloway answered that question for this district. The door is a door. I am asking to hold it open for thirty-one days. Mr. Fenwick is asking you to close it, and to close it in a way that guarantees no one else can open it in time."

She sat down. Fenwick rose for rebuttal and spent eleven minutes on the phrase *slippery slope*, which Ruth privately scored as a forfeit.

Judge Brandt took the matter under advisement for one hour — an interval Ruth spent on the courthouse steps in the cold, not because she needed air but because she refused to be seen waiting in the hallway like a litigant with something to lose. When the clerk came out and handed her the order, she read it standing up.

*Petitioner's limited appearance is GRANTED, narrowly, for the purposes of the fraud petition only. The court notes that the right of access to the courts belongs to the petitioner, not the representative. The court further notes that objections to the representative are not objections to the evidence, and will be treated accordingly.*

*Accordingly.* Ruth read the word three times. It was Brandt telling Fenwick, in the politest possible judicial dialect, that his suppression motions were about to have a very bad month.

* * *

The suppression motions were heard on the twenty-ninth, and the courtroom was full in a way small courtrooms rarely are.

Ruth had expected the town to stay home. Towns like this one — she'd grown up in one, three counties east, a town with the same church and the same diner and the same families holding the same debt — watched from a distance while the machinery ran, and only came to court for sentencings, because sentencings were the only part with a guaranteed ending. But the gallery on the twenty-ninth was standing-room. She recognized faces from the funeral three years ago. She recognized the pastor. She recognized, sitting alone near the back with his hat on his knees, Emmett Doyle, who would not meet anyone's eyes, including, Ruth noticed, Hazel's.

Fenwick argued suppression in three movements, and he was good. The will: provenance unestablished, chain of custody anecdotal, the locker key delivered by anonymous mail — fruit of an unverified tree. Marisol Vega's log: an amended entry, self-serving, made under pressure, inadmissible hearsay wrapped around a career-saving revision. Vivian Wren's recording: made without consent, in a diner, an ambush dressed as a conversation, and — here Fenwick's voice dropped into genuine sorrow — *obtained by a petitioner represented by a disbarred attorney with an evident personal grievance against the respondent's counsel of record.*

That last one was a lie, and Ruth stood to say so, and Brandt said, "Sit down, Ms. Okafor. The court heard it. The court also heard it was a lie."

The gallery made a sound like a wave deciding whether to break.

Fenwick recovered, because men like Fenwick always recovered, and he closed on the theme he'd been building all morning: that the petition was not a search for truth but an assault on a family that had given this county its library, its scholarship fund, its flood-relief money, its funeral for Tobias Wren, paid in full, no expense spared. "The court is being asked," he said, "to undo three years of settled affairs on the strength of a photocopy, a diary entry, and a secret recording, all assembled by people with grudges. Settled affairs, Your Honor. The estate has been administered. The trust has operated in good faith. The town has moved on."

*The town has moved on.* Ruth wrote the phrase in her margin and underlined it twice, because she had heard it before, in every case she'd ever lost, and in every case she'd ever won, and it meant the same thing both times: *we were comfortable, and comfort is a legal argument now.*

Brandt ruled from the bench, which Ruth had hoped for and not expected. Judges who ruled from the bench had already decided. Judges who took matters under advisement were deciding how to decide. Brandt had decided.

"The will comes in," Brandt said. "Provenance goes to weight, not admissibility. A document in the decedent's hand, recovered from a sealed locker, with a contemporaneous envelope postmarked the day of his funeral — the court finds the foundation sufficient for a fact-finder to consider it. The log comes in. An officer's contemporaneous duty log, even as amended, is a business record, and the circumstances of amendment are themselves relevant. The recording comes in. This is not a criminal proceeding, and the respondent's counsel should know better than to cite consent statutes at a civil fraud hearing; if he'd like, the court can explain the difference on the record."

Fenwick did not want the explanation on the record.

"What remains," Brandt went on, "is the question of whether the transfer of the Wren estate to the Harrow Trust was procured by forgery, and if so, what the estate looked like before it was touched. That is a question of fact. The court will hear it beginning Monday morning, and the court will hear it to conclusion — the parties should plan for full days. Any motion to continue is denied in advance, so that no one wastes the court's time drafting it." She looked at Fenwick over her glasses. "The statute runs Thursday at five p.m. This court intends to finish before the statute does. We are adjourned."

* * *

Afterward, in the hallway, with the gallery draining past them in a river of coats and murmurs, Hazel caught up to Ruth by the water fountain.

"You heard it," Hazel said. "The whole town heard it. Brandt didn't leave them a single door."

"She left them one," Ruth said. "The best one. The truth. Fenwick's whole play now is to make the forgery look plausible — a sick man, a confused man, a man who changed his mind. He'll put character witnesses on the stand until the gallery falls asleep. He'll make Tobias Wren into an unreliable narrator of his own life." She capped her pen. "Which is why Monday matters more than any hearing I've argued in eleven years. Because the truth, Hazel, is not a legal argument. It's a witness. And we have to put it on the stand."

"Vivian's ready."

"Vivian's ready. Vega's ready, as ready as a woman can be who's about to end her own career in open court. Doyle —" Ruth glanced down the hallway, but the funeral director was already gone. "Doyle is the hinge. His recantation is in Fenwick's file. If he takes that stand and repeats it, the whole structure wobbles. If he takes that stand and tells what's in that sealed envelope instead —"

"He won't," Hazel said. "He mailed the letters. He won't testify."

"People surprise you," Ruth said. "That's the only reason I still do this."

Hazel looked at her — that coroner's look, the one that weighed and measured — and Ruth had the sudden, uncomfortable sense of being the one on the table.

"You've been disbarred eleven years," Hazel said. "Why does this one matter so much?"

Ruth considered lying, decided it would be wasted on Hazel Crane, and told the truth instead. "Because eleven years ago I lost a case exactly like this one, and I lost it because I was bad at my job and arrogant about being bad at it, and a family spent a decade paying for my tuition. Tobias Wren has been dead three years and his town has been lying to itself the whole time, and everyone who could have stopped it found a reason not to. I found reasons not to for a long time too." She shouldered her bag. "This is the case you don't lose, Hazel. Not because winning is guaranteed. Because losing it twice would be a life sentence."

She pushed through the courthouse doors into the cold. Monday was three days away. The statute ran Thursday at five.

And as she crossed the square, she saw, parked across from the diner with its engine running and its wipers ticking against a sky that had finally decided to snow, a truck she recognized from a duty log three years old — and a man in the driver's seat, watching the courthouse doors, watching her, making sure she saw him watching.

Ruth Okafor smiled at Aldous Harrow, and kept walking.
