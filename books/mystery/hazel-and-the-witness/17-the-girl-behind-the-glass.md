## 17. The Girl Behind the Glass

The woman at the laundromat on Delancey Street folds her towels in thirds, always in thirds, and I have decided this means she is careful.

I have been watching her for eleven days.

Her name is Wren Whitlock, though the name tag she no longer wears said *W.* when I last saw it, three years ago, across a marble lobby that smelled of lilies and money. She was the concierge then—the one who remembered that I took my tea without sugar, who noticed my mother's birthday before I did because I'd mentioned it once, offhand, while checking in. She was kind to me. That is exactly why I should not be here.

But here I am, third row of dryers from the window, pretending to read a paperback with a cracked spine, watching through glass gone soft with steam as Wren Whitlock feeds quarters into machine number six like any ordinary person doing an ordinary thing. As if she hasn't spent the past two weeks pulling threads out of a tapestry someone very powerful wove to hide a body.

My body, technically. The one they never found.

* * *

I should explain how a dead girl does her laundry.

You learn things when you stop existing. You learn that cashiers don't look at your face if you count out exact change. You learn that churches will let you sleep in the back pew if you arrive after the janitor and leave before the priest. You learn which shelters ask for ID and which only ask for your first name, and you learn—this one takes longer—that a name is just a coat. You put it on. You wear it until it fits.

Mine is June. June Calloway. I chose it because it was the month everything ended and the month, I told myself, everything could begin again, and because *Calloway* sounded like a woman who had no history, a woman whose ancestors were too boring to leave records behind. June works at a florist's three days a week under the table. June pays rent on a room above a nail salon where the landlady doesn't ask questions because June fixed her leaky faucet and never raised the subject of a lease. June has brown hair now, cut short, and glasses with plain lenses that fog up when she cries, which is useful, because nobody looks twice at a crying girl behind smudged glass.

June is careful. June folds her towels in thirds.

Hazel Marsh died in a service corridor of the Hotel Aurelia—or close enough to died that the difference stopped mattering—and Hazel Marsh stays buried, even though there was never a grave.

* * *

It started, this watching, with the diary.

I want to say I left it deliberately, but that would be a lie, and I promised myself when I became June that I would stop lying to the only person still listening. The truth is: I hid it in the wall of the old Alderman Building the night I ran, because I couldn't carry it and because I couldn't burn it either. It was the only proof I'd ever been real. A girl can survive without a lot of things, but not without proof she existed. So I walled it up like a heart in a story, and I told myself some future demolition crew would find it in fifty years, when everyone involved was dust, and some archivist would shake her head over the sad case of Hazel Marsh, runaway, presumed fine, probably married somewhere under another name.

I didn't count on the renovation happening now. I didn't count on the diary landing in the lost-and-found of the very hotel I'd fled. And I certainly didn't count on Wren Whitlock being the one who opened the box.

Ruth called me. That's how I knew. My mother—who doesn't know where I am, who has never known where I am, who I let believe for one terrible year that I might be dead because believing I was dead kept her safer than hoping I was alive—my mother called the number I gave her for emergencies only, emergencies meaning funerals, meaning arrests, meaning *never*, and she read me a paragraph from the newspaper about the Aurelia's centennial preparations, and then, voice dropping to almost nothing, she said: *They found something during the construction next door, sweetheart. Something with writing in it.*

She didn't say *diary*. She didn't dare. But I heard it anyway, the way you hear your own name across a crowded room.

* * *

So I came to look. Just to look. That's what I told myself the first day, standing across Delancey Street with a coffee I bought so I'd have a reason to stand anywhere, watching the hotel's side entrance through the plate glass of a shuttered pharmacy. Just to see what Wren did with it. Whether she'd hand it to the police like a good citizen, whether Detective Ashford would come collect it with his kind cop smile and his cufflinks the color of old blood, whether the whole thing would vanish into an evidence locker and then into nothing, the way everything else had vanished.

Instead, on the second day, I watched Wren Whitlock stand at the bus shelter across from the depot on Corliss Avenue—I followed her there, God help me, I followed her into the neighborhood where I used to sleep—and I watched her stand very still in front of the wall where I'd marked the months.

I used to keep a calendar on the brick. Pencil, because pencil washes out. Little ticks for each day survived, little codes underneath that meant nothing to anyone but me: the license plate of the car that circled too often, the days the man with the ledger came to count his people, the nights I heard trains that never stopped at the platform that wasn't a platform anymore. I'd written *JUNE* across the top in letters big enough to read from the tracks, because I am sentimental, and sentimentality is the one luxury a ghost can afford.

Wren stood in front of that wall for four minutes. I timed her. Four minutes, motionless, in the cold, reading my handwriting like it was scripture, and then she took out a notebook—not a phone, a notebook, paper, the kind of thing that can't be subpoenaed by people who own the courthouse—and she copied every mark I'd made.

That was the moment I stopped sleeping properly.

Because here is the thing about kindness: I have a catalog of it now, three years deep, and every entry ends the same way. Mrs. Okafor, who fed me soup in the church basement, whose basement got raided two weeks after I left it. Danny, the kid at the record store who let me crash on his couch, who moved cities abruptly and won't answer mail. My mother, who lost her job at the clinic six months after I disappeared, officially for budget reasons, though everyone knows budgets are where institutions hide their knives. Everyone who was kind to me paid for it. Kindness, in my experience, is the bait in a trap I cannot see the shape of until my foot is in it.

And yet.

* * *

Day five, she went to Ruth's kitchen. I know because Ruth told me afterward, breathless, whispering into the phone from her bedroom with the door closed, the way we do our one call a week, ten minutes, burner to burner, a ritual more secret than anything I did wrong. *There's a woman here,* Ruth said. *From the hotel. She remembers you. She asked about the phone call, Hazel, the one you made me—* and I made her stop, made her promise not to tell the concierge anything, not the date, not the words, not the way the line went dead mid-sentence because I saw a car I recognized idling outside the payphone and hung up with my heart going like a bird in a box.

But Ruth being Ruth, she'd already said too much, and I lay on my narrow bed above the nail salon staring at the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a country no one has named, and I thought: *she knows about the call now. She knows I'm alive. Or she suspects. And what does a person do with a suspicion like that?*

A good citizen takes it to the police.

I needed to know which kind of person Wren Whitlock was. Not which kind she seemed. Which kind she *was*, when it cost her something.

* * *

So I tested her. I'm not proud of it, but pride is another thing I buried with my name.

Day eight, I sent her a message. Not signed, not traceable—a postcard, actually, bought from the rack at the bodega, a picture of the city skyline at dusk, all gold and lying light. I wrote on the back, in handwriting I haven't used in three years, my real handwriting, the slanted one with the loops: *Some doors are closed because they're locked. Some are closed because of what's behind them. Ask room 412 why the key was changed in March.*

Room 412. The room they gave me my first week, before they moved me to 407, before the corridor, before everything. In March of that year the lock was changed overnight, no explanation, and I later understood why: that was the week the man with the ledger started using the fourth floor for his meetings, and the hotel was sealing him a private throat in its public body. If Wren checked the maintenance logs—and she would, she's the type, she reads buildings the way priests read faces—she'd find the date, and the date would match the dates in my code, and the whole skeleton of it would start to show through the skin.

I mailed it unsigned. Then I hated myself for three hours. Because if she took it to Ashford, I'd have handed the detective a map to my own pulse. If she ignored it, I'd know she was safe but useless. And if she followed it—

If she followed it, she'd be walking into the same dark I walked out of, and I'd have lured her there myself, and I'd be, what? Another person who hurt someone kind?

I watched the mail slot of the Aurelia's staff entrance from the pharmacy doorway for two days like a lunatic. Nothing. Then, on the morning of day ten, I saw her come out early, before her shift, wearing a coat I hadn't seen before and carrying her notebook, and she walked—not toward the police station, not toward the hotel offices, but toward the municipal records annex on Halsey Street, the ugly beige building where the city keeps its maintenance filings and its shame.

She followed it. She followed *me*.

I stood across the street from the annex for an hour, telling myself to leave, to move cities, to become August or September or some other month with no history, because the smart thing—the June thing—was to run. Every instinct I've sharpened since the corridor screamed run. And my feet stayed planted on the cold pavement like they'd grown roots, because underneath the fear, underneath everything, there is a smaller and more stubborn thing that has kept me alive this long:

I want to be found.

God, I want it. I want it the way the drowned want air. Three years of being no one, of folding myself into doorways, of flinching at white cars and men with neat briefcases, of eating dinner alone in rooms where nobody knows my middle name—it grinds you down to a nub of wanting. Some nights I've stood outside my mother's building just to watch her kitchen light go off, and once I got close enough to smell her cooking through the stairwell, onions and bay leaf, and I had to press my hand over my mouth not to make a sound, because the sound would have been her name and her name would have been the end of both of us.

I want to be found. I fear it more. Both things, all the time, like two hands squeezing the same heart from opposite sides.

* * *

Which brings me to today. Day eleven. The laundromat.

I didn't plan this either—there's very little I plan anymore; planning is for people with futures—but I saw her carry the canvas bag down Delancey and something in me simply followed, the way water follows a slope. Now I'm in the third row of dryers with my cracked paperback and my plain-glass glasses, and she's twenty feet away folding towels in thirds, and the steam has fogged the window so the street outside is all soft gold, and I am studying her the way she studied my calendar wall: looking for the truth under the surface.

Here is what I see.

I see a woman who is tired in a specific way—not work-tired, hunt-tired. There are shadows under her eyes like bruises that haven't decided. I see that she checks the door every time it opens, without seeming to, a small flick of attention, the same reflex I have. I see that she's washed her clothes at nine p.m. on a Tuesday instead of Sunday afternoon like a person with a normal life, which tells me she's been busy following leads, or avoiding her own apartment—which, given what happened to my couch-sleeping friends, might be wisdom. I see that her hands are steady but her jaw is set, the way mine gets set when I've decided something frightening.

And I see, folded half into her canvas bag where she probably thinks it's hidden, the corner of a manila envelope, and poking out of the envelope, unmistakable even edge-on, the marbled cover of my diary.

She carries it with her. She doesn't trust the hotel safe, doesn't trust her room, doesn't trust anyone enough to leave it behind. She sleeps next to my secrets the way I used to sleep next to the wall.

Something in my chest cracks along a seam I didn't know was there.

A dryer buzzes. Mine. I gather my clothes—warm, smelling of other people's detergent, the anonymous clean of laundromats everywhere—and I fold them badly, quickly, because my hands have stopped being careful, and I am aware of her awareness, the flick of her eyes, the naturalist noticing movement at the edge of the clearing. Don't look at me. Look at me. Please don't look at me. Please.

She looks. Of course she looks. She's Wren Whitlock; she notices everything; that's the whole problem with her, the whole terrible gift of her. Our eyes meet through the steam-fogged glass of the folding counter for maybe one and a half seconds, and in that second and a half I feel catalogued, weighed, *read*—and then she does the most extraordinary thing.

She looks away.

Deliberately. A small, courteous withdrawal of attention, the way you'd step back from a bird that had landed near you, giving it room to decide. She goes back to her towels. Thirds. Careful. And she leaves the space beside her at the counter open, unclaimed, the way you leave a door ajar.

Not a trap. Traps don't have ajar doors.

I stand there with my warm clothes clutched to my chest like a shield, and I understand that I have just been tested back, that she clocked me in one glance—the girl who watches, the girl with the paperback she isn't reading, the girl whose laundry came out of the dryer untouched by fabric softener because ghosts don't own softener—and that she offered me the one thing no one has offered me in three years: the choice. Come closer or don't. Be seen or stay a stranger. Her kindness isn't bait. It's a door, and it's ajar, and it's mine to walk through or walk past.

I could walk past. June would walk past. June has survived precisely by walking past every ajar door in this city.

I stand in the yellow light of the laundromat with my heart going like a bird in a box, and I think of my mother's kitchen light, and the man with the ledger, and the sound a body makes in a service corridor—no, I don't think of that, I never think of that—and I think of a postcard in a concierge's pocket, and a pencil calendar on a brick wall, and a woman who copies down a dead girl's handwriting like it matters, like the girl mattered, like girls matter.

The dryer hums. The steam climbs the glass. Outside, the streetlights come on all at once, the way they do, like the city blinking awake to something.

I take one step toward the counter. Then another.

"Excuse me," I hear myself say, and my voice comes out rusted, unused to its own name. "Is this machine—"

And Wren Whitlock turns around, and looks at me, and I watch her realize—watch it move through her face like weather, recognition arriving not all at once but in stages, the glasses, the hair, the eyes, the eyes, *the eyes*—and I watch her decide, in the same breath, to give absolutely nothing away, to keep my secret right there in the open air between us, wrapped in the mundane language of dryers and detergent.

"All yours," she says quietly. "Take your time."

And I understand, standing in the warm hum of that ordinary room, that whatever happens next has already begun—that I have stepped through the ajar door, and there is no becoming June again, not entirely, not after this—and that tomorrow, or the day after, one of us is going to say the true thing out loud.

I only pray that when we do, the walls of this city aren't listening.
