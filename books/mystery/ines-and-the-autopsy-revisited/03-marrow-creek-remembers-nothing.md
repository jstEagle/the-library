## 3. Marrow Creek Remembers Nothing

The letters had been in his briefcase for six days now, riding back and forth between the office and his house like two passengers who refused to get off at their stop. He hadn't filed them. He hadn't burned them. He'd done something worse than either: he'd carried them.

On Thursday morning he decided to walk through Marrow Creek and see if the town still fit inside its own story.

He told himself it was routine. Lawyers did this—drove the roads, checked the landmarks, kept a feel for the geography of their cases. But Marrow Creek wasn't his case anymore. It hadn't been since the day three years ago when he'd stamped the last page of the Ashcombe estate file and watched the courthouse clerk carry it into the records room like a casserole to a wake. Whatever this was, it wasn't routine. It was a man checking whether the walls of his house were load-bearing.

He parked on Delacroix Street, in front of the Blue Heron Diner, because if you wanted to take the temperature of Marrow Creek there was no better thermometer than a counter stool at eight in the morning.

* * *

The Blue Heron smelled the way it always had: bacon grease, burnt coffee, and the faint industrial sweetness of the pie warmer. Ruthie Calloway was behind the register, same as she'd been behind it for thirty years, her reading glasses pushed up into gray hair that defied gravity out of pure habit.

"Oscar Sandoval." She said it the way she said everything, flat and warm at once, a woman who could make your name sound like both an accusation and a welcome. "Haven't seen you in here since the Ashcombe business wrapped up. Sit down before I change my mind about liking you."

He took a stool at the end of the counter. Two farmers down from him were arguing about soybean futures with the passion of men who had never once considered another topic. A deputy sheriff he half-recognized worked through eggs over easy without looking up.

"Coffee," Oscar said. "And whatever's fresh."

"Nothing's fresh. It's all been sitting under glass being beautiful." Ruthie poured anyway. "You hear about Cassius Ashcombe?"

The cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "What about him?"

"Confessed." She said it with the relish of a woman delivering weather news. "Walked into the sheriff's office Monday morning and said he killed his sister. Can you imagine? Three years after we all buried that poor girl, and her own brother stands up and says *I did it*."

"That is remarkable," Oscar said carefully.

"Course, nobody believes him."

The words landed with a strange weight, because they were delivered not as opinion but as fact—the way you'd say nobody believes the moon is made of cheese. Oscar turned on his stool. "Nobody?"

Ruthie shrugged and rang up the deputy's check. "Cassius has been off since the accident. You know the one. Hit his head skiing in '18, came back different. The family's been carrying him ever since. Sweet of them, really. Most families would've put him somewhere."

"Sweet," Oscar repeated.

"Mm." She slid a plate of pie in front of him that he hadn't ordered. "Pecan. On the house. You look like a man who needs sugar."

He ate a bite he didn't taste. Around him the diner hummed its morning hum, and he listened the way he used to listen to depositions—not to what people said, but to what they agreed not to say.

"You were here when Vivian died," he said. "Three years ago. What do you remember?"

Ruthie's rag stopped moving across the counter. Just for a second. Then it resumed, small circles, efficient. "Drowned. Out at the Hall, in the old swimming pool. Terrible thing. She was always swimming alone at night, that girl. Headstrong."

"The groundskeeper found her, I understand. Tomás Iriarte."

"Tomás." She nodded. "Poor man. Died himself not long after. Heart, I think. 2019. Whole town went to the funeral—well, whole town meaning the twelve of us who liked him. Quiet fellow. Kept to himself."

"He found the body?"

"So they say." The rag moved faster now. "Look, honey, you want more coffee?"

It was a deflection so smooth it deserved a professional courtesy. Oscar gave her one. "Please."

But as she poured, he watched her eyes, and he saw what he'd spent twenty years watching juries watch for: the flicker that comes when a person reaches for a memory and finds someone else's version of it already installed. Ruthie Calloway didn't remember Tomás Iriarte finding a body. She remembered being told about it, repeatedly, by people whose job it was to be believed.

"Regina must have taken it hard," he said. "Losing both of them."

Something crossed Ruthie's face then—something quick and complicated, gone before it could be named—and Oscar would spend the rest of the day trying to decide whether it had been grief or fear.

"Regina Ashcombe is the strongest woman in this county," Ruthie said, in the tone of a woman reciting a line she'd been assigned. "That family built half this town. The library. The clinic roof. My grandson's scholarship. You don't survive what they've survived without being strong."

She walked away to refill the deputy's cup, and Oscar sat with his pecan pie and understood that he had just heard a prayer. Not to God. To Regina.

* * *

The courthouse sat on its little hill like a wedding cake left out in the rain—white columns gone gray, the clock in the tower stuck at 4:17 for as long as anyone could remember, which the county claimed was a funding issue and which everyone privately understood to be a metaphor.

Oscar climbed the steps at ten forty-five. His key still worked; his name was still on the brass directory downstairs, third floor, SANDOVAL & ASSOCIATES, where & Associates meant Dolores Vann and a ficus. He passed his own door without going in. Today he wanted the records room.

The clerk on duty was a young man named Petey Grange, whose entire personality had been assembled from courthouse surplus. He looked up from a crossword and brightened.

"Mr. Sandoval! You never come down here unless somebody's subpoenaing somebody."

"Just doing some follow-up on the Ashcombe estate," Oscar said. "Housekeeping. You know how probate files are—they breed."

Petey laughed like this was the funniest thing a lawyer had ever said, which told Oscar something sad about Petey's life, and buzzed him through.

The records room was cold and smelled of toner and old paper, the smell Oscar had loved for thirty years, the smell that had lately begun to feel like the inside of a coffin lined with receipts. He signed the log—ASHCOMBE, V., ESTATE—because he was still, God help him, a man who signed logs—and carried the box to a table under the fluorescent light.

He knew what was in it. He had put every page in it himself. But he opened it anyway, the way you touch a bruise, and there it all was: the will dated March 2nd, the death certificate, the letters testamentary, the inventory of assets, the closing statement bearing his signature and the court's seal. A complete life reduced to a folder. A woman reduced to a transaction.

And underneath the closing statement, where the autopsy report should have been, the pale rectangle of a page that had been removed—a gap in the paper trail shaped exactly like the truth.

He'd sealed that report himself, or rather Judge Pell had, at Regina Ashcombe's request, citing family privacy and the dignity of the deceased. *Sealed pending resolution of any future litigation,* the order read, which was lawyer language for *forever*. Oscar had stood in Pell's chambers while the old man signed it, and Pell had avoided his eyes, and Oscar had told himself then—as he told himself now—that a judge's order was a judge's order, and a lawyer's job was not to ask why.

A lawyer's job. Three years of hiding behind that phrase like a child behind a screen door.

"Find what you need?" Petey called from the doorway.

"I'm looking for the coroner's report," Oscar said. "I know it's sealed, but I want to confirm the seal date. For the file."

"Oh, that one's not here."

Oscar turned. "Excuse me?"

"The Ashcombe autopsy." Petey came in, apologetic, hands in his pockets. "Judge Pell had it transferred to chambers. Personal custody. Said it was sensitive. That was—" he counted on his fingers— "yeah, right after the funeral. Never came back down. People ask sometimes, you know, true-crime people, podcast people. We just send them up the hill to the Hall, and the Hall sends them back down, and everybody goes home happy."

"Personal custody," Oscar repeated. "A sitting judge took physical possession of a coroner's report and stored it in his private chambers."

"Well, when you say it like that." Petey grinned. "Judge Pell's a good man, though. Everybody says so. And he's family to the Ashcombes—cousin or something on his mother's side—so it's not like it went to a stranger."

*It's not like it went to a stranger.* Oscar felt the sentence go through him like a fishhook. The autopsy—the document that said how Vivian Ashcombe died—had been locked inside the house of the dead girl's cousin, and the town found this not alarming but tidy. Sensible. The way you'd keep grandma's ring in the family.

"Petey," Oscar said, "the witness. Tomás Iriarte. Did you know him?"

"Sure. Groundskeeper up at the Hall. Big quiet guy, drank his coffee black, tipped in exact change." Petey's face did something odd then, a small collapse, like a chair folding. "Died in 2019. Car accident, I thought? Or heart. One of those."

"Which one?"

"I—huh." Petey frowned, genuinely puzzled at himself. "Everybody says heart. But I got this picture in my head of a wrecker on Route 9, so maybe that was somebody else." He shook it off. "Anyway. Dead's dead. Why, you need his statement for something? Estate stuff?"

"No," Oscar said. "No, I don't suppose I do."

He signed the log again on his way out—he noticed his hand hesitated over the purpose column, and he wrote *housekeeping*, and the word looked like what it was, a fig leaf—and walked back out into the white October light feeling the first real vertigo of the whole affair.

Not because of what anyone had told him. Because of how they'd told it. Everyone in Marrow Creek remembered the same things in the same words with the same gaps, like a choir singing from the same hymnal, and the hymnal had been printed by the Ashcombes.

* * *

He drove out Route 9 past the feed store and the shuttered cinema and the water tower with MARROW CREEK: HOME OF GOOD NEIGHBORS painted on it in 1987 and never repainted since, because in Marrow Creek even decay was traditional. At the junction where Route 9 met the county road, he pulled over.

There was a guardrail there. New-ish. And beyond it, a scar in the embankment where a vehicle had gone through—not recently, but not anciently either; the brush had grown back thin and wrong, like hair over a scar. He got out and stood looking at it, telling himself he was imagining the connection, telling himself that rural counties had a thousand guardrails and a thousand scars.

Then he walked to the diner's newspaper rack memory in his head and pulled up the item he'd skimmed three years ago and never read twice: *LOCAL MAN KILLED IN ROUTE 9 CRASH*, page six, two paragraphs, no photograph. He couldn't remember the name in the headline. He'd been busy that week. There had been a filing deadline. There was always a filing deadline.

His phone was in his hand before he'd decided to use it. He searched the county obituary archive, and there it was, and the name in the headline was IRIARTE, T., and the date was November 2019, and the cause of death listed in the funeral home's notice was *complications following an automobile accident*—

Except.

Oscar read it four times. The notice was dated November 14th, 2019. And the crash, according to the state patrol archive he pulled next, had occurred on November 21st. Seven days *after* the obituary announced his death, Tomás Iriarte had died again.

A dead man cannot die twice. But a paper corpse can be buried twice, and whoever wrote that obituary had been in such a hurry to kill him that they'd jumped the gun by a week.

Oscar stood at the guardrail with the wind pushing at his coat, and the whole town rearranged itself around him. Not amnesia. Not grief. Consensus—suffocating, seamless, total. Ruthie with her rag. Petey with his crossword. *Heart, I think. Car accident, I thought.* Nobody remembered anything because remembering was unnecessary; the story had been distributed, installed, maintained. Marrow Creek didn't remember nothing. Marrow Creek had been *given* its memories, and it had accepted delivery.

And somewhere under all that consensus, a groundskeeper who tipped in exact change had been declared dead by people too careless to check the calendar—or too confident that no one ever would.

Oscar got back in the car. His hands were steady now, which frightened him more than shaking would have, because steadiness meant decision, and decision meant he was no longer a bystander to whatever came next.

He put the car in gear. He did not drive back to the office. He drove toward the county jail, where a man named Cassius Ashcombe was waiting—smiling, Ruthie had said, or at least the radio had said it, smiling like a man who knows a joke no one else gets—to explain why he'd confessed to a murder everyone agreed had never needed solving.

Oscar had a feeling he already knew the answer. He just needed to hear it said out loud, in a room with a table bolted to the floor, so it would count.
