## 9. The Break-In That Wasn't

The trouble with living alone for eleven years, Ines Varga had learned, was that you became the sole custodian of your own normal. Nobody else knew which mug went where, which floorboard sighed under the third step, which window stuck in October and forgave you in May. There was no witness to the small order of your life. And so when the order broke, the only person who could testify to it was you—and Ines had spent a decade and a half perfecting the art of not testifying to anything.

She noticed it first at the coat hooks.

Her apartment sat above the old pharmacy on Corben Street, three rooms and a kitchenette that the landlord charmingly called "a galley," though no ship had ever been so narrow. She came home from Halloway & Sons at 6:40 in the evening, as she always did, and hung her coat on the left hook, and her scarf on the right, and her keys in the ceramic bowl shaped like a swan that her mother had bought at a church rummage sale in a previous century. Except tonight the swan was facing the wall.

Ines stood in her doorway with her hand still on the knob and looked at the swan's ceramic tail and felt the temperature of the room change without any actual change in temperature.

She turned the swan around. She told herself she had turned it around herself, absentmindedly, the way she sometimes put the milk back in the cupboard and the cereal in the refrigerator after a long day with the deceased. She told herself this with the flat, dutiful voice she used on grieving families who asked whether their loved one had suffered: *I can't say for certain, but I can tell you what I observed.* What she observed was that she never turned the swan around. The swan faced the door. The swan had faced the door since 2013.

She made tea. She checked the window latches—locked, all of them, which meant nothing, because the latch on the bathroom window was the sort that could be turned with a butter knife and a patient wrist. She checked the closet, where her remaining cardigans hung in their colorless rows, and the box under the bed where she kept the letters she'd never mailed and the one photograph of her mother she'd never been able to look at directly, like an eclipse.

Everything was there. That was the thing. Everything was exactly, perfectly there, and it was the *exactly, perfectly* that made her hands go cold around her mug.

Burglars were messy. Ines had read enough case files—had *fed* enough case files, anonymously, in careful block capitals on unlined paper, to a man with a microphone three hundred miles away—to know that burglary was chaos with a duffel bag. Drawers upended. Mattresses slashed. The whole grammar of violation.

Her apartment had been *edited*. Someone had come in and read her like a book and put her back on the shelf in the right order, spine uncreased.

* * *

She didn't sleep, which was not unusual, but she slept badly in a new way, lying on top of the covers with the kitchen light on and a cast-iron skillet on the nightstand, which she recognized even in the moment as theater. If someone wanted to be in this apartment, a skillet would not stop them. If someone wanted to be in this apartment, they had already been in this apartment, possibly several times, possibly while she slept, possibly for years, and the skillet was a prop in a play she was losing.

In the morning she called in sick to Halloway & Sons—she never called in sick; Mr. Halloway's surprise came through the phone as a kind of offended silence—and then she did what she had trained herself never to do, which was to look at her own life as a stranger would.

She started with the door. The deadbolt was fine. But the little screw that held the strike plate was loose—had it been loose? She honestly couldn't remember. That was the humiliation of it, she thought, kneeling on her own hallway floor with a screwdriver at nine in the morning: she had built a life so quiet that even she couldn't distinguish its ordinary wear from tampering. Invisibility had a cost, and the cost was that there was no baseline. No witness. Not even herself.

She went through the kitchen. The tea tin was where it always was, but the lid sat a half-turn off from where her thumb expected it. The spice rack was alphabetized—she was not an alphabetizer; she was a "whatever fits" person—and had been alphabetized *badly*, the cumin filed under C but the cinnamon under S, which was the kind of mistake a person made who did not know spices, who had only glanced at the rack long enough to reproduce it. Someone had catalogued her life and then reassembled it from memory, and their memory was good but not perfect, and the imperfections were like fingerprints made of absence.

She laughed—a short, unhinged sound in the empty kitchen—because the alternative was to sit down on the linoleum and stay there.

*They alphabetized my spices,* she thought. *They came into my home, and they alphabetized my spices, and they got the cinnamon wrong.*

* * *

It took her until early afternoon to work up the nerve for the bedroom, and she made herself do it methodically, the way she did everything, the way she had been taught by the dead: one thing at a time, in order, with respect.

The dresser drawers: clothing, folded, correct. The nightstand: the novel she'd been failing to finish for two years, the earplugs, the little tin of sewing supplies. The closet. The floor.

Then the bed.

She stripped it the way she stripped it every other week, sheets into the hamper, and then she stood there with the bare mattress in the gray afternoon light and told herself there was nothing, that she was a woman who had read too many case files and mailed too many letters and had finally, inevitably, cracked under the strain of her own double life, that the paranoia was the symptom and the swan and the cinnamon were the evidence of nothing but her own unraveling.

Then she pressed her palms flat against the mattress and felt it.

Not a lump, exactly. A *firmness*. A rectangle of firmness, low, near the foot of the bed, on the side she didn't sleep on, because she slept on the side nearest the door because she had always slept nearest the door, even as a child, even in that house, even before she had anything to guard against except the dark. She had never once slept on the far side. The far side of her bed was a country she had never visited.

She got the kitchen shears. She hesitated—eleven years of careful living, and the mattress had been the one extravagant purchase of her life, a proper one, with springs—and then she cut along the piping with the steady hands she saved for work, and the fabric opened like a mouth, and there it was.

A single sheet of paper, folded in quarters, tucked into the batting between the springs.

She knew what it was before she unfolded it. Some part of her had known since the swan.

The paper was old. Cream-colored, heavy stock, faintly ribbed under her fingertips, the kind of paper that came in ledgers and minutes books and the bound annual reports of institutions that intended to outlast the people who wrote in them. The ink had gone the brown of old tea. And the writing on it was a column of dates and initials, small and precise and slanting slightly to the right, in a hand that was neat without being beautiful—the hand of someone who had been praised for penmanship as a girl and had built a whole self out of the praise.

Ines sat down on the floor beside her gutted mattress with the paper in her hands and did not read it, not properly. She didn't need to. She had spent three nights hunched over the mortuary's embalming ledger with a jeweler's loupe and a desk lamp, photographing pages with hands that wouldn't stay still, decoding the second column that didn't belong there, the shadow-record beneath the official one. She knew the dates. She knew the initials. She knew the cipher.

What she was holding was a *copy*. A faithful, deliberate, hand-transcribed copy of a page of that shadow-column, aged to look decades old, tucked into the mattress of the woman the police had already named a person of interest.

Not stolen. Planted.

The break-in that wasn't. The theft that was an authorship. Someone wanted the ledger's code to be *hers*. Someone wanted Detective Okafor to come back with a warrant, cut open her mattress the way she'd just cut it open, and find the smoking page—the source, the origin, the hand that had invented the whole conspiracy. Not a witness. An author. The killer, in the story they were building, would be Ines Varga, mortuary assistant, recluse, the strange woman who lived above the pharmacy and never spoke to anyone and had no one to say otherwise.

And the terrible, vertiginous elegance of it—the thing that made her laugh again, that awful laugh, into her empty bedroom—was how *well* it would work. She had spent fifteen years making herself into exactly the person no one would defend. No close friends. No lover. No phone full of witnesses. A landline, for God's sake, because cell phones were traceable and she'd mailed her tips from the drop box in Millbrook, forty minutes away, in gloves, in block capitals. She had constructed a life with no corroborating detail, and now that life was a vacuum, and the paper in her mattress was rushing in to fill it.

*You did this to yourself,* said a voice in her head that sounded like her own but colder. *You built the perfect frame and waited fifteen years for someone to hang it on you.*

*No,* said another voice, quieter, and this one she recognized as her own actual voice, the one that had been getting louder since the reservoir, since the cardigan, since the scarf. *Someone else built it. Someone who knows exactly how I live. Someone who has been in this apartment, more than once, long enough to learn that I don't alphabetize.*

She looked around the room—at the swan bowl visible through the doorway, tail to the wall because she hadn't had the heart to turn it back—and felt the full architecture of it settle over her like cold water. This wasn't a stranger. Strangers didn't know her habits. Strangers didn't know she slept on the door side, didn't know her spices were chaos, didn't know the swan faced the door. Whoever had done this knew her the way she knew the dead on her table: intimately, completely, from long observation.

The killer knew who she was.

Not *a* reclusive mortuary assistant. Not the woman from the police interview who'd lied so badly about the break-in she never reported. *Her*. Ines Varga, who had a mother with initials that appeared in the shadow column, who had a face that had been nine years old in a courtroom hallway in 1987, who had spent her whole adult life being nobody on the theory that nobody was safe.

The theory had a flaw, she saw now. The theory had always had a flaw. You could make yourself invisible to the world, but invisibility wasn't the same as safety. Invisibility just meant that when someone finally looked at you, they were the only one looking—and they could look for as long as they liked, and you'd never feel it.

* * *

She sat on the floor until the light went blue, holding the page with a dish towel, because she'd had the presence of mind—some distant, professional corner of her brain still on duty—to stop touching it with her bare hands. Fingerprints. She'd already put her prints all over it by unfolding it, of course, which was possibly the point, possibly the whole point, the page designed to be found by hands, handled, claimed. She couldn't undo that. She could only stop making it worse.

What she could do was think, and thinking was the one discipline she'd never let rust.

Fact: the page was a transcription, not the original. The original ledger sat in the basement office of Halloway & Sons, and she had photographs of it on a camera card in her left boot, because she did not trust her apartment, and had not trusted it, apparently, for reasons deeper than she'd admitted to herself.

Fact: the transcription was *aged*. Tea-brown ink, softened stock. That took skill, or knowledge, or access to the Historical Society's conservation room, where Vivian Marsh had been known to spend entire Saturdays coaxing documents back from the brink with humidifiers and bone folders and that gentle, proprietary touch Ines had watched through the doorway at last week's planning meeting, the touch of a woman who loved old paper the way some people loved children.

Fact—and this was the fact she kept circling back to, the fact with teeth—the handwriting was right. Not Vivian Marsh's present hand; Ines had seen enough of Vivian's recent minutes-entries to know the difference. But the *old* hand. The hand from the 1980s pages of the ledger's shadow column. Whoever had made this copy could reproduce that hand from memory, or from practice, or—

Or from a lifetime of signing things in it.

Ines got up off the floor. Her knees cracked. She went to the kitchen, put the page in a zip-top bag from the drawer, and put the bag in the cookie tin where she kept the emergency twenty and the spare key to the roof, and then she stood at her counter and made tea she did not want, because her hands needed a job or they were going to shake, and she did not permit them to shake. Shaking was for later. Shaking was a luxury for people who had someone to shake in front of.

The kettle climbed toward a whistle. Through the window over the sink she could see Corben Street going about its evening, Mrs. Adeyemi walking her terrible small dog, the pharmacy's neon flickering on in its familiar stutter, a young couple laughing at something outside the wine shop. A town that had no idea it was a stage set. A town where the beloved archivist was probably, at this very moment, pouring tea for someone or pressing a flower into a minutes book or doing whatever beloved archivists did with their evenings, and somewhere in that evening, at some point, had come the knowledge—calm, patient, long-deferred—that the witness from the boathouse had grown up and taken a job with the dead.

*She's known the whole time,* Ines thought. The certainty arrived not as fear but as something almost like relief, the relief of a diagnosis after years of symptoms. *She's known since the funeral. She watched me grow up invisible. She let me be invisible because invisible was safe—her kind of safe. And now I've started mailing letters to a man with a microphone, and invisible has stopped being safe, and so the mattress.*

The kettle screamed. Ines took it off the heat and did not make the tea.

Because here was the thing, the thing she turned over and over like a stone with something underneath it: the frame was *too good*. That was the flaw in it, if there was one. A page planted to be found by police would be found by police—Okafor was thorough, Okafor was already suspicious, Okafor would have that mattress open within the week. But the page had been planted *weeks* ago, she judged by the dust patterns in the batting, maybe longer. Planted and then left. Not triggered. Waiting.

Which meant it wasn't only a frame. It was also a *message*. A thing left where only she would ever plausibly find it, or a thing left to be found on a schedule the planter controlled. Either way, it said: *I know where you sleep. I know what's under you. I can put anything there I want.*

And if it was a message, then it wanted an answer.

Ines dried her hands on the towel. She looked at the cookie tin. She thought about the voicemail she'd almost left Theo Fairweather two nights ago—she had gotten as far as *Theo, it's me, well, it's not me, it's—* before she'd hung up with her heart going like a trapped bird. She thought about the photographs in her boot. She thought about the copy-shop man who always gave her the computer with the sticky S key, who was the closest thing to a confidant her double life had, and how she'd stood beside Theo Fairweather at the copy machine last week, close enough to smell his terrible cologne, saying nothing.

The page in the tin said she was alone. The killer wanted her alone. The killer had spent fifteen years making sure of it.

Ines picked up the phone—the landline, the heavy beige thing—and dialed the number she knew better than her own, the tip line, the one she'd called forty-one times in block-capital anonymity. It rang. The machine picked up. She heard his voice, warm and quick and a little too loud, the voice she'd known for years and stood beside last week without a word.

She opened her mouth.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, sitting on the floor of her violated bedroom with the skillet back on the nightstand and the mattress gaping like a wound she hadn't the money to close—she would try to reconstruct what she'd said, and fail, because the machine had cut her off at the beep's end and the words had come out in a rush she hadn't authorized. She would remember only fragments: the word *ledger*, the word *mattress*, the phrase *she knows my name*, and then her own finger finding the erase command on the machine with the speed of long practice, killing the message before the second ring of doubt could finish.

But the machine in that rented office three hundred miles away had a quirk Ines didn't know about. It saved the first four seconds of every deleted message to a buffer file—a glitch, a bug, the kind of small mechanical failure that history's biggest secrets were always built on.

Four seconds was not enough for a confession.

It was enough for a voice.
