## 13. Okafor's Doubt

Detective Emeka Okafor had been a police officer long enough to know that the universe had a sense of humor, and that its favorite joke was evidence.

Evidence was supposed to be the honest part of the job. Witnesses lied, suspects lied, lawyers lied with charts, but evidence just sat there on a table under fluorescent light and told you what it knew. That was the whole romance of it. That was why he'd taken the badge in the first place, back when he still believed the world sorted itself into things that happened and things people said happened.

So when the evidence against Ines Varga came together so quickly, so tidily, so *helpfully*, some old animal part of his brain sat up and growled.

He didn't listen to it right away. He had a captain who wanted the case closed by Friday, a district attorney who'd already used the phrase "slam dunk" twice in one phone call, and a town that wanted a monster it could point at — preferably one who worked with dead bodies, because that practically wrote itself. Ashcombe had spent three weeks being frightened of its own reservoir. People want their fear to have an address.

Ines Varga's apartment was the address they'd been given.

Okafor sat at his desk at seven-forty in the morning, before anyone else arrived, with two case files open side by side and a cup of coffee going cold beside them like a witness nobody called. On the left: Dana Pell, pulled from the reservoir on a Tuesday, dressed in a cardigan and scarf that belonged to Ines Varga. On the right: Ines Varga, thirty-four years old, mortuary assistant, no criminal record, no traffic tickets, no noise complaints, nothing. A woman whose paper trail was so clean it looked less like a life than like a freshly mopped floor.

"Okay," he said to the files, because talking to himself was cheaper than therapy and the department's health plan covered neither. "Let's do this the boring way."

The boring way was his wife's term for it. Chidi had learned, over nineteen years of marriage, that when Emeka spread papers across the kitchen table on a Sunday and announced he was doing something "the boring way," it meant she wouldn't see much of him until Wednesday. The boring way meant timelines. It meant taking every statement, every timestamp, every receipt, and laying them out like dominoes to see which ones fell the wrong direction.

He started a fresh page in his notebook.

*Day one.* Cardigan reported missing? No. That was the first thing. There was no break-in report. No missing property report. Nothing from Ines Varga saying *someone has been in my home*. When he'd asked her about it, sitting across from her in that little interview room with its water-stained ceiling tile, she'd told him she thought maybe she'd misplaced them. Misplaced a cardigan. Misplaced a scarf. Both at once. Both, coincidentally, ending up on a drowned woman.

People lied to him all day long; that was the job. But most liars lied *forward* — they built something up, added floors, hung curtains. Ines Varga had lied the way a person lies when lying is a language she barely speaks anymore. She'd gone small. She'd folded her hands and given him the shortest possible answers and looked at him with the expression of someone watching a car crash happen very slowly to somebody else.

And here was the thing that had kept him up half the night, staring at the ceiling while Chidi slept the sleep of the righteous: if you were going to frame a woman, would you steal her clothes and put them on your victim *before* anyone knew there was going to be a victim? Because that was what the timeline required. The cardigan had been in Dana Pell's closet — or wherever it lived between theft and drowning — for days. Maybe weeks. You don't steal a specific woman's clothes unless you already know how you're going to use them. Which meant either Ines Varga had planned a murder weeks in advance using items from her own wardrobe, which no murderer with a functioning frontal lobe does —

— or someone else had been inside her closet, and had known exactly what they were doing.

He turned the page.

*Day two through five.* The duplicate ledger page. Found tucked into Ines Varga's mattress, of all places. Not hidden in a drawer, not burned in a sink, not flushed — *in the mattress*, where any search warrant worth its salt would find it in eleven minutes flat. He'd watched her face when they'd pulled it out. He'd been watching faces professionally for twenty-two years, and hers hadn't done guilt. It had done something worse. It had done recognition. Like a woman finding a spider in her bed and understanding, in one cold flash, that the spider had been placed there.

Guilt flinches. Guilt performs outrage, or it performs collapse, or it performs calm a little too carefully. What Ines Varga had done was go very still and look at the page the way you'd look at a letter addressed to you in your own handwriting that you knew you never wrote.

Okafor set down his pen and rubbed his eyes.

Because that was the other thing. The handwriting. The lab had come back on the ledger page yesterday afternoon — the coded column, the initials, the dates. Partial match to Ines Varga's hand. Not a match. A *partial*. Enough for the DA, who heard "partial" and translated it directly to "close enough for government work." But Okafor had grown up copying his father's signature to sign his own report cards, and he knew what forged handwriting looked like. Forgers get the letter shapes right and the rhythm wrong. This page had all the right shapes and a rhythm like a metronome with a limp. Someone had practiced. Someone had practiced *well*.

Too well. That was the trouble. Every piece of evidence in this case was like a witness who'd rehearsed. The clothes, found exactly where a search would look. The ledger page, planted exactly where a search would find it. The motive — thin as communion wine, but present: neighbors recalled Varga was "odd," "kept to herself," "you never know about people who work with the dead." The whole case fit together like furniture from a showroom. Nothing scuffed. Nothing lived-in.

Real cases were never like this. Real cases had holes in the upholstery and coffee rings on the timeline. Real cases were a mess, because people were a mess, and even murderers were people.

This case had been *arranged*.

* * *

At nine-fifteen, Captain Reyes stuck his head into the bullpen with the expression of a man approaching a dog he suspected might bite.

"Okafor. Got a minute?"

"I've got forty," Okafor said, not looking up. "I'm budgeting."

Reyes came anyway, perching on the corner of the desk, which was a power move and also blocked Okafor's view of the timeline, which was possibly the actual move. "DA's office called again. They want to move on Varga by end of week."

"Mm."

"That's not an 'mm' kind of question, Emeka."

"It's the only answer I've got right now." Okafor finally looked up. Reyes was a decent man wearing a tired suit; twenty years of small-town policing did that to you, sanded you down until you were mostly procedure with a face attached. "Captain, can I ask you something off the record?"

"Nothing's off the record in an open bullpen."

"Then let's take a walk."

They walked. They walked the length of the hallway past the evidence lockers and the break room where somebody had microwaved fish again, an act of domestic terrorism Okafor intended to investigate separately. And out by the vending machine, under the hum of the fluorescent lights, he said it plainly.

"The Varga case is too clean."

Reyes's jaw tightened. "You've got her clothing on the victim."

"I've got her clothing on the victim, yes. Clothing that went missing without a break-in report, from a woman with no history of violence, in a town where a second body is about to surface if my gut's worth anything." He kept his voice level. "Somebody handed us this case, Captain. Wrapped it. Tied a bow on it. And I keep asking myself who has the patience to wrap a thing like that."

"You're saying she's being framed."

"I'm saying I don't know yet. I'm saying I know what a gift looks like, and this is one." He paused. "And I'm saying that whoever wrapped it knew our procedures better than most civilians do. Knew where we'd search. Knew what we'd weight. Knew we'd stop looking the moment we had a name."

Reyes was quiet for a long moment. The vending machine buzzed and clicked through its idle cycle, dreaming of candy bars.

"If you're wrong," Reyes said finally, "and she did it, and it comes out you sat on evidence because it felt too tidy —"

"Then I'll write my own suspension."

"And if you're right?"

Okafor looked down the hallway, toward the front doors, beyond which Ashcombe sat in its autumn light, all church steeples and leaf piles and founding-family names on bronze plaques. A town that had produced, in fifty years, an extraordinary number of accidental deaths among an extraordinarily small circle of people. He'd pulled the numbers last night, the boring way, at his kitchen table while Chidi watched him with the resigned affection of long marriage. Twelve deaths since 1971. All ruled accidents. All victims connected, however loosely, to the Historical Society's founding families. Statistically, it was like flipping heads twelve times. You could. Somebody always won the lottery.

But you checked the coin.

"Then we've got a bigger problem than one drowned woman," he said. "And I'd rather find that out now than after we've buried an innocent woman under our paperwork."

Reyes exhaled through his nose. "Off-book," he said at last, which was not permission but was adjacent to it, the way a doorway is adjacent to a room. "You hear me, Emeka? Off-book. No overtime requests, no subpoenas I have to explain, no interviews with town council members' cousins. You find something real, you bring it to me first, before the DA, before anybody. Something *real*. Paper. Chain of custody. Not feelings."

"Feelings are admissible in my kitchen," Okafor said. "Chidi takes them as evidence daily."

"Go do your boring way." Reyes pushed off the wall, then stopped. "For what it's worth — I hope you're right. About the frame. Because the alternative is that we've got a killer in this town who thinks like us."

"That's exactly what scares me, Captain," Okafor said. "She doesn't think like us. She thinks like the people we work *for*."

* * *

He started, sensibly, with the physical evidence — the way you'd start checking a coin by weighing it.

The duplicate ledger page sat in the evidence locker under seal, and he signed it out at ten o'clock with the slightly guilty pleasure of a man skipping a meeting. Back at his desk, he laid it under the desk lamp beside the photographs of the original ledger pages from Halloway & Sons, the ones the mortuary had surrendered voluntarily, Mr. Halloway wringing his hands the entire time as though the ledger were a family heirloom rather than a business record.

The paper was the first thing. He held the duplicate page up to the light. Good stock. Heavy, cream-colored, with a faint watermark he couldn't quite read without equipment. Then he pulled the request slip for the Historical Society's records — because of course he'd requested those too, weeks ago, back when this was still a simple drowning and he was still a simple detective — and compared.

Same stock. Same watermark. Same faintly yellowed edge, same weight in the hand.

Paper wasn't proof. Paper was a thread. But threads were how you did the boring way: you pulled one, and then another, and eventually either a sweater came apart or you discovered you'd been holding a rope the whole time.

He photographed everything with his personal phone — a habit he'd developed after the Delgado case, when a hard drive had mysteriously failed between intake and trial, and he'd sworn never again to trust a chain of custody he couldn't carry in his pocket. Then he made a list, because lists were prayer for men like him:

1. Paper stock matches Historical Society archives.
2. Handwriting: practiced forgery, rhythm wrong.
3. No break-in reported at Varga's residence — check building super, neighbors, door logs.
4. Who benefits from closing this case fast?
5. Dana Pell. Why her? Why now?

Item four was the one that made him uncomfortable, because the honest answer was *practically everyone*, in a town where the Historical Society's board read like a reunion of every family name on the war memorial. Item five was the one that made him sad, because Dana Pell had come to Ashcombe six weeks ago with a suitcase and a laptop and a reputation as a genealogist, and nobody — nobody — had asked what she was researching until after she was dead.

Okafor had asked. Late, but he'd asked. Her sister in Ohio had told him Dana took freelance research jobs, mostly tracing family trees for people who wanted to know where they came from. One recent client, the sister thought. Paid well. Kept Dana busy for a month. She didn't remember a name — "some local historical thing, she said it was delicate."

Delicate. Okafor wrote the word down and boxed it.

A genealogist hired to trace something delicate, in a town with twelve convenient accidents, dies in a reservoir wearing a borrowed cardigan, and the investigation closes around the nearest quiet woman like a fist.

He sat back. Down the hall, the bullpen was filling with the ordinary noises of an ordinary morning — phones, coffee, someone complaining about parking. The machinery of the case ground on toward Friday without him, well-oiled, pointed at Ines Varga like a lighthouse beam pointed at the wrong shore.

His phone buzzed. Chidi, texting him a photo of their daughter's science project — a baking-soda volcano, mid-eruption, foam everywhere — with the caption *she gets this from you.*

He smiled despite everything, typed back *that's all natural talent, no baking soda involved,* and then, because conscience is a thing you either feed or starve and he'd decided decades ago which one he did, he opened a new note on his phone, titled it with a single word — *offbook* — and began typing.

*Whoever framed Ines Varga needed three things: access to her apartment, access to Historical Society paper, and knowledge of police procedure. Start there. Start small. Start the boring way.*

He was reaching for his coat when the desk phone rang. The front desk clerk, young Ramirez, sounding puzzled.

"Detective? There's a guy down here says he needs to talk to whoever's running the Pell case. Podcaster, he says. Theo Fairweather?" A pause, and the particular tone of a young officer reading a name off a business card with suspicion. "He says he's been getting tips about Ashcombe for years. Says he thinks his anonymous source might be local."

Okafor stood very still.

An anonymous source. Tips, for years, about Ashcombe. About the accidents.

"Well," he said slowly, shrugging into his coat, feeling the first real pull of the thread go taut in his chest. "Tell him the boring way starts downstairs. And Ramirez — bring him up for coffee. I have a feeling this man knows more about this town than the town does."
