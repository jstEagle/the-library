## 4. The Ninth Stone

The cathedral kept its own hours after dark, and Coral Dravenmoor had long ago learned to move among them the way a fish moves through water—without displacement, without sound. He knew which floorboards in the north ambulatory sighed under weight and which held their breath. He knew that the third bay of the cloister walk whistled when the wind came from the fen, a thin note like a finger run along a wet glass rim. He knew how to carry a lantern so that its light fell only where he permitted it to fall.

He had not, in ten years of service, used this knowledge for anything but tidiness. That was the strange arithmetic of it: all his stealth, all his practiced invisibility, had been honed for the sake of dusting shelves at midnight without disturbing anyone's sleep or anyone's assumptions about what an archivist did between compline and dawn. Tonight he was using it to commit what was, by any honest accounting, a crime.

*Breaking into the crypt of Saint Verity's Cathedral,* he thought, easing the vestry door shut behind him until the latch kissed home without clicking. *If Ines Marchetti could see me now.*

But that was precisely the trouble. Ines Marchetti could see nothing, because Ines Marchetti had been dead—or vanished, which the town had always treated as a gentler synonym—for thirteen years. And yet her hand had moved across paper three years after the town buried her memory in its annual memorial file. The dead did not write. Which meant either that she had never died, or that someone wanted very badly for him to believe she had lived—and both possibilities frightened him more than the dark did.

He carried the diary against his chest inside his coat, wrapped in acid-free tissue like a relic, which in a sense it was. He had spent two days with it before daring this. Two days of comparing its script against the letters in the Marchetti accession file—the thank-you notes she'd written to parish donors, the reading lists she'd drafted for her fourth-formers, one postcard of the coast sent to Dean Fenn's predecessor with a wry line about gulls. The hand was unmistakable. The slight forward lean of the letters, the way the crossbar of the *t* drifted upward like something reaching, the idiosyncratic double loop in a capital *M* that looked almost like a bird in flight. No forger alive could reproduce that bird so consistently across forty pages without once growing tired of it.

And on the final page, in ink gone brown at the edges:

> *They think I am a story they finished telling. Let them. Stories can be reopened. When someone finally asks—not wonders, asks—I will answer from where they cannot look. Begin under the ninth stone.*

*Ninth stone.* He had turned the phrase over for two days the way one turns a hard sweet under the tongue, tasting all its angles. The crypt beneath Saint Verity's held no numbered stones, but it held a sequence: fourteen memorial slabs set into the floor of the lower vault, one for each prior of the cathedral's founding chapter, laid in order from the eastern wall westward. Anyone who had grown up in Hollowmere knew them the way children know the stations of a game—hopping across the cold names on dare days when the crypt door stood unlocked, which it no longer did, and had not for years.

The ninth slab belonged to Prior Aldous Vane.

Coral had checked that twice, then a third time, as though repetition might dissolve the coincidence. It did not. The ninth stone bore the name of the family whose descendants still sat in the front pews, still endowed the school where Ines had taught, still signed the annual donation ledger with a flourish that had not changed in a century except to grow smaller, as flourishes do when hands grow old.

He descended the crypt stairs now with the lantern hooded to a coin of light, and even so, the light found things he wished it hadn't.

Fresh mortar dust lay in the hollows of the treads.

He crouched, holding the lantern low, and examined it with the same attention he gave to foxing on a title page. Not ancient dust. Not the grey sift of centuries that collected in this place regardless of seals and doors. This was pale, almost white, still faintly gritty, ground into the tread of the second step and again on the fifth, and—when he swept his own sleeve carefully aside and bent until his knee nearly touched the stone—a smear of it on the iron hasp of the crypt door itself, as if someone had recently worked mortar near the frame. As if someone had recently *repaired* the frame, or pretended to.

Someone had been here. Recently. Perhaps within days. Perhaps within hours.

Coral remained crouched in the dark for a long moment, listening to his own pulse count out the seconds. The rational response—the response he would have counseled any patron, any visiting scholar, any version of himself that existed four days ago—was to go back up, lock the vestry, and petition the dean formally, with paperwork, in triplicate. Paperwork was armor. Paperwork was the whole architecture of his life.

But the dean had already refused him, with a sharpness that sat wrong in Coral's memory like a stone in a shoe. *The crypt is sealed for structural reasons, Mr. Dravenmoor. Structural reasons do not require your curiosity to be satisfied.* Ambrose Fenn had said it while looking at a point slightly to the left of Coral's head, which was unlike him; the dean's courtesy was always precisely aimed. And there had been that other thing, small and damning: a fleck of the same pale mortar on the dean's cuff, noticed and covered by the turn of a wrist, quick as a card sharp palming a queen.

So. The church was complicit, or the church was afraid, and in Hollowmere those had historically amounted to the same thing.

Coral rose, and went down.

* * *

The lower vault received him with the indifference of all deep places. The air tasted of cold limestone and old candle smoke and, beneath that, something mineral and sweet that he could never name—the smell, he supposed, of time itself going about its work. His lantern threw his shadow enormous against the barrel-vaulted ceiling, a giant hunched over a dollhouse of tombs, and he disliked the image enough to angle the light away.

Fourteen slabs. He counted them aloud under his breath, an old habit, numbering as prayer. One, Prior Edmund. Two, Prior Gervase. Three through seven, a litany of men whose virtues were carved in Latin and whose vices, if any, had been sanded away by the masons' tact. Eight, Prior Osric, whose effigy wore a smile that generations of children had sworn was a smirk.

Nine.

Prior Aldous Vane, 1611–1663, *Fidelis Servus et Veritatis Amator*. A faithful servant and lover of truth. Coral knelt beside the slab and felt the irony settle over him like the crypt's own cold.

The stone was flush with the floor, as the others were. But when he ran his palm across its surface—slowly, reading it the way Tamsin Pell had once taught him to read a lock, by feel rather than sight—he caught the difference immediately. The others had a certain give at their edges, a hairline breathing room where centuries of settling had loosened the mason's grip. The ninth slab sat tight. Too tight. Tight as something recently reset.

His heart began a slow, heavy percussion against his ribs.

He had come prepared; he was Coral Dravenmoor, and preparation was his native language. From his coat pocket he drew a thin steel palette knife, an archive tool intended for lifting brittle pages, and worked its edge into the seam at the slab's western corner. The blade sank a full quarter inch and stopped against something that was not mortar. Lead. A poured seal, soft enough to yield, deliberate enough to mean *keep out*—or perhaps, he thought with a lurch, *keep safe*.

He worked by degrees. The lead came away in grey curls that he collected in an envelope without thinking, cataloguing even now, even here, because the habit was the man. Then the slab shifted—no lever required, only a steady pull—and slid a hand's width sideways on some hidden channel, grinding softly, revealing a cavity beneath.

A cavity lined with lead sheeting, dry as a sermon.

Empty.

Not entirely empty. Coral lowered the lantern into the dark and saw, resting in the exact center of the cavity, a single sheet of paper folded in quarters, and atop it, a wax rubbing.

He took them out with gloved hands, though the gloves were more ritual than necessity now, and unfolded the rubbing under the light. It was done in black shoemaker's wax, the kind sold in sticks at the ironmonger's on Cooper Lane, pressed over carved or engraved lettering to lift its form. What it lifted was a grid. Columns of figures, column headings in a cramped mercantile hand, dates running down the left margin in a sequence spanning—he tilted the light, checked, checked again—nearly forty years. Payments. Sums. Initials. The columns marched down the page like a regiment, and at the bottom, where a total should have been, someone had instead written a single word in the same cramped hand:

*ENOUGH.*

No ledger. No original document. Only this ghost of one, this fossil impression, left behind like a calling card.

And beneath it, the folded paper. He opened it and found a note, unsigned, undated, the handwriting brisk and unlovely:

*You are late. Whoever you are, you are years late. The true book is elsewhere. She told us where, but we were followed, so we moved it. If you have the diary, read the margins. Count what counts. — A friend of hers, once.*

Coral sat back on his heels in the cold and let the two feelings arrive together, because they refused to be separated. Dread first, cold and specific: someone else knew. Someone else had opened this stone, taken the ledger, left this message—and *we*, the note said, *we were followed*, which meant there were others still, watchers, the machinery of the founding families grinding on as it had ground for decades, and he had just committed burglary in their cathedral above their families' stone. His invisibility, his one great talent, was spending itself fast.

And underneath the dread, rising through it like heat through floorboards, exhilaration. Because the note said *she*. *She told us where.* Ines had spoken to these people. Ines had organized this hiding place, this fallback, this message-in-a-bottle addressed to whoever came asking—which meant that when she wrote her last entry, whenever and however she had written it, she had not been writing from the grave. She had been writing from hiding. The dead did not write, and therefore—

Therefore she might not be dead.

The thought hit him physically, a warmth behind the sternum so sudden it was nearly pain. Thirteen years of memorial files, of annual remembrance, of the town's tidy grief rehearsed every autumn in the cathedral he kept—and she might have been alive the whole time, watching them mourn her, letting them, because mourning was the safest disguise ever devised. *They think I am a story they finished telling. Let them.*

"Read the margins," the note said. "Count what counts."

Coral looked down at the diary where it lay against his knee, wrapped in its tissue, patient as a sleeping animal. Margins. He thought of the small marks he had noticed and dismissed—faint ticks, dots, occasional short strokes in the outer margins of certain entries, which he had taken for the idle pen of a teacher accustomed to marking exercises. He had catalogued them mentally as *incidental annotations, non-textual*.

Count what counts.

Above him, far above, the cathedral settled and ticked in the night cold, its stones conversing in the language of contraction. Coral gathered the rubbing, the note, the diary, and the lead curls, and rose. On impulse—an impulse so foreign to his nature that he afterward wondered whether Ines had infected him through mere proximity to her handwriting—he took the wax rubbing and pressed it flat into the empty cavity, face down, weighted with a pebble from the windowsill of the stair. A reply of sorts. *Received. Understood. Still asking.*

Then he climbed toward the dark nave, moving without sound, a man carrying the beginning of a map out of a tomb—and behind him, at the top of the crypt stairs, in the black vestibule where no light reached and no one had entered, something shifted. The softest scrape of a shoe on stone. There, and gone.

Coral stood motionless on the bottom step, lantern hooded, breath shallow, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever came next, he was no longer the only person in Saint Verity's who knew how to move unseen.
