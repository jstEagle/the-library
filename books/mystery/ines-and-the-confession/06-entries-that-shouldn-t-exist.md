## 6. Entries That Shouldn't Exist

Coral Dravenmoor had a system for reading dangerous documents. He had developed it over ten years of handling the cathedral's secrets—the wills that disinherited, the letters that accused, the parish registers with pages razored out. The system was simple: gloves, good light, and a strict refusal to read ahead. A document was a territory; one entered it at the border and walked it in order. Skipping ahead was how archivists contaminated evidence, how they saw patterns that were only shadows of their own wanting.

He broke his own system that night.

He told himself it was methodology. He told himself that a diary whose final entry postdated its author's disappearance by three years required a different protocol—that one must first establish the extent of the anomaly, map its boundaries, before proceeding in an orderly fashion. This was true, and it was also a lie, and Coral knew the difference the way he knew the difference between the cathedral's two clocks, the one in the tower that kept honest time and the one in the vestry that had run four minutes fast since 1911 because no one had ever admitted the error. He was lying to himself with the same precision he applied to everything, and the lie had a shape, and the shape was hope.

The reading room of the archive wing was his after ten o'clock. He had the keys, all of them, and the habit of the place so deeply worn into him that his body navigated it without light—the three steps down from the corridor, the turn at the map cabinet, the particular reluctance of the reading room door, which swelled in damp weather and required a hip applied at the correct angle. He did not turn on the main lamps. He sat in the circle of a single green-shaded desk lamp, the diary before him on a foam cradle, and he turned past the final entry—the one dated three years after Ines Marchetti walked out of Hollowmere and into the fog of every unsolved thing—and he began, at the border, to walk the territory backward.

Because that was the thing about an impossible document. If the last page was impossible, then the question was not *what does the last page say* but *when does the impossible begin*. Somewhere between the entry everyone assumed was her last—the damp, hurried scrawl of a Tuesday in October, the one the police had photographed and the coroner had initialed—and the entry that could not exist, there had to be a seam. A place where the handwriting stopped being hers, or the dates stopped being true, or the woman stopped being dead.

He read backward through the autumn of her disappearance. The entries were ordinary and increasingly strained. *The Vane boy was at the market again, watching me. I have begun to take the river path.* *Judge Harrow's clerk came to the school asking questions about my attendance book. I told him nothing, because there was nothing to tell, and he looked at me the way one looks at a locked drawer.* *I dreamed of the fen again. In the dream the water is warm, which is the worst part.*

Coral's hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was the one inheritance from his father that he had not resented. But behind the steadiness something was moving, the way water moves under ice—unseen, insistent, going somewhere.

* * *

The diary itself was a cheap thing, and Coral had noted this from the first with a professional's disapproval that was really a kind of tenderness. A schoolteacher's salary in Hollowmere did not run to fine bindings. The cover was cloth over board, once green, now the color of old moss, and the spine had been repaired with surgical tape at some point in its life—a hasty, competent mend, the kind a practical woman does at a kitchen table with the tape pulled between her teeth. The pages were ruled, the ink was the same blue-black throughout, a common brand Coral could still buy at Wren's stationers on the high street, and the handwriting was the handwriting he had verified against four archived letters until two in the morning: the small, forward-leaning script with its distinctive single-story *a* and the way she crossed her *t*'s with a single impatient stroke that sometimes caught the next word.

The dead do not write. He had thought it the previous night, standing in the archive's cold with the diary open under lamplight, and he thought it again now, and this time he made himself look at it squarely, the way one looks at a dog in the road. The dead do not write, and therefore either Ines Marchetti was not dead, or someone had spent years forging her hand in a dead woman's diary for reasons that would have to be more elaborate than any reason he could imagine, and Coral Dravenmoor, who had spent a decade imagining the elaborate reasons of other people, could imagine quite a lot.

He turned pages backward, past the last assumed entry, past the police evidence tag's ghost—there was a faint rectangle of adhesive residue on the flyleaf where the tag had been, and he had already photographed that too, because residue was testimony—and into the entries that followed the disappearance. That was the wrong way to say it, he corrected himself. There were no entries that *followed* the disappearance. There were only entries, continuous, in the same ink, in the same hand, dated in an unbroken sequence across the very weeks in which the whole town of Hollowmere had agreed, formally, officially, with affidavits and a coroner's verdict of *presumed drowned, body unrecovered*, that Ines Marchetti had ceased to be.

The first post-disappearance entry was dated four days after she vanished.

*The fog has not lifted in three days. I have decided to write anyway. It seems absurd to stop now, when the diary is the only conversation I can trust. If I am to be a missing woman, I shall at least be a missing woman with accurate records.*

Coral read it four times.

The tone was hers. That was the thing no forger could have managed, he thought, and then immediately: *that is exactly the thing a forger would study*, and the two thoughts circled each other like cats. The dryness of it, the small joke turned against catastrophe—*a missing woman with accurate records*—that was Ines Marchetti's voice as he had come to know it across a decade of cataloguing her surviving papers: lesson plans with wry marginal notes, a letter to the school board objecting to the condition of the girls' lavatories with a politeness so precise it was nearly a weapon. A woman who met fear with exactitude.

*If I am to be a missing woman.* Not *if I am dead*. Not *if they have taken me*. *If I am to be*—as though it were a role she had accepted, a costume she had put on, with the diary the one mirror in which she could still see her own face.

He sat back in the green light and let the cathedral sounds move around him: the settling of old timber, the tick of the vestry clock carried faintly through the walls, four minutes fast, always four minutes fast. Outside, October rain had begun against the high windows, a sound like static, like the sea, like the fen.

The fen. She had dreamed of the fen, in the last weeks of her known life, and the water in the dream was warm, and the coroner had ruled the fen had taken her.

* * *

He read through the night, and he did not permit himself to read ahead, and this time he kept the discipline—not out of method now but out of something closer to courtesy. These were her days. However impossible, they had been lived in order, and she had written them in order, and he would receive them in order, the way one receives a letter, the way one receives a confidence.

The entries that should not exist were, one after another, the most ordinary entries he had ever catalogued. That was their horror and their unbearable sweetness: their ordinariness.

*Rain all week. The gutters on this house sing a different song than the school ones—lower, more patient. I am learning the patience of gutters.*

*I went to market on Thursday. It is extraordinary what one can buy in a small town when one is no one. I bought apples and a wool scarf and no one looked at me twice, and I nearly wept over the apples, which the seller must have thought very strange.*

*A dog has adopted me. He is brown and disreputable and he has decided, with the absolute conviction of dogs, that I am his. I have named him Bracken because he found me in the bracken, and because I am not feeling original these days.*

Coral read the entry about the dog three times, and on the third reading he understood that he was smiling, and that the smile hurt his face, because his face was not used to it. Bracken. A brown disreputable dog who had chosen her. He catalogued the detail without meaning to—*dog, brown, mixed breed, self-appointed, named for bracken*—because cataloguing was how he loved things, though he had never before had occasion to love a detail in a document that should not exist.

She was alive. The entries said it in market days and gutter-song and a dog's loyalty; they said it in the small accumulating texture of a life continuing—*the woman next door leaves milk out for Bracken, which is against every rule I have ever taught, and I have stopped correcting her*—they said it in the way the handwriting changed over the months, loosening, the impatient *t*-crossings relaxing into something almost leisurely, as though the hand itself were healing. She had not died in the fen. She had gone somewhere—some other town, some other house with patient gutters—and she had lived, hidden, ordinary, watched by no one, and she had kept her records.

And the question that rose in Coral then was not *how* and not *who helped her* and not even *why*, though all of those questions stood in line behind it. The question that rose first, hot and shameful and clarifying, was: *then why did no one look for her?*

Because someone had helped her. That was arithmetically certain. A woman does not vanish from a town where she is known, does not cross the fen road and the county line and establish a life, without hands. Someone had driven her, or hidden her, or forged something, or looked away at the right moment. And the same machinery that had failed to find her—the constabulary that searched the fen for six days and then stopped, the coroner who wrote *presumed drowned* with a body unrecovered, which was a verdict Coral now saw was less a conclusion than a door being closed—the same machinery had perhaps not failed at all. Perhaps it had *worked*. Precisely as designed.

He thought of the fresh mortar dust on the crypt stairs, and of Dean Fenn's face when Coral had asked about the sealed crypt—Fenn, who was a mild and careful man, going sharp as a slammed drawer—and he filed the thought where he filed dangerous thoughts, in the drawer that did not officially exist.

* * *

It was nearly four in the morning when he reached the entry that changed the temperature of everything.

He had been reading for hours in the small warm circle of the lamp, and the entries had settled into a rhythm that was almost domestic, almost safe—market and rain, Bracken's various crimes, a winter of small observations, a spring. She had been in her hiding place a year and a half by then. The handwriting had gone easy. And then:

*They have found me.*

Coral's breath stopped. Not literally—he was a man whose body did not indulge in drama—but the reading room went very quiet around him, the way a room goes quiet when a door opens somewhere below.

*Not certainly. Not yet. But there was a car on the lane yesterday, twice, and this morning a man asked at the shop about houses to let, and the shopkeeper told me he had a southern accent, and there are not many southern men with an interest in this village's empty cottages.*

*I have been a fool. I thought distance was safety. I thought they would grieve a while and then forget, because I was only a schoolteacher and what I saw was only a moment on a dark road. But the Vanes do not forget. The Vane brothers do not forget, and they do not forgive, and what I saw in the fen that night was worth more than a schoolteacher's silence, and I have been sitting here for eighteen months writing about apples and gutters while they decided whether I was worth the trouble of finding.*

*Bracken growled at the car. Dogs know.*

*If this is the last entry, then whoever reads it—I am sorry to have made you the keeper of an unfinished record. I always taught my girls that a sentence should be completed. Forgive me this one.*

Coral set the diary down on the foam cradle with the exaggerated care of a man handling something that might go off.

The Vane brothers. The name sat on the page in her small forward-leaning hand, and it sat in Coral's memory too, because everyone in Hollowmere knew the name Vane the way one knows the name of the weather: the family that had endowed the cathedral's restoration, the family whose name was on the hospital wing and the school prize and the brass plaque by the west door. *Given in memory of Edmund Vane, 1921.* Generosity, generations deep, woven into the town's stone.

And Ines Marchetti, writing in fear eighteen months into her hidden life, had written *the Vane brothers* the way one writes the name of a thing that hunts.

He read the entry again, and then the entries after it, because the diary did not end there—the diary went on, page after page, more entries that should not exist, and Coral forced himself through them with his heart going hard and slow. There was a week of terse entries—*Still here. Car gone. Perhaps.*—and then the ordinariness resumed, but changed, the way a house changes after a burglary even when nothing is taken. She wrote about Bracken. She wrote about rain. But now there were other sentences folded in, small and cold as needles: *I have begun to sleep with my shoes by the bed.* *I wrote to no one today, which is my custom, and my safety.* And once, in the margin, in letters so small they were nearly a secret from herself: *the ninth stone*.

Coral went still.

He had seen the phrase before. It was the reason he had petitioned Dean Fenn, the reason he had gone down to the crypt at night with a torch and a chisel and found the ninth stone hollow and empty, violated before he ever reached it—empty except for a wax rubbing, columns of a merchant cipher, someone having gotten there first. He had thought *the ninth stone* was a destination. A hiding place. A thing one went to.

But here it was in the margin of a diary entry dated years into her hiding, written by a woman a hundred miles from Hollowmere, and she had not gone to the ninth stone. She had *written* it. The way one writes an address. The way one writes *in case*.

The ninth stone was not where she had hidden something. It was where she had *told* someone something could be found—told them in the only place she could be sure would survive: a diary. And the diary had come back to Hollowmere. The diary had come back to him.

Coral looked at the green circle of lamplight, at the rain against the windows, at the diary lying open on its cradle like a small patient animal, and he understood that he was no longer cataloguing a mystery. He was inside one. Ines Marchetti had spent years being no one, buying apples unremarked, learning the patience of gutters—and she had left a trail anyway, threaded through ordinary days, for a reader she could not name and could not be sure would ever exist. *Whoever reads it.* She had written to the future, and the future had a name, and the name was his.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere above him the tower clock turned over the hour, four minutes fast, and Coral Dravenmoor, invisible man, keeper of other people's secrets, closed the diary with hands that were still steady and a mind that was no longer still at all.

The Vane brothers. He would begin, in the morning, with the Vanes.

And he would begin, he thought as he turned off the lamp and sat a moment in the dark, by finding out which of the Vane brothers was still alive—and why, of all the weeks in ten years, a lawyer had chosen this one to come asking for his files.
