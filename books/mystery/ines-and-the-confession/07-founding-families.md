## 7. Founding Families

The archive at Saint Verity's kept its own weather. Coral had long ago stopped noticing it—the chill that pooled in the lower stacks like water in a cellar, the dry warmth above the map cases where the radiators hissed, the particular smell of old rag paper and leather and, beneath it all, the faint mineral breath of stone. It was the smell of the town's memory, and he had breathed it for ten years the way other men breathed sea air: without thought, until it became the definition of breath itself.

Tonight he noticed it. Tonight everything was noticed.

The wax rubbing lay flat on the reading table beneath a sheet of glass he had requisitioned for exactly this purpose, though until three days ago he could not have said what purpose that was. The columns rose off the paper in graphite ghost-script, merchant cipher rendered into a merchant's grammar: dates, initials, sums. Tamsin Pell had deciphered the notation system in an evening, with the offhand competence of someone picking a lock, and had gone home to her uncle's workshop leaving Coral alone with the thing that had been hidden under the ninth stone for—how long? The earliest legible date said 1961. Ines Marchetti had vanished in 1994. The ledger, if the rubbing told the truth, was older than the crime it concealed.

He had not slept. He had not wanted to. Sleep would have meant setting the thing down, and he was not certain he could pick it up again with the same hands.

So he worked. Work was the shape his courage took; it had always been. He pulled the finding aids the way a surgeon pulls instruments, and the archive answered him, because the archive always answered him. That was the one covenant of his life: ask the collection a true question, and it would not lie.

He began with the column headings.

* * *

There were four of them, repeated down the rubbing's length in the cipher's blocky hand. Three were initials. The fourth was a word: *FUND*.

The initials were V., Q., and H.

Any schoolchild in Hollowmere could have told him what those stood for, and indeed schoolchildren were taught it, in the cheerful civic history unit that occupied the first six weeks of every autumn term: the Founding Families, the three names carved into the cathedral's west lintel and the town's self-regard alike. The Vanes, who had built the wool exchange and half the terraces above the fen. The Quills, who had endowed the school where Ines Marchetti taught, and the almshouses, and the annual harvest festival that still closed the high street every October. And the Harrows, who had given the town its courthouse, its magistracy, and—generation upon generation—a judge, as reliably as other families gave sons to the sea.

Coral had catalogued their papers. That was the thing he could not stop circling as he worked: he had catalogued all of them. He knew the texture of their generosity the way he knew the grain of the oak shelving. He had spent a decade describing, box by box, folder by folder, the philanthropy of Hollowmere's founding families—the endowment deeds, the subscription lists, the thank-you letters in copperplate from orphanages and libraries and the cathedral roof fund itself. He had admired the order of it. The neatness. He had thought, in his unexamined way, that neatness was a kind of virtue.

He went to the Vane papers first.

* * *

The Vane deposit had come to the archive in 1978, forty-one boxes of it, when the wool exchange closed and the family seat above the fen passed out of direct Vane hands. Coral knew the finding aid by heart—had written half of it himself during his first years, when his predecessor, old Mr. Aldous, still ruled the stacks like a mild ghost. He pulled box 17: financial correspondence, 1958 to 1971.

The letters were exactly what they appeared to be. That was the first lesson of the night, and he wrote it in the margin of his working notes in his small, upright hand: *the ledger does not contradict the archive; it completes it.*

Here was Edmund Vane, 1961, writing to the cathedral bursar about the annual contribution to the *Poor of Hollowmere Fund*—a discretionary charity, administered by the dean, distributed at the dean's sole discretion, no accounting required. Here was the bursar's receipt. Here, the following year, the same letter in the same hand, the sum increased. Here, in 1964, a letter Coral had catalogued without a second glance: Edmund Vane requesting that the Fund's purpose be broadened to include *expenses arising from regrettable incidents, at the Dean's judgment.* Regrettable incidents. He had read that phrase a hundred times and filed it under charity administration, series B, and moved on.

The Fund. The fourth column heading.

Coral sat very still in the pool of his reading lamp and let the sentence assemble itself in him, clause by clause, the way he would assemble a catalogue entry—except this entry had no series number and would never be described in any finding aid he controlled.

The town's charity was a payment schedule. The Poor of Hollowmere Fund—the fund that had kept the almshouses heated, that had paid the school's coal bills, that had bought boots for fatherless children every winter for as long as anyone could remember—was funded, year upon year, by three families in sums that matched, to the shilling, the three initialled columns of a ledger hidden in a crypt. And the fourth column, the *FUND* column, was the cathedral's own hand reaching out to receive it.

He checked. He was Coral Dravenmoor; he checked. He pulled the bursarial receipts, 1961 through 1989, and laid them against Tamsin's transcription of the rubbing, and the sums married like well-matched pages. 1961: twelve pounds ten from V., eight from Q., fifteen from H. The almshouse coal was bought that winter with exactly thirty-five pounds ten. He found the invoice. He found, filed behind it with bureaucratic tenderness, a note in the bursar's hand: *Distributed per the Dean's discretion. No itemization required, per the Founders' wishes.*

No itemization required.

The archive had told him the truth for forty years. It had simply told it in the language archives speak—invoices and receipts and the silence between file headings—and no one had thought to ask it a true question until a dead woman's diary crawled out of a donation box and asked it for him.

* * *

He worked until the cathedral clock struck two, and then he kept working, because the alternative was to sit in the dark with what he knew, and Coral Dravenmoor had spent his whole life arranging never to sit in the dark with anything.

The Quill papers were next, and they were worse, because the Quills had been kind. That was the discovery that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: the cruelty was not the hard part. The hard part was that it was braided through with genuine goodness, the way rot is braided through a beam and holds it up even as it eats it.

Margaret Quill's correspondence, 1970s and 80s, showed a woman who visited the almshouses weekly, who knew the residents' names and their grandchildren's names, who fought the district council for a proper nurse when the fund's own doctor retired. The same Margaret Quill whose initial headed a column in the crypt ledger. The same fund. In 1987—Coral found the letter and read it four times—Margaret Quill wrote to Dean Aldous's predecessor about *the Marchetti matter*, requesting assurance that *the annual provision be continued for the girl's mother, who is not to know its source.*

The girl's mother.

Ines Marchetti's mother had lived until 1991 in the almshouses on Quill Row—Coral checked the admission registers; she had been admitted in 1988, two years before she died of her heart, and her rent had been marked *remitted, benefactor's instruction*. The town had whispered, after Ines vanished, that the Quills were good to the grieving mother, that it was the mark of a Christian town, that the founding families never forgot their own.

They had paid her. Month by month, out of the column with the Q at its head, they had paid her the wage of her silence—except she had nothing to be silent about; she had only a daughter who was gone and a town that had decided how gone. The money was not hush for her. It was hush for everyone who watched her take it. It made the town complicit in the shape of charity, made every warm coal in every almshouse grate a small, unanswerable argument for not asking questions.

Coral put the letter down and found that his hands were not entirely steady, and he regarded them with a kind of clinical interest, as though they belonged to a catalogued object whose condition he was obliged to record. *Hands, Coral's: trembling. Provenance: unknown. Access: restricted.*

* * *

Judge Harrow was the column he had dreaded, and the Harrow papers confirmed the dread with the dull efficiency of a verdict.

The Harrow deposit was smaller—the family had kept its private papers at the courthouse until 2003, when the last Judge Harrow, Sir Edmund Harrow the fourth, had retired and, in a fit of what the accession file called *civic housekeeping*, sent over six boxes of what he evidently considered the harmless residue. Coral had catalogued them himself, in his fourth year. He remembered the boxes smelled of pipe tobacco and the particular pomade old judges used.

Sir Edmund the fourth was still alive. Ninety-one years old now, living in the tall grey house on Harrow Hill, still writing the occasional letter to the *Hollowmere Gazette* about the decay of standards. And it was his father's, and grandfather's, initials that headed the third column—H., fifteen pounds, twenty pounds, in some later years fifty, the sums swelling through the 1980s like a fever chart.

Coral pulled the box of magisterial correspondence and found what he had catalogued five years ago without seeing: a 1972 letter from Judge Aldous Harrow the third to the cathedral bursar, discussing the Fund, in which the judge observed that *the arrangement has served us all admirably and I trust will continue to do so, whatever the diocesan auditors may mutter.* Whatever the diocesan auditors may mutter. The arrangement. Not the charity—the arrangement. A man does not call a gift an arrangement unless the gift has terms.

And there, in the 1994 box—the year of Ines Marchetti—Coral found the file he had described in his own finding aid, in his own handwriting, five years ago, as: *Misc. judicial correspondence, incl. inquest summaries, 1993–1996.*

The inquest summaries. He had read them once, long ago, in his first sweep of the collection, and they had meant nothing: an unidentified man recovered from the fen in the autumn of 1994, drowning ruled accidental, inquest opened and closed in a single afternoon, presided over by Judge Harrow in his capacity as coroner—an old arrangement, the county coroner's office having been a Harrow sinecure for three generations. *Unidentified male, approx. 40–50 years. No next of kin traced. Verdict: accidental drowning.* Signed. Filed. Catalogued by Coral Dravenmoor under *Judicial, misc., 1994*, and not thought of again by any living soul.

Except one. Except the schoolteacher who had seen something in the fen in the autumn of 1994, who had written in a diary that should not exist, and who had vanished six weeks later.

Coral laid the pages side by side on the glass: the inquest summary, the rubbing, the diary's final dated entry with its single terrified line. *The Vane brothers know I know.* The dates arranged themselves without his help. The man in the fen in October. Ines's last entry in the true chronology, November. Her disappearance, December. And running beneath all of it, patient as groundwater, the Fund—thirty-three years of payments, three families, one cathedral, and a judge whose signature turned a drowned stranger into an accident with the stroke of a pen.

The town had not been protected by its founders. The town had been purchased by them, annually, in advance, and the purchase price had been distributed to the poor as a dividend, so that every soul in Hollowmere held a small share in the silence and none of them knew it.

He understood, then, with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like frost—the cold clarity the night had been building toward all along—why the diary had been hidden in the crypt. Not for secrecy. Ines could have buried a diary anywhere in the fen and no one would ever have found it. She had hidden it under the ninth stone because the ninth stone was above the ledger, and the ledger was the proof, and she had wanted the two things to be found together, by someone, someday, by whoever was patient enough to read the town's own paper trail. She had hidden the truth in the one place in Hollowmere that was contractually, endowingly, financially incapable of looking at it.

And the cathedral had kept her secret the way it kept all its secrets: reverently, and for a fee.

* * *

The clock struck four. Coral became aware, gradually, of the light going grey-blue at the tall east windows, and of his own body's complaints—stiff neck, dry eyes, the headache that lived just behind them when he worked too long—and he catalogued these too, because cataloguing was how he kept the world at a survivable distance, and tonight the world needed to be very far away indeed.

He gathered his working notes, the rubbing, the diary, and locked them in the document case, and then he sat for a while with his hands flat on the reading table, in the silence of the archive, which was not silent at all but murmuring, as it always had, with the paper breath of ten thousand documents that had been telling the truth all along to anyone who asked.

He thought about Edmund Vane's phrase—*regrettable incidents*—and Margaret Quill's—*the girl's mother, who is not to know its source*—and Judge Harrow's—*whatever the auditors may mutter*—and he understood that he was looking at three generations of people who had never once, in thirty-three years, written a sentence they did not intend to survive them. They had been careful. They had been legible. They had hidden their crime in plain archival sight, in the one place where hiding and preserving were the same act, because they had understood something Coral was only now learning: that archives do not forget, but neither do they accuse. They wait. They wait for a reader.

He was the reader. It had taken a dead woman's impossible diary to make him one, but he was the reader now, and the collection had answered his true question, and it would go on answering, box by box, for as long as he had the nerve to ask.

The nerve. That was the variable the ledger had never accounted for. Thirty-three years of careful men, and not one of them had planned for a reclusive archivist with a locksmith's apprentice and a locked document case and nothing left to lose except the habit of invisibility.

Coral stood, stiffly, and began to carry the case toward the inner office, where it would sleep behind two locks until morning. He was halfway across the reading room when he saw the slip of paper on his desk—the desk he had locked, he was certain he had locked, before he went down to the crypt three nights ago—and he stopped.

It was a calling card, heavy cream stock, and it bore a name he did not know and a firm of solicitors he did: *Pemberton & Vane, of the Inner Close.*

Vane.

The card was blank on its reverse except for a single line in a lawyer's small, immaculate hand, and Coral read it twice before the words settled into meaning, cold as the fen in October:

*Requesting a routine appointment regarding restricted access. — R.P.*

Routine. Restricted. The vocabulary of the ledger, arriving on cream card at four in the morning, in an office he had locked.

Coral Dravenmoor stood in the grey light with the card in his steady hands, and understood that the archive had stopped waiting, and the town had started reading him.
