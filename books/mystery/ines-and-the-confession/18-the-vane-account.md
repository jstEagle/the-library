## 18. The Vane Account

The archive at three in the morning had a different voice than the archive by day. By day it murmured—dust settling, radiators ticking, the distant complaint of the town through stone. At three it was silent in the way of a held breath, and Coral Dravenmoor, who had spent ten years learning to be as quiet as his shelves, found that he no longer wanted to be quiet. He wanted noise. He wanted witnesses. He wanted the whole cathedral to hear what he was about to read.

He worked under a single green-shaded lamp at the long table in the reading room, the true ledger open before him beside Ines's second journal, Tamsin's cipher key copied out in her careful apprentice's hand. The fire had taken the east wing—the catalogue room, forty years of finding aids, the donated boxes that had started everything—but it had not taken this. He had carried the diary and both journals out in a document case while the smoke peeled the paint from the corridor walls, and now they lay on the table like survivors of a shipwreck, water-spotted, smelling faintly of char, entirely legible.

*Paper burns,* the anonymous note had said. *Archives burn easily.*

But ledgers, Coral thought, turning a page with a conservator's tenderness, are written twice. Once in ink and once in habit. And habit does not burn.

* * *

Tamsin had cracked the merchant cipher on the second night after the fire, working cross-legged on the reading room floor with the wax rubbing from the ninth stone propped against her knee like a guitar she didn't know how to play. The columns were dates and initials; the amounts were rendered in an old Hollowmere grain measure, quarters and bushels, so that a payment read not as money but as harvest—as though the founding families had convinced themselves, all these years, that they were paying in wheat.

"It's a tithing book," Tamsin had said, looking up at him with soot still shadowed in the creases of her knuckles. "That's the joke of it. They called it charity. Saint Verity's Poor Fund. Every payment is logged as alms." She tapped the column heading. *"Alms disbursed for the quiet of the parish."* Somebody wrote that down. Somebody sat at a desk like yours and wrote *for the quiet of the parish* and went home to supper."

Now, alone, Coral did what he had been trained to do and had spent a decade doing too well: he followed the paper.

The earliest entries predated Ines Marchetti by eleven years. Small sums, irregular—a quarter here, two bushels there—marked with initials that matched nothing in the parish rolls until he cross-referenced the vestry minutes and found them: **V** for Vane, **Q** for Quill, **H** for Harrow. Payments to unnamed recipients. *For services rendered to the parish.* For silence, he understood, was also a service, and Hollowmere had always paid its servants well.

Then, on the fourteenth of October, thirty-one years ago, the ledger changed character. The sums grew. The entries clustered—one, then three, then seven within a fortnight—and the initials shifted from the payers to the paid. And there, dated the night after the drowned man had been pulled from the fen and buried under Judge Harrow's tidy verdict of *misadventure*, Coral found the entry he had been dreading and seeking since the reliquary wall opened:

***One bushel, four quarters — to the ferryman's boy, for discretion re: the fen road. — V.***

The ferryman's boy. Coral closed his eyes and saw the old coroner's map again, the fen road running along the water's edge, the only approach to the reed beds where a body could be weighted and slipped without a lantern being seen. Someone had watched. Someone young enough to be bought cheap.

And below it, in a different hand—heavier, more deliberate, the letters pressed deep enough to emboss the next page:

***Two quarters — to E.Q., for the escorting of Miss M. beyond the county line, expenses settled, all accounts closed. — H.***

E.Q. Edith Quill.

Coral sat very still. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere above him, in the tower, a pigeon rearranged itself against the dark.

He had assumed—I think we all would have assumed—that Edith Quill's name in the ledger meant complicity. That she had paid, or been paid, or held her tongue with the rest of them. But the entry said *escorting*. It said *beyond the county line*. It said *all accounts closed*, which in the grammar of men like Harrow meant *she will never come back*, and yet here was Ines's handwriting in a diary dated years past her disappearance, describing rain on a window, a dog named Bracken, bread rising on a sill.

Edith Quill had not silenced Ines Marchetti. Edith Quill had driven her out of Hollowmere in the night and then spent thirty years holding a letter she was paid to hold, waiting for someone—anyone—to finally ask.

*They paid her to keep the secret,* Coral thought, *and she kept the secret, and the secret she kept was that Ines was alive.*

He laughed once, a short, disbelieving sound that the reading room swallowed whole. The conspiracy was unraveling, but it was not unraveling into a single villain. It was unraveling into people. Into choices. The Vanes who killed and paid. The judge who ruled and signed. A dean's predecessor who burned evidence with shaking hands and told himself it was duty. A ferryman's boy who took a bushel of grain and carried the weight of it for thirty years. And one dying woman in a house full of dog photographs who had looked at the money in her palm and decided that the only way to spend it honestly was to buy a schoolteacher's life with it.

Good and bad, braided together like the roots of the cathedral yew. You could not pull one without disturbing all the others.

* * *

He worked until the windows went grey, and when Tamsin arrived at seven with two paper cups of tea and a baker's bag of lardy cakes, she found him surrounded by a constellation of index cards, each one a person, each card threaded to the others with red string that made the table look like a detective's fever dream or a very strange Christmas.

"You look terrible," she said, setting down his cup.

"I look like a man who has finished something."

"Finished decoding?"

"Finished understanding." He pulled the critical cards forward. "Look. The payments don't stop after Ines leaves. They continue for six more years—smaller, routine. Maintenance. Whoever received them—" he tapped the recurring initial, **F.B.**, the ferryman's boy grown into a ferryman's man— "was being retained. Not thanked. Retained. In case anyone ever asked questions."

"And then it stops."

"Six years after the drowning. Which means either he died, or he left, or—" Coral hesitated over the last card, the one he had written last night at some hour he couldn't name, "—or someone decided the account was settled. There's a final entry. One line. No amount. Just: ***Account of A. Vane considered closed by mutual agreement. — E.Q.***"

Tamsin set down her tea. "Edith closed the account?"

"She signed it. Whatever happened between the Vanes and the ferryman—whether he threatened to talk, whether they threatened him—it ended with Edith Quill's signature on the line. She wasn't just the escort, Tamsin. She was the auditor. The one honest clerk inside a dishonest office. They needed someone to hold the letter, someone to close the accounts, someone whose word even Judge Harrow couldn't question, because she was a Quill." He shook his head slowly. "They gave her power because they thought her loyalty was purchased. And she used every ounce of that purchased power to keep one woman breathing."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. Then: "My mum says Edith used to leave bread on the churchyard wall for the tramps. Everyone thought she was soft in the head." Her voice caught slightly. "She wasn't soft. She was paying interest."

"Yes," Coral said. "I believe she was."

* * *

By noon the reading room had filled—not with the public, the doors were still officially closed after the fire, but with the people who had been coming anyway since the smoke: the retired constable Wren with his box of unsent reports, Dean Fenn pale and punctual with the surviving vestry minutes he had rescued from his predecessor's burning, Mrs. Aldous from the bakery who remembered her mother whispering about the fen, and Detective Inspector Sorrel, who stood at the edge of the group with her notebook open and the particular stillness of a woman watching a case assemble itself out of order.

Coral laid the decoded pages out along the long table in chronological sequence, thirty-one years of quiet-of-the-parish, and read the final cluster aloud because some things should be heard in a human voice:

*"To the ferryman's boy, for discretion re: the fen road."*
*"To E.Q., for the escorting of Miss M. beyond the county line."*
*"Account of A. Vane considered closed by mutual agreement. — E.Q."*

"The escort," Sorrel said quietly. "We've been looking for a body. We should have been looking for a getaway driver."

"Edith Quill," said old Constable Wren, and took off his cap, which he had not done indoors in anyone's living memory. "I arrested her son for poaching in '79. She brought me a pie while he was in the cell. I always thought—" he stopped, cleared his throat. "I always thought she was apologizing for something. Now I expect it was for everything else."

Dean Fenn had gone the colour of candle wax. "She never told me," he said. "All those years on the restoration committee. She sat across from me at meetings and never—" He stopped himself, and Coral watched him arrive at the truth the rest of them had already reached: that Edith had never trusted him, and had been right not to.

"She told us," Tamsin said. "In her way. She told us to ask."

* * *

It was nearly dusk when Coral found himself alone at the table again, the others gone home to suppers and telephones, the evidence photographed by Sorrel and boxed for the station in evidence bags that seemed, to Coral, a strangely small container for thirty-one years.

He sat with the ledger open to the last meaningful page and let himself feel it—not the cold clarity of the archivist but the thing underneath, which was grief, and gratitude, and a fury that had nowhere left to point itself except forward.

Every entry in this book was a person making a choice. The Vanes choosing murder and then maintenance. Harrow choosing respectability over a stranger's drowned face. Fenn's predecessor choosing endowments over truth. The ferryman's boy choosing a bushel of grain and thirty years of nightmares. Edith choosing, over and over, across decades, in small unglamorous installments, to keep one impossible promise.

And Ines—Ines choosing to write it all down in a code she must have learned hoping someone patient enough would someday read it. Choosing marginal marks that mapped her hiding place. Choosing to stay hidden rather than risk a town that had already chosen its story.

Nineteen days. That was what Tamsin had found in the statute books before the fire, and the fire had not changed it. Nineteen days until the concealment charges expired and the law's long arm withdrew from Hollowmere forever. After that, only this table remained. Only voices. Only ceremony.

Coral looked at the ledger's final line—*all accounts closed*—and understood that it was a lie, the way so much tidy paperwork was a lie. Accounts were never closed. They were carried forward. They waited for a new hand to take them up.

He turned to a fresh leaf in his own notebook, and at the top, in his precise archival hand, he wrote the heading he had been carrying in his chest for days:

**Hollowmere, Unsealed.**

Below it, the first entry: *Day minus nineteen. The account of A. Vane, reopened.*

When he looked up, Tamsin was standing in the doorway with her coat half on, backlit by the last of the light, and her expression stopped his pen.

"Coral," she said. "You need to see who's just walked into the nave."
