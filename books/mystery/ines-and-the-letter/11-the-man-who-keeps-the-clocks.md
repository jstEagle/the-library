## 11. The Man Who Keeps the Clocks

Every town has one person who knows where the bodies are buried. Marrow Bay, in its infinite thrift, had consolidated: it had a man who knew where all the *clocks* were buried, and I was beginning to suspect that in this particular town, the two professions overlapped.

Silas Marsh. Retired harbormaster, sixty-eight years old by the county records, which also listed him as a licensed marine mechanic, a notary public, and — I checked twice because I didn't believe it the first time — the town's official timekeeper. Not a joke title, either. There was an actual ordinance from 1951 designating the harbormaster responsible for the regulation of all public clocks, back when Marrow Bay's fishing fleet needed synchronized departures and the cannery whistles needed to agree with the church bells or half the town showed up to work at the wrong hour and the other half got defensive about it.

The harbormaster kept the clocks. The clocks kept the town honest. And one clock, thirty years ago, had stopped at 4:47 in the afternoon on the day Edmund Hale burned, and nobody in three decades of official paperwork had ever asked why a fire that started at 4:47 had been reported by a witness who claimed to have seen it start at 5:15.

I'd spent the morning confirming that discrepancy, sitting in the county library annex with microfilm and a thermos of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the Clinton administration. The *Marrow Bay Sentinel* of October 14th, 1994, ran a photograph of the burning cannery on its front page — the same photograph I'd studied until my eyes ached, the one with the child and the locket in the background. Below it, the article gave the timeline. Fire discovered at approximately 5:15 p.m. by a passing fisherman. Fire department arrived at 5:31. The cannery's wall clock, visible in a second interior photo taken during the investigation, frozen at 4:47.

Twenty-eight minutes. A stopped clock doesn't lie; it just stops being consulted. Everyone had assumed the heat had killed it, or the water, or simple mechanical surrender. Clocks die in fires the way witnesses do — quietly, and then everyone builds their story around the absence.

But Silas Marsh repaired every clock in town. If anyone had ever wound that clock back up, set it right, or — more interestingly — been asked to leave it exactly as it was, it was him.

I found his shop the way you find everything in Marrow Bay: by asking the woman at the bakery, who told me to ask the man at the hardware store, who told me to ask the woman at the bakery, who finally just pointed down a street called Anchor Row and said, "Green door, smells like oil, don't touch anything."

The green door was more of a suggestion than a color at this point, paint gone chalky with salt air, and the smell hit me before I reached it — machine oil, brass polish, and underneath both, something older, like the inside of an antique. A hand-lettered sign taped to the glass read MARSH CLOCK & MARINE REPAIR, HOURS: WHEN THE LIGHT'S GOOD. It was 2:40 in the afternoon on an overcast day. By that standard, I should have come back never.

I knocked anyway.

"Open," said a voice from within, gravel over warmth, the voice of a man who'd spent forty years shouting over engines and had recently decided he didn't have to anymore but hadn't fully committed.

Inside was less a shop than an argument between a workshop and a museum. Clocks covered every vertical surface — cuckoo clocks, ship's chronometers, mantel clocks, a grandfather case standing in the corner like a chaperone at a dance. On the long workbench, disassembled movements lay in labeled trays with a precision that made my investigator's heart sit up and take notice. This was a man who catalogued. This was a man who knew where every small thing belonged.

And in the middle of it all, under a cone of lamplight, sat Silas Marsh himself: broad through the shoulders even now, white beard trimmed short, forearms like dock rope, wearing a canvas apron gone dark at the pockets. He held a jeweler's loupe to his eye and didn't look up.

"If you're here about the church clock, it's the escapement again, and I told Father Anselm in March I'd get to it when the herring boat's out of my bay."

"I'm not here about the church clock."

He lowered the loupe. Looked at me the way harbormasters look at weather — assessing, unhurried, already deciding whether to bring in the boats. "Claims auditor," he said. "Ruth Quill called down the row this morning. Said there was a stranger asking after the fire files." He wiped his hands on the apron with the deliberate slowness of a man buying time. "You've been busy."

"Audits usually are."

"Mm." He gestured at a stool near the bench, and I took it. Up close, the shop resolved into further detail: a framed certificate of retirement from the Port Authority; a photograph of a younger Marsh standing beside a fishing boat named — I noted it without meaning to, the way I note everything, the curse and craft of it — the *Edmund Hale*.

I let him see me see it.

His eyes followed mine to the frame, and something in his face did a small, controlled collapse, like a movement losing a spring.

"Boats get named for people," he said. "It doesn't mean anything."

"No," I agreed. "It usually means quite a lot."

Silence. The grandfather clock in the corner ticked with the patience of something that had outlived everyone it was built to serve. Around us, maybe forty other clocks kept their own times, none of them synchronized, each one faithful only to itself. It occurred to me that I was sitting inside the mind of the most reliable witness in Marrow Bay, and that every instrument in the room was designed to do exactly what I did: record, measure, refuse to editorialize.

"I'm going to ask you about October 14th, 1994," I said. "And I want to tell you first that I'm not the enemy you think I am."

"You don't know what I think."

"I know you looked at my business card for four seconds longer than the name required."

That landed. He set the card face-down on the bench, under a tray of gears, as if putting it away. Then he said, quietly, almost to himself:

"Lockridge."

Not my name as a question. My name as a weight.

"That's right."

"There was a Lockridge in this town." He wasn't looking at me anymore. He was looking at the middle distance, at 1994, apparently, which appeared to be furnished much like his shop. "Before you were born, or around it. A young woman. Quiet. Kept to herself, worked summers at the cannery office." His jaw worked. "Left after the fire. Nobody talked about why, because nobody talked about anything after the fire."

The room got very warm, or I did. I kept my voice level, which is the one skill my memory has never managed to automate. "What was her first name?"

He turned and looked at me straight on, and I watched a man do arithmetic he'd been avoiding for thirty years — my age against his memory, my face against some younger face, my surname against a ledger entry in his head that he'd promised someone he'd never open.

"You have her chin," he said.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only inheritance I trust.

I did not grab him by the apron. I did not raise my voice. What I did was worse, probably: I went still, the way I go still when a file finally balances, when every number agrees with every other number and the only thing left unsettled is the human being holding the report. Somewhere behind my sternum, a door I'd kept locked since childhood — the door with no records behind it, the door my adoption paperwork sealed with a bureaucratic shrug — swung open onto a lit room.

A young woman. Worked summers at the cannery office. Left after the fire.

My mother had stood in the building that burned. Had worked among ledgers — *the* ledger, possibly, the one with razored pages and pressure indentations and payments flowing forward like a river finding the sea. Had left the week the town's whole story cracked, carrying whatever she carried, and nine months later or thereabouts, there was me, filed away in a system that asked no questions because I'd learned early to give it no reasons to.

"Her name," I said again. Not a question either. We were both past questions.

Silas took off his glasses, which I realized he'd been wearing pushed up on his forehead, and cleaned them on his apron with tremendous care, a man performing normalcy with both hands. "Margaret," he said. "Margaret Lockridge. Called Maggie. She used to bring her father's watch in for cleaning every spring, regular as tide tables. Elgin, gold case, hairline crack across the crystal. You'd know it if you saw it."

I would know it if I saw it. Because I had seen it — once, at eight years old, in a shoebox of documents the state had released to my first foster family along with a birth certificate listing no father and a mother's name redacted by a marker so thorough it had bled through three pages. But there had been a photograph in that box, soft-cornered, of a young woman on a dock, and pinned to her blouse, catching the sun, a small watch on a chain.

Photographic memory is sold to the world as a gift. Mostly it's a warehouse with no loading door. Things go in; nothing comes out; you live among the crates. But every so often, thirty years later, a man in a salty shop says *hairline crack across the crystal*, and the crate opens, and there she is, twenty-four years old and squinting into a sun I can't remember feeling.

"She left in November '94," Silas said. He was watching me now with an expression I couldn't fully parse — guilt braided through something gentler. "Pregnant, some said. Nobody said it loud. Vera Quill drove her to the bus station herself. Came back alone, sat in her truck in the cannery lot for an hour, and then walked into the office and worked the rest of the day like nothing happened. That was Vera. She could carry a thing the size of a house and balance the books around it."

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "You've spent thirty years not telling people things. You're good at it. I can see the trophies."

He almost smiled. It didn't take. "Because I owed your mother a debt," he said, "and I've been paying it to the wrong address for a long time." He set his glasses back on. Behind them, his eyes were wet and furious about it. "And because you're going to keep pulling this thread whether I help you or not, and I'm too old to watch another Lockridge walk out of this town with nothing."

"Another?"

The word was out before I could weigh it, and I watched it land. Silas Marsh closed his mouth. Closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was a harbormaster again, reading weather, deciding about boats.

"The cannery clock," he said instead. "You came about the cannery clock. Ask me."

"It stopped at 4:47. The fire was reported at 5:15. Twenty-eight minutes unaccounted for, and every account of that evening depends on those minutes staying empty."

"Yes."

"Were you asked to repair it?"

"I was asked to *leave it alone*." He said it flatly, a fact delivered like freight. "Three days after the fire, Vera Quill came to this shop — this exact stool, if you want the poetry of it — and she put the key to the cannery on my bench and said, 'Silas, that clock stopped when it stopped, and it stays stopped.' And I asked her why, because I'm a fool, and she said—" his voice caught, steadied— "she said, 'Because someday someone will need to know what time it really was.'"

The grandfather clock struck the quarter hour behind us, and I swear every other clock in the room seemed to lean in.

"And did you ever wind it? Set it? Touch it?"

"Once." He held up a hand before I could speak. "Once, in 1996, on Vera's instruction. I reset it forward — moved the hands to match the official time of the fire, 5:15, so the insurance photographs would show consistency. She wanted the record clean. Then I stopped it again at 4:47 and we never spoke of it."

My head did the quiet, vertiginous math it does best. A clock reset to protect a story, then returned to protect a truth. Vera Quill hadn't been hiding the discrepancy. She'd been *preserving* it — keeping the evidence in plain sight, stopped at the moment that mattered, waiting thirty years for someone with the patience to ask what time it really was.

Waiting, maybe, for someone with her chin.

"Mr. Marsh," I said. "The debt you owe my mother. Does it have a shape? A number? A name attached to it besides hers?"

He looked at the tray of gears. At the photograph of the *Edmund Hale*. At me.

"It has a name," he said. "But it isn't mine to say, and it isn't Vera's either, and if I speak it in this room, every clock in Marrow Bay will have heard it." He picked up his loupe, turned back to his workbench, and became, with visible effort, a man repairing a watch. "Come back tomorrow. Or don't. But if you do — knock twice. And Felix?"

It was the first time he'd used my name. It sounded strange in his mouth, familiar, like a word from a language he'd spoken as a child.

"When you find out what time it really was — and you will, you have her way of not letting go — be kinder with it than the truth usually gets to be."

I let myself out through the green door. Behind me, forty unsynchronized clocks ticked on in the dark, each one faithful only to itself, and somewhere among them, I knew, was a stopped one keeping perfect time.

The evening fog was coming in off the bay, erasing Anchor Row street by street, and I stood on the sidewalk doing the thing I never do: forgetting to take notes. Because for the first time in my career, the file in my head had a heartbeat in it, and its name was Margaret, and it had left this town pregnant in November of 1994, and every road I'd been walking since the two letters arrived had just narrowed to a single point of origin.

Vera had driven her to the bus station. Vera had come back alone. Vera had carried it thirty years.

And tomorrow, Silas Marsh would either say the name or he wouldn't — but tonight, walking back through a town dissolving into salt and mist, I understood something with total clarity: I hadn't come to Marrow Bay investigating a fire.

I'd come to investigate my own beginning, and the fire was just where the smoke started.
