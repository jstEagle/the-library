## 9. Barataria

The storm came up out of the southwest like a rumor nobody had the nerve to repeat out loud.

Rosalind felt it before she saw it—the way the air over Barataria Bay went thick and sweet, the gulls lifting off the marsh grass in one long gray exhalation, the water flattening to a dull pewter that reflected nothing. Three days of watching the derelict boathouse had taught her the bay's small grammar: the tide's punctuation, the pelicans' commentary, the particular silence that meant someone had recently been somewhere they shouldn't. She had learned to read silence the way other people read print. Thirty years of night dispatch would do that to a woman. You spend enough hours listening to a city breathe through a headset and you develop an ear for the breath that isn't there.

The supply run came at dusk, as it had come the two evenings before. A flat-bottomed pirogue slipped out from behind the ruined ice house on the bayou side, moving low and deliberate through the dying light, and Rosalind counted its cargo by silhouette: a cooler, a wrapped bundle, a jerry can. The man poling it wore a slicker with the hood up, and he did not look back once. People carrying guilt look back. People carrying errands don't.

She let him get two hundred yards out before she followed.

Her own boat was nothing—seventeen feet of borrowed aluminum with an outboard that idled like a man muttering in his sleep—but she kept it behind the line of dead cypress that screened the boathouse channel, matching his wake without touching it. The rain arrived in fat, warm, intermittent drops, each one landing with a small percussive authority on the water, on the aluminum, on the back of her neck. She thought, absurdly, of the dispatch board on a bad night: all those little lights blinking, each one a voice asking to be found, to be delivered, to be gotten home. She had spent her whole life getting other people home. Tonight she intended to find the one person who had made herself unfindable on purpose.

The pirogue rounded the spit of marsh where the channel widened, and there it was.

The shrimp boat sat at anchor in the lee of a drowned timber camp, its hull the color of an old bruise, its rigging hung with ghost nets that moved in the wind like something breathing. A single lantern glowed in the wheelhouse, amber and small, the only warm thing in a world going the color of iron. Rosalind cut her engine and drifted the last fifty yards, the rain thickening now, the first real thunder rolling in from the gulf with a sound like furniture being dragged across the floor of the sky.

She saw her at the rail.

Ines Marchand stood with her hands flat on the gunwale, watching the pirogue approach, and she did not look like a missing woman. She looked like a woman who had finally stopped performing her own disappearance. Her hair was longer than the photographs the lawyer had given Rosalind, shot through with gray she hadn't bothered to cover, and she wore a man's oilskin coat two sizes too big, and when she turned her head and looked directly across the water at the drifting aluminum boat—at Rosalind—her expression held no surprise at all.

"I wondered which night you'd come," Ines called, her voice carrying clean over the water. "The tide tables gave you away eventually, didn't they? I wrote them too well."

Rosalind brought the boat alongside, tied off, and stood there in the rain with her hand still on the line, suddenly aware that she had rehearsed this moment a hundred ways and none of the rehearsals had included the missing woman greeting her by name.

"Ines Marchand," Rosalind said. "You're supposed to be dead. Or gone. The whole point was gone."

"The whole point," Ines said, "was *found*. There's a difference. You found me. Come aboard before the storm does."

* * *

The wheelhouse smelled of diesel and coffee and old canvas. A chart of the bay was spread across the chart table, weighted at the corners with a flashlight, a tin cup, and a copy of the parish baptismal register from 1994—wrapped in plastic, annotated in Ines's small, upright hand. Rosalind recognized the handwriting from the diary's earlier pages, the true pages, and felt the strange vertigo of seeing a document she'd been chasing for weeks sitting under a coffee cup like a grocery list.

"Odile Fontenot's man," Rosalind said. "The one who's been running your supplies. He works for her."

"He works for whoever pays, and lately that's been a complicated question." Ines poured coffee into a second tin cup and set it on the table, an act of hospitality so ordinary it was almost unbearable. "His name is Landry. He drove the car that night, thirty years ago. He was nineteen. He's been paying his own debt ever since, one supply run at a time. He didn't know who he was feeding until last week."

Rosalind sat down slowly. "You've known he'd come for you."

"I've known what Odile would send when she understood the diary was a message and not a confession. There's a difference between those, too." Ines sat across from her, and up close her calm had a grain to it, a texture, like wood that had been underwater a long time and come out harder. "I staged it, Ms. Delacroix. The disappearance. Three weeks ago I walked into St. Brigid at three in the morning, logged a fare with your company because I needed a record that would outlive me in a database nobody thought to burn, and then I let the parish believe the water had taken me the way it took that boy. I wrote the diary where Odile could find it. I let her think she was editing my ghost."

"The entries after the end. The water debt."

"Mine. Coded. Written in the hand I learned from my mother's ledgers, because I knew a graphologist would eventually read them as an older woman's and point straight at Odile." Ines smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of someone who had set a trap and was watching it hold. "I needed the truth to walk into the light on its own legs. If I'd simply come forward, I'd have been a bitter archivist with a grievance and thirty-year-old paper. If I *drowned*, I became a story. And stories about drowned children get told whether the prominent families like it or not."

Thunder cracked close now, close enough to feel in the deck plates, and the lantern guttered. Rain hit the wheelhouse windows in sheets. Rosalind looked at the woman across the table—thin, salt-bitten, absolutely steady—and felt the thing she'd been refusing to feel since the boathouse: not the thrill of a case closing, but the raw, unwelcome recognition of a kindred creature. Two women who had spent their lives in the service trades of other people's emergencies. Two women the city looked through.

"You could have been killed," Rosalind said. "Out here alone."

"I could have been killed in my apartment. Odile's people are patient, not creative." Ines turned her head toward the window, toward the black water. "Besides. The bay and I have an understanding. It took something from me when I was eleven years old. I watched that boy go under from the church steps, and I watched the grown-ups decide, in real time, with their eyes, that he had never been there at all. My mother took the money. I took the silence. Thirty years is long enough for both."

The engine sound came out of the rain like a knife coming out of a sheath.

Rosalind was on her feet before she'd decided to stand. Through the streaming glass she made it out: a center-console skiff running dark except for a single running light, coming hard across the bay from the direction of the marina, plowing spray, indifferent to weather that would keep any honest boat ashore. Ines rose more slowly. She picked up the baptismal register, the diary, a manila envelope thick with photocopies, and set them on the chart table in a neat stack, and then she put her hand flat on top of the stack the way a person lays a hand on the shoulder of a child.

"Landry," she said.

"Or someone Landry told."

The skiff swung alongside with a bang of rubber against hull. The man who came over the rail was big through the shoulders and wrong in the eyes—the particular wrongness Rosalind had heard a thousand times in voices on the dispatch line, the flat calm of a man who has already decided and is only now arriving at the place where the decision happens. He carried a red jerry can, and it was not full of fuel. The reek of it cut through the diesel the moment he stepped into the wheelhouse. Gasoline.

"Miss Ines." His voice was gentle, which was worse. "Miss Odile's real sorry it come to this. She says the bay's been generous to you long enough. She wants the papers and she wants you to understand there's no shame in it. Accidents happen on the water. Everybody knows that about you by now."

"Everybody knows it because she paid to make it known," Ines said. She did not move from the chart table. Her voice did not rise. It had the quality Rosalind had heard once or twice in thirty years of dispatch, from callers in burning buildings and flooded cars—the voice that arrives when terror has been used up and what's left is something harder and cleaner. "Emile. You were nineteen. You were drunk, and you were driving her daughter's car, and the boy from the parish ran because he was frightened, and he went into the water, and you drove home. And Odile Fontenot sat you down the next morning and told you what your silence would cost and what it would pay, and you took the same deal my mother took. You've been rowing supplies to the woman whose testimony could hang you, for three weeks, because some part of you has been rowing toward this night for thirty years."

Landry's face did something complicated. The jerry can shifted in his grip.

"Put it down," Rosalind said, and her dispatcher's voice came out of her, low and bored and absolutely certain, the voice that had talked a hundred panicked strangers through the worst hour of their lives. "You're standing in a wheelhouse full of gasoline fumes holding an open can in a lightning storm, and I have already radioed the Barataria sheriff's substation from the channel marker twenty minutes ago, and they are slower than Christmas but they are coming, and when they come they're going to find whatever we leave them. Decide what that is."

It was a bluff. Mostly. She had made one call, from the boathouse, to a number she'd gotten from Manny—a sergeant who owed the old dispatcher a favor and had grunted something noncommittal. But Landry couldn't know that, and the storm pressed in on the windows like a fist, and the lightning came again, blue-white, close, and in its light Rosalind saw the man's eyes go from the jerry can to the stack of papers to Ines's face, and saw thirty years arrive in him all at once.

The can hit the deck with a hollow boom.

"I watched him go under," Landry said, and his voice cracked down the middle like the thunder. "I watched him, and I drove home, and I have driven home every night since."

"I know," Ines said. And she came around the chart table and stood in front of him, small and salt-worn and unafraid, and she put the manila envelope into his hands. "So carry it in. Carry it to the sheriff, to the parish, to the memorial, wherever it needs to go. You've been paying Odile's debt long enough. Start paying your own."

Lightning filled the wheelhouse white. When it faded, Landry was standing in the rain on the deck with the evidence clutched to his chest like a child, and the skiff was pulling away toward the lights of the distant coast, toward whatever reckoning waited there, and Ines Marchand turned to Rosalind with the storm breaking over the boat and said, in the tone of a woman confirming a fare:

"Well, dispatcher. I believe I'm ready to be found."

* * *

They rode out the worst of it at anchor, the shrimp boat rising and falling on the black swells while the lightning stitched the horizon and Ines told it all—the flood, the money, the coded diary, the tide tables, the long patient architecture of a truth built to walk into the light on its own legs. Rosalind listened the way she listened on the night shift, completely, without once looking away, and somewhere in the small hours the terror drained out of her and left behind the other thing, the fierce and unexpected thing: the feeling of being seen by someone who knew exactly what it cost to live invisible.

When the sky went the gray of old dishwater and the rain thinned to a whisper, Ines stood at the rail beside her and looked east toward the parish, toward the steeples and the shipping offices and the prominent houses with their prominent families, all of them about to wake into a world where the water had finally given back what it took.

"They'll come for me now," Ines said. "Odile, the lawyers, all of them. The next part is louder than this part."

Rosalind started the outboard. It caught on the second pull, muttering, faithful.

"Then you'll want a dispatcher," she said, and turned the bow toward home.
