## 12. Emory's Garden

There is a particular kind of patience required for watching a man tend roses, and I discovered that week that I do not possess it.

I had told myself the surveillance would be simple. Emory Vance was a creature of habit, and habits are just schedules with dirt under their fingernails. He rose before dawn—I knew this because the cathedral's heating system groaned to life at five-forty and he was always the first body in the nave, straightening hymnals no one had yet disturbed. He took his lunch at half past noon on the low wall by the memorial garden, always the same: bread, an apple, and whatever the refectory was pretending was soup. And on Thursdays, after the noon office, he worked the garden behind the sexton's cottage until the light went amber and the bells drove him back inside.

So on Thursday I sat in the archive with the blinds tilted, holding a volume of burial records I had no intention of reading, and watched through the gap as a gentle man in a canvas apron deadheaded roses with the concentration of a surgeon.

Here is what nobody tells you about suspicion: it curdles. For years my work had been a kind of devotion—taking the cases the police closed too fast, holding them up to the archive's cold light, finding the hairline crack. It had never once required me to crouch behind a hedge watching someone I'd known for a decade, someone who had once spent forty minutes helping me find a misfiled deed because he could see it mattered to me. Watching him now, I kept catching myself making excuses for him. The apron. The careful hands. The way he paused to let a bee finish its business before pruning around it. Men like this did not kill magistrates in locked crypts.

But men like this also did not tear pages out of registries, and somebody had.

* * *

I want to be honest about how long I sat there doing nothing, because honesty is the only currency I have left these days.

Three days. I watched him for three days before I did anything at all, and even then what I did was make a list. Not of evidence—of reasons not to act. He was sixty-one years old with arthritis in his left knee; he couldn't have hauled a grown man down a crypt stair. (He could have used the trolley for moving flower arrangements. I'd seen it.) He had no motive visible from where I stood. (Neither had the letter-writer, and the letter existed.) He was kind to me. (My father had been kind to everyone, and my father had founded a death cult in a vestry.)

That last one was the one I kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Because here was the thing I hadn't said aloud to anyone, including myself, including Brandt when she'd slid that cold-case file across her desk and watched my face for the reaction she knew was coming: the founding signature in The Verdict's old ledger wasn't just a name I recognized. It was written in a hand I'd grown up watching sign birthday cards. My father didn't merely belong to them. According to Brandt's file, he'd been their keeper—the archivist of a murder society, which meant that somewhere in my bloodline ran a man who believed that justice was a thing you could file.

And if Emory Vance had been his protégé—and the torn registry page, signed with my father's cipher, said he had—then Emory hadn't learned gardening from him. He'd learned the other thing. The filing. The verdicts.

The problem, laid out plainly on my list, was this: confronting Emory meant telling him what I knew, which meant telling him who I was, which meant handing a vigilante network the daughter of its founder on a plate. Waiting meant Judge Halvard Reyes—the third name, the one whose line in the confession letter was still uncrossed—died on whatever schedule Emory had set, probably within the week, probably ruled accidental, probably filed and forgotten by men who had learned the trick from my father.

Paralysis, then. Beautiful, well-documented paralysis. I almost admired it. It was the most thorough piece of cataloging I'd ever done and it pointed entirely away from action.

It was Nell Okafor who broke the stalemate, though she never knew it. She came to the archive Thursday evening, after Emory had gone in for supper, still wearing her court lanyard like a noose she'd gotten used to. She stood in the doorway turning her hat brim in her hands and asked, in a voice carefully sanded flat, whether I'd ever found anything in the records about a ruling called *Halloway v. the City*. And I watched her ask it, and I saw what it cost her—not curiosity but dread, the specific dread of a woman checking whether the water rising around her had a bottom—and I understood that waiting was not neutral. Waiting was a choice too, and I was choosing Reyes's death over my own comfort, and dressing it up as prudence.

Nell left without me answering. I sat in the dark a while. Then I put on my coat, because the garden would be empty by seven, and Emory's shed had a lock older than either of us.

* * *

The sexton's cottage sits at the cathedral's eastern edge, past the memorial garden, backed against the old boundary wall where the yews go dark early. The tool shed leans against the wall like something the wall is tolerating. Its lock is a padlock, iron, older than the cathedral's plumbing, and I had assumed—wrongly, as it turned out—that a man as meticulous as Emory would keep better.

He did keep better. That was the trap of it. The padlock opened with a stiff shoulder and a curse, and inside was exactly what a tool shed should be: rakes fanned on nails, twine on a spindle, terracotta pots nested like sleeping birds, the sweet rot-smell of loam and cut stems. Nothing hidden. Nothing wrong. I stood there in the failing light feeling like a burglar in a museum of ordinary life, and I was already composing my apology to a man I'd slandered in my own head for a week—

—when I noticed the floor.

Not the floor itself. The geometry of it. The flagstones were old and uneven, as they should be, except for one patch near the north wall where three stones sat level. Perfectly level. In a shed where every other stone had settled into its own opinion over two centuries, three stones lying flush was not tidiness. It was intention.

I knelt. The middle stone lifted on a finger-loop of wire set into the seam—a mechanism, not an accident, the kind of thing a sexton builds and only a sexton could build—and beneath it was a cavity lined with oilcloth, and in the cavity, wrapped in a second layer of cloth gone soft with age, was a book.

I knew it before I touched it. God help me, I knew it by its weight, by the way the oilcloth crinkled, by the smell of it—leather and lamp oil and something faintly metallic underneath, like the memory of blood in a room long scrubbed. I had grown up seeing that book on my father's shelf. I had been told it was a parish register. I had believed it the way children believe things, completely and without examination, right up until Detective Brandt laid a photocopied page in front of me and the world tilted.

The Verdict's ledger. Missing for thirty years. Sitting under the roses of a man who deadheaded them with surgical tenderness.

I carried it to the doorway where the last light still reached and I opened it, and my hands were steadier than I deserved.

The early pages were my father's hand—small, upright, merciless in its neatness. Dates. Names. Outcomes. Each entry formatted identically: the grievance, the target, the method disguised as accident, and then, in a column at the margin, a single word. *Kept.* As in: justice witnessed is justice kept. As in: the law failed, so we filed the failure and corrected it. There were entries going back decades, and reading them was like reading my own father's diary written in a language I'd refused to learn. One entry, dated the year I turned nine, concerned a man who had walked free after crippling a family in a fraud case. The method column read *stairs*. The marginal word read *kept*. I closed my eyes and saw my father at breakfast that year, buttering toast, asking me about my lessons.

But it was the later pages that stopped my breath.

Because the ledger hadn't ended with my father. The handwriting changed—once, twice, three times across the decades, each successor trained into the same format, the same margins, the same terrible word—and the most recent hand was one I knew. I had seen it a hundred times in the key registry, in the maintenance logs, in the careful inked labels on the seed drawers. Emory Vance. Emory had inherited the book the way you inherit a watch or a debt, and he had kept it current.

The fresh entries began fourteen months ago. Magistrate Aldous Wren: grievance listed, method listed (*crypt*), date scheduled, and in the margin—*kept*, in fresh ink, the entry completed after the fact. Below Wren, Judge Corliss Marsh: *fall*, scheduled, executed, *kept*. Below Marsh, Judge Halvard Reyes: *exhaust*, with a date.

The date was Sunday. Four days from now.

And below Reyes, two more lines, waiting. Judge Amara Solis. And a fifth name I didn't recognize yet, though I knew with a sick certainty that I would.

I sat down on the shed floor among the terracotta pots and held the book and did not move for a long time. Somewhere outside, the cathedral bells rang the hour, and the sound came through the walls the way it comes through everything in this city—muffled, patient, indifferent.

Here is what went through my head, in order:

If I take the ledger to Brandt, Emory will know within a day—he checks this cache, a man like him checks—and the moment he knows, Reyes dies ahead of schedule or vanishes beyond reach, and the network's sympathizers inside the courts bury whatever evidence survives. If I leave the ledger and tell no one, Reyes dies on Sunday and I will have watched it happen from behind a hedge, the way I have watched everything my whole life, invisible and clean-handed and complicit. If I confront Emory, I trade my invisibility—the only armor I have ever worn—for a chance at talking him out of four days of murder, with no guarantee the trade buys anything at all.

Every door led through the same wall: me, seen. Me, known. Me, the founder's daughter, standing in front of a killer and saying *I know what you are*—which is also saying *I know what my father was*, which is also saying *look at me*, which is the one sentence I have spent my entire adult life arranging never to say.

I thought about Nell's hat brim turning in her hands. I thought about the Halloway family, ruined by a ruling some clerk had decided to correct with a red envelope instead of an appeal. I thought about my father's cipher on the torn page, borrowed by a man who loved him, and how grief can be forged into a weapon by someone patient enough.

Then I heard the gravel.

A footstep, then another, unhurried, coming up the path from the cottage—the particular tread of a man who has nothing to fear on his own ground. The shed door stood open behind me. The ledger lay in my lap like a confession. And through the doorway, framed against the last grey light, came the silhouette of Emory Vance, canvas apron still on, secateurs in one hand, stopping mid-stride as his eyes found me sitting on his floor with thirty years of murders open across my knees.

For a moment neither of us moved. A bee droned somewhere in the roses. His face, when I could finally read it in the dimness, held no anger at all—only a sorrow so deep and so calm that it frightened me more than rage ever could, the sorrow of a man whose worst expectation has been confirmed and who must now do something difficult about it.

"Petra," he said gently, the way you speak to someone you've known since she was small. "Your father always said you'd be the one to find it."

He stepped into the shed and closed the door behind him.
