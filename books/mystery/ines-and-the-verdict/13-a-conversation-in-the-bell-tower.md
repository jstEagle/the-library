## 13. A Conversation in the Bell Tower

### Petra

I had been following Emory Vance for six days before he invited me to follow him.

That was the humiliating part. I'd told myself I was being careful—varying my routes through the cloister, cataloging his movements the way I cataloged everything, in a small notebook with no name in it. I knew that he rose at five, that he rang the Angelus at noon with a precision you could set a watch by, that on Thursdays he bought lilies from the flower stall on Merton Street and always overpaid by a coin and never took the change. I knew where he kept the ledger. I had held it. I had read my father's cipher in its margins and felt the floor of my life tilt.

And then on Tuesday evening, as I crouched behind the vestry's woodpile like a woman in a bad play, Emory stopped at the corner of the chapter house, did not turn around, and said:

"The bell tower stairs are easier if you take them slowly. There are two hundred and eleven steps, and the last forty are worn smooth."

I stood there with a fistful of splinters and my dignity in ruins.

He waited. He was very good at waiting; I suppose hanging is mostly waiting. "I've made tea," he added, as though we were neighbors discussing a fence line. "It will go cold otherwise. Your father hated cold tea."

I should have walked away. Every instinct I had spent thirty-eight years cultivating—the instinct to be furniture, to be wallpaper, to be the person people forgot was in the room—every one of them said *walk away, Petra, write what you know in your ledger, put it in Brandt's hands, let other people do the standing in front of dangerous men.*

But he'd said *your father*, and I have learned this week that I am not the person I believed I was, because instead of walking away I brushed off my coat and followed him up two hundred and eleven steps.

* * *

### Emory

She came. Of course she came.

I had watched her watching me for six days, and I confess I enjoyed it more than I should have. She was clumsy at it—Silas would have despaired of her tradecraft—but she was thorough, and thoroughness is rarer than cleverness. She moved through the cathedral like a woman taking inventory of the world, which is precisely what she was. Her father used to do the same thing. Same stoop of the shoulders when she read. Same habit of touching her left cuff when she was frightened.

The tower room at the top is mine in every way that matters. The bells live below; up here there is only the clock mechanism, the ropes, the pigeons, and a view of the city that puts all of it—courthouse, banks, the magistrates' row—in its proper scale. Small. Containable. I set out two chairs and the tea things on the ledge beside the great escapement wheel, because I am not a barbarian, and because Silas's daughter deserved better than an ambush.

She sat. She looked at the ledger on my knee—I had brought it deliberately—and then at me, and she said nothing, which told me everything. A guilty woman talks. An innocent woman asks questions. A woman who has already worked it out waits to see how much you'll give away.

"You found the shed," I said.

"Your padlock is rusted shut. You've been opening it with a screwdriver. The hasp is scarred." She wrapped both hands around the teacup without drinking. "You wanted me to find it."

"I wanted you to understand it. Finding is your own accomplishment." I turned the ledger so the cover faced her. Silas's cipher marched down the spine: five strokes, a circle, a crossbar. *Verdict.* "Your father kept this for nineteen years. I've kept it for eleven. It seemed right that the third custodian should be a Zhou."

"I'm not—" She stopped. Recalibrated. I could almost hear the gears. "There is no third custodian. There's you, and there's whatever is left of a dead man's vanity, and there are three names on a list, one of whom is a clerk who never hurt anyone."

"Nell Okafor." I nodded. "You care about her."

"She's twenty-six. She testified truthfully knowing what it might cost her. That's more courage than most judges manage in a career."

"And yet courage isn't the question, is it? The question is whether the verdict stands." I opened the ledger to the current page. Five lines. Two struck through in red—Wren, Marsh. Reyes, struck through only days ago, the ink barely dry when she read it; I saw her flinch at it, and I noted the flinch, because guilt is a door and I intended to walk through it gently. "Three judges who sold the Harrow Lane families their own streets. Wren took eleven thousand pounds to bury the inquiry. Marsh signed the order. Reyes quashed the appeal. And Amara Solis holds the last word in her hands this week."

"Solis is honest."

"Solis is *alone*," I said. "Which in that building is a temporary condition. You know what happened to the last honest magistrate who tried to stand against them. Or rather—you don't, because no one wrote it down, because no one was left to write it down." I let that sit. "The law failed them, Petra. Not once. Systematically. For nine years the Harrow Lane families filed complaints, petitions, appeals. Every door was a wall painted to look like a door. Your father sat in this room one night in the autumn of '09 and said: if the courts will not render verdicts, then the faithful must. He didn't say it with anger. That's what people never understood about Silas. He said it the way he said everything—like a man reading an inventory. *Item: justice, missing. Item: remedy, required.*"

Her eyes went to the ledger. To the cipher. To me.

"You were his protégé," she said.

"I was his sexton. He was my magistrate's clerk before they made him archivist-adjacent, before the church gave him a shelf to hide on. We buried the ones the courts wouldn't mourn." I poured her more tea she hadn't drunk. "He taught me the lock. He taught me the cipher. He taught me that a verdict is not a piece of paper—it's a debt, and debts don't expire just because the creditor dies."

* * *

### Petra

Here is the terrible thing I will not put in any report, not even Brandt's: for about ninety seconds, sitting in that bell tower with the pigeons muttering and the whole crooked city laid out below us like evidence, I understood him.

Not agreed. Understood. There's a difference, and the difference is the width of a grave, but it exists. Nine years of Harrow Lane. Families who did everything right and were buried under paperwork. My father—a man who alphabetized his grief, who taught me that a record kept honestly was a kind of prayer—my father had looked at that machine and concluded it could not be repaired from inside. And then he had built another one.

Emory watched me arrive at this understanding, because Emory watches everything, and I realized with a cold slide in my stomach that he had brought me here not to threaten me but to recruit me, and that the most frightening weapon in the room was the tea going lukewarm between my hands while part of me listened.

"You want me to take his seat," I said.

"I want you to take his seat," Emory agreed, pleasantly, as if offering a post as warden of the flower fund. "The network needs a keeper of records. It has always needed one—that's the lesson of the fire, the lesson of everything. Men with keys forget; men with ledgers remember. You are the best archivist this cathedral has produced in a century, and I include your father. You traced the confession letter from a postmark. You reconstructed a torn page from fiber ghosts. Detective Brandt couldn't have done it. I couldn't have done it." He smiled, and it was a genuinely kind smile, which made it worse. "We could finish the list together. Solis reads the verdict Friday. After that, the ledger closes. No more names. Ever. Your father designed it to end, Petra. Justice isn't a profession. It's a debt, and then it's done."

"And Nell?"

"A regrettable necessity."

"There's no such thing."

"There is," he said quietly, "when the alternative is letting eleven thousand pounds buy the last honest courtroom in the city. Your father called those moments 'the arithmetic.' He hated the arithmetic. He did it anyway. So will you, eventually. You're already doing it—you've been doing it since the crypt. You cataloged a dead man's watch running two hours slow and you didn't tell the police, because some part of you knew the police were the wrong audience. You started a private ledger. You broke your own rule of invisibility. You've been keeping records for a verdict the law won't hand down." He leaned forward. "The only difference between us, Petra Zhou, is that I act and you file."

The bell rope swayed somewhere below us. A pigeon resettled on the sill. I thought about my father's unsent letter in my coat pocket—I had carried it for three days like a stone—and what it had said, or rather what I hadn't yet been able to make myself read past the first line.

"No," I said.

Emory tilted his head, patient as a sundial.

"I understand the arithmetic," I said. "I even feel its pull, and I'm telling you that so you know I'm not refusing out of ignorance. But you've made one error, and it's the error the law makes too. You think a verdict is something you can execute on someone else's behalf. It isn't. The moment you decide for the Harrow Lane families, you erase them—they become props in your creed. My father kept the ledger because he believed the record mattered more than the man holding it. You've inverted that. You've made yourself the verdict and the record a souvenir." I stood. My legs were steadier than I expected. "And Nell Okafor is not arithmetic. She's a person who did the brave thing, and if you touch her, then everything in that book stops being justice and becomes exactly what you claim to hate: power deciding who counts."

For a long moment he simply looked at me, and I braced for the rope, or the drop, or the gentle terrible efficiency I now knew he was capable of.

Instead he stood, collected the teacups, and washed them in a basin by the window, because he was Emory Vance and he would be tidy at his own execution.

"You may go," he said. "The stairs are slippery after dark. Take the lantern."

I got four steps toward the stairwell before he spoke again, not unkindly, which was somehow the worst sound I had heard all week.

"Your father refused twice, you know. Before he ever took up the pen. I have his letters. I'll leave them somewhere you'll find them." A pause, and then, softly, with the certainty of a man ringing a bell he knows will be heard for miles: "Come Friday, when Solis stands up and the doors seal and the crypt waits beneath everyone's feet—one of us will be right, Petra. I can wait to learn which."

I took the lantern. I climbed down two hundred and eleven steps with my heart hammering out a rhythm I refused to name, and only at the bottom, in the dark of the nave, did I finally pull my father's letter from my pocket and break the seal I'd been carrying for three days.

The first full sentence read: *If you are reading this, then Emory has offered you my chair, and you must understand—he does not see it as a chair. He sees it as a debt. And he will collect it from whoever sits closest.*

Below that, in handwriting gone thin and hurried, was a list of names.

Nell Okafor's was at the bottom.
