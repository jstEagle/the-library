## 3. A Death Already Served

The county records annex smelled like pencil shavings and old rain, which was to say it smelled like every government building Mara Vasquez had ever loved. She'd been coming here since she was twenty-two and newly licensed, back when the coroner's office still shared a wall with the water department and the whole of Hollow Creek's civic life could be heard through the plaster — flushes, arguments, someone's radio playing gospel too loud on Fridays.

Now the annex stood alone on Cord Street, half its windows papered over, staffed by a single archivist named Wendell Pruitt who had worked there so long that people had stopped asking him questions for fear he'd answer them.

"Wendell," Mara said, setting her elbows on his counter. "I need a file."

"Course you do." Wendell didn't look up from his crossword. He was seventy-something and built like a filing cabinet himself, all drawers and no wasted motion. "Which one?"

"Brandt. Elise Brandt. 2014."

The crossword pencil stopped moving.

It was a small thing, that stop. A quarter-second hitch, the kind of pause most people wouldn't register. But Mara spent her professional life reading bodies — the ones on her table and, more usefully, the ones standing in front of her — and Wendell Pruitt had just flinched with his whole hand.

"That's an old one," he said.

"I'm aware. I can count."

"Sealed file, that one. Sealed files need a court order or next-of-kin request." He said it the way a man recites a lock combination he hopes you'll forget before reaching the door.

"And yet," Mara said pleasantly, "you haven't told me to leave."

Wendell looked up at last. His eyes behind his bifocals were watery and shrewd at once, like a basset hound doing arithmetic. "You're the coroner," he said. "Half these files are technically yours. The other half you could argue your way into, and I'm too old to argue anymore. Row twelve, box forty-one. I never saw you."

"You see everybody, Wendell. It's your whole job."

"Not today it isn't." He went back to his crossword, and if his hand shook a little on the way down, Mara had the grace not to mention it.

* * *

Row twelve was in the back of the annex, under a fluorescent tube that buzzed like a trapped hornet. Box forty-one was a gray banker's box, standard issue, its label typed on a manual typewriter: **BRANDT, ELISE M. — DECEDENT FILE — CASE NO. 14-0387**.

Mara carried it to the reading table by the window, where the light came through ambered glass and made everything look older and more forgiving than it was. She put on her reading glasses. She poured coffee from her thermos. And then she opened the file, and felt the first cold thread of something she couldn't yet name.

The file was thin.

Not thin the way old files get thin — pages lost, corners crumbled, the natural erosion of paper and neglect. Thin the way a haircut is thin. Deliberate. Groomed. She counted twice to be sure: eleven documents. For a death involving an heiress whose family name sat on the hospital wing, the library annex, and the fountain in Miller Park, eleven documents wasn't a record. It was a press release.

She laid them out in order and read the way she read autopsy reports — slowly, twice, letting the silences between lines speak.

**Document one:** Death certificate. Elise Marie Brandt, age thirty-one. Date of death, October 9th, 2014. Cause of death listed as *cardiac arrhythmia secondary to undiagnosed myocarditis*. Attending physician: Dr. Harlan Voss, out of county. Signed, sealed, filed.

Mara frowned at the cause of death. Myocarditis killed plenty of young people, that part was plausible enough. But arrhythmia as a primary finding usually meant somebody had watched the heart stop — telemetry, an EKG strip, at minimum a witnessed arrest. There was no EKG in the file. No strip, no tracing, nothing.

**Document two:** Report of death by the attending medical examiner. Not the county ME. Not even the regional one. An out-of-town examiner brought in from three hours east, a Dr. — Mara squinted at the signature — *A. Rensselaer*, whose credentials were listed but whose license number, when she checked it against the state registry later that afternoon, belonged to a man who had retired to Florida in 2011 and, according to the registry's own note, had performed no examinations after that date.

So either Dr. Rensselaer had come out of retirement for one very quiet weekend in Hollow Creek, or the signature was worth exactly what it cost to forge it.

**Document three through seven:** Burial permit. Transit permit. Embalming report. Cemetery interment order. Cremation authorization — signed but, per the interment order, never executed. Each form filled out in the same tidy hand. Each form correct. Each form *perfect*, and that was the thing that kept snagging in Mara's chest like a fishhook. Real paperwork has errors. Real paperwork has a smudge where someone sneezed, a date corrected in pen, a middle initial forgotten and added later. This file had none. Eleven years old and it looked like it had been printed yesterday by a machine that did not sweat.

**Document eight:** A single-page family statement, unsigned by anyone but a notary, declaring the family's wish for privacy and a closed casket. No request for autopsy review. No independent examination. No second opinion. The Brandt family — a family that, per town legend, once demanded a recount when August Brandt lost a church raffle by two dollars — had declined to verify their own heiress's death.

Mara set that page down and looked out the window at the parking lot for a while.

**Documents nine and ten:** Correspondence. Two letters, both brief, both on Brandt Industries letterhead, thanking the county for its discretion. *Discretion.* Twice. In writing. Filed, as though it were a routine courtesy rather than a word that made Mara's back teeth itch.

**Document eleven:** The witness attestation. The form confirming that the death had been properly attended, identified, and processed. Standard county form, the same one Mara signed a dozen times a month. It required three signatures: the attending physician (Voss), the medical examiner (Rensselaer), and the attending witness — the person physically present who could swear the body on the table was the body in the paperwork.

Mara read the third signature.

Then she took off her glasses, cleaned them on her shirttail, and read it again.

*Rosa Ilić.*

That name meant nothing to her. But the handwriting — the handwriting rang a bell she hadn't known she owned, a small dull chime somewhere below memory. She stared at it, certain she'd seen those looping capitals recently, and unable to place where, and hating the feeling the way she hated an unidentified odor in the prep room: professionally, personally, and with a growing conviction that it was going to get worse before it got better.

She photographed every page with her phone, returned the file exactly as she'd found it — Wendell would know otherwise; Wendell knew everything otherwise — and walked out into the gray afternoon with the chime still ringing.

* * *

It came to her at the morgue, of all places, standing at her own counter with Daniel Okafor's retainer check in front of her and his intake paperwork fanned beside it.

Every client filled out intake forms. Even clients paying triple rates for services that sounded like a bad joke. Name, address, employer, emergency contact. Most people left the employer line blank or wrote something useless like *self* or *it's complicated*. Daniel Okafor had written an actual answer, in careful block letters:

**EMPLOYER:** Ilić Consulting & Estate Services

Mara had skimmed past it two nights ago, filed it under *rich people have strange companies*, and moved on. Now she pulled the folder across the counter and read it four times.

*Ilić.*

Rosa Ilić, attending witness to the death of Elise Brandt, October 9th, 2014.

Ilić Consulting & Estate Services, employer of the sweating man who had slid a photograph across her counter and said, *her name was already on your books once.*

Coincidence existed. Mara believed in coincidence the way she believed in ghosts — theoretically, respectfully, and without ever expecting to meet one in the wild. Surnames repeated. Towns were small. Eleven years was long enough for a woman named Ilić to marry, divorce, die, or vanish, and for some unrelated Ilić to open a consulting firm and hire a nervous man with damp palms.

But.

But the file was too clean. But the examiner's license belonged to a retiree. But a family famous for litigating everything hadn't asked a single question about their own daughter's heart. And now the man paying her to prove a murder hadn't happened yet was employed by a firm bearing the surname of the one witness who'd sworn the death was real.

Mara sat down on her stool, which complained in the manner of all her furniture, and pulled the photograph of the woman from the folder. Dark hair. Sharp jaw. Eyes that looked at the camera the way people look at doors they're planning to leave through. Elise Brandt, alive in the picture, dead on paper, and buried — supposedly — under a stone Mara walked past every Sunday on her rounds through the cemetery she tended like a garden.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the empty room, because talking to herself was cheaper than therapy and the morgue had excellent acoustics. "Somebody's lying. Somebody's always lying. The question is who, and whether they're lying to me or just around me."

She thought about Daniel Okafor's face when he'd said it. *Prove a murder hasn't happened yet.* He hadn't looked like a con man. Con men were comfortable; comfort was the whole product. Daniel had looked like a man carrying something heavy in a bag with a hole in it, hoping to reach his destination before the contents spilled.

Or he looked exactly like a man who wanted her to think that. She was honest enough with herself to hold both possibilities, the way she held two organ weights in a pan, waiting for the scale to settle.

She opened her laptop and searched. *Ilić Consulting & Estate Services.* The results were sparse in a way that was itself informative: a registered address in a neighboring county, a filing date six years ago, no website, no reviews, no phone number that connected to anything but a voicemail with a full mailbox. Companies like that weren't companies. They were envelopes. Something went in, something came out, and nobody was meant to look at the paper between.

Except Daniel had come to *her*. Out of every official in the county — sheriff, deputies, district attorney, three judges — he'd walked into the morgue of a coroner with no ties to the Brandt trust and asked her to prove a negative. Either he was the best judge of character in Hollow Creek, or someone had told him exactly which door didn't answer to August Brandt.

Both thoughts were flattering. Neither was comforting.

Mara closed the laptop. Outside, the light was going the color of old bone, and the first real chill of autumn pressed its face against the windows. She had a body drawer to log, a supply order to finish, and a cemetery to walk before dark — she liked to check the stones after windstorms, righting the small flags, noting the leaning markers the way a gardener notes drooping stems.

Before she left, she did one last thing. She printed the witness page from her phone, the signature enlarged, and taped it inside the cabinet above her desk where she kept the cases that wouldn't leave her alone. There were only three other pages there, from three other years, and she liked to think of them as her garden too — things planted in suspicion, waiting to see what grew.

*Rosa Ilić,* the page said, in loops and capitals too neat for a frightened woman and too careful for a casual one.

Tomorrow, Mara decided, she would find out who Rosa Ilić had been, and where she'd gone, and why her name was the only living thread in a file stitched shut by dead men and retired doctors. Tomorrow she would start asking the questions this town had apparently agreed, eleven years ago, to stop asking.

She turned off the lights. In the dark, the morgue did what it always did — settled around its silence, patient as a held breath — and Mara locked the door behind her and stepped into the evening thinking about closed caskets.

Nobody in Hollow Creek had seen Elise Brandt's face in death except the people paid to swear they had. No family viewing. No open casket, though the Brandts could have afforded a cathedral of flowers and a glass lid. Just a closed box, lowered into good soil, under a stone with dates on it.

And a town that had looked at that closed box and, to a person, decided not to wonder what was inside.

Mara wondered. It was, she reflected as she walked to her truck, beginning to look like the most dangerous thing a person in Hollow Creek could do.
