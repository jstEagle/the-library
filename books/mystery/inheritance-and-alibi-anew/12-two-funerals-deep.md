## 12. Two Funerals Deep

The reservoir at dawn looked like a sheet of hammered tin, gray and dented with rain, and I stood on the boat ramp thinking that in eleven years of coroner work I had never once been called to a body before my coffee.

Deputy Corinne Ash was already there, hands jammed in her jacket pockets, watching two men from the county dive team wade out of the shallows. Her face had the particular set it got when she was angry and trying to look merely professional — jaw forward, eyebrows neutral, the whole arrangement about as convincing as a screen door on a submarine.

"Morning, Mara," she said. "You're going to want to see this one."

"I always want to see this one. That's the job."

"Not like this." She lowered her voice as the divers came closer, hauling the stretcher between them. "It's Harold Finch."

I knew the name before she finished saying it, the way you know a word you've read a hundred times but never heard spoken aloud. Harold Finch. County archivist, retired. The man whose neat, patient signature sat at the bottom of Elise Brandt's death certificate like a period at the end of a very long sentence.

"Harold Finch," I repeated, because sometimes saying a thing out loud tells you whether you believe it.

The diver nearest us nodded, water streaming off his hood. "Fisherman found him snagged on the intake grate around five-thirty. Called it in."

* * *

Harold Finch lay on my table looking smaller than any man has a right to look, though to be fair, most people do by the time they reach me. He'd been a slight man in life — I remembered him vaguely from records requests, a stooped figure in cardigans who spoke of filing systems the way other men speak of grandchildren. Now he was pale, swollen at the extremities, and wearing a navy windbreaker that someone had buttoned wrong.

I started where I always start: with the hands.

There were no defensive wounds. No ligature marks, no bruising on the wrists or forearms, no petechial hemorrhaging in the eyes that would suggest strangulation. His fingernails were clean — too clean, actually, scrubbed to the quick, which is not what drowning does to a man's hands. Drowning gives you silt under the nails, fibers from whatever you grabbed at, skin cells from your own frantic clawing. It does not give you a manicure.

"Huh," I said, to nobody, which is how I do my best thinking.

The lungs told the next part of the story. A true drowning fills them — frothy edema, waterlogged tissue, diatoms in the alveoli if the water's right. Finch's lungs held some fluid, certainly, but the pattern was wrong. Post-mortem immersion, the passive flooding of a body that went into the water already dead. And the water in his lungs didn't match the reservoir anyway. I sent samples off to the lab, but I could smell the difference before the results ever came back. Reservoir water smells like stone and algae and old boats. This smelled faintly of iron — well water. Cistern water. Somebody's basement.

Time of death put him underwater twelve to eighteen hours before the fisherman's call. Which meant he died the previous afternoon, somewhere dry, and took his evening swim posthumously.

Then there was the matter of the note.

Corinne brought it to me in an evidence bag, and I have to hand it to her — she carried it like it was something dead, which, in a way, it was. A single sheet, folded once, found weighted under a rock on the boat ramp. Neat handwriting. *I did what they asked me to do eleven years ago and I can't carry it anymore. Tell Daniel Okafor he should have stayed away.*

"Well," I said. "That's convenient."

"That's what I said." Corinne pulled a chair over and sat down heavily, the way she only did when we were alone and the door was shut. "Sheriff wants to call it suicide-with-confession. Says Finch cracked under guilt, drowned himself, left a note naming his accomplice. Open and shut by lunch."

"And you?"

She was quiet for a moment. Outside, rain ticked against the window in the rhythm of a town that had learned long ago to mind its own business.

"Mara, I ran Okafor's alibi last night. For the window the sheriff's working with — say Finch dies between noon and six. Daniel Okafor was at the feed store arguing about fence posts until four-fifteen, then at the diner having meatloaf with Reverend Calloway until nearly seven. Three witnesses, one of whom is a *minister*, and another of whom is Wendell Pruitt, who would sell his own grandmother's alibi for a discount on chicken feed but apparently gives honest ones away free." She rubbed her eyes. "It's airtight. That's what scares me. Whoever wrote that note either didn't check Okafor's movements, or checked them and didn't care, because the point was never to convict him. The point was to make sure that when the exhumation came back wrong, there was already a story waiting. A man. A confession. A tidy little bow."

I looked down at Harold Finch, who had spent thirty years keeping the county's secrets in careful alphabetical order, and felt something cold settle behind my ribs.

"They're not framing him for murder," I said slowly. "They're framing him for *narrative*. You don't need a jury when you've got a story good enough that nobody asks questions."

* * *

The sheriff held his press conference on the courthouse steps at four o'clock, because small towns hold their press conferences on courthouse steps the way cathedrals hold mass — it's liturgy, not logistics. There were maybe nine reporters, two of whom were from the county paper and one of whom was Wendell Pruitt's nephew, which meant the coverage would be thorough but might contain a coupon for tractor tires.

I stood in the back and listened to Sheriff Dale Hutchins explain, in the warm baritone of a man announcing a church picnic, that Harold Finch had taken his own life out of remorse for his role in an eleven-year-old fraud, and that a Mr. Daniel Okafor, recently arrived in Hollow Creek, was being sought for questioning in connection with related irregularities.

*Related irregularities.* Dale Hutchins had a gift. He could make a corpse sound like a zoning dispute.

He got through the whole speech without once mentioning that the medical examiner's preliminary findings contradicted every word of it. Because he hadn't asked for my findings. Because he'd declared cause of death before I'd finished the autopsy, which is a little like a chef declaring the meal delicious before the cow is slaughtered.

When he opened the floor, I raised my hand.

Now, I want to be clear about something. I am not a brave woman. Bravery implies a choice made against fear, and I mostly operate on a kind of stubbornness so deep-seated it doesn't register as courage — it registers as indigestion. But when Dale pointed at me with the expression of a man hoping I'd ask about parking, I heard myself say:

"Sheriff, did you find water in Mr. Finch's lungs?"

A pause. The kind of pause that has weight and texture. "The investigation is ongoing—"

"Because I performed the autopsy, Sheriff, and I did. Water, yes. Just not reservoir water. The mineral content is consistent with well water — hard water, high iron, the kind you'd find in a cistern or a basement sump. There are perhaps forty houses in this county with cisterns, and I'd be happy to provide the list." I let that sit. Nine reporters leaned forward in unison, like a field of sunflowers finding the sun. "Additionally, Mr. Finch showed no signs of antemortem struggle, his lungs lack the frothy edema consistent with live drowning, and his fingernails were scrubbed clean after death. Whatever happened to Harold Finch, Sheriff, he did not walk into this reservoir alive, and he did not write that note with his own hand — the tremor in the script doesn't match the mild palsy in his writing hand, which I documented from his archived signatures. So when you say 'suicide,' I'd appreciate it if you'd add the phrase 'according to a narrative currently under review.'"

The silence on the courthouse steps was the loudest thing I'd heard all week.

Dale smiled. It was a good smile, practiced, the smile of a man who had shaken ten thousand hands at ten thousand pancake breakfasts. But his eyes had gone flat and still, like water over something sunk deep.

"Dr. Vasquez's findings are preliminary," he said pleasantly. "We thank her for her service."

*Her service.* Like I was a volunteer fire department. Like I'd bake something.

I walked back to my truck through a gauntlet of reporters shouting questions, and Corinne fell into step beside me, and neither of us said anything until we reached the square.

"You know what you just did," she said quietly.

"I gave a press conference without a podium."

"You made yourself a problem. For people who solve problems permanently." She glanced up at the Brandt estate on the hill, where the windows caught the last of the gray light. "Eleven years ago, Harold Finch filed a false death certificate, and everybody in this county agreed not to notice. Last week you dug up the lie. Yesterday somebody drowned him in a basement and threw him in the reservoir with a confession in his pocket. Mara — you're the only person left who's publicly said the official story is wrong. Do the math on who's next on the list."

I did the math. It was short arithmetic.

That night I went home, fed the cat, checked the locks twice, and slept with the file on Elise Brandt on the nightstand like a talisman, which is a stupid thing to sleep next to unless you understand that paper is the only weapon I've ever been any good with.

Somewhere around three in the morning, the phone rang.

It was Corinne, and she wasn't whispering, which meant she was past whispering. "Mara. I just pulled the anonymous tips the department's been getting. Weeks of them. All about Okafor. All in the same neat handwriting." A breath. "It's the same hand as the note in Finch's pocket. And Mara — I ran it against the archive samples. Against the old county correspondence."

"Don't," I said, though of course she already had.

"It matches the letters Harold Finch used to send to the commissioners' office. It's close enough to be the same person practicing the same penmanship for years." Another breath, shakier. "Whoever killed him learned to write like him first. They studied him, Mara. They studied him the way you study a man you're going to replace."

I sat up in the dark, cat complaining, heart doing something unscheduled.

And that was when I remembered — with the particular, sickening clarity that arrives only at three a.m. — that Harold Finch hadn't just filed Elise Brandt's death certificate. He'd kept the originals. Every document, every signature, every page of the thinnest, cleanest file I'd ever seen. Filed away in the county archive for eleven years.

And now the archivist was dead, and the sheriff was asking the clerk's office this morning to seal the Brandt materials pending investigation.

Whatever Harold Finch had been carrying all these years, somebody had finally come to collect it.

And I had a terrible feeling they weren't done collecting.
