## 4. Anniversary Preparations

The invitations went out on cream stock, letterpressed, two hundred and forty of them, and Corinne Hale had personally approved the font.

That was the kind of detail people laughed at until they understood it. The typeface was Caslon Old Style, the same face used on the original 1874 town charter, and when the guests held their invitations under the chandeliers of the Marlowe House ballroom, they would feel — without knowing why — that they were holding something continuous. Something unbroken. A hundred and fifty years, set in metal and ink. That was the whole art of a celebration like this: you didn't tell people the story was intact. You made them feel it in their hands.

Corinne stood at the long window of her office on the second floor of city hall, watching the workmen string lights across Founders' Square, and allowed herself ten seconds of satisfaction. The square had been power-washed on Tuesday. The cracked flagstones around the fountain were being replaced with salvaged granite from the old mill foundation, which was either a lovely gesture toward authenticity or an irony so perfect she refused to look at it directly. Bunting in the town colors — deep green and gold, Edith Marlowe's colors, though nobody alive remembered choosing them — draped the bandstand where the high school orchestra would play.

One hundred and fifty years since Elias Marlowe signed the charter with eleven other men whose names now lived on street signs, a reservoir, a scholarship fund, and the brass plaques inside the First Congregational vestibule. One hundred and fifty years, and the town had exactly one founding family left with any money, three with any land, and one name that belonged to everyone: Marlowe.

Edith Marlowe, who had built the library wing and endowed the hospital and paid for the bandstand roof twice. Edith Marlowe, dead these nineteen years at seventy-seven, mourned by the whole valley, buried with full honors beneath a granite marker in Hillcrest Cemetery.

Edith Marlowe, whose coffin had come up out of the ground last Thursday empty as a promise.

Corinne turned away from the window.

* * *

The meeting was scheduled for nine-fifteen and Police Chief Danvers arrived at nine-twelve, which told her he'd been sitting in his car working up to it. She let him wait four minutes anyway while she finished annotating the dinner seating chart. Not cruelty. Calibration. Danvers was a decent man in the way of men who had risen exactly as far as their decency could carry them, and he responded better to a small demonstration of hierarchy than to charm.

"Close the door, Chief."

He did. He sat across from her desk with his hat on his knee like a man visiting a sick relative, and she noticed he hadn't brought a file folder. That was bad. Men brought folders when they wanted you to read something. Men came empty-handed when they wanted you to hear something.

"Tell me about the exhumation," she said.

"You know about the exhumation."

"I know what's in the summary your office sent me, which is one page and reads like it was written by someone being paid per word." She folded her hands. "I want the version you'd tell your wife."

Danvers looked at the window, the flags, the floor. "Cemetery relocation for the east expansion. Standard order, standard contractor. Crew gets down to the vault, opens the box, and there's no one home." He said it flatly, but his jaw worked around the last word. "No remains. No clothing. No jewelry. The coffin's original to the burial — 2006, funeral home seal intact, lead lining unbreached. Whatever happened, happened before she went in the ground."

"Or the seal was forged."

"Or the seal was forged." He shifted. "Corinne, I've got a gravedigger who won't come back to work, a funeral director asking me questions I don't have answers for, and a reporter from the *Clarion* who filed a records request this morning. This is not a thing that stays quiet on its own."

"It stays quiet if we close it."

"With what finding?"

"With whatever finding is true and boring." She stood and moved to the window, because looking at the square made the argument for her. "Danvers. In nine days, two hundred and forty people are going to sit in that ballroom and eat chicken in champagne sauce and listen to speeches about continuity and character, and every single one of them is going to be thinking about one thing, and it isn't the orchestra. You cannot give them an open grave and a shrug. So you're going to give them a closed question."

"And if the answer isn't boring?"

She turned. "Then I'll need to know that before the *Clarion* does. That's all I'm asking. Sequence, Chief. Truth has a schedule like everything else."

He put his hat on. It was his way of ending meetings he hadn't won. "There's one more thing," he said at the door. "The exhumation order. The original one, from '06, authorizing the burial — that's normal paperwork. But there's a second signature on the relocation file. Recent. Somebody signed off on moving her again last month, before the cemetery project ever came to council."

Corinne felt the room tilt very slightly, the way a picture frame tilts when a truck passes. "Whose signature?"

"That's what I'm trying to find out. The copy in the file is a stamp copy. The original's in county records." He paused in the doorway. "You didn't know about it."

It wasn't a question, and she understood suddenly that the whole meeting had been him checking. Checking whether the empty coffin was hers. Whether the machinery of the town had done this thing without him, the way it did so many things.

"No," she said. "I didn't."

He nodded slowly, and left, and Corinne Hale sat down at her desk and looked at the seating chart for a long time without seeing it.

* * *

Her family name was on the charter too. HALE, in the same proud capitals as MARLOWE, third column, ninth line, and she had spent forty-six years being introduced by it. At boarding school it had opened doors. At the bank it had opened credit. When she ran for council at thirty-one, unopposed, the name had done the campaigning while she shook hands. People said *the Hale girl* the way they said *the Marlowe legacy* — as if both were heirlooms kept in glass, dusted by servants, insured against loss.

Her grandfather, Aldous Hale, had been on the development committee in the fifties. Her father had chaired it through the seventies. Corinne herself had signed the papers creating the Heritage Development Fund in 1998, a vehicle so clean and so dull that no journalist had ever gotten past its first page. The fund bought land. The fund sold land. The fund had, over three generations, moved rather a lot of the town's history into rather few pockets, and every transaction had been legal, or legal enough, or had been made legal retroactively by men who understood that legality was also a thing you could build.

She had never stolen anything. That was the sentence she carried, polished smooth from handling. She had never stolen anything; she had inherited arrangements, the way you inherit a house with wiring you didn't install and fires you didn't set and a smell in the walls you can't locate and can't escape. And she profited from it daily, quarterly, annually, in distributions she no longer read closely because reading them closely would require deciding something, and she had structured her entire life to avoid the moment of decision.

Now there was an empty coffin in a town getting ready to throw itself a party, and a second signature she hadn't known about, and somewhere out in the dark a question was walking around wearing her family's name like a borrowed coat.

* * *

At noon she walked the ballroom of Marlowe House herself, because delegating inspection was how things got missed. The caterers were installing the bar along the east wall. The florist had delivered white peonies — Edith's flower, another detail nobody questioned — and the rental company was unrolling the dance floor over parquet older than the state highway system.

On the platform at the north end stood the thing she'd come to see: the memorial, shrouded in green velvet, six feet tall, waiting for Friday night. Underneath the cloth was a bronze relief of Edith Marlowe's profile at forty, cast from a photograph, commissioned quietly, paid for from the discretionary line. Beneath the profile, once the cloth fell, guests would read the inscription Corinne had written herself, revised eleven times:

*EDITH MARLOWE — WHO GAVE THE TOWN ITS SHAPE.*

She had fought with herself over that verb tense for a week. *Gave.* Past, settled, complete. A woman who gave the town its shape was a woman safely inside history, a woman with a fixed quantity of shape-giving already accomplished, a woman who could not give anyone any further trouble.

A woman who was, presumably, in the ground.

"Mrs. Hale?" The event coordinator, a competent young woman named Priya, appeared at her elbow with a clipboard. "Question about the toast sequence. Mr. Voss wants to go after the mayor, but the historical society thinks the oldest descendant should—"

"Voss goes fourth," Corinne said. "After the historical society, before the video tribute. If he complains, tell him the program follows the chronology of the families' arrival, and the Vosses came in 1881."

Priya wrote it down. Then, hesitating: "There's also — the police called the venue office this morning. Asking about our security camera coverage for the parking lot. For last week? They wouldn't say why."

Something cold walked up Corinne's spine and took a seat between her shoulder blades.

"Did they say which evening?"

"Thursday. Night of the exhumation."

The exhumation had happened in daylight. Whatever the cameras might show, it wasn't the digging. Which meant the police were looking for someone who had gone to Hillcrest Cemetery after dark, after the empty coffin was discovered, and stood over it. Or knelt. Or taken something. Or left something.

"Forward anything they request," Corinne said. "Promptly. We have nothing to hide."

The words came out smooth and true and completely beside the point, and Priya nodded and moved on down the guest list, and Corinne stood alone in the beautiful room looking at the shrouded memorial, thinking: *someone went to the grave at night.* Someone in this town had heard the news and driven out to look at an open hole in the earth, and now the police were pulling thread, and nine days stood between her and two hundred and forty witnesses.

* * *

She made one call she shouldn't have, from her car in the municipal lot, using the phone she kept for calls she shouldn't make. It rang four times.

"It's handled," said the voice on the other end, before she spoke. An old man's voice, unhurried, dry as ledger paper. "The county record will take weeks to pull. Longer, if the clerk's vacation extends."

"I didn't call about the record."

Silence, the particular silence of a man recalibrating. "No," he said. "You wouldn't."

"The chief says there's a second signature. On the relocation order. Last month." She watched a taxi roll past the lot entrance, one of the fleet's older cars, its roof light glowing yellow in the noon glare. "If someone in our arrangement is acting independently—"

"Corinne." The voice softened into something almost paternal, which was how she knew to be afraid. "In ninety years, this town has survived fire, flood, two wars, and the seventies. It will survive a hole in the ground. Go plan your dinner. Let me worry about signatures."

The line went dead. She sat with the phone in her lap and realized she had just learned two things at once: that the second signature belonged to the arrangement, and that the arrangement had acted without telling her — which meant there were now layers inside the thing she'd inherited, doors in her own house she'd never been shown.

Across the lot, the taxi pulled to the curb and idled, waiting on a fare outside the pharmacy. Just a cab. Just the town going about its business, its thousand small movements, deliveries and pickups and shifts changing hands — the circulatory system she'd spent her life managing from the top, confident that blood went where it was pumped.

She thought, briefly and absurdly, of her grandmother's saying: *a town keeps its secrets the way a river keeps its stones. You only see the ones it wants to turn over.*

The taxi pulled away. Corinne started her car, checked her lipstick in the mirror, and drove back toward the square, where the lights were half-strung and the bunting fluttered and the granite from the old mill foundation was being laid over the cracks, stone over stone, the past paving over the past, everything smooth and level and ready for company in nine days.

Behind her, at the curb, the dispatcher in the taxi's front seat wrote something in a log book, and Corinne Hale did not see it, and would not think of it again until it was far too late.
