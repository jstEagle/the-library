## 11. Corinne's Ledger

The archive room smelled of dust and old toner and, faintly, of the river that ran beneath the municipal building — a damp mineral breath that seeped up through the foundation no matter how many dehumidifiers the city budgeted. Corinne Hale had spent forty minutes of her Tuesday morning in that smell, and she was beginning to understand that she had made a mistake by coming herself.

She should have sent someone. She should have sent a junior clerk with a records request and a plausible reason, someone whose face meant nothing, whose name would never appear in a sentence that began *the deputy mayor was seen*. Instead she had walked down two flights past the planning office, past the assessor's window where Dolores Kwan had looked up with that small, courteous curiosity that Corinne had spent eleven years learning to read, and let herself into the archive with the master key that only three people in Halloway possessed.

The route books sat where they had always sat. That was the first wrong thing.

They occupied the bottom shelf of the steel case nearest the door — the taxi company's records had been absorbed into the municipal archive in 1989, when the city bought out the last of the independent operators, and nobody had touched them since except the occasional history student and, once, a man doing a genealogy who'd cried at the counter because his grandfather's name appeared in the fare logs. Corinne remembered him. She remembered everything, that was her curse and her craft: she remembered the crying genealogist, the water stain on the ceiling tile above the case, the exact gray of the boxes, the way the spines of the route books leaned in a tired row like old men at a bus stop.

Now the row leaned wrong. There was a gap between 1973 and 1975, a gap the width of a single volume, and the remaining books had slumped into it like teeth shifting after an extraction.

Corinne stood very still in the archive's green light and let the knowledge arrive the way bad news always arrived in her life — not all at once, but in pieces, each one worse than the last, delivered by her own memory with the dispassion of a court reporter.

She pulled the neighboring volumes out. 1973. 1975. She checked the accession ledger clipped inside the case door — a habit from her first year on the council, when she'd still believed the town's records were kept for the town's benefit. The checkout column was blank. Of course it was blank. Whoever had taken the book hadn't signed for it, which meant they hadn't come through the front desk, which meant they'd either picked the lock like a burglar or walked in with a key like a colleague.

There were three keys. Hers. The city clerk's. And the spare that hung, God help her, on a hook in the dispatch office of Halloway Taxi, where it had hung for thirty years because the taxi company's records had been deposited by the company and the company had never surrendered its right of access.

Bram Dravenmoor.

Corinne closed the case door with a click that sounded, in the silent room, like a small door locking in a much larger one.

* * *

She did not go straight back to her office. That was the discipline her mother had taught her, or perhaps her mother had taught her nothing and Corinne had simply invented the discipline out of the raw material of watching her mother fail at everything else. When the ground shifted under you, you did not run — running drew the eye, and the eye was where power lived. You walked. You smiled at Dolores Kwan. You asked after her daughter's college applications and listened to the answer with your whole face, and you carried the catastrophe upstairs in your chest like a stone in a coat pocket, weightless to everyone but you.

In her office she closed the blinds halfway — fully closed blinds were a signal, half-closed were a habit — and sat at her desk and did not touch the phone.

Her grandfather's portrait watched her from the wall above the filing cabinet. Not her actual grandfather; the town's grandfather, which was worse. The sesquicentennial committee had commissioned the portrait two years ago, Edith Marlowe's founding circle rendered in oils: Marlowe herself at the center, young and iron-eyed, and flanking her the four families whose names were carved into the courthouse lintel and the reservoir dam and the scholarship fund and the cemetery gates. Hale. Voss. Ashcombe. Brand. Her great-grandfather stood second from the left with his hand on a surveyor's tripod, and the painter had given him a look of such benign industry that Corinne, who had read what the man had actually signed, sometimes had to leave her own office to avoid his painted gaze.

The development fund. That was the polite name for it, the name on the letterhead, the name that had built the community center and the industrial park and half the marina — and had also, across four generations, in a mechanism so old and so oiled that it no longer required anyone to understand it, moved the town's common wealth into the town's five families and called the motion growth. Corinne hadn't built the machine. Her great-grandfather had built it, or bought it, or married it; the records blurred before 1930. She had simply inherited the keys, the way you inherited a house with a bad foundation, and by the time she understood what the foundation was made of, the house was her address and her name was on the deed and there was nowhere else in the world she knew how to live.

She was not a thief. She needed that to be true, and it was true, in the way that mattered at three in the morning. She had never taken a dollar that wasn't routed to her by paperwork older than her father. She had never forged a signature, never threatened a soul, never — she was almost certain — been in a room where a death was arranged. What she had done was quieter and more total: she had maintained. She had signed the annual certifications. She had smoothed the audits. She had, last spring, made a phone call to the state archivist that resulted in a records request being quietly narrowed, and she had told herself it was for the town's stability, for the sesquicentennial, for the two hundred employees at the Voss mill whose livelihoods depended on the town's credit rating, and the telling had almost worked.

And now a taxi dispatcher with no family, no stake, no name that appeared in any of the founding documents — a woman the town had never made room for and therefore could not be shamed into protecting — was walking around with a 1974 route book under her arm.

Corinne opened her desk drawer and took out the folder she thought of, only in the privacy of her own skull, as the family Bible. Inside were the things she'd assembled over eleven years, not to use but to know: the list of the fund's disbursements by decade, the names of the auditors who had looked and the names of the auditors who had been helped to stop looking, the obituary of a mill foreman named Garrett Voss — no relation, the town had always enjoyed that coincidence — who had fallen into the sluice gate in March, and the obituary of a council clerk named Peter Ibarra whose car had left Route 9 in November, and the clipping about a teenage runner, Alyssa Chen, dead on the rail trail in June. Three accidents in one year. The police had closed all three with the efficiency of a town that had practice.

And then, last month, the exhumation order. Corinne had signed the cover memo herself, routing it to Judge Ashcombe because the Ashcombes had always handled the Marlowe estate's legal instruments and it would have been stranger not to. She had told herself the exhumation was housekeeping: the cemetery relocation required it, the families wanted certainty, and above all it was better that the city controlled the opening of that box than that some contractor opened it by accident with a backhoe and a cell phone camera.

She had not known, when she signed, whether the box was empty. She had suspected. There was a difference, and she had clung to it for a month, the way you cling to a rail in a flood: *I didn't know, I only suspected, suspicion is not a crime.* And then the box had come up empty on a Tuesday morning with a county medical examiner present and a gravedigger named Tuck whose discretion was legendary and whose silence had been purchased by people whose names Corinne didn't let herself say, and for four weeks the story had been contained — a clerical anomaly, a misfiled burial, a bureaucratic ghost — and Corinne had begun, very carefully, to breathe.

Now the route book was gone, and the only person in Halloway with a key to the archive and a reason to want it was the dispatcher who had, by every quiet report, been asking questions about Edith Marlowe's handwriting.

* * *

She called Marty Brand at noon, because that was what she did instead of panicking — she gathered information, and Marty was the information's most convenient door. Marty Brand, the taxi company's mechanic, third of his name, a man whose family had been in the founding portrait's orbit for a hundred and fifty years and had come out of that orbit with nothing but a garage and a loyalty that Corinne had always found slightly unnerving in its purity.

"Marty. Corinne Hale."

"Deputy Mayor." He always used the title, flat as a level. "What's broke?"

"The archive, possibly. Has anyone from your shop been into the municipal records lately? The route books. We're doing a condition survey before the anniversary and a volume seems to be misplaced."

A silence on the line, three seconds long, which from Marty was an oration. "Nobody from the shop," he said. "Bram handles the records requests. She's been in and out all month with the sesquicentennial pull. You'd have to ask her."

"I intend to." Corinne kept her voice at the temperature of municipal business, warm as a waiting room. "You'll be at the dinner, of course. Bringing your mother?"

"Wouldn't miss it," Marty said, in the voice of a man who would rather miss everything, and hung up.

Corinne set the phone down and looked at her grandfather's portrait and felt the thing she always felt, the thing she had never said aloud to anyone, not to her husband, not to her therapist, not to the dark: *I did not choose this. I was born into the debt the way you're born into weather.* Her great-grandfather had signed the fund's founding instrument with Edith Marlowe across a table in the Marlowe house in 1911, and every Hale since had signed the renewals, and the renewals had grown teeth, and the teeth had grown habits, and the habits had, somewhere in the 1970s, acquired a body count that the town called accidents because the alternative was a grief the town could not have survived and remained the town.

She had a conscience. That was the joke of it, the private, unfunny joke: she was the only one of them who did, or at least the only one whose conscience still sent invoices. Old Man Ashcombe was past feeling anything but his digestion. The Voss cousins thought in quarterly numbers. The Brands had spent their conscience generations ago and lived on loyalty instead, which was a cheaper fuel and burned dirtier. But Corinne lay awake. Corinne knew the foreman's widow's name. Corinne had once, drunk at a conference in the capital, looked up Alyssa Chen's high school track records and read them twice and then deleted the browser history like a criminal, like a woman with something to hide, which was the whole of it, wasn't it — she had nothing to hide and hid everything anyway, because the hiding was the inheritance, the actual estate, the thing handed down with the china.

And now she had a choice, and she knew both of its faces because she'd studied them her whole life.

Face one: cut losses. Go to Bram Dravenmoor, or have someone go, and find out what she had and what she wanted. Everyone wanted something. The dispatcher wanted, what — to belong, finally, to matter, to be seen by the town that had used her as furniture for twenty years? That was purchasable. Recognition, a position, a consulting arrangement with the police department, her name in the sesquicentennial program. People who had been ignored all their lives could be bought with attention cheaper than with money, and Corinne was very good at buying.

Face two: protect the name. The name was not hers, that was the thing her conscience kept insisting on at the worst hours — the name was a trust, a ruinous trust, a trust that had been administered with sluice gates and brake lines, but a trust nonetheless, and there were living people whose whole world stood on it. Dolores Kwan's pension. The mill's two hundred paychecks. Her own daughter, who was at college believing her grandfather had built the community center out of goodness.

Face one was surrender and survival. Face two was war and the family. Corinne had spent her whole life being told these were the only two doors, and it had taken her fifty-three years to notice that both doors were painted on the same wall, and that the men who built the wall had never intended for a woman with a conscience to be the one standing in front of it.

She picked up the phone again and dialed a number she kept in her head, not in any device. It rang four times.

"It's Corinne," she said. "I need someone watched. Quietly. A woman, Bram Dravenmoor, she drives for the taxi company, nights mostly, dispatches days. I want to know where she goes, who she sees, and what she carries in and out of that archive. Nothing else. No contact, no accidents—" the word left her mouth before she could catch it, and she heard it land on the other end of the line like a coin in a collection plate, and she hated herself with a fluency that had become almost meditative. "No contact. I want to know what she knows before I decide what to do about it."

A voice said something brief and professional about rates and timelines.

"Fine. Start tonight." She paused, and then, because the conscience insisted on its invoice, she added: "And if she's just a woman doing her job — if this is nothing — you tell me first, before anyone else hears a word of it. That's the arrangement."

She hung up and sat in the half-light of her office with the portrait above the filing cabinet and the folder in her drawer and the stone back in her chest, and she thought about the crying genealogist, and about how a town was just a story that enough people agreed to keep telling, and about how she had spent her whole life as one of the tellers, and how the story was now, for the first time, being read by someone who hadn't agreed to anything.

Below her window, at the curb, a Halloway Taxi cab idled at the light, its roof light off, its driver's face unreadable behind the glare of noon. Corinne watched it turn toward the archive side of the square and did not see it again.

She opened the family Bible, took out a fresh sheet, and began — the way her great-grandfather had begun, the way every Hale in a crisis had begun — to write down what the town must never find out, so that she would know exactly how much she had left to lose.

The list was shorter than it had been a month ago. That was the part that frightened her. Not the names on it. The arithmetic.

By Friday, she knew, she would need to decide whether Bram Dravenmoor was a problem to be solved or a witness to be survived — and somewhere across town, in a dispatch office smelling of coffee and carbon paper, the woman with the missing route book was already, at this very hour, reading.
