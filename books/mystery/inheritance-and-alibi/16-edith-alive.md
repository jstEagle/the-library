## 16. Edith Alive

The lake kept its own calendar.

For fifty-two years it had told Edith Marlowe nothing but weather — the ice going out in April, the loons returning like creditors, the water going glass-still in October as if holding its breath for something. She had learned to read it the way she'd once read the faces of the founding families across a hundred anniversary dinners: patiently, cynically, with an eye for the moment the surface lied.

Tonight the surface was lying beautifully. The moon had come up fat and white over the eastern ridge, laying a silver road across the water straight to her dock, and the pines stood black and ceremonial along the shore, and everything — everything — looked like the postcard the chamber of commerce had been selling since before she was born.

Edith sat in the window chair with a blanket over her knees and the television on with the sound low, because her hearing aids turned sudden noises into accusations. The news anchor was a young woman with the kind of polished sympathy they teach you in broadcasting school, and behind her, in footage shot from a helicopter, was the cemetery.

Edith had known this moment would come the way you know a winter is coming. Not the date. Never the date. But the weight of it, the particular gray pressure in the air.

The anchor said the words *exhumation*, *relocation*, *historic cemetery*, and then the anchor said the other words, the ones that had traveled fifty-two years to find her:

*—officials confirmed this evening that the coffin of Edith Marlowe, the town's beloved founder, was discovered to be empty.*

Edith turned the sound up one notch. She wanted to hear how they'd say it. She had imagined this sentence so many times, in so many decades, that hearing it spoken aloud by a stranger felt almost like eavesdropping on her own funeral — which, in a sense, it was. The second one. The one that mattered.

*—sources say the grave was sealed in 1973 and has not been disturbed since. Investigators have not commented on—*

She turned it off.

The cabin went quiet in the way only lake cabins can go quiet, a silence with texture to it, filled with the small industrious sounds of an old house: the tick of the wood stove cooling, the mouse-nothing in the walls, the lake lapping at the pilings below like a slow, patient clerk counting something.

"Well," Edith said to the room. "There it is."

Her voice surprised her. It was smaller than the voice in her head. Ninety-six years will do that — the interior voice stays twenty-five and furious while the exterior one dwindles down to a whisper, until you feel like a radio broadcasting on a frequency nobody can receive.

She reached for the bell on the side table — an actual brass handbell, absurd, but her legs had stopped negotiating with stairs two winters ago, and the woman who came up from the village, Ruthie, slept in the back room and was a sound sleeper in the way of the young, who have not yet learned what the night is for.

She didn't ring it. Not yet.

Instead she looked at the desk across the room, at the stack of paper on it, at the fountain pen lying across the top page like a spent match.

The confession.

Fifty-two years of dictation, revision, addition, and subtraction, and it was still unfinished. It had been unfinished in 1974 when she sealed it in the oilskin packet. It had been unfinished when she added the pages about the mill foreman — God, the foreman, Walter Brice, who had climbed the catwalk on a Tuesday morning with an audit in his briefcase and come down the fast way. Unfinished when she'd added the clerk, the runner, the terrible litany of *maintenance*, that word the families used among themselves the way surgeons say *procedure*.

It would be unfinished the day she died, she suspected, because the truth of a town does not have an ending. It has only the last sentence you manage to write before the town writes its own.

* * *

Ruthie came down in her robe, hair in a scarf, squinting. "Miss Marlowe? You rang?"

"I didn't. But sit down anyway. Turn on the radio — no, the television. The late news. There's something you should see before the morning people get here."

Ruthie was thirty-one and had come to Edith four years ago through an agency that described her, in the language of brochures, as a *companion*. What she actually was, was the only person in the world who knew where Edith Marlowe lived, and she had never once, not in four years, asked why an old woman needed to live under a false name at the end of a road that didn't appear on maps. She had asked other things — how Edith took her tea, whether the loons bothered her, what the lake was like to swim in — but never that. Edith had decided, somewhere in the first winter, that this silence was either loyalty or indifference, and that at her age she could no longer afford to know which.

Ruthie turned on the set. The anchor, mercifully, was repeating herself. Edith watched the girl's face as the sentence came around again — *the coffin was empty* — and saw the moment it landed, the small widening of the eyes, the glance toward the back room where Edith slept, the arithmetic happening behind the young face at terrible speed.

"Miss Marlowe," Ruthie said carefully, "that's—"

"That's me, yes."

"I know." Ruthie sat down on the arm of the sofa, very slowly, as though the floor might be listening. "I've known since the second week. You sign things 'E. Marsh' but you hold the pen like you own it."

Edith laughed — a dry, real laugh, the first in days. "You hold a pen like you own it?"

"Everyone in this house does." Ruthie's hands were folded tight in her lap. "Are the people on the news going to come here?"

"No. Not yet. Maybe never. But someone else might, and I need you to do exactly what I say tomorrow, and I need you to not ask me why until it's done. Can you do that?"

Ruthie looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a loon called across the water, that wild wavering note that sounds like laughter and warning in equal measure.

"I've been doing it for four years," Ruthie said. "I just didn't know what for."

"Neither did I, some nights." Edith pulled the blanket higher. "Now go back to bed. And Ruthie — the papers on the desk. If anyone but me touches them, you take the boat across and you don't come back for three days. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And don't look so frightened. I've been dead for fifty-two years. It's the living who should worry."

Ruthie went up the stairs. Edith sat alone with the dark window and the black water and the moon road that led, if you followed it far enough, all the way back to town.

* * *

She did not sleep. She had stopped expecting to, years ago. Sleep, for Edith Marlowe, had ended in 1973, the night she'd lain down in that coffin — a rehearsal, they'd thought, a founder's odd ritual, a closed casket because she'd *insisted*, and the families had been only too happy to oblige, because a dead Edith was a silent Edith, and a silent Edith couldn't sign anything, couldn't audit anything, couldn't remember anything out loud.

She had lain in the box for eleven minutes while the funeral director went to find his glasses, and she had listened to the families in the next room, and she had heard Corinne Hale's grandfather say, in a voice of perfect administrative calm, *"Well. That's the last of that."*

*That.* The development fund. The mill payroll skimmed across four decades. The land taken from the river families in 1931 and resold at ten times the price. The history of the town, laundered year by year into the fortunes of five families, until the theft was indistinguishable from the heritage — until the theft *was* the heritage, polished and commemorated and carved into the anniversary monument.

She had gotten out of the box, and the driver — Marty's father, a quiet man named Aldous Fenwick who drove cab number six and never asked questions until the night he asked one — had driven her to the train station with her own death warrant folded in her handbag.

She had meant to stay gone. That was the part the confession kept circling back to, the part she could never get right on paper. She had meant to vanish cleanly and let the town keep its beautiful lie, because she was seventy-four years old and tired, and because the ledger — her ledger, the one she'd kept in the taxi company's route books where no one would ever look, because no one had ever looked at anything in that office — was insurance, not ammunition. *If they come for me, this burns them. If they don't, it sleeps.*

She had not counted on the itch. That was the honest word for it, the word she'd finally used in the draft Ruthie typed up last spring: *the itch*. Fifty years of watching from the outside — clipping newspaper stories, sending money quietly to the families the town had broken, reading every anniversary speech with a red pen in her mind — and the ledger had grown teeth. Every year the town celebrated her, the lie got a little more load-bearing. Every statue, every scholarship in her name, every *founder's legacy* toast was a brick in a wall built over the bodies of Walter Brice and a council clerk whose name the papers had misspelled and a sixteen-year-old runner whose death they'd called a joyride gone wrong.

She had not sent the ledger out. She had never sent the ledger out. But she had left it findable, and she had learned — late, too late, in the way of all old women — that leaving a weapon findable is the same as pointing it.

* * *

Near three in the morning, she got up — slowly, the way she did everything now, each motion a negotiation — and went to the desk. The lamp threw a small yellow island on the paper. She sat, uncapped the fountain pen, and read the last page she'd dictated, the one from October, and found it wanting.

*The ledger was never meant to surface this way,* it said. *I intended—*

She crossed it out.

That was the trouble with the whole document, she thought. It kept trying to explain her intentions, as if intentions were the thing that mattered. As if the town would read *I never meant for anyone to die* and feel better about the empty coffin, the empty fund, the empty graves of the people the families had *maintained*.

She dipped the pen and wrote instead, in a hand that had gone thin but still held the line like it owned it:

*I have spent fifty-two years being a monument. Monuments are how the guilty forgive themselves — they point at the stone and say, she would have wanted it this way. I am done being stone. Let this page be the last thing I ever permit them to put in my mouth.*

She sat back. The stove had gone cold. The lake had gone silver-to-black, the moon road withdrawn, and somewhere down the shore a loon called again, closer now, that same note — laughter, warning.

She thought about the exhumation order. Two signatures — Ruthie had a cousin who worked at the courthouse and passed along courthouse gossip like contraband, and the second signature had reached Edith a month ago, and it had frightened her more than anything in fifty years. Because there was only one reason to exhume a woman you'd already buried: someone needed to know she was in the box. Someone needed *proof*. And you only need proof when you've started to suspect the thing you buried might have gotten out.

Which meant the families were frightened. Which meant the ledger had moved. Which meant someone — some dispatcher, some clerk, some nobody the town had never made room for — was holding it.

Edith looked at the confession, at the crossed-out line, at the page that now said *I am done being stone.*

She had weighed it all night, the way she'd weighed it for five decades: her peace on one side, the truth on the other. Her peace was a cabin at the end of an unmapped road, a lake that told her the weather, a young woman who held her pen like she owned it. It was not nothing. It was, in fact, everything she had traded the town for, and she had told herself for fifty-two years that the trade was fair.

But the trade had never been hers alone. The price had been paid by Brice, by the clerk, by the boy, by Aldous Fenwick, who had driven her out of her own death and then vanished into his — and by every person in that town who raised a glass to her name without knowing what it stood on.

She uncapped the pen one more time and wrote the final instruction at the bottom of the page, the one she'd been avoiding since October:

*If the ledger has surfaced, do not retrieve it. Do not negotiate. Send the confession — all of it, the whole weight of it — to whoever is holding the ledger, and let it burn the guilty. I would rather be a fire than a monument. I would rather the town hate me for the truth than love me for the lie. I have been dead a long time. It is the living who need the truth, and I am done protecting my peace at their expense.*

She signed it. Not *E. Marsh*.

*Edith Marlowe.*

The pen made a small scratching sound, and then the cabin was quiet, and the old woman sat in the yellow island of lamplight with her name on the page for the first time in half a century, feeling the strangest thing — not fear, not relief, but something like the moment the ice goes out in April: a crack running through everything, and underneath it, the whole dark lake beginning, at last, to move.

She reached for the brass bell, and this time she rang it.

Ruthie came down the stairs two at a time, wide awake now, and stopped when she saw the page.

"Pack a bag," Edith said. "We're going to town."

Ruthie stared. "Town? Why?"

Edith looked past her, out the window, at the water where the moon was finally breaking through — the silver road rebuilt, running shore to shore, all the way back to the place she'd died.

"Because," she said, "I believe someone's about to raise a glass to me."
