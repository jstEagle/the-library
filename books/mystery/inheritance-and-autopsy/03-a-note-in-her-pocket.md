## 3. A Note in Her Pocket

The rain came back around four in the morning, a thin gray hiss against the morgue windows, and Wren Coldharbour was still there.

He had told himself he would go home. He had said it aloud, actually, standing at the sink at eleven, scrubbing the last of the river silt from under his nails, telling the empty room, *Go home, Wren. Sleep.* But home was a house on trust land with his mother's coat missing from its hook and his father's scarf gone from the drawer where it had lain folded for nine years, and sleep was a country he no longer had papers for. So he stayed. He made coffee in the dented percolator that tasted faintly of formalin no matter how many times he ran vinegar through it, and he sat across from the drawer where Jane Doe lay under a sheet, and he waited for her to tell him something.

That was the trade. That had always been the trade. The living lied to him—grieving families, nervous deputies, doctors padding charts, judges signing things they shouldn't—and the dead were the only honest citizens of Marrow's Bend. They couldn't perform. They couldn't soften. A body said what it said, and Wren had built twenty-two years of professional calm on that single foundation: *the dead do not lie.*

But this one was lying. Or something was lying through her. Her lungs had been full of clean river water, yes—but the water carried no silt from below the dam, only surface debris, as if she'd been submerged gently, deliberately, like a letter slipped into an envelope. And now there was the coat. His mother's coat. He'd turned out his own closet at midnight with a flashlight in his teeth, heart going like a trapped bird, and found the gap where it should have hung, and stood there in the cold of his own hallway feeling the first real vertigo of his life—not fear of a thing outside himself but fear of the architecture itself, the floor of his ordered world giving that particular soft creak that precedes collapse.

He went back to her at dawn.

* * *

The autopsy suite smelled of disinfectant and cold steel and, beneath it, the river—that green mineral breath that never fully left a drowned body or the room that held one. Wren tied his apron, pulled on fresh gloves, and uncovered her face first, because that was his ritual. You owed them that. The face before the work. A name might come later; dignity came now.

She looked calmer than she had by the dam. The bloat had settled some overnight in the cooler, and in the flat white light she seemed less like a stranger and more like someone sleeping badly—a woman who had argued with her dreams and lost.

"All right," he said quietly. It was a habit; deputies teased him about it. Talking to the dead like they'd answer. "Let's find out who you are."

He worked the way he always did, methodical, narrating half-aloud for the recorder. Adult female, late thirties, five foot six. Well-nourished. Hands uncalloused except for a writer's callus on the right middle finger—she'd held a pen the way Ada Voss used to hold a pen, low and hard, and he shut that thought down the way you'd trap a wasp under a glass, quickly and without looking at it. No defensive wounds. Lividity consistent with post-mortem submersion. Cause of death: drowning, pending tox.

Then the clothes.

He removed them carefully, item by item, because clothing was evidence and evidence was the one language he trusted. The coat—his mother's coat, wool, dove-gray, brass buttons gone dull—he laid flat on the side table and examined seam by seam under the lamp. Nothing in the pockets. Nothing in the lining. He turned it, checked the hem, checked the cuffs.

And then he saw it.

The left lining, just above the pocket bag, had been restitched. Not factory work. Small, tight, even stitches in black thread—the kind of sewing done slowly, by hand, by someone with patience and time and a reason. Someone hiding something. Someone who wanted the hiding to be found, eventually, by exactly the right person, because anyone else would have missed it entirely, but a coroner undressing a body never misses a repaired seam.

Wren stood very still for a moment. The percolator ticked as it cooled. Rain pressed at the window like fingers.

"Someone sewed into your lining," he told her. "You knew they would. Or you hoped."

He took the scalpel and opened the stitches with the same care he'd use on living flesh, and inside the lining, folded into a square small enough to disappear against the fabric, was a scrap of paper wrapped in waxed cloth to keep the river out.

His hands were steady. Twenty-two years of practice kept them steady. It was everything behind his hands that shook.

He unwrapped the cloth. Inside was a fragment of paper, torn from a larger page, written on in blue-black ink:

**Ask Wren about the winter of the flood.**

Seven words. He read them once at arm's length, professionally, the way he'd read any note recovered from a decedent. Then he read them again from six inches away, and the room tilted—not dramatically, not cinematically, but with the slow sick lean of a boat taking water, the world's horizon sliding off true.

Because he knew the handwriting.

Not recognized—knew. Knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark. The tall ascenders that leaned slightly right, like the writer was always half-turned toward something ahead of her. The crossbar on the *t* that swept back over the whole word like a hand reaching backward. The peculiar open *e* that looked unfinished, impatient, alive. He had read pages and pages of that handwriting once, three years ago, in letters that arrived weekly and then daily and then stopped. Letters he had burned in the cast-iron stove in his office on a night in March, one by one, watching each page curl and blacken and lift, because keeping them felt dangerous and destroying them felt like grief and grief was the only honest option left.

Ada Voss's handwriting. On a scrap sewn into a drowned woman's coat. Addressed—to whom? To whoever found it. To the police. To the state. Or to him, specifically, by name, which meant it was addressed to him, because no one else in Marrow's Bend would understand what the winter of the flood meant, and everyone who did understand had spent three years pretending it meant nothing.

He set the note down on the steel table beside the scale, aligned it parallel to the edge, adjusted it twice, caught himself adjusting it, and made himself stop. His reflection floated ghost-pale in the stainless steel, superimposed over the paper, so that for a moment the words appeared written across his own face.

*Ask Wren about the winter of the flood.*

"I don't know anything about the winter of the flood," he said aloud, and heard how it sounded. Like a man rehearsing.

* * *

Three years ago, in the winter the river took the lower mill road, Ada Voss had come to his office with wet shoes and dry eyes and testimony in her fist. He remembered that much. He remembered the sound of her boots on his tile floor, squelching slightly, and the way she set a manila envelope on his desk and said, "Four men died when that dam let go, Dr. Coldharbour, and the paperwork says two, and I need you to be the second honest person I've met in this town." He remembered the weight of the envelope when he picked it up. He did not remember what happened after. There was a hole where the after should have been—a smooth-edged absence, like a tooth socket healed over, painless until you probed it with your tongue.

What he remembered instead, with terrible clarity, was her funeral. March, gray sky, Pastor Hale's voice doing its practiced tremble over the open ground in the new section of the cemetery. A closed casket, which had struck him even then as odd for a drowning victim recovered whole, but grief makes us polite about oddness. He remembered standing apart from the mourners because he was the coroner and coroners stand apart, and he remembered thinking, as the casket lowered, that he could not recall her face.

Not that it had faded. That it had never been there. He had known Ada Voss—corresponded with her for two years, interviewed her formally twice, drunk bad coffee with her in this very morgue while she asked questions no one else dared ask—and yet at her own funeral his memory offered him nothing above the collar. A scarf, maybe. A shape. The rest was smooth water.

He had told himself it was shock. Everyone had told him it was shock. Dr. Pell had told him, kindly, in the clinic's pale-green consultation room, that traumatic memory loss was common, that the mind protects itself, that Wren had been through a great deal that winter. *Treatment for grief,* the file would say, if Wren ever thought to ask for the file, which he hadn't, because why would he? Grief was weather. You waited it out.

Now he stood in his own autopsy suite holding seven words in handwriting he had personally reduced to ash, and the weather inside him turned.

* * *

He did what he always did with evidence: he documented. Photographed the note in situ, photographed the stitching, measured the scrap, logged it into evidence with a chain-of-custody form filled out in his small precise hand. Item 1, Jane Doe, recovered below the old mill dam. Then he sat down at his desk with the note in a plastic sleeve and pulled the case file he swore he wouldn't pull.

Voss, Ada M. Witness, State v. Coldharbour Family Trust (dismissed). Deceased.

The death certificate was in the file, and he read it the way he read all certificates, hunting for the small dishonesties that live in the margins. Time of death: February 14th, three years ago. Cause: accidental drowning, Marrow River. Recovered: not applicable—body recovered downstream by persons unknown, identified visually by next of kin, none. Signed: Ellery Crane, County Judge. Filed and sealed by court order.

Sealed. He'd signed the receipt for the seal himself, three years ago, another piece of paper passing through his hands without passing through his mind. Sealed records were common enough—witnesses, minors, messy families—but sealing a death certificate was rare, and sealing it *by order of Judge Crane*, Crane who played cards with his father's ghost every Sunday at the Legion hall, Crane who'd held Wren's shoulder at his mother's funeral and said *your daddy would be proud*—

Wren took off his glasses and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw red constellations.

The woman on his table was not Ada Voss. He knew that with a coroner's certainty: age, dentition, bone structure, all wrong, all different. This was some other woman, thirty-eight perhaps, dark-haired, with a writer's callus and a seamstress's patience, who had died of drowning in water that hadn't touched the bottom of the river. Some other woman had been chosen—or had chosen herself—to carry a message sewn into a dead woman's coat, wearing a dead witness's borrowed clothes, floating down to the one man in Marrow's Bend who would recognize the stitching of the lie.

Which meant Ada Voss was either dead and avenged by a stranger, or alive and directing events from somewhere, using the dead as stationery.

Both possibilities were unbearable. He found, to his surprise, that he didn't prefer either one. That was the vertigo of it—grief and suspicion entangling like roots in a shallow grave, each feeding on the other, until he could no longer tell whether what he felt was mourning or paranoia or hope, and hope was the worst of the three, because hope was the one thing in Marrow's Bend that had gotten people killed before.

* * *

Near noon, Deputy Sorrel knocked and stuck his head in, young and freckled and trying too hard to look casual. "Doc. Sheriff wants to know if you've got an ID on your floater yet."

"Not yet." Wren kept his voice level, kept his hands flat on the desk, away from the evidence sleeve. "Dental's coming back. Probably tomorrow."

Sorrel nodded, lingered. His eyes flicked to the table where the coat lay under a sheet, and something crossed his face too fast to name—recognition, or the performance of non-recognition, which looks nearly identical. "Funny thing," he said. "Clothes like that. Nice coat, nice scarf. You don't see quality like that in this town anymore."

"No," Wren agreed. "You don't."

"Well." Sorrel tapped the doorframe twice, a tic Wren had never noticed before and would notice forever after. "When you get that dental back, send it straight up the chain, okay? Don't sit on it. Judge Crane likes to be kept current on the unusual ones."

The door closed. Wren sat in the humming quiet and understood that the message had been delivered in both directions. *Send it up the chain.* And underneath it, gentle as Pell's clinic voice: *we know what you have, and we know you know.*

He looked at the note in its sleeve. Seven words in blue-black ink, leaning slightly right, always half-turned toward something ahead.

Ada Voss had burned through his life once already—her testimony, her disappearance, her funeral with the faceless casket, the letters he'd fed to the stove because keeping them felt like conspiracy and burning them felt like loyalty and neither had brought him peace. Now she had reached up out of whatever grave or hiding place held her, threaded a needle, and sewn her name into a stranger's skin.

*Ask Wren about the winter of the flood.*

He picked up the phone, dialed the county records office, and asked for the sealed file on the Voss estate. The clerk's voice went careful and flat, the way voices did in this town when you asked about certain names. "That record's restricted, Dr. Coldharbour. Court order. You'd need Judge Crane's authorization."

"Of course I would," Wren said, and hung up, and reached for his coat—his own coat, the plain brown one, the one nobody had stolen from his closet yet—and stopped with his hand on the hook.

Tomorrow the dental records would arrive. Tomorrow he would learn whose teeth matched whose, and whether the court-sealed records belonged to the woman on his table or to a girl who'd drowned three winters ago and been buried behind a faceless casket. Either answer cracked the town open wider.

Either way, someone had gone to great lengths to make sure the question landed on his desk.

He switched off the lamp over the autopsy table last, the way he always did, and in the sudden gloom the covered shape of Jane Doe looked less like a patient and more like a letter waiting to be delivered—and Wren Coldharbour, coroner of Marrow's Bend, keeper of the town's honest dead, walked out into the rain carrying the first lie anyone had ever mailed to him directly, addressed in a dead woman's hand.
