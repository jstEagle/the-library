## 7. The Second Body

The call came at 4:17 in the morning, and it did not come from dispatch.

It came from Judge Ellery Crane himself, his voice arriving through the telephone receiver with the dry, papery calm of a man who had already been awake for hours and had already decided what the morning would contain.

"Wren. There's been a death at the county building. Deputy Sorrel."

I sat up in the dark with the phone pressed to my ear and my mother's coat still draped over the chair where I'd left it after three nights of not sleeping. "Found by whom?"

"Night custodian. The evidence locker." A pause, measured out like medicine. "You'll want to come down right away."

"You'll want to" — as though I were a boy being summoned to a recital. As though this were a courtesy rather than a corpse. I said I'd be there in twenty minutes, and Crane said, "Take your time," which was the first wrong thing, because no one says take your time about a body unless they've already finished with it.

* * *

The county building stood at the top of Cordwainer Street like a tombstone someone had decided to build offices into — gray limestone, iron-framed windows, a clock that had run four minutes slow since before I was born. Rain had been falling since midnight, a thin cold rain that made the gaslights halo and the gutters whisper. When I arrived, the night custodian, a stooped man named Ferro whose hands shook so badly he could barely hold his mop, was sitting on the front steps under an umbrella held by a patrolman who looked seventeen.

Ferro wouldn't look at me. He stared at the wet flagstones between his feet and said, over and over, very quietly, "He was just hanging there. Just hanging there like laundry."

Inside, the corridor to the evidence locker smelled of floor wax and old paper and, beneath it, something else — that sweetish, copper-bottomed smell I knew better than my own name. Two deputies stood at the locker door, and when they saw me coming they did not step aside so much as arrange themselves differently, the way men do when they've been told to let someone through but not to help him.

Deputy Marcus Sorrel hung from a pipe bracket near the ceiling of the evidence locker, a folding chair tipped over beneath his polished boots. His belt was gone. The belt had been used. His face was congested, dark, his tongue protruding, and his eyes were open and fixed on nothing with the terrible patience of the dead.

I had known Marcus Sorrel for eleven years. He had driven me to my mother's funeral. He had once spent an entire harvest festival following a pickpocket through three counties on his own time because the man had lifted Ida Brum's coin purse, and Ida Brum was eighty years old and Sorrel said nobody steals from Ida Brum. He chewed tobacco and spat it into a coffee can he kept behind the dispatcher's desk, and he called me "Doc" even though I wasn't a doctor, and last week — four days ago, Tuesday afternoon, in this same building — he had told me, in a voice pitched low enough that only I could hear it: *Doc, you didn't hear from me, but that dental file you asked about? It got pulled yesterday. Pulled, not copied. Somebody went into records and took it out of the drawer.*

That was what Marcus Sorrel had done with his fear. He had brought it to me.

Now he hung in front of me with his belt around his throat, and the first thing I noticed — the thing I will notice until I die — was his hands.

They were clean. Not washed-clean. *Unmarked*-clean. No ligature abrasions across the palms or the fingers. When a man hangs himself, truly hangs himself, instinct makes him claw at the band constricting his neck even after consciousness leaves him. The fingernails tear. The skin of the throat shows crescents of self-inflicted damage, petechial hemorrhaging blooms in the eyes, and the hands tell the story the mouth can't anymore. Marcus Sorrel's hands hung at his sides like gloves someone had emptied. And the ligature mark itself — I got close enough to see it without touching, close enough to count — ran in a perfectly horizontal line across the front of his throat, level both sides, rising slightly at the back of the jaw.

A horizontal furrow. From a suspension point *behind* and *above*, you get a V — an inverted V, apex at the knot. From a fixed overhead point with the body dropped, you get an oblique line climbing toward the anchor. What you do not get, not cleanly, not without struggle, is a horizontal band — unless the pressure was applied horizontally first, while the man was still on the ground, facing whoever held the other end.

I knew what I was looking at. Any coroner with ten years of dead bodies behind him knows. This was not suicide. This was homicide dressed in suicide's clothes, and the clothes fit badly, and everyone in the room was pretending otherwise.

* * *

Judge Ellery Crane arrived at six o'clock sharp, which meant he had been dressed and waiting somewhere nearby since well before the custodian found the body. He came down the corridor in his long gray overcoat with two men I didn't know — city men, soft-shoed, carrying leather document cases — and he stopped at the locker door and looked at Sorrel's hanging body the way a man looks at a stain on a carpet he has recently purchased.

"Poor devil," Crane said.

"Judge," I said, "I need the scene preserved. Nobody touches anything until I've—"

"There won't be any examination, Wren."

The words landed in the corridor like coins dropped down a well. I turned. Crane was watching me with those pale, patient eyes, the eyes of a man who had signed off on sealed dental records and falsified death certificates and whatever else Marrow's Bend required of him, and who had never once, in forty years on the bench, raised his voice.

"I'm sorry?" I said.

"The family has requested cremation. The request has been granted. The body will be released this morning." He consulted a pocket watch, an affectation, since the courthouse clock kept the official time. "Dr. Pell has signed the certificate. Suicide by hanging. It's all quite settled."

"Dr. Pell signed—" I heard my own voice go flat and strange. "Pell isn't the coroner. I'm the coroner. Pell runs the clinic. He doesn't have standing to certify anything."

Crane closed the watch. "The certificate is signed, Wren. That's really all that matters now, isn't it? The paper exists. The paper is the truth. You taught us that — you're always telling us. A coroner's report is the town's memory of its dead." He smiled then, gently, and it was the gentlest horrifying thing I had ever seen. "Marcus Sorrel hanged himself in the evidence locker because he was a troubled man. Debt, drink, loneliness. You'll find the ledger entries confirm it. Everything confirms it, if you look at the right pages."

Behind him, one of the soft-shoed men had already produced a clipboard and was speaking quietly to the deputies, and the deputies were nodding, and the body of Marcus Sorrel — my friend, my informant, the only man in uniform who had tried to hand me a thread of truth — was being discussed in the past tense, in the completed tense, as though he had been dead for weeks instead of hours.

"He came to me," I said. "Tuesday. He told me the dental file was pulled."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes — not surprise; confirmation. The way a man checks a lock he already knows is broken. "Did he," Crane said softly. "Well. Troubled men say troubled things."

* * *

They took him down at 6:40 while I stood in the doorway and watched, because there was nothing in the law that let me stop them and everything in the law — their version of it, the version written on paper that existed and therefore was true — that said I had no standing here at all. They cut the belt and laid him on a stretcher and covered his face, and the horizontal ligature furrow disappeared under the sheet, and by seven o'clock the evidence locker smelled only of floor wax again, and by eight o'clock Dr. Pell's certificate was in the county file, typed, signed, witnessed, dated, complete.

I read it at nine, standing at the counter in the records office while the clerk — a nervous young woman named Tilly who would not meet my eyes — pretended to inventory forms.

*Cause of death: asphyxia due to hanging. Manner: suicide.* Pell's signature sat at the bottom in his small, immaculate hand, the same hand that had written *treatment for grief* on my own hospital file three years ago. Below it, a witness line: *Observed scene and confirmed findings, E. Crane, J.P.*

Confirmed findings. Crane had confirmed Pell's findings, and Pell had confirmed Crane's, and neither of them had examined the body, and the one man whose job it was to examine it had been locked out of his own morgue's jurisdiction by a piece of paper that had materialized faster than grief travels.

"Tilly," I said. "Who delivered the certificate?"

She smoothed the form on the counter as if it might wrinkle under my gaze. "Courier. Early. Before five."

"Before five." The custodian found the body at four. The certificate was typed, signed, and hand-delivered within the hour. Which meant Pell had signed it before anyone officially knew Sorrel was dead — or, more precisely, before anyone was supposed to know. The paperwork hadn't followed the death. The paperwork had preceded it. The death had been fitted to the form like a foot fitted to a shoe, and if the foot was too big, well, someone had trimmed it.

"Thank you, Tilly."

She flinched. Actually flinched, a small full-body contraction, and gathered her forms and left the counter, and I understood that she was afraid of being seen talking to me, and I understood what that meant about what the town already knew, or believed it knew, or had been told to know.

* * *

I walked home through the rain without opening my umbrella. I don't remember deciding to. I remember the water running down my collar and the gaslights smearing in the wet and the town moving around me with its ordinary morning sounds — shutters cranking open, the bakery wagon, someone calling a dog — all of it continuing as though the machinery underneath hadn't just shown me its gears.

Because that was what I had seen. Not a murder, exactly. Murders are passions, mistakes, desperate arithmetic. What I had seen was *maintenance*. Somewhere above me — above Crane, maybe, or through him — a decision had been made that Marcus Sorrel was a loose thread, and the thread had been cut, and the cut had been hemmed, and by noon the garment would hang in the closet looking exactly as it always had. The town's machinery had turned on one of its own parts, ground it fine, and kept turning, and the only unusual sound in all of Marrow's Bend this morning was the bakery wagon bell.

And the message was not lost on me. It was not meant to be lost on me. Sorrel had talked to me. Sorrel had touched the dental file, the sealed record, the thread that led back to Ada Voss and the winter of the flood and the note sewn into a drowned woman's lining reading *Ask Wren about the winter of the flood.* Four days later he was hanging in the evidence locker with a certificate signed before his body cooled.

The next name on that list was mine. They weren't hiding it. Hiding it would have required them to believe I might miss it.

At my house, I hung my soaked coat on the hook and stood dripping in the hallway, and my eye caught on the row of pegs by the door — my father's scarves, my mother's coats, the clothes of the dead woman below the mill dam, the clothes someone had dressed her in so that I specifically, of all people, would have to look at her and know.

Sorrel's hands had been unmarked. I kept coming back to that. A man fights, even unconscious, even dying; the hands tell the truth the certificate lies about. But there had been no marks because there had been no fight, or because the fight had happened elsewhere, earlier, in some room where the marks could be explained or erased — and then they had carried him, or propped him, and built the scene around him the way you build a stage set, and the chair had been kicked over just so, and the belt had been threaded just so, and every detail had been placed by someone who understood that details are what coroners read.

Someone who understood it because someone had trained alongside me. Or trained me.

I thought of Dr. Pell's gentle hands adjusting a blanket over a patient, three years ago, in a white room I could not properly remember. *Rest now, Wren. You've had a shock.*

I went to my desk, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote in my own hand, in the plain declarative style I use for reports because plainness is harder to argue with:

*Deputy Marcus Sorrel. Found 0417, evidence locker, county building. Ligature furrow horizontal, non-oblique, inconsistent with drop suspension. No defensive markings on hands — anomalous. Certificate signed prior to discovery. Witnessed by E. Crane. Requested: independent examination. Denied.*

Then, beneath it, smaller:

*If I am found hanged, I did not hang myself.*

I folded the page and did not put it in my desk, because my desk could be searched — my home had already been entered once, subtly, the books on my shelf reshelved a half-inch out of true. Instead I sealed it in an envelope, walked four blocks through the thinning rain, and slipped it under the door of Ida Brum's shop, the one person in Marrow's Bend who had hesitated, four days ago, when everyone else recited Ada Voss's drowning word for identical word.

Hesitation, I was learning, was the only honest language left in this town.

As I turned away from her door, I saw, across the street, parked beneath the elms outside the shuttered mill office, a black motorcar with mud on its wheels up to the hubs. Mud the color of the riverbank below the dam. It had not been there yesterday. It idled without lights, engine ticking, patient as a held breath, and I understood that from this moment forward I would be watched the way I watched the dead — closely, professionally, without mercy.

I straightened my collar and walked home in the open, slowly, letting them see my face.

Let them read it. I had stopped writing reports for them. Now I was writing one for the truth, and the truth, unlike Marcus Sorrel, still had breath left in it — and I intended, whatever it cost, to keep it breathing until the whole town heard it.
