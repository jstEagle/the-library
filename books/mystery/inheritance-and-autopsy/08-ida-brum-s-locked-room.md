## 8. Ida Brum's Locked Room

The bell over the door of Brum's General Goods had a crack in it, so it rang half a note flat, like a piano that had been left out in one of Marrow's Bend's long winters. Wren had heard that broken note his whole life. He'd never once thought about what it meant—that even the town's smallest sounds were damaged and nobody bothered to fix them.

Ida Brum stood behind the counter with a bolt of blue flannel in her arms, measuring it against her elbow, and when she saw him she didn't smile. None of the warmth of a shopkeeper greeting a customer. Just a stillness, the stillness of a woman deciding something.

"Coroner." She said it the way Pastor Hale had said *drowned*—flat, final, a word designed to end a conversation.

"I need five minutes," Wren said.

"You had your five minutes yesterday. With everyone else in this town." She set the flannel down and smoothed it with both hands, pressing out creases that weren't there. "You went to Hale first. Then Sorrel. Then me. Alphabetical."

It wasn't alphabetical. He'd gone to Hale because Hale buried people, Sorrel because Sorrel wore a badge, Ida last because he'd saved her for last—because of all the rehearsed voices in Marrow's Bend, hers had been the only one with a hairline fracture running through it.

"You hesitated," Wren said.

"I did not."

"You did. Right here—" he touched his own throat, where hers had tightened, just for a second, before the recitation began. "*Ada Voss drowned in the winter of the flood. Her body was recovered below the dam. She was buried in the Coldharbour plot.* Word for word what Hale said. Word for word what Sorrel said. Three people who don't share a church, a job, or a single conversation, telling me the same story in the same order with the same words."

"Small town," Ida said. "People repeat each other."

"Not like that. Not like scripture." He came closer to the counter. The shop smelled of lamp oil and molasses and the cold dust of things unsold. "Ida. A woman washed up under the mill dam wearing my mother's coat. There's a note sewn into her lining in handwriting I burned three years ago. And every person in this town tells me the same bedtime story about Ada Voss like they learned it from the same book."

"Maybe we did."

The words dropped between them like a coin into a well. Ida's hands stopped moving on the flannel. For a long moment the only sound was the tick of the wall clock behind the tobacco shelf, and outside, the wind coming down off Coldharbour Hill, carrying the smell of the millpond.

Then Ida turned, unhurried, and walked to the front window, and turned the cardboard sign from OPEN to CLOSED, and drew the shade.

"Back room," she said. "And Wren—if anyone asks, you came to buy rope."

* * *

The back room of Brum's General Goods was longer than the shop itself, running deep into the hillside, and colder than the day warranted. Shelves of canned goods gave way to crates, and the crates gave way to a door—a real door, oak, with a lock that looked older than the town charter. Ida produced a key from a chain around her neck, tucked beneath her blouse, worn smooth as a river stone.

She unlocked it, then paused with her hand on the handle.

"Forty-one years I've run this store," she said. "My mother ran it before me. You know what a general store is, in a town like this? It's the ledger of everything. Every sack of flour, every coffin nail, every bottle of laudanum. People think money is the record of a place. Money lies. Inventory doesn't." She looked at him over her shoulder. "I've been keeping my own books since 1979. Everything the trust bought. Everything the trust buried. Everything the trust made disappear."

"Why are you showing me?"

"Because yesterday you asked me about Ada Voss and I lied to your face, and you caught it, and you didn't let go." She pushed the door open. "Because someone has to catch it before they catch *you*."

Inside, the room was lit by a single bulb on a cord. Metal shelving lined both walls, and on the shelves sat ledgers—dozens of them, bound in oilcloth against damp, spines labeled in Ida's small square hand: years going back four decades. A desk. A chair. A kerosene lamp. And pinned to the far wall, above the desk, a photograph.

Wren crossed the room without feeling his legs move.

The photograph was a snapshot, slightly blurred, taken through what looked like a car window. A woman stood at the edge of the frame near the feed store on Main Street, mid-stride, head turned away—but not fully away. Enough of her face showed. Enough of her profile. The jaw, the ear, the way she held her chin.

He knew that face. He knew it the way you know a room you grew up in—with your body, not your mind. His chest hurt. His eyes stung. Some part of him reached for a memory and found the shape of it burned away, a hole cut clean, edges cauterized.

"That's her," he said. His voice came out wrong.

"That's Ada Voss, standing on Main Street, last April." Ida came to stand beside him. "Eight months after this town buried her. Eight months after you carried her casket yourself—I was there, Wren. You wept at her funeral. Everyone said how devoted you were."

"I don't remember her face." He touched the photograph, careful, as if the paper might dissolve. "I remember the funeral. I remember the rain. I remember the hole in the ground. But when I try to see her—" His hand closed slowly into a fist. "There's nothing there. There's a space where her face should be, and the space is *smooth*. Like it was never anything at all."

Ida was quiet a moment. "That's how it works, I'm told."

Wren turned. "Told by whom?"

"Sit down," Ida said.

He sat in the desk chair. It groaned under him. Ida pulled a ledger from the shelf—one marked with no year, just a black band across the spine—and laid it open on the desk. Inside, in her cramped inventory-hand, were columns: dates, names, amounts. And beside some entries, a small notation in red ink.

"Pell bills the trust quarterly," she said. "Dr. Elias Pell, the clinic. Routine line items—supplies, salaries, maintenance. But look at the red ones."

Wren read them. *Consultation, extended. Grief protocol.* *Follow-up session, sedation-assisted.* *Maintenance visit, seasonal.* Each with a date. Each with a name.

His own name was on the list. Three entries. The dates matched, exactly, the three days after Ada's funeral—the three days he remembered as a fever, a blur, Dr. Pell sitting beside his bed saying *you need rest, Wren, grief does terrible things to the mind.*

But there were other names too. Eleven others. Judge Crane's clerk. Two of the mill workers' widows. Deputy Sorrel's predecessor. Half the town council.

"What is 'grief protocol'?" Wren asked, though the cold spreading through his chest already knew.

"I don't know what he does in that back room of the clinic," Ida said. "I know what it costs, and I know what it buys. It buys agreement. It buys the same story told the same way by everyone who matters." She tapped the ledger. "Hale. Sorrel. The council. They've all had visits. Some of them more than once. You go to them asking questions, and they recite, because as far as they know, they're telling the truth. Pell didn't give them a lie. He gave them a *memory*. There's no difference, inside your own head."

"And me?"

"You're on the list three times. But you're also—" she hesitated, and for the first time since he'd known her, Ida Brum looked afraid, not for herself but for him— "you're the only one who ever started asking again. That shouldn't be possible. Whatever he did to you, it's supposed to hold."

"The woman in the river," Wren said slowly. "Wearing my clothes. With the note. Someone wanted to break whatever he built in my head."

"Or someone wanted you blamed for it." Ida straightened. "Either way, Wren, listen to me now, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to pretend this room doesn't exist, same as I have for forty years." She gripped the edge of the desk, knuckles white. "Stop. Stop digging. Stop asking. Take the Jane Doe, sign whatever report they put in front of you, and let this town keep its story. Because I have watched what happens to people who don't stop. Sorrel's predecessor asked about the flood. He's in county records as a suicide. One of the mill widows kept writing letters to the state. She's in a care facility upstate now, doesn't recognize her own daughters. And Ada—"

Her voice caught. Actually caught, on the name.

"Ada trusted one person in this whole rotten town," Ida said. "One person she thought would help her get out. And that person forgot she existed. If Pell can do that to the man she loved most, what do you think he'll do to a shopkeeper? To you, a second time? They call it being *corrected*, Wren. That's the word. I heard Crane use it once, years ago, thinking I was deaf and old. *We'll have him corrected.* Like a typo. Like a page torn out."

The bulb overhead buzzed. Somewhere above them, in the shop, the flat cracked bell rang—someone entering, though the sign said CLOSED. Ida went rigid. She pressed two fingers to her lips, then pointed Wren toward the back of the room, where a second door, low and unmarked, stood half-hidden behind a shelf of seed packets.

Footsteps crossed the shop floor. Measured. Unhurried. Then a voice, pleasant and warm as bathwater:

"Ida? It's Elias. I saw the shade drawn, dear, and I thought I'd check on you. You missed your appointment this month."

Ida's hand found Wren's arm in the dark and squeezed hard enough to bruise. Her mouth formed words without sound: *the cellar door. Out through the coal chute. Go.*

Pell's footsteps moved toward the counter. A drawer slid open. Closed.

"I'll leave the samples on the step," the voice called cheerfully. "You know how important it is to stay current, Ida. We wouldn't want you getting *confused*."

A pause. Long enough for Wren's heart to stop twice.

Then the bell rang again, flat and broken, and the footsteps faded down the boardwalk.

Ida exhaled against Wren's ear. "Samples," she whispered. "That's what he calls them. Sedatives, dressed up as vitamins. Half the town takes them and thanks him for it." She stepped back, and in the dim light her face was old suddenly, and fierce. "Go home through the alleys. Don't be seen leaving here. And Wren—" she pressed something into his hand, folded small, torn from one of her ledgers, "—that's the date of Pell's next billing cycle. Whatever you decide to do, do it before then. After that, the trust meets, and men like Crane decide who needs correcting."

Wren unfolded the scrap. A date. Ten days out.

When he looked up, Ida had already turned back to her shelves, resuming her inventory of forty years of sins, and the locked room swallowed its secrets behind him like water closing over a stone.

He climbed out through the coal chute into the gray afternoon light, the scrap of paper burning in his pocket like a coal, and behind him, faint through the hillside, he could hear Ida Brum singing to herself in the dark—some old hymn, off-key, defiant—as if to prove to whoever might be listening that her mind was still entirely, dangerously her own.
