## 17. The Woman Below the Mill

The last tape ended at 3:14 in the morning, and it ended with a sound I will carry to whatever grave they dig for me: a woman's voice, low and steady, reciting numbers.

*Lower level. East stair. The water keeps what the town won't.*

Then, after a pause long enough that I'd thought the recording finished, she added—almost gently, as if she knew exactly who would be listening and how it would land:

*"He'll know which stair. He used to hold my hand on it."*

I sat in the dark of my office with the reel still turning, its hiss like breath, and I did not move for a long time. The east stair of the old mill. The one we'd climbed as teenagers, when the mill was already dead but not yet drowned, when Ada Voss would dare me to go down into the dark and I would dare her back and neither of us ever admitted we were both afraid.

The water keeps what the town won't.

I knew where she was. I had known, maybe, since the moment I pulled that coat from the river and felt the weight of my mother's buttons in my palm. Everything since—the ledgers, the tapes, the empty grave—had only been the slow, reluctant education of a man being taught to read his own life again.

I packed a bag with the calm of a man performing an autopsy on himself. Tapes. Ledgers. My father's scarf, because some superstition in me insisted. And the coroner's kit, because old habits are the last things to drown.

* * *

The mill stood at the bend of the river like a rotted tooth the town had stopped noticing. By day it was a landmark, a photograph in the chamber of commerce brochure, its broken windows softened by ivy. By night it was something else—a black mass that drank the moonlight, its lower level flooded to the second step, the water inside it blacker than the river around it because the river moved and this water did not.

The trust had sealed the mill after the flood. Fenced it, posted signs, paid the county to look away. Three years of official neglect, and underneath the neglect, three years of someone keeping a door oiled.

The east stair was behind the loading dock, down a corridor that smelled of wet stone and rust. I went in with a flashlight and the particular courage of a man who has nothing left to lose that isn't already lost. The water came up over my boots at the first landing, cold enough to make my teeth ache. Somewhere ahead, a light burned—not flashlight-yellow but lamplight, warm, domestic, absurd in that drowned place.

"Ada," I said. My voice went out flat against the stone and came back to me smaller than I'd sent it.

Silence. Then, from below: "Say the rest."

I knew what she wanted. She wanted proof I wasn't a puppet with my strings rethreaded. She wanted the thing Dr. Pell's gentle hands couldn't manufacture, because Pell had never known it existed.

"Winter of the flood," I said. "You made me tea in your father's kitchen and put too much honey in it because you said grief needed sweetening. You told me your testimony would be the end of you and asked me—" my voice caught, and I let it catch; some cracks are load-bearing— "you asked me if I'd remember you right. And I said yes. And then I didn't."

A long quiet. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a metronome.

"The honey," her voice came back, "was because you looked like a man who hadn't eaten in a week. Which you were." A pause. "Come down, Wren. Mind the third step. It's gone soft."

* * *

She was thinner. That was the first thing, and it shamed me that I noticed it first, that three years of hiding had carved her down while I'd been eating dinners and signing reports and sleeping soundly through nights I had no right to have slept through. Her hair was shorter, gray coming in early at the temples. She wore a man's sweater and stood beside a camp stove with two tin cups already set out, because of course she'd known I'd come. She'd planned for me the way she planned everything—three years deep, patient as sediment.

"You watched," I said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse than that—it was a question I needed answered before I could stand in the same room with her. "At the funeral. You were there."

"I was there." She didn't flinch. She handed me a cup of tea with too much honey in it, and my hands shook around it. "Behind the fence, in a coat that wasn't mine. I watched them lower an empty box into a hole Crane had certified full, and I watched you stand at the head of it with your hands folded like a good coroner, and I watched you cry."

"I don't remember crying."

"I know." Something moved across her face—not cruelty, never cruelty, but the terrible tenderness of a person who has had three years to study a wound from the outside. "That's the part that kept me alive, Wren. Do you understand? Every day I wanted to walk up to your door and knock. Every day. And every day I remembered the way you cried at my funeral without knowing why, and I understood what they'd done to you, and I understood that if I knocked, they'd do it again. Or worse. So I stayed here. In the place they sealed. Because the one spot in Marrow's Bend the trust never watches is the spot they've declared dead."

"And the woman in the river."

Her face changed. Grief came into it like water into a hull.

"Maren," she said. "Maren Ostrander. Her brother was one of the four who died in the flood. She found me two years ago—came down the east stair with a lamp and a bag of evidence her brother had mailed her before he died, and she said, *You're the witness. I've been looking for you since the trial.* We've been building the case together ever since. The tapes were hers. The plan was hers."

She turned away toward the lamplight, and when she spoke again her voice had gone very level, the way mine went level over bodies.

"She wore your clothes, Wren. Your mother's coat, your father's scarf. We took them from your closet—I did, I'm sorry, I'll answer for that—and it was her idea. She said the town wouldn't notice a drowned stranger, but you would notice those clothes. She said you were the only man in Marrow's Bend who read a body instead of reading a report. She said—" Ada's voice finally broke, just once, cleanly, like ice cracking. "She said, *If anyone can find us, it's him.*"

"So she went up to the dam road. In the flood season. Carrying the message."

"She was going to leave the note at your office door and be back before midnight. The river took her at the crossing." Ada pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I heard her go out. I heard the water. I've been sitting here for six days knowing she was dead and not able to say so, because saying so meant going up, and going up meant leading them to everything. You buried her for me, Wren. You did the one decent thing left to do in this town, and you did it without knowing."

I set down the cup. My hands would not stay steady, so I let them hang.

"I can take you out of here tonight," I said. "My car, the north road, the state line by morning. There's a state investigator—I have his number, I've had it for a week, waiting until I had all of it—"

"No."

The word came out quiet and absolute, and it stopped me the way a closed door stops a man mid-stride.

"No?"

"Not yet." She crossed to a shelf cut into the stone wall—her shelf, built plank by plank over three years—and lifted down a metal document case, military green, dented at one corner. "There's one more witness, Wren. Maren wasn't the last. There's a man who worked the dam gates the night of the failure. He signed the maintenance log that proves the spillway was sabotaged, not neglected. He's been running from the trust longer than I've been hiding. Maren found where he is. It's in her handwriting, in this case, and I am not leaving Marrow's Bend until he's standing in front of a state investigator next to me."

"Ada—"

"They killed four men and called it weather," she said, and now the level voice was gone and what was under it was three years of cold and dark and tea with too much honey drunk alone. "They erased me. They opened up your head and rearranged you like furniture. And yesterday they hung Deputy Sorrel in an evidence locker and called it despair. If I run now, all of that stands. All of it, forever, with Crane's signature on top like a bow." She stepped closer, close enough that I could see she was crying without any sound at all. "You spent three years being their instrument. I spent three years being their ghost. I'm done being either."

I looked at her—at the woman I had mourned, at the woman whose funeral I had attended without remembering her face, at the woman who had watched me forget her and loved me anyway, from inside a flooded mill, for a thousand days—and every duty I had ever sworn came up against one plain fact: she was alive, and she was asking, and I had already failed her once at the cost of everything I was.

"All right," I said. "Not yet. But we go together, when we go. No more messages through rivers. No more coats in closets. Whatever comes next, it comes to both of us or neither."

"That," she said, "is the first honest sentence anyone's said to me in three years."

She reached out and took my hand—the same hand that had held hers on these stairs twenty years ago—and for a moment the water dripping in the dark was just rain on a roof, and the mill was just a ruin, and we were just two people who had been robbed of each other by men with good manners.

Then the lamp flickered.

Not the wind. Not the damp. The flame bent sideways, once, twice, and somewhere above us—up the east stair, out past the loading dock—a floorboard settled under weight that had not been there a minute ago.

Ada's grip tightened on my hand.

"How many ways out of here?" I whispered.

"One," she breathed. "And someone's standing in it."
