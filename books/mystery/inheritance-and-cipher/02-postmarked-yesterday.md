## 2. Postmarked Yesterday

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone but me, and even to me it should have meant nothing. Tuesdays were intake days at Blackwood & Associates — estate of one, the associates being a filing cabinet and a coffee machine with opinions. I sorted the morning's post the way I always did: bills, a client's amendment request, a postcard from a notary in Duluth who still hadn't learned that exclamation points have no place in probate correspondence.

And then the envelope.

It was cream stock, heavy, the kind that costs more per sheet than some people spend on lunch. No return address. My name and office address typed in a serif font — Courier, or something pretending to be Courier — with the deliberate, even spacing of a typewriter rather than a word processor. I'd handled enough documents in my career to know the difference. Word processors are honest about their uniformity. Typewriters have moods. The *i* in *Blackwood* sat a half-millimeter higher than the others, the way old machines drift when the typebar wears unevenly.

The postmark read the day after Vivian Ashcombe's funeral.

I stood in my office doorway holding it for a long moment, the radiator ticking behind me, the city doing its gray November business outside the window. Postmarks don't lie — that's the first thing they teach you and the last thing they mean. A letter can be written in five minutes or five years; it enters the mail stream when it enters it. But a letter postmarked the day after a funeral, arriving three weeks later, means one of two things. Either it had been sitting somewhere it shouldn't have been — in a dead letter office, in a drawer, in someone's coat pocket — or it had been written long before the funeral by someone who knew exactly when the funeral would be.

Both possibilities were interesting. Only one of them was frightening.

I put on gloves. Not because I expected to need them — I'm a lawyer, not a forensic examiner, whatever certain police chiefs in my past might say about my habits — but because gloves change how you touch a document. They slow you down. They remind your hands that paper is evidence before it is information. I slit the envelope along the top edge with a letter opener, tipped the single folded sheet into a clean tray, and read it standing up, because I have never once been able to sit down for a first reading.

*Ms. Blackwood,*

*You read her will on Friday. You found what you found. I know you did, because she designed it that way, and she designed everything.*

*You will want to know about the night of March 14th. You will be told it was an accident, because everyone was told it was an accident. Ask about the second set of headlights. Ask who was driving Edmund's car that night, and ask why the driver was never named in any report.*

*I did not kill her. But I know who did, and I know why, and I know that you are the only person alive who will believe it when they hear it.*

*Come to the house. Ask the family about Ruthie. Watch their faces when they say her name. They have all practiced.*

*Burn this if you are wise. You were never wise.*

*No signature.*

I read it three times. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, the coffee machine hissed through its morning argument with itself.

Then I sat down — finally, my legs deciding the matter for me — and let the feeling arrive, the one I knew was coming, the one I had been waiting for without admitting it since I'd stood in Vivian Ashcombe's drawing room holding a codicil dated four days after her death.

Dread first. Dread is always first. Dread is the body's honest employee; it clocks in before the mind has finished its coffee. Because this letter was not a confession, whatever its opening posture. Confessions are written to unburden, or to negotiate, or to wound. This was written to *direct*. *Ask about the second set of headlights. Ask who was driving.* It was a map with my name at the top of it, and maps are written by people who want you to go somewhere — which means someone, somewhere, benefits from my going there, and I had no way of knowing yet whether that somewhere was a truth or a trap.

And underneath the dread, rising like warmth through cold floorboards: the thrill.

God help me, the thrill. I have tried to be ashamed of it. I have sat across from therapists — two of them, sequentially, each of whom developed a certain tightness around the eyes when I described my relationship to documents — and tried to explain that it isn't pleasure, exactly, when the paper starts to speak. It's more like recognition. Most of my professional life is reading language that is designed to be smooth, to close doors, to make death administratively tidy. And then occasionally — rarely, maybe four times now, counting this — a document arrives that is designed to be *found*, that has been built like a lock with me as the intended key, and every nerve I own stands up at its desk.

The fourth death that isn't what it seems.

I didn't say that out loud. I almost never say it out loud. But I thought it, and thinking it felt like stepping off a curb into traffic.

* * *

I spent the morning being a professional about it, which is to say I spent the morning lying to myself with excellent documentation.

First: the physical evidence. I laid the letter out under my desk lamp beside the codicil copy I'd brought back from Ashcombe House. Different papers — the codicil on the firm's own stock, this on cream laid paper with a watermark I'd need raking light to read. But the typewriter — I held the two typed passages side by side, tilting them, and there it was again: the drifting *i*, the slightly heavy strike on the lowercase *e*, the same machine or the same model, and typewriters of that vintage are not common. The codicil and the letter had come from the same hands or at least the same keys. The document that shouldn't exist and the letter that arrived too late were siblings.

Second: the postmark. I called a friend at the postal inspection service — *friend* is generous; we'd testified on opposite sides of a mail-fraud case in 2019 and had gotten drinks afterward out of mutual respect — and asked, hypothetically, how a letter postmarked three weeks ago might only surface now. He gave me the menu without being asked twice. Dead letter recovery. Mis-sorting, though three weeks was long even for that. Or the letter was mailed late and backdated — which required either a cooperative small-town post office or someone with access to a postage meter and no scruples. *Or,* he said, and I could hear him smiling, *it's exactly what it says it is, and someone sat on it. Why? What did you find, Ines?* I told him it was nothing, which he correctly interpreted as everything, and hung up.

Third — and this was where I stopped being able to pretend — the content. *Ask about the second set of headlights.* I didn't know what that referred to. Not yet. But the letter assumed I would soon, assumed the family would tell me things, assumed I would be sitting in that drawing room asking questions — which meant the writer knew about the codicil, knew about its terms, knew that Vivian's will required me to find the witness. The letter wasn't reacting to events. It was anticipating them. It had been written by someone standing further up the timeline than I was, watching me walk toward a place they'd already been.

And then the last line. *Burn this if you are wise. You were never wise.*

That was personal. That was not the voice of a stranger. That was the voice of someone who had read about me — or, less comfortably, someone who knew me. There are perhaps six people alive who could accurately characterize my relationship to wisdom, and I made a list of them on a legal pad, and then I looked at the list, and then I turned the pad face down on my desk like a superstition I was too professional to admit to.

* * *

In the afternoon, I did what I always do when a document frightens me: I went looking for its family.

Every document has one. A letter has parents in the hand that wrote it and the machine that struck it; it has siblings in the paper stock and the postage; it has a birthplace in the postmark's sorting code, which I read with a magnifier until I'd extracted the facility number and confirmed what I'd half expected — the letter had entered the mail stream at a regional facility forty miles from Ashcombe House. Close enough to be local. Far enough to be deliberate.

I spread Vivian Ashcombe's file across my desk the way other people spread tarot cards. Death certificate: natural causes, signed by a physician I would be looking into by nightfall. The will itself: immaculate, drafted by the family's firm forty years ago and amended with the tidy patience of a woman who treated her estate like a garden. And the codicil — the impossible codicil, dated four days after her death, naming me, Ines Blackwood, a stranger with no connection to the Ashcombe name, as executor, with instructions that read less like legal drafting and more like a letter that had put on a suit and learned to cite statute.

*To my executor: find the witness. She is not where they say she is. She never was.*

I had read that line a dozen times since Friday. I read it again now, and for the first time I noticed what my own excitement had let me skip past: *she never was.* Present tense folded inside past. Vivian hadn't written *she is no longer there* or *she has been moved.* She had written that Ruthie had never been where the family said she died — that the accident itself, the whole official shape of 1998, was a fabrication from its foundation.

And the letter on my desk said: *ask the family about Ruthie. Watch their faces. They have all practiced.*

Practiced. Twenty-five years of practiced grief, if the letter was honest, and I was increasingly, unwillingly convinced that it was — honest about the facts, at least, even if it was lying by omission about its own purpose. Because here was the thing I kept circling back to, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: the letter called itself a confession. *I did not kill her.* Confessing to a murder you didn't commit, in a letter that never names the murder, to a lawyer who has no case. It was a confession the way a lure is a fly — shaped to attract, built to be struck at.

Someone wanted me on this case. Someone had wanted me on it badly enough to forge a codicil — or to know about one — and to type a letter on the same machine and mail it into the stream with a funeral-day postmark, timing my receipt of it like a conductor timing a cue. And the question that mattered, the question I wrote at the top of a fresh legal pad and underlined twice, was not *who wrote this letter.*

The question was: *what do they want me to conclude?*

Because there are two kinds of people who leave trails. There are the guilty, who leave trails by accident and spend the rest of their lives sweeping, and there are the desperate, who leave trails on purpose because a trail is the only shout they have left. I had spent my career learning to tell the difference. The guilty sweep in circles; the desperate leave signs that are almost too clear, too well-marked, the way a lost hiker breaks branches so searchers can find the body — or find them alive.

Vivian Ashcombe, I was beginning to understand, had been a desperate hiker. Every marginal mark in her correspondence, every odd phrasing in that codicil, every instruction that bent probate law into the shape of a rescue — she had been breaking branches for twenty-five years, hoping someone with the right eyes would come walking through her forest.

But the letter was not Vivian. The letter was someone else walking the same trail, and I could not yet tell if they were a searcher or a hunter.

* * *

The light had gone blue-gray by the time I looked up. The city had turned its lights on without consulting me. I had a cold cup of coffee, a legal pad with three pages of timeline, and the specific, humming exhaustion that comes from a day spent entirely inside a document.

I should have gone home. I want that on the record, wherever this record ends up: I knew I should have gone home. Instead I called the Ashcombe estate's number from the letterhead of the will — the house line, the old landline that a house like that keeps the way a ship keeps its anchor — and let it ring four times before a voice answered.

"Ashcombe House."

A woman's voice. Older, level, with the flattened patience of someone who has answered that phone through four decades of other people's emergencies. The housekeeper — Agnes, the file said, though the file said it in the tone of a footnote.

"Good evening. This is Ines Blackwood. I'm the executor of Mrs. Ashcombe's estate. I'd like to come by tomorrow to continue my review. There are questions I need to put to the family."

A silence on the line. Not the empty kind — the loaded kind, the kind where you can hear someone deciding. When she spoke again, her voice had not changed at all, and the fact that it hadn't changed told me more than any tremor would have.

"Of course, Ms. Blackwood. The family will be informed. Shall I have coffee ready? Mr. Marcus takes his at eleven."

"That would be fine. One more thing, if I may." I kept my voice in its professional register, light, almost idle. "I'm reviewing the estate's records going back some years. There's a name that keeps appearing in Mrs. Ashcombe's correspondence that I'd like to ask the family about. Ruthie."

This silence was different. This one had a floor that dropped out of it — half a second, maybe less, the pause of a woman who has spent twenty-five years not saying a word and has just heard it said aloud in her own kitchen.

"I'm afraid I wouldn't know anything about that," Agnes said. "Ruthie died in 1998. Before my time with the... no." A small, terrible correction, smooth as a page turn. "No, that's not right. I was here. I've been here forty years. Ruthie died in 1998. It was an accident. The family doesn't speak of it."

"Of course," I said. "Tomorrow, then."

"Tomorrow," she said, and hung up with a click that sounded, in the dark of my office, like a lock turning.

I sat for a while. *They have all practiced,* the letter said, and already I had my first specimen: a housekeeper whose loyalty had a seam in it, visible only because she'd been asked a name she'd spent a lifetime keeping flat. She had corrected herself. People who are telling the truth don't correct their own histories; they don't need to. Only rehearsal requires revision.

I gathered the letter, the codicil, the file. At the door I stopped, went back, and photographed the envelope's postmark one more time — the day after the funeral, in faded violet ink, the date sitting there like a signature on a check drawn against an account that shouldn't exist.

Someone had mailed me a map. Tomorrow I would start walking it, into a house full of rehearsed grief, toward a dead woman who wasn't dead, past a second set of headlights I could not yet see.

And whoever had written the letter — whoever was watching me walk — would be watching to see whether I followed their route exactly.

I intended to disappoint them. I intended to follow it perfectly — and then, at the moment they expected me to arrive, go somewhere they hadn't marked on the map at all.

I turned off the lamp. In the window, my reflection held the cream envelope against the city's lights, and for one unprofessional second, I let myself feel it — all of it, the dread and the thrill, twined together like the two strands of a cipher, indistinguishable, inseparable, and pointing the same direction.

Ashcombe House. Eleven o'clock. Ask about Ruthie, and watch their faces.

They had practiced. I intended to find out for how long.
