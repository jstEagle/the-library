## 9. The Inheritance Clause

The will had been read aloud on the last Friday of September, 1994, in the parlor of Hollis & Crane, Attorneys at Law, and Sable knew this because they had read the minutes of that meeting eleven times.

Eleven times, and it was only tonight—sitting cross-legged on the concrete floor of their basement archive at two in the morning, surrounded by the smell of acid-free folders and old toner—that the sentence finally detonated.

*"...the whole of the Voss estate, including but not limited to the orchard lands, the boathouse parcel, and all liquid holdings, to my daughter Marguerite Anne Voss; or, failing her, to my daughter Colette Marguerite Voss."*

Sable read it again. Then a third time, lips moving now, the way you do when your own eyes have stopped being trustworthy.

*My daughter Colette.*

Not *niece*. Not *goddaughter*. Not some orphaned cousin folded into the household out of charity. Daughter. The testator, Edmund Voss, had named two daughters in his will, and thirty years of Ashford Hollow's collective memory contained exactly one of them.

Sable reached for the legal pad they kept for moments like this—the good moments, the ones that made the loneliness feel like a vocation instead of a wound—and wrote in block letters:

**WHERE IS COLETTE?**

The question sat there on the yellow paper, small and enormous.

They should have asked it weeks ago. They should have asked it the first time Edith Voss poured them tea in the archive's reading room and said, with that warm, rueful smile, *"Cousins, distant ones—I'm afraid the family tree gets tangled past a certain generation. Nobody keeps track anymore."* Sable had nodded. Everyone in town nodded. Edith was the archivist; she was allowed her tangles. She'd been keeping the town's records for twenty-two years, longer than anyone else had held any post in Ashford Hollow, and she was beloved the way lighthouses are beloved—useful, constant, taken for granted until the night you need her.

But nobody keeps track anymore. That was the phrase. That was the door left open in an otherwise locked house.

Sable pulled the county death index—the microfilmed one, not the digitized version, because the digitized version had gaps shaped like convenience—and began at C.

There was no Colette Voss.

They searched the state index. They searched the neighboring counties, because families in trouble drove bodies across county lines more often than people liked to admit. They searched under *Voss-Colette*, *Colette-Voss*, hyphenated, misspelled, transposed. They searched for a girl who might have died anywhere between 1950 and 1994, since Edmund Voss had written his will sometime before his own death in 1989 and would presumably have named a living daughter.

Nothing. Not a birth record they could find either, though births from that era were patchier. But deaths were the state's business. Deaths got paper. Even the poorest family, even the most forgotten child, generated a certificate when they stopped breathing.

Colette Marguerite Voss had generated nothing.

Which meant one of three things. Either she had never existed—a ghost name in a lawyer's document, which happened, though rarely with a middle name so carefully chosen. Or she had died somewhere outside every index Sable could reach. Or—

Or she hadn't died at all.

Sable set down the pen. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Somewhere upstairs the furnace clicked on, and the house settled around them with the familiar groans of an old building keeping its opinions to itself. This was the hour when the archive felt least like a hobby and most like a marriage: just Sable and the dead, keeping each other company, asking each other honest questions.

And the dead were answering. The dead had been answering all along. Sable simply hadn't known how to hear them.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin, and Sable drove to the county records annex with the windows down despite the cold, needing the air, needing the feeling of moving toward something instead of circling it.

The clerk was a woman named Dotty Pell, sixty-ish, bifocals on a beaded chain, a woman who had worked the counter since before Sable was born and who remembered everyone's business with the serene amorality of a church register. She was also, crucially, a listener. Episode forty-three of the podcast—"The Woman Who Filed the Town"—had been about her, and she had sent Sable a thank-you card with a pressed violet in it.

"Dotty," Sable said, laying the printout of the will clause on the counter, "I need everything you can pull on a Colette Marguerite Voss. Birth, death, anything."

Dotty read the name twice. Her bifocals caught the fluorescent light and went opaque, twin white coins, and when she looked up over them her face had done something careful.

"Voss," she said. "That'd be the orchard family. The one with the girl who went in the lake."

"The one with the girl who went in the lake," Sable agreed. "But I'm asking about the other daughter."

"There wasn't another daughter."

"Edmund Voss's will names one."

Dotty was quiet for a moment. Behind her, the shelves of bound volumes rose into the dimness like a cliff face made of dates. "Wills name all kinds of things," she said finally. "You want me to search?"

"I want you to search."

She took her time about it, which Sable understood was not obstruction but ritual; Dotty did not hurry the dead. The card catalog drawers rolled, the microfilm reader clattered forward through decades, and Sable stood at the counter watching the town's paper memory scroll past—births, deaths, the slow sediment of a place that had decided its story long ago.

When Dotty came back, she was carrying a single folder, and she carried it flat, both hands, the way you carry something that might still be wet.

"No death certificate," she said. "No birth certificate in this county. But there's this." She opened the folder on the counter between them. "Probate affidavit, 1991. Two years after Edmund died. Someone had to swear to the court that the named secondary heir couldn't be located. It's short."

Sable read it.

*I, Edith Marsh Voss-Hale, being duly sworn, depose and state that Colette Marguerite Voss, named secondary beneficiary of the estate of Edmund Voss, deceased, is deceased, having died in childhood while resident outside the jurisdiction, and that no certificate of said death is available to the affiant.*

Sworn. Signed. Notarized by—Sable felt the floor tilt slightly—by R. Aldous Merrick.

Edith. *Edith*, whose surname had once been Hale, who had married into and out of the founding families the way water marries into stone, who had signed a sworn statement declaring a child dead without producing a single piece of paper to prove it, and Aldous Merrick—notarized it. The same Aldous Merrick whose mansion held, if Sable's source was right, a drawer full of alibi letters all written in the same hand.

"Edith Voss," Sable said aloud. "She swore this."

"That's what it says." Dotty's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman closing a drawer she wished she'd never opened. "Course, Edith was a Hale then. Married Walter Hale. Divorced him in '96, took back Voss—her mother was a Voss cousin, or so the story goes." She paused. "You know what struck me, hon, filing things all these years? Everybody in this town is somebody's cousin until it matters. Then nobody's related to anybody."

"When did Edith become the town archivist?"

"Nineteen ninety-eight." Dotty didn't hesitate. "Town council appointment. Unanimous. Aldous Merrick moved the motion himself."

Sable stood very still. The annex hummed around them—fluorescents, the tick of the microfilm reader someone had left running, the vast indifferent patience of paper.

A woman with no verifiable identity had sworn a child dead, been appointed keeper of every record the town owned, and held the post for twenty-two years with the power to seal, misfile, or quietly lose anything she wished. And the founding families had handed her the keys unanimously, gratefully, the way you hand keys to family.

*"Failing her,"* the will said. Failing Marguerite, everything went to Colette. And if Colette could be declared dead—if a kindly cousin could sign a piece of paper and a Merrick could stamp it—then failing Marguerite, everything went to whoever stood closest to the name.

Marguerite had failed. Marguerite had gone into the lake, or the orchard, or wherever the ground-penetrating radar had found her bones, and the estate had flowed onward—to the families, to the trust, to the tidy dissolution that kept five dynasties solvent through the nineties and beyond.

Unless Colette hadn't failed. Unless Colette had come back.

Sable thought of Edith's hands around a teacup, steady and white-knuckled at once. Thought of the sealed family papers offered so generously, so warmly—*come look at anything you like, dear, we have nothing to hide.* Thought of the birthday. Sable had seen it in the archive's staff file months ago and thought nothing of it, because why would you?

Edith Voss's birthday was March 3rd.

Sable had no birth certificate for Colette Marguerite Voss. But they had something better, something they'd catalogued years ago and never once thought to weigh: the Voss family Bible, donated to the historical society in 1972, its flyleaf inscribed with births in Edmund's mother's hand. Sable had photographed every page. The photographs lived in folder 94-B-11, third shelf, and Sable knew—knew with the cold certainty of a person who has spent ten years learning to trust their own filing system—what was written there.

*Colette Marguerite Voss, b. March 3rd, 1961.*

March 3rd. The same day, every year, that the town archive closed early for "the archivist's annual inventory."

* * *

Sable didn't go home. They went to the historical society, where the original Bible still lived in a glass case, and they stood over it in the empty reading room with their photograph open on their phone, comparing ink, comparing hands, letting the verification take as long as it needed to take because this was the kind of thing you did not get wrong twice.

It matched. Of course it matched. Sable had photographed it themselves.

But standing there, alone in a room full of other people's ancestors, Sable felt the shape of the whole thing assemble at last—not the facts, those were still coming, but the *motive*, the engine under thirty years of polite silence.

Everyone assumed the Voss case was about a girl who ran away, or drowned, or met a stranger on the lake road. Every theory, every episode, every drunk confession in every bar had orbited Marguerite herself—her money, her restlessness, her plans to leave. But Marguerite was the trigger, not the target. Marguerite had told people—Sable had the letters now, recovered, photocopied, backed up in triplicate—that she intended to contest the estate's management, expose the land fraud, and leave. If she left and lived, the estate stayed contested. If she died, the estate passed per the will: to Colette, *failing whom*—

To no one. To the trust. To the families.

Colette's death certificate wasn't missing because a child had died unrecorded. It was missing because a child had never died, and the adults who swore she had were the same adults whose alibis fit together like a quilt stitched by one pair of hands. They had buried a living heir on paper so that when they buried the other one in the orchard, the money would have nowhere to go but to them.

And then—one of them, or none of them, or the buried child herself—had come back to collect.

Sable's phone buzzed against the glass case. A text from an unknown number, and Sable's chest tightened the way it had tightened since the body in the borrowed clothes, since the notes arriving in their own handwriting.

But it was only Dotty. Sent eleven minutes after Sable left the annex:

*Hon. One more thing I remembered after you went. That probate affidavit in '91 — I filed the follow-up myself, years later. Court required proof of death eventually or the clause lapses. Never got it. Somebody at the courthouse has been sitting on that file a long time. You might ask who had access.*

Sable stared at the screen. Who had access. Who had spent twenty-two years with access to every file in Ashford Hollow, sealing and unsealing at will, beloved, untouchable, trusted with the town's entire memory.

Someone who could make a child disappear twice—once from the world, once from the paper.

Sable looked down at the Bible, at the faded brown ink of a birth entry from 1961, at the date that matched a birthday celebrated quietly behind a locked archive door every March.

Then they looked at their phone again, and typed a message to the one person in law enforcement still willing to take their calls:

**Reyes. I know why Marguerite died. It was never about what she knew. It was about who she was. Meet me tonight — and bring a warrant for the archive.**

The reply came fast, three dots appearing and disappearing, appearing again. Then:

**Whose archive, Sable. Say the name.**

Sable's thumb hovered. Outside, the gray light was going amber, evening pooling in the streets of a town that had rehearsed its innocence for thirty years. Saying the name would burn the last bridge Sable had—the tea, the warmth, the sense of being *known* by someone in this place. Saying the name would make it real.

They typed it anyway.

**Edith's.**

And beneath it, almost without deciding to, the second message, the one that had been waiting in them since the teacups and the sealed papers and the kindness that had felt, even then, like being measured for something:

**I think she's been Colette the whole time.**
