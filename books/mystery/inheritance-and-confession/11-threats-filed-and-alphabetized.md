## 11. Threats, Filed and Alphabetized

The first note arrived on a Tuesday, tucked into the mail slot of my basement archive like a bill I'd forgotten to pay.

I almost didn't open it. The envelope was plain, cream-colored, the kind sold in boxes of fifty at the stationery shop on Corliss Street—the same shop where I bought mine. That should have been my first warning. Instead it felt like a small, absurd kinship: whoever sent this shopped where I shopped.

Inside, on an index card, in careful block capitals:

*YOU SHOULD HAVE STOPPED AT NINETY-NINE.*

No signature. No postmark—hand-delivered, then. I turned the card over. Blank. I held it under the desk lamp, tilted it against the light the way I'd been taught by thirty years of reading other people's paper, and saw nothing but the faint tooth of cheap card stock.

I did what I always do with documents. I filed it.

That's the part that would come back for me later, so let me be precise about it now, while I still can: I filed it under T, for *Threats*, cross-referenced under V for *Voss*, because everything in my life eventually funnels down to Marguerite Voss the way all rivers in Ashford Hollow funnel down to the lake. I wrote the date and time of receipt on the card's lower edge in pencil—my own habit, my own hand—and slid it into place between *Trespassing, Merrick Estate* and *Voss, Marguerite—Timeline Discrepancies*.

It fit there perfectly. It looked like it had always belonged there.

I want you to understand what that means, because it took me three more notes to understand it myself. Someone had written me a threat in a format indistinguishable from my own filing system. Not just my handwriting style—though we'd get to that—but my *taxonomy*. My alphabet of the dead. Whoever they were, they had stood inside this room long enough to learn its grammar.

Or they had never needed to stand inside it at all. Because they'd built one just like it.

* * *

The second note came two days later, and this time it wasn't block capitals.

It was my handwriting.

Not close to my handwriting. Not an imitation with the seams showing. It was *mine*—the slightly forward slant, the way my lowercase g's drop below the line like hooks, the idiosyncratic way I cross my t's with a single flat stroke instead of a curve. I have been told by listeners who've seen my research scans that my handwriting is distinctive enough to identify at a glance. Apparently someone agreed, because when I pulled that second index card from beneath my door mat, my knees went soft in a way they hadn't gone soft even at the boathouse, standing over a dead man wearing my clothes.

The card read:

*THE ORCHARD. YOU KNOW WHAT'S UNDER THE ORCHARD. YOU'VE KNOWN FOR MONTHS. STOP PRETENDING YOU JUST FOUND OUT.*

My own hand. My own card stock. Filed, by anyone finding it later, exactly where I would file it: under O, *Orchard*, which sat directly adjacent to *Notes, Anonymous* in my cabinet, because in my system O came before N only in the drawer labeled *Evidence Inbound*—a drawer that now contained four items, all of them threatening me, all of them looking increasingly like they'd been written by me.

I sat on the concrete floor with my back against the filing cabinets and laughed until it turned into something else, something with no name, and then I got up and did the work. Because that's what I do. When the world becomes unbearable, I alphabetize it.

Here is what the work told me:

Whoever wrote those notes had access to samples of my handwriting going back years. My podcast website hosted scans of my research documents—I posted them as episode supplements, proof of rigor, receipts for the true-crime faithful. Every marginal note, every timeline annotation, every index card I'd ever photographed was out there, high-resolution, downloadable. I had built, over five years and a hundred episodes, a complete forensic specimen book of my own hand and handed it to the entire internet for free.

I had done the killer's forgery homework for them.

And the notes weren't random threats. Read them together and they constructed a narrative: *Sable knew about the orchard before the radar scan. Sable has been obsessed for months. Sable's obsession predates the body.* Every sentence was a brick in a wall being built around me, and the mortar was my own documented history. Episode ninety-three, where I'd speculated aloud about whether Marguerite had ever actually left town? A brick. Episode eighty-seven, where I'd interviewed the retired county surveyor about old property lines through the Voss land? A brick. Five years of honest curiosity, archived and timestamped, reassembled by hostile hands into a confession of prior knowledge.

You can't un-ring five years of episodes. You can't explain to a jury that a podcaster's speculation is not a murderer's foreknowledge without also explaining why the podcaster kept such meticulous records of every speculation—which is to say, without handing over the very archive that damns them.

I understood then, sitting on my floor surrounded by the neat green cabinets, that I was not being threatened.

I was being *drafted*. Someone was writing me into their story as the villain, using source material I had provided, in a genre I had taught the whole town to read. True crime has rules. Motive, means, opportunity, obsession. And here was obsession, arriving daily, in my own handwriting, filed in my own system.

* * *

I called Imogen Reyes from the payphone outside the laundromat on Delacroix Lane—not my cell, not my landline, because chapter eight of my paranoia had arrived sometime around dawn and installed itself permanently. She met me at the diner off Route 9, the one with the laminated menus and the waitress who'd stopped refilling my coffee the moment Reyes sat down across from me, as if guilt were contagious and could be transmitted through refills.

Reyes set a evidence bag on the table between us. Inside it: an index card.

"You got one too," I said.

"Slipped under my car door last night." She didn't take it out. "Your handwriting. Says I'm protecting you because we're involved. Says to check the motel registry from the night the boathouse call came in." She watched me across the table with those flat cop eyes that never blink quite enough. "There is no motel registry entry. There's no involvement. But if a jury reads that card after reading your podcast transcripts—"

"They'll see a detective covering for her lover."

"They'll see exactly that."

I wrapped my hands around the coffee cup because they needed something to do. "Reyes. I need you to hear how insane this is. Someone is forging my handwriting well enough to fool *me*. I stared at that second note for forty minutes. The g's are right. The t-crossings are right. The pressure variation on the downstrokes is right."

"Which tells us what?"

"It tells us they've practiced. Extensively. You don't get that good from casual study—you get that good from repetition, from filling pages, from—" I stopped. The thought arrived cold and complete, the way the worst ones do.

"From what?" Reyes said.

"From living with my handwriting for years." I lowered my voice. "Reyes, who has spent years handling my documents? Who has catalogued my research? Who asked me, politely, officially, for copies of my episode files 'for the historical record'?"

Something moved behind her eyes. "Edith Voss."

"The town archivist keeps duplicates of everything touching the founding families. My podcast is the most significant document collection about the Voss case in existence. Of course she has copies. Of course she's handled them. Hundreds of hours of my handwriting, indexed, preserved, *archived*—by a professional whose entire skill set is faithful reproduction of historical documents." I heard my own voice go thin. "She restores papers, Reyes. She repairs damaged manuscripts. She's trained in period-accurate reproduction. Forging my hand would be a Tuesday afternoon for her."

Reyes was quiet for a long moment. The diner hummed around us—a trucker paying his check, the fryer sighing, the ordinary machinery of a town that had been lying in unison since 1994.

"You realize," she said finally, "that from where I sit, this is exactly what a guilty person would say. Cleverest suspect, most elaborate theory, pointing at the sweet old lady everyone loves." She held up a hand before I could speak. "But. I pulled the metadata on the card slipped under my door. The card stock matches yours. Same supplier lot, probably same box." She leaned forward. "Corliss Street stationery. They keep purchase records?"

"For six months, maybe. Then they purge."

"Then somebody bought a lot of index cards recently, and we might catch it if we're fast." She stood, dropped bills on the table, and paused with her coat half on. "Delacroix. One more thing. The third note—the one you haven't shown me yet, the one you're carrying in your jacket pocket right now because you haven't decided whether it makes you look worse or better—"

My hand went involuntarily to my breast pocket. She almost smiled.

"—bring it in. Today. Whatever it says. Because a paper trail you hide looks exactly like a paper trail you wrote."

* * *

The third note had come that morning, and I hadn't shown it to anyone, because it was different from the others.

The others were accusations. This one was an invitation.

It was waiting for me taped to the archive door when I got home from the diner, and it was written not in block capitals, not even in my forged hand, but in a flowing, antique cursive—the kind of penmanship they taught in boarding schools in the fifties, the kind you see in old letters behind glass in museums. Edith Voss's hand. I'd seen it often enough on the placards in the town archive, on her meticulously labeled finding aids, on the birthday card she'd given me last spring in her warm, looping script: *To Sable, who gives the past its voice.*

This note said:

*Dear Sable — You are asking the wrong questions in the wrong order, and it will get you hurt. Come to the archive Thursday evening, after closing, and I will show you what the founding families paid to keep sealed. Bring nothing. Tell no one. Some inheritances must be handed over in person. — E.V.*

I read it eleven times on the front steps, the autumn light going amber and then gray, and I ran the trap through my head from every angle the way I'd run the 1994 timeline a hundred times.

If I went, I walked alone into the domain of a woman who might be Colette Voss, who might have killed twice, who had certainly forged my hand and mailed my ruin in cream-colored envelopes. If I didn't go, the invitation became another exhibit: *note from Voss found in suspect's possession, meeting arranged and never reported.* Either way, the paper trail thickened. Either way, I was being filed.

But underneath the fear, quieter and more shameful, was the thing I couldn't file anywhere: *she wants to talk to me.* After weeks of deflections from the Hales and the Merricks, of word-for-word alibis polished like heirloom silver, of a whole town rehearsing its silence in my face—one person wanted to sit across a table and tell me the truth. Even if it was bait. Even if it was the spider's invitation. Some starved part of me heard *come Thursday evening* and answered *yes* before the rest of me could object.

I photographed the note, front and back, and emailed the images to myself, to Reyes, and to a listener in Seattle who maintained redundant backups of everything I sent him, because paranoia, properly organized, is just archival science applied to survival.

Then I went downstairs and did the hardest thing I have ever done in that basement.

I took the three notes out of my cabinets—all of them, including the ones in my own handwriting—and I put them in a plain manila envelope, and I sealed it, and I wrote across the flap, in block letters deliberately unlike my own: *RECEIVED AS FOUND. NOT AUTHORED BY ME. CHAIN OF CUSTODY BEGINS HERE.* I dated it and signed it and would deliver it to Reyes in the morning, surrendering the only originals of the forgeries into official custody, where they could be compared against my known handwriting samples by experts who would, I prayed, notice what I had noticed at two in the morning with a magnifying lamp:

The forged g's were perfect. Almost too perfect. Identical across both notes, letter after letter, the way handwriting is identical only when it's traced or mechanically reproduced—because real human hands vary, even unconsciously, even when forging. Real Sable g's wobbled. These didn't.

Machine-made. Or made by someone whose training treated letters as reproducible objects rather than expressions. An archivist's hand. A restorer's discipline.

I stood in the middle of my archive, in the green glow of the banker's lamp, surrounded by twenty-two years of the dead arranged in alphabetical order, and I understood the shape of the thing being built around me. The stolen clothes placed my body at the boathouse. The notes placed my obsession everywhere. The episodes placed my knowledge before the facts. Brick by brick, exhibit by exhibit, someone was constructing a Sable Delacroix who had done it all—and they were building that person out of my own materials, in my own hand, in my own filing system, until the investigator and the killer shared a single paper body and no jury on earth could tell them apart.

They weren't trying to destroy my reputation.

They were trying to *complete my collection*—with me as the final item, alphabetized under D, filed between the dead.

Thursday was two days away. I looked at the invitation's photograph on my phone, at that warm looping cursive offering me the truth like a cup of tea, and I made the decision I would spend the rest of my life explaining.

I was going to the archive.

And I was going to bring something after all—because Edith Voss had made one mistake, the same mistake I make every day, the mistake of a person who believes paper obeys the hand that writes it.

She had signed her name.
