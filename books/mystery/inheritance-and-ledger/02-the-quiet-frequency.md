## 2. The Quiet Frequency

There are two kinds of people who work the night shift at Meridian Cabs: those who can't sleep and those who don't want to be found. I am the second kind, though for eleven years I've told supervisors the first. Insomnia is a sympathetic disease. Invisibility is a suspicious one.

My apartment on Delacroix Street had one lamp, one chair, one kettle, and—until last night—one envelope's worth of mail per month, all of it addressed to Occupant. Now there were two letters sitting on the table like a pair of witnesses who'd agreed beforehand to contradict each other. One said *I killed her*. The other said *she left you everything*. Both used my mother's phrase, the one she'd say when tucking me in during the years before tucking-in became a memory: *the quiet ones hear everything first.*

I did what any reasonable man does when his dead mother starts speaking through strangers' stationery. I made tea I didn't drink and opened my dispatch logs.

Here is what you need to understand about the logs. Meridian runs a proprietary routing system—ancient, ugly, beloved by exactly no one except me. Every call that comes in gets assigned a channel. Channels map to zones; zones map to cars; cars map to drivers who are mostly awake. The dispatcher's terminal sits above all of it like a lighthouse nobody looks at. Calls route *through* my channel before they go anywhere else. That's by design. It means every booking in the city passes across my screen, timestamped, tagged, logged.

It also means the log of my shift is, in a sense, a diary of the whole sleeping city. And I have kept that diary faithfully for eleven years, because a man who erases himself from every other record likes to know that somewhere, in machine language, the night still happened.

The night Marguerite Voss died was March 14th. I remember it the way I remember most nights: nothing. Fog advisory after midnight, a bachelor party that ended badly near the riverfront, a woman crying in car 22 for forty minutes without ever saying why. Ordinary weather for human beings.

I pulled the file. Meridian's archive system is so old it predates the habit of making things easy to search, which suits me fine. I scrolled with the patience of a man who has spent a decade watching other people's emergencies scroll past.

The first anomaly showed up at 11:47 p.m.

A call logged to channel nine. My channel.

I stared at it the way you'd stare at your own name in an obituary. Channel nine doesn't take calls. Channel nine is the dispatcher's loopback—the internal line, the one that exists so the system has somewhere to put its own housekeeping traffic. No customer has ever called channel nine. No customer *can* call channel nine. It isn't published, isn't listed, isn't wired to anything a phone could reach.

And yet there it was: 11:47 p.m., March 14th, inbound, duration four minutes twelve seconds, routed through channel nine, assigned to car 31, zone 6—the old industrial district, the depot row, the part of town where even the fog goes to be forgotten.

I never took that call.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only inheritance I've ever trusted. On March 14th, between 11:30 and midnight, I was at my terminal. I took eleven calls. I dispatched eleven cars. I drank one cup of vending-machine coffee that tasted like it had been brewed as a punishment. At no point did a call come through channel nine, because channel nine cannot receive calls, because I checked that fact years ago the way other men check their locks.

But the log said what it said. Four minutes, twelve seconds. Someone had spoken into my frequency for the length of a slow song, and the system had written it down in my handwriting, so to speak, and neither I nor anyone else had heard a word.

* *

I did what any careful man does when he finds a ghost in his machinery: I went looking for more ghosts.

The system keeps ninety days hot, then archives to tape—actual tape, in a closet in the basement that smells like pencil shavings and regret. March 14th was eighty-one days back. Still warm. I pulled the full night, 10 p.m. to 6 a.m., and laid it out beside the official timeline of the Voss case, which I had reconstructed from three newspaper articles and a coroner's summary that read like it was written by someone being paid per comma.

Marguerite Voss died sometime between 11:30 p.m. and 1:15 a.m. on March 14th–15th. Found the next morning by her housekeeper, at the foot of the east staircase, in a house with eleven rooms and no forced entry. The police called it a fall for six weeks. Then someone found the bruising on her upper arms, and the word *homicide* arrived wearing its little hat.

Now, here is where I should tell you what I found in my own logs, and here is where I will fail to tell it calmly.

There were seven calls that night routed through channel nine. Seven. At 11:47, 12:03, 12:19, 12:31, 12:58, 1:06, and 1:14 a.m. Each one assigned to a different car. Each one in or around zone 6, then zone 4, then zone 6 again—a little constellation of bookings orbiting the Voss estate like moths that had learned to use radio.

Seven calls. I took none of them. I heard none of them. But every single one lived in the record under my channel, my shift code, my operator ID: PV-114. Paloma Voss, dispatcher, third watch. The system didn't just say calls happened. It said *I* handled them.

Do you know what that feeling is? It's not fear, not exactly. Fear is for things coming at you. This was the other thing—the cold, patient horror of realizing something has been inside your walls for a long time and has only now let you see it, the way a burglar leaves a drawer open on purpose so you'll know he could have closed it.

Someone had been driving my hands.

* *

I did not sleep. Obviously. Instead I did the thing I'm actually good at, the thing eleven years of nights have trained into me like a scar trains skin: I looked for patterns.

The seven ghost calls weren't random. I mapped them against the timeline and felt my tea go cold in my hand, which was fine, since I hadn't drunk any of it.

At 11:47, a car booked to the corner of Halden and Sixth—two blocks from the Voss estate gates. At 12:03, a pickup logged at the estate's service road, passenger destination listed as the riverfront hotel. At 12:19, another car, zone 6, no passenger recorded, engine time logged but no fare. At 12:31, a call from a payphone—I checked; the number belonged to the broken phone outside the shuttered laundromat on Corvus Street, half a mile from the estate. And so on, down the hour, each booking a small stone laid in a path, until 1:14 a.m., when the last call placed a car at the top of Depot Row and the trail simply stopped, tidy as a sentence ending in a period.

Every stone of that path sat inside the window in which Marguerite Voss stopped being alive.

And every stone had been placed through channel nine. Through me.

Here's the elegant, filthy genius of it, and I want you to appreciate it the way I did, hunched over my table at 4 a.m. with the dawn still an hour off and my reflection looking like a man who'd been robbed: whoever built those calls hadn't hacked the system. They'd *used* it. Properly. With the right codes, the right sequence, the right knowledge of how Meridian's routing chain worked—which channels existed, which were silent, which terminal ID would make a booking look routine rather than remarkable. This wasn't a vandal scrawling on my walls. This was a colleague. Someone who knew the house well enough to move through it without letting the floorboards speak.

There are maybe four people alive who know channel nine the way I know it. Two are retired. One is me.

That math kept me company for a while, and not pleasantly.

* *

By six a.m. I had done three things. First, I copied the entire night onto a thumb drive I keep taped under the kettle, because a man who erases himself keeps backups of the one record that proves he existed. Second, I printed the seven ghost calls and burned the printout in the sink, watching the timestamps curl—because paper talks, and I wasn't ready to let it talk yet. Third, I called in sick for the first time in four years, using a voice I keep in reserve for doctors and landlords, flat and congested and forgettable.

Then I sat in my one chair and thought about the letters.

*I killed her.* *She left you everything.* Both signed with variations of the same closing sentiment. Both carrying my mother's phrase like a key slipped under a door. Whoever wrote them knew Marguerite Voss. Knew my mother. Knew—or suspected—that the two women's lives had touched, which was more than I knew, and I was the one who'd spent thirty years trying not to know anything about either.

And now I had to consider the possibility I'd been avoiding since 11:47 p.m. appeared on my screen: that the letters and the ghost calls came from the same hand. That someone had killed Marguerite Voss, laundered their alibi through my frequency, and then—weeks later—mailed me a confession and an inheritance like a man tossing meat to a dog whose bark he wanted to hear.

Or, worse, the other possibility, the one with teeth: that the confession letter was bait, and the moment I walked into a police station waving it, the record would close around me like water. *Dispatcher fabricates bookings, receives mysterious inheritance, confesses to murder.* A tidy story. The kind of story a file tells about a man who has spent his whole life making sure files had nothing to say about him.

They had chosen the wrong invisible man, if that was the plan. You don't frame a ghost unless you're prepared for him to haunt you back.

* *

At noon, having decided nothing and prepared everything, I did the one thing the letters demanded and my instincts forbade: I looked up the Voss family.

It didn't take long. Reclusive heiresses generate surprisingly thick paper trails, mostly obituaries and property disputes. The son-in-law surfaced immediately—Detective Inspector Corliss Hale, married to Marguerite's daughter Celeste, deceased these five years. A policeman. A policeman married into the house on Halden Hill, in the same city where my frequency had been borrowed to build an alibi, in the same week a confession letter arrived at the address of a man who officially did not exist.

I read his name three times. It sat on the screen looking respectable, the way names do when they've had practice.

There was a photograph attached to some chamber-of-commerce piece about a charity gala. Tall, silver at the temples, a smile calibrated to the millimeter—the face of a man who has never once entered a room without knowing where the exits are. I have spent my life studying faces on the far side of glass and radio static. I know what performed warmth looks like. His was excellent. That was the thing that frightened me. Not the name, not the badge. The craftsmanship.

*The quiet ones hear everything first,* my mother used to say, and I had built an entire existence on the principle. For eleven years the night shift had been my monastery: I listened to the city breathe and asked nothing in return, and in exchange the world agreed not to look at me.

Someone had come into my monastery and left footprints in the incense.

I turned off the lamp, though the daylight made that pointless, and I sat in the dark anyway, out of respect for the occasion. Tomorrow I would visit the Voss estate. I would present myself as the heir the second letter claimed I was, and I would walk into a house full of mourners and suspects and watch their faces the way I watch a board of flickering lights—looking for the one signal that doesn't belong, the call that routes through a channel that shouldn't exist.

Somewhere in that house was a person fluent in my systems. Fluent enough to speak in my voice.

I intended to learn the language back.
