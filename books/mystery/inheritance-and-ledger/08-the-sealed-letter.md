## 8. The Sealed Letter

Renata called me on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the most forgettable day of the week—the day even the calendar seems embarrassed to acknowledge—and I had built my entire life in the image of a Tuesday.

"Come to the house," she said. No greeting. Renata treated phone calls like telegrams; every word cost money. "Bring nothing. Touch nothing. And Paloma—" a pause long enough that I checked whether the line had died, "—come in through the garden gate. Not the front."

The front door of Voss House was where Corliss Hale lived his life. I understood immediately.

* * *

I took a cab to the estate, which is the kind of joke only a dispatcher would find funny. I sat in the back of unit 214 and listened to my own voice crackle over the radio assigning routes across the city, calm and anonymous, a god of small logistics who existed nowhere at all. It occurred to me, somewhere around the third traffic circle, that this might be one of the last nights I could do that. I didn't know why yet. I just felt it, the way you feel weather changing behind your back.

The garden gate was iron, older than the house, hinged with a complaint it had been voicing since before either of us was born. Renata was waiting inside it with a flashlight she didn't turn on, because Renata considered darkness a reasonable security measure and electric light an extravagance.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I work nights."

"That's not what I meant." She studied me for a moment with those flat, unimpressed eyes that had apparently survived forty years of service to a woman even flatter and more unimpressed. Then she turned and walked toward the greenhouse, and I understood I was expected to follow, and I did, because that's what happens to people around Renata. You follow. Resistance is noted and ignored.

The greenhouse smelled of wet earth and dead summer. She'd set up a card table under the largest window, and on it sat a single object: an envelope, heavy cream stock, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. My name was written across it in a hand so precise it looked typeset.

Marguerite Voss's hand. I'd seen it now on two letters and a dozen pages of case file. It was the kind of handwriting that belonged to someone who had never once wondered whether her opinion would be welcome.

"She gave me this eleven months ago," Renata said. "Eleven months and four days before she died."

"Why are you giving it to me now?"

"Because now I know who you are." She folded her arms. "You came here pretending to be a stranger with a lawyer's letter, and I let you pretend, because Marguerite told me someone would come pretending. She said, *Renata, when he arrives he'll apologize for existing. Don't let him.*"

My mouth opened. Closed. There was no adequate response to being read that accurately by a dead woman through a living interpreter.

"Sit," Renata said.

I sat. The folding chair complained less than the gate had.

* * *

"I'll tell you what she told me first," Renata said, standing over the table like a sentry who'd decided the envelope couldn't be trusted alone with me. "Then you read it. In that order. She insisted."

"Of course she did."

"Don't take that tone with a dead woman. She earned her tones."

I raised both hands in surrender, which surprised me—I hadn't made a gesture that large in years. Something about the greenhouse loosened things. Maybe it was being surrounded by growing things in a house full of performing ones.

"Marguerite kept ledgers," Renata began. "Not just money. Everything. Who visited, what they ate, what they lied about. She started when her husband was alive and never stopped. And for the last three years, the ledger had a problem in it, and the problem was Corliss Hale."

"The detective inspector."

"The embezzler," Renata corrected. "He'd been siphoning from the estate accounts since before Ilsa married him. Small amounts at first—she almost admired the craft of it, she said. Then not small. He was moving money through a charity foundation, a scholarship fund, some property shell company with a name I can't remember because it was designed to be unrememberable. By last spring he'd taken enough that Marguerite could have rebuilt the east wing twice over."

"And she confronted him?"

Renata looked at me the way a teacher looks at a student who has asked whether water is wet. "She documented him. For three years. Every transfer, every date, every forged signature—he'd learned to imitate hers, which she found flattering and unforgivable in equal measure. She was building a case. Not for the police." A thin smile. "She didn't trust the police. Her son-in-law *was* the police. She was building it for probate court, for the moment she rewrote her will."

"And then she died."

"And then she died," Renata agreed quietly. "Nineteen days after she told me the new will was drafted. Nineteen days after she put this envelope in my hands and said, *When the boy comes, give him this, but not until you're sure he's the boy.*"

"The boy," I repeated. The word sat strangely in my chest, warm and foreign, like a coin from a country I'd never visited.

"Her word, not mine." Renata pushed the envelope an inch toward me. "She talked about you, you know. Toward the end. Your mother's name mostly. She'd say it like she was testing whether it still hurt. *Ana's boy.* She said you'd inherited Ana's habit of disappearing."

"We're not close, my family and I."

"No," Renata said. "You're the last of them, and none of you were ever close, and that's exactly why she chose you. She wanted the estate to go to someone Hale couldn't reach through love or leverage. Someone with no debts, no attachments, no—" she gestured at me, taking in all of it, the thrift-store coat and the posture of a man perpetually about to leave a room, "—no visible self to threaten."

"So I'm the heir because I'm nobody."

"You're the heir because you're *unbuyable*," Renata snapped. "There's a difference, and she saw it, and I wish to God you would."

* * *

I broke the seal.

Inside were six pages, and I will not reproduce them all here because some documents belong to the dead, but I will tell you what they did to me.

The first page was the ledger summary: dates, amounts, account numbers, Hale's forgeries photographed and annotated in that merciless script. Three years of theft laid out like an autopsy. The second and third pages were copies of correspondence between Marguerite and a firm of probate solicitors in the city—a new will, executed, witnessed, filed. The fourth page named the beneficiaries. Renata, a sum that would have made the housekeeper laugh out loud if she were capable of it. Ilsa, held in trust, with conditions that amounted to *get away from your husband*. And the residue—the house, the accounts, the land, everything—to:

*Paloma Voss, grandnephew, son of my niece Ana, whom I failed, and whose failure I intend to correct posthumously as I am no longer capable of correcting it in person.*

I read that sentence four times. On the fourth reading my vision blurred, which I attributed to the greenhouse humidity, and which was not the greenhouse humidity.

The fifth page was worse. The fifth page was personal.

*I do not know you,* she had written, *and I know that my money cannot purchase forgiveness for what this family did to your mother—we turned her out, we let her name go unspoken, we let her son grow up thinking he came from nowhere. But you come from somewhere. You come from here. I am told you erase yourself from every record you touch. Consider this letter the one record you cannot erase. You are written down now. You are in the ledger. Live with it.*

The sixth page was a single line, and it was the one that turned my blood from warm to cold in the space of a heartbeat:

*If I die before the will is filed publicly, assume Corliss Hale killed me, because he will know by then what the document says, and men like him do not lose gracefully.*

* * *

I sat there for a long time. Somewhere outside, a night bird was conducting its shift change, and I thought absurdly that we should compare schedules.

"This is why the letters came," I finally said. "After she died. The confession letter, the inheritance letter. Someone sent them to flush me out."

"Or to warn you," Renata said. "Or to frame you. We don't know who sent them, and neither does whoever is watching this house—which, incidentally, is why you came through the garden."

"Is someone watching?"

"There's a gray sedan that parks on the ridge road most evenings. Plate's always dirty. Hale's department uses gray sedans." She said it the way she'd say *it's raining*, and I felt the full weight of what she was telling me settle onto my shoulders: she had been living inside this knowledge for a year, alone, guarding an envelope, waiting for a boy who apologizes for existing.

"Why give it to me tonight?" I asked. "What changed?"

Renata was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into something rawer than I'd yet heard from her.

"Because yesterday a man from the solicitor's office called the house asking whether Marguerite had ever discussed revisions to her will. Official-sounding. Except I phoned the office myself this morning, and no such man exists, and the firm confirmed the will was filed properly last week—which means Hale now knows. Which means—" she tapped the sixth page, "—everything she predicted is now in motion, and the only person that document protects is sitting in my greenhouse wearing a coat with a broken zipper."

I looked down at the coat. The zipper was, in fact, broken. I'd been meaning to fix it for two years.

"Paloma." Renata leaned across the card table, and for the first time she touched me—two fingers on my wrist, brief and hard, a pulse-check disguised as reassurance. "She chose you because you can't be bought. So don't be stupid enough to be scared instead. Fear is just another currency, and he spends it better than you do."

* * *

I left through the garden gate, the letter against my chest inside my jacket, six pages of dead woman's certainty riding over my heart like a second pulse. The gray sedan wasn't on the ridge road tonight. That somehow made it worse. Absence, in my experience, is where surveillance keeps its best manners.

Halfway back to the road, my phone buzzed. A message from dispatch—an automated route confirmation, the system's nightly courtesy.

Except I hadn't booked a return trip.

The confirmation showed my address, my name, and a pickup time twenty minutes from now, dispatched from terminal four.

Terminal four is my terminal. It doesn't exist on any roster. Only two people alive know it's real, and one of them taught me how to build it.

I stood in the dark between the hedge and the wall, holding a dead woman's handwriting against my ribs, and understood that Marguerite Voss had given me more than an inheritance tonight.

She'd given me a reason to be found.
