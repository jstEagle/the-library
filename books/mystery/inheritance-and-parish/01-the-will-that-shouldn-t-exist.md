## 1. The Will That Shouldn't Exist

The taxi would go no farther than the iron gate.

Isandro Reyes paid the driver, watched him turn the car around with the unhurried relief of a man escaping a debt, and stood for a moment in the road with her document case in one hand and her coat collar up against the wind. The Fenlow valley spread below her in the grey light of a March morning — flat, drowned country, the fields stitched with drainage channels that caught the sky and held it, so that the whole landscape seemed made of tarnished mirrors. Somewhere out in that silver haze, a heron lifted off on slow wings, and the sound of the wind through the reeds was like the sound of a large room being swept.

Vane Manor sat at the head of the valley on the only rise for miles, a house that had been built to be seen and was now, evidently, trying not to be. Its east wing was shuttered. Ivy had taken the north face entirely, climbing to the eaves in thick ropes, and several of the tall windows had been boarded from the outside with a neatness that suggested habit rather than emergency. The gate itself stood open — the hinges had rusted in that position, Isandro would learn later, and no one had forced them since — and the drive beyond was a riverbed of gravel and standing water.

She had taken the case, as she took all her cases, because it was strange.

Not strange in the way the London firms meant it — a contested bequest, a mistress with a folder of letters, a family business split by sibling grievance. Isandro Reyes had spent fourteen years in probate and she could smell ordinary grief at a hundred paces. This was different. The instruction had come by post, on paper so heavy it felt like currency, in a hand so controlled it might have been engraved: *Edric Vane, of Vane Manor, Fenlow, requests the attendance of Isandro Reyes, solicitor, for the reading and execution of his last will and testament. Terms enclosed. Do not telephone. Come at once.*

The terms enclosed had been generous enough to make her suspicious, and the *do not telephone* had made her certain. She had looked up what there was to look up — which was very little. Edric Vane, seventy-nine, landowner, unmarried, no children of record. The Vane name appeared in the county histories as a footnote and in the parish registers, she supposed, as a column of births and marriages and deaths going back three centuries. One death in particular. She had found it the night before, in the digitized records, while her tea went cold beside her.

*Maren Vane, drowned, 11th of March, eleven years ago.*

She had told herself it was coincidence. Executors' files were full of the dead; that was the whole of the profession. But the name had stayed with her through the night the way a stone stays in a shoe, and now, standing at the gate of the house that had produced it, she felt the stone shift.

The front door opened before she reached it.

A woman stood in the frame — tall, mid-forties, dressed in mourning grey so dark it was nearly black, with a brooch at her throat like a closed fist. She had her uncle's face, Isandro would later understand: the long jaw, the pale eyes set deep under a shelf of brow. She did not smile. She looked at Isandro the way one looks at weather that has been forecast and has finally arrived.

"Miss Reyes. Cordelia Vane. You found us, then."

"Your uncle's letter was thorough."

"He was thorough about everything except dying. Come in. The others are waiting, and Aldous dislikes waiting more than he dislikes most people."

*The others.* Isandro stepped into a hall that smelled of cold stone and beeswax and, beneath both, the sweetish ghost of a fire that had been allowed to go out years ago and never fully forgiven. The floor was black oak worn pale in two paths — one from the door to the stairs, one from the door to a door on the left, and nowhere else, as though the family had spent decades walking only between the rooms where they could be seen.

The left-hand door opened onto a drawing room where a fire did burn, small and precise, and where three people rose as she entered like figures in a music box. An elderly man — broad still, though the breadth had gone soft, with a face built for jowls and currently achieving it — extended a hand that managed to be both offered and withheld. Aldous Vane. A younger man in a suit a decade out of fashion stood behind him with the posture of furniture; a solicitor's clerk, or a nephew of no account, or both. And by the window, a woman in her sixties with a housekeeper's keys at her waist, who did not rise at all and did not apologize for it.

"You'll take tea," Aldous said. It was not a question. "Cordelia, the tray. Mrs. Petch will pour — she's been in this house since before you were born, Miss Reyes, she'll remember your visit longer than you will."

Mrs. Petch's face did not change. Isandro had the impression, fleeting and unprovable, that the woman was looking not at her but at the document case, and that what she felt was fear.

*Well,* Isandro thought, taking the chair Aldous indicated — the one with its back to the light, a host's trick as old as hospitality — *let's see what you're all so careful of.*

* * *

The will was in her hands within the hour, and it was, in every particular, a beautiful document.

Edric Vane had executed it eight months before his death, before two witnesses Isandro would need to trace, on paper watermarked with a firm that had dissolved in the nineties, which meant the paper was old stock and the will had been drafted long before it was signed. That was the first thing. People who draft wills years in advance are people who expect to be contested. The second thing was the hand: the same controlled engraving that had summoned her, deteriorating only at the final clause, where the letters leaned like a fence in wind.

She read it aloud, as the letter had instructed, in the drawing room, with the fire ticking and Mrs. Petch pouring tea no one drank. The opening formalities. The revocation of all prior wills — *there had been prior wills, then; Aldous's jaw tightened at the word, a small movement, a man swallowing something* — the specific bequests: the housekeeper to receive her cottage and a hundred pounds for every year of her service, which made Mrs. Petch, briefly, a woman of means, though her face said she'd have traded it all for one honest answer. The land, the tenancies, the shooting rights over the fen, to be held in trust and sold as a whole, not in parcels. *Not in parcels.* Isandro noted the emphasis and filed it.

And then the residuary clause, and Isandro read it, and the room changed.

"I give, devise, and bequeath the whole of the residue of my estate, real and personal, wheresoever situated, unto my niece, Maren Vane—"

Cordelia set down her cup. The china touched the saucer with a click that in the silence was like a shot.

"—of this parish," Isandro continued, because the clause continued, and because her voice, she found, was steadier than the rest of her, "should she be living; and should she not be living, then the whole of the residue unto the parish church of St. Brannoc, Fenlow, for the maintenance of its yard, its registers, and its poor."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight and grain and temperature. Isandro sat with the will in her lap and let the silence do its work, because fourteen years in probate had taught her that the reading of a will is a seance, and the dead speak through pauses as much as through words.

It was Aldous who broke it, and he broke it with a laugh.

"Well," he said. "Well. The old man's mind went at the end, you see it? Maren. Maren's been dead eleven years, Miss Reyes. Drowned in the fen, poor girl, before you ever heard the name Vane. There was an inquest. There's a grave in the churchyard with her name on it. I put the stone there myself." He said it easily, comfortably, the way a man says things he has said many times, and that was the thing Isandro noticed — not the words, but the *wear* on them, the smoothness of repetition, a story handled so often it had lost its edges.

"Then the gift lapses," she said. "The residue passes to the parish. It's a well-drafted clause — your uncle anticipated exactly this contingency."

"Did he." Aldous looked at her, and for a moment the comfort fell away and she saw what was under it, which was not grief and not confusion but a kind of alert, animal attention, the look of a man counting exits. "And which contingency would that be, Miss Reyes? That his favorite was dead? Or that she wasn't?"

The fire ticked. Out on the fen, faint through the old glass, a bird called — three notes, falling, like a question being asked and abandoned.

"I couldn't say, Mr. Vane. I execute wills. I don't interpret them. If the named beneficiary is deceased, the law is clear and the parish is rich. If she is not—" Isandro closed the will with care, aligning the pages, "—then I am obliged to make reasonable inquiries to satisfy myself of the fact before the estate is distributed. That is not my preference. It is my duty."

"Reasonable inquiries," Aldous repeated. He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, worse than the boarded windows, because it was warm. "In a village like this? Everyone you'd ask is in that churchyard, Miss Reyes, and the rest have memories like sieves. You'll be here a month asking questions that were answered before you were called to the bar." He stood, and the standing had a finality to it, an audience-dismissing architecture. "But you're the executor. Do what you must. Cordelia will see you have a room — you'll stay at the manor, of course. The Grey Room. It's the warmest."

*He wants me inside the walls,* Isandro thought, taking his hand, feeling the dry strength of it. *Not out in the village, asking.*

"I'd rather lodge in the village," she said. "The inn."

Something moved through the room then, fast, like a current under ice — through Aldous's stillness, Cordelia's lowered eyes, Mrs. Petch's motionless hands. The inn, then. The village. The place where memories were sieves.

"Of course," Aldous said. "Whatever suits you. We're a hospitable parish, Miss Reyes. You'll find that out."

* * *

They let her out at dusk, and the valley had turned the color of pewter, and Isandro walked the mile to the inn because she wanted the walk and wanted, more, to be seen walking it.

The village of Fenlow was one street and a church, the street bending along the edge of the drained fields like a sentence that doesn't quite end. There was a shop, a pub, the inn with its sign repainted so many times the saint on it had become a smudge of blue. And there was the church — St. Brannoc's, squat and Norman, its tower the highest thing for miles, its yard full of leaning stones furred green with eleven years of weather and a hundred more. Isandro stopped at the lychgate without meaning to. She was a lawyer; she did not believe in atmosphere. But the yard had it, a stillness like held breath, and she found herself reading the stones the way she read documents, quickly, professionally, and then not professionally at all.

The Vane plot was in the southeast corner, fenced in iron, the largest stones in the yard. Edric's was fresh — granite, unweathered, the dates still sharp. And beside it, older, smaller, a stone with a carved willow bending over a name:

*MAREN JANE VANE. 1971–2011. TAKEN BY THE WATER, KEPT BY GOD.*

Eleven years of lichen on it. A real stone, a paid-for stone, a stone someone had stood in front of and wept at, or pretended to. Isandro stood there as the light went out of the sky and the first lamps came on down the street, and she did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since the gate.

A will naming a dead woman is an error, a sentiment, a lapse — common enough, sad enough, closed in an afternoon. But a will naming a dead woman *with a contingency for her being alive* is a different instrument entirely. It is a message. Edric Vane had drafted it years in advance, had leaned over that final clause with a fence-in-wind hand, and had written, in the only language the law would enforce: *she may come back. Leave the door open.*

And then there was the date. Isandro had checked it twice. Edric Vane had died on the 9th of March. Maren Vane, according to the register, had drowned on the 11th of March — eleven years ago, almost to the day. The will had been signed in July. But the man had died two days before the anniversary of the drowning, and had summoned his executor with instructions to come *at once*, and had arranged for the reading to fall on—

Isandro took out her phone and looked at the calendar, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the fen wind.

Tomorrow was the 11th of March.

She was standing there, phone in hand, when the church door opened.

She hadn't seen the figure approach — the yard's shadows had simply produced it, a woman in a sexton's canvas coat, wheelbarrow at rest, a spade over one shoulder with the ease of long habit. Middle-aged, weathered, ordinary, the kind of face villages are made of. The woman looked at her across the graves with pale, level eyes, and said nothing, and Isandro said nothing, and the silence between them lasted three full seconds and contained, Isandro would swear to it afterward, an entire conversation.

Then the sexton touched her cap — a small, ironic, almost courtly gesture, a gesture from another century — and walked on into the dark of the yard, and the church door closed behind her, and did not lock. Isandro heard the latch. Heard it not catch.

She walked back to the inn with her collar up and her mind moving fast now, the professional unease gone, burned off, replaced by the other thing, the thing that had brought her into this profession in the first place and that she had learned, over fourteen years, to distrust precisely because it was never wrong.

Fascination.

At the inn, behind the bar, the landlord was telling a story to two farmers, and he stopped when she came in — stopped a half-beat too long, the way people stop when the story being told is about you. Then the talk resumed, about lambing, about the drainage board, about nothing.

In her room, Isandro laid the will on the bed and read the residuary clause again by lamplight, and then read it aloud, once, quietly, the way Edric Vane had perhaps intended it to be read — on the 11th of March, in the valley, with the family assembled.

*Unto my niece, Maren Vane, of this parish, should she be living.*

The lamp hissed. Out on the fen, the bird called again — three notes, falling.

And Isandro Reyes, who did not believe in atmosphere, who executed wills and did not interpret them, sat down at the inn's small desk, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote at the top of it, in her own careful hand, the question she intended to spend the next weeks asking:

*Where is Maren Vane?*
