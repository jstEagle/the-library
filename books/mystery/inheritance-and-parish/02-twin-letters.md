## 2. Twin Letters

The letters arrived on a Tuesday, which Isandro Reyes would later consider the last ordinary day of her professional life.

She had been at her desk since seven, working through the Vane estate inventory with the methodical patience the work demanded. Eleven days had passed since the reading at Vane Manor, eleven days since she had stood in that cold drawing room and spoken a dead man's words aloud — *to my daughter Maren, should she live* — while Aldous Vane's teacup paused halfway to his mouth and Cordelia's smile went stiff as varnish. Eleven days of correspondence with the probate registry, of quiet inquiries that returned nothing useful, of the particular frustration that comes from holding a document that cannot be true and being unable to prove it false.

Maren Vane had been declared dead eleven years ago. Drowned, the record said, in the Fenlow river, her body never recovered but her death certified all the same, witnessed and sworn by the parish vicar himself. And yet Edric Vane — who had signed his will with a shaking hand eight months before his death, in the presence of two witnesses whose statements Isandro had personally verified — had left her the east meadow, the cottage at its edge, and a sum of money large enough to matter.

Dead men do not inherit. Dead women certainly do not. And yet there it was, in iron-gall ink on paper that smelled faintly of the manor's damp: *my daughter Maren.*

Isandro had spent those eleven days telling herself there was an explanation. A clerical error. An old man's confusion. A second Maren, perhaps, some cousin's child given the family name. The law offered her these comforts the way a doctor offers a placebo — not because they cured anything, but because the patient needed to hold something in her hands.

Then the post came.

Her office occupied the upper floor of a narrow building on Chandler's Lane, above a stationer's shop that smelled of glue and cheap paper. The landlady's boy brought the mail up at half past nine each morning, and Isandro had long ago learned to sort it in a single pass: bills left, client correspondence center, the rest to the tray by the door for burning. She was halfway through this ritual when her hand stopped.

Two envelopes. Same heavy cream stock. Same handwriting — no, not quite the same, and it was the *not quite* that made her set down the rest of the post and simply look at them for a long moment. Both addresses were written in a fine, slanted script, elegant and old-fashioned, the kind of hand taught by governesses to girls who would never need it. But one envelope was addressed in ink gone slightly brown with age, the strokes thinner, pressed hard enough in places to emboss the paper. The other was fresh. The ink still shone faintly where the light caught it.

One letter had been written years ago and kept. The other had been written now.

Isandro sat down slowly. Outside the window, Chandler's Lane went about its morning — the fishmonger hosing his step, two women arguing cheerfully over a barrow of cabbages — and none of them knew, could not have known, that something had just arrived in the world that would not be put back.

She opened the older letter first. It seemed right, somehow, to keep their order.

---

The paper inside was the same cream stock, four pages, closely written. No salutation. No signature. It began without preamble, the way a confession begins when the confessor has rehearsed it so many times that greeting has worn away:

*I was in the boathouse the night my father's dog was killed. I want that understood first, because everything else depends on it. Silas Crane did not kill the dog. Silas Crane was not even there. I know this because I was hiding behind the tarred canvas on the north wall, and I saw my uncle Aldous come down the path with the dog already limping, already bleeding from the mouth, and I heard what he said to it before he finished the work.*

Isandro read the sentence twice. Then a third time. Her tea went cold beside her untouched, and she did not notice.

The handwriting was Maren Vane's. Isandro was certain of it, and the certainty frightened her more than the content, because certainty required evidence and she had only one piece: the will itself, Edric Vane's own hand, and the signature of Maren Vane appended to a codicil dated six years before her death — a codicil Isandro had found in the manor's deed box, unremarked, filed between a lease and a livestock valuation. She had compared the hands late one night, lamp low, telling herself it was diligence. The loops were identical. The peculiar backward tilt of the capital *M*, like a bird folding its wing. The way the descenders of *g* and *y* curled left instead of right, a habit no forger thinks to counterfeit because no forger notices it.

Whoever wrote this letter had written that codicil. And the codicil predated the death certificate by five years.

She made herself continue.

*My uncle drowned the dog because it bit him. It bit him because he was hurting my mother, and the dog defended her as dogs do, without arithmetic. My mother did not die of fever that winter. I will write what happened to her when I am able. Tonight I can only write this much: I saw Aldous Vane kill the dog, and I saw him kneel afterward in the mud of the boathouse floor, and I heard him say, "Now we see how the story goes."*

*Silas took the blame because I asked him to. That is mine to carry and I have carried it eleven years. He confessed to a thing he did not do because I was drowning — not in the river; the river came later — and he thought if the village had a villain it would stop looking for a witch.*

*They held a funeral for me with stones in the coffin. I helped carry them. That is the strangest sentence I have ever written and I have written it three times tonight and burned it twice.*

*If you are reading this, then my father is dead, and he has done what he always meant to do, which is to make trouble from beyond the reach of anyone's anger. He knew I lived. He has known for years. He wrote to me through the vestry and I never answered, because answering is a door and doors open from both sides.*

*There is more. There is so much more. But more is a country you cannot enter alone, and I need to know what kind of lawyer you are before I show you the map.*

Isandro lowered the pages. Her hands were steady — she noted this with a strange professional detachment, the way one notes a fact in evidence — but beneath the steadiness her pulse was doing something irregular and insistent, like a knock at a door in a house that should be empty.

*What kind of lawyer you are.* Not *whether* to help. Whether she could be trusted with it. The letter presumed her help; it only weighed her character. Someone had watched her. Someone had been in that drawing room eleven days ago, or heard of the reading, or — the thought arrived cold and complete — someone had been watching for years, waiting for Edric Vane to die, waiting for the will to be read, waiting for exactly the person Isandro turned out to be.

She looked at the second envelope. Fresh ink. It seemed almost to hum.

She opened it.

---

Inside was a single sheet, and on it a single paragraph, in the same slanted hand but written quickly, the pen pressed hard enough to tear the paper in one place:

*You kept reading. Good. Most people stop at the first impossible thing and spend the rest of their lives defending themselves against it. You didn't. I watched your face in the drawing room — don't look out the window, I'm not there now — and I saw you do the arithmetic no one else in that room would do. You counted the years. You looked at the codicil nobody else remembered existed. You are either very good or very dangerous, and I have decided, provisionally, to believe you are both.*

*The first letter is eleven years old. I wrote it the winter after they buried my stones, and I have kept it ever since the way one keeps a loaded thing. I am sending it to you now because the trial is coming — you know which trial; everyone in the valley knows — and because Silas Crane has run out of winters.*

*More will come. Not all at once. I have learned that truth delivered whole is a weight that crushes the bearer, and I have no wish to crush you. Read what arrives. Ask nothing yet. When you understand what kind of lawyer you are, write to the parish church and ask the sexton whether the bell rope wants replacing. If the answer comes back yes, we may speak.*

*Burn neither letter. Burn neither page of what follows. You are the archive now. That is the price of knowing, and I am sorry for it, but I did not choose you any more than you chose me. My father chose us both, in his way. He was very good at choosing.*

*M.*

No signature beyond the initial. No date. Isandro turned the sheet over. Blank. She held it to the window light, looking for a watermark, a maker's mark, anything. The paper was good stock, expensive, the kind sold by perhaps two shops in the county.

She became aware, gradually, that she had been sitting motionless for the better part of an hour. The lane below had emptied and refilled; the fishmonger had closed for lunch; somewhere a clock struck twelve with the mild indifference of clocks.

*Don't look out the window, I'm not there now.*

She looked out the window anyway. Of course she did. The lane was ordinary, sunlit, innocent. A cart horse dozed between its shafts. Nothing moved that should not move. And yet the sentence worked on her the way a draft works on a candle — not extinguishing anything, but making every flame in the room lean and gutter. Someone had stood here. Or stood across the lane. Or stood in the drawing room of Vane Manor eleven days ago, among the mourning family, and watched a lawyer count years on her fingers.

Isandro rose and locked the office door, then felt foolish, then did not unlock it. She laid the two letters side by side on the blotter — the old confession and the new summons — and looked at them the way she had been trained to look at contradictory testimony: not asking which was true, but asking what each was *for*.

The confession was a weapon, disassembled and wrapped in oilcloth, offered to her piece by piece. The second letter was the manual. And both of them assumed — this was the part that settled into her chest like a swallowed stone — both of them assumed she would continue. *You kept reading. Good.* As though continuing were the only conceivable response, as though she might as well be expected to stop breathing as to stop reading.

And the terrible thing, the thing she would not admit until much later, sitting in a dark office with the door locked: the terrible thing was that the assumption was correct. She wanted the next page. She wanted it the way one wants the answer to a riddle that has been whispered outside one's window for years without one's knowledge. Dread and curiosity had arrived in the same envelopes, addressed in the same hand, and they were not opposites. They were the same appetite wearing different clothes.

*Silas Crane has run out of winters.*

She knew the case, of course. Everyone in the valley knew it, the way everyone knows a scar. Eleven years ago, a handyman named Silas Crane had confessed to killing Edric Vane's dog — a vicious little business, animal cruelty, months in custody, a fine that had eaten everything he owned. But the confession had never quite fit, the way a borrowed coat never fits, and over the years the story had grown a second life in the taverns of the Fenlow valley: that Crane had taken the blame for someone, that the dog had died for a reason no one spoke aloud, that the Vane family's grief had been performed a shade too carefully. Now, apparently, there was to be a trial — a real one, new charges, old accusations resurfacing — and Isandro understood with sudden clarity that the letters had not arrived *despite* the trial.

They had arrived *because* of it. Timed. Deliberate. Someone was using her, or trusting her, or both, and the difference between those two things was a door she had not yet found, let alone opened.

She sat back down. She took out a clean sheet of her own paper — good stock, she noticed, the same supplier — and wrote at the top of it, in her careful drafting hand: *Questions.* Beneath it she wrote nothing, because every question she formulated led to the same place: *Who is Maren Vane, and where is she?* And every answer the letters permitted was the same: *Ask the sexton about the bell rope.*

The parish church. St. Brannoc's, in the village below the manor, its tower visible for miles across the fen like a finger raised in permanent caution. Isandro had driven past it a dozen times in her life without once considering what went on inside it, what records it kept, what hands rang its bells, what names were written in its registers in ink that could be brown with age or shining and new.

Registers. The word arrived with a small cold shock. Parish registers recorded baptisms, marriages, deaths. A death declared by a vicar's own testimony. If Maren Vane's death was a fiction — and the coffin full of stones, the letter said, *I helped carry them* — then the fiction lived somewhere in ink, in a book, under a lock in a church where a sexton kept the keys.

Isandro looked at the clock. Half past twelve. The stationer's boy would come tomorrow with more post, and she would sort it in a single pass, and perhaps there would be nothing, and perhaps there would be another page ending mid-sentence, another installment of a story she was now, apparently, the archive of.

She gathered the letters, considered the fire in the grate — *burn neither* — and instead unlocked the drawer of her desk where she kept deeds too valuable for the safe downstairs, and laid them flat at the bottom, beneath a lease for a pasture she had closed out two years ago. Then she put on her coat, took her hat from the peg, and stood a moment in the middle of her own office feeling like a woman standing on a riverbank deciding whether the water was a crossing or a drowning.

*When you understand what kind of lawyer you are.*

She understood this much already: she was the kind who did not burn letters. She was the kind who counted years. Whether that made her very good or very dangerous remained, provisionally, to be seen — and someone in the Fenlow valley was waiting, patiently, with pages in reserve, to find out which.

Outside, the afternoon had turned soft and grey, the fen haze creeping up the valley the way it did before rain. St. Brannoc's tower stood above the mist, and Isandro found herself walking toward it before she had quite decided to, her boots finding the lane down through the village as though the decision had been made for her somewhere in the brown ink of an eleven-year-old letter.

The churchyard gate was unlatched. Of course it was.

* * *

She told herself she had come only to look — to stand in the churchyard and confirm that the place existed, that the tower was climbable, that the vestry door was where a vestry door should be. Diligence, she called it. Reconnaissance. The vocabulary of a profession that prefers its curiosity dressed in Latin.

St. Brannoc's yard was older than the village around it, the stones leaning like tired congregants, the grass grown lush and indifferent over generations of the Fenlow's dead. Isandro walked the paths slowly, reading names without meaning to — Ellery, Tregowan, Pascoe — and then stopped, because ahead of her, at the edge of the newer ground, stood a stone she had not expected to see.

*MAREN JANE VANE. 1861–1879. TAKEN BY THE RIVER, RESTING IN GOD.*

Eleven years dead. Twenty-three years old forever. The carving was fresh enough to have been cut within the decade, the letters still sharp, the lichen not yet claimed. Someone had paid for this stone. Someone had chosen these words. *Taken by the river* — passive, gentle, a sentence constructed to end inquiry rather than invite it.

Isandro stood before the stone for a long time. Somewhere beyond the wall, water moved in the ditches, patient as paperwork. And it occurred to her, with the clarity that comes when dread finally stops pretending to be something else, that she was standing at the grave of a living woman — that somewhere in this valley, behind one of these cottage windows or beyond one of these hedges, the person whose handwriting she now knew better than her own was going about the ordinary business of a hidden life, buying bread, tending graves, ringing bells, waiting to learn what kind of lawyer Isandro Reyes turned out to be.

The vestry door stood at the side of the church, black oak, iron-hinged. As Isandro turned toward it — meaning only to note it, only to fix its position in her mind the way she fixed all facts — she saw that a light burned behind its small window. Evening had not yet fallen, but the light was lit regardless, steady and yellow, as though someone inside were working late over books.

Or registers.

Isandro stood in the grass with the dead all around her and the living light in the vestry window, and understood that she had already gone too far to turn back — that the letters had done precisely what they were designed to do, which was to set a lawyer walking, one careful step at a time, toward a door she would not be able to close again.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow she would come back in daylight, properly, with a reason a lawyer could say aloud.

But her feet, traitorous things, had already carried her to within ten feet of the vestry door — close enough to hear, faintly through the oak, the sound of a pen moving across paper. Scratch, pause. Scratch, pause. The rhythm of someone writing something that mattered.

Isandro Reyes stood in a churchyard at dusk, listening to a stranger write, and knew — with the dread that had stopped disguising itself, with the curiosity that had stopped apologizing for itself — that whatever was being written in that room, sooner or later, it would be addressed to her.
