## 7. Counsel for the Dead

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Isandro would later think was fitting — Tuesdays were the days the courts did their dullest damage, and Harrow Blake was a man who had made an art of dullness.

It came by courier, not post: heavy cream stock, a wax seal she hadn't seen used outside of costume dramas since law school, and a hand so even it might have been set in type. *Mr. Blake requests the pleasure of Miss Reyes's company at four o'clock, Thursday, at the offices of Blake & Fenwick, Grayling Row.* The word *pleasure* sat there like a cat sitting on a birdcage.

She could have declined. She considered it, actually, standing at her office window with the letter held up to the light as though the paper itself might confess something. But declining a courtesy call from opposing counsel was a move that cost more than it bought, and besides — she wanted to see him. She had spent two weeks feeling the shape of someone watching from behind the hedgerows of this case: the way Cordelia Vane's letters had grown shorter and cooler; the way Tom Ellery had stopped meeting her eye at the churchyard gate; the way the second letter's promise — *more, if you keep reading* — had begun to feel less like an invitation and more like bait left out where something larger could feed.

Harrow Blake, she suspected, was the something larger. Or at least its mouthpiece.

Grayling Row was the oldest street in Fenlow town, cobbled and narrow, its buildings leaning toward one another like conspirators. Blake & Fenwick occupied the whole of a Georgian house whose windows had been reglazed recently enough to catch the afternoon sun in flat, modern panes — new money wearing old clothes, or perhaps the reverse. A clerk took her coat. Another offered tea. She declined the tea and noted, with professional interest, that neither of them asked her name. They had known exactly who she was before she crossed the threshold.

Blake received her in a first-floor study lined with law reports bound in green buckram, arranged not by date but by color, fading from dark to pale like a tide going out. He rose from behind a desk that was almost aggressively bare — no papers, no pens, nothing that could be read over his shoulder or photographed — and extended a hand that was dry, cool, and precisely calibrated to be forgotten.

"Miss Reyes. Thank you for coming."

"Mr. Blake." His name was already familiar to her; she'd done her homework after the courier arrived. Harrow Blake, forty-something, called to the bar in London before drifting back to the provinces with a reputation for never losing a case he'd chosen to take, and for choosing very few. "You have me at a disadvantage. I wasn't aware we were opposed."

"Aren't we?" He gestured her into a chair opposite his own, both positioned with the window behind him and the light full in her face. She recognized the staging. She'd sat clients through depositions in rooms arranged just so. She moved her chair six inches to the left as she sat, casually, breaking the geometry, and saw the flicker in his eyes that told him she'd noticed and that he'd noticed her noticing.

"My client," he said, settling back, "is Miss Cordelia Vane."

"I know who your client is. I haven't yet been told what she imagines I've done."

"Nothing." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, empty at the center — the smile of a man who had learned that people trusted faces more than words and had therefore made his face into a document. "That's rather the point. You've done nothing wrong. You've been retained to execute a will, and you are executing it. Diligently. Perhaps more diligently than anyone anticipated." He let that sit for a moment. "My client simply wondered whether all that diligence was necessary."

"And she hired you to ask?"

"She hired me," Blake said mildly, "to make sure the question got asked properly. There are ways of asking things that create records, Miss Reyes, and records have a way of growing teeth. I prefer conversations that leave nothing behind." He spread his hands, palms up, a gesture of beautiful harmlessness. "So. Nothing behind. Just two professionals talking about an estate."

Isandro folded her hands in her lap and waited. Silence was the only tool she owned that couldn't be confiscated, and she had learned long ago that most men filled silence the way water fills a crack — inevitably, and to their own detriment.

Blake didn't fill it. That was the first genuinely unsettling thing about him. He let the quiet stretch five seconds, ten, fifteen, watching her with the patient interest of a man timing something, and when he finally spoke again his voice hadn't changed register at all.

"You've received correspondence," he said. "Two letters. One unsigned."

The room did not tilt, but something in it rearranged itself — the bookshelves, the light, the man — so that Isandro felt briefly like the subject of an examination rather than a participant in one. She kept her face still. She had practiced that stillness in front of juries; she deployed it now.

"I receive a great deal of correspondence."

"One confessing to a crime committed eleven years ago this autumn. One promising—" he paused, and here, for the first time, something moved behind the polish, something quick and cold, like a fish turning under ice— "*more*. If the recipient keeps reading." He tilted his head. "Have I summarized accurately?"

"You've described documents I may or may not have seen with suspicious precision."

"I've described them exactly," Blake said, "because I've read them."

The admission landed without flourish, which was what made it land hard. Isandro felt her pulse tick up once against her throat and forced it down. Her copies were locked in her office, in a drawer, in a building she'd believed secure. Either Blake was lying — possible, but he didn't strike her as a man who lied when the truth served better — or someone had access to her office that she hadn't granted.

"That's a serious claim," she said. "Intercepting privileged correspondence."

"It isn't privileged. Privilege attaches between solicitor and client. Whoever wrote those letters isn't your client — is she? Unless there's something you'd like to tell me." The smile again, thinner. "And they weren't intercepted. They were *copied*. There's a difference, legally speaking, and I'm sure you appreciate distinctions." He rose, crossed to the window with his hands clasped behind his back, and looked down at the cobbled street where a woman was struggling with market bags and a dog was worrying at something dead in the gutter. "You want to know how I came by them. Everyone always wants to know that first. It's the wrong question."

"What's the right one?"

"The right question is: what do the letters change? And the answer, Miss Reyes, is *nothing*. Consider it with me — indulge an old litigator. A will names a beneficiary declared dead eleven years ago. Fine. Dead beneficiaries take nothing; the estate passes per the residuary clause. An anonymous confession arrives alleging a crime. Anonymous confessions are worth precisely the paper they're printed on, and less, because paper can at least be fingerprinted — although these can't, we checked." He turned back to face her. "A man named Silas Crane has already confessed to that crime, publicly, under caution, in a statement he hasn't retracted. The Crown is satisfied. The court calendar is set. In six weeks a jury will do its duty, and the Vane estate will pass cleanly to my client, and the parish will go back to being a parish." He said it all gently, the way one explains weather to a child. "Whatever you're holding, whatever you think you're holding, it changes none of that. It can only complicate your position. Executor's fees, Miss Reyes, are not worth what exposure costs."

"There it is," Isandro said quietly.

"There what is?"

"The warning. You wrapped it in enough courtesy to get it through customs, but it's a warning."

Blake returned to his chair but did not sit. He stood behind it, resting his fingertips on the bare desk, and studied her with what looked almost like respect. "I don't give warnings. Warnings imply future consequences, and future consequences imply threats, and threats are actionable. I gave you a *professional assessment*. You're free to disregard it. Most people who disregard my assessments come to regret it, but that's their affair." He straightened. "My client asks only one thing, and I'll put it plainly so there's no ambiguity later. Conclude the administration of the estate. File your report. Take your fee. Let the past stay where eleven years have buried it — and believe me, Miss Reyes, this valley buries things thoroughly. We've established that."

"We have?"

"The grave was opened last month," Blake said. "Tom Ellery. Under your instruction. Stones and a boot." He watched her absorb it. "You see my difficulty. Every step you take, I learn of it within a day. Not because I have informants — I dislike the word — but because this valley watches itself. Everyone here has something buried, and everyone here knows everyone else's shovel-work. You are the only stranger in the machine, Miss Reyes, and strangers rattle."

*Being watched, not warned.* The phrase assembled itself in her mind with the click of a lock turning. Blake wasn't threatening her. He was demonstrating capability. Every detail he'd shown her — the letters, the exhumation, the timing — was a receipt. Proof of purchase. *This is what we know. Imagine what we haven't shown.*

She stood, unhurried, and gathered her coat over her arm. "Thank you for the assessment, Mr. Blake. I'll weigh it carefully."

"Do." He walked her to the door himself, an honor she suspected he extended to no one, and paused with his hand on the handle. "One more thing, purely as a courtesy. The handwriting on that confession — the unsigned one. You've been comparing it, I imagine, against samples. Parish registers, funeral books, anything Maren Vane touched before she died." His voice stayed perfectly level. "You'll find the match is excellent. Almost too excellent. Handwriting doesn't survive eleven years underwater unchanged, Miss Reyes. If the hand matches, ask yourself who had eleven years to practice it."

He opened the door. The clerk appeared as if summoned, holding her coat, and the interview was over with the smoothness of a stage change.

* * *

She didn't go straight back to her office. She walked, instead, the long way along the river path, letting the fen wind strip the study's warmth out of her coat and off her skin. The water lay low and brown-green under a sky the color of dishwater, and somewhere out in the reeds a bittern boomed its one dry note, like a stick dragged along a fence.

*Who had eleven years to practice it.*

It was a poison pill, and she knew it, and she swallowed it anyway because that was what pills were for. If the confession was forged — if the hand that wrote it had learned Maren Vane's letters the way an actor learns a role — then either the writer was Maren herself, changed and careful, or someone else entirely was using Maren's voice as a mask. And if someone else was using it, then the second letter — *more, if you keep reading* — was not a witness reaching out. It was a fisherman checking his line.

Or Blake wanted her to believe that. That was the other possibility, the one that coiled in her stomach all the way home: that the warning was designed to make her doubt the one document that could undo his client, to shake her confidence precisely where it needed shaking. Surgical courtesy, cutting in a place no one could see.

At her office door she stopped, key in the lock, and looked at the frame. The paint was unbroken. The dust on the sill lay in its usual gray velvet. Everything was exactly as she'd left it, which was, she understood suddenly, exactly the problem. A searched office should show *something* — a drawer not quite shut, papers a half-inch out of true. Hers showed nothing, and Harrow Blake had described her letters down to the promise of *more*.

She went inside, unlocked the drawer, and counted. Both letters, present. The confession, present. She read the last line of the second letter again, the one that had kept her up three nights running:

*There is a page that names him. Keep reading and it will find you.*

Below it, in pencil, faint as breath on glass, someone had added a single word that had not been there when she'd filed it away:

*Soon.*

Isandro stood very still in the failing light, listening to the building settle around her, and understood that the conversation at Grayling Row had not been the message. This was the message. She was not being warned off the case.

She was being kept on it — like a wick, like a fuse — and somewhere in the valley, someone was waiting to see how far she'd burn.
