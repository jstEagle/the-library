## 12. More

The first installment came on a Tuesday, in an envelope the color of weak tea.

Isandro knew before she opened it. She had learned the handwriting by then — learned its particular stubbornness, the way the ascenders leaned east as if walking into wind, the way certain letters were pressed so hard they embossed the paper's reverse like braille. Maren Vane wrote the way people dig: with intent, with weight, with a sense that what was buried ought to stay reachable.

She had been at her desk since seven, drafting responses to Harrow Blake's second letter — he had sent one, of course; Blake was a man who considered silence a debt unpaid — when her clerk, Piotr, brought up the morning post and set it down with the careful neutrality of someone who had noticed her hands go still over certain envelopes before.

"This one's heavier," he said.

"I can feel it from here."

"Also no return address. Also—" he squinted at the postmark, "—mailed from Fenlow itself. Village box, if I'm reading the code right."

Fenlow. The village that did not speak. Isandro took the envelope and turned it over twice, as though the answer might be printed on the flap. It was not. It was only paper and glue and eleven years of something folded very small inside.

"Thank you, Piotr."

He lingered a half-second longer than professional courtesy required, then left. He was a good clerk. He knew when a room needed emptying.

She opened it with the letter knife Edric Vane's estate had, absurdly, paid for — silver-handled, monogrammed *E.V.*, one of forty items she was legally obligated to inventory and could not yet bring herself to catalog. Inside were nine pages, written on the backs of parish bulletins, the kind handed out on Sundays and discarded by Monday. The paper smelled faintly of candle wax and damp stone.

At the top of the first page, in that digging hand:

*You said you wanted to know. Here is knowing. I will send it as I can bear to write it. Do not come looking between installments. You will find nothing but my garden and my shovel, and you will embarrass us both.*

Isandro sat back. The radiator under the window ticked like a patient clock. Outside, October was doing its work on the plane trees along Chancery Row, and somewhere below a bus sighed at its stop, and all of it — the whole ordinary machinery of the city — seemed suddenly very far from the fen country where this letter had been written by a woman who was legally dead and practically invisible and, apparently, a correspondent now.

*Here is knowing.*

She read.

---

The account began, as these things always do, with weather.

*It rained the night my uncle killed my father. I want to say that plainly, because everything else I will tell you will be crooked, and I want one straight line to hold onto. It rained. The fen rose. The ditches ran brown and fast, and the boat Aldous kept moored behind the boathouse strained at its line like something that already knew what it would be used for.*

*I was fourteen. I had gone down to the study to ask about school fees — imagine that, imagine being fourteen and worried about fees — and the door was open because Aldous is the sort of man who closes doors only after, never before. I saw the poker. I saw my father on the floor with his ear bleeding and his eyes open, and I saw Aldous standing over him breathing like a man who has just carried something heavy upstairs, and I made a sound. I have thought about that sound every day for eleven years. If I had been silent, I would be dead too, or worse, I would be Cordelia — no. That is unkind. Cordelia was asleep. Cordelia knew nothing until she knew everything, which is its own kind of wound. But I made a sound, and Aldous turned, and I ran.*

*You know the rest the way the village tells it: the girl drowned in the flood, swept off the footpath, poor thing, God rest her. What the village does not tell you is who walked the banks the next three days calling my name in a voice that shook, and it was not Aldous. It was Silas Crane. Silas, who the police later decided had done the killing. Silas, who stood in the rain shouting for a girl he knew was alive because he was the one who pulled me out of the ditch at two in the morning, half-frozen, my boot gone, my lungs full of fen.*

*He wrapped me in his coat. He carried me to the church because it was the only building with a fire lit, and the sexton — the old sexton, Ruth's husband, before Ruth died and before he followed her — the sexton looked at me and looked at Silas and asked exactly one question: "Who is looking for her?" And Silas said, "Her uncle." And the sexton nodded, the way men nod at arithmetic they've done before, and went and got his dead wife's clothes.*

Isandro set the pages down.

Her office was quiet in the way offices are quiet at midday when the phone has stopped pretending anyone wants to talk. She got up, poured coffee she didn't want, drank it anyway, and returned to the desk. This was the discipline: read it as evidence, not as story. Read it as a lawyer. Mark the verifiable claims. Note the corroborable details. Do not — she warned herself, and heard in the warning the exact tone of her old professor of evidence, a woman who had prosecuted three wrongful convictions and trusted no narrative, including her own — do not fall in love with the voice.

But the voice. That was the trouble. The voice on the page was steady and unsparing and it spared itself least of all, and Isandro had spent enough years reading confessions — real ones, coerced ones, fabricated ones — to know how rare that was. Guilty narrators flatter themselves. Coached narrators flatten. This voice did neither. It simply dug.

*You will want to know why I did not go to the police that night, or any night after. I will get there. Not yet. A letter can only carry so much weight before it tears, and I am writing on the backs of church bulletins because paper in Fenlow is watched — you'd be surprised what is watched in Fenlow — and because the bulletins are mine by right. I print them. I fold them. Eleven years of Sundays, and every week I set the type for the psalms and think: this is the closest I come to speaking.*

*One more thing before I stop for today. The confession you received — the first letter, the one in my hand — read it again. Read the parts you did not write and I did not write either. There is a third hand in that document, and when you find it, you will understand why I am sending you this account in pieces instead of walking into your office and saying it aloud.*

*Some sentences, once spoken in a room, cannot be taken back. On paper they can at least be burned. I have burned four drafts already. Ask me how I know the smell of burning ink.*

*— M.*

---

The second installment arrived four days later, thinner, three pages, ending — deliberately, unmistakably — in the middle of a sentence.

Isandro noticed it the way she'd been trained to notice things: first with irritation, then with a cold, climbing recognition. The final page broke off at *"Aldous came to the boathouse that night carrying a lantern and a length of rope, and he said to Silas, 'You'll want to be elsewhere tomorrow,' and Silas said—"*

And stopped.

No period. No dash. Just white space, and beneath it, in smaller script, almost apologetic:

*More next week. Forgive me. Writing this is like opening a vein on a schedule.*

She laughed — one short, involuntary note in the empty office — and then pressed her knuckles to her mouth, because the laugh had come out wrong, had come out sounding like fear dressed in amusement, and there was no one there to hear it and be fooled.

She understood what was happening. Of course she understood. She was a probate lawyer, not a fool, and she had spent six weeks inside the gravitational field of the Vane estate long enough to recognize an orbit when she was captured by one. The installments were not correspondence. They were a leash. Each one ended mid-sentence, and each mid-sentence was a hook set gently in her jaw, and each envelope that arrived would pull a little tighter, and the woman at the other end of the line knew precisely what she was doing because — Isandro remembered the calluses, the pen finger, the way the sexton had held the church register like a brief — because Maren Vane had once been trained to build arguments out of withheld conclusions.

Law school. Or something like it. Before the fen, before the erasure. There was a life before the life, and Isandro was reading its wreckage one bulletin-back at a time.

"You're making me complicit," she said aloud, to the envelope, to the fen, to the woman folding bulletins by candlelight. "You know that."

The radiator ticked. The city went on not answering.

Because here was the thing she could not put in a file memo: she had not reported the letters. She should have. A confession to manslaughter — the first letter had confessed, in Maren's hand, to helping conceal a death — was evidence. It belonged with the prosecution, with the defense, with the court, with someone whose job was custody of dangerous paper. Instead it lived in the bottom drawer of her desk, beneath a false bottom Piotr had built her as a joke years ago, in a folder labeled *VANE — MISCELLANEOUS*, and every day she chose, actively, consciously, with full knowledge of the ethical weight of the choice, not to surrender it.

She told herself it was strategy. She told herself that premature disclosure would expose Maren to Aldous before Isandro could establish protections, that the letters were safer as leverage than as exhibits, that a good lawyer keeps powder dry. All true. All beside the point. The truth underneath the truths was simpler and less flattering: she wanted to know how the sentence ended. She wanted the next envelope. She had become, in the precise and terrible meaning of the word, a reader.

And readers, Maren Vane understood better than anyone, can be led.

The third installment came during a rainstorm that turned Chancery Row into a river of umbrellas, and this one was different. Thicker. Seven pages, plus something else — a photograph, old, creased into quarters so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

It showed the boathouse. Behind it, half in frame, a young man with dark hair and a laborer's shoulders stood holding a lantern, and though the face was blurred by cheap film and bad light, Isandro knew him. She had sat across from him in a visiting room three weeks ago and watched him fold his enormous hands and ask her, with the last of his dignity, to tell a dead girl that his dog had died happy.

Silas Crane. Younger by eleven years. Standing outside the boathouse the night after the killing, and — Isandro held the photo closer — there, at the waterline, just visible: a second figure, small, wrapped in a coat too big for her, half-turned toward the camera as if caught between flight and farewell.

Maren. Alive, in a photograph that should not exist, dated — she checked the back — in faded pencil, *the last night*.

The pages themselves continued the interrupted sentence as though no time had passed, which was its own small cruelty, its own demonstration of control:

*"—and Silas said, 'I'll be nowhere, Mr. Vane. I'll be everywhere but here.' And Aldous laughed, and gave him money, and told him the girl's body would wash up eventually and it would be kinder to everyone if Silas had been seen drinking in Tullamore that night. Kinder. That was the word. Aldous Vane has one word for every crime he has ever committed and the word is kinder."*

Then the account moved forward through the days Isandro had only glimpsed in fragments: the forged register entry, the coffin filled with stones from the ditch itself — *he made Silas gather them, made him kneel in the mud choosing stones the right size, and I think that was the moment Silas stopped being afraid of Aldous and started being afraid of himself* — the funeral, the village's closed ranks, the slow machinery of suspicion finding its shape around the handyman because suspicion, like water, flows downhill toward whoever is lowest.

And then, on the sixth page, the account arrived at the place Isandro had been circling for weeks without letting herself land:

*Why I stayed silent. Why I let Silas take the weight. You will hate me, and you should, and I will give you the arithmetic anyway because you are a lawyer and lawyers deserve honesty even when no one else does.*

*If I had testified, I would have had to say where I was that night and who saved me. My testimony would have placed Silas at the ditch at two in the morning — with me, soaked, half-dead, in his coat. The prosecution would have woven it: he drowned the father, he fished the daughter out, he hid her, he silenced her. My rescue was his rope. Every fact that proved I lived proved he killed. Aldous built it that way. He built it the way he builds everything — patiently, with other people's hands.*

*So I chose. I chose a living man in a cell over a dead girl in a grave, and I chose wrong, and I have chosen wrong every Sunday for eleven years when I set the type for the psalms, and I am still choosing it, and the only reason I am writing to you now is that Silas is dying in that cell — not of the law, of his heart, the doctor says — and a man should not die for love of a girl who let him.*

*Which brings me to the part I have not been able to write. Four drafts burned. This is the fifth.*

*Aldous did not act alone.*

*There was money before the murder — Father was going to change the will, you see, he had found out about the debts, he had found out what Aldous and—*

The page ended there. Mid-word, this time. Not even mid-sentence — mid-*name*, the em-dash hanging like a blade that had stopped falling.

Below it, in the smaller script:

*Next week. I promise. And Isandro — check the land records. Check what the estate has been selling, and to whom, and when the selling started. The answer to the name I could not write is sitting in the county office in a ledger nobody reads.*

*You are in this now. I am sorry. I chose you the way Aldous chooses tools — carefully, and for what you can cut.*

*— M.*

---

Isandro sat for a long time with the photograph in one hand and the unfinished page in the other, and the rain worked at the windows, and somewhere below in the street a vendor called out something cheerful about chestnuts, and none of it touched her.

*Check the land records.*

She reached for her coat. Then stopped. Then took the photograph, slid it into an inner pocket, took the pages, locked them in the drawer, and stood in the middle of her office feeling the exact shape of the trap she was in — which was not a trap at all, really, but a partnership, offered without consent, sealed without signature. Maren Vane was feeding her a case one installment at a time, timing each delivery, withholding each conclusion, and Isandro Reyes — officer of the court, keeper of wills, reader of dead men's testaments — was following the breadcrumbs into a story that was rapidly becoming hers.

*You are in this now.*

"Yes," she said to the empty room. "I am."

She locked the office, took the stairs rather than wait for the lift, and walked out into the rain with her collar up and her mind already composing the records request: *all conveyances, leases, and transfers of title executed on behalf of the Estate of Edric Vane within the past five years, together with consideration paid and identity of grantee.* Routine language. Innocuous. The kind of request filed ten thousand times a day in county offices across the country, unread, unremarked.

Except that in Fenlow, everything was watched. Maren had said so. Which meant that somewhere between the county clerk's stamp and the manor's drawing room, someone might notice a city lawyer asking questions about land that Cordelia Vane had been quietly turning into cash.

Let them notice, Isandro thought, hailing a cab. Let them wonder. Let Aldous Vane lie awake doing the same arithmetic she was doing — weighing what a dead woman's letters could prove against what a live lawyer could be bought or frightened out of proving.

The cab splashed away toward the county records office, and in Isandro's coat pocket, creased soft as cloth, Silas Crane's lantern burned on in its small square of silver, lighting a girl's face at the edge of the frame — half-turned, half-gone, waiting to see whether anyone would follow.

Someone had.
