## 16. Midnight in the Bell Tower

### Isandro

Evensong ended at seven, and the parish emptied the way water leaves a lock — slowly, then all at once. Isandro did not leave with it. She sat in the third pew from the back with her hat in her lap and let the last of the candle-smoke settle into the rafters, watching the sexton work.

The woman moved down the aisle collecting hymnals with a methodical grace that had never once belonged to grief or habit. She stacked them spine-out, aligned to the millimetre. When she knelt to retrieve one that had slipped beneath a pew, she braced herself on her left hand and kept her right clear of the floor — the instinct of someone who had learned, somewhere, that hands were instruments to be protected.

Lawyer's hands. Isandro had spent twenty years reading hands the way other people read faces. The callus on the middle finger of the sexton's right pen-hand was not a spade's callus. A spade raised a ridge across the palm and thickened the skin at the base of every finger evenly. This was a single polished groove, worn by years of a nib dragged across paper.

Maren Vane wrote. Maren Vane had *always* written. And for eleven years, apparently, she had written under a dead wife's name while burying the parish's dead.

The sexton finished her rounds, doused the candles but two, and paused — Isandro felt it rather than saw it, a stillness in the woman's shoulders like a deer catching wind — before continuing up the side aisle toward the vestry door. She did not look back. But when she pulled the great oak door shut behind her, it did not latch. It swung open again by an inch, maybe two, and stayed there, breathing cold air into the nave like an invitation written in draught.

Isandro waited until the churchyard clock struck nine. Then she rose, settled her coat, and followed.

The night was the kind the Fenlow valley specialised in: fog lying over the graves like a second congregation, the yew trees black against a sky the colour of wet slate. Isandro crossed the churchyard on the flagstone path, past the Vane plot — she could not help herself; she looked; the disturbed earth of the opened grave had been neatly re-turfed, which told her Tom Ellery had been paid again, or threatened again, or both — and came around the tower's north face where a narrow door stood ajar.

Inside, the stair wound upward through air that smelled of stone, pigeon lime, and rope-hemp. Isandro climbed without hurrying. There was no need to hurry. Whatever waited above had been waiting eleven years; it could wait another ninety seconds.

The bell chamber opened around her like the ribcage of some enormous patient animal. Moonlight came through the louvred openings in pale bars. The great bell hung motionless above, its lip furred with dust, and beneath it, standing at the parapet with her back half-turned, was the sexton — no hat now, her hair coming loose from its knot, grey threading through brown the way frost threads through a hedgerow.

"You took your time," the woman said, without turning. Her voice was low and even, a voice built for small rooms and long silences. "I've watched you circling this parish for three weeks like a heron after eels. I wondered if you'd ever simply strike."

"Mrs.—" Isandro began, and stopped, because she did not know what name to use, and the pause said everything.

"Maren," the woman said. "Here. Only here." She turned at last, and Isandro saw her face properly for the first time: a face that had been handsome once and had since been sanded down by weather and watchfulness into something harder and more interesting. Her eyes were the colour of the fen in November. "You have my letters."

"I have your letters."

"Then you know why we're standing in a bell tower at midnight instead of a solicitor's office at noon."

"I know why *you'd* prefer it," Isandro said. "I'm less certain why I've agreed."

Something moved at the corner of Maren's mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Because you read the confession twice. Because you found the second handwriting. And because you're the first person in eleven years who looked at a forged death entry and asked *who profited* instead of *isn't it sad*." She gestured to the parapet wall, a broad stone ledge worn smooth. "Sit if you like. It's a long story, and I find I can only tell it looking down at the graves."

Isandro did not sit. She stood with her back to the stair, coat buttoned, hands in her pockets, and waited.

Maren looked out through the louvres at the fog-bound churchyard. "You want to know why I didn't come forward. Why I let Silas Crane take the blame. Why I buried myself and let everyone grieve conveniently." She folded her arms. "Ask me properly, Miss Reyes. You're a lawyer. Ask me the question you'd ask in court."

Isandro considered her. "Where were you on the night of the ninth of March, eleven years ago?"

"In the river," Maren said. "Being drowned."

* * *

### Maren

She had rehearsed this testimony for eleven years — in the dark of the vestry loft, in the pauses between digging graves, walking the sentences over and over like a woman pacing a cell — and now that the moment had come, all the rehearsal fell away and what remained was the night itself, whole and undiluted.

"The ninth of March," she said. "Aldous had summoned me to the estate office after dinner. Edric was away — he went monthly to his solicitor in Ashcombe, always overnight. Aldous said there were papers to sign. My father's settlement. I was twenty-four and I trusted my uncle because I had never been given a reason not to." Her hands had gone still on the parapet. "There were papers. Deeds of assignment. He wanted me to sign over my interest in the Fenlow acreage — he said Father had promised it to him, and that Edric would honour the promise once I signed. I said I would want our solicitor to look at them first. That was all. That was the entire crime. I said *let me read it first*."

The fog below had begun to move, rolling slow off the fen like something breathing.

"He didn't shout," Maren said. "That's what no one understands about Aldous. He has never needed to shout. He simply became very reasonable, very quiet, and began explaining to me how inconvenient I was. How Edric's health was failing and how much simpler everything would be if I were not in the valley. How my father had died owing money that would fall to me. And then he picked up the poker — not to threaten. To *tidy*. He set it back in the stand. And he said, 'Think carefully, Maren,' and I understood that thinking carefully was the only thing standing between me and being tidied too."

"And you ran," Isandro said. Not unkindly. Precisely.

"I ran. Out the garden door, down through the paddock, into the fen path. I knew the paths — I'd walked them since I was a child. But it had rained for a week and the ground was soup, and I heard him behind me — or thought I did; I've never known whether he actually followed or whether fear invented his footsteps — and I cut off the path to lose him and went straight into the drain-cut." She heard her own voice flatten as she said it, the way voices do around the worst facts. "March water. I went under twice. The current took me toward the sluice. I remember grabbing willow root and it tearing, and thinking — quite calmly, this is the strange part — *so this is how the story ends, and no one will even be surprised, because girls who argue with their uncles are always falling in rivers.*"

Isandro had gone very still. "Someone pulled you out."

"The sexton." Maren's throat tightened around the word. "Josiah Crane. Silas's father. He was setting eel traps below the weir — he heard me, or God heard me through him, I've stopped needing to decide which. He hauled me out, wrapped me in his coat, and carried me to the vestry. His wife had died the previous autumn. Ruth. He sat me by the stove in Ruth's clothes and looked at me for a long time, and then he said, 'The river gives back what it takes, sometimes, changed.' And I knew what he was offering."

"A death," Isandro said quietly.

"A mercy. The vicar owed Josiah more than he could repay — you'll have found that by now, or you will. Josiah asked him to enter a drowning in the register. Maren Vane, lost in the Fenlow waters, body unrecovered. Empty coffin, river stones, one boot the river kindly provided. Aldous paid for the funeral and the stone and never once looked inside the box, because men like Aldous pay for outcomes, not details." She turned to face Isandro fully. "And Silas — Josiah's son, the handyman, the man they have in custody now — Silas helped carry the coffin. Silas knew. He was the only one besides Josiah and the vicar and me. And when Josiah died four years later, Silas kept the secret the way you keep a coal alive under ash. All those years. Until they arrested him for Edric's murder and he confessed to *that* rather than say my name."

"Why?" Isandro asked. "Why would he—"

"Because if I testify about the ninth of March, I testify about where I went and who hid me, and the defence tears the alibi apart — hers, mine, the truth is a tangle — and Aldous walks, and Silas hangs anyway, only now knowing he's damned me too." Maren's voice cracked, just once, and she hated it. "He confessed to protect a dead woman. That is the arithmetic I have lived inside for eleven years. My life purchased with his rope."

* * *

### Isandro

The bell hung above them like a held breath. Isandro found she had taken her hands out of her pockets without noticing, and that they were cold.

"Then tell me the rest," she said. "Edric. Who killed Edric Vane?"

Maren laughed — a short, terrible sound with no humour in it, the laugh of a woman hearing a question she had answered a thousand times in a room with no one in it. "You think I know. Everyone thinks the witness knows. But I was in the vestry loft, Miss Reyes, when Edric died. I heard the argument through the floor — Aldous and Edric, shouting about the Fenlow deeds, about *my* signature, about a document Edric had hidden. I heard Edric say 'she's alive, Aldous, and she'll sign nothing' — and then I heard the sound. One sound. And then silence, and then Aldous moving through the house very calmly, and then nothing." Her eyes were bright and dry in the moonlight. "I don't *know* he killed his brother. I know what I heard. And what I heard is worth nothing unless I am alive in a courtroom to say it — and the moment I am alive in a courtroom, Silas's false confession collapses, and Cordelia's land sales collapse, and every arrangement this family has made for eleven years collapses, and men like Aldous do not permit collapse. They tidy."

"So you sent me letters instead," Isandro said. "Confessions in installments. Making me your instrument. Do you know how that looks from where I stand? Like cowardice wearing a lawyer as a coat."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "It looks exactly like that. I've had eleven years to become acquainted with my own cowardice. We're on Christian terms now." She stepped closer, and the moonlight caught the lines of her face, and Isandro saw that underneath the hardness there was exhaustion so old it had become architecture. "But understand what I am asking, because it isn't forgiveness. I'm not asking you to absolve me. I will carry Silas's sacrifice until I die and it will not lighten by one ounce. What I am asking is whether the truth is worth what it costs — *all* of it. Your career. Tom Ellery's peace. The vicar's grave shamed. Cordelia ruined. Silas free but broken. Me, exhumed in every sense. Is the truth worth the bill, Isandro Reyes? You've read the confession twice. You've opened the grave. Tell me honestly: when you imagine the courtroom, do you see justice? Or do you see a machine that eats the innocent and calls it procedure?"

Isandro was silent for a long moment. Below them the fog shifted, and for an instant the churchyard lay bare — the Vane plot, the fresh-turned earth, the yews — and then closed over again.

"I see both," she said finally. "That's the honest answer. The law is a blunt instrument wielded by frightened people, and it fails constantly, and it is still the only thing standing between us and the alternative. And the alternative," she nodded downward, toward the fog, toward the fen, "is what Aldous Vane has been practising for eleven years. I won't choose it. I won't choose his version of tidiness just because the lawful version is ugly and slow."

"No," Maren agreed softly. "Neither will I. Not anymore." She reached into her bodice and drew out a fold of papers, worn soft at the creases, tied with black thread. "The full account. Every name, every date, the document Edric hid — he'd shown it to me once, years before; I remembered enough to describe it. Sworn before God, though not before any court, since God was the only officer available." She held it out. "If I give you this, there's no going back for any of us. So I'm asking you — the lawyer, the stranger, the only person in this valley who hasn't already decided what I am — is it worth it? Say it plainly. I need to hear it said aloud by someone whose word is worth something."

Isandro took the papers. They were lighter than they should have been; eleven years weighed nothing in the hand.

"It's worth it," she said. "Not because the outcome will be clean. It won't. But because Silas Crane dies on Friday week otherwise, and because you have been dead long enough, and because a lie that costs this much to maintain is more expensive than any truth." She met Maren's eyes. "I'll file the declaration. I'll invoke the supplementary testament clause. And I will put you on that stand under your own name if it's the last thing I do in this valley."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment she looked young — twenty-four, soaked, running — and then the moment passed and she was the sexton again, and she nodded once, like a woman accepting a sentence.

"Then you should know," she said, opening her eyes, and her voice had changed — dropped into something flat and urgent, "that we are not alone in this decision anymore. Someone searched your office Tuesday. Someone has been asking Tom Ellery who paid him to reopen the grave. And yesterday morning I found this nailed to the vestry door."

She reached beneath the parapet ledge and brought out a small square of card, and handed it to Isandro. On it, in a hand Isandro recognised with a lurch — the same reasonable, unhurried hand that had signed the forgery receipt in the dead vicar's papers — were five words:

*The tower has poor locks.*

Below them, out in the fog-wrapped churchyard, a gravel crunched once beneath a deliberate footstep, and then went quiet, and neither woman moved, and the bell hung above them, waiting to be rung.
