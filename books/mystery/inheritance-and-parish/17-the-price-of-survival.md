## 17. The Price of Survival

The bell tower smelled of pigeon droppings and cold stone, and Maren Vane had spent eleven years learning to love it.

She climbed the last of the steps with the lamp held high, and Isandro Reyes followed, her lawyer's shoes whispering against stairs worn hollow by three centuries of sextons' feet. Below them the churchyard lay silvered under a rising moon, the Vane plot a pale rectangle among the humbler stones, and beyond it the fen stretched black and breathing toward the valley's edge. Somewhere out there a bittern boomed, that ancient pumping cry the old folk said was the sound of a drowned soul asking for its body back.

Maren set the lamp on the sill of the louvred window and turned to face the woman who had cornered her here an hour past midnight.

"You want the rest of it," she said. "The why. Not just what happened in the tower at Vane Manor — you've had that from my own mouth, and God help me I don't know why I gave it to you except that you stood there looking at me like a person and not a problem to be solved." She folded her hands. They were sexton's hands now, or they had been for so long that the lawyer's callus on her right forefinger had nearly grown over, smoothed down by spade-haft and rope and the brass fittings of coffin lids. Nearly. Isandro Reyes had seen it anyway. That was the trouble with lawyers; they read hands the way priests read souls.

"I want to understand," Isandro said quietly, "why a woman would let another man hang for what she survived."

"And there it is." Maren laughed, but there was no humour in it, only the dry rattle of a thing long dead being made to move. "Put so plainly. Let me tell it plainly, then, since we've come this far up into the dark together. Sit, if you like. The bell frame makes a fair bench. I've sat on it many a night when sleep wouldn't come and the fen was too loud to ignore."

Isandro did not sit. She stood with her back very straight, as she had stood in the vestry, in the manor, in the dock-side corridor of the county gaol, and Maren thought: *she has been carrying this case like a stone in her chest for weeks, and she doesn't even know how heavy it's got.* Well. Maren knew about carrying stones. She had helped bury one, hadn't she? River stones and a boot, down in the Vane plot, where the parish believed Edric's daughter lay sleeping. She had dug that grave herself, on the night of the funeral, by lantern light, while Tom Ellery stood at the lychgate pretending to be drunker than he was and weeping all the same.

"Eleven years ago," Maren began, "I was twenty-six years old, and I was going to be married in the spring."

* * *

The spring had been Silas Crane's idea, though he'd never have dared say so first. He was the manor's handyman then — thirty-one, quiet, hands like tree roots, the kind of man who fixed a gate hinge without being asked and never once looked at his employer's daughter the way the village gossips insisted he must. The gossip was wrong about the looking. It was wrong about everything, which was the whole tragedy of Fenlow valley in miniature: everyone certain, everyone mistaken, and one girl drowned in the middle of it.

They were to be married in secret. She had it planned down to the ribbon on her hat. She would take the morning boat across the fen to Marsh Hailing, where nobody knew her face, and they would be wed by a dissenting minister who asked no questions for five pounds, and she would write to her father from the far side of the world if she wrote at all. Edric Vane would rage, then grieve, then — she truly believed this — come round. He loved her. He was a hard man but not a cruel one, and cruelty in the Vane house came from elsewhere.

It came from Aldous.

"You know some of this already," Maren said to Isandro. "You've read the confession — the true one, the pages that arrive cut off mid-sentence like a serial in a cheap magazine. You know that the night before I was to run, I went down to the boathouse to fetch the things Silas had hidden for our journey, and I heard voices. My uncle Aldous and a man named Quill, who did the estate's dirty business and was paid to forget it. They were talking about my father's will. About how Edric had redrafted it — had *told* Aldous he'd redrafted it — leaving the fen meadows to a land trust instead of to the family, because he'd found out what Aldous intended to do with them. Drain them. Sell them to the peat cutters and the gravel men. My father loved that fen the way other men love their mothers."

The lamp flame leaned in a draught through the louvres. Far below, an owl crossed the churchyard, a soft grey shadow over the stones.

"Aldous couldn't allow a new will," Maren went on. "And he couldn't allow me, either, because I'd heard him say so. He didn't know I was in the boathouse. Not at first. And then Silas came down the path — Silas, early, because he always was, because he wanted one more hour with me before the world woke up — and Aldous knew that whatever happened next, someone had to drown for it. Someone had to be guilty. And between a labouring handyman with no family and no friends worth the name, and his brother's daughter—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Between those two, there was never any question in Aldous's mind. There never is, with men like him. The poor are guilty by default. It saves time."

What had happened in the boathouse, Maren told in a voice gone flat as fen water in February. The struggle. Silas struck down — not killed, thank God, not killed, only clubbed senseless across the temple and left bleeding among the nets. Herself seized, her mouth clamped, the smell of her uncle's brandy and the river rot off the pilings. And then the water. He had meant to drown her in the fen channel and let the reeds keep her, the way the fen kept everything, and he would have managed it too, would have walked away whistling, except that she had grown up on that water the way he had never bothered to. She had gone limp. She had let the cold take her down and had waited, lungs screaming, until his grip loosened in the black confusion of it, and then she had swum — not up, but *along*, underwater, blind, hands out before her like a blind woman's, until she surfaced thirty yards downstream in the reed beds with her blood singing and her heart trying to tear its way out through her ribs.

She had crawled out on the old peat cutting bank. She had lain in the rushes shaking until dawn, watching the lights of the search party move along the far shore, hearing them call her name — *Maren! Maren Vane!* — in voices that grew more sorrowful and less hopeful as the hours passed, and understanding, with a clarity that felt like a second drowning, that every one of those voices was also pronouncing her free.

"Free," she repeated now, tasting the word. "Do you know what it is to hear your own death announced and feel *relief*? I lay in the mud and I heard my father weeping on the far bank — I knew his voice through half a mile of fog, I'd have known it through the whole width of England — and I lay there and I did not go to him. Because if I went to him, I was a witness. And witnesses to what Aldous had done did not survive to give evidence. Quill saw me, or thought he did, in the boathouse dark. Aldous would know soon enough that the body never came up. A dead girl who might not be dead was more dangerous than a live one. So I stayed in the mud, and I let my father weep, and that was the first payment."

* * *

"The arithmetic," Isandro said. She had sat down after all, on the bell frame's lower beam, her hands clasped between her knees, and she looked younger in the lamplight than she ever did in daylight. "That's what you called it. In the tower, before. The terrible arithmetic."

"Because that's what it is. Nothing else fits. Not cowardice — well, yes, cowardice too, I won't pretend I'm brave, I hid in a belfry for eleven years like a bat. But underneath the fear there was sums. Cold ones. Do them with me, Miss Reyes, since you're the professional." Maren held up one finger. "If I surface, I testify. Second finger: if I testify, I swear to what I saw — Aldous striking Silas, Aldous holding me under. Third finger: Silas's alibi for the murder of my father—"

Isandro's head came up sharply. "His alibi. The night Edric died. Silas swore he was alone in the boathouse workshop, mending the punt. Nobody could confirm it. That unconfirmed alibi is half of why the jury convicted—"

"—and the other half is that the prosecution's star witness, the person who could place Aldous at the manor that night with murder already in him, was a dead girl at the bottom of the fen." Maren's voice did not rise. It never rose anymore; eleven years of being nobody had sanded all the volume out of it. "Silas wasn't protecting himself with that empty alibi, Miss Reyes. He was protecting *me*. If he'd said where he really was that night — if he'd said he was hiding me, feeding me, rowing me supplies to the derelict eel-smoke house on Marlowe's Eyot while the whole valley searched for my corpse — then I existed. And if I existed, I was a witness, and Aldous would have had me inside a fortnight, and Silas knew it. So he sat in that dock and he let them believe the worst of him, and he watched them put the rope's shadow on his calendar, and he never once said my name. Not to save himself. To save me. Because my silence was the only wall between Aldous Vane and a second murder, and Silas Crane built that wall with his own neck."

The lamp guttered. Maren trimmed the wick with a fingernail gone grey at the edge, and the light steadied, and she saw that Isandro Reyes had gone very still, the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic of her own and hating the total.

"But it failed," Isandro said at last. "The arithmetic. Aldous was never convicted. Silas sits in gaol. You buried yourself alive. Three losses out of three."

"No." Maren shook her head slowly. "You're counting wrong. Count again. Eleven years ago, the choice was this: I come out of the fen, I testify, and one of two things happens. Either the court believes me — in which case Silas hangs anyway, because my testimony puts *him* at the boathouse that night too, bleeding and unconscious beside a murdered man, with no witness but a daughter who'd been caught running away with him and had every reason to lie — or the court disbelieves me, in which case I'm a fraud and a runaway and Aldous walks free *and* knows exactly where I am. Look at it honestly. What jury, in this valley, in that year, takes the word of a disgraced gentleman's daughter in love with the handyman over the word of Aldous Vane and his solicitor and his ten tenants? Harrow Blake would have shredded me before luncheon. He'd have done it politely, over tea, the way his kind does everything."

"So you chose erasure."

"I chose the only door that kept Silas breathing." Her voice cracked at last, just once, like ice on a pond taking weight. "Don't you see? That's the price. That's the whole horrible ledger of it. Every year I stayed dead was a year he lived. My silence was his alibi, his sentence, his protection — the only currency I had, and I paid it out year upon year upon year, and I'd have paid it to my grave, and I *did* pay it into a grave, did you know that? I dug it myself. Tom Ellery stood at the gate and cried and handed me the shovel, and we filled that coffin with stones from the very channel Aldous threw me into, and I laid my own boot in among them because I thought — God forgive me, I thought it was *fitting*. I thought: here lies Maren Vane, who died in the fen, and good riddance to her. The woman who climbed out of that water was nobody. The sexton. The widow Crane, on paper, though I never took his name to my lips until it was safe to, and by then taking it felt like theft, because he was paying for it in ways I couldn't stop."

Below them, the fen breathed. The bittern called again, closer, or perhaps it was only the wind finding the reeds.

"And now?" Isandro asked softly. "Six weeks. The trial. Why break the arithmetic now, after eleven years of it balancing?"

Maren was quiet a long moment. When she answered, she looked out the louvred window at the churchyard, at the pale rectangle of the Vane plot, at the whole sleeping valley that had believed her dead so long and so comfortably.

"Because the sums changed," she said. "Because Aldous has started selling the fen — did you know? Draining the east meadows, the very ground my father died defending. Because Cordelia needs her inheritance cleared of questions before the creditors finish circling, and Blake moves like weather. Because Silas is dying in that cell — his cough, did you hear it? Did you hear what eleven winters of stone floors did to his lungs? — and if he dies inside, then I paid eleven years of nothing for nothing, and my father's real killer keeps the land and the name and the peace of the parish, and I will have been a ghost for *nothing*." She turned back, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "A ghost for nothing, Miss Reyes. That I cannot bear. A ghost for something — for him, for his life — that I could carry to my own grave with a kind of pride. But the trade has to buy what it costs. And it stopped buying six weeks ago."

Isandro rose. For a moment neither woman moved, and the space between them filled with everything unsaid — with the question of what testimony would cost now, with the confession executed as declaration sitting in a lawyer's office, with the law itself, that great slow machine that had chewed Silas Crane once and might yet be made to spit him out.

"Then we do it properly," Isandro said. "Sworn, witnessed, untouchable. If your word is going to hang a Vane, it will hang him by the throat of the law and not by ours."

Maren almost smiled. "You sound very sure."

"I'm a lawyer. Certainty is the occupational disease." Isandro lifted the lamp. "Come down before the whole parish sees your light. We'll need—"

But Maren had gone rigid, her hand out for silence, her head cocked toward the window.

Down in the lane beyond the lychgate, where no honest carriage came at half past midnight, gravel crunched under slow tyres. Headlamps swept once across the yew trees and went dark. And in the new quiet, faint but unmistakable, came the small bright click of a car door being closed by a man who did not care who heard it.

Maren knew that tread on gravel. She had heard it crossing the boathouse boards eleven years ago, and she had heard it in nightmares ever since.

"Aldous," she whispered. "He knows I'm here."
