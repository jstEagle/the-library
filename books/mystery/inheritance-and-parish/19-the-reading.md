## 19. The Reading

The morning of the reading came in grey and silver, the Fenlow valley wearing its fog like a borrowed coat it intended to keep. Isandro Reyes stood at the window of the Vane Manor drawing room at half past eight, watching the mist move off the water meadows in slow, deliberate sheets, and thought that the valley had always known how to hide things. It had hidden a woman for eleven years. It had hidden a body that was never in a grave. It had hidden, most successfully of all, the truth inside a family that smiled over tea and passed the sugar with steady hands.

Today it would hide nothing.

She turned back to the table she had claimed as her own for the duration — a long mahogany slab beneath the tall window, its surface scarred by a century of rings from glasses set down carelessly by men who had never once been asked to use a coaster. Upon it, arranged with the precision she reserved for documents that might be challenged, lay her arsenal: the original will of Edric Vane, executed fourteen months before his death and witnessed by two parishioners now conveniently beyond the reach of cross-examination; the twin letters, which she had photographed and notarized copies of before her office was searched, the originals now sleeping in a bank box in Thetford under a name that was not hers; the vicar's papers, yellowed and trembling in their folder, chief among them the receipt for forty pounds paid to one Reverend Ambrose Fell for "clerical services, registration, and discretion," signed in a hand Isandro had come to know the way one knows a scar — *Aldous Vane*, the letters looping and confident, the signature of a man who had never doubted his own impunity; and last, in a folder of its own, sealed with wax she had watched set with her own eyes, the confession.

Not the confession as it had first arrived — unsigned, in Maren Vane's careful hand, with the second handwriting ghosting beneath it in corrections and additions. That document was a letter. What lay in the folder now was a *declaration*, sworn before a commissioner for oaths in Thetford ten days ago, executed with full formalities, witnessed by Isandro herself and by Tom Ellery, gravedigger of Fenlow parish, who had signed his name with the grave concentration of a man writing something he expected to be judged by. Maren Vane had signed it *Maren Vane*. Beneath, in smaller characters, she had added: *formerly known as Ruth Ellery, sexton of this parish.*

The law was a blunt instrument in most hands. In Isandro's, this morning, it was going to be a scalpel.

The door opened behind her, and she did not need to turn to know who it was. Harrow Blake moved through rooms the way cold air moves under a door — silently, thoroughly, and always toward the warmest seat.

"Miss Reyes." His voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man who billed by the quarter-hour and had never once been surprised by a client. "You're early."

"Probate waits for no one," Isandro said, turning. Blake was dressed in the grey of a man who wished to be mistaken for weather. Behind him, in the corridor, she could hear the household assembling — Cordelia's heels on the parquet, quick and brittle; Aldous's slower tread, heavier, the tread of a man who had spent the last week learning what it was to be watched. "Clause nine of the Vane trust instrument, Mr. Blake. You'll have read it."

"Of course." He took the chair opposite her table without being offered it, crossing his legs. "The *ancillary testamentary provision*. A curiosity of old Fenlow drafting. Supplementary declarations may be read into the record of an estate where they bear upon the disposition of assets, provided they are sworn." He smiled thinly. "It hasn't been invoked in this county since 1911."

"Then it's overdue for an airing." Isandro sat, and laid her hands flat on the table. "You know why I'm invoking it. You've known since the letters arrived. I'd have thought you'd advise your client to settle rather than sit through it."

"I advise my client on law, not on theatre." Blake's eyes went to the sealed folder, and something moved behind them — not fear, Isandro thought. Calculation. The rapid arithmetic of a man pricing a disaster. "A sworn declaration is not a conviction. It is not even evidence at trial, not yet. It is words, read aloud, in a drawing room, to an audience of interested persons."

"Words signed by a woman the family buried twice," Isandro said. "Once in a register and once under river stones. Words corroborated by a paid forgery receipt in your client's father's hand. Words that will be read into the probate record, Mr. Blake, where they become a matter of public register, citable, permanent, and *discoverable* — which means that when Silas Crane's trial begins in three weeks, the Crown will know exactly where to look." She let that sit. "You didn't come in here to argue. You came to see the folder. To see if it's real."

Blake's smile did not waver, but it thinned to paper. "And is it?"

"Sworn, sealed, and signed." Isandro leaned back. "You may verify the commissioner's stamp. I'd offer you coffee, but I suspect you'd rather not accept anything from my hand today."

For the first time since she had met him, Harrow Blake looked briefly, genuinely tired. "You should have taken the partnership," he said quietly. "I offered in good faith. Half the estate fees. A decade of work, wrapped and delivered. All you had to do was lose a file."

"I don't lose files," Isandro said. "I read them. It's the whole of my profession, and it appears to be the whole of yours — the difference is what we do with what we find."

He rose, adjusted his cuffs, and paused at the door. "Cordelia will settle," he said, without turning. "She's been drowning respectably for years; she'll be almost relieved. But Aldous—" He stopped. Started again, in a lower voice, almost gentle. "Aldous has a boat, Miss Reyes. He's kept it fuelled for eleven years. Men like that don't sit in drawing rooms and listen to their sins read aloud. Men like that run." He left before she could answer, which she suspected was the closest thing to a warning his professional ethics — such as they were — would permit him.

Isandro looked at the fog on the windows, and at the folder, and made a small note in the margin of her reading order: *watch the door.*

* * *

The drawing room filled at ten.

They came in by degrees, as families do when gathering for a reading — the old choreography of grief and greed performing itself one more time, though this time the script had been rewritten and none of them had seen the new pages. Cordelia arrived first, dressed in black that cost more than Tom Ellery earned in a year, her face composed into the expression of a woman who had practiced looking bereaved until the practice had worn grooves. She did not look at Isandro. She looked at the folder on the table the way one looks at a diagnosis.

Aldous came in behind her, and Isandro watched him the way she had learned to watch men in examination — not at the face, which could be schooled, but at the hands. His were still. Too still. The stillness of hands that had decided, in advance, exactly what they would and would not do today. He wore a country tweed and an expression of patient, faintly wounded dignity, the expression of a man who intended to be the victim of whatever was about to happen. He took the chair nearest the window — nearest the door, Isandro noted. Nearest the terrace, and the garden, and the fen beyond.

The solicitor's clerk she had retained sat in the corner with the probate register open on his knee. Tom Ellery stood at the back of the room, hat in his hands, having refused every offer of a chair. He had dug the Vane graves for thirty years, he'd told her, and he would stand in this one too. Beside him, in a plain grey dress with her hands folded, sat the sexton of Fenlow parish — Ruth Ellery, the register would say, if anyone asked. No one asked. But Isandro saw Aldous see her, and saw the moment his stillness acquired a second layer, the stillness of ice over moving water.

Maren did not look at Aldous. She looked at the folder, and at Isandro, and gave the smallest nod — the nod of a woman who had spent eleven years being no one, and had decided, at last and at cost, to be someone for the length of a reading.

"Thank you all for coming," Isandro said, and her voice carried in the high room without effort. "You are gathered for the supplementary reading in the estate of Edric Vane, deceased, under the ancillary testamentary provision of the Vane trust instrument. Before I begin, I am required to establish the character of the document." She opened the folder. "This is a sworn declaration, executed the tenth of this month before a commissioner for oaths, witnessed by myself and by Thomas Ellery. The declarant is present in this room. The declaration bears upon the disposition of the Vane estate, upon the validity of the death registration of one beneficiary, and upon the circumstances of the death of Edric Vane. Mr. Blake has reviewed the formalities and raises no objection to their sufficiency."

"I raise no objection to the *formalities*," Blake said from his chair, precisely. "The contents speak for themselves."

"Indeed," Isandro said. "That is rather the point."

She looked down at the first page. The handwriting was Maren's — the careful, self-effacing script of a woman who had spent a decade writing other people's names in a parish register and never once her own. Isandro had read the document perhaps forty times. She had sworn to herself that this reading would be the last time she would feel nothing.

She had been wrong. It was worse aloud.

*"I, Maren Vane, being of sound mind and full memory, do declare as follows. On the night of the fourth of November, eleven years past, I left Vane Manor by the garden door after an argument with my uncle, Aldous Vane, concerning the disposition of my father's share of the estate, which my uncle had administered and which I had discovered, from my father's own papers, he had never lawfully held. My uncle followed me to the fen path. What passed between us there I have set down in the pages that follow, and I set them down now in the presence of witnesses, not for vengeance, which has had eleven years to cool and has cooled into something else, but because a man named Silas Crane is to stand trial for a crime I saw him prevent, and because the dead deserve better bookkeeping than they have received in this parish."*

Cordelia made a small sound, quickly mastered. Aldous did not move. Isandro read on.

*"I saw my uncle strike my father. I saw it twice. The first blow was an argument; the second was not. I saw Silas Crane come up from the boathouse, and I saw my uncle put the stick into Silas's hand, and I saw Silas drop it into the water rather than hold it, which is the whole of that man's guilt — that he was there, and that he was afraid, and that his fear has kept him in a cell for a year while the man who swung the stick has slept in linen."*

She turned the page. In the corner of the room, the clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked, once, like a knuckle on wood.

*"I did not come forward. I will not pretend the reason was only fear, though there was fear enough to fill the fen. I had seen the register by then — the vicar showed me, in his shame, or in whatever the old man felt instead of shame. I saw that I was dead. And I understood that if I stood in a court and said what I had seen, my uncle's counsel would ask where I had been for the months between, and the answer would be that I had been nowhere, that I was a dead woman, that my resurrection would look like a fraud or a madness, and that Silas's presence at the fen that night — the one thing that might have been twisted into an alibi for my uncle — would be buried under the scandal of me. My silence kept the lie simple. A simple lie convicts a poor man faster than a complicated truth. That is the arithmetic I did, standing in the vestry in a dead woman's clothes, and I have done it every day for eleven years, and I am tired of the sum."*

Isandro's voice held. She had read wills to hostile rooms before; she had read disinheritances to weeping daughters and surprises to men who gripped their chair arms until the wood creaked. But she had never read a document in which every sentence was a stone being taken out of a wall that had stood for eleven years, and she could feel the room listening to the wall come down — Cordelia with her hand at her throat, Tom Ellery with his eyes shut, Blake motionless and taking the measure of everything, and Aldous Vane sitting by the window with his hands folded on his knee, wearing the face of a man listening to a stranger's troubles.

Until the page where the second handwriting began.

*"My father kept accounts,"* Isandro read, and here the declarant's text yielded, in the middle of a paragraph, to the corrections that had been made in the other hand — the hand Isandro had found beneath Maren's in the first letter, the hand that had promised *more*. She had asked Maren about it, once, in the bell tower, and Maren had said only: *Edric wrote to me. At the end. He knew he was dying and he knew what his brother was, and he wrote to me care of the parish, because he had guessed, men like him always guess too late. Those pages are his. I only carried them.* Now the room heard them, in the dead man's own recorded words, read into the probate record of his own estate: *"My brother has had my signature before. He will have it again. I have signed what he put in front of me for forty years, and I sign this now so that the last of it will hang him: the estate was never his to administer, the register was never Maren's death, and the forty pounds he paid Ambrose Fell is written in the vicar's own book. Let them read the book. Let them read it aloud."*

Isandro looked up. "I have here," she said, "the vicar's book."

She lifted the receipt, and read the amount, and the date — the fourth of December, eleven years past, four weeks to the day after the night at the fen — and then the signature, slowly, so that every syllable of the name had room to be heard in the high room and no corner of it to hide in.

"*Aldous Vane.*"

The stillness broke.

It broke quietly, which was the terrible thing — no shout, no denial, no chair thrown back. Cordelia simply bowed her head over her hands, and Isandro understood in that instant that the daughter had known, or had always half-known, and that her respectable drowning had been a way of never once looking down into the water. Blake closed his notebook with a soft click, the sound of a professional concluding that his retainer had become a liability. And Aldous Vane stood up, unhurried, and straightened his cuffs, and said, in a voice of mild reason that carried to every corner:

"This is a circus, and I have no part in it. I'll be at the boat house if anyone wants me — I have the winter moorings to see to."

And he walked, at a gentleman's pace, out through the terrace doors and into the fog.

Isandro was already moving. Tom Ellery was faster — the gravedigger went through those doors like a man answering a bell that had been ringing for thirty years, and behind him, unbidden, unhurried, her grey dress darkening at the hem with the wet of the garden path, came the sexton of Fenlow parish, walking out of her own name and into her own at last.

Through the tall windows Isandro watched the three of them dwindle down the lawn and into the fog that lay over the fen like a held breath — a man running at last, after eleven years of keeping a boat fuelled; a gravedigger with his shovel off the wall of the tool shed; and a woman in grey, walking, not running, because she had spent eleven years learning that the fen gives back what it is given.

The fog closed over them, and Isandro Reyes reached for her coat.
