## 10. Someone Is Editing the Past

The first thing to vanish was a box of county water permits from 1993, and the second thing to vanish was the record that it had ever existed.

Cyrus discovered both facts in the same hour, on a Tuesday gray enough to make the courthouse windows look like frosted glass. They had come for the permits because Chapter Nine of their reconstruction — the night of November third — had a hole in it shaped like a drainage easement. Voss Development had filed for quarry water rights in '93, and Cyrus wanted the engineer's stamps, the surveyor's notes, anything with a signature that might place one of the five families at the quarry before the winter of the disappearance. The clerk, a young man named Toby whose cowlick Cyrus could have drawn from memory, went back into the stacks and came out twice: once empty-handed, once with his face gone strange.

"That's odd," he said. "The index card's still there. But the card says *withdrawn*, and there's no withdrawal slip."

"Who has authority to withdraw?"

"Council attorney. County counsel. Or—" Toby hesitated, and Cyrus watched him decide not to finish the sentence.

Or the sheriff. Or someone who used to be.

Cyrus stood at the counter and let their memory do what it always did, which was to lay the past out flat like a map. Three weeks ago they had requested the same box — no. Not the same box. An adjacent one. But while waiting for it they had read the index drawer itself, top to bottom, because that was what Dell Marsh had taught them to do when they were nineteen and hungry for method: *read the whole drawer, kid. The gaps tell you more than the paper.* And three weeks ago, the card for Box 114-C, Water Permits 1993, had been there, plain as a hymnal, no stamp on it at all.

Someone had not stolen a document. Someone had stolen the *absence* of a document, and then gone back and forged the absence backward in time.

That was not a burglar's work. That was an archivist's work. That was the work of someone who understood that records are only ever testimony about other records, and that if you edit carefully enough, the lie becomes load-bearing.

Cyrus thanked Toby, said something mild about clerical errors, and walked out into the rain feeling the first true cold of the investigation settle into their sternum. Not fear exactly. Fear was a temperature; this was a pressure. The sense of a hand moving through the town's paper body, delicate as a restorer cleaning a painting — and removing figures from the background one by one.

* * *

The insurer's basement told them how far the hand could reach.

Meridian Mutual kept its cold storage under the main office, down a stairwell that smelled of concrete and old toner, where the heating pipes sweated in summer and the fluorescent tubes buzzed at a pitch only Cyrus seemed able to hear. The records manager, a woman named Okafor who had worked there thirty-one years and remembered Cyrus's father bringing them in as a child to look at the fire maps, let them down herself this time. She had heard about the Whitlock interview. Everyone had heard about the Whitlock interview. Ruth Whitlock had died on a Thursday and talked to Cyrus on the Sunday between, and towns like Meridian Falls metabolized such things fast.

"I'll be honest," Okafor said, unlocking the cage. "I did a spot check after your last visit. Made myself nervous."

"And?"

"And the '94 claim files are fine. All five. I counted them twice." She paused with her key ring in her fist. "But the denial letters — the copies we sent out, the ones that would've gone back to the families — those are gone from the correspondence files. Every one. Five letters, five families, one winter." She looked at Cyrus with the flat professional calm of someone who had spent her career around paper and had recently begun to distrust it. "I've been telling myself it's misfiling. Thirty years, you lose things. But those five were together in one folder, Mr. Okonkwo. Folders don't lose their spines selectively."

"No," Cyrus said. "They don't."

They stood together in the humming cold among the gray boxes, and Cyrus felt the pattern assemble itself behind their eyes with the terrible ease of a jigsaw finishing itself. The county archive had lost the permits. The insurer had lost the denials. Both losses were surgical. Both losses touched only the documents that mattered to the shape Cyrus had been building — the shape that pointed, with increasing weight, toward the quarry and toward whoever had closed Dell Marsh's case file in nine immaculate days.

And here was the thought that arrived uninvited, the way the worst thoughts do, fully dressed: *only three people knew which files mattered.*

Three people knew what Cyrus had requested, what Cyrus had read, what threads Cyrus had pulled. Three people had access across both the county system and the insurer's. Three people had the skill to forge a withdrawal stamp or lift a folder spine without leaving a ripple.

Dell Marsh, who had taught them that gaps testify.

Ada Reyes, who had hired them, whose letter had started all of it, whose grief was either the realest thing Cyrus had ever witnessed or the best performance they'd ever failed to catch.

Priya Nair, who was young and eager and had been in every room Cyrus had been in for two months, holding the flashlight, carrying the boxes, learning the filing system the way Cyrus had once learned it — which meant she now knew how to read the gaps too.

Cyrus hated the arithmetic and did it anyway, because that was who they were. You did the sum even when the answer hurt. Especially then.

* * *

Their own office was broken into on the Friday.

They knew it before they opened the door — knew it from the smell, faintly, of someone else's breath in the room, and from the fact that the radiator ticked at a different interval than it had when they left, because the window above it was open a half inch and the draft changed everything. Cyrus stood in the hallway of the converted bank building where they rented space above the old teller cages, and instead of going in, they took out their phone and photographed the door, the frame, the hallway, the light fixture with its dead moth. Then they called Priya.

"Don't touch anything," she said, before Cyrus finished the first sentence. "I mean — right, you wouldn't. Should I call it in? Because if I call it in, it goes through dispatch, and dispatch—"

"Goes through the department. Yes." Cyrus looked at the door. "Come anyway. Through the back stairs. Bring gloves and bring your notebook, and write down the time I called you."

She arrived in eleven minutes, slightly breathless, hair damp, and stood in the doorway staring at the room with the expression of someone seeing a wound in a familiar face. The office had not been tossed. That was the horror of it. It had been *visited*. The filing cabinets were closed. The chairs were pushed in. On the desk, the lamp sat at its usual angle. Only Cyrus — only someone with a memory that held the exact position of every object in every room they'd ever stood in — could have seen what was wrong.

"The Hollis file is out of order," Cyrus said quietly. "Second drawer, third hanging folder. It's a quarter inch forward of where it sits. Behind it, the Ngo folder has been refiled upside down — tab toward the back. And the box from the insurer, the photocopies I made of the five claims—" They stopped. Closed their eyes. Opened them. "Gone. Just those. Nothing else in the box was taken. The 1993 annual report is still there. The policy application is still there. Only the claims."

Priya wrote it down. Her pen scratched loud in the quiet. "They knew exactly what to take."

"They knew exactly what I'd found." Cyrus moved along the shelves, reading the room like a page. "Which means they didn't just know my office. They knew my *method*. They knew I'd copy the claims and keep the originals theory in reserve. They knew which folder I'd touched last." They turned. "Priya. Who knew I made those copies?"

"You told me. You told Ada. You told—" Priya stopped. Her face did something complicated. "You told Sheriff Marsh. When we visited him last week. You told him you'd copied everything, because you were trying to get him to react."

"And he didn't react," Cyrus said. "He offered me pie."

The word *pie* hung in the room like smoke. Priya looked down at her notebook. Cyrus looked at the window, at the half-inch of gray daylight, at the fire escape beyond it that anyone could climb and no one would see using.

"There's something else," Priya said. She hesitated, and Cyrus watched her decide to be brave, the way you watch weather change. "When I came up the back stairs just now — there was a cigarette butt on the landing. Third floor. Nobody smokes in this building; the lease prohibits it. I bagged it. Unofficially. It's in my car."

"Brand?"

"I didn't look. I didn't want to contaminate—" She caught herself. "I'm learning."

"You're learning." Something in Cyrus's chest loosened and tightened at once, and they recognized, with a kind of vertigo, the feeling Dell must have had watching a nineteen-year-old Cyrus photograph a crime scene with their eyes alone. This was what it cost, passing the method down. You handed someone a lantern and thereby showed them all the dark places you'd rather they never went.

"Priya," Cyrus said. "From today, we assume everything we say in this office is overheard. We meet elsewhere. We write nothing down that we can't afford to lose — except in your head and mine." They tapped their own temple, then, gently, hers. "They can burn every file in this town. They can't burn these. Which means, starting tonight, we're not investigators anymore. We're the archive."

Priya swallowed. "Is that supposed to make me feel better? Because honestly it makes me feel like a target."

"It should make you feel both," Cyrus said. "That's what accuracy feels like."

* * *

That night the rain came hard off the ridge, drumming on the tin awning over the pharmacy next door, and Cyrus sat in their apartment with the lights off and let the sound do what it always did, which was to open the doors in their memory whether they wanted them opened or not.

Rain on tin. November third, 1994, had rained like this — they knew that now, knew it from thirty small testimonies assembled over weeks, from a waitress's complaint about a leaking awning, from a police log entry noting reduced visibility on Quarry Road. Rain on tin, and five cars parked nose-in at the quarry rim, engines ticking, wipers going, and men's voices raised over the drumming — voices they had not yet heard but could almost reconstruct, the way you can reconstruct a chord from knowing its notes.

And somewhere in that rain, Harlan Voss had died. Not vanished. Died. The divers had confirmed it, and the confirmation had changed the flavor of everything: the missing files were no longer the acts of a man protecting a secret. They were the acts of a man protecting himself.

Cyrus sat in the dark and did the arithmetic again, slower this time, because the stakes had changed. If the killer was editing the record, then the record was winning somewhere — some document, some testimony, some stone with a name on it was being erased even now, tonight, while the rain fell. And Cyrus's own memory was the only complete copy left in existence. Every file destroyed made them more valuable. Every file destroyed made them more dangerous. There was no version of this in which they stayed a bystander; the moment the first box went missing, Cyrus Okonkwo had become the most important document in Meridian Falls.

Which meant, they thought, watching headlights sweep the wet ceiling of their apartment, that they were also the most important thing in Meridian Falls to destroy.

The phone buzzed. Ada.

"You've heard," she said, without greeting. Her voice had a raw edge he hadn't heard since the quarry. "The historical society. The Voss bicentennial collection. The original portrait — the oil painting of Harlan that hung in the council chamber for forty years — it's gone off the wall. Not stolen, according to them. *Removed for restoration.* Except the restoration conservator in Ashford has never heard of it, and the society's own accession ledger shows no deaccession entry." A breath. "Cyrus. They're taking the founder himself out of the record. What does that tell you?"

Cyrus looked at the rain, at the tin, at the dark.

"It tells me we're close," they said. "You only erase the parts of the picture that show what's standing behind them. Ada — tomorrow morning, first light, I want to walk the quarry road again. And I want you to think hard about everyone who's known, week by week, exactly where our attention has gone. Because somebody has been following our attention like a shadow follows a body."

"And the list is short," Ada said.

"The list is three names long," Cyrus said. "And one of them taught me everything I know about how to make a thing disappear."

The line went quiet. Out in the dark, the rain kept up its patient percussion on the tin, keeping time for a story that had waited thirty years for someone to write it down — and for someone else, still out there in the wet streets of Meridian Falls, desperate to unwrite it first.
