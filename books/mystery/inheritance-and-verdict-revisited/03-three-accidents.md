## 3. Three Accidents

The Ferrant file sat on Tamsin's kitchen table like a guest who'd overstayed its welcome, and she sat across from it at four in the morning with cold coffee and a habit older than either of them.

She read files the way her mother had read scripture — aloud, under her breath, looking for the verse that didn't fit. But the method wasn't her mother's. The method was Ambrose Kettle's, and it had come to her twenty-two years ago in a records room that smelled of toner and dead men's paperwork, when she was a dispatcher with a bad marriage and a worse habit of reading other people's incident reports over the shoulders of the officers who came in for coffee.

*You don't read a file,* Ambrose had told her, tapping the cardboard cover of a suicide that wasn't one. *You play it. Every statement is a note. Every timestamp. Every contradiction. A lie is a rest where there should be a sound. Find the rest, and the whole piece goes sour in your ear.*

She'd thought he was being poetic. She'd learned, over three deaths, that he was being literal.

Tamsin pulled a legal pad toward her and wrote the three names at the top of the page, the way a pianist runs scales before a concert. *Delia Moss. Hector Ruiz. The Przbylewski garage.* Three accidents she had unmade, one rest at a time.

* * *

Delia Moss had fallen down the stairs of her own brownstone on a Tuesday in March, and the city had filed her under *unfortunate* in eleven minutes flat. Tamsin hadn't even meant to get involved. A cab she'd dispatched had delivered a witness to the scene — a neighbor, shaken, chatty — and the woman had said, in the back seat, to no one, *funny, she always held the banister, her hip was bad, she held that banister like it owed her money.*

Tamsin had pulled the incident summary from the public docket the next day, because pulling files was what she did instead of sleeping, and she'd found the rest.

The sound. There was no sound. A woman of seventy-one with a bad hip, falling thirteen stairs, and the neighbor directly below — the chatty one, the one who'd ridden in Tamsin's cab — reported she'd heard nothing. Not a thud. Not a cry. Not the banister coming loose, though the banister *had* come loose, one bracket sheared clean, and sheared brackets scream.

She'd brought it to Ambrose at his kitchen table, because by then she brought him everything, the way you bring a tuning fork to the only man who can hear the pitch. He'd read the summary twice, made her a cup of tea she never drank, and said: *Who benefits from a quiet staircase?*

The neighbor. The chatty one. The one who'd been first on the scene, who'd moved the body — *just to check her breathing, officer* — and in moving it, explained away the position that would have told the truth. Delia hadn't fallen. She'd been walked to the top step by someone she trusted and helped over the edge, and the neighbor had been in the house, had heard everything, and had spent eleven minutes of silence arranging the room before she called it in.

The conviction had taken fourteen months. The card Ambrose sent afterward said only: *Good ear.*

Tamsin had pinned it above her desk, and it was pinned there still, and she looked at it now across the kitchen table with the Ferrant file between them, and she let herself feel, for exactly as long as it took to finish her cold coffee, the old warmth. The pride. The sense of being *played well* — of being, herself, an instrument in tune.

Then she turned the page and wrote the second name.

* * *

Hector Ruiz drowned in a retention pond behind a distribution center in August, and everyone agreed it was a heart attack in the water, because Hector was fifty-eight and heavy and the pond was on his evening walking route, and men like Hector died in ponds the way leaves came down in October. Natural. Seasonal.

Tamsin got involved because of the taxis. That was the joke she'd made at the time — that everything in this city eventually passed through her dispatch board, and if you sat at the switchboard long enough, the whole city confessed to you. A driver named Okafor had picked up a fare at the distribution center at 9:40 p.m., forty minutes after Hector was presumed in the water, and the fare had been Hector's business partner, and the partner had been *wet*.

Not soaked. Not dripping. Damp — at the collar, at the cuffs, in that specific way a man is damp when he has waded in somewhere shallow and hurried out. Okafor had mentioned it in the break room the way drivers mention everything, as weather, as nothing, and Tamsin had felt the rest open up in the file like a hole in the sheet music.

Because the timeline said Hector went in at nine. The partner's alibi said he was at dinner until ten. But the pond was a fifteen-minute drive from the restaurant, and the partner's shirt was damp at nine-forty, and damp dries, and a man who'd merely *found* a body — which was his eventual story — does not wade in to a drowned man and stand there getting his collar wet. You find a body, you stand on the bank. You call out. You do not go in unless you are checking for signs of life, and a man checking for signs of life calls for help with his mouth full of pond water, and the 911 call had been bone dry.

*The water is the witness,* Ambrose had said, when she laid it out for him in his kitchen, his reading glasses pushed up into white hair gone to thistle. *Water doesn't lie and it doesn't forget. It only dries. So you ask what the water remembers, and you ask it fast, before it forgets.* He'd leaned back. *The partner went in to hold Hector under. He went in a second time to make sure. And then he got in a cab with a wet collar and a dry mouth and thought he'd beaten the clock.*

He hadn't. Tamsin had made sure he hadn't — Okafor's log, the restaurant receipt, the weather report that said the night was warm enough that damp should have dried in twenty minutes and hadn't because the partner had been in the water at 9:35, not 9:15. The arithmetic of evaporation. Ambrose had taught her that too: *Everything decays on a schedule. Alibis decay fastest of all.*

Second card. *Good ear.* Pinned under the first.

* * *

The garage fire was the one that had made her name, such as it was — a name in a police blotter, a name in a newspaper feature nobody read, a name that Detective Reyes would someday pull out of a database and squint at. But that was later. In the year of the fire, it was only Tamsin and the Przbylewski brothers and a building that burned so cleanly it looked apologetic.

Old Man Przbylewski had died in the garage behind his own house, and the fire marshal called it electrical, because the wiring was forty years old and the garage was full of solvents and the math was easy. Tamsin got involved because the son — the younger one, the one who'd been *estranged*, the word doing heavy lifting in every statement — had called a cab at 11:15 p.m. from a bar nine blocks from the fire, and the cab had arrived at 11:22, and the fire had started at 11:30, and the son had been standing in his mother's kitchen comforting her by 11:50, which was a nine-minute drive, which was possible, which was *just barely* possible, and possibility was where liars lived.

But that wasn't the rest. Tamsin had almost missed it. The rest was the dog.

The Przbylewskis had a dog, an old beagle, and the beagle was in the statements three times, and in two of them it was in the yard, and in one of them — the son's statement, the third interview, the one where he'd relaxed — it was *in the garage with Dad, poor thing, lucky it got out.* The dog had gotten out. The garage had burned from the inside, the marshal said, origin near the workbench. And a beagle does not stay in a burning garage out of loyalty. A beagle leaves a garage the moment the first whiff of smoke arrives, because beagles are cowards with excellent noses, and the dog had been let out. Opened the door, let the dog out, closed the door, struck the match.

*The animal is the hinge,* Ambrose had said. He'd been quiet that night, quieter than usual, and she'd remember that quiet later and misread it, the way you misread everything in the warm years. *People rehearse their statements. They rehearse the big things — where they were, who they saw. They never rehearse the small things. The dog. The damp collar. The banister. The truth is always hiding in the detail they had no reason to lie about, so they never bothered to make it lie.* He'd looked at her over the glasses. *You have a gift, Tamsin. Most people hear noise. You hear the arrangement.*

She'd found the receipt that cracked it — a lighter fluid purchase, the younger brother's card, two days before the fire, at a hardware store he had no earthly reason to visit. And the estrangement had been theater, months of it, staged so the son would be the last man anyone suspected of wanting the old man's house and the insurance that came with it.

Third card. *Good ear.* She'd laughed when it came, and pinned it, and called him, and he'd said, *Three for three. You know what that makes you?*

*A busybody with a library card,* she'd said.

*It makes you a method,* he'd said, and she'd glowed for a week.

* * *

Now she sat at her kitchen table with the Ferrant retainer in a manila envelope and the three cards on the wall above her, and she tried to play the new file the way he'd taught her, and something was wrong with the music.

She'd been turning it over all night — the phrasing of the instruction. *Prove that Odile Ferrant will not be murdered before her trial.* She'd written it out on the legal pad in her own handwriting, the way Ambrose had taught her to handle statements that smelled wrong: copy them out, slow, and listen to where your own pen hesitates.

Her pen hesitated at *prove.*

You don't pay a private investigator to prove a negative. You don't hire anyone to prove a negative. If the Ferrant family feared a murder, you hired protection. You went to the police, you hired security, you moved the matriarch to a hotel under another name. You did not retain a night-shift dispatcher with a hobby and ask her to *prove* a thing that hadn't happened yet — because a thing that hadn't happened yet could only be proven one way.

By waiting for it not to happen. And then waiting some more. Forever.

Unless — and here her pen stopped altogether, and the kitchen clock ticked into the rest like a metronome falling off a table — unless the client already knew the murder was scheduled. Unless the client knew the date, the manner, the window. Unless *prove she will not be murdered* was a lawyer's way of dressing up a plainer sentence: *be there when it does, and watch.*

Tamsin put the pen down. The house was very quiet. Down the block a car passed, headlights raking the ceiling, and she thought about Ambrose — about how he'd say this, if she called him, if she laid it out the way she'd laid out the banister and the pond and the dog. He'd take off the glasses. He'd make the tea she never drank. He'd say: *When the instruction is impossible, the instruction isn't the point. Ask what the client gets from you failing.*

And she'd say: *And what do I get?*

And he'd say — and this she was sure of, sure the way she'd been sure of nothing else in twenty-two years of listening — *You get to be there. That's what they're buying, Tamsin. They're buying your eyes.*

She looked at the wall. Three cards, three dead people, three times she'd been the good ear in the room. She had never once asked herself, until this moment, with the Ferrant money in the envelope and the Ferrant file on the table, a question that should have been the first question of all.

*Who taught me to listen?*

The phone rang.

Not the dispatch line — her kitchen phone, the old landline she kept for exactly this kind of call, unlisted, paid in cash. She stared at it through two rings, three, the way you stare at a rest in the music where a note should be.

On the fourth ring, she picked it up.

The voice on the other end was old, and dry, and utterly unafraid, and it said: "You're the one they paid. I want to know something, Miss Villalobos, and I want you to think carefully before you answer."

A pause, precise as a cut note.

"Who's paying you — and what exactly did they tell you I'd die of?"
