## 7. Person of Interest

Detective Marisol Reyes had been on the job nineteen years, and in that time she had developed a private taxonomy of the people who called in tips. There were the helpful, who wanted nothing but to be useful. There were the guilty, who wanted to be seen helping. There were the lonely, who wanted to be seen at all. And there was a fourth category, rare and precious, that she thought of privately as the collectors — people who collected other people's deaths the way some men collected stamps, handling them carefully, admiring the detail, keeping them in albums no one else was allowed to open.

Tamsin Villalobos was a collector. Reyes knew it before the woman sat down.

She'd spent the morning pulling the file, and the file was a small museum. Three deaths over six years — a fall from a balcony in Delridge, a drowning in Green Lake, a garage fire out past Boeing Field. All three ruled accidental. All three reopened, quietly, after letters arrived — anonymous, detailed, uncannily right. All three closed again as accidents, but closed differently this time, with the family settlements restructured, with an insurance payout denied in one case, with a man named Dario Pell walking out of a marriage that had been worth more dead than alive.

And in the margin notes of all three, in different officers' handwriting, the same phrase: *per civilian consultant, T. Villalobos.*

Reyes had made a pot of coffee she didn't drink and read the whole thing twice. Then she'd called down to records and asked for the original letters, and held the first one under her desk lamp for a long time. Whoever had written it understood death the way an engineer understood load-bearing walls. Not a single wasted word. Not a single wasted death.

Now the consultant was sitting across from her in Interview Room 2, and she was smaller than Reyes had imagined — a compact woman with a night-shift pallor and the particular stillness of someone who spent her working hours listening to strangers' voices in the dark. Her hands were folded on the table like she was waiting for a bus. Her eyes did not wait. They were already reading the room, the recorder, the two-way glass, and Reyes herself, in that order.

"Thanks for coming in," Reyes said.

"You said it would help the investigation." Villalobos's voice was flat, dispatch-calm, the voice of a woman who had spent years saying *units en route* to people having the worst night of their lives. "I'd like that to be true."

"It can be. Depends what you give me."

"Depends what you're looking for."

Reyes let the silence sit. It was an old trick and Villalobos knew it was an old trick, and neither of them blinked, and Reyes thought: *oh, this is going to be interesting.*

"Odile Ferrant," Reyes said finally. "Found dead yesterday, six-forty a.m., in the conservatory of her own home. Locked from the inside. Her physician signed a preliminary of cardiac arrest. The medical examiner hasn't ruled yet, but between us, he's leaning that way."

"Between us," Villalobos said, "you requested the interview anyway. So you're not leaning that way."

*Sharp.* Reyes made a note she didn't need to make, just to have something to do with her hands. "What's your relationship to the deceased?"

"None. I never met her."

"Then why did you spend four nights parked across the street from her house?"

She watched the woman absorb the hit. To her credit, it barely showed — a fractional stillness in the folded hands, the kind of tell you'd only catch if you were looking for it, and Reyes had been looking since the door opened.

"You've been to the estate," Villalobos said. "Talked to the neighbors."

"One neighbor. Retired teacher. Very observant. Says there was a cab parked on Delacorte Road four nights running, engine off, dome light on for exactly ninety seconds at intervals. She thought it was a cheating spouse. She was disappointed to learn it was a taxi."

"Dispatchers run checks on vehicles. It's part of the job."

"At one in the morning. On a vehicle registered to a private estate."

Villalobos uncrossed her hands and recrossed them, and Reyes understood she was watching the woman decide something — how much truth to spend, and on what. Collectors were like that. They'd give you a beautiful specimen, but never the whole album.

"I was paid to be there," Villalobos said.

The room changed temperature. Reyes kept her face neutral and her pulse didn't. *Paid.* In nineteen years, the honest answers were always the ones that arrived too fast and too clean, gift-wrapped, and this one had a bow on it.

"Paid by whom?"

"I don't know. A courier. Cash retainer, five figures, delivered to my office with a lawyer's covering letter. No name on the letter that survived scrutiny — I checked, believe me. The instruction was one sentence." Villalobos recited it from memory, and Reyes heard the recitation in her voice, the way you could hear a thing that had been turned over ten thousand times in the dark. "*Prove that Odile Ferrant will not be murdered before her inheritance trial begins.*"

Reyes wrote it down, though she'd memorized it on the first pass. "That's an unusual sentence."

"It's an impossible sentence. You don't prove a negative. You don't pay five figures to prove a negative. I took the money anyway, which I'm aware makes me look like either a fool or an accessory, and I've spent the last two days trying to work out which."

"And which is it?"

"I was hoping you'd tell me." For the first time, something moved behind the flat voice — not fear exactly, but the shape fear leaves when it passes through. "Detective, I'm going to say something, and you can put it in your report or not. The manner of death described in that retainer — the window, the location, the presentation of the scene — matches what happened to Odile Ferrant. Not approximately. Exactly. Which means whoever wrote that instruction knew how she was going to die before she died."

Reyes set down her pen. "Or wrote it after the fact and backdated the delivery."

"I thought of that. The courier was a kid, maybe nineteen, hired off a gig app, paid in prepaid cards. He described his client as a woman's voice, phone-filtered, no name. If that's staged, it's staged three layers deep, and nobody stages three layers deep unless they want the staging found — which is its own message." Villalobos's eyes came up and held hers, and for a moment the dispatch-calm cracked and Reyes saw the thing underneath, the thing that had solved three deaths the department had been happy to bury: a mind that could not stop pulling threads. "You pulled my history. I know you did, it's written on your folder. Three cases. Ask me about them."

"I intend to."

"Ask me now, because it matters. The letters that cracked those cases — the anonymous ones — I didn't write them. I received them. Or rather, I received the *questions* in them, and I did the work, and the work was right. Three times right. If you're building a theory where I find dead people convenient, you should know that in all three cases I tried to stop what happened and failed, and in this case I was paid to prevent a death and failed, and I am *tired*, Detective, of being right about deaths I couldn't stop."

The recorder's red light burned steady between them. Reyes had interviewed hundreds of people in this room and could count on one hand the ones who'd turned the table around this fast. Villalobos hadn't raised her voice. She hadn't asked for a lawyer. She had done the one thing guilty people almost never did: she had handed over a motive to suspect her and then dismantled it in front of her, piece by piece, daring Reyes to find the flaw.

Reyes found one. She was almost sure she found one.

"The balcony case," Reyes said. "Delridge. Elena Vasquez-Pell."

Something crossed Villalobos's face — not guilt, not fear. *Recognition.* The look of a chess player seeing a move she'd studied coming from a different opening than expected.

"What about it."

"The anonymous letter. It named the loose railing bolt, the insurance rider, the second will. Detail like that doesn't come from a passerby. It comes from someone with access to the file." Reyes leaned forward. "The Vasquez-Pell file was sealed pending a civil suit. Court-sealed. The same way—" she let it hang, let it land, watched it land— "the Ferrant estate file is sealed pending trial. The same way whoever wrote your retainer would have needed access to know exactly how Odile Ferrant's death would look."

The silence stretched. Outside the room, Reyes knew, a uniform was walking the hall, and somewhere in the building a printer was jamming, and the world was going on, and in here two women were circling the same fire from opposite sides, each convinced the other was holding the match.

"You think I have access to sealed files," Villalobos said.

"I think somebody does, and you're the common denominator."

"I dispatch taxis."

"You solve murders."

"Three," Villalobos said. "Out of how many deaths in this city in six years? If I were laundering killings through my hobby, Detective, I'd have a worse batting average than the weather."

It was almost funny. Reyes didn't smile. "Here's what I see," she said, and laid it out flat, card by card, because she wanted to watch the woman's hands. "A dispatcher with a documented history of involvement in deaths ruled accidental. A cash retainer she can't source, instructing her to prove a murder won't happen — a murder that then happens on schedule. A four-night surveillance of the victim's home she can't explain except as paid. And a pattern, across all her prior cases, of anonymous correspondence containing sealed-file knowledge." She sat back. "If I took this to a prosecutor today, what would you say my theory is?"

Villalobos was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone somewhere Reyes hadn't heard yet — lower, wearier, almost gentle, which was somehow worse.

"You'd say I'm either the killer or the killer's instrument. That someone is using me — my methods, my history, my presence at the scene — to build themselves an unassailable death. That when this goes to trial, the defense won't even need a theory, because the state's own file will contain a woman who was paid to predict the murder and parked outside the house when it happened." She unfolded her hands at last, and Reyes saw that they weren't steady. "You'd say I'm the alibi or the scapegoat. And you'd be right. That's the trap. I've been in it since the courier walked through my door, and I don't know yet which side of it I'm standing on."

*The alibi or the scapegoat.* Reyes turned the phrase over. It had the ring of something rehearsed — or of something understood so completely it had become native. That was the trouble with collectors. Everything they said sounded like evidence and wisdom at once, and you couldn't tell which until it was too late.

"Here's what's going to happen," Reyes said. "You're going to give me the courier's app records, the covering letter, the retainer, and the serial numbers if you were smart enough to log them. You're going to give me the dates and times of every night you sat on Delacorte Road. And then you're going to go home, and you are not going to leave the city, and you are going to answer your phone."

"And if I find something first? If I work the case?"

"Then you're obstructing."

"Am I?" Villalobos stood, unhurried, and Reyes noticed for the first time how tired she looked up close — the night-shift pallor wasn't cosmetic, it was structural, years of it. "Because from where I sit, Detective, the person who wrote that retainer wanted exactly this. Wanted me interviewed. Wanted me in a file, in a theory, in a box. I'm the contingency plan. You're not investigating me — you're *executing* someone else's plan for me." She paused at the door, hand on the frame, and didn't turn all the way around. "One thing, free of charge. The sealed Ferrant file — the estate inventory, the will contest exhibits, the medical summary. Pull the access log. Somebody with a badge or a bench order has been in it. When you find out who, you'll have your killer. And I'd bet my three cases it isn't me."

The door closed behind her, soft as a taxi door at 3 a.m.

Reyes sat alone in Interview Room 2 for a long time, the recorder's red light burning, and told herself the crawling feeling at the base of her neck was just fatigue. Then she went back to her desk, pulled the Delridge file, and read the anonymous letter one more time — the one signed, like all of them, with a single initial.

*K.*

She'd assumed it was a crank's flourish. Now, with Villalobos's voice still in her ears — *somebody with a badge or a bench order* — she pulled the access log on the sealed Ferrant file, and found what she was afraid she'd find: a retrieval three weeks ago, authorized, documented, unremarkable.

Signed out under the name of a retired detective. A consultant brought in, once, years ago, on the Vasquez-Pell case.

Ambrose Kettle.

Reyes stared at the name for a long moment, and somewhere across the city a taxi dispatcher was reaching for her phone, and neither of them knew yet that they were both reading the same signature at the bottom of everything.

* * *

The call came at 9:47 that night, while Reyes was still at her desk, the Ferrant file open under her lamp.

"You pulled the log," Villalobos said. No hello. Dispatchers didn't say hello.

"How did—"

"I know how your day goes, Detective. I've spent six years learning how your day goes. Did you find the name?"

Reyes looked at the name. Kettle. Ambrose Kettle. Thirty-one years of commendations, a retirement plaque somewhere, a man whose name still came up in training seminars on interview technique.

"I found a name," she said carefully.

"Then you know I'm not your killer," Villalobos said, "and you know I'm not crazy, and you know that whoever signed out that file has been three steps ahead of both of us since before Odile Ferrant died." A breath, and when the voice came back it had dropped into something Reyes recognized from her own mirror on bad nights — the sound of a person deciding to stop running. "I have the retainer money traced to a shell account inside the Ferrant estate. I have a nephew whose alibi was built through my own dispatch board. And I have a feeling, Detective, that you and I are holding two halves of the same file. Meet me tomorrow. Not at the precinct."

Reyes should have said no. She should have said *come in, formally, on the record, with a lawyer.*

"Where," she said instead.

"The ferry terminal. Ten a.m. And Detective—" a pause, the held breath of the night shift, "—bring the letters. All three. I think I know who wrote them, and I think when I say the name out loud, you're going to wish I didn't."
