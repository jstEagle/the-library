## 10. Mentor's Counsel

The house smelled the way it always had: pipe tobacco gone cold in a glass dish, old paper, lemon polish worked into wood that had been polished for sixty years. Tamsin stood on Ambrose Kettle's front step with a folder under her arm and let the smell do what it always did — take ten years off her and put her back at his kitchen table, twenty-three years old, watching him spread a coroner's report out like a man dealing patience.

He opened the door before she knocked twice. Of course he did. He'd heard her cab pull up; she hadn't even tried to park quietly.

"Tamsin Villalobos." He said her whole name the way he always did, as if it were a citation he was awarding her. "You look like you haven't slept."

"I haven't."

"Good." He stepped back to let her in. "Sleep makes people stupid. Coffee?"

"Ambrose, I'm not here for coffee."

"You're here because you have nowhere else to go, which means you're here for both." He shuffled toward the kitchen, one hand trailing the wall — the hip had gotten worse since she'd last visited, the walk more of a negotiation than a gait. "Sit. You'll want to be sitting when I tell you what's wrong with your thinking."

She sat. She laid the folder on the table between them, and watched him look at it without touching it, the way he always did first — reading the weight of it, the way the pages sat, what that told him about how often it had been opened.

"Odile Ferrant," he said.

Her spine went cold. "I didn't say—"

"You didn't have to. A woman dies in a locked conservatory three days after an anonymous retainer lands on the desk of the city's only unofficial death-auditor, and then that auditor shows up on my doorstep at nine in the morning looking like she's carrying her own indictment." He filled two mugs, set hers down in front of her. "What else would it be?"

* * *

Ambrose had been retired eleven years, and in eleven years he had made retirement into a discipline. Mornings began at five-thirty with the police scanner kept low enough to be weather. He read three newspapers and annotated two. He maintained, in a filing cabinet disguised as a sideboard, summaries of every open case worth thinking about — most of them reconstructed from public records, court dockets, and the occasional document that passed through the hands of a former colleague who owed him more than money could express.

The Ferrant file lay open on his desk now, though by the time Tamsin arrived it had been returned to its sleeve, and the sleeve to the drawer, and the drawer locked. On top of it, in his own hand, a single index card:

**Conservatory. Lock engaged from inside. Heart failure per responding medic. Window sealed — check the sealant date. The nephew's alibi is a gift. Ask who wrapped it.**

He had written the card four days ago, the morning after the death, working from the evidence summary that should never have left the courthouse. It had cost him a favor he intended to collect later, from a man whose name he kept in the same drawer as the file. That was the arithmetic of a career like his: favors were currency, currency bought access, access bought certainty, and certainty was the only thing he had ever loved besides the work itself.

And the work, now, was walking up his garden path wearing yesterday's clothes and carrying a folder full of almost-right conclusions.

He had planned this part carefully. Not the visit — he'd known she'd come, but not when, and so he'd prepared the ground rather than the hour. For weeks he had tended the trail the way a gardener tends a bed: the taxi timestamp on Julian Ferrant's alibi, planted through a burner account that dispatched a phantom fare to the corner of Delancey at 11:42 p.m.; the shell company that laundered the retainer back through the estate's own accounts, elegant as a Möbius strip; the page from Odile's notebook left in Tamsin's dispatch booth like a signature. Every thread led somewhere specific. Every thread led away from him.

But threads were not enough. Threads were for detectives. What he needed was for *her* to pull them — because Tamsin Villalobos's deductions had a purity his never could. She was a civilian. She had no warrant, no chain of custody, no institutional taint. When she solved something, it solved clean. Three times now her conclusions had landed on police desks and been confirmed, and three times the credit had scattered harmlessly while the method — *his* method, laundered through her the way the retainer was laundered through the shells — had gone unexamined.

Julian Ferrant would be her fourth. And Julian Ferrant was guilty of nothing except being where Ambrose needed a guilty man to stand.

There was a risk, of course. There was always a risk, and the risk had a name, and the name was sitting in his kitchen drinking his coffee. She was good. Better than good — she was the finest instrument he had ever tuned, and instruments that fine sometimes developed opinions about the music. But she trusted him. That was the foundation everything else was built on, poured thirty years ago when he'd pulled a sharp-eyed dispatcher out of a dead-end life and taught her to read files like sheet music. Trust, once installed correctly, held loads it was never rated for.

He carried the coffee in and sat across from her, and gave her the warmest version of himself, the one he kept maintained for exactly this purpose.

"Now," he said. "Show me what you've got. Start from the beginning, and don't skip anything because you think I'll find it boring. Boring is where bodies hide."

* * *

She started from the beginning. The retainer — cash, couriered, no name, one instruction: *prove Odile Ferrant will not be murdered before her trial.* The phrasing that had bothered her from the first night. The surveillance. Odile's 3 a.m. call. The conservatory, locked from inside, heart failure on the certificate. The money traced home to the estate. Julian's alibi built on a fare she herself had dispatched. Marisol's lie about the evening of the death, cracked open by timeline work. The break-in at the booth. The forensic accountant, alive and hiding, holding a dossier that pointed at a police pension fund.

Ambrose listened the way he always listened — motionless except for one finger tapping the table in slow quarter-notes, eyes half-closed, like a man hearing an orchestra tune up and already knowing which violinist was flat.

When she finished, he was quiet long enough that she counted four ticks of the mantel clock.

"All right," he said finally. "Three things are wrong with your case, and one of them is going to hurt."

"Tell me."

"One. You're treating the retainer as the crime's prologue. It isn't. It's the crime's *centerpiece*. Whoever paid you wasn't buying prevention or failure — they were buying a witness. Not just any witness. A witness whose entire reputation rests on spotting staged deaths, standing in plain sight when a staged death occurs and doing nothing to stop it. Think about what that does to you in a courtroom, Tamsin. Your credibility is the murder weapon's silencer."

She had thought of this. Hearing him say it still landed like a slap. "Two?"

"Two. You're chasing the money backward, which is correct, but you're stopping at the estate because the estate is where the money visibly lives. Money doesn't live anywhere. Money *visits*. A pension fund, a family trust, a shell company — those are rooms money passes through. Ask yourself who has spent thirty years learning how institutions move money without leaving fingerprints." He sipped his coffee. "Not a heir. Heirs are amateurs with allowances. Someone who has watched courts work from the inside for decades."

"And three?"

He set the mug down and looked at her with an expression she'd seen maybe five times in their acquaintance — the one he wore when he was about to hand her something heavy and wanted her to lift it herself.

"Three. Julian Ferrant's alibi. You're treating the taxi timestamp as evidence someone framed him. Consider the alternative: that it's evidence someone *protected* him. Badly. Clumsily. By a person who knows dispatch systems but not well enough — who used a real fare, a real cab, a real corner, and got the timing right but the *texture* wrong. Amateurs build alibis out of documents. Professionals build them out of habits." He leaned forward. "Who benefits if Julian goes down? Everyone. Who benefits if Julian looks *framed*? Also everyone, because a frame-up poisons the whole investigation. But who would build an alibi that airtight and leave it sitting there like bait?" He tapped the folder. "Someone who wants you looking at Julian. Which means Julian is either the killer — or the decoy. And Tamsin—" his voice gentled, and the gentleness was the cruelest thing in the room, "—you've already decided. I can hear it. You've decided it's the boy, and you came here hoping I'd agree with you."

Had she? She turned her mug a half-circle on the table. "He had access. He had motive. His alibi came through my own board, which means whoever built it knew my systems—"

"Or knew *you*," Ambrose said softly. "Knew you'd trust your own machine. Knew you'd feel violated and angry, and angry people chase the nearest target." He sat back. "Don't give them you, girl. Give them nothing until the shape is undeniable."

* * *

After she left — after he'd walked her to the door, squeezed her shoulder, told her she was the best mind he'd ever trained and to call him the moment anything moved — Ambrose stood in his hallway and allowed himself, briefly, the pleasure of a job nearly done.

She had fought him on Julian. Good. He'd expected her to fight, and he'd prepared the counterweight: the pension-fund thread, which he had salted with just enough genuine corruption — old, cold, deniable corruption belonging to other men — to keep her digging downward instead of sideways. Let her spend her fury on a pension scandal. Fury was fuel, and fuel burned where you pointed it.

He returned to the sideboard, unlocked the drawer, and took out the index card stack. Four cards now. One per death. He read them in order, the way he read everything: bottom to top, cause before effect.

The fall. The drowning. The garage fire. The conservatory.

Each one a lesson plan. Each one a seminar with a single student, though the student hadn't known she was enrolled. He had chosen the deaths the way a piano teacher chooses études — each harder, each teaching a new technique, each solved by her in a way that made the city notice her and made the police grateful and made no one, ever, ask the obvious question: *where did the night-shift dispatcher learn to read a crime scene like a forty-year veteran?*

From me, they'd have found, if they asked. From thirty years of me, poured into her over cheap coffee and cold case files.

He slid the cards back into the drawer and paused, his hand on the lock, feeling the small familiar pride curdle into something he refused to call doubt.

She had brought him the accountant's name today. He hadn't expected that — the accountant was supposed to stay buried another week, until the trial's final day, when Ambrose would need to reach her and she would need to be reachable. Now Tamsin knew about her. Now Reyes might know about her. The timetable had shifted, and timetables shifting was how men like him ended up in rooms with tape recorders.

He picked up the phone and dialed a number he knew by heart, and when it answered he said only: "Move the accountant up. Before the verdict. I don't care how clean it looks anymore. Clean is a luxury."

He hung up. Outside, the city went on making its ordinary noises.

In the kitchen, two coffee mugs sat drying on the rack, side by side, and one of them still had her lipstick on the rim, and Ambrose looked at it for a long moment with an expression no one who loved him would have recognized.

Then he washed it, carefully, and put it away.

* * *

Tamsin drove back across town with Ambrose's voice running in her head like a second engine. *You've already decided.* Had she? She pulled into the dispatch lot and sat with the engine ticking, and made herself do the thing he'd taught her: strip the conclusion, keep the facts, rebuild from zero.

Facts. The retainer originated in the estate. The alibi ran through her board. The break-in left Odile's page behind. The accountant pointed past the family entirely.

And one fact she hadn't yet said aloud to anyone, least of all to Ambrose: the retainer's instruction — *prove she will not be murdered* — had been phrased in a construction she'd seen before. Twice. In anonymous letters signed **K**, the ones Reyes was pulling apart, the ones that had cracked her first three cases wide open.

*Prove a negative.* Only one person she knew talked like that. Only one person had ever taught her that the most beautiful lies were built out of true things arranged slightly wrong.

She sat very still in the cab, listening to the engine cool, and told herself it was exhaustion. Told herself grief made shapes in the dark. Told herself that tomorrow she'd run the handwriting comparison properly, scientifically, and that the odds were a thousand to one against—

Against nothing. Against the one man who had never lied to her.

Her phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, six words long:

**Ask Ambrose who taught him Latin.**

She stared at it until the screen dimmed, and outside the booth, the night shift lights blinked on one by one, patient as a jury filing in.
