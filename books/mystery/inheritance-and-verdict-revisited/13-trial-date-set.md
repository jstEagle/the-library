## 13. Trial Date Set

The courthouse on Calloway Street had been built by men who believed justice should feel like weather — heavy, inescapable, pressing down on you the moment you crossed the threshold. Granite columns, a rotunda with a dome painted to look like sky, and a hush that wasn't silence so much as held breath. Tamsin had been inside twice before: once for a traffic citation she'd beaten on a technicality, once to identify a body.

She'd liked it better the second time.

The Ferrant will contest had drawn a crowd the way drownings draw gulls. Reporters filled the press benches and spilled into the aisle, cameras stacked like cairns outside the doors because Judge Okafor had banned them from her courtroom. The gallery was standing room only, half of it occupied by people who'd never met Odile Ferrant but had opinions about her money strong enough to skip work for. Tamsin slid into a seat at the back corner, third row behind a pillar, where she could see everything and be seen by almost no one.

Almost no one lasted eleven minutes.

She felt it first as a prickle between her shoulder blades, then caught the reflection in the brass railing beside her: a young woman with a phone held low, lens up, angled not at the witness stand but at the back corner. At her. The shutter sound was off, but the woman's thumb moved in the small rhythmic taps of someone photographing in bursts.

Tamsin didn't turn around. Turning around was confirmation, and confirmation was currency, and she was done handing out currency for free.

Instead she opened her notebook and wrote the date at the top of a fresh page, and beneath it, in letters small enough to be deniable:

*Day one. Five days left.*

Because that was the arithmetic nobody in this room knew but her. The retainer — the obscene, impossible retainer — had specified a window. *Prove that Odile Ferrant will not be murdered before her inheritance trial begins.* And then, buried in the courier's paperwork like a footnote in a contract designed never to be read aloud: *coverage through the close of proceedings.* Whoever had paid her hadn't just scheduled a murder. They'd scheduled a deadline, and they'd written it into her fee agreement so precisely that when she'd finally understood what she was looking at, she'd had to sit down on the curb outside her own dispatch office and breathe until the street stopped tilting.

Odile Ferrant had died on schedule. Which meant the schedule had more pages.

* * *

Judge Okafor took the bench at nine sharp, a compact woman with reading glasses on a chain of office and the expression of someone who had already read every filing twice and found all of it wanting. She surveyed the gallery the way a lighthouse surveys a rocky coast.

"This is a probate matter," she said, "not a circus. Anyone who treats it as one will discover I have contempt powers and a full calendar." A pause. "Counsel, proceed."

What followed was theater, and everyone knew it, and everyone performed anyway.

Counsel for the estate — a silver-haired man named Aldous Wren whose voice had the practiced warmth of a funeral director — opened by painting Odile Ferrant in broad, loving strokes: matriarch, philanthropist, iron-willed founder of a shipping concern that had outlived three recessions. He described a will executed eighteen months before her death, clean and witnessed, leaving the bulk of the estate in trust with Julian Ferrant as caretaker-trustee.

Then counsel for the contest — Marisol Ferrant's attorney, a sharp-featured woman named Devi Raghavan who dressed like a verdict waiting to happen — rose and did something Tamsin recognized instantly, because she'd have done the same thing.

She went for the death.

"Your Honor, the estate asks this court to honor a will executed by a woman who died under circumstances the medical examiner chose not to investigate. We intend to establish that Mrs. Ferrant's final months included a documented fear for her safety, a planned revision of her testamentary documents, and an appointment with a forensic accountant scheduled for the week after she died. The court cannot weigh her intentions while ignoring the manner of her passing."

Murmurs rolled through the gallery like water finding its level. In the front row, Julian Ferrant sat very still, hands folded, face arranged into the careful blankness of a man who'd been coached on his expressions. Beside him, Marisol wore black and grief the way some women wear perfume — generously, and where everyone could smell it.

Tamsin wrote: *Raghavan knows about the accountant.* Then, underneath: *Or wants us to think she does.*

Because here was the thing that kept her pen moving and her stomach tight: the forensic accountant was supposed to be a ghost. Tamsin had spent two weeks chasing the thread of Odile's last appointment, and the trail had gone cold in a way trails don't go cold naturally — not dead, but *tidied*, every record pulled, every reference softened, as if someone had gone through Odile's calendar with an eraser and good manners. If Raghavan was raising the accountant in open court, either she had a source Tamsin didn't, or she was baiting the same trap Tamsin was.

Or both were being fed by the same hand.

* * *

The morning's first witness was the family physician, Dr. Ellen Cho, a small precise woman who answered questions the way a metronome keeps time. Yes, she had treated Odile Ferrant for hypertension for eleven years. Yes, the condition was managed. No, she had seen no indication of cardiac risk warranting sudden failure. No, she had not been surprised by the death certificate's conclusion, because — and here her voice acquired an edge fine enough to shave with — "I was not asked to perform an autopsy, Detective Reyes was not permitted to order one, and the family declined independent review. I signed what the process allowed me to sign. What I signed is not the same as what I believe."

The gallery made a sound like a wave deciding whether to break.

Tamsin's pen stopped. She looked at the witness and thought, with a clarity that felt almost physical: *Someone paid for that certificate to say heart failure. Someone with reach into the medical examiner's office or the family's consent rights, or both.* And the list of people with that kind of reach was short enough to memorize and long enough to include a retired detective who'd spent thirty years learning exactly which levers moved which offices.

Ambrose Kettle's name sat in her chest these days like a swallowed stone. She carried it everywhere. She slept with it. She'd spent three nights drafting and redrafting the letter she'd send him — *I know, I know, I know* — and three mornings burning them in the sink, because a letter was evidence, and evidence was a language Ambrose spoke better than anyone alive, and she would not hand him the pen.

Not yet. Not until she had something he couldn't argue his way out of.

* * *

They broke for lunch at 12:40, and the rotunda became a market. Reporters worked their phones against the pillars; lawyers clustered in knots of murmured strategy; spectators dissected testimony with the confidence of people who'd watched four seasons of a legal drama. Tamsin bought coffee she didn't want, for the prop of holding something, and stood near the notice board pretending to read a fire evacuation map.

"You're the taxi woman."

The voice came from her left. She turned and found a reporter — mid-forties, corduroy jacket, press credential on a lanyard he wore like a license to loiter. His eyes had the particular shine of a man who'd found his story and knew it.

"I'm a dispatcher," Tamsin said.

"Sure." He smiled. "Marcus Idowu, *Courier*. You're the one who called in the discrepancy at the conservatory. The locked-door detail. Reyes's report cites an 'unnamed consultant.'" He said the word *consultant* the way other men said *mistress* — with relish and a little envy. "Three deaths in five years, three closed cases, one anonymous expert. That's a hell of a hobby."

"Is there a question?"

"Just making conversation." He sipped his own coffee. "Funny thing about this trial. Everyone in that courtroom is arguing about a will. But everybody in this hallway is wondering about a murder. And you—" he tipped his cup toward her, friendly as a knife, "—you're the only person who appears in both stories."

He walked off before she could decide whether to be flattered or afraid, which was probably the point. She stood with her cooling coffee and understood, with the claustrophobic certainty of a ceiling lowering, that she was no longer investigating from the shadows. The shadows had been repossessed. From here to the end of the trial, every eye in the building that wasn't fixed on the witness stand would eventually swing toward her — and one pair of those eyes belonged to a man who needed her exactly where she was: visible, implicated, and quiet.

*Day one,* she wrote when she got back to her seat. *I am now part of the record.*

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the housekeeper.

Her name was Petra Voss, sixty-one years old, twenty-two years in service at the Ferrant estate, and she testified with the flat courage of someone who had decided the truth was simply a task to complete, like scrubbing a floor. Yes, Mrs. Ferrant had been frightened in her final weeks. Yes, there had been a man — Mrs. Ferrant had called him "the accountant," never a name — whom she'd met twice in the garden room with the door locked. Yes, Mrs. Ferrant had changed the locks on her study in March. Yes, she had started sleeping with a bell pull rigged to the headboard, "like a hotel, madam said, except the hotel is my own house."

"And did Mrs. Ferrant say whom she feared?" Raghavan asked.

Petra Voss's hands folded and unfolded in her lap. "She said the trouble with family is you can't fire them."

The gallery laughed — a short, guilty bark — and the judge let it live for exactly two seconds before silencing it.

On cross, Wren was gentle, almost tender, walking the housekeeper back from the cliff edge of implication: Mrs. Ferrant had been dramatic, hadn't she, all her life? Had enjoyed an entrance, a mystery, a well-placed suspicion? Petra Voss agreed to each point without conceding any of them, the way a wall agrees to hold up a picture.

Tamsin watched Julian during the exchange. He didn't look at the housekeeper. He looked at his hands, and his jaw worked in a slow rhythm, and Tamsin — who had spent years reading faces across a dispatch board, listening to voices lie about where they'd been and when — saw something she couldn't file neatly. Not guilt. Grief, maybe. Or the specific exhaustion of a man who suspected the truth and dreaded it equally whichever way it fell.

*Julian's alibi is too clean,* she reminded herself. *Timestamped by my own board.* Someone had built his innocence out of her own system, brick by brick, and the fact that he might also be genuinely innocent — that the frame might be a gift rather than a weapon — changed nothing about the architecture. Someone had still built it.

* * *

At 4:15, Judge Okafor adjourned for the day and the courtroom exhaled into chaos. Tamsin stayed seated, letting the crowd drain past her pillar, watching the principals gather their papers. Raghavan leaned close to Marisol and spoke without moving her lips much. Wren shook Julian's hand and held it a beat too long. And at the rail, talking to a bailiff with easy familiarity, stood Detective Reyes — plainclothes, notebook in pocket, attending a civil proceeding about a will with the focused boredom of a man hunting something else entirely.

Their eyes met across the emptying room.

Reyes didn't smile. Didn't nod. Just held the look long enough to communicate a message Tamsin translated without effort: *I know why you're here. I haven't decided yet whether that makes you useful or finished.*

Then Reyes touched two fingers to the brim of an imaginary hat — the smallest possible courtesy — and walked out through the side door.

Tamsin gathered her coat and notebook and joined the river of bodies moving toward the rotunda, and it was there, under the painted sky of the dome, in the noise and the camera flashes and the churn of strangers, that she felt it land: the true shape of her situation, arriving all at once like cold water.

Five days. The retainer's window closed with the trial. Whatever her client had scheduled — whatever second act the sealed file contained — it would happen before the jury foreman stood up. The accountant was still missing. The pension-fund thread was still frayed and dangerous. And somewhere in this building or beyond it, a man who taught her everything she knew about reading a case file was reading hers.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. She checked it in the shadow of a column.

Unknown number. No text. Just a photograph.

It took her a moment to understand what she was looking at: her own dispatch office, shot from across the street, night angle, the light above the door glowing. Her office. Empty. Taken sometime in the last few hours, while she'd sat in a courtroom learning things she already knew.

Below the image, a single line of text:

*You should check your files. Some pages are missing. One was added.*

Tamsin stood very still while the crowd flowed around her like water around a stone, and the dome's painted sky seemed suddenly very far away, and very small.
