## 7. What Marguerite Knew

The dictaphone had belonged to her husband, Gerald Ashcombe, who had used it for exactly two purposes: composing his memoirs, which no one wanted, and leaving angry messages for his solicitor, which everyone dreaded. It was a heavy old machine, brass-cased, with a red button that required the confidence of a woman who had spent seventy years pressing buttons and being obeyed.

Marguerite pressed it now, alone in the blue drawing room at half past ten in the morning, with the fire lit against an October that had arrived early and intended to stay.

"Testing," she said. "One, two. If this plays back sounding like a ghost, so much the better. Ghosts are taken more seriously than old women."

She stopped the tape. Played it back. Her voice emerged thin and papery, but unmistakably hers — the voice of a woman who had once made three barristers apologize in the same afternoon.

"Good," she said to the empty room. "Now. Where does one begin?"

She had rehearsed this, of course. She rehearsed everything; it was why she was still alive at eighty-one, when by all medical estimation she should have been dead twice over. But rehearsal, she was discovering, is a coward's comfort. You can say a thing perfectly to a mirror. The mirror never asks you why you waited forty years.

* * *

Quentin Winterbourne came at noon, as she'd instructed through Mrs. Brack, the housekeeper, who had served the family long enough to know that instructions from Marguerite were less like requests and more like weather.

He arrived looking, Marguerite thought with some satisfaction, like a man who had not slept well. Good. Sleepless lawyers think more clearly than rested ones, provided you catch them before they've fortified themselves with coffee and cynicism.

"You wanted to see me," he said, standing just inside the door with his coat still on, as though he might need to leave quickly. A sensible instinct. She wished she could tell him how right he was.

"I wanted to *tell* you something," she said. "Sitting down is optional. Listening is not."

He sat. He sat the way careful men sit in unfamiliar rooms — on the edge of the chair, knees together, briefcase within reach of one hand. She found herself almost fond of him already, which annoyed her. Fondness was a leak in the hull, and she could not afford leaks. Not now. Not with so little water left in her to sink.

"You've read the will again," she said. "I assume. Several times."

"I've read it."

"And?"

"And I have questions about clause numbering that don't behave like clause numbering."

Marguerite laughed — a dry sound, like a branch giving way. "Oh, you're better than Gerald said. He said you were competent but joyless. He said that about everyone, of course. Including himself, which showed unusual self-awareness for him." She gestured at the dictaphone on the table between them. "Do you know what that is?"

"A recorder."

"It was my husband's. He dictated his memoirs into it. Four hundred pages about drainage improvements to the home farm." She let that sit. "You will be pleased to know my own use of it will be considerably shorter, and considerably more interesting."

Quentin's eyes went to the machine, then to her face, then back to the machine. She watched him do the arithmetic lawyers do — the weighing of what a recording might mean for his fee, his liability, his sleep. He was quick. Not brilliant, perhaps, but quick, and honest in the way of men who are honest mostly because lying requires energy they'd rather spend elsewhere.

"Mrs. Ashcombe—"

"Marguerite. If you're going to hear what I'm about to say, you may as well hear it from someone on first-name terms with their own sins."

"Marguerite." He said it carefully, as if testing whether the floor would hold. "Before you begin. Whatever this is — whatever you intend to put on that tape — I should tell you that anything you record implicating yourself, I may be obliged to—"

"To report? To whom, Mr. Winterbourne? The police?" She smiled at him, and saw him understand, slowly, the shape of the thing. "The statute of limitations on what I'm about to confess has expired. That is precisely the obscenity of it. What I did cannot be prosecuted. Only inherited."

* * *

She began, as she had decided to begin, not with the night itself but with the money. Money was the language the Ashcombes spoke most fluently; even confession sounded more credible in it.

"My father-in-law built this family's fortune on two things," she said. "Timber, and the certainty that the law is a door that opens for those who can afford the handle. Gerald inherited both. I married into both. And in the spring of 1979, both were threatened by a housekeeper named Lorna Pell, who fell — or was pushed, or stumbled, the distinction mattered enormously to us and not at all to her — from the east stair landing at Ashcombe Hall."

Quentin did not move. She gave him credit for that. Most men would have flinched at *pushed*, would have looked away or leaned forward, would have done something. He simply listened, the way she imagined good doctors listen — cataloguing symptoms without recoiling from them.

"The coroner called it an accident," she went on. "The coroner was paid, indirectly, in the way such men are always paid — not with envelopes, Mr. Winterbourne, we were never vulgar — but with appointments, recommendations, a quiet share arrangement his brother-in-law found very agreeable. But there was a complication. There is always a complication. The complication was Thomas Reeve, the gardener, who had been seen arguing with Lorna that afternoon, and who had the misfortune to be poor, foreign-born, and inconveniently present."

"The ledger says payments began six weeks after the verdict," Quentin said quietly.

"The ledger says correctly. My husband wanted to pay Reeve's counsel to lose. I told him that was wasteful. Better to pay them to win badly — to win in a way that looked like winning while ensuring every avenue of appeal closed like a well-oiled drawer." She heard her own voice flatten into the register she'd used in boardrooms for thirty years, and hated it, and continued anyway. "I hired the lawyers, Mr. Winterbourne. Not Gerald. Gerald wanted to, but Gerald dithered, and dithering in matters of that kind produces loose ends. So I interviewed three firms and selected the one whose senior partner owed me a favor dating back to a land dispute in 1971, and I instructed them, and they did exactly what I paid them to do. Thomas Reeve went to prison for nine years for a death he did not cause. He served seven. He came out with nothing, because we had also — I did this part personally — arranged for his cottage tenancy to be terminated while he was inside. One does not want the aggrieved living within sight of the house."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a clock struck the quarter hour, patient and idiotic.

"And Ruth Pell?" Quentin asked.

"Ah. Ruth." Marguerite closed her eyes briefly. This was the part she had not rehearsed, because rehearsal had failed her here every time. "Ruth was nineteen. She adored her sister. And Ruth saw something that night — I have never known precisely what, and I have spent forty years not knowing, which is its own punishment, and I recommend it to no one. We bought her silence. Monthly. Forever. It seemed humane at the time, which tells you everything about how people like us think. We didn't buy her silence the way one buys a thing. We bought it the way one buys a hostage's cooperation — indefinitely, and with the unspoken understanding that the alternative was available."

"And last month the payments stopped."

Something cold moved through her chest that had nothing to do with her heart, though her heart took the opportunity to remind her it was failing on schedule. "Yes," she said. "That is the question, isn't it. I did not stop them. Gerald is eleven years dead. Which means someone in this house found the ledger, understood it, and made a decision." She opened her eyes and looked at Quentin directly. "And whoever made that decision believes, as I once believed, that a witness who won't die can simply be *closed*. Like an account."

* * *

She stopped the tape then, because her hands were shaking, and shook hands make a sound on a recording, and she refused to be recorded shaking. Pride, Quentin might have called it. She preferred to call it stagecraft. A confession delivered steadily convicts; a confession delivered trembling merely pities.

They sat a moment in silence. Outside the window, October light lay flat on the lawn like something spilled.

"Why now?" Quentin asked finally. It was the question she'd been waiting for, and she was pleased — genuinely pleased — that he'd gotten there himself rather than needing it fed to him.

"Because I am dying, Mr. Winterbourne, and dying slowly, and slow dying is the only honest mirror there is. You have time, in a sickbed, to count things. I counted. Forty years of monthly payments: four hundred and eighty envelopes. Seven years of a man's life stolen: two thousand five hundred and fifty-five days. One sister dead on a staircase: incalculable, and I have tried to calculate it every way there is." She folded her hands. "And I discovered that guilt, left alone, doesn't fade. It compounds. Like everything else in this family."

"So the will. The murder you predicted. Your own murder, dated three days in the future."

"Yes."

"That wasn't a prophecy."

"It was a *mechanism*." She felt the old sharpness come back and welcomed it like an old friend arriving slightly drunk. "I knew that if I simply confessed, it would go nowhere. Confessions from dying matriarchs are filed under sentiment. But a will that names a murder — a document, dated, witnessed, specific — forces the law to look. Forces *you* to look. And I chose you, Mr. Winterbourne, because you are honest enough to be unpredictable and ambitious enough to be thorough. The perfect instrument. Do forgive the phrasing; I have spent my life acquiring instruments."

"And if your prediction comes true?" His voice was very level. "If someone in this house decides that a dying woman with a dangerous tape is a problem to be solved before Thursday?"

"Then the mechanism works perfectly," Marguerite said, "and the murderer walks directly into the frame I built. Either way, the truth comes out. Either way, the Ashcombes end. That was always the design." She paused, and allowed herself one small honesty among the large ones. "Though I confess I hoped, rather childishly, to survive long enough to watch."

Quentin stood. He picked up his briefcase, then set it down again, which she noted with interest.

"One more question," he said. "Lorna Pell. Did she fall, or was she pushed?"

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. The fire had burned low. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled around its secrets the way it had for two hundred years.

"I have asked myself that question every day for forty years," she said, "and I will give you the answer I have earned, which is the only honest one left in me: I don't know. I wasn't there." She met his eyes. "But I have recently begun to suspect that someone else was. Someone whose name appears nowhere in my ledger — because I never knew to write it down."

Quentin frowned. "What do you mean?"

"I mean, Mr. Winterbourne, that when I checked the trust records yesterday, the payments to Ruth Pell did not simply stop." She reached out and stopped the dictaphone, and let the silence land. "They were redirected. Every remaining penny of them — to an account I have never seen before, opened last month, in this county, under a name that means nothing to me and I suspect everything to you. Find out who has been collecting a dead woman's hush money, and you will find out who in this house knows exactly what happened on that staircase — and what they intend to do about the last witness who remembers it."
