## 8. The Stopped Payments

The morning after my visit to Ruth Pell, I did what any sensible lawyer does when confronted with evidence of a forty-year conspiracy: I made tea and rearranged my desk.

Not because I was avoiding the problem. Because the problem had a shape, and I am a man who cannot think until the shape is on paper. My father, a solicitor of the old school, used to say that a case was like a hedge — you couldn't prune what you couldn't see. He said this while pruning an actual hedge, once, through the study window, which is why the rosebush died. But the principle holds.

I took a fresh sheet of foolscap and wrote at the top, in my careful drafting hand: **ASHCOMBE — WORKING NOTES (NOT FOR DISCOVERY)**.

Then I wrote down everything I knew, and the everything was alarmingly short.

One: a gardener named Thomas Reeve had gone to prison in 1979 for the death of a housekeeper named Lorna Pell, and the Ashcombe family's lawyers had made very sure of it.

Two: a woman named Ruth Pell — sister, witness, silence — had been paid monthly for four decades, and the payments had stopped.

Three: a will dated three days in the future predicted a murder, and the murdered woman was my client, and my client was paying me to prevent it, which is the sort of sentence that makes one's tea go cold in the cup.

I stared at the page. The tea went cold in the cup.

* * *

The question, when I finally let myself look at it straight, was not *who stopped the payments*. That answer, I suspected, was already sitting in the east wing of Ashcombe Hall, wearing a tweed jacket and an expression of patient stewardship.

The question was *what did Edmund know, and when did he know it*.

I had met the eldest Ashcombe three times now, and each time I came away with the same impression: a man who had spent his whole life managing an inheritance the way one manages a chronic illness — carefully, gratefully, and with a private dread of the day the symptoms worsen. He was not stupid. That was the trouble. Vivian was clever, but her cleverness was ornamental, deployed like the good china. Casper was clever in the way of a man who has learned to watch. But Edmund was *thorough*, and thoroughness is the quality that finds ledgers.

Because that was the other thing I had to face. The ledger in the marginalia — Marguerite's hidden arithmetic of guilt — was not hidden from the family. It was hidden in *her own will*, in her own rooms, in her own house. And Edmund, as executor-designate and self-appointed keeper of the estate's papers, had access to all three.

I telephoned my clerk, Dawes, who answers the phone in the manner of a man who resents the invention.

"Dawes. I need you to do something slightly irregular."

"Mr. Winterbourne, everything you ask me to do is slightly irregular. It's why I drink."

"Find out who handles the Ashcombes' private banking. Not the estate accounts — the personal ones. Discreetly."

A pause, in which I could hear him deciding how much to charge me for the word *discreetly*. "And what am I looking for, sir, once I've found them?"

"A payment schedule that stopped last month. Monthly sum, forty years' standing, payee a Mrs. Ruth Pell."

Another pause, longer. "That's a long-running arrangement," he said, and I could hear the lawyer in him waking up like an old dog hearing the leash. "Forty years is not sentiment, sir. Forty years is a contract."

"Exactly," I said. "And someone has breached it."

* * *

I drove out to Ashcombe Hall that afternoon under a sky the color of wet slate, which felt appropriate. The house always did that thing great houses do — pretending to be smaller than it is, the way a large dog curls up apologetically on a small rug.

Marguerite was in the morning room, wrapped in a shawl that had probably witnessed more history than most museums, playing patience. She played patience the way she did everything: with total concentration and visible contempt for chance.

"You look like a man with news," she said, without looking up.

"I look like a man who hasn't slept."

"You look like that most days. It's why I hired you. A rested lawyer is a lawyer who thinks he understands things." She turned over a card, scowled at it, and set it aside with the finality of a judge passing sentence. "Sit down, Quentin. Tell me about Ruth Pell."

I sat. "You know I've seen her."

"Of course I know. I designed the ledger so that someone would see her. I simply didn't expect it to take you a week. I'd begun drafting a letter to your professional body."

"Your generosity is noted."

"Your slowness is noted back." But there was no real edge in it. She gathered the cards with hands that trembled slightly — the tremor had been getting worse, and we both pretended it hadn't, in the way of two professionals maintaining a fiction for each other's benefit. "Tell me."

So I told her. The stopped payments. The fear in Ruth Pell's narrow sitting room, the way she'd kept every envelope, every receipt, forty years of paper filed like ammunition. The way she'd said, *they've stopped the money, Mr. Winterbourne, and in my experience when people stop paying you to be quiet, it's because they've decided you'll be quiet anyway.*

Marguerite listened with her eyes closed, and I had the unsettling sense that she was checking my account against some private transcript of her own.

"Edmund," she said at last.

"You can't know that."

"I can know it perfectly well. Edmund found the ledger in March. He came to my rooms with it in his hand — he'd gone through my papers while I was in hospital, which I permitted, incidentally, because I wanted him to find it. He stood exactly where you're sitting and asked me what it was." She opened her eyes. They were pale and clear and terribly tired. "I told him it was an accounting of old debts. He said, *Mother, this is a list of payments to a witness.* He is not a subtle man, but he is a precise one."

"And then?"

"And then he asked me whether the woman was still alive." Marguerite smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen her make — a small, cold, satisfied thing. "That was the question he chose. Not *why* were we paying her. Not *what did she see*. Whether she was still *alive*. I have raised three children, Quentin, and I can tell you that a man reveals himself in his second question, not his first. The first question is manners. The second is the man."

I wrote it down, because writing things down is how I keep my hands from doing anything else.

"You wanted this," I said. "You let him find the ledger so he'd go to her."

"I let him find the ledger so that whatever he decided to do about her, he would do it *before* I died, while I was still here to see it, and while you —" she tapped one trembling finger toward me — "were already retained, already suspicious, and already, I hoped, fond enough of being right to make a nuisance of yourself."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes," she said comfortably. "It's also the only kind of trap that works on my family. They've spent forty years being careful. Careful people aren't caught by evidence; they're caught by *temptation*. I removed the payments, Quentin. I instructed the bank myself, a month ago. I stopped Ruth Pell's money and waited to see which of my children would come to finish the job."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the house a clock did its patient work.

"And did one come forward?"

"Not forward," Marguerite said. "That's the trouble with my children. They only ever come *around*."

* * *

I found Edmund in the estate office, which was the one room in Ashcombe Hall that looked like it belonged to the twentieth century — ledgers of the ordinary, legal kind, a filing system, a telephone with a long curly cord that he wound around one finger while he talked.

He wound it around his finger now, though he wasn't talking to anyone. He was standing at the window looking out at the walled garden, and when I came in he didn't startle, which told me he'd heard the car.

"Mr. Winterbourne." He turned. "Mother's been enjoying your visits, I think. She's been almost cheerful. It's very wearing."

"I imagine cheer in a dying woman is difficult for the household."

"Cheer in a dying woman with *plans* is difficult." He unwound the cord from his finger with great deliberation. "You've been to see Ruth Pell."

Not a question. I gave it the respect of not pretending to be one. "How do you know that?"

"Because I went to see her first." He said it flatly, without pride, which somehow made it worse. "Two weeks ago. After I found the ledger. I wanted to know what she'd seen."

"And what did she tell you?"

"Nothing. She's a tough old bird. Forty years of envelopes and she never once told me anything except that the tea was too weak." He moved to the desk and sat behind it, and I noticed — because noticing is my trade — that his hand wasn't quite steady on the blotter. "But I'll tell you what I learned anyway, since you'll only dig it up yourself and charge the estate for the privilege. I learned that my mother has been paying a witness to my family's crime since before I could drive. And I learned that she stopped the payments a month ago, which means she's decided the account is closed. One way or another."

"Those are your mother's words?"

"Those are my mother's *implications*. She's never said anything plainly in her life. It's how she's kept us all dancing for eighty years." He looked at me then, and for a moment the steward's mask slipped and I saw the boy underneath — a boy who had grown up in a house where the gardeners were criminals and the family were victims, because that was the story he'd been fed with his meals. "What do you want, Winterbourne? Truly. Not the lawyer answer."

"I want to prevent a murder," I said. "Your mother's will predicts one. I'm retained to prove it won't happen."

"And you think it's me." He said it wearily, without outrage, which frightened me more than outrage would have. "You've got a suspect list of three and I'm the one who found the ledger, so it's me. Vivian would be too squeamish and Casper's too new. That leaves the eldest son, the dull one, the one who actually knows where the keys are."

"Is it you?"

And here is the thing I will remember about Edmund Ashcombe longer than anything else in this affair: he laughed. A short, genuine, appalled laugh, the laugh of a man who has just seen the shape of his own life from outside.

"No," he said. "God help me, no. But I'll tell you what I *did* do, because you'll find out anyway. After Ruth Pell wouldn't talk, I sat in my car outside her cottage for an hour trying to decide what a man does about a witness who won't die. And then I drove home, and I poured a drink, and I went to my mother and I asked her to *fix it*. To pay the woman again, to move her somewhere, to do whatever she'd been doing for forty years — because I am not a murderer, Winterbourne, I'm a *manager*, and management is all I have ever been good for." He pressed both hands flat on the desk. "And my mother looked at me and said, *Edmund, the account is closed*, and smiled, and I have not slept properly since, because I know that smile. It's the smile she wore at my father's funeral."

I stood there in the estate office with the rain starting against the window, and I believed him. That was the horror of it. I believed him completely, which meant the ledger had done its work: Edmund had found the witness, failed to move her, and reported his failure to the one person in the house who might act.

Which left the question standing in the room like a third person.

If Edmund knew and did nothing, and Marguerite had engineered the whole thing as bait — then someone else had taken it.

* * *

I went looking for Casper and was told by Mrs. Halloway, the housekeeper, that the young gentleman had gone out walking and not said when he'd be back. This was delivered in the tone housekeepers reserve for young gentlemen generally, a tone containing forty years of mud on floors.

Vivian, I was told, was in the drawing room writing letters.

I found her at the writing desk by the window, and I will say this for Vivian Ashcombe: she wrote a beautiful hand, and she did not stop writing when I came in, which was either rudeness or a message.

"Mrs. Ashcombe."

"Mr. Winterbourne. Do come in. I'm nearly finished." She finished, blotted the page with a small gold instrument, and looked up with the pleasant attention of a woman who has never once been surprised in her life and resents the suggestion that she might be. "You've been busy. Ruth Pell, was it? The old woman from the village?"

"You know about that too."

"Everyone knows everything in this house. It's the one inheritance that's shared equally." She rose and moved to the fire, holding her hands to it though it was not a cold day. "You should be careful, Mr. Winterbourne. My mother collects clever men the way she collects first editions — she enjoys them enormously and then she puts them on a shelf."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a *cataloguing*." She turned, and the firelight did something kind to her face that her eyes declined to join. "You're wondering who stopped Ruth Pell's money. Everyone is. Edmund thinks it was Casper. Casper thinks it was Edmund. I think —" and here she smiled, briefly, and the smile had a crack in it, fine as a hairline fracture in good china — "I think it was my mother, and I think whatever game she is playing, it has a board we haven't seen yet, and pieces we haven't counted."

"And you're not curious what's on the board?"

"Mr. Winterbourne." She picked up her letters, squared their edges against the desk. "I grew up in this house. Curiosity is the one luxury we were never allowed. Do give my regards to Ruth Pell, if you see her again. And do give her my *particular* regards —" the crack in the smile widened by a millimeter — "and see that she stays well."

She left the room with her letters, and I stood by the fire doing arithmetic I did not like.

*See that she stays well.* From Vivian, that was either courtesy or a threat, and Vivian Ashcombe did not waste words on courtesy.

* * *

That evening I sat in my room at the Hall — Marguerite had insisted I stay, over the mild objection of the entire household — and added the day's findings to my foolscap.

**The ledger is bait. The payments stopped by design. Edmund found the witness, failed, confessed his failure. Vivian knows everything and has told me nothing, except to note the witness's health. Casper is unaccounted for between four and seven o'clock.**

I looked at the list for a long time.

The murder Marguerite had predicted was no longer an abstraction in a badly dated document. It had a shape now. It had a house, a family, a witness, a motive forty years old and a motive forty days old. Somewhere in this building, or out on the dark lanes between the Hall and the village, someone was doing the arithmetic of Ruth Pell's remaining value and finding the sum inconvenient.

And Marguerite, in her room at the end of the corridor, was lying awake waiting for it — because that, I understood at last, was the true horror of her design. She hadn't predicted her murder. She had *scheduled* it, and then hidden the schedule from herself, so that even she could not call it off.

A knock at the door. Soft. Twice.

I opened it to find Casper Ashcombe in the corridor, in the dark, holding a torch he hadn't switched on, and his face in the gloom was not the pleasant blankness he wore at dinner.

"Mr. Winterbourne," he said quietly. "I think we should talk. Not here, and not about what you think. About what's in the west copse, behind the garden wall."

"What's in the west copse?"

"Freshly turned earth," said Casper, "and a shovel that someone put back in the wrong place."
