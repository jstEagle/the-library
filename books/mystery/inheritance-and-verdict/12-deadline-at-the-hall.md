## 12. Deadline at the Hall

The trouble with a house that watches you, Quentin decided on the third morning of his stay at Ashcombe Hall, is that it never blinks. Doors he had closed stood open. Chairs he had angled toward the fire had been squared to the walls. Even the grandfather clock in the corridor seemed to tick a half-second after he passed it, like a footman counting his steps.

He was becoming the sort of man who noticed things like that. He wasn't sure he liked him.

Quentin had been an estate lawyer for twenty-two years, and in that time he had developed a professional philosophy he liked to call *aggressive indifference*: care about nothing too much, and nothing can be taken from you. It had served him well through two divorces (one legal, one emotional), the slow death of his practice's better angels, and any number of clients who wept over bequests of candlesticks. But aggressive indifference required a certain ignorance, and Marguerite Ashcombe had stripped that from him like paint from old wood.

He knew things now. He knew about Thomas Reeve, convicted in 1979 of a manslaughter he almost certainly hadn't committed. He knew about Lorna Pell, dead at twenty-six on the ice behind the boathouse, and about Ruth Pell, paid monthly for forty years to keep her sister's truth in a drawer. And he knew — because Casper had shown him, with the grim satisfaction of a man handing over a live grenade — that the ledger's final entries were in Marguerite's own hand.

*Schedule: M.A., cardiac event, expected between the 14th and 17th.*

*Instrument: digoxin substitution. Already effected.*

Today was the fifteenth.

"Cheerful reading," Quentin muttered to the ceiling of his room, which did not answer but seemed, somehow, to take notes.

* * *

Marguerite received him in the blue drawing room at eleven, propped in her wingback chair like a queen holding court in a country that had already fallen. She was thinner than she'd been a week ago. Her hands, resting on a lap rug the color of storm clouds, trembled in a way they hadn't before — or perhaps they always had, and he'd only now learned to look.

"You look terrible," Quentin said, because she had made it clear early on that flattery would be treated as a breach of contract.

"I look *punctual*," she said. "There's a difference. Sit down, Mr. Winterbourne. You have the face of a man who has been doing arithmetic all night."

"I've been doing worse than arithmetic." He sat across from her, close enough to see the fine map of veins at her temple. "I've been doing chemistry. Your heart medication has been swapped. The ledger says so. You say so. Which means somewhere in this house there is a bottle of pills that will stop your heart on schedule, and I intend to find it and un-find it."

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear. Something older and more tired than fear.

"And if you can't?"

"Then I'll stand outside your door with a defibrillator and a thermos of coffee until the seventeenth."

"How heroic. Do bring marshmallows." She coughed then, a small dry sound like paper tearing, and waved off his half-rise with two fingers. "Mr. Winterbourne. May I tell you something that will annoy you?"

"You usually do."

"The swap was my idea. The *plan* was mine. But I don't know who executed it." She let that settle. "I wrote the script. Someone else took the part."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"It's supposed to make you think. I designed a trap with three doors, and I genuinely do not know which heir walked through it. That was rather the point. A confession you force is worth less than one you catch." Her voice thinned. "But I confess the experiment has grown teeth. I find myself lying awake wondering whether I am the author of my death or merely its first draft."

"Charming. Write that down," Quentin said. "It'll look lovely in the published confession."

For a moment — just a moment — she smiled at him like a grandmother, and it was so startlingly human that he forgot, briefly, to be afraid of her. Then the smile folded away and she was Marguerite Ashcombe again, chessboard and all.

"Go and save my life, Mr. Winterbourne. Try not to enjoy it too much."

* * *

The problem, as Quentin laid it out on his desk that afternoon with the methodical despair of a man alphabetizing a fire, was evidence chain of custody.

He had the swapped bottle. He'd taken it from Marguerite's medicine tray four days ago — plain white pharmacy vial, label immaculate, contents subtly wrong. Casper had helped him verify it: the tablets were the right shape, the right color, the right imprint, and the wrong drug. Digoxin where the prescription read something gentler. A forgery good enough to fool a nurse, a coroner, and God.

He had photographed it, bagged it, logged it in a small notebook with the fussy precision of a man who had once watched a murder conviction collapse because someone forgot to initial a page. The vial lived in his leather evidence bag, which lived locked in the bottom drawer of his desk, which lived in his room, which lived in a house full of people rehearsing for a funeral.

At four o'clock he went to check on it.

The lock was intact. The drawer slid open with its usual reluctant groan. The bag sat where he'd left it, fat and patient.

His stomach knew before his hands did. Twenty-two years of estate law teaches a man many useless things and one useful thing: the weight of paper. The bag was light.

He opened it with fingers that felt borrowed from someone else.

Inside: his notebook. His photographs. His spare cufflinks, because he was a lawyer and even his paranoia was organized. And where the vial should have been — a hollow, a shape, an absence with edges.

"No," Quentin said aloud, to the room, to the house, to whatever was listening. "No, no, no."

He turned the bag upside down. He shook it. He checked the lining, the pockets, the zip seam, the place where the handle attached, as though a vial of substituted heart medication might reasonably hide behind a stitch. He unpacked the entire drawer onto the bed and repacked it twice.

Gone. Cleanly, completely gone.

And here was the thought that arrived next, cold and unwelcome as a process server: whoever took it now held the one piece of physical proof that the swap existed. Better still — from their perspective — they held a piece of proof that pointed, if found in *his* possession, at *him*. A lawyer, newly retained by a dying woman, carrying a bottle of doctored pills. He could see the headline. He could practically hear the barrister's closing statement. He had spent a week studying how the Ashcombe machine had destroyed Thomas Reeve, and he recognized the parts list: motive, opportunity, and an object placed in the wrong hands.

"They're not stealing evidence," he told the empty room. "They're *planting* it. On me."

The radiator clanked. Somewhere below, a door closed with the soft deliberateness of a period drama.

* * *

He went looking for Casper first, because Casper was the only person in the house whose guilt Quentin had personally ruled out, and because ruling people out was a luxury that didn't survive contact with Ashcombe Hall.

He found him in the library, ostensibly cataloguing first editions, actually standing very still by the window with the expression of a man memorizing who came and went in the garden.

"My bag's been opened," Quentin said, without preamble. "The vial's gone."

Casper turned. For a heartbeat something flickered across his face that Quentin couldn't file — not surprise exactly. Confirmation, maybe. The look of a man whose worst estimate has been beaten.

"When?"

"Sometime since yesterday evening. Lock wasn't forced. Whoever it was had time, access, and either my key or patience with hairpins."

"Or a copy." Casper crossed to him, voice dropping. "You realize what this means."

"I realize several things, most of them bad. Enumerate yours."

"It means they know what you know. If they'd just wanted the pills out of the house, they'd have taken the whole bag — notebook, photographs, everything. They took the vial and left your records." Casper's jaw worked. "That's not destruction. That's *positioning*. They want the vial to surface later, in a place of their choosing, attached to your name."

"Yes, thank you, I got there during the shaking-the-bag phase."

"And there's a second thing." Casper hesitated, and Quentin watched a man decide how much truth a fragile alliance could carry. "Marguerite's been worse today. Dr. Hale came at noon. She wouldn't let him change the prescription."

"Of course she wouldn't. Changing the prescription ruins the experiment."

"She's not an experiment, Winterbourne. She's a woman with a failing heart and a deadline she wrote herself, and everyone in this house keeps talking about her like she's a plot device." There was real anger under it — old anger, inherited anger, the kind that skips generations like a gene. "Including you. Including me, probably."

Quentin absorbed that the way he absorbed most unpleasant truths: silently, and with a mental note to bill someone eventually.

"All right," he said. "New plan. We don't need the vial. We need the *pharmacy record*. That prescription was filled in the village. Somebody either altered the fill or supplied the substitute tablets, and both leave paper. Paper doesn't get stolen out of locked drawers."

"Paper gets burned."

"Not by people who need the vial to point at me, they don't. Think it through — if our friend burns the paper trail, the vial becomes worthless as a frame. They need the record intact and misleading." He was pacing now, three steps and turn, the way he paced court corridors. "Which means the record still exists, and still lies, and lies are documents, and documents—" he stopped, "—are *my* native habitat."

Casper almost smiled. "You're enjoying this."

"I'm *professionally engaged*," Quentin said with dignity. "There's a difference. Ask Marguerite; she invented it."

* * *

The village pharmacy closed at six. Quentin made the drive with the windows down despite the cold, partly for air and partly because he no longer trusted the acoustics of Ashcombe Hall — a building he was now fairly certain had ears in every cornice.

Mr. Tallow, the pharmacist, was a small round man with spectacles thick enough to start fires and the cheerful fatalism of someone who had dispensed to the same families for forty years.

"Ashcombe?" he said, pulling the ledger book — an actual paper ledger, God bless rural England — from beneath the counter. "Oh, aye. Mrs. Ashcombe's repeats come monthly. Been filling them since before your father was in short trousers, probably." He licked a thumb and turned pages. "Here we are. Digoxin, quarter-milligram, ninety tablets, filled on the third. Signed out by—" he squinted, "—Mrs. Danvers. The housekeeper. Regular as church."

Quentin leaned in. The entry was neat, initialed, unremarkable.

"And the previous month?"

Tallow turned back a page. Same drug, same quantity, same initials. And the month before. Quentin's hope performed a small graceful dive.

"Nothing altered," he said flatly.

"Altered? Good lord, no. Why would—" Tallow paused. He was a small man but not a stupid one, and forty years behind a counter teaches a fellow when a question has furniture behind it. "Mr. Winterbourne, is there something I ought to know?"

"There's something I ought to know, and I'm beginning to suspect it isn't here." Quentin straightened. One last idea, thin as thread. "Mr. Tallow. When Mrs. Danvers collects the prescription — does she collect *only* Mrs. Ashcombe's?"

Tallow's eyebrows climbed. He turned back through the ledger, page by page, month by month, running his finger down the signature column. Then he stopped.

"Well," he said slowly. "Well, well. Mrs. Danvers signs for the Ashcombe household generally. Has done for years. But look here — last month, and this month." He tapped the page. "She signed for a second item. No name against it, just the household account. And I remember now, because it was odd — she asked for it quiet-like, said the young master wanted it handled discreet. Tablets. Small quantity." He looked up over his spectacles. "I assumed it was for nerves. These big houses, you know. Everyone's got nerves."

"What tablets, Mr. Tallow?"

"Digoxin." The pharmacist frowned. "Separate supply. Quarter-milligram. Which struck me as strange at the time, because why would a household need two lots of the same—"

"—when one lot is already going to the same heart," Quentin finished quietly.

The shop was very silent. Outside, a delivery van rattled past, absurdly ordinary.

"Mr. Winterbourne," Tallow said, in the tone of a man stepping carefully off a ledge, "is Mrs. Ashcombe quite well?"

"No," Quentin said. "But she's exactly on schedule."

* * *

He drove back through lanes going purple with dusk, turning it over. So the second supply existed — a duplicate bottle, drawn discreetly, presumably the one now sitting in some heir's pocket or drawer while its twin sat in *his* former custody. The frame was nearly complete. All it needed was a body, a search, and a lawyer with suspiciously fresh fingerprints on the evidence.

His mobile buzzed against the passenger seat. Casper.

"Don't come in the front drive," Casper said, no greeting. "There's a police car at the gate."

Quentin's hands went very steady, the way they did in court, which he had long ago accepted was his body's cruelest joke.

"Whose car? Local constable?"

"No. County. Two officers, and a third man in a coat too nice for the weather. Edmund's meeting them on the steps himself." A pause. "Winterbourne — Edmund called them an hour ago. I heard him on the phone in the study. He told them there'd been a break-in at his mother's rooms."

"There hasn't been a break-in."

"No. But something's been reported stolen, and I'd wager my grandfather's good name — such as it is — that whatever it is, it's going to be found somewhere you've recently stood."

Quentin pulled the car onto the verge and sat, engine ticking, watching the lights of Ashcombe Hall glow through the trees like a stage awaiting its cast.

"Casper," he said. "Check my room. Carefully. If anything turns up in there that I didn't put there, photograph it before you touch it, then touch it anyway, because we may not get another chance."

He hung up and looked at the house for a long moment. Somewhere inside it, Marguerite Ashcombe lay listening to her own heart count down a schedule she had written herself, and somewhere else inside it, a bottle of tablets waited to be discovered in exactly the wrong pair of hands.

His, ideally. His would be convenient.

Quentin Winterbourne had spent his career on the tidy side of other people's disasters, drafting clauses about what happened *after*. Now he was inside the clause. And the thing about being inside the clause, he thought, pulling back onto the lane, is that you finally learn what the drafting really means — that every document is a weapon aimed at someone, and somebody, in this house, had drafted him.

The gate lamps swung into view. Behind them, on the wide stone steps, three figures stood waiting in the cold, and one of them was wearing a coat too nice for the weather, and smiling.
