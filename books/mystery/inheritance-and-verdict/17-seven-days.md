## 17. Seven Days

### I. Edmund

Edmund Ashcombe had spent forty years learning that every problem in life was, at bottom, a negotiation with insufficient documentation. His father had taught him this over ledgers and brandy. His mother had taught him the corollary: when the documentation is against you, improve it.

He stood at the window of his study watching Quentin Winterbourne cross the frost-killed lawn toward the gatehouse, where the lawyer had taken rooms "for the duration of the inquest," as though Ashcombe Hall were a hotel and he a paying guest rather than a vulture with a briefcase.

"He's still here," Edmund said to the room.

The room did not answer. Rooms rarely did, though lately his mother's study seemed to hum with everything unsaid.

He poured two fingers of the good whisky — the 1968, the one he'd been saving for a celebration that kept refusing to arrive — and considered the man walking away from him. Winterbourne was not what Edmund had expected. He'd expected either a coward or an idealist, both of which could be bought or broken. Instead he'd gotten something worse: a tired professional who read documents the way Edmund's father used to read faces, patiently, all the way through, including the parts you hoped he'd skip.

*Partnership,* Edmund thought. It wasn't even cynical, really. It was arithmetic. Winterbourne had the second will, the authority to convene the inquest, and — Edmund suspected — pieces of the ledger Edmund himself hadn't found. Edmund had the estate, the lawyers, and thirty years of knowing where every body in this family was buried, metaphorically speaking. Together they were worth more than apart. That was all inheritance ever was: the art of combining assets before they destroyed each other.

He drafted the offer himself, because he didn't trust the family solicitors not to leak, and because there was a certain pleasure in writing your own terms. *Ten percent of the gross estate, in perpetuity, plus named executorship.* He signed it with the fountain pen his grandfather had used to sign things that ruined other people's lives, which felt appropriate.

Then he walked down to the gatehouse in the rain, because some negotiations you conducted by letter, and some you conducted standing in a doorway while water dripped off your nose, so the other man could see exactly how much you wanted it.

"Winterbourne."

"Mr. Ashcombe." The lawyer didn't invite him in. He stood in the frame like a man guarding a small country.

"I've brought you a proposal." Edmund held out the envelope. "Ten percent. Executorship. Your name on the foundation paperwork if you want the philanthropy — I know your type, you'll want the philanthropy."

"My type," Quentin said.

"Lawyers who take cases for dying women out of season, in the country, for fees already paid. You're either a romantic or a gambler. Both types like a legacy."

Quentin took the envelope. Edmund watched him open it, read it with the maddening calm of a man pricing a horse, and then hold it out again.

"You've made an error," Quentin said.

"Have I?"

"It says 'in perpetuity.' Nothing about my profession is perpetual anymore, Mr. Ashcombe. The bar council is circling me as we speak — I assume at your sister's instigation. And nothing about this family is perpetual either. Your mother saw to that." He handed back the paper. "You're offering me a share of something she designed specifically to be taken apart."

"She was old," Edmund said. "Old people write strange wills. You know that better than anyone."

"She was precise," Quentin said. "That's much more frightening." He paused, and Edmund saw the lawyer do the thing lawyers do — the thing his own counsel did in depositions, the small settling of weight before the blade. "For what it's worth, Mr. Ashcombe, I looked into the estate accounts yesterday. There's a company called Pellmere Holdings. It has no employees, no premises, and it has paid out four hundred thousand pounds over six years to recipients I can't identify. If I were you, I'd spend less time negotiating with me and more time remembering what you signed."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"That's not a threat," Quentin added, almost kindly. "It's free advice. I charge for the rest."

He closed the door. Edmund stood on the step a moment longer than dignity allowed, then walked back across the lawn doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for three days: *Pellmere Holdings. Four hundred thousand. Who else knew?*

His mother had known. His mother had known everything, and had written it down, and had left it in a house full of people with reasons to burn it.

He went back to his study and, for the first time since childhood, locked the door behind him.

### II. Vivian

Vivian Ashcombe-Hale had been married to a judge long enough to know that guilt was not a feeling but a filing system. You did not experience guilt; you experienced the fear of discovery, which was a different animal entirely — colder, more organized, and far more useful.

She sat in the morning room with her tea going cold and composed her third letter to the Bar Standards Board. The first two had been too angry. Anger suggested stakes. The third was perfect: regretful, procedural, concerned for the reputation of the profession. *It has come to my attention that Mr. Quentin Winterbourne may have acted outside his professional competence in the drafting and execution of Mrs. Marguerite Ashcombe's testamentary instruments...*

Her husband would have hated the letter. Gerald believed in letting institutions work slowly, so that by the time they arrived, everyone had forgotten why they'd been sent. But Gerald was in London, safely distant from the house where his father-in-law's signature had once sent a gardener to prison, and Vivian had learned that distance was a luxury purchased by someone else's proximity.

Someone had to be close. Someone always had to be close.

Through the window she could see Casper in the walled garden, crouched by the herb beds like a crow examining roadkill. He'd been out there every morning since the reading of the second will, and every morning Vivian watched him, and every morning she asked herself the same question her mother had taught her to ask about men: *what does he actually want?*

Not justice. She'd seen justice wanted before — Ruth Pell's face at twenty-six, if you could call it a face under all that terror — and it looked nothing like Casper's patient, inventorying stare. Casper wanted *evidence*. Justice was what you called evidence after you'd won.

And Edmund wanted money, obviously, pathetically, the way Edmund had wanted money since he was nine years old and charged their cousins admission to the orchard. And Mother —

Mother had wanted something Vivian still couldn't name, and the not-naming of it was the thing that kept Vivian awake at night. A woman does not swap her own heart medication and document the swap unless she is punishing someone. The question that sat in Vivian's chest like a swallowed stone was: *punishing whom?*

Because Vivian had taken the pills. She knew what she'd done and why — Gerald's name, Gerald's court, the verdict that had built the Hale judicial reputation brick by brick on top of Thomas Reeve's ruined life — and she knew, now, that her mother had known she would do it. Had counted on it. Had perhaps arranged the whole household like a hand of cards and waited to see which one Vivian would play.

Which meant the pills themselves might be part of the design. Which meant —

Vivian set down her cup with a click that sounded, in the empty morning room, like a lock turning.

There was a knock. Winterbourne, of course. The man had the timing of a debt collector.

"Mrs. Hale. May I?"

"You may not, but you will." She gestured to a chair. "I assume Edmund offered you money."

"He did."

"And I'm offering you ruin, so between us we've covered the traditional incentives." She smiled the smile she used on difficult witnesses. "Let me save us both the afternoon. Whatever you think you have — ledger, testimony, recordings — it dies in a courtroom. My husband's colleagues sit on every bench in the county. The Bar Council will strip you by spring. The police will lose interest by summer. And the Ashcombe name will go on being the Ashcombe name, because names are the one thing in England that never goes to prison."

Winterbourne listened with his head slightly tilted, like a man hearing music through a wall.

"Finished?" he said.

"For now."

"Two things. First: I'm not taking any of this to a courtroom. Your mother's instrument doesn't ask for a courtroom. It asks for a room — this room, or one like it, with the family in it. So your husband's benches don't apply." He leaned forward. "Second: you warned me about Casper. You told me he isn't what he seems. That was generous, Mrs. Hale. Almost protective. And I've been wondering all week why a woman protecting her husband's legacy would bother protecting a stranger."

Vivian felt the stone in her chest shift.

"Unless," Quentin went on gently, "you weren't protecting him. You were warning me off him, because you know what he's looking for, and you know it's not the same thing you're afraid of. And a woman who knows the difference—" he stood, buttoned his coat, "—is a woman who knows exactly where the pills went."

He let himself out. Vivian sat very still among the cold tea and the chintz and the portrait of her grandmother, and admitted, in the privacy of her own filing system, that she had made an error.

She had assumed the danger was exposure.

But Winterbourne hadn't threatened exposure. He'd threatened *accuracy*. And accuracy, unlike a courtroom, could not be bought, delayed, or appealed.

That evening she went down to the still room off the kitchen, where the household medicines were kept, and stood a long time in front of the shelf where a brown glass bottle had sat for eleven days before she'd moved it.

The bottle was gone.

### III. Casper

Casper Reeve — and he permitted himself the name only inside his own skull, a private ceremony performed each morning like brushing teeth — had learned surveillance from a man in Marseille who'd learned it from a man in Algiers, and the first lesson in both curricula was the same: *the watcher must have a reason to be where he watches.*

His reason was herbs. He genuinely liked herbs. This was convenient.

From the walled garden he could see the gatehouse door, the morning-room windows, the kitchen yard, and the still-room's outer stair. In seven days of watching he had compiled, in a notebook disguised as a planting journal, a complete traffic report of the siege: Edmund's midnight visits to the study safe (three), Vivian's letters (four, posted in the village, never by herself), Winterbourne's trips to see the Pell woman (two, and on the second trip he'd come back changed — lighter, and heavier at once, the way men came back from real evidence).

And tonight, a new entry: the still-room light at eleven p.m., though Cook swore the room was never used after supper.

Casper put down his trowel and considered the shape of the week. His grandfather had rotted in a cell for nine years and died in a hostel bed with a state pension and a criminal record, and Casper had crossed half the world and forged half a life to stand in this garden holding a trowel, and the temptation — the daily, hourly temptation — was to stop being patient. To walk into the great hall and shout the truth at these silk-wrapped strangers until the walls rang with it.

Exposure at any cost. That had been the plan, once. Before he'd understood what cost meant here. Cost meant Ruth Pell, eighty-one years old, whose testimony would be shredded by lawyers who'd had forty years to practice shredding it. Cost meant a verdict overturned on technicality while the family issued a statement about *historical complexities*. Cost meant Thomas Reeve becoming a footnote in someone else's redemption arc.

No. If the truth was going to come out of this house, it would come out whole, in one piece, with the ledger and the recordings and the confession attached, or it wouldn't come out at all.

He climbed the still-room stair quietly, out of habit, and stopped at the top because the door was ajar and someone was inside.

Winterbourne. On his knees before the medicine shelf with a torch in his teeth, going through the bottles one by one, checking labels against a list, and the expression on his face was one Casper recognized from his own mirror: the look of a man who has just discovered that the game board has been rearranged while he slept.

"The brown bottle's gone," Winterbourne said, without turning around. He'd heard the stair. Of course he had.

"When?" Casper asked.

"Sometime today. Or it was never here, and the list my client gave me describes a bottle that existed only in her design." He sat back on his heels. "Either someone removed the evidence, or the evidence was never evidence. With your grandmother—" he caught himself, and Casper let the slip pass, filed it, treasured it — "with Mrs. Ashcombe, those are usually the same thing."

Casper came fully into the room. For a moment the two of them regarded each other across the shelves of labeled bottles — the lawyer and the impostor, the heir to the debt and the heir to the damage — and Casper felt the odd kinship he'd been resisting all week finally settle over him like a coat.

"Seven days," he said. "She gave us seven days. Do you think she picked the number at random?"

"No," Winterbourne said. "I think she picked it the way she picked everything. Precisely." He stood, joints cracking, a middle-aged man in a cold room at midnight, and Casper thought: *this is who she chose. Not a detective. A clerk. Someone who reads to the end.*

"There's one place none of us has looked," Casper said. "We've searched her rooms, the study, the safe, the chapel. We haven't searched the one room in this house that was hers twice over."

Winterbourne frowned. "Which room?"

"The nursery," Casper said. "Where she raised the children she was about to disinherit. Mothers hide things where they were happiest. Even mothers like ours."

They looked at each other, and somewhere below them a clock began striking twelve, and neither of them moved until it finished — because in this house, lately, you learned not to interrupt anything that was counting.
