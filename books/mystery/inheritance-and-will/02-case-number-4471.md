## 2. Case Number 4471

The envelope sat on my desk for three days before I touched it.

Not because I'd forgotten it. Because I couldn't stop feeling it. It was there the way a body is there in a river—you can look away, but the current keeps knocking it against your ankles. Fourteen hundred euros in used notes, soft as cloth from handling, and a single line of instruction in a hand so careful it looked printed: *Prove that Marta Serrano has not died. Payment on delivery of proof.*

I'd run dispatch long enough to know what money like that meant. Money that arrives before questions is never payment. It's a down payment on someone's fear.

The night shift thinned around me. At 3:14 a.m. I logged a fender-bender on the Avinguda del Port—no injuries, driver sober, just rain and bad luck. At 4:02 an old woman called because her husband hadn't come home and she was too proud to call it fear. I took her details and told her men his age always stopped for one more glass somewhere, and she laughed, and I lied well enough that she believed me, and by morning he'd be home or he wouldn't, but either way the night would have an ending. That was the job. Every call had an ending. Fifteen years of endings, catalogued in code, filed in my head like weather.

And then this man Emilio, with his soft voice and his shaking hands I could hear through the phone, handing me a case with no ending at all. A murder that hadn't happened yet. A dead woman who was, per the client, alive.

You don't prove a negative. Any cop will tell you that; any lawyer; any priest. You especially don't prove a death didn't happen when the courts already said it did. The whole architecture of the state had weighed in six years ago: trial, verdict, sentence. Tomás Serrano, guilty. Case closed, stamped, shelved.

So why couldn't I stop hearing his voice? *Please. Before the anniversary.* Like a man asking you to check the locks twice, knowing which night they're coming.

On the fourth night, I opened the archive request under a pretext I didn't examine too closely—a dispatcher verifying historical call records for a training module, which was even true, in the way most lies are true. The archives lived in the basement of the municipal records annex, a place I knew the way other people know churches: fluorescent hum, rows of grey cabinets, air that tasted of paper dust and cold concrete. The archivist, a woman named Berta who had watched me grow from a rookie dispatcher into whatever I was now, waved me toward the terminal without looking up from her crossword.

"Case number," she said.

"I don't have one yet."

"Then you have a name and a date, or you have nothing."

"Marta Serrano." I gave the date—the night six years ago that Emilio had called *the anniversary*, though the anniversary of what, exactly, he hadn't said. "Homicide."

Berta typed. Her fingers paused over the keys longer than typing takes.

"Serrano," she repeated. "That one had news coverage. Vineyard family, out past the hills." She looked at me then, over her glasses, with the flat professional gaze of someone who has processed every kind of grief the city produces and learned to sort it like mail. "Why does a dispatcher want a homicide file?"

"Training module," I said again, and she let it go, because Berta believed in letting people carry their own weight. She printed the retrieval slip and pointed me down the row where the closed cases slept.

* * *

Case Number 4471 came up in a box the color of old teeth.

I carried it to the reading table under the one lamp that worked and lifted the lid, and the smell rose out of it—that particular smell of a case file, paper and rubber bands and something underneath, something like the ghost of everything that had been said into evidence and then sealed away. I put on gloves because procedure demanded it, and because some part of me wanted the ritual. Gloves make touching a stranger's tragedy feel like work instead of trespass.

The photograph came first. They always do.

Marta Serrano, twenty-nine years old in the image, though the image made her younger and older at once the way morgue photography does. Dark hair pulled back. A face built for laughing, you could tell even flattened into official grain—the cheekbones sat high, the mouth had a shape that suggested it spent its time curved. The photo was taken post-mortem, at the scene, and the scene was a boathouse on the estate grounds, and I will not describe what the water and the six years between then and now have softened for me, except to say this: someone had hit her very hard, once, and then arranged her with care, and the care was the part that stayed with me. Rage doesn't arrange. Rage leaves things where they fall. Someone had knelt beside Marta Serrano after killing her and thought about how she would look to whoever found her.

I turned the page.

Summary of facts, in the dry language that turns human catastrophe into inventory: Marta Serrano reported missing by family on the morning of October 9th. Body recovered from the boathouse on the family vineyard estate, Finca La Solana, evening of same day. Cause of death, blunt force trauma. Time of death estimated between 21:00 and midnight. No signs of forced entry anywhere on the property. No weapon recovered. No witnesses.

Suspect: Tomás Serrano, brother of the deceased, twenty-four years old at the time.

I read his statement. Or rather, I read the summary of his statement, because the actual transcript was in a separate volume I'd have to request, and the summary told me everything about the shape of the case and nothing about the man inside it. Tomás had been at the estate that night—he lived there, worked the vines, the youngest of Aurelio Serrano's three children. He had argued with Marta earlier in the evening—witnessed, overheard, attested by household staff. He had no alibi for the critical hours; he claimed he'd walked out to the far terraces alone to check irrigation lines, which was something he apparently did often enough that no one could confirm or deny that night specifically. His fingerprints were on a boat oar found near the body—not *the* oar, the file noted with pedantic honesty, an oar, because boats have two, and the matching one was missing.

Circumstantial. All of it circumstantial, stacked like stones: motive thin (an argument), opportunity real (he was there), physical evidence suggestive (the oar, fibers on his jacket consistent with the boathouse). And the jury, after eleven hours, had done what juries do with stones—they'd stacked them higher until they formed a wall with a man behind it. Guilty. Fourteen years.

I've listened to fifteen years of confessions, denials, alibis, and dying declarations, and I'll tell you what I know about guilt: guilty people narrate. They fill silence. They give you details you didn't ask for because they've rehearsed the story in the dark. Innocent people—or people telling a truth that sounds impossible—go quiet, or they repeat themselves, or they say things that don't fit the shape of a defense.

Tomás Serrano's statement, as summarized, contained this line, recorded verbatim by the interviewing officer:

*"Check the harvest ledger. Everything is in the harvest ledger."*

The officer had noted it as irrelevant. The prosecution had never mentioned it. The defense had called no agricultural experts. Somewhere in a cell right now, a man had been saying *check the ledger* for six years, and everyone who heard him had filed it under grief-madness and moved on.

I sat back under the lamp and let the basement hum fill my ears, and I made myself do the thing I'm actually good at, the thing nobody pays dispatchers enough for: I made myself be skeptical. Skepticism is a discipline. It's holding both possibilities in your hands at once and not letting either one warm up.

Possibility one: Emilio Serrano was a grieving, delusional brother who couldn't accept that the sister he loved was dead and the brother he loved was guilty, and he was spending his inheritance on a fool's errand, and I was the fool he'd hired. This happened. Grief hires investigators the way drowning men hire swimmers—it doesn't matter if they can swim; it matters that holding on feels like living. Fourteen hundred euros was a lot of money to a man like that, but grief doesn't count.

Possibility two: the file was wrong.

And possibility two was the problem, because files are almost never wrong, and yet—and yet. The care in the boathouse. The missing second oar. The harvest ledger. The fact that Emilio hadn't asked me to find the killer, or free his brother, or reopen anything. He'd asked me to prove his sister wasn't dead. Not *might not be*. Not *I believe she isn't*. He'd said it the way you report a discrepancy in a count: *prove that Marta Serrano has not died.* As if somewhere in his private arithmetic, the numbers didn't add up, and he needed an outsider with clean hands to find the error.

You don't pay fourteen hundred euros for denial. Denial is free. You pay for verification.

* * *

I went back the next night. And the next. Berta started leaving the lamp on for me.

I read the witness statements until I could recite them. The housekeeper, Señora Ibáñez, who heard the argument between brother and sister around eight in the evening—raised voices, doors, the word *money* audible through wood. The foreman, who confirmed Tomás walked toward the upper terraces around nine. The father, Aurelio Serrano, whose statement was a masterpiece of composure: he'd been in his study with correspondence all evening, saw nothing, heard nothing, and expressed his devastation in sentences so balanced they might have been notarized. Reading his words was like reading a menu. Nothing on it was edible.

Emilio's own statement was the shortest in the file. He'd been away—at university, two hours north. Phone records placed him at a friend's apartment until nearly one in the morning. He'd arrived at the estate the following noon, after the body was found. In six years, according to the file, he had never varied a word of it.

But here's what snagged me, the splinter I kept turning over: the timeline.

Marta was last seen alive by anyone at approximately 8:40 p.m., when the housekeeper brought her tea and Marta told her she was going out to the boathouse to think. The medical examiner put death between nine and midnight. Tomás went to the terraces at nine. Fine. Clean.

Except the housekeeper's original statement—there were two versions in the file, an initial interview and a later one, and I only caught it because Irena Kovac trained me to always read the first version of everything, because the first version is the one memory tells before anyone tells it what to say—in the first version, the housekeeper said Marta had received a phone call that evening, around seven-thirty, and had gone quiet afterward. In the second version, the phone call was gone. Just gone. Not contradicted—omitted. And in the margin of the first statement, in pencil, faint as breath: a case number that didn't match this one. 4471-B.

There is no 4471-B in the index. I checked. Three times. Berta checked for me on the fourth visit, running the number with the patience of a woman humoring a child, and the system returned nothing, and she shrugged, and I thanked her, and we both pretended the nothing was ordinary.

Files don't lose their own appendices. People remove them.

I started keeping notes in a marbled school notebook, the cheap kind, because expensive notebooks look like evidence and cheap ones look like groceries. I drew the timeline on the inside cover. I wrote the housekeeper's two statements side by side and stared at the space where the phone call had been erased, and I felt the thing I hadn't let myself feel since Emilio's voice first shook through my headset: the pull. Not curiosity exactly—curiosity is light, recreational, a cat with a string. This was heavier. This was the dispatcher's instinct, the one that lives below thought, the one that knows when a caller's pause is hesitation and when it's someone standing behind them. Something in that file was standing behind the file.

On the sixth night, I requested the trial transcript volume. On the seventh, I read Tomás Serrano's testimony, all four hundred pages of it, and here is what I found: a young man who answered every question with the harvest schedule. Planting dates. Rainfall totals. Sugar levels in the grapes, week by week, like a rosary. The prosecutor lost patience with him around day three; the transcript records the exchange where the prosecutor asks, *Mr. Serrano, did you kill your sister?* and Tomás replies, *The merlot comes off the south slope the third week of September. It always has. Ask anyone.* Eleven hours of jury deliberation, and I understood them completely. What do you do with a defendant who answers murder with agriculture?

Either the boy had shattered, or the boy was hiding inside the one place no one would search—the calendar of a working vineyard, dates and numbers that meant nothing to lawyers and everything to whoever could read them.

I closed the volume at 5 a.m., in the blue hour when the basement windows go from black to bruise, and I laid my notebook open next to the file box, and I looked at what I'd become in seven days: a woman with a timeline, a phantom case number, an erased phone call, and a convicted man's liturgy of grape harvests memorized against her will.

Skepticism said: walk away. Refund the money. Some puzzles are just sad people in pieces, and your job is endings, not beginnings.

But skepticism had a flaw, and the flaw was this: I had called the number listed in the file for the estate—Finca La Solana, still operating, still selling wine to tourists—and a woman answered on the second ring, and when I asked, in my best wine-buyer's voice, whether the señorita Marta might help me with an order, there was a silence. Not confusion. Not grief. Silence with a shape to it—the specific, held-breath silence of a person looking over their shoulder.

Then the woman said, very evenly, that the señorita had passed some years ago, God rest her, and hung up.

Everyone says that. Of course they say that. She's dead; it's in the file; it's on the memorial wall of every obituary database in the region.

But here's the thing I know from fifteen years of voices: people who are reporting a death grieve on the phone. Their voice goes down. Hers went *careful*. There's a difference, and the difference is the width of a life.

I packed the file back into its box, returned it to Berta, and stood outside the annex in the grey morning while the city shook itself awake around me—buses sighing, shutters rattling up, the first delivery trucks groaning through wet streets. Somewhere across town a man named Tomás was waking up in a cell, reciting rainfall. Somewhere in the hills a patriarch sat in a chair at the center of a house full of locked rooms. And somewhere—maybe—a woman with high cheekbones and a laugh-shaped mouth was alive under another name, counting the days to an anniversary that celebrated her murder.

Fourteen hundred euros in a drawer. One line of instruction. *Prove that Marta Serrano has not died.*

I got home as the sun came up, slept four hours without dreaming, and woke with the answer to a question I hadn't consciously asked: not *whether* to take the case, but *where to start*. Because the file had given me one more thing, buried in the evidence log, item forty-one, seized from Marta's room and never entered at trial because it was deemed irrelevant: a receipt from a taxi company.

My company.

Dispatch record, October 8th, 7:31 p.m.—one year to the night before her death, a pickup at Finca La Solana, destination unlisted, fare paid cash, driver's note in the margin: *quiet lady, cried the whole way.*

Someone had driven Marta Serrano away from that estate a full year before she supposedly died there, and she had cried the whole way, and the call that handled that pickup would have been logged by the dispatcher on shift.

I already knew who was on shift that night, a year before the murder. Everyone in the trade knows. It was the desk before mine. It was Irena Kovac's desk.

I reached for the phone to call her, and stopped, because some instincts arrive ahead of reasons, and mine said: not yet. First the address. First the ground itself.

Finca La Solana. I wrote it at the top of a fresh page, and under it, the date of the anniversary dinner Emilio feared, six weeks out, and under that, the only plan I had, which was barely a plan at all:

*Go see what kind of house buries a daughter twice.*
