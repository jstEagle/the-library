## 4. The Vineyard Address

The money came from a bank that smelled of old leather and older secrets.

I'd spent two days on it, which was two days more than I'd planned to spend. Emilio's envelope had held forty crisp hundreds, and cash leaves no trail—everyone knows that—but cash has to come from somewhere, and the withdrawal slip tucked inside the envelope like a bookmark, either forgotten or left on purpose, told me where. Banco Provincial, branch on Calle Herrán. A cashier's withdrawal, not an ATM. A person had walked in and asked for this money by name.

I couldn't walk into a bank and ask questions. Banks have lawyers for that. But banks also have tellers, and tellers have eyes, and I have fifteen years of listening to people describe things badly over bad phone lines. I know how to ask a question that sounds like small talk and comes back carrying freight. So I put on my one good coat—the one I wear to funerals and, apparently, to banks—and I stood at the counter buying a cashier's check for my own rent, and I said, to the woman stamping my slip, "Busy morning? I'm waiting on a friend who's supposed to withdraw for me and he's always late."

She glanced at the clock, then at her register log, out of pure professional habit. "Depends on your friend. We had a big withdrawal earlier this week. Slowed everything down."

"Big how?"

"Big enough that the manager had to authorize it." She handed me my check. "Serrano account. You know them? The vineyard people."

I did not let my face do anything. Fifteen years of night shift teaches you that. The face stays a closed door and the voice stays level, because somewhere on the other end of every call there's a person listening to your tone for news of their own future.

"The vineyard people," I repeated. "Out past the ridge road?"

"Where else." She was already looking past me at the next customer. "They used to come in monthly. Now it's just the son, and only when the old man sends him."

The son. Emilio had said his name once, quietly, the way you say a word you're ashamed of. Emilio Serrano. And the account that paid me to prove a murder hadn't happened yet belonged to a family whose name the teller said the way you'd say a weather system—something large, something that had been overhead for a long time, something you'd stopped looking at directly.

I walked out into the rain with the check in my pocket and the whole shape of it rearranging itself behind my eyes. A failing vineyard. A patriarch who sent his son for money. A son who paid a stranger to unmake a murder. None of it fit yet, but the pieces had stopped being random. They'd started being a pattern, and I have spent my whole life reading patterns in static, and I know the feeling when static becomes signal. It feels like a hand closing around the back of your neck.

* * *

The ridge road out to the Serrano estate was forty minutes of wet gravel and fog, the kind of drive where you keep expecting the road to admit it's a mistake. The vineyards began appearing in the fog like half-remembered things—rows of bare vines, winter-stripped, staked in lines that ran up the hillsides at angles that looked almost mathematical. Beautiful, in the way of things that are being maintained by someone who can no longer afford to maintain them. I passed a tractor under a tarp that had been under that tarp for years, by the look of the tarp. I passed a tasting room with a CLOSED sign that had faded from white to the color of bone.

The house appeared all at once, the way big houses do when the fog thins—a long, low sprawl of pale stone, two stories, wings extending in both directions like a bird that had landed once, decades ago, and never flown again. Cypress trees flanked the drive. A wrought-iron gate stood open, which surprised me until I saw the camera mounted on the gatepost, older model, but aimed with care.

I had called ahead. That was the performance: a buyer named Rafaela Duarte, calling from a restaurant supply company that did not exist, representing a hotel group that existed even less, interested in bulk purchases of mid-tier red for a winter promotion. I'd practiced the voice. It wasn't hard. I've spent fifteen years being the calmest person on any phone line; sounding like a woman who cares about case discounts was beneath my skill set.

A woman met me at the door. Housekeeper, by the apron and the way she stood—slightly behind the threshold, as if the door were a border she was authorized to defend. Sixtyish, gray hair pinned with a severity that suggested she'd been pinning it that way since before I was born.

"Señora Duarte. Señor Serrano is expecting you. He asks that you forgive him for not standing; his legs are not what they were."

"I understand completely."

She led me through a hallway that I would think about for days afterward.

It was long, and dim, and lined with photographs. That's normal for old houses—photographs are what old houses are made of, under the plaster. But this hallway was curated. That was the word that came to me and would not leave: curated. The frames were all dark wood, all identical, all hung at exactly the same height, and they progressed down the hall in chronological order like stations of a cross. A young couple in front of the first vines—Aurelio and a woman with a smile that hadn't yet learned what was coming. Christenings. Weddings. A boy on a tractor, a girl holding a basket of grapes too big for her arms. The girl appeared again and again as the decades turned, and I knew her face before the housekeeper said the name, because I'd spent a week staring at it in a case file.

Marta Serrano. Alive in every frame. Smiling in most of them.

And then, near the end of the hall, the photographs stopped. The last frame held a portrait—studio-lit, professionally done, the kind of memorial photo that gets enlarged and printed for a funeral. Marta at maybe twenty-eight, dark hair, a face caught between smiles, looking slightly off-camera at something the photographer had never seen. Beneath it, on a small brass plate: *Marta Serrano. 1984–2019. Siempre en la viña.*

Always in the vineyard.

The housekeeper saw me looking and said nothing, which was itself a kind of statement. We walked the rest of the hall in silence, past the dead girl's face, into a study that smelled of wood smoke and tannin and, underneath both, something faintly antiseptic, like a room that had been cleaned very thoroughly and recently.

Aurelio Serrano sat in a wheelchair by the fire.

I had prepared myself for a dying patriarch, but preparation is not the same as recognition. He was smaller than his house, smaller than his name, folded into the chair like a suit that had been packed away for a season. But his eyes were another matter entirely. They were dark, quick, and absolutely awake, and they went over me the way a customs officer goes over luggage—thorough, unhurried, looking for the false bottom.

"Señora Duarte." His voice was dry and quiet and carried anyway, the way old men's voices do when they've spent fifty years being obeyed. "You found us. Most people don't bother anymore."

"The fog makes it an adventure."

"The fog makes it a filter." He gestured at a chair across from him, and the housekeeper poured wine without being asked—his gesture, her hands, a choreography worn smooth by repetition. "Anyone willing to drive out here in January either wants wine or wants something else. Which are you?"

I smiled the Duarte smile. "I want wine at a price that lets my hotel group pretend they're getting a bargain."

He laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, practiced, deployed. "Honest. I like that. The last three buyers who came out here wept about terroir and ordered nothing." He lifted his glass an inch. "Taste. Then we'll insult each other over numbers."

I tasted. It was, annoyingly, excellent—a garnacha with dark fruit and a long, smoky finish, the kind of wine that makes a liar's job harder, because I found myself meaning the compliments I'd planned to fake.

"This is good," I said, and meant it. "This is very good. Why is your tasting room closed?"

Something moved behind his eyes. Not a flinch—nothing so legible. More like a door closing somewhere deep in the house of his face, one of many doors, all of them well-oiled.

"Distribution," he said. "The modern market is a casino and I am too old for the tables. So we sell private. Which brings me to a question of my own, Señora Duarte." He set down his glass and folded his hands, and the hands were liver-spotted and trembling very slightly, and I understood suddenly that the trembling was not weakness. It was voltage. A live wire doesn't shake because it's frail. "Your company. Duarte is a Basque name. Your Spanish is from the capital—no, wait." He raised a finger before I could speak. "Don't tell me. Let an old man guess; it's the only game I have left. You're from the north. You learned your Spanish from radio, not from school."

I kept the smile fixed. "Radio?"

"You have the diction of someone who talks for a living but not to an audience. To one person at a time." He picked up his glass again. "My daughter was like that. She could talk to one stranger for an hour and couldn't give a toast to save her life."

*My daughter.* Past tense, worn smooth as the brass plate in the hall. He'd said it the way you'd say *the weather*, and I understood that I was sitting across from a man who had killed a version of his own child with grammar long before anything else, if anything else had happened at all, and I still did not know what had happened, and the not-knowing was beginning to feel less like ignorance and more like the thing itself—the crime, the real one, committed daily, in every sentence.

We talked numbers for twenty minutes. I quoted absurdly low, he countered with theatrical offense, we settled somewhere plausible, and I left a deposit—my own money, spent gladly, the cheapest tuition I'd ever paid—on three hundred cases that would never be delivered to a hotel that did not exist. He signed nothing. He said his son would handle the paperwork, and something in the way he said *my son* was different from the way he'd said *my daughter*, and I filed the difference away the way I file everything: without knowing yet what drawer it belongs in.

* * *

The housekeeper walked me back through the hallway of photographs. On the way out, I did the thing I'd come to do, the small, deniable thing, the thing I'd been rehearsing since the gate.

I stopped, as any visitor would, at the memorial portrait. I leaned in slightly, the way people lean toward the dead. And I let my coat sleeve brush the frame's edge, and I looked—not at Marta's face, which I knew, but at the frame itself, the way it sat against the wall, the small visible margin of backing paper at the corner where the frame had been opened and resealed.

The paper was white. Clean, matte, unyellowed. Every other frame in that hallway had been hanging since before the fog had names; their backing paper would be foxed, browned, aged in place. This one had been opened recently. Within the last year. Possibly within the last months.

Someone had taken Marta's memorial photo out of its frame and put it back. Recently. In a house where the official story was six years settled, six years closed, six years *always in the vineyard*.

"Señora Duarte?" The housekeeper had stopped at the end of the hall. She was watching me with an expression I recognized, because I wear it myself on every call where I already know how it ends: the look of someone who cannot say what they know and is waiting for you to stop making them carry it.

"Beautiful house," I said. "So much history."

"Too much," she said, very quietly, and opened the door.

* * *

The fog had thickened while we talked. I drove back down the ridge road with the vineyards vanishing around me, rows of bare vines appearing and dissolving like the frames of a film run at the wrong speed, and I did what I always do with a hard call: I ran it back. Play it again. Listen to what was underneath.

Aurelio Serrano, guessing my profession from my diction in under a minute. A man that sharp does not let a stranger into his house by accident. He'd known—or suspected—something, and he'd let me in anyway, the way you leave a light on to see what comes toward it. The deposit I'd left wasn't tuition. It was bait, and I'd swallowed it, and somewhere in that stone house there was probably a file with *Duarte, R.* written on it, and by tomorrow it would have a second line under the name.

And the frame. The white backing paper. Someone in that house had recently handled the dead girl's portrait—had opened it, looked at it, closed it again. Grief does that. Guilt does that. And a third thing does it too, a thing I didn't have a word for yet: the act of a person checking whether a story they're telling is still holding, the way you check a lock you're not sure you threw.

At the bottom of the ridge, where the gravel met asphalt, my phone buzzed. A text from a number I'd come to know: Emilio.

*Did you go?*

I pulled over. The fog pressed against the windshield. I typed: *I went. I met your father. He's sharper than you said.*

The reply came fast, faster than a man who was supposed to be merely nervous.

*He's always been sharper than anyone says. That's the problem. Did you see the hall?*

*The hall of photographs. Yes.*

A long pause this time. Long enough that I watched the little typing indicator appear and vanish twice.

*Look at the photos again. All of them. Not at the faces.*

*At what, then?*

The answer came, and I sat in the fog with it for a long time before I drove on, because it was the first thing Emilio had said that sounded not like a request but like a warning, and because it was also, unmistakably, an invitation:

*At what's missing.*
