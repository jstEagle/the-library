## 1. The Prepaid Fare

The switchboard at Harrow's Bend Taxi & Livery hummed the way it always did on a Tuesday night — a low, patient drone, like an insect trapped behind the wall. Felix Sterling sat with his feet hooked on the rung of his stool, a mug of coffee gone the color of river water beside him, and let the quiet settle over him like a coat he'd worn so long he'd stopped noticing its weight.

Eleven-forty. The board had lit up twice since ten: Mrs. Okonkwo wanting a ride to her sister's for cards tomorrow afternoon, and a kid from the gas station whose car wouldn't start and who'd spent six minutes explaining the problem in detail Felix hadn't asked for. That was the shape of a Tuesday. That was the shape of most nights, if he was honest, and honesty was the one luxury Felix allowed himself freely, because it cost nothing when nobody was listening.

He knew things, though. That was the point of him, though nobody in Harrow's Bend would have put it that way. He knew that Dr. Pell's office called for cabs on Thursdays because Thursday was when she told people the worst news — you could hear it in their voices when they climbed in, that particular hollowed-out quiet. He knew that the Deacon at Second Baptist took taxis to the racetrack two towns over and always, always asked to be dropped off a block away from the track entrance, as if God's sightlines ended at the county line. He knew which houses left porch lights on all night out of habit and which did it out of fear, and he knew the difference mattered.

Felix Sterling had memorized every route and every rumor in Harrow's Bend, and in return the town had given him exactly nothing: not a wife, not a friend who called first, not even a nickname. He was the voice on the phone. *Cab'll be there in ten.* People said thank you to him the way they said thank you to weather.

The line rang at 11:52 p.m., and he answered on the second ring, the way he always did, because a dispatcher who answered fast was a dispatcher who cared, even if caring was just muscle memory by now.

"Harrow's Bend Taxi, this is Felix."

"Good evening, Mr. Sterling."

A man's voice. Low, level, unhurried. Not old, not young — one of those voices sanded down until age stopped mattering. There was no background noise behind it. No television, no traffic, no wind. Whoever was calling was sitting somewhere very still.

"Evening," Felix said. "What can I do for you?"

"I'd like to book a fare. Friday night. Midnight."

"Friday midnight. Pickup where?"

"The Ashcombe house. Ashcombe Hill, the main drive off Corliss Road. You'll know it."

Felix knew it the way he knew the sound of his own front door. Everyone in Harrow's Bend knew the Ashcombe house, even the ones who'd never seen anything of it but the iron gates and the dark suggestion of roofline beyond the cedars. The Ashcombes were the closest thing the town had to royalty, and royalty didn't call for taxis. Royalty had drivers. Royalty had Isandro Reyes, the caretaker everyone spoke about in the softened voice reserved for saints and invalids.

"You'll want the livery service, then," Felix said, reaching for the booking pad. "Sedan's forty-five flat, town rates—"

"Triple. Paid in advance. Tonight."

The pen stopped moving. Felix had been doing this job for eleven years, four months, and some number of days he'd stopped counting after the divorce, and in all that time he could count on one hand the fares paid in advance, and on none of them the ones paid triple. "That's generous," he said carefully. "But the rate's the rate. Company policy."

"Consider the difference a retainer." A pause, brief and deliberate, like a man setting down a card face-up. "I'm not booking a ride, Mr. Sterling. I'm booking your attention."

Felix leaned back on the stool. The springs complained. Outside the dispatch window, Main Street lay empty under the streetlights, wet-looking asphalt even though it hadn't rained, the diner sign buzzing its half-dead buzz. Everything exactly where it should be. "I'm not sure I follow."

"Friday at midnight, I want a car dispatched to the Ashcombe house. It doesn't need to pick anyone up. It needs to be there. Or near enough. And then I need you to confirm something for me by Saturday."

"Confirm what?"

"That nothing happened."

The words sat in the ear strangely. Felix found himself looking at the switchboard as if the phone itself might explain. "Nothing happened," he repeated. "You want me to confirm that nothing happened."

"That's right."

"Nothing happening is the default, friend. Nothing happens here most nights. I don't charge triple for it."

"I know what I'm asking." The voice didn't rise, didn't sharpen. If anything it grew quieter, the way men get quiet when they mean business. "And I know what it sounds like. I'm asking anyway. Will you take the booking or not?"

Felix looked down at the pad. His handwriting waited there, neat as a schoolboy's: *Fri 12:00 — Ashcombe Hill — prepaid.* Below it, the space where a name went, blank.

"Who do I put this under?" he asked.

"No name. You'll have the payment confirmation before I hang up. Card on file will read H.B. Holdings — that's fine, it'll clear."

H.B. Holdings. Felix knew that name too; everybody did. Half the riverfront carried it on deeds and leases, the way barnacles carry the hull. The Ashcombe money wore that letterhead like a glove wears a hand.

"And the confirmation," Felix said slowly. "Saturday. Confirm to who? To this number?"

"To me. I'll call you. Saturday morning, before noon. All you have to say is whether anything happened at that house Friday night. Whether anyone came or went who shouldn't have. Whether—" the smallest hitch, the first crack in the lacquer, "—whether everything was ordinary."

"Ordinary," Felix said. "At the Ashcombe house. At midnight."

"Now you're beginning to understand why I'm paying triple."

There was a long moment where neither of them spoke, and Felix became aware of the sounds of the room again — the hum of the board, the tick of the wall clock, the coffee going cold. Somewhere in his chest, unease was putting on the costume of routine, buttoning itself up, trying to pass inspection. This was how it worked in Harrow's Bend: strange things arrived dressed as errands, and you either opened the door or you didn't, but either way they stood on the porch a while.

"How do you know my schedule?" Felix asked. It came out flatter than he intended.

"What do you mean?"

"You knew my name before I gave it. Now you're asking me to sit up Friday midnight, which happens to be the dead stretch of my shift, when I'm alone in this office. That's not a guess. That's a fact you already had."

The pause this time lasted three full seconds. When the voice returned it had something almost like respect in it. "You're the only person in this town who sees everything and says nothing, Mr. Sterling. I did my homework. I'd have been disappointed if you hadn't done yours."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll find another way to watch that house, and you'll go back to your coffee, and none of this ever touched you." No threat in it. That was the unsettling part — no threat at all, just the calm arithmetic of a man who had already priced out both answers.

Felix turned the pen over in his fingers. He thought about saying no. He thought about it seriously, the way you think about a door marked EXIT in a building you've lived in too long. But underneath the unease, in the place where he kept the thing that had gotten him through eleven years of nights — the hunger, call it, the appetite for the shape of other people's lives — something had lifted its head. Something had smelled Friday coming.

"Payment clears, I take the booking," Felix said. "One condition. Nobody rides in that cab unless they ask me direct. I'm not ferrying anybody anywhere they don't know they're going."

"Understood. And Mr. Sterling —"

"Yeah."

"If Saturday comes and everything was ordinary, you'll be glad you took the money. And if it doesn't—" the voice paused, and for just a moment it sounded tired, human, almost sorry, "—then you'll understand why I couldn't tell you more tonight."

The line clicked into silence before Felix could answer. On the pad, the confirmation number printed itself in his neat hand, timestamped 11:58 p.m., Tuesday. Four days before the fare.

* * *

He didn't sleep well, which was unusual; sleep was another thing the town had never bothered to give him trouble over. He lay in his apartment above the laundromat listening to the dryers turn below like slow hearts, turning the call over. *Confirm that nothing happened.* Who paid triple to have nothing confirmed? Who watched a house they weren't inside of?

By Wednesday noon he'd filed it where he filed everything that didn't fit: the mental drawer marked *odd*, between the woman who'd once booked a cab to the cemetery at 3 a.m. and the envelope of cash someone had mailed the office with no note at all. The drawer stayed shut. Mostly.

Mostly — except that Wednesday evening, driving the last shift cab back to the lot, Felix took the long way home. Down Corliss Road, past the cedar hedge, slowing without meaning to at the gap in the trees where the Ashcombe house showed itself: gray gables, black windows, the gates shut against the dusk. Every light in the place was already off, though it wasn't fully dark yet, and standing at the gatepost with a ring of keys in his hand, locking up hours early, was the caretaker. Isandro Reyes. A small, precise figure in the failing light, checking the lock twice, three times, the way a man checks a wound.

Isandro looked up as the cab rolled past — looked straight through the windshield, straight at Felix, with an expression Felix couldn't file anywhere. Not suspicion. Not fear. Something closer to recognition, as if the caretaker had been expecting a car to come down that road eventually, and was only surprised it had taken this long.

Felix drove on. In the rearview mirror the house shrank back into its cedars, and the drawer marked *odd* rattled softly on its runners, and did not quite close.
