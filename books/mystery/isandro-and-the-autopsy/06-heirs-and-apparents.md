## 6. Heirs and Apparents

The dead, Felix had learned over three years of dispatching Harrow's Bend's grief, traveled in two directions: out, toward the county cemetery on the flatlands, or up, into the hills where the old families kept their dead like heirlooms. Vivian Ashcombe would go up. But first she had to lie in her own parlor for an afternoon while the town filed past to confirm what it already believed — that the matriarch had simply stopped, the way a clock stops, without scandal or violence, at a convenient hour.

Felix hadn't been invited. That was the point.

He arrived at four o'clock, when the wake was fullest and the scrutiny thinnest, wearing his second-best jacket and carrying a casserole dish borrowed from Mrs. Okafor at the diner — a passport made of noodles and cheese that no one at the door thought to question. The Ashcombe house opened around him the way he'd imagined it from the outside all these years: high ceilings gone amber with age, a staircase wide enough for three mourners abreast, the smell of lilies laid over something older, something like dust and lemon oil and money that had stopped trying.

He set the casserole on the long table among its brothers and sisters, then did what he did best. He listened.

A dispatcher learns early that a room is just a switchboard with furniture. Every conversation is a call waiting to be connected; every face is a number you can look up later. He took a glass of sherry he didn't want and began to work the lines.

* * *

Marcus Ashcombe he found by the window, half in the light, holding a drink like it was a rope thrown to a drowning man. He was thirty-something and handsome in a way that had begun to cost him — the jaw softening, the eyes doing arithmetic behind the grief. Felix knew his voice before he knew his face. Marcus called the cab company eleven, twelve times a month, always after midnight, always from the casino road or the marina bar, always asking the driver to wait while he ran inside for his wallet.

"Terrible thing," Felix said, arriving beside him with the ease of a man who belonged there. "I only met her once. She tipped in exact change."

Marcus laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly strangled. "That sounds like her." He looked at Felix properly, and Felix watched him do the math: not family, not close family, someone's plus-one, safe to talk to. "She counted everything. Every cent, every favor. You know what she said to me at Christmas?" He drained half the glass. "'Marcus, I intend to be a burden for another twenty years.' Like it was a threat."

"She wasn't ill?"

"Not that anyone—" He stopped. Restarted. "No. No, she was fine. She was fine." The repetition had the ring of something rehearsed, and rehearsed badly. His hand shook slightly as he refilled from a passing tray. "It's just — timing. You know? It's a hell of a time for her heart to quit." He said it lightly, the way men say things they've already said to lawyers, and then seemed to hear himself, and went pale, and added, too loudly, "Not that there's anything wrong with the timing. It's terrible. It's all terrible."

Felix let him go. A man who protests the calendar at his grandmother's wake is a man who has already checked it against something. Felix didn't yet know what — but he'd find the document, because in Harrow's Bend every debt eventually found its way through the switchboard.

* * *

Dorothea Vane was easier to read because she wanted to be read. She stood near the fireplace receiving condolences like a consul, tall and silvered and dressed in a gray so precise it looked commissioned. When Felix drifted within range, she was saying, "—of course, Aunt Vivian relied on me entirely these last years. Power of attorney, medical decisions, the estate accounts. It was a full-time responsibility, and I never once complained."

To Felix's ear, that sentence had the shape of testimony. She was building a record in real time, witness by witness, laying bricks of narrative that would harden into fact by the time any official came asking. He'd heard dispatchers do it after bad calls — retell the incident until the retelling replaced the memory.

"You must be Miss Vane," Felix said. "I'm a friend of the family. Distant."

"Everyone is distant," Dorothea said pleasantly, "until they're useful." Her eyes performed a quick inventory of him — jacket, shoes, the casserole he'd carried in — and filed him under harmless. "You knew my aunt?"

"By reputation. They say she ran this town from that chair by the window."

"They say correctly." Something moved behind her composure, quick as a fish under ice. "And now the chair is empty, and everyone will discover how much of Harrow's Bend was held together with tape and Vivian's willpower. The lawyers come Monday. There will be documents. There are always documents." She smiled with real warmth, which was somehow worse than none. "If you were a friend of hers, you'll want the estate handled properly. Order matters, at times like this. Order is what separates grief from chaos."

*Order*, Felix thought, is exactly the word a person uses when they've spent four days arranging some. He thanked her and moved on, and felt her attention stay on his back like a hand between his shoulder blades.

* * *

Camille Ashcombe he almost missed, because she wasn't performing. She sat alone in the small sitting room off the parlor — the one with the window facing the river — in a black dress that fit her badly, as if bought in a hurry, or worn out of obligation rather than ownership. She was maybe thirty, with her grandmother's dark eyes and none of the Ashcombe polish; her hands were wrapped around a teacup gone cold hours ago.

Felix knew about Camille the way he knew about everything in town: fragments. Left seven years ago after some rupture with the family. Came back rarely, always off-season, always slipping past the switchboard unannounced. The granddaughter who refused the money — though Felix wouldn't learn that phrase until later. For now she was just the only person in the house who looked like the death had actually happened to her.

"I'm sorry," he said from the doorway. "I don't mean to intrude. Everyone in there seems very busy being composed."

Her mouth twitched — not quite a smile. "Composed. Yes. They've been composing since Tuesday." She looked up at him. "Do I know you?"

"No. Felix Sterling. I drive a cab, mostly nights. I brought a casserole I can't take credit for."

"A cab driver." Something flickered across her face — recognition, maybe, or the ghost of a story. "My grandmother used to say the only honest people left in Harrow's Bend were the ones who drove other people's secrets around at night." She turned the cold cup in her hands. "Did you ever drive her?"

"Once or twice. She paid exact change."

This time the smile came all the way, wet and brief. "Exact change. God." Then it collapsed, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes, and for a moment the whole polished machinery of the wake fell away and there was just a woman grieving in a room that smelled of lilies. "They keep saying it was peaceful. Her heart, in her sleep. Peaceful." She lowered her hand. "I talked to her Wednesday night. She was sharp. Sharper than me. She was making jokes about Dorothea's flowers. People don't die two days after sounding like that. Do they?"

"Hearts are unpredictable," Felix said, and hated how smoothly the town's sentence came out of his own mouth.

"Yes," Camille said quietly. "That's what everyone keeps telling me. Everyone, all together, like a chorus." She looked at him then — really looked, the way nobody in Harrow's Bend ever bothered to look at a dispatcher — and said, "You don't believe it either. I can tell. You have a dispatcher's face. My grandmother described it once. 'The face of a man who knows what everyone in town did last Thursday.'"

Felix felt the floor tilt a degree beneath him. "I should check on the casserole," he said, which was cowardice, and they both knew it.

He was three steps into the hall when he felt it: a slight weight against his chest, inside his jacket, where no weight had been when he'd walked past the coat rack twenty minutes earlier. He kept walking, casual, all the way to the front porch, all the way to the dark of the side garden, before he drew out the folded slip of paper.

The handwriting was small, precise, unhurried:

*You were paid to prove it didn't happen. It did. Ask why.*

* * *

Felix stood a long time in the cold among the bare hydrangeas, reading the note until the words stopped being words. The river ran below the lawn, black and patient. Somewhere up the hill a dog barked twice and gave up.

Someone knew. Not suspected — *knew*. Knew about the call on Tuesday, the triple fare, the prepaid booking that existed nowhere but his logbook and the company's locked records. Knew enough to find him in a crowded parlor and slip paper into his pocket without his feeling a thing, which meant either a pickpocket's hands or a professional's calm, and both possibilities sat in his stomach like swallowed stones.

He ran the room backward in his head, the way he replayed calls. Marcus, shaking, checking calendars. Dorothea, brick-laying her testimony, her eyes doing inventories. Camille, alone, grieving honestly — or grieving honestly as cover, because the truly dangerous ones grieved best. Isandro Reyes hadn't appeared at all; the caretaker had not come out of the guest wing all afternoon, if he'd come at all.

Any of them. None of them. Someone else entirely — a fifth presence in the house, moving through the crowd with a purpose, who had identified Felix not as a stranger with a casserole but as *the man who was paid to watch*.

Which raised the question underneath the question, the one he'd been avoiding since dawn on Saturday: if his client had known Friday midnight was the appointed hour, then the payment had never been a shield. It had been a seat. Someone had bought him a ticket to a death and asked him, afterward, to confirm the show had gone on as planned.

*Ask why.*

Felix folded the note along its creases and slid it deep into his breast pocket, over his heart, where it felt less like evidence than like a summons. Above him, the lit windows of the Ashcombe house glowed against the darkening hill, warm and golden and full of people arranging their stories. Somewhere inside, whoever wrote those twelve words was raising a glass and accepting condolences, and watching him watch them.

He started down the driveway toward the road where he'd left his car, and only at the gate did he let himself think the thought that changed the shape of everything:

Whoever slipped him that note hadn't warned him.

They'd *recruited* him.
