## 3. Room 214

The master key weighed more than it should have.

Ulyana Nystrom had carried it for eleven years — a brass thing gone soft at the edges from her thumb, worn smooth as sea glass. It opened every door in the Saltglass Hotel except the ones she kept locked in her own head. She had used it to deliver forgotten umbrellas, to rescue guests locked out by their own carelessness, to check on an elderly widow who hadn't come down for breakfast and who turned out to have died peacefully with the radio still playing. The key was a tool of service. Of stewardship. She had never once thought of it as an instrument of trespass.

She thought of it that way now, standing in the third-floor corridor with room 214's number glowing faintly green under the emergency light, because 214 wasn't on the third floor. It was on the second. And she was standing here anyway, because she'd climbed one flight past it without deciding to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*Stop it,* she told herself. *You're a concierge. You fix things. Go down, open the door, look, and be done.*

But she stood a moment longer in the corridor's hush. The Saltglass breathed differently at night — the radiators ticked like patient clocks, the old pipes murmured in the walls as though the building were reciting something it had memorized long ago, and beneath it all ran the sea-sound, that low tidal exhale the fog made when it pressed against the windows. Guests paid good money for that quiet. They said it was restful. They didn't know that the hotel's silence was not emptiness but attention — the whole old house listening, the way it always listened, keeping what it heard.

She went down the stairs. Her shoes said nothing on the runner.

Room 214 sat at the end of the east wing, last door before the window that looked out on the garden and, beyond the garden's iron fence, the gray suggestion of the cannery ruins across the cove. The do-not-disturb sign was gone; housekeeping had flagged the room vacant two days ago, after the guest who'd held it failed to answer checkout. Marta had already stripped the bed. Samir had vacuumed. By every operational measure, 214 was finished — a clean slate, ready for whoever came next.

Except that its former occupant had mailed an exhumation order from this room. Except that Aldous Vey's coffin had been opened this morning before half the town and found full of stones.

Ulyana slid the key into the lock. It turned with the small compliant click of all her hotel's locks, and she stepped inside, and pulled the door shut behind her out of habit — then stood very still, because shutting herself into a suspect's room felt different from shutting a door anywhere else.

* * *

Daylight came thin through the curtains, the fog diffusing it into something like milk. The room was ordinary in the way the Saltglass did ordinary: high ceiling, water-stained plaster painted over so many times the cornice had gone soft-edged, a writing desk scarred by a century of rings from glasses set down carelessly or in grief. A chair. A wardrobe. A window seat with a cushion Marta had recovered in blue corduroy back when Ulyana was new. The bed was made with hospital corners because Marta made every bed that way, even empty ones, even beds no one would see. There was a discipline in that which Ulyana loved and could not have explained to anyone who didn't work in hotels.

She began the way she began everything: by standing still and letting the room speak.

Guests lied with their luggage and told the truth with their rooms. Eleven years of reading those truths had taught her a taxonomy. There were the rooms of people passing through — bare, untouched, the soap wrapper still sealed. There were the rooms of people hiding — drawers pushed shut too carefully, suitcases turned so the labels faced the wall. There were the rooms of people waiting for something, and the rooms of people who had given up waiting, and these looked almost identical until you noticed whether the chair faced the door or the window.

Room 214 spoke first through smell. Under the lemon of Marta's cleaner there was something older, medicinal and sweet — camphor, maybe, or the ghost of some tincture, the scent of a body being managed rather than lived in. A sick man's room. Or an old man's. The two smells were cousins.

She checked the obvious places first, because she was thorough before she was clever. Wardrobe: empty but for one wire hanger angled wrong, as if a coat had been lifted off in haste or lowered off gently — she couldn't decide which, and the not-knowing irritated her. Desk drawers: nothing. Not a matchbook, not a pen, not the Gideon Bible the Saltglass had never stocked and therefore could not be missing. Someone had swept this room. Not housekeeping — Marta's sweeps were thorough but left the small archaeology of living: a hair tie, a receipt, a button. This was a different kind of empty. This was curated.

Which meant the guest had cleaned up after himself. Which meant he'd known someone would come looking.

"Then you left me something," Ulyana murmured to the room. "People like you always leave something. You can't help it. You want to be found by exactly one person, and you're gambling it's me."

She worked outward. Under the mattress — nothing but the mattress's honest lumps. Behind the radiator — dust, and a bottle cap some earlier guest had lost years ago, rusted to the color of dried blood. The window seat's cushion she lifted and pressed, and the corduroy gave up nothing. She was kneeling at the bedside, running her palm along the underside of the box spring in the undignified posture of all searchers everywhere, when she saw it.

The hem of the curtain, on the window side, hung wrong.

It was a small wrongness, the kind ninety-nine people out of a hundred would walk past forever. But the Saltglass's curtains were hung by Samir, who was fussy about them the way monks are fussy about prayer — each hem weighted, each fall identical. This one pooled a half-inch heavy against the floor. Ulyana rose, crossed to the window, and drew the curtain back along its rod.

Pinned inside the fold, low, where only a kneeling person or a person who knew to look would ever find it, were two things.

A flower. Pressed flat between two small squares of wax paper, a violet — the flower itself, five petals gone papery and pale, the color drained out of it by years in the dark until it was less purple than the memory of purple. Old. Decades old, if the brittleness of the paper meant anything. Someone had kept this a long time before pinning it here.

And beneath it, folded in thirds, a single page torn from a book.

* * *

She carried both to the desk and unfolded the page under the lamp, though the fog-light was nearly kind enough to read by.

Vey's memoirs. *Letters from the Founding: Being the Correspondence & Reminiscences of Aldous Vey, Founder of Marrow Bay.* The town's secular scripture; every gift shop in Marrow Bay sold it beside the saltwater taffy. Ulyana knew the book the way she knew the hotel register — by shape, by habit, without affection. The page was from the middle chapters, the letters of 1893, Vey's account of the winter the town's founding trust was established. She knew because the passage was famous, quoted on the historical society's plaque, recited by schoolchildren at the Founder's Day pageant: *A town is a promise made in stone and kept in weather.*

Someone had circled a letter.

Not a word. A letter. In pencil, in a hand so light and controlled it might have been drawn with a draftsman's compass: the final *e* of *weather*, looped around once, the circle closing precisely where it began. Below it, in the margin, a tiny mark — a looping flourish, descending, curling back under itself like a wave deciding to become a knot.

Ulyana stared at it, and the back of her neck prickled with recognition before her mind caught up to why.

She'd seen that mark before. Not in books. In ink. In the guest ledger downstairs, entry after entry, year after year, in the same careful hand: *Mr. Ilya, Rm 214.* He signed with his name and then, always, that little flourish underneath — a signature's tail so distinctive she'd once teased him about it, early on, and he'd looked at her with those sunken eyes and said, "It's how I know my own name is mine."

At the time she'd taken it for the odd poetry of an odd man. Now it sat on the page in front of her, identical, and the room seemed to tilt a degree on its foundations.

Mr. Ilya had read this book. Had marked this letter. Had pinned a thirty-year-old violet behind the curtain of the room where he'd mailed the order that cracked Marrow Bay's founding myth open like a geode.

Or — the colder thought, arriving uninvited, wearing Corliss's voice — Mr. Ilya wanted her to find exactly this, exactly arranged, exactly incriminating.

She sat down in the desk chair, which complained the way all the Saltglass's chairs complained, and laid the violet and the page side by side, and made herself think like a concierge instead of a woman whose sanctuary had developed cracks. What did she actually know? A guest, six years in residence, quiet, punctual with his payments, gaunt as a winter branch, who took his meals in his room and walked the garden at dusk. Who had mailed — allegedly, supposedly, according to a postmark — a legal exhumation order from this address. Who was either the author of today's scandal or its intended scapegoat.

And who had left her a dead flower and a circled letter, which was either a confession, a message, or bait.

"A town is a promise made in stone," she read aloud, softly, "and kept in weather." Her finger hovered over the circled *e*. One letter. Why circle one letter? Unless it wasn't one letter. Unless somewhere else in this book — other editions, other printings, other pages — other letters were circled, and together they spelled something. She was a hotel woman, not a scholar, but she knew codes the way she knew luggage: you didn't need to understand them to recognize their silhouette. And this had the silhouette.

The knock, when it came, made her flinch — actually flinch, hand to throat, which was absurd, which was the tell of a guilty woman, and she knew it even as her heart hammered, and she hated herself for it.

"Miss Nystrom." Sheriff Dane Corliss's voice through the door, flat as the cove in January. "Door was unlocked. We should talk."

* * *

He filled the doorway the way authority fills doorways — not large, exactly, but occupying. Dane Corliss was fifty-ish and going soft at the jaw, with his father's heavy brow and his father's way of looking at a person slightly to the left of their eyes, as if the true face floated just beside the visible one. He wore his uniform the way some men wear apologies: correctly, and without conviction. His hat was in his hands. That was new. Dane Corliss without his hat was a man trying to look like a neighbor instead of an officer, and Ulyana had served enough difficult guests to know that the attempt itself was the warning.

"Sheriff." She didn't rise. Let him come into the room; let him see her seated at the desk, the violet and the page squared neatly before her. If she acted found, she'd be treated as found. "You've caught me doing housekeeping."

His gaze went to the page. Stayed there a beat too long. "That from the historical society's set?"

"It's from room 214. Which I'm responsible for. Which makes it hotel business." She let that sit. "What makes it sheriff business?"

"The exhumation order was mailed from this hotel." He said it mildly, conversationally, the way you'd remark on weather, and somehow that was worse than an accusation. "From this room, per the return address. Room 214." He nodded at the walls around them. "Your guest's room."

"My former guest's room. He checked out."

"Did he." Corliss moved to the window, unhurried, and looked out at the fog the way locals look at fog — not seeing it, using it. "Funny thing, Miss Nystrom. Man checks out of your hotel, and the same week somebody mails papers that dig up the founder of this town and turn him into a box of rocks. Whole county's talking. Half of them asking who'd want to embarrass the Vey family." He turned. "Other half asking who'd benefit."

"And which half are you?"

"I'm the half that asks questions." He set his hat on the desk, deliberately, next to the violet — crowding it, she thought, or maybe she was imagining slights now, seeing intent in hat placement, which was what suspicion did to a careful mind, it made everything evidence. "Where were you last Tuesday?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Last Tuesday. She cast back automatically, the way she cast back over ledgers — Tuesday, fog all day, the boiler acting up, Samir bleeding the radiators while she handled a booking dispute with the Merrick wedding party. Ordinary. Documented. Every hour of her life at the Saltglass was documented; the hotel was her alibi the way it was her home, and she understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that this cut both ways. A woman who never left her hotel had nowhere else to have been.

"Why do you ask, Sheriff?"

"Routine." He picked a piece of lint off his sleeve. "We like to account for everybody near a matter. Standard."

"Standard," she repeated. "Dane. I've known you since you were a boy stealing penny candy from the lobby jar. Your mother stayed here every autumn for nineteen years. You're standing in my hotel telling me I'm routine."

Something moved behind his face — quickly, and gone. Not guilt exactly. The cousin of guilt. The expression of a man carrying something heavy in a family where everyone carries something heavy and nobody sets theirs down.

"That's why I'm telling you first," he said quietly. "Off the record, unofficial, man-to-neighbor. There'll be others asking, and they won't ask nice. The Hale family's already been in touch. The Vanes. When a thing like this touches the founding families, Miss Nystrom, it doesn't stay a curiosity. It becomes a wound." He picked up his hat. "So. Last Tuesday. For your own good."

"I was here," she said. "All day. Ask my staff. Ask the Merrick bride, who screamed at me for forty minutes about table linens. My whole life is a witness list, Sheriff. Can you say the same?"

He paused at the door. For a moment — one unguarded moment, there and gone like a fish turning under gray water — he looked tired. Truly tired, in the marrow, in the way of men who have inherited a debt they don't fully understand and pay interest on anyway.

"No," he said. "I can't."

Then he was gone, boots fading down the corridor, and the door clicked shut behind him, and Ulyana sat alone in room 214 with a pressed violet and a circled letter and the first cold taste of what it meant to be a suspect instead of a solver.

* * *

She stayed a while after that. Long enough for the fog to thicken against the glass and turn the garden lights into smudged halos. Long enough to notice, finally, what she should have noticed at the start — what the room had been saying all along beneath the camphor and the lemon, beneath the curated emptiness.

The chair by the window was angled toward the glass. Toward the garden. Toward the bench beneath the old rowan tree where a gaunt man in a gray coat walked every evening at dusk, alone, feeding nothing, watching nothing, simply moving through the fog like a man keeping an appointment with the dark.

Six years of evenings. Six years of a dying man watching another man walk.

Ulyana looked down at the memoirs page, at the circled *e* sitting inside its perfect pencil ring, and understood that she was holding the first word of a sentence — and that somewhere out there, in old bookshops and estate sales and the damp private shelves of Marrow Bay's oldest families, the rest of the words were waiting.

She wrapped the violet and the page in her pocket handkerchief, turned off the lamp, and went downstairs to make a telephone call to the county archive, where an archivist named Petra Osei had once mentioned — laughing, over bad coffee — that Aldous Vey couldn't spell his way out of a paper bag, and that his printers had been even worse.

Every edition, Petra had said. Every printing, a new crop of misprints. Somebody ought to compare them sometime.

Somebody was about to.
