## 4. The Cipher in the Letters

Ulyana Nystrom had learned long ago that a hotel was never quiet — it only pretended to be. Even at six in the morning, with the fog pressing its gray face against the lobby glass and the radiators ticking like patient clocks, the Saltglass murmured. Pipes spoke in the walls. The elevator hummed to itself on the third floor, thinking about things. Somewhere above her, in room 214, the dust she had left deliberately undisturbed sat waiting for whoever might come back to disturb it.

She stood behind the front desk with the torn page from Vey's memoirs flattened under the brass lamp, and she read the circled letter for the hundredth time.

It was an *f*. A small, unremarkable *f* in the word *fog*, printed in the 1987 edition of *Letters from the Waterline: The Memoirs of Aldous Vey*, founder, benefactor, pillar of Marrow Bay. Someone had drawn a careful circle around it in pencil so old the graphite had gone soft as breath. And that was all. No note. No name. Just one circled letter and a violet pressed flat as a secret between pages that smelled of another decade.

"You're doing it again," said Samir, coming through the service door with a tray of coffee cups he hadn't been asked to bring. He set one beside her elbow without ceremony. "The staring."

"I'm not staring. I'm reading."

"You're reading one letter."

"One letter is often enough," Ulyana said. "A whole confession can hide inside a single wrong character. I've seen guests check out under the wrong name because of one letter. I've seen marriages end over one letter left on a nightstand."

Samir considered this, then drank his own coffee. Outside, the fog moved down Harbor Street in slow procession, swallowing the cannery's rusted silhouette, the church spire, the bronze statue of Aldous Vey himself — arm outstretched toward the sea he'd supposedly loved, though Ulyana had always thought the pose looked less like welcome and more like a man hailing a boat that never came back for him.

Three days since the exhumation. Three days since the coffin had come up out of the frozen ground holding nothing but river stones and a coat gone soft with rot, and the whole town had stood there in the wind making sounds it didn't know it was capable of. Three days since Sheriff Dane Corliss had turned the exhumation order over in his gloved hands and let Ulyana see — or thought he was letting her see — the return address typed neatly at the top.

*Saltglass Hotel. Room 214.*

Her sanctuary, mailing orders to open graves.

"Petra called," Samir said. "Twice. She says it's urgent, which from Petra means she found something, and from anyone else would mean the building's on fire."

"Then we should go before it's both."

* * *

The Marrow Bay Historical Society occupied the old customs house at the foot of Pier Lane, a granite building the color of a storm's intentions, and Petra Osei ruled its basement the way small monarchs rule: absolutely, affectionately, and with a card catalog no one else was permitted to touch. She was waiting for them at the bottom of the stairs when Ulyana came down into the smell of paper and cold stone, and there was something in her face Ulyana had never seen there before — not the usual archivist's calm, but the bright, barely contained ferocity of a woman who has caught the truth by the tail and is being dragged.

"Close the door," Petra said.

Ulyana closed the door.

Petra led them past the maritime charts and the whaling ledgers to a reading table where four copies of *Letters from the Waterline* lay open side by side, weighted down with brass rods. Four identical books, Ulyana would have said. Same navy cloth binding, same gilt anchor stamped on the cover, same portrait of Vey scowling handsomely at middle age. But Petra laid her dark hand on them one after another like a dealer laying out cards.

"1974," she said. "First printing. 1981, second. 1987, third — that's the edition your page came from. And 1994, the memorial printing they rushed out after he died." She paused, corrected herself with a small, bitter smile. "*After he supposedly died.* Now look at page forty-one. All of them. Look at the fourth line."

Ulyana leaned over the table. The passage was the famous one, the paragraph every schoolchild in Marrow Bay could recite — Vey describing the morning he chose the site for the town, standing on the bluff while the fog lifted off the water *like a curtain rising on something holy.* She read the line in the 1974 printing. Then the 1981. Then the 1987. Then the 1994.

They were not the same.

In the first edition, the fog rose off the water **like** a curtain. In the second, **liek**. In the third, **lkie**. In the fourth, **ekil**.

"It's a typo," Samir said slowly. "Four typos."

"Thirty-one typos," Petra said. "Across four editions, in eleven passages that repeat through the book — Vey quotes himself constantly, the vain old peacock, the same anecdotes recycled in letter after letter. Every time those passages reappear, the 'misprint' changes. Different letters scrambled, different positions." Her finger moved across the table, tapping each copy in turn. "Typesetters make mistakes, yes. But typesetters do not make *systematic* mistakes. They do not scramble the same word four different ways across twenty years of printings. This was designed. This was built into the plates themselves, or corrected by hand at proof stage, by someone who knew exactly what they were doing."

"A cipher," Ulyana said. The word came out of her quietly, the way you name a thing you've already begun to fear and admire at once.

"A cipher," Petra agreed, and now the ferocity in her face broke open into pure joy, the joy Ulyana imagined she must have shown as a girl, whatever girl she had been before Marrow Bay took her in. "But here's the beautiful part — the part that kept me up until three in the morning, the part I nearly telephoned you about at three-thirty and only didn't because I know how you feel about the telephone before dawn. It's not a cipher you can solve from any single book. You need *all* the editions. The key isn't hidden in the text. The key is hidden in the *differences between the texts*. Each printing carries a fragment. Only by comparing them — setting them side by side, letter against letter, error against error — does anything emerge. Which means Aldous Vey built a message that could only be read by someone who collected him. Who owned every version of his life. Who watched how his story changed over time."

"And who would do that?" Samir asked.

"Scholars," Petra said. "Fanatics. Historians." She looked at Ulyana. "And a man who needed to leave instructions he could be sure would survive him, but that no casual reader would ever stumble over. Think about what we know now. The coffin was empty. He didn't die in '94 — or whenever the official date is; I'm still pulling the death certificate, and interestingly, the file at the county office has been checked out and never returned, by someone whose signature I am still working on. If Vey faked his death, if he ran, if he needed someday to tell someone where he went and why and what was buried with the stones — he couldn't write it down plainly. Plain letters get read. So he wrote it crooked. He scattered it across his own memoirs, edition by edition, trusting that someday either he'd come back for it, or someone with patience and a table big enough would sit down and compare."

Ulyana drew the torn page from her coat pocket and laid it on the table, the circled *f* uppermost. "Room 214. Whoever stayed there left me this. One letter, circled. Why one letter?"

Petra bent over it. For a long moment the only sound was the building settling around them, the harbor groaning somewhere overhead, and then Petra went very still in the particular way she did when the archive had just handed her something enormous.

"Because it's not a letter," she said. "It's a *position*. Third edition, page forty-one, fourth line — the circled letter marks which misprint matters, in which printing. It's a pointer. An index entry. Whoever left this wasn't giving you a piece of the cipher, Ulyana. They were giving you the *table of contents*. They were telling you where to start reading."

* * *

After Samir left — sent back to hold the desk, though he went reluctantly, glancing at the four open books like a man leaving a séance early — Ulyana helped Petra carry the rest of the society's holdings up from the deep shelves. There were seven copies of the memoirs in the collection altogether, including two first printings and a battered advance copy with Vey's own corrections penciled in the margins, and Petra handled that last one the way midwives handle newborns.

It was while they worked that Ulyana noticed the gaps.

The catalog cards told the story the shelves couldn't. The historical society had acquired copies steadily for decades — donations from estates, purchases at the annual book sale, the slow sediment of a town's reverence for its founder. But the acquisition ledger, when Petra pulled it, showed something stranger. Over the past fifteen years, private buyers had been approaching the society again and again, offering generous prices for any duplicate editions of *Letters from the Waterline*. Always polite. Always cash. Always the same request: *anything you can spare, particularly the earlier printings.*

"Fifteen years," Ulyana said, running her finger down the entries. "Someone's been harvesting these books since before I ever set foot in this town."

"Longer, maybe. These are just the offers the society recorded. God knows what's been bought privately — estate sales, the library discard shelf, the bookshop on Cordage Street. Old Halvorsen used to say a gentleman came through every autumn asking after Vey first editions, paid whatever was asked, never haggled." Petra closed the ledger. "I always assumed a collector. Vanity biographers, that sort of thing. Now?" She shook her head slowly. "Now I think someone has spent fifteen years trying to gather up every copy of the key. Not to read the cipher. To *destroy* it. To make sure no table in the world could ever hold all four editions side by side again."

"The fire," Ulyana said.

"What fire?"

"Historical society storage annex burned eight years ago. Before my time at the desk, but the older staff still talk about it. Officially electrical." She met Petra's eyes. "How many copies did the society lose?"

Petra was already moving, pulling the old insurance inventory from a filing cabinet with the urgency of a woman counting her own pulse. Her finger traveled down the list and stopped.

"Eleven," she said. "Eleven copies of the memoirs. Including two first printings." She looked up, and the joy had gone out of her face entirely, replaced by the cold clarity of a historian who has just recognized the shape of the thing she's excavating. "Ulyana. Someone burned a room full of books eight years ago, and everyone in this town nodded and blamed the wiring. And now the coffin comes up empty, and your guest in 214 leaves you a map to the cipher, and within seventy-two hours Edmund Hale is asking questions at the society about Vey's correspondence — he was here Tuesday, I forgot to mention it, he wanted to know if we held any of Vey's *original* papers —"

"He was here Tuesday," Ulyana repeated. The day the exhumation order was mailed. The day the paperwork bore her hotel's address.

"— and now Hale is dead in the marsh," Petra finished, quieter. "Or he will be. Corliss hasn't released anything yet, but Marta heard it from the ambulance dispatcher's wife, who heard it from the dispatcher. Three days dead, they're saying. Clutching paper."

The lamp buzzed above the reading table. In its light the four editions lay open, their small corruptions gleaming — *like, liek, lkie, ekil* — four ways of saying the same word, none of them true, all of them deliberate. A town, Ulyana thought, was much the same. Four versions of the founding story, told by four founding families, each one slightly misspelled, and the truth living only in the space between them.

"Then we don't have years," Ulyana said. "We have days. Whoever is buying the books has decided that gathering is no longer enough. Can you reconstruct the cipher? From what's here — seven copies, plus my page, plus whatever fragments survive in private hands?"

Petra looked at the table, at the scattered lifework of a man who had staged his own death and salted his own legend with errors, and something in her spine straightened.

"I can try," she said. "But Ulyana — if I'm right about what this is, if these differences spell out what I think they spell out, then the person who's been collecting these books for fifteen years is going to notice a concierge and an archivist checking out the complete works of Aldous Vey in the same week." She began, carefully, to close the 1974 edition. "So before we start, I want to know one thing. When they come — and they will come — are we readers? Or are we the next fire?"

Ulyana thought of room 214, swept clean and waiting. Of the pressed violet. Of the gaunt man in the corner chair by the lobby window who signed his name with a strange looping mark and slept, as far as she could tell, hardly at all.

"We're readers," she said. "But keep the extinguisher close."

* * *

That evening, back at the Saltglass, Ulyana climbed the stairs to the third floor with a fresh copy of the 1994 memorial printing tucked under her arm — purchased that afternoon from Cordage Street Books, the last surviving copy in the shop's back room, sold to her by old Halvorsen with a look she was still turning over in her mind.

Room 214 stood open and unswept at the end of the hall, exactly as she'd left it. She entered, set the book on the desk beneath the window, and opened it to page forty-one, fourth line.

*Ekil,* said the fog.

She took out her pencil. And in the silence of the room where someone had once mailed an order to open a grave, Ulyana Nystrom began, letter by careful letter, to write down everything that was wrong with the story of Aldous Vey.

Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, the front door chimed — a guest arriving, late, unhurried, letting himself in with a key he should not have had.

She did not go down to greet him. Not yet.

But she marked her place first.
