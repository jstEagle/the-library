## 16. Ilya's Last Night

I have died once already, and it was the easiest thing I ever did.

Dying properly — now, at last, with the fog pressing against the window of room 214 like a creditor who will not be put off — is harder work. It takes all night and most of my remaining strength, and still I am not finished when the knock comes.

Three knocks. Soft, precise, spaced like the syllables of a word she has said to me a hundred times: *Good evening, Mr. Ilya.*

I know her step on the stairs. Thirty years of listening to footsteps in hotel corridors teaches a man things he never wanted to know. Ulyana Nystrom walks the way she does everything — deliberately, without apology, as though the floorboards should be grateful for the attention. Tonight they creak under her anyway. Everything in this old house creaks tonight. The Saltglass is settling into the cold like an animal curling up to sleep, and I am settling with it.

"Come in," I say, before she can knock again. "You've been standing out there for four minutes. Your floor squeaks on the third board. You always stop there."

The door opens. She fills the frame the way weather fills a doorway — not large, but total. Her coat is still buttoned. She has come from somewhere. From the cannery, I would guess, from the ledgers, from the truth I buried so carefully that even I sometimes forget where the grave is.

"You knew I was coming," she says.

"I've known you were coming for thirty years." I gesture to the chair by the window. "Sit down, Miss Nystrom. Or may I call you Ulyana? We are past registers, I think."

She does not sit. She stands with her back to the door, which is sensible of her, and studies me the way she must study every guest who crosses her lobby — cataloguing, weighing, filing away. I have watched that look for years from behind newspapers and teacups and the fiction of a tired old man named Ilya who never slept well. I told myself she never looked at me twice.

I was lying to myself. She looks at everyone twice. That is her gift and her curse, and tonight it is mine.

"The exhumation order was mailed from this hotel," she says. "Room 214. My hotel. And for six years you have signed my register with a mark that isn't a signature at all — it's a key. A cipher key built out of the misprinted letters in Aldous Vey's memoirs." Her voice does not rise. It lowers, if anything, until it is almost gentle, which frightens me more than shouting would. "So before I ask you anything else, I'm going to ask you this. Who are you?"

*Who are you.* What a question. What a luxury, to be asked it plainly after three decades of being no one.

I am very tired. The illness in my chest has been patient all these years, the way debts are patient, and lately it has stopped waiting politely. Every breath costs me more than the last. I could lie again. I have had enormous practice. But there is a strange freedom in having nothing left to protect — the body finally keeps its promises, and a man whose account is overdrawn everywhere else can afford honesty.

"Would you like the long answer," I say, "or the one printed on the founding stone in your town square?"

Her eyes narrow. "Say it."

"Aldous Vey," I tell her, "at your service. Founder. Benefactor. Embezzler. The man in your square with his hand extended over the harbor as though he were giving Marrow Bay to itself, when in fact he was picking its pocket while it slept." I spread my hands — thin now, spotted, the hands of a stranger. "And the man who let an innocent dockworker hang for what I did. That part I did not plan. That part I simply allowed. Which is worse, I've decided. Planning can be repented. Allowing just gets comfortable."

The fog breathes against the glass. Somewhere below, Samir is doing his rounds; I hear the particular rhythm of his keys, third-floor first, always. They never asked questions, these people. Six years of my coughing and my insomnia and my odd hours, and the staff of the Saltglass asked me nothing. Marta brought tea without being asked. Samir carried my trunk up the stairs himself when the lift was broken and refused a tip. Petra — well. Petra is another matter, and we will come to her.

They gave me a life without ever asking me to explain it. I do not know how to repay that except by telling the truth to the one person sharp enough to have earned it.

Ulyana has not moved. "Prove it," she says quietly.

"There is no proof left. That was rather the point." I reach for the drawer of the bedside table, and my hand shakes — age, illness, and something older than either. "But there is this."

The letter is where it has been since Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth because I have learned to fear fire. I hold it out to her and watch her decide whether to take it. She takes it the way she takes everything — firmly, without apology.

"One page?" she asks.

"One page. The last of them. Vey's letters were published in '89, and I salted the printings with errors — a wrong letter here, a transposed line there — because a man planning to disappear wants a map only he can read. Each edition carries a piece. Compare enough printings and the errors spell out a route, a name, a confession. I spent years buying up copies through intermediaries, then realized I'd made myself the only man alive who could read my own cipher, and stopped caring who else collected them." I smile, and it hurts my face. Vanity is the last thing to go. "Arrogance, Miss Nystrom. The founding sin of Marrow Bay. I had it in abundance."

"And this one?"

"This one isn't a map. It's a naming." I lean back against the pillows and the room tilts gently, the way the harbor used to tilt when I was young and drunk on my own cleverness. "Thirty years ago I did not die. I went to sea on a Merrick trawler with a coffin full of stones waiting ashore, and four families helped me vanish — Hale, Merrick, Corliss, Vane. Because I had embezzled the founding trust, and because I had framed Tomas Ferreira for it, and because if I fell, I would not fall alone. Their alibis for the week I 'died' are perfect because they spent that week building my death. Edmund Hale drove me to the boat himself. He held my hand at the rail. He wept."

I have to stop. Not for breath — for the memory. There are things a man carries so long they grow roots in him, and pulling one out tears something loose.

"Edmund tried to confess," I go on. "Last month. He came here — did you know that? Sat in your lobby with his hat in his hands and asked to see Mr. Ilya, and I sent down word that I was unwell. I was a coward for one more week, and it cost him his life. Someone killed him in the marsh and left him holding a scrap of violet-scented paper, because someone has been watching me far longer than you have, and someone does not want the last letter read."

Ulyana's fingers tighten on the oilcloth. "Marta," she says.

The name lands in the room like a dropped glass.

"No," I say. "No — listen to me. Whatever you suspect, the letter will tell you, and I need you to hear it from me first. Marta Ferreira lost her father to my crime. If she has done terrible things in thirty years of grief, then I built the machine and handed her the crank. But the person who found my coffin empty — the person who mailed that exhumation order from this very room, who has been burning the editions one by one, who killed Edmund Hale when he grew a conscience — that person is not avenging anything. Revenge burns hot and spends itself. This has been *patient*. This has been fed on us for decades. Read the letter, Ulyana. It names its author. And when you read the name—" my voice fails, and I press on, "—when you read it, you will want to burn this room down with me in it. I'm asking you not to. Not yet."

She unfolds the oilcloth. Unfolds the page. Reads.

I watch her face the way I have watched faces all my life — a founder's habit, reading a town the way other men read weather. I see the recognition arrive. I see the refusal arrive right behind it, chasing it, trying to catch it and drag it back. I see her jaw set against whatever the name is, set hard, the way a woman sets her jaw when the truth has walked into her own house wearing slippers she knows by sound.

"No," she says softly. To herself, not to me. "No. Anyone but—"

She stops herself. Good. Some doors should stay shut until you're ready for what's behind them.

"It can't be right," she says, louder now. "Your cipher could be wrong. Thirty years old, misprinted letters, half the editions burned — how would you even verify—"

"I wrote it," I say. "It is not wrong."

She looks at the page again, and I watch something break in her and something else take its place, something harder and sadder and steadier. This is why I chose her, in the end — not chose, I had no choice, but trusted. She breaks cleanly, like good ice, and freezes back into shape around the crack.

"Why give it to me?" she asks. "Why not burn it? Why not die quiet, the way you've lived quiet? One more month and the fog takes you and nobody ever knows."

"Because Edmund Hale is dead because of me. Because Tomas Ferreira's daughter grew up fatherless because of me. Because a town has spent thirty years laying flowers on a box of rocks." I pull myself up against the headboard, and the effort costs me, and I pay it. "I faked my death to escape prosecution, Ulyana. I succeeded completely. Do you understand what that means? It means justice never touched me. Not the law's justice, not God's, not my own. I have lived six warm years in the kindest building on this coast, drinking tea brought by people who never asked my business, and every kindness has been a sentence I haven't finished serving. Giving you that letter is the last installment. After that, whatever happens to Aldous Vey is finally fair."

"So run." She says it fast, and I hear what's underneath it — not mercy exactly, something more complicated, loyalty at war with law. "You're dying anyway. Take the staff car, take the morning boat, die wherever you like with a false name and a clean exit. I'll write the certificate myself. I'm apparently good at paperwork from this room."

For a moment — a real moment, a whole heartbeat — I consider it. The sea is out there. The sea took me once and gave me back a new man, and it would take me again, kindly, without questions, the way this hotel has never questioned me.

"No," I say.

"They'll kill you."

"Perhaps. Probably. The person in that letter has killed once already and burned twice, and I am the last copy of the crime." I meet her eyes. "But if I run, the letter becomes a dead man's accusation and the families bury it a second time, deeper, and Marta Ferreira's father stays a thief forever, and your hotel becomes a place where a fugitive hid and no one had the courage to say so. I am done running. Running is the only thing I was ever truly good at, and I refuse to die practicing it."

The fog presses closer. Downstairs, faintly, the grandfather clock in the lobby strikes eleven, and each note travels up through the bones of the building like a pulse.

"Then what do you want?" Ulyana asks. And it is not a concierge's question. It is a priest's, or a friend's.

"Tomorrow," I say. "Tomorrow evening, I want to walk down your stairs — slowly, mind, Samir will insist on helping and I'll allow it — and I want to stand in your lobby one last time and look at the sea through the front windows. And then I want you to take me to the cannery."

Her head comes up sharply. "Absolutely not."

"He'll expect me to run or hide. Instead I'll come to him, with you, in the open, and end it. I'm the only bait that works, Ulyana. I'm the only thing in Marrow Bay that person has wanted for thirty years." I smile at her horror. "Besides — I'm worth more to you alive until tomorrow night, and worthless after. Even a murderer respects arithmetic."

"That's not funny."

"No," I agree. "Very little of this has been. That was rather the tragedy of it. I meant everything as a joke, once. The cipher, the letters, the grand disappearing act — I thought I was wittier than consequences." I close my eyes briefly. The room swims. When I open them she has stepped closer, and the lamplight makes a small gold country around us both, an island in the blue dark of the fog. "There is one more thing. In the bottom of the oilcloth. A pressed violet. I've kept it thirty years. It was on the pillow of the bunk I sailed away in. I never learned who put it there. I've wondered every day since."

She goes still. Utterly still, in the way of someone hearing their own name spoken in a voice they thought was silent.

"Ask your sisters," I say gently. "Or don't. Some kindnesses are better left unsigned."

She puts the letter inside her coat, over her heart, which is where I hoped she'd keep it. At the door she stops, hand on the knob, and does not turn around.

"Mr. Ilya," she says — using the false name deliberately, tenderly, a gift of continuity — "if you die tonight, I will be very angry with you."

"Then I'll try to be inconvenient a little longer," I tell her. "It's the one talent I have left."

The door closes. Her footsteps cross the third board, descend the stairs, fade into the lobby's hush. I am alone with the fog and the clock and the enormous, light, terrifying fact of having told the truth.

I sleep better than I have in thirty years, and dream of a boat, and a rail, and a violet on a white pillow — and wake near dawn to a sound I know too well, a sound that has followed this secret across three decades like a dog that cannot be lost:

Smoke.
