## 1. Alphabetized Secrets

The red light on the mixer has been staring at me for eleven minutes, and I've been staring back, the way you do with a dog that might bite.

"Take three," I say, mostly to myself. "Or four. I've lost count."

I know what my listeners would say if they could see me right now — all forty thousand of them, give or take the ones who subscribe just to leave one-star reviews calling me a vulture. They picture a podcast studio as something sleek. Foam panels in tasteful gray, a microphone that costs more than a used car, maybe a neon sign with a clever slogan. What they'd get instead is my spare bedroom: a desk built from two sawhorses and a hollow-core door, shelves bowing under the weight of banker's boxes, and a wall of index cards arranged alphabetically by surname like some kind of shrine to other people's misery.

Quade, Marisol. Card 1 through Card 214.

Two hundred and fourteen cards. Seven years. One missing girl.

I press record again, and this time the words come.

"This is *The Quiet Part*, episode one hundred. I'm Coral Voss." A pause — I've learned to let pauses do the heavy lifting. "One hundred episodes. When I started this show, I figured I'd run out of things to say by episode twelve. Instead, I ran out of town willing to talk to me somewhere around episode nine, and everything since has been archaeology."

The microphone is a Rode NT1 I bought secondhand from a church that was upgrading its sound system for choir practice. It smells faintly of hymnals. Everything in this room smells faintly of something else — old coffee, cardboard, the cedar blocks I put in the boxes to keep the damp out. My whole life is a storage solution for a story nobody here wants told.

"For those just joining: on the night of June 14th, seven years ago, Marisol Quade, seventeen years old, walked out of her shift at the Bayside Freeze at 9:41 p.m. She never made it home. Her house is a twelve-minute walk. Eleven, if she cut through the church parking lot, which she did most nights because the gravel there doesn't ruin your sneakers the way the beach path does. We know this because her mother, Dolores Quade, told me so, in interview number six, timestamp 34 minutes and 12 seconds, and because Marisol's sneakers were found on the porch the next morning, wet, laces still tied."

I glance at the card. I don't need to. That's the thing about cataloging a town's silence for seven years — eventually the silence starts talking to you in your sleep.

"The police theory, such as it was, held that Marisol went into the water. Accidental, voluntary, criminal — they never quite committed to a flavor. The tide took her, they said. The tide keeps its own records, they said. And then, nineteen months later, a sixteen-year-old boy named Isandro Reyes confessed to killing her, and the town exhaled so hard it blew every open file shut."

I reach for my coffee and remember, mid-reach, that I drank it an hour ago. The mug sits there anyway, a prop in the theater of my competence. Some habits are load-bearing.

"That confession is where this show began, though I didn't know it yet. Because I was there, sort of. I was twenty-six, working nights at the *Gazette*, writing obituaries and little league scores, and when Isandro Reyes stood up in a courtroom and said the words *I did it*, I wrote down the date. That's all. Just the date, in the margin of a page about zoning permits. I didn't know why it mattered. I still don't, entirely. But I have learned to trust the part of me that writes things down."

Here's what the listeners don't hear, what no amount of editing can put into an episode: the way this room feels at 2 a.m., when the only light is the monitor's glow and the only voice is mine. Here's what they can't know: that I moved back to this town seven years ago for a funeral that wasn't Marisol's, that I stayed for reasons I've stopped examining, that the alphabetical wall of cards is the closest thing I have to a family portrait.

My mother used to say I organized things because I couldn't organize people. She said it like an accusation. She lives in Tucson now, sends a birthday text at 11:58 p.m. on the day itself, and we are both fine with the arrangement. The town, meanwhile, has made its own arrangements with me. There's a version of me that exists in Port Lachlan's collective imagination: the podcaster, the ghoul, the man who profits off a dead girl — except she isn't dead, officially, which makes it worse somehow, makes me a man who profits off an absence. At the hardware store, the clerk rings up my purchases without meeting my eyes. At the diner, they seat me by the kitchen door, near the noise, as if I'm something to be hushed.

I keep recording anyway. Not spite. Spite burns too fast. This is something slower. Call it devotion, if you want to be kind. Call it compulsion if you want to be accurate.

"So tonight, on our hundredth episode, I want to do something different. I want to read you the silences."

I pull the first box toward me. Inside, cassette tapes — yes, cassettes; I started cheap and got superstitious — each labeled in my handwriting, dated, numbered. Interview 1: Dolores Quade. Interview 3: Ray Tullis, marina owner. Interview 9: Father Bram, who remembered Marisol cutting through his parking lot and remembered nothing else, ever, about anything, with remarkable consistency.

"Over seven years, I sat down with sixty-one people. Forty-three agreed to be recorded. Eighteen spoke off the record, and I have honored that, mostly, in ways I'll explain another time. Of the forty-three, thirty-one told me versions of the same story: a girl walked home and vanished. But twelve of them — twelve — paused before answering. Not hesitated. Paused. There's a difference. Hesitation is doubt. A pause is a decision."

I let that sit in the air, then move to the wall of cards. The camera — I film these now, for the video feed — catches my hand as I tap the Q section.

"Card 88. Mrs. Aldous, retired schoolteacher, taught Marisol in tenth grade. On tape, she says: 'She was a sweet girl.' Off tape, walking me to her car, she says: 'Ask the Halloways why their foundation paid for the Reyes boy's lawyer.' Then she gets in her car and doesn't speak to me again for five years."

The name lands in the room the way I knew it would. Even alone, even in a spare bedroom in a rented house on Cordgrass Lane, saying *Halloway* aloud feels like dropping a glass. The Halloways are Port Lachlan's first family the way some towns have a first church or a first fire — foundational, load-bearing, untouchable. Richard Halloway's face is on the hospital wing. Evelyn Halloway's garden tours raise more money in an afternoon than my podcast has raised in awareness in seven years. Their son Daniel runs the foundation now, handsome as a bank brochure.

And their money, according to a retired schoolteacher who then drove away very fast, may have bought a sixteen-year-old boy a lawyer whose job was not defense.

"I'm not saying that tonight," I tell the microphone. "Not yet. Tonight I'm saying this: I have spent seven years learning the shape of what this town won't say. I've mapped it. I could draw you a map — and in fact, I have. It's behind me. Two hundred and fourteen index cards, and every blank space on them is a place where someone decided silence was safer than speech. Episode one hundred is dedicated to the blanks."

I look at the wall. Honestly, sometimes I think the wall looks back. Two hundred and fourteen small white rectangles, and between them, the negative space of a whole town holding its breath.

"Marisol Quade would be twenty-four years old today. She liked mint chocolate chip, she wanted to study marine biology, she had a laugh that her mother describes — quote — 'as louder than she meant it to be.' Somewhere out there is the truth of what happened to her, and it is being kept by people who pause before they answer. I intend to keep asking until the pausing stops."

I stop the recording. The red light dies. The room goes quiet in that particular way a room does after you've spoken truth into a microphone — heavier, somehow, than before.

* * *

Later, I do what I always do after an episode drops: nothing, deliberately, for one hour. No stats, no comments, no refreshing. An hour of grace before the numbers start telling me whether anyone cares.

I use the hour badly, as usual. I stand at my kitchen window with a glass of water I won't finish, looking out at Cordgrass Lane, where the streetlights come on one by one like witnesses deciding, reluctantly, to testify. Down the block, the Alvarez kids are riding bikes in figure eights. A dog barks at nothing with total conviction. Normal life, performing normalcy.

Seven years ago I came back here for my father's funeral, planning to stay a week. I sorted his affairs into boxes — of course I did — and somewhere in the third box I found the clipping: *LOCAL TEEN STILL MISSING*, yellowed, folded, tucked inside a fishing magazine he'd never gotten around to reading. My father, who spoke to me maybe four hundred words a year, had kept it. I don't know why. Maybe he clipped it because it was local news. Maybe he kept it because he couldn't do anything else about it either. I stayed because of that clipping, and I've never once managed to explain that to anyone without sounding unwell.

The hour ends. I check the numbers.

Episode one hundred is moving. Faster than usual — by midnight it's outpacing my best-performing episode in the first hour, and the comments are coming in warm. People love a milestone. *Congratulations, Coral.* *Seven years, incredible dedication.* *Never stop asking.*

And then, at 12:47 a.m., this:

**Anonymous:** *You should do an episode on the clothes. Ask him whose clothes he was wearing.*

I stare at it. The phrasing is strange — *the clothes*, definite article, as if I'd know which clothes. As if everyone would. I type a reply, delete it, type another, delete that too. Rule one of seven years of this: never feed the weird ones. Most anonymous commenters are lonely people practicing cruelty or fans practicing intimacy, and neither deserves oxygen.

But this one nags at me the way the margin note about the confession once nagged at me. Something about the specificity. Something about *him*.

I refresh. The comment is gone. Deleted by the user, the interface tells me, primly.

I sit in the dark kitchen for a long time, listening to the refrigerator hum its one note. Then I get up, walk back to the studio, and pull the box marked MISC — UNFILED, because of course I have one; even my chaos is archived. I flip through tapes and printouts and a dead phone I keep meaning to wipe, looking for nothing in particular, feeling for whatever it is a fisherman feels when the line goes taut.

At 1:30 a.m., my phone rings. Unknown number. Nobody calls me at 1:30 a.m. except wrong numbers and my mother, and my mother texts.

I let it ring out. It starts again immediately.

On the fourth ring, I answer, because I am, above all else, a man who writes things down, and a man who writes things down needs material.

"Coral Voss," I say.

The voice on the other end is young, roughened, careful. A voice that has rehearsed this call.

"You're the one who does the show," it says. "About Marisol. I need to tell you — the confession I made? I need you to know it wasn't—"

And that's when the headlights sweep across my kitchen window, slow, deliberate, a white hand passing over everything I own — and the line goes dead.
