## 11. Family, However Found

### Coral

The phone rang at 2:47 in the morning, and I want it on the record that my first thought was not *fire*. My first thought was *Solano has finally found a legal pretext to arrest me*. That's what seven years of true-crime podcasting does to your threat assessment: you stop fearing the obvious catastrophe and start fearing the procedural one.

"Coral." Isandro's voice, stripped down to wire. "The house is burning."

"Your halfway house?"

"The whole thing. They got everybody out, but—" A cough, wet and wrong. "I can't go back there tonight. I can't be on the street."

I was already pulling on jeans, already doing the math I'd been avoiding for weeks: one spare room, currently functioning as an archive annex, currently full of banker's boxes labeled in my own handwriting like the world's most depressing garage sale.

"Come here," I said. "Don't take the main road. Cut through the lot behind the church."

"You think they're watching the roads?"

"I think whoever set that fire is having a very good night, and I'd rather not let them enjoy it longer than necessary."

He arrived twenty minutes later smelling of smoke and adrenaline, wearing a borrowed sweatshirt from some other resident and carrying everything he owned in the world in a plastic grocery bag. I looked at that bag — a bag, singular — and something in my chest did a slow, ugly turn. Seven years I'd spent cataloging Marisol Quade's absence, and I had somehow missed the boy living three towns over whose entire life fit in produce packaging.

"Bathroom's down the hall," I said. "Towels are clean. The shower does this thing where it alternates between scalding and glacial every ninety seconds, so treat it like interval training."

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. "You always this hospitable at three a.m.?"

"No. Usually I'm insufferable about it too, but I'm pacing myself."

* * *

He slept until noon, which told me more than any interview ever had. People who are safe don't sleep like the dead; people who haven't been safe in years do. I spent the morning being normal on purpose — coffee, dishes, the small theater of domesticity — because I remembered, from my own stretch of unbelonging, that the kindest thing you can offer someone is evidence that ordinary still exists somewhere.

When he finally surfaced, hair wrecked, eyes clear, he stood in my kitchen doorway and surveyed the operation: the corkboard, the timeline ribbon, the wall of index cards arranged alphabetically by surname because — yes — I alphabetize my secrets. It's a system. Don't judge me.

"This is what you actually do," he said. Not a question.

"This is what I actually do."

"My lawyer said you were a crank with a microphone."

"Your lawyer sounds perceptive. Was he also bought, or just wrong?"

That landed. I watched it land. Isandro Reyes had a face built for concealment — flat, watchful, giving away nothing at retail — but underneath it there was a kid who'd learned at sixteen that the truth was a currency he couldn't afford. He crossed the kitchen, pulled a chair from under the table without asking, and sat down across from me like a man taking a seat at his own trial.

"The fire wasn't electrical," he said. "I checked the panel when we evacuated. Old building, sure, but that panel was new. Somebody paid for a renovation and then torched it anyway."

"Insurance fraud by the owner?"

"Maybe. Or maybe somebody wanted a dozen ex-cons scattered around town with nowhere to sleep, so the cops would know exactly where to find each of us." He shrugged. "Or maybe they wanted me specifically and didn't care who else got smoked out."

"And you came to me anyway."

"You're the only person in this town whose interest in me isn't a debt," he said simply. "Everyone else either owes me or thinks I owe them. You just... want to know what happened. That's the first honest thing anyone's offered me since I got out."

I didn't have a response prepared for that, which for me is roughly equivalent to most people fainting. So I did what I do instead of feeling things: I opened a box.

"Takeout first," I said. "Then files. House rule. Nobody reads about arson on an empty stomach."

* * *

We ate pad thai off the tops of evidence boxes because I own four plates and needed two for the noodles. Somewhere between the spring rolls and the section of the file marked *QUADE, M. — WITNESS STATEMENTS (INCONSISTENT)*, something shifted. I can't timestamp it precisely. These things don't come with timecodes.

It started when he read a statement aloud — his own, age sixteen, transcribed in the flat bureaucratic voice that makes even terror sound like paperwork — and stopped halfway through.

"I don't talk like this," he said. "'I proceeded to the vicinity of the quarry.' I was sixteen. I said 'I went down by the rocks.' They wrote it fancy so it would sound rehearsed. Like I'd practiced."

"They made it sound rehearsed so it would *be* credible," I said. "A spontaneous confession is messy. A tidy one reads better in court."

"A tidy one reads better in court," he repeated, softly, like he was filing it somewhere permanent. Then he looked up. "You noticed that. In seven years of episodes, you never once called me a monster."

"I called everyone a monster. It's kind of my brand. But the evidence never pointed at you. It pointed at a story people preferred."

He laughed — a short, startled bark, like the laugh had escaped custody. And then, God help me, we worked. For six hours. He knew things no transcript held: which cops walked him to which rooms, who offered him the soda, who mentioned his mother's health problems in a tone that wasn't quite a threat. I knew things no witness held: the dates, the money shapes, the way the Halloway Foundation's charitable disbursements bent toward certain surnames like iron filings toward a magnet.

Between us, on my kitchen table, the case stopped being a cold file and started being a living animal.

Around nine p.m., he fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, one hand still resting on a photocopy of his own confession. I put a blanket over him and stood there a moment feeling something I hadn't felt since I was a kid waiting for a father who kept promising to come back for weekends he never took.

This, apparently, is what family is. Not blood. Not obligation. Just someone asleep at your table, trusting you with their back turned, while outside the window a town that prefers its lies comfortable plots against you both.

I turned off the lights and let him sleep.

* * *

### Isandro

Here's what nobody tells you about prison: you get good at sleeping anywhere, but you forget how to sleep *safe*. There's a muscle for it. Seven years inside, mine atrophied to nothing. First night in Coral Voss's guest room, I lay awake listening to the house settle, cataloging exits, counting the seconds between car sounds on the street outside.

Second night, I woke up at noon with drool on the pillow and no memory of falling asleep.

I sat there on the edge of the bed feeling stupidly, dangerously grateful, and I hated that. Gratitude is a leash. I learned that young. Every kindness in my life came with an invoice attached — my guardian's roof, the state's mercy, the Halloway money that bought my silence dressed up as a settlement. You learn to read the fine print before you say thank you.

But Coral didn't seem to keep invoices. He seemed to keep *files*. Which, honestly, might be worse.

By day three I'd stopped pretending I was leaving. He'd cleared half a shelf for my stuff — all eight pounds of it — and hung a second hook by the door without comment, the way you'd hang a hook for someone who lived there. I noticed it. I pretended not to notice it. That's how men in my family say things.

We developed a rhythm. He talked in paragraphs; I talked in sentences. He narrated his research like a man reading aloud from his own brain; I corrected him when he romanticized. ("You can't call the fixer 'a ghost in the machine,' man, he drove a Camry.") He took notes on my corrections. He actually took notes.

And every night, takeout containers between us, we rebuilt the crime.

That fourth night, we were deep in the marina victim — the dead man in Coral's clothes, the one Solano wouldn't tell us about — when Coral spread out the financial pages and tapped one line.

"Look at the timing. The foundation payment to your old guardian clears on a Tuesday. Your confession is entered Wednesday morning."

"So?"

"So confessions aren't entered overnight. There's processing. Review. The paperwork was ready *before* you confessed. Someone wrote the ending first and waited for you to sign it."

I stared at the line. I'd seen these numbers before, in discovery, in lawyer-speak, in seven years of turning them over in my bunk at night. But laid out on a kitchen table next to lo mein, next to a man who'd stayed up past midnight because my life mattered enough to ruin his sleep schedule — they looked different. They looked like what they were: a receipt. Proof I'd been purchased.

"They'll burn your place next," I said. It wasn't pessimism. It was forecasting.

"Probably," Coral said cheerfully, which is the most disturbing thing about him. "I've got duplicates of everything in three locations. Habit. My dad was a paranoid accountant; some gifts skip a generation and land on the grandkid."

"You don't talk about your family much."

"There's not much to talk about. Estranged before it was trendy." He gathered the papers into a stack, squaring the edges, always squaring the edges. "You?"

"My mom died while I was inside. I found out from a chaplain who pronounced her name wrong." I said it flat, because flat was how it survived being said. "So. No."

Coral was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "Well. This house runs on pad thai and grudges. Room and board's free if you alphabetize."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

It was a little funny. I laughed anyway, and it didn't feel like escaping custody this time. It felt like something getting returned to me.

Later — much later, past two a.m., both of us running on caffeine and righteous anger — he pinned the last card to the board and stepped back. The whole shape of it hung there: the Quade girl, the false confession, the money, the fire, the body in the marina wearing another man's jacket. One line connecting all of it, leading back to a family whose name was on half the buildings in town.

"They hid her backpack somewhere," I said. "I've been thinking about it all week. You don't destroy proof like that — not if you might need leverage someday. You *store* it."

"Store it where?"

"Someplace theirs. Someplace nobody's looked in seven years because nobody wants to look at the Hallaways that hard." I was thinking of the boathouse as I said it. I'd been thinking about the boathouse for days, the shuttered one on the point, tarp over the windows, padlock older than me. "Coral. What do you know about the Halloway boathouse?"

He turned from the board slowly, and I watched the same thought arrive in his face that had arrived in mine, and for one warm, stupid second we were just two brothers-in-arms standing in a kitchen full of Thai food and dynamite.

Then his phone buzzed on the table. He glanced down, and the warmth went out of the room like someone had opened a door onto winter.

"What?" I said.

He held the screen where I could see it. An unknown number. Four words:

**STOP DIGGING. START PACKING.**

Below it, a photograph — taken from across the street, tonight, through Coral's own front window. Two men at a kitchen table, bent over evidence, lit up like specimens.

Whoever sent it wanted us to know they could see us.

They were right. We could see us too. And for the first time in seven years, so could everyone else — if we lived long enough to show them.
