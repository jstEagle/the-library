## 18. Taken

The diner on Delmore Street still had the same pie carousel it had when I was sixteen, which felt like an insult. Everything else in my life had been disassembled and reassembled wrong — my record, my name, the entire architecture of who people decided I was — but the pie carousel turned on, indifferent, offering lemon meringue to a town that had eaten me alive.

I'd started coming here because Coral Voss liked the corner booth. He didn't know that yet. Or maybe he did; the man noticed everything except how obvious he was when he noticed things. He'd sit with his little recorder and his two coffees — one for himself, one going cold across from him, because he couldn't stand an empty table — and he'd ask questions like he was defusing a bomb. Patient. Gentle. Sweating anyway.

Tonight he wasn't there. Tonight I was alone with a slice of cherry pie I hadn't ordered and a waitress named Dot who remembered me from before, back when "before" meant something different. She'd put the pie down without a word and squeezed my shoulder on her way past, and I'd understood that this was her version of an apology for seven years of everyone else's silence.

That's the thing nobody tells you about being the town's designated monster: kindness arrives in these small, furtive doses, like contraband. A shoulder squeeze. An unordered pie. You learn to live on crumbs because the whole loaf was never offered.

I was three bites in when the man slid into Coral's booth across from me.

He was big in the way furniture is big — present, functional, designed not to draw attention. Gray jacket, gray eyes, a face you'd struggle to describe to a sketch artist five minutes after leaving the room. I knew him anyway. Not by name. By *category*. Every town like ours has one, the man who does the errands respectable people can't be seen doing. Seven years ago, he'd been the one who drove me to the lawyer's office where I signed my life away in exchange for a promise that never got kept.

"You've gotten tall," he said.

"You've gotten old," I said. "Funny how time works."

He smiled like a door closing. "Mr. Halloway would like a word."

"Then Mr. Halloway should buy a phone like everybody else." I kept my voice level and my hands visible on the table, because I'd learned in prison that the fastest way to escalate a situation is to make someone think you're hiding something. My hands were shaking anyway. I hoped he couldn't see it. I hoped Dot could see it, actually, and would call someone, anyone, though even as I thought it I knew how pathetic the hope was. Nobody calls the police about a man having a friendly chat over pie. That's the genius of men like this. They commit their crimes at a volume only the victim can hear.

"He's old-fashioned," the fixer said. "Prefers face-to-face."

"Tell him I prefer breathing free air, and we'll both be disappointed."

He stood, dropped a twenty on the table next to my pie — payment, insult, threat, all in one gesture — and said, quietly enough that only I could hear it: "You can walk out to the car on your own feet or arrive some other way. Your choice. But you're coming."

Here's what I want on the record, since apparently records are the only thing that survives in this town: I considered running. I want that noted. I sat there with my heart slamming against my ribs like something trying to get out of a locked room, and I genuinely weighed the options, and I want credit for the math I did in those four seconds.

Running meant he caught me in the parking lot, or worse, he didn't catch me and went looking for softer targets. Coral. Dot. The halfway house guys who already looked at me like I was radioactive. Seven years ago I'd gone quiet because they told me quiet was the price of survival, and quiet had bought Marisol Quade nothing but a shallow grave and a town full of comfortable lies. I was done purchasing other people's comfort with my silence.

So instead of running, I finished my pie.

All of it. Fork scraped the plate clean. Then I wiped my mouth with the napkin, stood up, and said, "Fine. But I'm driving."

"You're not driving."

"Didn't think so. Worth asking." I left the twenty under the plate. Whatever else happened tonight, I wasn't going to owe Daniel Halloway's errand boy so much as a slice of cherry pie.

* * *

The car smelled like pine air freshener and old cigarettes, which is the smell of every bad decision ever made in this county. He put me in the back seat. There was another guy driving — younger, quieter, the kind of silent that isn't calm but rehearsed — and I filed them away the way I'd learned to file everything inside: guard rotations, shift changes, which COs took bribes and which took offense. Observation had kept me alive for seven years behind walls. It would keep me alive now, or at least it would make sure somebody got a very detailed description.

"Seatbelt," I said, and buckled it, and the fixer almost laughed.

"You're a strange kid."

"I'm twenty-three. And I'm not strange. I'm thorough."

We pulled out of the diner lot and turned left onto Delmore. Left again onto Cane. I repeated the turns in my head, building the map the way you build a fire — one small piece at a time, protecting the flame. Left on Delmore. Left on Cane. The driver took Cane south past the shuttered textile mill, its windows boarded up like the building itself had closed its eyes.

"So," I said, because silence was a luxury I couldn't afford — silence was where they did their best work on me last time — "does Daniel pay you by the job or by the year? Because if it's salary, I have notes about your benefits package."

The fixer turned around in his seat. Up close his eyes were pale as pond water. "You want to be careful with that mouth."

"Do I?" I leaned forward. "Here's the thing you should understand about me. Seven years ago, a man exactly like you — maybe it was you, honestly, you all blur together — told me that if I just said the words, if I just confessed, everything would be fine. I'd go home in a couple years. They'd take care of my mom. Remember that part? They promised to take care of my mother." My voice cracked on *mother*, and I hated it, and I let it crack anyway because anger sounds better with grief underneath it. "She died thinking her son killed a girl. So no, I don't want to be careful with my mouth. My mouth is the only thing I have left that they haven't taken."

Something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Men like him don't feel guilt — I don't believe they're built with the equipment — but they recognize leverage when they hear it, and they know when a kid has stopped being afraid of them, and that scares them more than anything.

"Daniel just wants to talk," he said.

"Sure. And I just want a public apology, a pardon, and my mother back. We're all wanting things tonight."

He turned back around. The driver took us over the rail crossing — the tracks sang through the tires, that low metallic hum I'd know blindfolded — and then right onto the old quarry road, and my stomach dropped out from under me like an elevator cable snapping.

The quarry.

Of course the quarry. Where it happened. Where Marisol Quade spent her last night on earth while a terrified sixteen-year-old recited a script written by lawyers. Daniel Halloway wasn't taking me somewhere to talk. He was taking me back to the scene, because men like Daniel need symmetry. They need the story to end where it started, tidy, with the loose end walked to the water and folded under.

I memorized the turn anyway. Right on the quarry road, past the rusted NO TRESPASSING sign that had been falling over since before I was born. Two miles of gravel. The headlights jumped with every rut.

Think, I told myself. Think the way Coral thinks. He'd taught me that without meaning to — sitting across from me in that booth, showing me case files, saying *evidence is just attention made permanent*. If I got out of this, it wouldn't be because I was strong or fast or lucky. It would be because I paid attention and left a trail.

So I left one. Subtle. When we slowed for the cattle grate, I pressed my thumbnail hard into the leather of the seat until it tore a small crescent. At the fork where the road split toward the water treatment plant, I unhooked my keychain — house key, halfway-house key, a stupid plastic skull Coral had won me at the county fair two weeks ago, back when we were both pretending this was almost over — and let it slip between the seat cushion and the door frame, silent, invisible, waiting for whoever came looking. Coral would come looking. Solano would come looking. I had to believe that, the way you believe in a rope while hanging.

"What happens if I don't get out of this car?" I asked. Conversationally. Like asking about the weather.

The fixer didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Because here's my problem," I went on. "If I disappear, Coral Voss releases a recording. Did you know about the recording? He's been making me do these sessions, everything I remember, everything I saw that night, timestamped, witnessed, uploaded to three different servers. If anything happens to me, it goes out automatically. That's the arrangement." This was ninety percent bluff. There were recordings, yes — Coral was religious about them — but no dead-man's-switch, no automatic release. But bluffs work best when they're built on true foundations, and Coral's paranoia about backups was legendary. "So killing me doesn't clean anything up. Killing me is the one move that guarantees everything comes out. You might want to mention that to your boss before he does something sentimental."

The driver's eyes flicked to the mirror. The fixer's jaw worked.

"That's a lie," he said finally.

"Probably," I agreed. "Can you afford to bet on it?"

Neither of them spoke for the rest of the drive, and I counted that as a win. Fear travels uphill. Somewhere ahead of us, Daniel Halloway was about to receive a message he hadn't planned for: the boy he'd bought at sixteen had learned, in prison, in poverty, in the long slow school of being abandoned by everyone, that a man with nothing left to lose is the most dangerous asset in any negotiation.

The gravel crunched. The trees opened up. Through the windshield, silvered by moonlight, I could see the quarry — black water lying flat and patient as a held breath, the limestone walls rising around it like the inside of a throat.

They'd brought me back to the beginning.

Good, I thought, and my hands had stopped shaking, and I don't know when that happened. Good. Because beginnings are where stories get told, and this time, whatever happened on that shore, I was going to be the one telling it.

The car stopped. The fixer got out first and opened my door, and beyond his shoulder, standing at the edge of the dark water with his hands in the pockets of an expensive coat, waiting for me like I was late for an appointment seven years overdue —

— was Daniel Halloway.

And he was holding a shovel.
