## 3. Person of Interest

The dead man at Pier 9 was wearing a better jacket than I've ever owned.

I want that on the record before anything else, because it matters to how the morning went sideways. Homicide detectives are not supposed to notice fashion. We're supposed to notice ligature marks and lividity patterns and whether the victim's shoes are tied — untied shoes mean they were put on him by someone else, tied shoes mean he dressed himself, and either way you learn something about the last hour of a life. But I stood over that body in the gray-pink light coming off the harbor, and what I noticed first, before any of the textbook things, was that the jacket fit him wrong.

Not badly wrong. Wrong the way a borrowed coat is wrong. The shoulders sat a half-inch wide, the sleeves broke just past the wrist bone instead of at it, and when the ME's assistant turned the man gently onto his side I could see where the seamstress had taken in the back panel — recently, by hand, with thread that didn't quite match. Somebody had tailored this jacket for a body that was not this body.

Somebody had tailored it for a narrower frame. A leaner one. Somebody like, say, the night dispatcher from Marisol Cabs who'd been standing at the police tape for forty minutes looking like a woman watching her own funeral from a polite distance.

Oscar Vasquez. I had the name from the responding officers before I'd even arrived. First on scene after the cab driver called it in, cooperative, articulate, and — here was the part my notebook underlined twice — wearing clothes identical to the ones on the corpse. Same charcoal wool jacket. Same oxford shirt, rolled at the cuffs. When the uniforms asked them about it, Oscar had said, flat as a tide table, "It's my closet. Apparently he shopped there."

You meet a lot of people at crime scenes. Most of them perform. They perform grief, or outrage, or helpfulness, or the studied blankness of people who've watched too much television and think shock looks like stillness. Oscar Vasquez wasn't performing anything. That was the unsettling part. They looked genuinely wrecked — not scared-wrecked, which is what guilty people usually manage, but something rawer, like the world had handed them a bill they couldn't itemize. And underneath all of it, threaded through the shock like wire through cable, was a current of recognition so strong I could almost hear it humming.

They knew that jacket. Not because it hung in their closet. Because they knew *why* it was on a dead stranger, or they knew why it might be, or they were afraid they knew. One of those three. My money, at that hour, was on all three.

* * *

The medical examiner's office smells like coffee and formaldehyde in roughly equal measure, and Dr. Priya Nandakumar has worked enough homicides to know that the detective standing next to her wants the highlights first and the poetry later. She gave me the highlights over two dead men's worth of morning.

"Male, mid-forties to early fifties," she said, pulling back the sheet to the collarbone. "No ID. No wallet, no phone, no keys. Nothing in the pockets except lint and a ticket stub."

"A ticket stub?"

"Marisol Cabs." She handed me an evidence bag. Inside was a scrap of thermal paper, water-warped, most of the print gone. What remained read: **MARISOL CABS — FARE 0047 —** and then nothing. No date. No destination. Just the number, sitting there like a dare.

"Forty-seven," I said.

"You've got a good face for numbers, Detective. Yes. Forty-seven." She let the sheet back down. "Cause of death is drowning. Saltwater in the lungs, diatoms consistent with harbor water, so he went into the bay alive and breathed it in. Time of death somewhere between eleven p.m. and two a.m., based on stomach contents and water temperature. Here's your first problem."

She turned his head. There was a bruise along the jaw, dark purple going green at the edges, and another blooming over the left temple, mostly hidden by hairline.

"Contusions," she said. "Perimortem — inflicted within hours of death, but *before* he entered the water. And before you ask: no corresponding water infiltration in the tissue damage pattern, no postmortem abrasion from the pier pilings. These happened while he was alive and upright, probably from a fist or a short blunt object, and then somebody put him in the bay. Or he fell in while running away from whoever hit him. Either way, drowning is the mechanism, but assault is the story."

"Second problem?"

"The tailoring." She said it without irony, which is one of the hundred reasons I respect her. "Clothes are good quality, well maintained, but altered. Taken in at the waist and shoulders, sleeves shortened. Whoever owned these originally is two inches shorter through the torso and maybe twenty pounds lighter than our guest here. This isn't a man who bought a suit off a rack. This is a man wearing someone else's suit, adjusted to fit him — or adjusted badly, anyway. The alterations are competent but rushed. Recent."

A drowned man in borrowed clothes, beaten before he went into the water, carrying a taxi receipt with a fare number and nothing else. No wallet. Nobody drowns accidentally without a wallet unless the wallet was taken, and nobody takes a wallet and leaves a receipt unless the receipt means nothing to them and everything to someone else.

Or unless the wallet never existed, and the receipt was left deliberately, like a signature. Like an address.

"Any prints?" I asked.

"On the stub? Nothing usable. On the body, we're running them through AFIS now, but the water's been chewing on his fingertips. Could be days. Could be never." Priya pulled the sheet fully up, a small kindness to a man who'd had very few lately. "Whoever he was, Detective, somebody wanted him unidentifiable. The clothes are the only fingerprint he's got left. And the clothes belong to your witness."

"My suspect," I corrected, mostly out of habit.

"Mm." She gave me the look doctors give lawyers. "Is that what they are yet?"

* * *

Here's what I had at nine a.m., laid out on my desk in the order I'd stacked it, which is to say the order of my own stubbornness:

One. A John Doe, drowned, assaulted pre-mortem, found at Pier 9 by a Marisol driver named Terrence Boyd, whose account was clean and boring and matched the dispatch logs exactly.

Two. Those same dispatch logs, which showed Oscar Vasquez working the night line until 11:40 p.m., logging out, and — per their statement — walking home alone via the waterfront, because they liked the air. Their apartment was a twelve-minute walk from Pier 9. The body went into the water inside their window. Everyone's window, technically; the piers are public. But windows are windows.

Three. A wardrobe match between witness and victim that no innocent explanation had yet survived contact with. Oscar claimed the obvious one — theft, someone rifling their locker or laundry. Possible. People steal clothes all the time. But you don't tailor stolen clothes. You don't pay a seamstress to take in a dead man's jacket unless the jacket matters, unless the *wearing of it* matters, unless somebody wanted a body found at Pier 9 dressed as Oscar Vasquez.

And four — the thing I kept turning over like a stone in my pocket — the way Oscar had said *"it's my closet"* with no inflection at all. Flat. Controlled. The voice of someone holding a door shut from the inside.

I'm not a cynic by nature. The job makes you a skeptic, which is different; cynics believe nothing, skeptics believe things provisionally and check. So I checked. I ran Oscar Vasquez through every database I could reach before lunch. Thirty-four years old, no record, not even a parking ticket. Ten years at Marisol Cabs, started as a switchboard operator at twenty-three, promoted to night dispatcher at twenty-six. Before that, foster system — a string of placements, a group home, emancipation at eighteen. No family on paper. Which meant the found family was real and load-bearing: the retired driver who'd come down to the tape and stood beside them like a wall, Ruthie Okafor, thirty years behind the wheel, hands like tree roots. The kid who answered the night line, some teenager named Dev whose existence I only knew from the dispatch logs' after-hours entries. And Sal — Salvatore Quintero, the founder, dead fourteen months, heart attack, whose name came up in every interview like a saint's.

People don't lie well about saints. They lie *around* them. Every time I mentioned Sal Quintero, the room got a degree warmer and everyone's sentences got a degree shorter. I wrote that down too.

* * *

I went back to Pier 9 at dusk, alone, which captains hate and which I do anyway because scenes talk differently when the circus has packed up. The tape was gone, the techs were gone, the gulls had reclaimed the pilings. Low tide had dragged the waterline back fifty yards of wet, gleaming mud, and the harbor was doing its evening trick of looking gentle while hiding everything it had ever swallowed.

I walked the length of the pier. Pier 9 is cargo traffic, mostly — container cranes, chain-link, a security booth that closes at six. Not a place a tourist wanders. Not a place a man in a tailored jacket strolls at midnight unless he was meeting someone, or being delivered, or delivering.

At the pier's midpoint, where the body had been found snagged against a ladder rung, I crouched and looked at the water the way you're taught to look at a witness: patiently, letting it decide what to show you. The tide tables said high water had come at 1:14 a.m. The body had surfaced at dawn on the ebb. Meaning it had gone in around high slack — the window where the current goes quiet and stops arguing with whatever you drop into it. Someone had known that. That's not luck. That's local knowledge, the kind you accumulate over years of watching this harbor, the kind dockworkers and fishermen and, yes, cab drivers who work the waterfront for a decade tend to have.

I stood there longer than I needed to, and I'll admit what I was actually doing: I was replaying Oscar Vasquez's face. The moment the uniform had lifted the tape and they'd seen the covered stretcher. There's a specific expression people make when a fear they've been carrying becomes official — relief and terror arriving in the same envelope. I'd seen it maybe a dozen times in eleven years. Oscar had worn it for about a second and a half before burying it under exhaustion, and I would have missed it entirely if I hadn't already clocked the jacket.

Professional curiosity, I told myself on the drive back. That's all this was. A witness with a suspiciously good poker face and a wardrobe connection to the deceased. Textbook person of interest. I'd interviewed hundreds. I'd clear them or charge them and move on.

Except.

Except that when I'd taken their statement — and I took it myself, at the depot, because something in me didn't want a uniform's summary of those eyes — Oscar had answered every question straight on. Never dodged, never lawyered up, never once looked at the door. People with something to hide watch exits. Oscar watched *me*, with a frank, tired attention, like they were trying to figure out whether I was safe, and had concluded — I saw the exact moment they concluded it — that I was not, but might be the safest thing available.

Nobody decides that about a homicide detective unless they're deciding whom to trust with something too big to carry alone.

And except that when I asked, casually, "Did you know the deceased?" Oscar had paused. Not long. A quarter second, maybe less, the pause of a person checking whether a lie will hold weight before lifting it. Then: "Never seen him before in my life."

True, probably. I believed they'd never seen him. But the pause hadn't been about the man. It had been about the question underneath the question — *did you know he'd be there* — and Oscar Vasquez had flinched from that one like a hand near a stove.

* * *

The stub sat in my evidence locker overnight and followed me home in my head. Fare 0047. No date, no destination, no total. Thermal paper fades, sure — but this stub had been in a pocket, in saltwater, for hours, and the header was still legible. Whatever had eaten the rest hadn't been the ocean. The rest had been blank. A fare number with no fare attached.

I called the cab company's business office the next morning, playing dumb, asking for a copy of recent fare records. The woman who answered was pleasant and useless in equal measure. Records, she explained, were kept per-vehicle and per-shift, archived monthly, available upon request through their legal department, seven to ten business days. Seven to ten days. In a murder case. I thanked her, hung up, and sat with the arithmetic of my own irritation.

Because here's the thing I couldn't stop noticing: a cab company that can't produce a fare record quickly is either incompetent or curated, and Marisol Cabs had a reputation for neither. Thirty years in this city. The kind of operation where drivers leave their kids in the break room and retirees come back to volunteer. The kind of place that runs on trust the way other businesses run on capital. And trust, in my experience, is the best cover currency there is. Nobody audits a family.

Fare 0047. I wrote it on a card and pinned it above my desk, next to a photo of the John Doe and a printout of the tide tables. Three data points and a hunch. Somewhere in this city there was a ledger — literal or figurative — that connected a numbered fare to a drowned man in borrowed clothes, and the person most likely to know where that ledger lived was currently answering a night line across town, lying to me beautifully by telling me nothing at all.

My captain wanted a name on the board by Friday. I had one. I just didn't believe it yet, and the gap between what I had and what I believed was starting to feel less like an investigation and more like an apprenticeship — like I was studying Oscar Vasquez the way you study a language, conjugating their pauses, translating their silences.

That should have worried me more than it did.

What actually worried me, though I didn't have words for it until later, was simpler: when I'd shaken Oscar's hand at the end of that first statement, they'd held on one beat longer than necessary, and looked at me with something that wasn't fear or guilt or calculation.

It was hope. Badly hidden, but hope. As if they'd already decided I was going to matter.

I got in my car, drove toward the harbor, and told myself I'd figure out why a dead stranger was wearing a dispatcher's clothes. I did not yet understand that I'd also just volunteered to find out everything else Oscar Vasquez was carrying — and that by the time I understood it, it would be far too late to want anything else.
