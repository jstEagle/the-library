## 13. Partners

I've been driving cabs longer than most of my doctors have been alive, and I'll tell you what nobody tells you about this city: it doesn't change. It just repaints itself and waits for you to forget where the bodies went.

That morning — the one after Oscar came to me with questions I'd been dreading for thirty years — I sat in my kitchen with a cup of coffee gone cold and a photograph gone soft at the corners, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the tide gave up that drowned boy at Pier 9.

Four of us built Marisol Cabs. Four. And now one of us was dead, one was asking questions with her dead mentor's cipher in her pocket, and the other two were men whose names got printed in the paper next to words like *philanthropist* and *civic leader* instead of words like *accomplice*.

Loyalty is a funny thing. Everybody talks about it like it's a warm coat. Nobody mentions that a coat can be nailed shut.

* * *

Nineteen eighty-something. The year doesn't matter; the weather does. It was raining the night Sal Ambrose knocked on my door with a business plan written on a diner placemat and a grin like he'd already won.

"Ruthie Okafor," he said, "you are the best driver this city has ever failed to appreciate, and I am going to fix that."

Sal was twenty-six and looked nineteen and talked like a man selling you the bridge he planned to build. He had a notebook full of figures that didn't add up yet but would someday, and he had two friends waiting in his car — because Sal never traveled alone, even then. Even when there was nothing to hide.

"Who's in the Buick?" I asked.

"The money and the muscle," he said. "Come meet them. You're going to love them. That's an order."

The money was Emilio Vasquez, who even at twenty-four wore a suit that fit him better than his own skin and shook my hand like we were closing on a house. He had a way of looking at you that made you feel *included*, which I learned later is not the same as feeling welcome. Inclusion is a technique. Welcome is a gift. Emilio only ever gave the first one.

The muscle was Marcus Hale, who wasn't muscle at all, really — he was charm, which is muscle for people who don't want calluses. Marcus could talk a cop out of a ticket, a landlord out of a month's rent, a woman out of a bad marriage and into a worse one. He laughed at everything, especially things that weren't funny, and I noticed early — before anybody — that the list of things Marcus found unfunny was very short and very specific.

"So," Sal said, spreading the placemat on my kitchen table between the four of us, "Marisol Cabs. Named after my mother, who drove a laundry truck for forty years and never once got thanked. We run it clean, we run it fair, we take care of our drivers, and in ten years we own the harbor district."

"In ten years," Emilio said mildly, "we own more than that."

And Sal laughed, because Sal laughed at everything too, and I remember thinking: these two laugh the same way. I didn't know yet what that meant. Give me a break. I was twenty-nine and flattered that four people wanted to build something together. You don't audit your family at the founding dinner.

We signed nothing. That should have told me something too. Sal said paperwork was for people who expected to betray each other, and we all toasted to that, and God forgive me, I believed him.

* * *

The first five years were the best years of my life, and I include my wedding in that calculation, which my ex-husband would find insulting and accurate.

We worked out of a garage on Delancey with a hand-painted sign and a radio crackling like a campfire. Sal dispatched half the time and drove the other half. Marcus charmed the city into giving us stands at the hotels. Emilio handled — well, at the time I thought he handled *licensing*. Later I understood that "licensing" was a word Emilio used the way a magician uses a scarf: something to watch while the real work happened underneath.

And me? I drove. Nights mostly. I knew every pothole from the shipyards to the stadium, every doorman's name, every shortcut that saved ninety seconds and every alley that cost you your hubcaps. When drivers got in trouble — a fare wouldn't pay, a drunk got handsy, a rookie got lost in the container yards after dark — they called for Ruthie, and Ruthie came.

That was my job before I ever had a title. I kept people safe. Hold onto that, because it matters later, and because it's the only piece of myself I've managed to keep unbroken through all of this.

The ghost fares started small. A pickup logged at 2 a.m., no fare recorded, driver paid cash from a separate envelope. Sal called them "favors." "Somebody needs a quiet ride," he'd say, and hand you a card with an address and a time and nothing else. No name. No destination spoken aloud — you'd get a second card halfway through, or sometimes the passenger would tap your shoulder and point.

"Who are these people?" I asked him once, maybe year three.

"People who need to not exist for an hour," Sal said. He wasn't smiling. Sal not smiling was rarer than snow in June, and it scared me more than any answer would have. "You want to know, Ruthie?"

I thought about it honestly, which I regret to this day, because honesty is how they get you. If I'd lied and said yes, maybe I'd have learned enough, early enough, to stop it. Instead I said, "No. Just tell me they're not being hurt."

"They're running *from* hurt," Sal said. "That I swear."

And here's the thing — I believed that too. Because the passengers I carried, the few I ever saw, were frightened in a particular way. Not guilty-frightened. Hunted-frightened. A woman with a bruise blooming under her powder. A man who flinched when I took a corner too fast near a police cruiser. Kids, once, two brothers, holding each other's sleeves like the other might be pulled away by wind.

Sal was saving people. I let myself believe that, because it was true, and because it was so much easier than the other thing, which was also true, which was that salvation makes excellent cover.

Nobody smuggles cargo in a cab company without somebody noticing. But everybody forgives a saint.

* * *

Emilio changed first. Or maybe he didn't change — maybe he just stopped pretending, which looks like change if you weren't paying attention at the founding dinner.

It was the year the port authority expanded, the year the piers got renumbered and rezoned, and Emilio started coming around the garage in better suits, talking about "opportunities" and "partnerships" with men whose names I wasn't supposed to remember. The ghost fares thickened. Where Sal's favors had been scattered — a ride here, a ride there, always people, always frightened — now there were routes. Regular runs. Times that repeated like a heartbeat. And the envelopes got fatter, and Sal got quieter, and Marcus got funnier, which I eventually understood was his tell. Marcus joked hardest when the water was deepest.

"You know what's in those crates?" I asked Marcus once, outside the garage, watching a night crew load a van that belonged to no route I knew.

"Crate of what?" He smiled his councilman smile, twenty years before he was a councilman. "Ruthie. Sweetheart. We drive people. That's the whole business. Everything else is above my pay grade, and my pay grade is *partner*." He squeezed my shoulder. "You worry like a grandmother. It's why we love you. Don't ever stop. But also — stop."

I want to be clear about what I did next, because I've spent thirty years telling myself it was something. I went to Sal. I said, "This isn't favors anymore. This is freight. Maybe worse. What did you sell?"

And Sal — Sal looked at me with the saddest eyes I have ever seen on a living man, and he said, "I sold my mother's name, Ruthie. Best I can do now is make sure it buys something worth having."

He never said yes. He never said no. He said things like that, koans wrapped in guilt, and I took them as confession and absolution both, because I needed to. Because by then Marisol Cabs wasn't a business. It was a family, and families don't audit each other, and besides — where exactly was I going to go? To the police? With what? A feeling? Some repeating numbers in a ledger I wasn't supposed to understand?

I stayed. I drove my nights. I collected my honest pay out of an envelope that wasn't entirely honest anymore, and I told myself the lie that keeps every cage door shut: *I'm staying to protect the ones who can't leave.*

Some of that was even true. That's the cruelty of it. A cage built entirely of lies, you can break. A cage with one true bar in it — that holds forever.

* * *

The dockworker. I have to say it now, so I might as well say it plain, though I haven't said it plain in thirty years, not even to Sal, not even to myself in the mirror.

His name was Tomás Reyes.

No relation to the detective — I checked, the week Isandro Reyes got assigned, I checked like a woman checking smoke alarms, because the universe has a sense of humor and I've learned to fear it. No relation. Just a man with a hard job and a young daughter and a habit of seeing things on the night shift at Pier 9 that respectable men preferred unseen.

Tomás came to Sal. That part I know firsthand, because he came to the garage while I was doing the books, and I poured him coffee, and he sat with his cap in his hands and said, "Mr. Ambrose, I know what goes on the boats. I don't want trouble. I want it to stop, or I want out, and either way I need to know I'm safe."

And Sal said, "You're safe, Tomás. I promise you. Nothing happens to you."

Three weeks later, Tomás Reyes walked off Pier 9 into the water, and the police wrote *accidental drowning* on a form, and Sal aged ten years in one night, and Emilio sent flowers to the funeral — *flowers*, I could have killed him — and Marcus made a joke at the wake that I didn't laugh at, and he saw me not laughing, and something passed between us that I have carried like a stone in my shoe ever since. Not guilt. Guilt would have been cleaner. It was *understanding*. Two people agreeing, without a word, that neither of us would ever ask the question.

The daughter. Elena. Sal handled that — Sal hid that child so well that I didn't learn she existed until years later, and even then only in pieces, in ledger entries I pretended not to read, in a monthly route Sal drove himself, always himself, rain or flu or bad knees. "Visiting my niece," he'd say, and I'd nod, and we'd both let the word *niece* stand for whatever it actually stood for.

Sal protecting that girl was the truest thing left in him. I believe that. I have to believe that, the way you have to believe a drowning man grabbed for something.

But here's what thirty years behind a wheel teaches you about the difference between a good man and a good intention: the road doesn't care. The road only records where you ended up.

* * *

Oscar came by yesterday. My Oscar, who dispatches like an angel taking dictation, who closes cases the way other people close doors — completely, and with a click. They stood in my kitchen holding a mug they didn't drink from and asked me about the old ledgers, about the repeating numbers, about whether Sal ever mentioned "the partners."

"Partners," I said, like the word was a fishbone. "Everyone was somebody's partner back then. It was the eighties."

They watched me with Sal's own eyes, which is the worst inheritance anyone ever left a person, and they said, "Ruthie. Who were the four?"

And I said, "Sal, me, and two men who turned out fine," and I smiled my practiced-warm smile, the one I use on fares who cry in the back seat, and Oscar nodded and left, and I stood at the window watching them cross the street toward the depot, toward answers, toward the exact place I've been standing guard for three decades.

Because here is what I couldn't say, what I will not say, what sits in my chest like ballast:

One of those drowned boys at Pier 9 — the one wearing Oscar's clothes, the one whose face I recognized from Sunday dinners at Emilio's table, because he was Emilio's sister's kid, Emilio's *nephew*, raised on stories about Uncle Emilio the important man — that boy had come to see me. Two weeks ago. Sat in this same kitchen, drank this same coffee, and asked me, nervous as a cat in a dog pound, whether the old ghost routes still ran.

"Why do you ask, sweetheart?"

"Because I saw something at the terminal I wasn't supposed to," he said. "And I think somebody's using the old system again. And I think if I ask my uncle, I'll be asking the wrong person."

I told him to let it go. I told him some doors are locked for a reason. I told him — God help me, I told him — *"Go home, mijo. Talk to your uncle. Family sorts these things out."*

Family sorts these things out.

I sent that boy to Emilio Vasquez with a question in his mouth, and two weeks later the tide brought him back dressed in a dispatcher's clothes, and every cop in this city is looking at my Oscar, who is innocent of everything except loving the wrong people thoroughly.

So this morning I did what I've done every morning for thirty years. I put on the coat. I buttoned the cage. I called Emilio's office the way I have every few months since the nineties — a check-in, a weather report, *everything's quiet, nobody's pulling threads* — except this time the voice on the line wasn't his secretary.

It was a man I didn't know, and he said, "Mrs. Okafor. Mr. Vasquez wants to know why the Ambrose girl is asking about the ledgers."

*The Ambrose girl.* Oscar. Sal's Oscar. Which means they've been watching, which means they know about Dev, which means the cage I've been keeping locked from the inside has been open from the outside this whole time, and everything I protected, I delivered.

I hung up the phone and my hands were steady, which surprised me. Old drivers' hands. They've held a wheel through black ice and held dying men on the way to hospitals and held this secret through three decades of tides.

I looked at the photograph on my table — four young idiots in front of a hand-painted sign, Sal grinning, Marcus mid-joke, Emilio's arm around my shoulders like we'd won something — and I heard myself say out loud, to the empty kitchen, in the voice I used to use on drunk fares and lost rookies:

"Hold on, baby. Ruthie's coming."

Then I picked up the phone again, and this time I dialed the number Sal made me memorize twenty years ago and swear I'd never use unless the water was over the windshield.

It rang twice. A young voice answered — wary, fast, whispering, the voice of a kid who knows the night line isn't safe anymore.

"Dev," I said. "Listen carefully. Get out of your apartment. Now. Take the rig and don't tell Oscar where you're going — not because Oscar's the danger. Because whoever's watching Oscar is about to come looking for everyone who loves them."

There was a pause, and then, small: "Ruthie... who *are* you?"

And that, children, is the question, isn't it. Thirty years late, I finally intend to answer it.
