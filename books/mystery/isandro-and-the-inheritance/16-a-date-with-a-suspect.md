## 16. A Date with a Suspect

### Isandro

There are a hundred ways to ask a suspect to dinner, and I'd rehearsed exactly none of them.

I stood outside the Marisol Cabs depot at seven in the evening holding two coffees like a peace offering, which was ridiculous, because I was the one who should be offered peace. The harbor wind came off the water carrying salt and diesel and the particular loneliness of a city that never quite sleeps but never quite wakes either. Through the glass, I could see Oscar Vasquez at the dispatch desk, headset on, one hand moving across a route map, mouth going — talking some driver through something. They looked, from this angle, like exactly what they claimed to be: a dispatcher doing their job on an ordinary Tuesday.

I didn't believe in ordinary Tuesdays anymore. Not since Pier 9.

The bell over the door announced me, and Oscar looked up, and I watched the whole sequence cross their face in about a second and a half: surprise, wariness, and then — this was the part that kept me up at night — something warmer underneath, quickly buried, like a light switched off before anyone could see it was on.

"Detective Reyes." They pulled off the headset. "Please tell me you're not here to arrest me. Dev's on the night line and he panics under pressure."

"I'm not here to arrest you."

"Then I'm cautiously optimistic." Their eyes dropped to the coffees. "Are those for me or are you just really into carrying beverages?"

"One's yours. One's mine. It's a set."

They took the cup. Our fingers didn't touch, which I noted with what I'd call professional disappointment if I were being honest, and I wasn't ready to be honest yet. "To what do I owe the caffeine?"

"Timelines," I said. "I need to go over your movements again. The night of the ninth. There are gaps."

"Gaps."

"Three hours where nobody can place you."

Oscar leaned back in the chair, and I watched them decide to be amused instead of afraid, which is a thing people do when they're smart and scared. "Detective, I work nights. Nobody can place anybody anywhere between two and five a.m. That's not a gap, that's a circadian rhythm."

"The diner on Alameda closes at eleven. You weren't home until two-forty according to your building's camera. Where were you?"

And there it was — that flicker. Not guilt. Something more like *calculation*, fast and quiet, behind their eyes. "Driving," they said. "Thinking. Sometimes I take the cab out after shift and just... drive. It's how I unwind."

"You don't log those trips."

"It's my uncle's company's cab, technically, but it's my gas money. Off the books." A small, tired smile. "You'd know all about off the books, wouldn't you? Isn't that why we're having coffee instead of a formal interview?"

Fair. All of it fair. I sat down on the edge of Ruthie's old desk — she wasn't in tonight; the depot felt emptier without her humming — and drank my coffee, and let the silence do some work. Oscar let it. That was another thing about them. Most people fill silence like a leaking hull fills with water. Oscar just floated in it, watching me back, patient as the tide.

"Here's the thing," I said finally. "I'm supposed to be narrowing my suspect pool. My captain uses the word 'focus.' Other people use other words. And every time I look at you, Vasquez, I can't make you fit."

"That's very flattering."

"I mean it as an observation, not a compliment."

"You delivered it like a compliment. You have a compliment voice. I bet you use it on witnesses all the time."

"I've never used it on a witness before."

The words were out before I could stop them, and Oscar went still, cup halfway to their mouth, and for a moment neither of us breathed. Then they set the cup down and said, lightly, too lightly, "So what am I, then?"

That was the first almost. The truth was sitting right there — *you're the person I think about when I should be thinking about the case; you're the reason I re-read your statement four times and it wasn't because the statement had problems* — and I swallowed it down with a sip of bad coffee, because I was a detective, and detectives don't hand suspects ammunition, even when the ammunition is their own stupid heart.

"Dinner," I said instead.

Oscar blinked. "Excuse me?"

"There's a place on Harbor Street. Good timelines require good food. It's a known fact. Forensics will back me up."

"You're asking me to dinner to go over timelines."

"I'm asking you to dinner to go over timelines."

"In a restaurant."

"Restaurants have tables. Tables are excellent for timelines."

Oscar stared at me for a long moment, and then they laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of them, brief and bright and gone, like a match struck in a dark room. "You're terrible at this," they said.

"At what?"

"Whatever this is." They picked up a pen, tapped it against the desk twice, and I understood I was watching someone weigh something heavy on very old scales. "Friday," they said. "Seven o'clock. But I'm driving myself, because if I disappear after dinner, I want it on record that I had independent transportation."

"Noted for the record," I said, and got up to leave before either of us could say anything true.

At the door I turned back. "Vasquez. The three hours. If there's something you're protecting — someone —"

"Goodnight, Detective," Oscar said, warm and closed as a locked door, and put the headset back on.

### Oscar

He asked me to dinner to go over timelines, and I said yes, which tells you everything about the state of my judgment.

Ruthie found me Friday afternoon in the depot's back room, elbow-deep in Sal's ledgers — the real ones now, the ones I'd started keeping at home because I didn't trust the office anymore, though I couldn't have told you who I didn't trust, which was its own kind of sickness. She looked at my face and knew immediately, because Ruthie has been reading faces since before I was born and mine has always been printed in large type.

"You're dressing up," she said.

"I am not dressing up. This is my regular face."

"You ironed a shirt. I watched you iron a shirt. You held it up to the light like you were appraising a diamond." She lowered herself into the chair across from me, slow the way her knees made her slow these days, and fixed me with a look. "It's the detective."

"It's a working dinner. Timelines."

"Mm." Ruthie poured herself coffee from the pot that has been burnt and reburnt since the Clinton administration. "You know what I did on my first date with my second husband? We went over timelines. His alibi. He was married at the time and so was I, and we sat in a diner going over each other's stories until two in the morning, and I knew right then I was in trouble." She sipped. "How'd the food compare?"

"We haven't eaten yet."

"How's the looking-across-the-table?"

I didn't answer, which answered it.

Ruthie set down her cup, and something moved behind her eyes — that shadow I'd been catching glimpses of ever since I'd shown her the decoded ledger entries, ever since she'd said *the partners* and then gone quiet like a radio cut mid-sentence. "Oscar. Be careful with that man. Not because he'll hurt you. Because you'll want to tell him things."

"Like what things?"

"Like anything. Like everything. That's how it works, mija. You start with timelines and end up handing him the whole map." She stood, patted my shoulder with a hand that trembled slightly, and said, so quietly I almost missed it: "Some maps get people killed."

Then she was gone, and I sat there with Sal's ledger open in front of me, columns of ghost fares marching toward the piers like a procession, and I thought: *she's not warning me about falling in love. She's warning me about what love makes you give away.*

* * *

The restaurant on Harbor Street was the kind of place with candles and cloth napkins and a wine list longer than most confessions. Isandro had already gotten us a corner table, and when I walked in he stood up — actually stood up, chair scraping, like something out of a movie — and I felt the full weight of what Ruthie had said settle onto my shoulders like a coat.

"You clean up well, Detective."

"You look—" He stopped. Restarted. "The shirt is ironed."

"Observant. That's why they gave you a badge."

We ordered. We made it through the bread. And then he unfolded an actual piece of paper — an actual printed timeline, typed, with my name at the top — and laid it on the table between the candle and the salt, and I laughed out loud because it was so absurdly, transparently him.

"You brought exhibits to dinner."

"I told you. Timelines." But he was fighting a smile, and losing. "Walk me through the ninth again. From eleven p.m."

"Eleven p.m., I close out the shift ledger. Eleven-twenty, I leave the depot. Then three hours of fog, Detective, and then I'm home."

"Fog."

"Coastal city. Very common."

Isandro turned the paper around and slid it across the tablecloth toward me, and his voice changed — dropped the flirtation, dropped the deflection, went quiet and level. "Oscar. Off the record. No tape, no notebook, no captain. Just the two of us and this extremely expensive bread." He paused. "Where were you?"

And God help me, I wanted to tell him. That was the terrifying part. Somewhere between the coffee at the depot and the candlelight, the answer had stopped being a tactical decision and started being a hunger. *I was following Sal's last route. I was standing where a drowned man wore my clothes. I have a ledger in my apartment that could hang half the people who raised me, and I've read it so many times I can recite it, and I haven't told you because telling you means it becomes real.*

"I was at the water," I said. Which was true. Which was nothing.

"Which water?"

"The wet kind."

"Oscar."

"Isandro." I said his name deliberately, without the *Detective*, and watched it land — watched him go still the way he'd gone still at the depot, and I realized we were playing the same game with the same terrible stakes, both of us holding truths like hot plates, neither willing to set theirs down first. "If I tell you where I was, you have to write it down. And if you write it down, it stops being ours and starts being the case's. And I'm not ready for that."

Something shifted in his face. Not suspicion. Recognition. He knew exactly what I was saying, because he was saying it too, in his way, with his typed timeline and his careful questions — *I want to trust you and I cannot afford to and I'm here anyway.*

"The ninth," he said slowly. "Pier 9 is a twenty-minute drive from your apartment. You left the depot at eleven-twenty. The medical examiner puts time of death between midnight and two." He let it sit there, ugly and unavoidable. "You see my problem."

"I see it. Do you see mine?"

"Show me."

"Your victim was wearing my clothes. Tailored to fit him — no, wait. Not tailored." I leaned forward. "Altered. Someone took my clothes and made them fit a bigger man. Who does that, Isandro? Who steals a dispatcher's jacket and remakes it for a dead stranger? Because that's not a frame job by someone who hates me. That's a message. Someone wanted me to *see* it and know it was meant for me."

For a long moment he didn't move. The candle flame bent in a draft from the door. And then he said, very quietly: "I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've known since week one. Clothes altered to fit, wallet removed, body placed where a driver would find it — that's theater, Oscar. That's a production. Frame jobs are lazy. This was a gift-wrapped present with your name on it." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "What I don't know is who's giving the gift, or why. And every time I pull a thread, the department pulls it back."

The second almost. It rose in my throat like tide water — *there's a ledger, there's a cipher, Sal was logging something at those piers for thirty years, Ruthie said the word partners and flinched* — and I opened my mouth, and he opened his, and for one suspended second I think we were both going to break at the same time, both going to slide our truths across the tablecloth and meet in the middle like people do in the movies.

Then his phone buzzed on the table. He glanced at the screen and his whole body changed — spine straightening, jaw setting, the detective sliding back over his face like a visor coming down.

"Work?" I asked.

"Work." He silenced it without answering. When he looked up at me again, the warmth was still there, but it had to shine through the badge now, dimmed. "Oscar. Whatever you're carrying — and I know you're carrying something, you have the posture of a person guarding a doorway — I need you to understand something. If it comes to a choice between you and the case, I—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"—I don't know what I'd do," he finished anyway, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving a sentence safe. "And that scares me more than the case does."

I reached across the table before I could stop myself and covered his hand with mine. His skin was warm. His knuckles were scarred. Neither of us pulled away, and neither of us said anything else true, and the waiter came with the check and broke the spell like ice cracking on a pond.

Outside, the harbor was black and breathing. He walked me to my car, hands in his pockets, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched and didn't.

"Same time next week?" he said. "More timelines?"

"There's a lot of timeline left," I agreed.

He hesitated at my car door — one breath, two — and I swear he was going to kiss me, and I swear I was going to let him, and then his phone buzzed again, insistent, and whatever was on that screen made him go gray around the mouth.

"I have to go," he said. "Oscar — go straight home tonight. Lock your door. Don't answer it for anyone you're not expecting."

"Isandro, what—"

"*Go home.*" And he was moving fast toward his car, phone at his ear, voice low and hard, and I caught only the last fragment as he drove off: "...found a body where? Which pier?"

I stood alone in the parking lot with the harbor wind pulling at my ironed shirt, and every instinct I'd inherited from Sal Vasquez lit up at once like a switchboard.

Pier 12. I didn't know how I knew. I just knew.

The tide was coming in, and it was bringing something with it.
