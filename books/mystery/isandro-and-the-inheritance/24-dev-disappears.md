## 24. Dev Disappears

The night line has rung at 11:47 p.m. every Tuesday for three years.

I know this because it's the one ritual Dev refuses to break — a fake fare request from "a guy named Cornelius who needs a ride to his own surprise party," which is not funny the first time and somehow funnier every time after that. Three years of Tuesdays. Ruthie keeps score of how many variations Dev has invented; we're up to forty-one, and last week's involved a parrot named Cornelius who could dial a phone, which I maintain violates the spirit of the game if not the letter.

So when 11:47 came and went on Tuesday with nothing but the hum of the radio and the coffee maker gurgling its slow death rattle, I told myself it was fine. Kids have lives. Kids have midterms, crushes, other phones to stare into. Dev is seventeen and brilliant and allergic to being predictable except in the ways they've chosen to be predictable, and maybe this was the week the joke died of natural causes.

At midnight I called their cell. It rang four times and went to voicemail — Dev's voicemail greeting is eleven seconds of them doing an impression of me saying "Marisol Cabs, this is Oscar, state your emergency," which I have never once found funny and have never once asked them to change.

"Hey," I said. "You're late for Cornelius. He's standing on a curb somewhere holding a cake. Call me back."

Ruthie looked over from her crossword. She'd been watching me make the call with the particular stillness she gets when she's already done the math and doesn't like the answer.

"They'll call," she said.

"Obviously."

"Oscar."

"I said obviously." I hung my jacket back on the hook instead of wearing it home, which was itself a tell, because I always wear it home. "One missed Tuesday isn't a pattern. We're not doing patterns."

"We're not doing patterns," Ruthie agreed, in the tone of someone agreeing to a lie so you can both get through the shift.

The phone stayed dark until 2:14 a.m., when it lit up with a text from Dev's number:

**cant talk tonight. dont wait up**

No emoji. Dev communicates exclusively in emoji, hieroglyphics, and screenshots. A plain sentence from Dev is like a taxi running silent — technically possible, deeply wrong.

I typed back: *you ok?*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again. Then nothing, for so long I set the phone down and picked it up twice more before the screen finally gave me my answer, or what I thought was my answer:

**all good. see you thursday**

Dev works Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday nights. Thursday is not a thing. There is no Thursday in our schedule, there has never been a Thursday, and if you'd asked Dev to name the days of the week they'd have said "dispatch days and the other ones."

I showed Ruthie the screen. She read it the way she reads accident reports — once fast, then again slow, then she took off her glasses, which is the worst sign in her entire repertoire.

"That's not them typing," she said.

"You don't know that."

"I raised two teenagers and buried one husband who lied badly. That's not them typing." She put her glasses back on. "Go home, Oscar. Sleep. In the morning, if you still think it's nothing, drive past their place on your way in. Knock on a door in daylight like a normal person instead of kicking it down at three a.m. like a cop show."

"And if it's something?"

Ruthie folded her crossword closed without finishing it. In thirty years of dispatching, I had never seen her abandon a puzzle.

"Then daylight won't save anybody either," she said. "But at least you'll have had some sleep."

* * *

I slept ninety minutes, in the way you sleep when your body shuts down out of spite rather than rest — flat on top of the covers, fully dressed, one hand around my phone like a rosary. At 6:40 I gave up, drove to Dev's apartment on Delmore Street, and sat across the road in the cab for ten minutes doing surveillance like an amateur, which is to say badly, drinking gas-station coffee and narrating my own stakeout in my head.

Third-floor walkup above a nail salon. Dev's window — the one with the cardboard cutout of a spaceship taped to the glass, because Dev says curtains are surveillance-adjacent — was dark. No spaceship visible. Either the lights were off or the ship was gone.

I knocked. Waited. Knocked again, longer, the way you knock when you're trying to sound official enough that whoever's inside decides answering is safer than not.

Nothing. Downstairs, the nail salon owner unlocked her door and gave me the look people give men loitering outside buildings at dawn, adjusted for the fact that I was in a Marisol Cabs polo and therefore rated as harmless infrastructure rather than threat.

"Ms. Okafor," I said. "The kid upstairs. Dev. You seen them?"

She softened immediately — everyone softens for Dev, it's a documented phenomenon — and shook her head. "Not since Sunday. They helped me carry boxes, very polite boy." She paused, recalibrated. "Person. Very polite person. Why?"

"Just checking. They missed a shift."

"Teenagers." She said it like a diagnosis and went inside.

Here is what I want on the record: I did not kick the door down. I want this noted because everything after this moment, I did worse. But at 7:05 a.m., standing in a hallway that smelled like acetone and old carpet, I tried the handle of Dev's apartment door, found it unlocked, and walked in without a warrant, a witness, or a single good idea.

"Dev?" My voice bounced off bare walls. "It's Oscar. I'm coming in. Don't throw anything at me."

Nobody threw anything. Nobody was there.

The apartment was small and usually chaotic in a way Dev claimed was a system — cables coiled like sleeping snakes, three monitors, a whiteboard covered in network diagrams and, in the corner, a small drawing of a taxi with a heart next to it that I pretended never to have seen and thought about constantly. The monitors were gone. The tower under the desk was gone. The cables were gone, coiled and bagged in ziplocks, packed with the neatness of someone who'd had time to be careful.

That was the thing that made my stomach drop, later, when I understood it. Not the mess. The tidiness.

Because it wasn't tidy everywhere. A chair lay tipped over by the kitchenette. One of the cabinet doors stood open, dishes untouched inside. On the floor near the couch: a sneaker, laces still tied, lying on its side like it had been stepped out of in a hurry. And on the counter, a glass of water with a ring of dried mineral scale around where it sat — days old, maybe a week — next to a second glass, clean, empty, recently placed.

Two timelines in one room. An old life interrupted, and a new arrangement layered on top of it.

I stood in the middle of it and let my eyes do what they do, the dispatcher's trick Sal taught me before I knew he was teaching me anything: don't look at the room, look at what the room is telling you. And the room was telling me a story with a seam in it. The tipped chair and the tied-lace sneaker said *left fast*. The bagged cables and the missing tower said *left prepared*. Both things were true, and they couldn't both belong to Dev.

Somebody had come here after Dev left. Somebody had searched — carefully, respectfully, almost professionally — and then staged what remained. Or somebody had taken Dev and wanted the scene to say *ran away*, and had gotten half of it right, because they were good at staging rooms and bad at knowing that Dev would sooner leave a kidney behind than leave a sneaker untied on the floor.

Or — and this was the version I carried out of the apartment like a stone in my chest — Dev had left in a hurry themselves, packing only what mattered, because they'd seen something coming. Because they'd known, the way kids who grew up watching their backs know, that the window for a clean exit was closing.

On the fridge, held by a magnet shaped like a chili pepper, was the takeout menu Dev had defaced months ago with a doodle of me as a superhero whose power was "knowing everyone's address." Underneath it, half-hidden, a sticky note in Dev's handwriting:

*if i'm ever actually missing check the—*

And then nothing. The note ended. Torn, or smudged, or finished in a way that made no sense. I photographed it with shaking hands, front and back, held it up to the light looking for indentations from a second page beneath it. Nothing.

*Check the what,* Dev.

* * *

By nine a.m. I was back at the depot, and I made the call I'd been turning over since Delmore Street: whether to bring Isandro in.

Here was the problem, laid out in the ledger logic I'd been living by for weeks now. Everything I had — the cipher, the ghost fares, Sal's route codes, the passenger, all of it — was evidence I was holding illegally, evidence that would burn me and possibly Ruthie the moment it surfaced. If I called Isandro and said *my hacker is missing*, the first question would be *your what?* And the second question would be *why does a seventeen-year-old volunteer have police archives?* And the third question would be asked in a room with a lawyer present.

But Isandro wasn't a question machine. Isandro was — God help me — the person I wanted to call first anyway, before any of this, about nothing, about coffee, about the way morning light came through the depot windows. Somewhere between the coffee and the almost-dinner, the case and the man had gotten tangled past my ability to separate them, and I no longer trusted myself to know whether I was calling a detective or dialing a feeling.

I called him.

He picked up on the second ring, voice alert in a way that meant he hadn't been asleep either. "Oscar."

"How'd you know it was me?"

"Who else calls at nine a.m. on a Wednesday sounding like they haven't blinked?" A pause. "What happened?"

"Dev didn't come home. Their apartment—" I stopped, restarted. "Their apartment looks like they left in a hurry. Or like somebody wanted it to look that way. Their equipment's gone. There's a note that just stops. And at 2:14 this morning I got a text from their number saying don't wait up, see you Thursday, and Dev doesn't have a Thursday, Isandro. There is no Thursday."

Silence on the line. When he spoke again, the detective cadence was gone and something plainer was underneath it. "Have you eaten?"

"What? No. That's not—"

"I'm coming to the depot. Don't touch anything else in that apartment. And Oscar—" another pause, and I could hear him deciding how much badge to put in the next sentence, "—forward me that text. From Dev's phone. Before anything else happens to it."

"It's on my phone, it's not going anywhere—"

"Everything is going somewhere," he said quietly. "That's kind of the whole case. Forward it."

He arrived twenty minutes later, out of uniform-ish, the detective equivalent of civilian clothes that fool nobody, carrying two coffees and a paper bag of pan dulce like showing up armed with breakfast was standard procedure for a missing persons report. Maybe for him it was. Maybe that was the whole reason I'd called him and we both knew it and neither of us said it.

He read the text. He read the sticky note photo. He listened to me describe the apartment — the two timelines, the staged mess, the sneaker — with his eyes getting flatter and flatter, which I'd learned was the opposite of calm. Flat was where Isandro kept his anger, in a drawer he opened carefully.

"A minor is missing," he said finally. "This goes formal. Right now. Missing persons unit, an actual report, canvass of the neighborhood, the salon owner's statement."

"The report asks questions I can't answer."

"The report," he said, "asks questions I will answer for you. Poorly, if necessary. I've been giving bad answers to my captain for weeks; I'm getting good at it." He tore the pan dulce in half and handed me the bigger piece without looking, automatic, like breathing. "Eat. You're no use to Dev running on adrenaline and guilt."

"I'm not guilty."

"You're guilty of something. You've been guilty of something since the pier." He said it without heat, almost gently, and it landed harder than heat would have. "Whatever it is, we'll get to it. Today the only thing that matters is a kid who answers phones at midnight for strangers because nobody ever answered for them."

That was the truest thing anyone had said about Dev in my hearing, and it came from a man who'd met them exactly twice, and I had to look out the window at the harbor for a second before I could speak.

* * *

The formal machinery ground into motion the way it does — slower than panic, faster than comfort. A detective named Okonkwo from missing persons took my statement in the depot break room while Isandro stood by the door radiating jurisdiction. Ruthie gave hers with her hands folded, steady as bedrock, though I saw her knuckles whiten around each other when Okonkwo asked when she'd last spoken to Dev directly.

Sunday, she said. Just checking in. Normal conversation.

I watched her say it and I believed her, and I also watched her choose not to mention whatever she and Dev talked about on Sundays, which was apparently a whole category of things Ruthie kept in a locked room behind her face. Later. Everything was later now. Later was stacking up like unpaid fares.

By early afternoon, Okonkwo had canvassed the street, pulled the salon's exterior camera footage, and come back with the sum total of the world's knowledge about Dev's departure: a figure matching Dev's description, hood up, backpack on, walking east on Delmore at 9:52 Sunday night, alone, unhurried. No vehicle. No one following on camera. The figure turned the corner onto Vane Street and out of frame, and that was the last anyone had seen of them.

Alone. Unhurried. Which fit the version where Dev left on purpose.

Except.

"Show me the timestamp again," Isandro said, leaning over Okonkwo's tablet.

"9:52."

"Pull the feed from the camera on Vane and Delmore. The one on the pharmacy."

Okonkwo pulled it. The corner where the figure disappeared — Vane and Delmore — had a camera. It had been recording Sunday night. And at 9:53, eleven seconds after Dev walked out of one frame, a gray sedan rolled through the other one, moving slow, passenger side down, and stopped just past the edge of view for a full forty seconds before pulling away.

Forty seconds. Long enough to lean out a window. Long enough to say a name.

Isandro and I looked at each other over the tablet, and everything we weren't saying filled the break room like water filling a hull.

Gray sedan. I didn't have to describe it to him. He'd seen the file photos from the container yards, from my statement about the tail, from the security firm registration we'd traced to Emilio Vasquez and Marcus Hale. Government plates that weren't government. The car that had followed me home and lost me among the containers, and that I'd filed away as paranoia because filing it as paranoia was cheaper than filing it as a promise.

They hadn't been following me that night. Or they'd been following both of us. We were the two people digging, and they'd started with the one who was easiest to carry.

My phone buzzed against the table.

I looked down expecting Okonkwo, or Ruthie, or the depot line. The screen said DEV, and for one full second the world reassembled itself — they're fine, they ran, they're angry at me for something, they're a teenager, teenagers vanish and return — and I grabbed the phone like a drowning man grabs driftwood and swiped it open.

A text. From Dev's number. To me.

**stop digging**

Three words. No emoji. No period even, this time — as if whoever was typing had learned from the last message that Dev's fingerprints were all over the punctuation and stripped even that away. As if they were getting better at this.

As if they'd had practice.

Isandro read it upside down across the table, and I watched the color change in his face, and I understood that we were both seeing the same thing: not a warning from a friend. A proof of life, inverted. Whoever held Dev's phone wanted me to know they held it. *Stop digging* wasn't a message about the investigation.

It was a message about leverage.

"They're alive," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You don't send ransom notes about dead people."

"Oscar—"

"They're alive, and they're scared, and they typed that with someone watching, and that means there's a version of this where they walk out." I was standing now. I didn't remember standing. "Which means every hour we spend being careful is an hour they spend in a room with those people."

"Every hour we spend being careless," Isandro said, low, "is an hour closer to them becoming a second body the tide brings back. You know what we're dealing with. You know what they did to Sal."

The break room went very quiet. Okonkwo had the grace to find a reason to be elsewhere.

I stared at the phone. Three words, gray bubble, Dev's name glowing at the top like a lamp in a window. And beneath the fear — beneath it, woven through it, in the part of me that had spent thirty years learning Sal's ciphers and Sal's silences — something else was stirring. Something ugly and useful.

Because here's what a lifetime of dispatching teaches you about messages: every transmission has a sender, and every sender, even under duress, even terrified, even typing one-handed with a stranger's breath on their neck — every sender makes choices. Word choice. Timing. Target.

*Stop digging.* Sent to me. Not to Ruthie. Not to the police. To me.

Dev wasn't asking me to stop. Dev was telling me where the dig was hurting them — which meant Dev knew what I'd found, which meant Dev had been keeping up from wherever they were, which meant Dev was still Dev, still three monitors' worth of brilliant, still playing the long game even from inside the cage.

And if Dev could get a message out, however dictated, however coerced —

Then Dev could get another one out. Given time. Given a reason. Given someone on the outside stubborn enough to keep the line open.

I looked up at Isandro, and I saw him arrive at the same arithmetic, and I watched him hate it, because the next step was obvious and terrible and mine.

"They sent it to me," I said. "Which means they expect me to answer. And I'm going to."

"Absolutely not—"

"Isandro." I picked up the phone, thumbs hovering over a reply I hadn't written yet, to a kid I might never see again, in a conversation where every word I chose could be the difference between a hostage and a body. "They took my family. They used my kid's phone. So now they're talking to me — and I have spent my whole life making sure that when somebody opens a line to Marisol Cabs, somebody answers."

Outside, the harbor light was going copper with the approach of evening, and somewhere across all that water, in a room I couldn't find yet, Dev was waiting to see if I'd take the bait — or turn it into a lifeline.

I typed: **ok. what do you want me to stop digging for?**

And hit send.
