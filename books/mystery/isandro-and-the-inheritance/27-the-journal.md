## 27. The Journal

I've been driving cabs for forty-one years, and in all that time I learned one thing worth passing on: the truth is like a fare who doesn't want to be found. You can circle the block all night, but sooner or later they step out of the shadows and get in your car, and then you have to take them where they're going, whether you like the destination or not.

The journal came out of Sal's boathouse in a Ziploc bag Oscar had sealed with the kind of care people usually reserve for evidence or organs for transplant. It sat on my kitchen table between the sugar bowl and a cold pot of coffee, and none of us wanted to touch it.

Dev got there first, which surprised nobody. That kid had been missing forty-eight hours — hiding from Emilio Vasquez's security firm in a cousin's basement in Alameda, eating gas-station snacks and refreshing burner forums — and walked through my door at nine in the morning looking like they'd slept in a dryer. They dropped into a chair, looked at the bag, and said, "So that's it? The whole thing?"

"That's it," Oscar said.

"Can I—"

"No," I said, and poured three cups of coffee. "You eat something first. Dead men's journals keep."

Oscar looked at me across the table. My kid — not by blood, but by thirty years of night shifts and bad coffee and me teaching them how to read a city by its streetlights. They had salt-dried tear tracks they probably thought I couldn't see, and a look on their face like someone standing at the edge of a pool trying to decide if the water's going to be cold.

"Ruthie," they said. "You knew him longest. You should do it."

And there it was. The thing I'd been dreading since Oscar called me from Pier 14, voice shaking, saying *Ruthie, there's a key taped under the bench and a boathouse and Sal's handwriting everywhere.*

Because here's what I never told them: I didn't need to read Sal's journal to know what was in it. I'd lived it. I'd driven half of it myself, back when we were young and broke and sure that what we were doing was mercy. The only question — the one that had kept me up every night since the drowned man washed up wearing my kid's clothes — was where the mercy stopped and the other thing began. And I was afraid that somewhere in those pages, in Sal's neat little hand, I'd find the exact date. The exact night. The moment I should have seen it and didn't.

So I dried my hands, sat down, and opened the bag.

* * *

The journal smelled like the boathouse — tar and old wood and river damp. Sal's handwriting was small and slanted, the same hand that filled forty years of fare ledgers, the same hand that wrote my name on a card when Marisol Cabs opened its doors: *To Ruthie, who drives like the angels are chasing her.*

I read the first entry out loud because my voice was steadier than theirs would've been. That's an old dispatcher's trick. You give the hard news yourself so nobody else has to fumble it.

*"March 4th, 1991. Tomas Ochoa came to me today about his wife's cousin. She's running from a husband in Guatemala who put her sister in the hospital. No papers, no money, no options. He asked if a cab could take her somewhere the husband couldn't follow. I said yes before he finished asking."*

"Tomas Ochoa," Dev repeated. "Dockworker. The one who disappeared in '93."

"The same," I said.

Oscar had gone very still. "Sal was moving people. Refugees."

"People who needed moving," I said, and turned the page. "Keep listening. It gets less simple."

It got less simple fast. Entry after entry, month after month, year after year. A boy whose father owed money to men who burned houses as reminders. A woman testifying against a cartel accountant. A teenager who'd aged out of foster care and into the sights of somebody I won't describe in front of breakfast. Sal logged them all in cipher, moved them all in cab four-oh-seven, and never took a dime beyond the cost of gas. The ledger entries Oscar and Dev decoded — the ghost fares, the pickups that never appeared on any books — weren't cargo. They were people. Sal had built an underground railroad out of a taxi company, and the four of us — Sal, me, Emilio Vasquez, Marcus Hale — had been its engine.

"Emilio funded it," I said, because they were both staring at me and I wasn't going to let them hear it secondhand. "In the early years. He had port connections, warehouse space, lawyers on retainer for the ones who needed documents. Marcus handled anything that touched City Hall. Sal ran the routes. I drove most of them." I took a breath. "We called ourselves the partners. Four people who decided the law was a suggestion and mercy was a schedule."

"You never told me," Oscar said quietly. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — hurt.

"There was nothing to tell until there was everything to tell. You don't hand a kid a live wire, Oscar. You wait until they're old enough to hold it."

"I'm not a kid."

"No," I agreed. "That's the problem."

* * *

I kept reading. Somewhere around the 2003 entries my voice started doing that thing voices do when you're holding it together with both hands, so Dev took over for a while — they read Sal's words in that flat teenage monotone that somehow made them hit harder, all this grief delivered like a grocery list.

Then Dev stopped mid-page.

"This one's different," they said. "Listen. *'Elena is two years old today. Tomas brought her to the depot. She fell asleep in cab 407's back seat with her thumb in her mouth. I told him: whatever happens to me, she must never go near the piers again. He laughed and said the piers are the safest place in the world, because everyone watches them and no one sees.'*" Dev looked up. "Who's Elena?"

My hands had gone cold on the coffee cup. Because I remembered that day. I remembered Tomas Ochoa laughing in the dispatch office with his daughter asleep against his shoulder, and I remembered thinking: *there goes a man who finally feels safe.* Two months later he was in the bay.

"Keep reading," I said.

The entries changed after that. Fewer passengers. More of a single name, recurring, monthly, then weekly. *Elena moved to the house on Delmore Street. Elena's school arranged. Elena asks why she can't see her abuela. Elena is asking questions about her father.* And threaded through all of it, starting around twelve years ago, a new note in Sal's writing — tighter, angrier:

*"Emilio wants to know where she lives. I told him no. He says it's for her protection. I know what his protection costs."*

"He hid her," Oscar said slowly, working it through the way they worked through a cipher, one number at a time. "From Emilio. But Emilio was one of the partners. He funded the whole—"

"He funded it right up until he found a better use for it," I said. "Twelve years ago the operation changed hands. Sal wrote it himself — you'll find the entry. After Tomas died, Emilio stopped seeing refugees. He started seeing infrastructure. Routes, safehouses, drivers who asked no questions. What Sal built to hide people, Emilio started using to move things. And the one person Sal would not let him touch was Tomas Ochoa's daughter."

"Why?" Dev asked. "Why her?"

I looked at the journal, at the entry dated March 1993, six weeks after they pulled Tomas's body from the water and closed the case as accidental drowning, with Sal listed as a witness who saw nothing.

"Because Sal knew how Tomas died," I said. "And I think Tomas knew something about Emilio that got him killed. And Sal spent the rest of his life making sure the only witness to any of it — that little girl asleep in the back of cab 407 — stayed alive and stayed hidden. Every month. For twenty-some years. The passenger. That was her. That was always her."

Nobody said anything for a long time. The refrigerator hummed. Down at the harbor a foghorn went off, patient as ever, like the water was just keeping time while we caught up.

Oscar finally spoke, and their voice was rough. "The photographs. In the boathouse. Dated last month. She's still alive, Ruthie. Sal's dead and she's still alive, which means somebody else has been moving her, hiding her—" They stopped. I watched them get there. "Sal didn't die of a heart attack in his garden. He died because he was the last wall between Emilio and that girl."

"And now Sal's gone," Dev said, "and the wall's gone with him."

* * *

I read the last pages myself. My voice, my choice, my old friend's last words.

The final entry was dated eleven days before Sal drowned. His handwriting had gotten shaky — he'd been sick that spring, though he'd lied to all of us about how badly.

*"If you are reading this, then either I am dead or I am careless, and I have never been careless. To whoever holds this book: Elena Ochoa is twenty-six years old. She has spent her entire life being protected, which is another word for imprisoned, and she knows it now — I taught her enough to know it. She deserves the truth about her father, and the truth about the man who killed him. Everything you need is in the ledgers, the route map, and the file I left at Pier 14. Do not trust the partners. Do not trust the police above Detective rank. And do not, under any circumstances, let them move her before the tide does its work. The bay keeps what the bay is given. I have given it proof. When the water gives it back, she goes free."*

I closed the journal.

"The tide," Oscar whispered. "Chapter thirty-five of his own damn book. He sank the evidence. He sank his car, or Emilio's weapon, or—" They stood up so fast the chair went over backwards. "Ruthie. The storm surge. The weather report. The bay is going to give it back in two days."

"I know," I said.

"Emilio will know it too. He's had thirty years to worry about that car. The first storm surge in a decade comes, and suddenly his men are crawling all over the ferry terminal, and Elena gets moved, or disappears, or—"

"Oscar." I put my hand flat on the table, the way I used to when the radio was screaming and everybody needed one calm voice. "Sit down and listen to me, because this is the part I have to say and I'm only going to say it once."

They sat.

"Twelve years ago, when the operation changed, I stayed. I could have walked away — Sal begged me to, more than once. But I stayed because I thought I could keep an eye on it, keep the routes clean, keep people like Dev safe inside these walls. And instead I watched, and I said nothing, and I told myself knowing was the same as stopping it. It isn't. Knowing is just guilt with better posture." I looked at each of them, my strange ragged family, assembled at my kitchen table out of loyalty and loss. "So here's what happens next. We don't run. We don't hide. We finish Sal's last route — all the way to the end of the book. And when the tide gives up what it took, we make sure Emilio Vasquez is standing in the open when it does."

Dev raised their hand like we were in school. "Question. What exactly do we do about the fact that Emilio's firm already tried to grab me twice, and I'm pretty sure they know I copied their archive?"

"You're staying here," I said. "Basement. Landline only."

"That's — okay, yes, fair."

Oscar was already pulling on their jacket, phone out, and I knew without asking who they were calling. The detective. The one they looked at the way Sal used to look at a solved case — like something broken finally clicking back together.

"Isandro needs to hear all of it," Oscar said. "Tonight. Before Emilio moves."

"Then call them," I said, and picked the journal back up, holding it the way you hold something that used to weigh nothing and now weighs everything. "But Oscar — one more thing. There's an entry I skipped. Dated the night Sal died. I haven't read it out loud yet, because once I do, there's no taking it back."

Oscar stopped with their hand on the door.

"What does it say?"

I opened the journal to the last page, and read it silently first, just to be sure my heart could take it. Then I looked up at my kid, and gave them the truth, straight, no detour, the way you deliver the news that changes the route forever.

"It says he didn't drown by accident," I said. "And it names who held him under."
