## 31. The Tide Turns

The weather report came to me the way all bad news comes to me: through the night line, at three in the morning, delivered by a voice that sounded far too cheerful about it.

"Marisol Cabs, this is Oscar."

"Oscar! It's Dev. Turn on the news. Channel six. Right now. I'll wait." And Dev did wait, breathing into the phone with the patience of a teenager who has grown up on loading screens.

I fumbled for the little television Ruthie kept above the coffee machine, the one with the coat hanger antenna and the religious station burned into its memory from some previous owner's habits. Channel six. A weatherman with a jaw like a weather balloon was standing in front of a spinning graphic of the harbor, gesturing at it the way a man gestures at a horse he's about to bet his house on.

"—storm surge advisory upgraded to a warning, with the National Weather Service projecting a surge of four to six feet above high tide, coinciding with a spring tide Thursday night. Low-lying areas along the waterfront, including the decommissioned ferry terminal district, are expected to see significant flooding of lower levels—"

I sat down on the dispatch stool, which is not a chair designed for sitting down on, and felt the floor tilt anyway.

Thursday night. Two days.

"Oscar?" Dev said. "You still there? You've got your thinking face on. I can hear it."

"You can't hear a face."

"I can hear yours. It sounds like a screen door slamming."

I looked at the ledger open on the desk in front of me — Sal's ledger, the key volume I'd been keeping in a fireproof box under my bed like a paranoid archivist, because paranoid was the only setting I had left. The latest decoded entry sat under my thumb. The ferry terminal. Berth level two. A date: this Friday.

Friday. The morning after the surge.

I'd spent three days telling myself the date in the ledger was a coincidence, or a code, or anything other than what it plainly was. Sal had written that entry knowing — or hoping — that whoever decoded it would read it in time. He'd been dead twelve years. He'd left me a schedule. And now the ocean was about to come in and erase the location off the map like a landlord repainting between tenants.

"Dev," I said. "Pull the tide tables for Thursday. Exact times. And the surge projection."

"Already did. High tide Thursday night is eleven-forty. With the surge on top, the waterline at the terminal district hits about four meters above normal. The lower levels of that ferry building — the ones in Sal's route codes — flood first. They're below street grade. They've been sealed since the nineties."

"Which means anything down there that isn't waterproof—"

"Goes away. Or gets moved. Or—" Dev paused, and I heard the kid doing the same math I was doing, the math that ended in a place neither of us wanted to say out loud. "Or whatever happens to Elena happens before Thursday, because after that, Emilio's options get a lot simpler."

Simpler. Dev meant drowned. Dev meant the sea doing the family's dirty work for free, the way it had done it for Sal, the way it had done it for the dockworker thirty years ago, the way it had been doing it for the partners since before I was born. The harbor keeps its mouth shut. That's why they'd built their whole rotten operation on top of it.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. Here's what we do."

* * *

Here is what I knew at three a.m. on Tuesday, laid out on the dispatch desk next to a cold cup of coffee and Ruthie's emergency scones, which she bakes when she's worried and had been baking all week, so we were up to our ankles in scone:

One. Elena Vasquez — though she'd been born under another name, the dockworker's daughter, a name Sal had buried in cipher to keep it off every book in the city — was alive as of last month, because Sal's photographs said so, and Sal's photographs had never once lied to me. His fares had. His ledgers had. The man himself, dead in the water with his secrets, had. But the photographs were true.

Two. She was held, sheltered, hidden — I still didn't know which word was honest — in or near the decommissioned ferry terminal, in an apartment Sal had built behind a wall when I was in elementary school learning long division, unaware that my mentor was doing it a few miles away with a hammer and a conscience.

Three. Emilio Vasquez, port authority board member, original partner, silk-wrapped menace, had spent twelve years inheriting an arrangement he'd never wanted and had since made his own. And arrangements like his don't survive exposure. Arrangements like his get cleaned up. The tide was going to hand him a mop.

Four. Isandro Reyes, detective, my — I searched for the word and settled on *problem* — had been suspended on Friday. Suspended on an anonymous complaint that quoted my dinner with Emilio word for word, which meant the leak went up past the captain, which meant the police department, my department, the one I'd spent my whole life believing closed every case because the people in it were good, had a hole in it shaped exactly like the people who raised me.

And five. The water was coming Thursday night, and it wasn't asking anyone's permission.

I called Isandro at three-forty, because I am a coward with excellent instincts, and three-forty felt like a time when a suspended detective might be awake and might answer a number he'd saved under something unflattering.

He answered on the second ring. "Tell me you have good news."

"I have news. Whether it's good depends on how you feel about the ocean."

There was a pause, and then a sigh that I felt in my sternum from across town. "Oscar. It's almost four in the morning."

"Which is exactly why you answered."

Another pause. "Fair. What have you got?"

I told him. The weather report, the surge, the spring tide, the date in the ledger, the sealed lower levels. I heard him moving while I talked — the creak of a couch, a lamp switch, the particular silence of a man getting dressed while listening, which is a skill I assume they teach at the academy between the handcuffing module and the part where they teach you to be gentle with witnesses so the witnesses fall in love with you by accident.

"Say the date again," he said.

"Friday. The entry's dated Friday. The day after the surge."

"That's not a coincidence."

"Sal didn't write coincidences. He wrote schedules."

"Then either the entry tells us when something's going to happen to her," Isandro said slowly, "or it tells us when someone's supposed to move her. And either way—"

"Either way, the clock's running. Two days, Isandro. Maybe less. If Emilio knows the surge is coming — and he sits on the port authority, of course he knows, he probably got the memo before the weather service did — then he doesn't have to risk moving her at all. He can just wait for the water."

Isandro was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into that register I thought of, privately and shamefully, as his dispatch voice — the one he used when he'd stopped being gentle and started being certain. "Then we don't wait for the water. We go in before it."

"We? Isandro, you're suspended. If you set foot in that terminal on official business—"

"I'm not on official business. I'm a private citizen with excellent hearing and a friend who owns a cab company." A beat. "That's you. You're the friend. In case the coffee hasn't kicked in."

"I knew that."

"You didn't."

* * *

We met at the depot at six, which is the hour when the night shift hands off to the morning shift and the whole world smells like burnt coffee and brake fluid. Ruthie was already there, of course. Ruthie is always already there; I have a theory she doesn't so much sleep as pause. Dev arrived twenty minutes later on a bicycle that had once been a bicycle and was now mostly a rumor, carrying a duffel bag of equipment I decided not to inventory for legal reasons.

I spread it all out on the dispatch counter: the tide tables, the surge projections, a printout of the terminal's old floor plans that Dev had liberated from the city archives at four a.m. — "don't ask, the password was the mayor's dog, it's always the mayor's dog" — and the ledger, open to the final route.

"Here's the situation," I said, and I heard myself using Sal's voice, Sal's cadence, the dispatch voice he'd taught me when I was nineteen and all elbows. Brief the crew. No drama. Just the road ahead. "Thursday night, high tide at eleven-forty, surge on top of it. The lower levels of the terminal flood. Sal's last entry is dated Friday morning. That gives us a window that closes Wednesday night, maybe Thursday midday if we're stupid and lucky."

"Those are my two favorite things," Dev said.

"The question is whether Emilio moves her before the surge or lets the surge do the work. If he moves her, we might catch the transport. If he doesn't—" I stopped. Ruthie was looking at the tide table with an expression I'd never seen on her face, and I've seen Ruthie's face do everything from fury at a fare-beater to tenderness at a stray cat. This was neither. This was arithmetic. Ruthie was doing arithmetic behind her eyes and hating the answer.

"Ruthie?" I said.

She didn't answer right away. She turned the tide table toward herself, tapped the eleven-forty high tide, and said, in a voice carefully sanded flat, "He won't move her."

"You don't know that."

"I know Emilio." She said it the way you'd say *I know weather*. "Moving her is risk. Witnesses, vehicles, a driver who might talk someday. Waiting is free. The water comes up, the lower levels flood, and whatever's down there is gone by Friday — no body, no crime scene, no questions. He's done it before." She stopped, and I watched her decide, in real time, how much of the before she was going to give me. "Oscar. Thirty years ago, the harbor solved more of our problems than any of us ever did."

*Our*. I let that word sit on the counter between us like a knife someone had set down handle-first. She noticed me noticing. She didn't pick it up.

"So we go in before Thursday," Dev said, too brightly, the way you talk when you're trying to outrun a silence. "Tonight. Tomorrow at the latest. I can get us into the building — the city decommissioned it in '98, the security contract lapsed in '04, and the padlock code is still the original one because nobody ever changed it because nobody ever cared. It's the terminal's birthday. 0-7-1-1. I checked."

"You checked the terminal's birthday," Isandro said.

"Everyone has a birthday. Even buildings. Especially buildings nobody loves."

Isandro looked at the floor plans, and I watched the detective come back into his face — suspended or not, badge or not, the man was a detective the way Ruthie was a driver, down in the marrow where it stops being a job. His finger tracked the lower levels, the sealed platforms, the old vehicle ramp that ran down below the waterline. "If the surge hits four to six feet above high tide, and the lower platform is at two meters below street grade—"

"It's underwater by ten p.m. Thursday," Dev said. "I ran the numbers twice. The whole berth level goes under. Everything on it."

Everything on it. I thought about Sal's photographs, dated last month. Elena, alive, a young woman with her father's jaw and thirty years of hiding in her eyes, eating breakfast at a table in a room behind a wall. A room that was, according to the floor plans, eleven feet above the berth level. Dry. Probably. Unless the surge ran higher than the projection, unless the sea decided to be generous, unless—

"Eleven feet," I said out loud. "The apartment's eleven feet above the platform."

"Which means she might stay dry," Isandro said. "And everything around her won't."

I understood then, all at once, the shape of it. Emilio didn't need to drown her. He didn't need to lift a finger. He needed only to make sure that when the water came, she had no way out — no stairs that weren't watched, no door that wasn't locked, no one coming. The surge would seal the building from the outside. The city would close the district. And whatever happened in that room behind the wall would happen with the whole harbor standing guard.

"He's going to let the tide do it," I said. "And if she drowns, it's a storm casualty. An act of God. He'll send flowers to a memorial he attends."

Nobody said anything for a second. Then Ruthie stood up, straightened her cardigan with both hands — her tell, the thing she did before doing something she'd already decided on — and said, "Then we'd better not let the tide get there first."

* * *

The rest of Tuesday was the strangest day of my life, and I say that as a man who once found a live peacock in the trunk of cab fourteen.

We planned. Isandro, suspended, off the books, kept saying *I can't* and then kept doing it anyway, and I kept not pointing out the contradiction because I liked the contradiction. He'd go back through the department's old files one more time — the Pier 9 file, the original dockworker case — looking for the one thing that would make the terminal search warrantable before Thursday, because Isandro Reyes, even burned and betrayed and hanging from his own department by his fingertips, still believed in procedure the way some men believe in saints. He wanted to do it right. He wanted to do it right so badly, with the water rising and the clock running, that it made my chest hurt to watch.

I wanted to kick the door in. I told him so. He said, "I know," with the weary tenderness of a man talking to a large dog on a leash, and I said, "One of these days that tone is going to get you bitten," and he almost smiled, and I hated how much I liked it, and then we got back to work, because there was a woman behind a wall and a sea on its way to collect her.

Dev built the comms rig — three earpieces, a relay through the cab company's own radio band, which was beautiful in its way: thirty years of ghost fares hidden on Marisol's frequencies, and now Marisol's frequencies were going to carry the signal that ended it. "If this works," Dev said, soldering, "the company finally closes a case. The big one. The one Sal never got to."

"We don't close cases," I said, out of habit. "We just stop losing them."

Ruthie made scones. Then she stopped making scones, which frightened me more than the surge did, and sat at the counter with the tide table in front of her and her hands folded, and at some point in the late afternoon she said, quietly, to no one in particular, "There's something I have to do. Before Thursday."

"Ruthie—"

"Before Thursday, Oscar." And she looked at me with those driver's eyes that had watched thirty years of nights come and go, and whatever she saw in my face made her reach over and squeeze my wrist once, hard, like a fare paid in full, and let go.

* * *

Tuesday night, I couldn't sleep, so I did what I always do when I can't sleep: I went to work. The depot at midnight is my church. Rows of cabs sleeping in their lines, the radio murmuring its small litany of the city's needs — a fare to the airport, a lost wallet turned in, a driver reporting a street blocked by a wedding party dancing in the rain. Ordinary. Beautiful. The whole living city asking to be carried home.

I sat in the dispatch chair with Sal's ledger open in my lap and read his last entry one more time, as if the numbers might have rearranged themselves into mercy overnight.

They hadn't. Friday. Berth level. The passenger.

"You knew," I said to him, to the dead man who'd taught me everything except when to stop. "You knew how it would end. You wrote it down and you taped it shut and you trusted the tide to bring it back to somebody." I turned the page. Blank. The ledger just — stopped. Sal's hand, so neat for thirty years, ending mid-column like a dropped call.

My phone buzzed. Isandro.

*Weather service just moved the surge window up. Landfall now projected Wednesday night, not Thursday. High tide Wednesday 10:55.*

I stared at it. Read it twice. Did the arithmetic that Ruthie had been doing behind her eyes all day, and hated the answer the way she had.

We didn't have two days.

We had one.

I typed back: *Then we move tomorrow. All of it. Whatever Ruthie has to do — she does it tomorrow morning, not Thursday.*

And beneath his reply — *Agreed. Get some sleep. You'll need it* — the radio crackled, and a voice I didn't know, flat and wrong for the band, said four words and cut the line:

*"Terminal. Tomorrow night. Be ready."*
