## 33. Storm Warning

The storm had a name — Marisol, which felt like the harbor's idea of a joke — and by Tuesday morning it was all anyone talked about. The radio in the dispatch office couldn't decide whether to give weather updates or cab calls, so it did both, interleaving a woman named Yolanda who needed a ride to the airport with a barometric pressure reading that made Ruthie's bad knee sing harmony.

"Named after your company," Dev said, feet up on the filing cabinet, laptop balanced on their shins. "That's either an omen or a compliment."

"It's a coincidence," Ruthie said from her corner chair, where she'd been sitting very still for two days in the way of someone rehearsing something terrible. "Weather doesn't take sides."

"Everything takes sides," Dev said. "That's basically physics."

I let them argue because arguing was easier than what we were actually doing, which was planning to walk into a decommissioned ferry terminal during a storm surge to pull a woman out of thirty years of hiding, using a retired cab driver as bait and a teenager as our entire communications infrastructure. If I said it out loud in that order, I'd have to lie down on the floor for a while.

So instead I checked the radios. Third time that hour. They were fine. I checked them anyway.

* * *

Isandro arrived at four, still technically suspended, technically not supposed to be within a hundred yards of an active investigation, wearing a rain jacket over civilian clothes and carrying a paper bag of pastries like a man committing crimes against his own career one concha at a time.

"You know," I said, taking the bag, "for a suspended detective you have excellent taste in baked goods."

"For a person illegally withholding evidence from a murder investigation, you have excellent taste in detectives." He shrugged off the jacket. Water beaded on his shoulders; he hadn't bothered with the hood. "Captain's office called me in this morning. Officially, they wanted my badge back. Unofficially—" he paused, choosing, "—unofficially, the captain is scared. Not of me. Of whoever's above him. He asked me twice if I'd 'shared anything with outside parties.' Outside parties. Like I'm a leak in a pipe."

"Are you?"

"I'm standing in a taxi depot eating a concha with a suspended suspect and a hacker who's seventeen." He took the pastry back out of my hand, bit it, gave it back. "What do you think?"

I thought: he trusts us more than his own department, and he said it by eating half my pastry, which is somehow the most honest thing anyone's said to me all week.

Dev spun the laptop around. "Okay, so. Terminal layout, since we're all pretending this is normal." The screen showed the old Harborline Ferry Terminal — a long shed of a building on pilings, lower vehicle deck, upper passenger concourse, both of them closed since before Dev was born. "Storm surge models put water over the lower deck apron by Thursday night. Full flood of the ground-level approaches by Friday morning. If Elena's down there—"

"She's down there," I said.

"—if she's down there," Dev allowed, with the patience of someone humoring me, "then whatever Emilio plans to do with her has a deadline. You don't keep a person in a building that's about to become an aquarium. Either she moves before the surge, or she drowns in a room she's lived in for twelve years, and Emilio doesn't strike me as a man who leaves loose ends floating."

Ruthie made a small sound. We all pretended not to hear it.

"The good news," Dev continued, "is that movement requires vehicles, and vehicles require radios, and radios—" they tapped their own rig, a scarred handheld wired into a scanner that had cost them three months of allowance money and one very confusing conversation with a ham radio club, "—are chatty. Their security firm runs on cheap commercial bands. I've been listening for two days. It's mostly a guy named Hector complaining about his ex-wife. But last night there were two calls referencing 'the package' and a window. Tonight, between ten and midnight, low tide before the surge hits."

"So we don't get to pick the time," Isandro said. "They already picked it."

"They picked it," I said, "because they think nobody's listening. Which means we have exactly one advantage, and it's a teenager with a scanner and a grudge."

"I prefer 'digital asset,'" Dev said.

* * *

We went over it until the words lost meaning. That's how I knew we were ready — when even Ruthie could recite the plan in her sleep, and she'd been trying not to engage at all.

The shape of it was simple, the way a knife is simple.

Ruthie walks into Emilio's office tomorrow afternoon. She asks for a meeting — she has the standing, she's one of the original partners, and she has spent forty years being the warmest thing in any room she enters. She tells him Oscar knows everything and is coming for him, which is true, and offers to deliver Oscar in exchange for her own clean exit, which is a lie so large it needed its own zip code. Emilio will believe her because Emilio has believed for thirty years that loyalty can be bought, and Ruthie has spent those same thirty years making sure he believes it.

She'll be wearing a wire. Not ours — Isandro's, pulled from evidence lockup by a man committing approximately nine felonies, a recorder the size of a matchbox taped under the collar of her good coat. Everything Emilio says goes onto that recording, and everything on that recording goes to Dev, and everything Dev hears gets timestamped and triple-backed-up to drives hidden in three separate locations, because we learned our lesson about recordings vanishing.

And when Emilio's men move Elena tonight — because Ruthie's approach will spook him, will make him accelerate, will flush the bird out of the bush exactly the way we want — Oscar and Isandro go through the terminal's maintenance side while his people are busy loading her out the front.

"You understand," Ruthie said, in the pause after the fourth run-through, "that if he searches me, I'm finished. All of it. The wire, the testimony deal, Dev's scholarship arrangement, everything."

"He won't search you," I said.

"You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But I know you. And I know that when Emilio Vasquez looks at you, he sees 1994, and a woman who brought him coffee and covered his routes and never once asked what was in the trunks. Men like that don't search their own history. It would be like searching himself."

Ruthie looked at me for a long moment. Her eyes were wet and furious and steady, all three at once, which is a trick I hope to learn someday.

"That boy Sal trained," she said finally, "turned out to be a dangerous creature."

"He had good teachers."

"Some of them better than others." She stood, knees cracking like the radio static, and took her coat off the hook. "I'm going home to sleep. Tomorrow I betray a man I've known for forty years, so I'd like to do it rested."

She left. The door swung shut behind her, and the office felt smaller without her in it, the way a stage feels smaller between acts.

* * *

Dev and I walked Isandro out to his car at dusk. The harbor had gone the color of a bruise, green-gray and swelling, gulls riding wind that smelled like iron and coming rain. The first drops hit the pavement fat and slow, testing us.

"Say the part you're not saying," Isandro said to me, quietly, while Dev fussed with the scanner twenty feet away.

"There are a lot of parts I'm not saying. I'm a dispatcher. Silence is my whole skill set."

"Oscar."

I watched the water. "The part I'm not saying is that if this goes wrong, if Ruthie gets hurt, or Dev, or you — that's on me. I found the ledger. I pulled the thread. Sal left it for me, maybe, or maybe he just died sloppy, but either way I'm the one who wouldn't leave it alone, and now everyone I love is walking into a storm because of it."

Isandro was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the roof of his car.

"When I was eight," he said, "my mother worked nights cleaning offices downtown. One night she didn't come home. Bus accident — nothing sinister, just ice and a bad curve. But for six hours I didn't know that. Six hours, I sat on the porch steps thinking I had done something, that the world had taken her because of something I did." He looked at me. "It wasn't true then and it isn't true now. Emilio Vasquez drowned a man thirty years ago and hid a woman for twelve. You didn't build that house, Oscar. You're just the first person who turned the lights on."

"And now everybody in it is shooting at the light."

"Yeah." He almost smiled. "Welcome to police work."

Dev appeared at my elbow, holding out an earpiece. "Comms check. You'll have me in your ear the whole time. Channel's encrypted-ish, which means encrypted enough for men who buy their radios at a big-box store." They hesitated, and for once the teenager bravado dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was just a kid, scared, trying to be brave about it. "Oscar. If it goes sideways — if I say run, you run. Promise me. I can't do my job if I'm worried you won't listen."

"I promise," I said, and meant it, and we both knew I was lying, and we both let it stand, because some lies are load-bearing.

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. I sat in the dispatch office with the lights low and the rain starting to come down in earnest, and I read Sal's journal again, the real one, the boathouse copy, looking for anything we'd missed.

On the last written page, dated eleven years ago, there was a line I'd read a dozen times and never understood:

*If they move her, they'll use the old route. He never could resist the old route.*

The old route. Sal's route codes. I spread the route map across the desk — the original, the one I'd pinned above the dispatch desk and then taken down again because it felt too much like a target — and traced the ferry terminal's access roads. There were two ways in: the main approach off Harbor Road, wide, lit, obvious. And the old route — a service lane running along the seawall, built for coal delivery in some previous century, narrow, unlit, ending at a rusted gate directly behind the terminal's lower deck.

If Emilio's men moved Elena, they'd avoid cameras and witnesses. They'd take the seawall lane.

Which meant the gate behind the terminal wasn't just an exit. It was a second entrance. Ours.

I sat back. The rain hammered the windows like it wanted in. Somewhere across the harbor, in a building filling slowly with the smell of salt and old diesel, a woman who had been a ghost for twelve years was waiting for a tide to decide her future, and a man who owned half the waterfront was deciding whether to save her or silence her, and tomorrow afternoon the warmest liar in the city was going to sit down across from him and smile.

I picked up the radio, keyed it once, and said, to no one, to everyone, to the night line that had raised me:

"All units, be advised. Storm's here."

* * *

At 2 a.m., Dev's voice came through my phone, flat and fast, stripped of every joke they'd ever made.

"Oscar. Scanner traffic. Two units just checked in from the seawall gate. They're not waiting for tomorrow. They're moving her now — they moved it up, they're doing it tonight, ahead of the surge. Ten minutes. Maybe less."

I was already pulling on my jacket — my own jacket, the one that fit, the one that had started all of this — and reaching for the door.

"Get Isandro," I said. "We're going in early."
