## 36. Emilio's Endgame

*Oscar*

The storm arrived the way bad news always does at Marisol Cabs — first as a rumor on the scanner, then all at once, everywhere, with no respect for your sleep schedule.

I was at the depot at four in the morning because I hadn't been able to be anywhere else. Ruthie was in Emilio's office wearing a wire the size of a shirt button, and Dev was in the back room surrounded by three laptops and a radio rig that would've made the FCC weep, and Isandro was technically suspended and therefore sitting in his personal car across the street like the world's most committed off-duty boyfriend, which — no. Not boyfriend. Colleague. Person of interest adjacent. The taxonomy of what Isandro Reyes was to me had gotten so complicated I'd stopped trying to file it.

The point is: nobody slept. We were all waiting for Ruthie's voice to come through Dev's receiver and tell us it had worked.

It worked. That was the problem.

"Got her," Dev said at 4:47 a.m., not looking up from the screen. Their voice had that flat calm they got when things were going wrong fast — the same tone they used reading me the weather alert. "Ruthie's wire is live. She's in his office. He's talking."

"Talking about what?"

"Elena." Dev's fingers were moving before I finished the word, pulling audio into a file, copying it to two drives, then a third, because we'd learned that lesson the hard way when the last recording evaporated out of police evidence like morning fog. "He's offering her a ride. Quote — 'the girl needs to be moved tonight, before the surge.' Unquote."

My stomach did something acrobatic and unlicensed. "Moved where?"

"That's the thing." Dev finally looked up, and behind the glasses their eyes were doing arithmetic I didn't like. "He's not using phones. He's not using the firm's channels. He just switched to marine band. Channel 68, low power, short bursts." They tapped a speaker, and a man's voice crackled through — clipped, bored, professional. *Harbormaster says slip's clear. Bring her around the north side, away from the cameras.*

"Marine band," I said.

"I've been sitting on it since midnight," Dev said. "Ever since the storm warning went up, I figured if they moved her, they'd move her by water. Storm means coast guard's busy, harbor patrol's pulled back, half the pier cameras are down from wind damage." A pause. "Emilio Vasquez has been planning this exit for thirty years, Oscar. He's just been waiting for weather like this to give him cover."

Outside, the rain hit the windows sideways, like it was trying to get in and arrest somebody itself.

I picked up my phone and called Isandro, who answered on the first ring, which meant he'd been holding the phone, which meant he hadn't slept either.

"Tell me you're hearing this," I said.

"I'm hearing it." His voice was rough with exhaustion and something harder underneath. "Dev patched me in twenty minutes ago. Oscar—" and there it was, the pause, the professional reflex wrestling the personal one, "—if they're moving her by boat, we have maybe an hour before she's gone. And I can't call this in."

"Because you're suspended."

"Because anyone I call might be Emilio's." I heard him exhale. "The leak sits above my captain. Every official channel is a gamble. If I roll a full tactical team to the waterfront on a hunch and a teenager's radio intercept, and one person in that chain makes one phone call—"

"She's gone. Yeah." I stared at the rain. On the dispatch board in front of me, the night's calls glowed their little patient colors — a fare to the airport, a driver out of gas, the ordinary machinery of a city that didn't know what was happening under its skin. "So we don't use the chain."

"Oscar."

"We use us." I said it before I could talk myself out of it, which is the only way I ever say anything. "You, me, Dev on comms, Ruthie when she's clear. Coast guard can't be reached through channels? Fine. I know a guy who knows a guy — Marisol's been running night fares to the station for twenty years, half those officers owe us rides home from their own retirement parties. One call, direct, no dispatch log. Man to man."

Silence on the line. Rain. Then Isandro, quietly: "This is exactly how people end up suspended permanently."

"This is exactly how people end up *right*," I said. "You can pick one. You've been picking wrong for a week."

Another silence, longer. When he spoke again, some of the exhaustion had burned off, replaced by the thing I'd been watching kindle in him since Pier 9 — that stubborn, careful fire he kept trying to smother under procedure. "Direct line to Sergeant Okafor, harbor unit. He owes me. I'll call him myself, off book. But Oscar — when we find that dock, we do not go in alone. Promise me."

"I promise," I said, and both of us knew I was lying, and both of us let it stand, because that's what love looks like at four in the morning during a hurricane: two people agreeing to believe each other's obvious lies.

* * *

*Isandro*

Here is what they don't put in the academy brochure: how quiet a city gets at five a.m. in a storm. Harborview's streets were rivers with streetlights drowning in them, and every intersection I drove through felt like passing through the inside of a bell after it's been struck — that ringing hush, everything vibrating with held force.

Okafor had come through. Of course he had; twelve years ago I'd talked a review board out of ending his career over a paperwork error, and men like Marcus Hale collect favors, but so do I. Two coast guard response boats were repositioned to "routine storm patrol" along the inner harbor — meaning within six minutes of any dock between Pier 8 and Pier 14. It wasn't a raid team. It was a prayer with an engine.

The problem was the dock itself. Dev's intercepts gave us movement, but Emilio was smart enough to keep the destination fluid — three possible slips mentioned across four transmissions, each one named once and never repeated. North side of the marina. The old ferry maintenance dock. And once, almost lost under static: *"Pier 9, if the surge covers the approach."*

Pier 9. Where the drowned man washed up wearing Oscar's clothes. Where this whole thing started. Emilio had a sense of theater, or a sense of symmetry, or both — the kind of man who returns to the scene because he believes the water that hid his sins once will hide them again.

I parked two blocks from the marina entrance and walked, because a car with government plates — even fake ones — was a beacon in a neighborhood this empty. Rain came off the harbor in horizontal sheets. My suspension letter was in my glove box. My badge was in my pocket anyway, because a piece of laminated metal doesn't stop being what it is just because a compromised captain said so.

Oscar met me at the corner of Berth Lane, hood up, soaked through anyway, holding a thermos like it was a weapon.

"You promised," I said.

"I promised I wouldn't go in alone." They nodded toward the shadows by the fence line. "I brought witnesses."

Ruthie stepped out from under the awning of the closed bait shop, seventy-three years old, hair plastered flat, holding a tire iron with the serenity of a woman who'd driven night shift in this city before I was born. "Emilio's office emptied out at five-fifteen," she said. "Him, two of the firm boys, and a woman with her face down and her hands cuffed loose in front — loose, Detective, like they wanted her able to walk but not run. They took the service road toward the water. I followed as far as the gate."

"They see you?"

"They saw a crazy old lady waiting out a bus in a hurricane." Ruthie smiled without warmth. "That's been my whole career, mijo. Being underestimated is a pension plan."

Dev's voice crackled in my ear — they'd rigged us each with an earpiece off the same intercept rig, a legality so far past questionable it needed a passport. *"Update,"* they said. *"Two more bursts on 68. First one: 'coming to you via the ferry approach, ten minutes.' Second one:"* a pause, and I heard Dev swallow, *"second one was a mayday relay. Somebody else on the band heard engine noise heading into the restricted zone and flagged it. Coast guard station logged it at 5:31."*

"So the cavalry's already sniffing," Oscar said.

"The cavalry's logging it," Dev corrected. "In a storm like this, a mayday relay goes in a queue. Could be forty minutes before anyone responds. Could be they write it off as ghost echo off the breakwater." Another pause. "Also — Oscar — the gray sedan just passed the marina gate camera. Heading in."

Everything in me went very still and very cold. "Hale's with him?"

"Can't confirm the passenger. But it's the sedan."

I looked at Oscar. In the sodium light bleeding from the bait shop sign, their face was pale and set, and I recognized the expression because I'd been wearing a version of it for weeks: the look of someone who has run out of road behind them and discovered the only direction left is through.

"Ferry approach puts them at the old terminal," I said. "But Pier 9 was named too. He'll have a contingency. Men like him always stage the escape twice."

"Then we split the difference," Oscar said. "Terminal's the primary. Pier 9's the fallback. Dev keeps listening. And when we hear which one—"

*"We won't have to hear it,"* Dev cut in, sharp. *"Just got a burst. Plain text, no codes — he's sloppy now, he's rushing. 'Slip nine. Now.'"*

For one second none of us moved. The rain filled the silence.

Then Ruthie handed Oscar the tire iron, handle first, like a sword being passed down. "Go get that girl," she said. "I'll watch the road. Nobody drives out of here past me."

And Oscar took it, and looked at me, and said the thing I'd been dreading and needing since the night we met over a dead stranger's borrowed clothes:

"Pier 9, Detective. Where it started. Let's go finish it."

We ran into the storm together, and behind us the harbor rose up black and hungry, keeping its appointments.

* * *

*Oscar*

Pier 9 in a storm surge is not a place. It's an argument the ocean is having with civilization, and civilization is losing.

The planking was already underwater in places, waves punching up through the gaps like the sea was knocking from below. The boats in the neighboring slips strained at their lines, screaming. And at the far end, floodlit by one surviving work lamp, sat a cabin cruiser I didn't need a registration database to identify — white hull, blue canvas, the kind of boat that costs more than a cab driver makes in a decade.

Emilio Vasquez stood on the deck in a rain jacket worth more than my car, and beside him, wrapped in a blanket, hood shoved back by the wind, stood a young woman with dark hair plastered to her face and eyes that scanned the pier like she'd spent her whole life memorizing exits.

Elena. Alive. Twenty feet of black water between us.

Behind Emilio, two of the firm's men wrestled with the mooring lines, and beyond them, at the landward end of the pier, idling with its lights off, the gray sedan.

Isandro's hand found my arm — *wait, wait* — but there was no waiting left. The surge was climbing the pilings. Every minute shrank the pier and grew the sea, and Emilio only needed one minute.

So I did the thing Sal's ledger had taught me, the thing thirty years of ghost fares had taught me, the thing this whole rotten beautiful inheritance had been teaching me since a drowned stranger washed up in my clothes:

I walked out onto the water's edge and made myself impossible to ignore.

"EMILIO!" My voice tore against the wind. "YOU FORGOT TO PAY YOUR FARE!"

He turned. Even at that distance, even through rain, I saw it — the flicker of contempt and curiosity, the look of a man who cannot believe the help is speaking. He said something to his men, and one of them started toward me, hand inside his jacket, and Isandro stepped out of the dark beside me with his badge held high and his gun drawn low and said, in his cop voice, the voice that had interviewed me with such unbearable gentleness a lifetime ago:

"Port Authority Police! Step away from the vessel!"

Which was, strictly speaking, a lie — he was suspended, and it wasn't his jurisdiction, and the badge in his hand belonged to a department currently leaking like the pier beneath our feet. It was also, strictly speaking, the truest thing he'd ever said. Some titles you don't carry. They carry you.

The armed man hesitated. Emilio didn't. Emilio laughed — I heard it over the storm, delighted, genuine — and shouted back:

"Dispatcher! Of course. Sal's little stray. You know what your mentor and I had in common, Oscar?" He spread his arms wide over the black water, master of his thirty-year kingdom, one tide away from escaping it. "*Neither of us ever leaves a fare unpaid!*"

The engines coughed to life.

And Elena Vargas — hidden daughter of a drowned dockworker, the passenger in Sal's ledger, the truth the tide had been carrying toward us for thirty years — looked straight at me across the water and jumped.

Not toward the boat. Toward *us*. Into the surge, into the dark, into the gap between Emilio's empire and whatever we were — and the sea, greedy old creditor, rose up to take its interest.

I was moving before I decided to.
