## 2. Room 704, Ten Years On

The Grand Meridian kept two sets of records. The first lived in the manager's office, in manila folders with official letterheads, in the kind of filing system an insurance auditor could love. The second lived behind the concierge desk, in a locked drawer that had been locked by every concierge before me and would be locked by every one after, containing the things the hotel preferred not to have happened.

I had inherited the drawer from a man named Osgood, who had inherited it from a woman named Pell, who had reportedly inherited it from nobody at all — she'd simply started it, the way you start any honest ledger: by writing down what everyone else agreed to forget.

The drawer held eleven files when I opened it that evening, after the lobby emptied and the night staff settled into their rhythms. Ten of them I knew like my own signature. The eleventh I had read exactly once, on my third week at the desk, and then returned to its place with the particular care one gives a thing that might still be warm.

The file was thin for what it contained. A guest death always generates paper; this one generated barely enough to line a birdcage.

*Halloway, Daniel R.* Business traveler, forty-six, from a city I've since forgotten because everything else about him was erased so thoroughly. Found on the morning of October 14th, ten years ago last month, in room 704. Cause of death per the medical examiner: fall in the bathroom, blunt trauma consistent with slipping against the tub edge. Blood alcohol twice the legal limit. Case closed within the day. The police report — a photocopy, the original never having come to us — ran to four pages and read like it had been written by someone eager to catch a train.

I laid the contents out across the desk in the order they'd accumulated. Incident report, typed by the then-duty manager. Statement from the housekeeper who found him — signed, terse, three sentences long. A maintenance log noting a "loose tile, bath area, rm 704" repaired two days *after* the death, which was either diligence or evidence-tidying depending on how much charity you were feeling. And at the bottom, a single sheet in Edmund Voss's own handwriting, which was distinctive — small, upright, almost architectural — instructing all staff to refer press inquiries to the corporate office and adding, in a sentence that had always struck me as slightly too much: *"We owe Mr. Halloway's memory our discretion."*

Discretion. The Meridian's favorite virtue. It sounds like kindness until you notice how often it does the work of fear.

I sat with the file for a long time, and then I took out the confession letter and set it beside the incident report, and I did what I do with every case, large or small, which is to look for the seam — the place where the story doesn't hold its own weight.

Here is what I found.

The confession said: *"I killed the man in room 704 ten years ago tonight."*

Room 704. Except room 704 hadn't been room 704 on the night Daniel Halloway died. Afterward — six weeks afterward, per the renovation schedule I pulled from the engineering office under the pretense of confirming a banquet setup — the hotel had renumbered the entire seventh floor. Rooms 701 through 712 became 715 through 726. It was done quietly, during a slow season, and the stated reason was fire-code compliance, some quirk about stairwell access that required shifting the numbering sequence. Plausible. Hotels renumber rooms the way other institutions rewrite history: bureaucratically, and only when they must.

But anyone who had actually stood outside that door on that morning — anyone who had found the body, or cleaned the room, or carried the stretcher down the service corridor — would have known the room by its old number the way you know your childhood address. You don't say *the house at 44 Marlowe* if it was 46 when you lived there. Memory keeps the number it was given.

So the confession writer knew the story of room 704 but not the room itself. Knew it secondhand. From the file, or from talk, or from the version of events that had calcified into hotel legend over ten years of staff turnover.

Which meant the confession was written by someone who wasn't there.

And someone who wasn't there, writing a false confession, mailing it to the concierge desk rather than the police — because it came to me, remember, folded inside the morning post like any other piece of the hotel's business — wanted something other than justice. You don't confess a murder to a concierge unless you want the concierge to do something. And you don't get the details wrong unless getting them right matters less than getting noticed at all.

It was bait. I wrote the word in pencil on a scrap of card stock and looked at it for a while. Bait for whom? For me? I was nobody ten years ago; I hadn't yet earned my drawer. Or for whoever *was* here ten years ago — whoever might still be carrying the truth around like a stone in a coat pocket, waiting for permission to set it down?

A false confession is a flare fired into the dark. The question isn't who lit it. The question is who it was meant to make move.

* * *

I did what I always do when a case turns strange: I made tea and I made lists.

The tea was oolong, left over from a guest who'd checked out without finishing the tin, because sentimentality is a luxury and waste is not. The list went up on the back wall of the mailroom, where no guest would ever wander, in my own shorthand — initials and arrows, the cartography of a decade-old night.

Who was on shift, October 13th to 14th?

Front desk: T. Okonkwo (gone — Lagos, per a Christmas card that stopped coming four years back). Relief clerk whose name I couldn't retrieve. Night porter: M. Vega.

Marisol. Of course Marisol. Marisol had been the night porter since before I arrived and would be the night porter when they pried the service entrance keys from her hands. If the hotel were a ship, Marisol would be its keel — invisible, structural, holding everything above the waterline.

Housekeeping supervisor that year: a woman named Brandt, retired to somewhere coastal. The chambermaid assigned to the seventh floor: Rosa Ibarra. Gone. The word *gone* doing a great deal of work in that sentence — gone how, gone where, the file didn't say, and neither, apparently, did anyone.

Security logs: purged on the standard five-year cycle, which is the standard cycle for nothing except making sure certain things expire.

And the duty manager who typed the incident report, who signed the statement, who coordinated with police and press and family — the man whose neat architectural handwriting sat at the bottom of the file telling everyone to be discreet —

Edmund Voss. Twenty-two years managing the Meridian. Retired six years ago to a standing ovation at his farewell dinner, at which the owner called him "the soul of this house" and meant it. There are staff members who still keep his photograph at their stations. There is a fountain in the courtyard he paid for personally. When guests ask, as they sometimes do, whether the hotel has ever had a truly great manager, the answer comes back like scripture: Voss.

I looked at his name on my list for a long time. Then I added a question mark, because that is the discipline of the work — you don't let suspicion harden into conclusion before the evidence votes. But the question mark felt less like doubt than like a promise.

Because here was the thing I kept circling back to, the thing that had tilted the floor beneath my professional calm that morning and hadn't righted it since: the renumbering. Six weeks after the death, the seventh floor changed its numbers. Renumberings require authorization. Authorization requires a reason. Fire codes are real, but they are also wonderfully convenient, and the timing — close enough to matter, distant enough to look routine — had the shape of a decision made by someone who understood that a room where a man died should stop being findable.

Voss had signed off on the renovation schedule. I checked. His initials, E.V., in the approval column, dated nine days after Halloway's body went to the morgue.

The man who told the staff to be discreet had also, very promptly, deleted the room.

* * *

At half past midnight I gave up on sleep and came back down, because the desk is where I think best — the low hum of the building, the elevator breathing through its shafts, the sense of a thousand sleeping lives I'm nominally responsible for. The overnight audit glowed green on the terminal. Somewhere upstairs a guest was running a bath, and I caught myself listening to the water with an attention that had nothing to do with plumbing.

Ten years ago tonight — well, nearly; the anniversary was still weeks out, though the letters had said *tonight*, another small wrongness, another detail bent just enough to snag — a man had fallen or been fallen upon in a bathroom on the seventh floor, and the whole apparatus of the Meridian had closed around the fact like water closing over a stone. Police in and out in a day. A doctor's certificate I hadn't seen yet but could infer. A chambermaid vanished. A porter who wouldn't meet my eye that morning when I'd mentioned the letters, her face going carefully, professionally blank — the face of someone standing in front of a door and deciding, visibly, whether to hold it shut.

I turned the confession letter over again. Same hand as the second letter — I'd compared them twice more, the looping g's, the flat-topped t's, the ink that looked like the same pen pressed at the same hour. One hand, two letters, two messages: *I did it* and *there is more.* A confession and a threat, mailed together, addressed to no one and therefore to whoever opened the post.

Designed to be unsolvable, I'd thought that morning. But unsolvable wasn't quite right. It was solvable in exactly one direction — toward attention, toward the anniversary, toward whatever was planned for the date the victim's family had chosen to gather and mourn. The letter-writer didn't want the truth found. The letter-writer wanted the wound reopened wide enough that something would finally crawl out of it.

Or — and this was the thought that followed me up the service stairs at one in the morning, past the laundry, past the ice machines humming their patient hymn — the letter-writer wanted to see who flinched.

I stopped on the landing between six and seven. The seventh-floor corridor stretched away from me, carpeted in the new wine-dark pattern, doors numbered 715 through 726, all of them asleep, none of them guilty of anything except standing where other doors used to be.

Somewhere behind one of these doors, ten years ago, a man named Daniel Halloway stopped existing, and a maid named Rosa Ibarra started doing the same, and the hotel — my hotel, the only home I have ever trusted — helped it happen.

I had solved small cases for fifteen years. Lost rings, wandering spouses, a senator's missing dog. I had never once opened a door the house itself had locked.

Tomorrow I would knock on Marisol's door, whatever she'd decided to hold shut behind it.

Tonight I stood on the landing a moment longer, and then I went down, and I unlocked the drawer, and I put the file back — but not in its old place at the bottom of the stack. I moved it to the top, where a working file belongs, and I left the drawer unlocked.

Some cases you choose. This one had chosen me, and it was rude to keep it waiting.
