## 2. Night Shift Frequencies

The dispatch office at 2 a.m. smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, the way it always did, the way it had for the eleven years August Okonkwo had worked the night desk for Parish Taxi & Courier, back when it was still called Parish Cab and before the county consolidated the ambulance runs into her queue. The building had been a bank once. You could still see the ghost of the teller line under the linoleum, a darker stripe of floor where a hundred years of shoes had worn the same path. August liked that about the place. It reminded her that most buildings in Parish were just older buildings wearing new paint, and most stories were just older stories wearing new names.

She had the desk to herself. Day shift came in at six, and the radio between now and then was usually nothing — a fare from the hospital to the east side, a kid locked out of his house, the occasional drunk who'd mistaken the number for the pizza place. Which was fine. August didn't take the night shift for the excitement. She took it because at 2 a.m., Parish stopped performing and started telling the truth.

Tonight she wasn't listening to the radio. She had three manila folders spread across the desk, and the coffin lid from that afternoon kept replaying behind her eyes — the way it had come up light, the way Dale Pruitt's face had gone the color of the silt inside the box.

*Only river silt,* she thought, *and a nameplate polished bright.*

You didn't polish brass with dirt. Somebody had handled that plate, and recently enough that the shine hadn't dulled. Dale had seen it too — she'd caught the look that crossed his face before he'd smoothed it into funeral-home blankness. But Dale Pruitt had been burying Vey family business for forty years, and whatever he knew, he'd take to his grave with the same professionalism he brought to everything else.

August didn't have professionalism. She had a log book, a county scanner, and a memory that her last boyfriend had once described, mid-argument, as *weaponized*. He hadn't meant it as a compliment. She'd taken it as one.

She pulled the first folder toward her. The sexton.

* * *

Emmett Grady had drowned in March, two years ago, in the retention pond behind St. Brigid's where he'd gone, according to the coroner, to check a drainage grate after heavy rain. Blood alcohol of 0.11. Boots found on the bank, neatly placed. The town had shaken its head — poor Emmett, always a few drinks past sensible — and the story had settled into the ground like everything else in Parish.

But August had been working the night Emmett died, and she remembered something the coroner's report didn't contain. At 11:42 p.m., forty minutes before Emmett's body was found, a call had come into the dispatch line from a payphone outside the Vey Memorial Chapel. She remembered it because the caller had asked for a cab to the cemetery, and then, when she'd quoted the fare, had gone quiet in a way that made her check the line was still open. A man's voice. Older. He'd said, *No — never mind. I'll walk,* and hung up.

She'd logged it. She logged everything; it was the one discipline of her life. Call number 2214, March 9th, 11:42 p.m., payphone, St. Brigid's chapel, requested destination: Parish Cemetery, cancelled.

The coroner put Emmett in the water sometime between 11:30 and midnight.

August sat with that for a while, the office clock ticking, the coffee going cold in its ring-stained mug. One cancelled call wasn't a pattern. One cancelled call was a drunk man changing his mind, the way drunks did. But she'd spent eleven years learning to hear the difference between a voice that was drunk and a voice that was afraid, and she'd replayed that call in her head a hundred times without ever deciding which it was.

She opened the second folder. The archivist.

* * *

Marion Fessler had burned in the county records annex fourteen months ago, on a Sunday night when the building was supposed to be empty. Electrical fault, said the fire marshal, origin in the basement stacks where Marion had gone, according to the sign-in sheet, to pull probate records. Marion had been the historical society's consulting archivist, a small precise woman who wore cardigans in July and could put her finger on any document in the county's collection within ninety seconds. August had met her twice. She'd seemed like the kind of person who checked the coffeemaker twice before leaving a room.

The fire marshal's report was three pages long and contained the phrase *no evidence of accelerant* twice, which August had always found interesting, because in her experience you only repeated yourself when you were trying to convince somebody.

She went to the taxi logs. This was the part that took time, and this was the part she was good at. The dispatch system was old — the county kept promising an upgrade the way the county kept promising a new bridge — but the call logs went back to 2004, indexed by date, and August had a habit of cross-reading them against the police scanner traffic she recorded every night, a habit born of boredom and refined into something close to scholarship.

Marion Fessler died on Sunday, June 14th. August ran the week before.

June 9th, 4:17 p.m., call from the historical society office, request for a cab to Parish Cemetery. Driver: Kowalski. Fare: a woman, alone. August remembered Kowalski mentioning it — he'd been annoyed, because the cemetery was a dead run, no return fare, and the woman had barely spoken the whole way. He'd called her *the cardigan lady* and hadn't thought about her again.

June 9th. Five days before Marion Fessler burned in a basement full of probate records.

August's pen stopped moving.

She made herself keep going. Made herself run the third folder the same way, mechanically, the way you'd run a checklist before landing. Because the cold thing was already happening in her chest, the thing she'd felt a handful of times in eleven years of listening to strangers' worst nights — the feeling of a pattern snapping into focus, each piece clicking into the next with the terrible satisfaction of a lock turning. She'd felt it twice on the job, both times before bad news. She felt it now, and it felt like standing on a frozen pond and hearing the ice speak.

* * *

The third one was the viaduct. Danny Ostrander, thirty-one, ironworker, fell from the Parish River viaduct on the night of October 2nd last year. The coroner ruled it consistent with a misstep — Danny had a history, the report noted, of drinking, and the rail on the east span was known to be low. His mother had stood up at the county meeting about the rail and said her son didn't drink, and the county had expressed its sympathies and replaced the rail the following spring.

August had taken a call that night too. Not from Danny — from a man, 9:51 p.m., calling from the gas station at the foot of the viaduct road, asking for a cab to the cemetery. She'd quoted the fare and the man had said, in a voice she still couldn't place, *How late are your drivers out?* and she'd said, *Until six,* and he'd said, *That's fine. That's fine,* in a way that wasn't fine at all, and then he hadn't shown at the pickup. Twenty-six minutes later, the first scanner call came in about a body on the rocks below the east span.

September 28th. Four days before Danny fell. Call from the Ostrander residence, request for a cab to Parish Cemetery. Driver: Kowalski again, as it happened — the cemetery run was his least favorite and he complained about it every time. Fare: Danny Ostrander. Destination, per Kowalski's recollection when August called him at home and woke him up: the Vey plot. Danny had asked to be dropped at the gate and had walked in alone, and Kowalski had waited twenty minutes on the meter because Danny had told him to, and then Danny had come back out looking, in Kowalski's words, *like he'd seen a ghost, no offense,* and hadn't said a word the whole ride home.

August put the pen down.

Three deaths. Three calls to the cemetery in the week before each one. A sexton, an archivist, an ironworker — three people with no connection to each other except, apparently, the Vey plot, and the town's one hundredth anniversary of Isandro Vey's death, which was now four days away and had been on every banner and bakery window in Parish since spring.

She got up and paced the length of the old teller line, her footsteps falling into the worn path of the ghost floor. The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere outside, a freight train dragged its whistle across the river valley, the sound Parish slept under the way other towns slept under highway hum.

Think, she told herself. Don't feel it yet. Feel it later.

* * *

The feeling could wait. The work couldn't. She went back to the desk and started building it properly, because a pattern in your head was a suspicion, but a pattern on paper was a case.

She took a fresh legal pad and drew three columns. Names. Dates of death. Dates of cemetery contact. Then a fourth column, and this was the one that mattered, the one she almost didn't write because writing it made the whole thing real: *What did they see?*

Emmett Grady. Sexton of St. Brigid's, caretaker of the parish cemetery, keeper of the Vey plot. A man whose job was graves. Who would know, better than any man alive, what was in the ground and what wasn't.

Marion Fessler. Archivist. A woman whose job was paper. Probate records, she'd gone to pull. Probate records, in a basement, on a Sunday, five days after visiting the grave of a man whose death certificate was the founding document of half the county's legal history.

Danny Ostrander. And here August stopped, because Danny didn't fit — and then, slowly, he did. She'd gone to Danny's memorial. Everybody had; he was well liked. And she'd stood at the back, as she always did, listening, and she'd heard his cousin say that Danny had been strange the last month of his life, talking about a woman he'd met out at the cemetery, a woman he said lived out there somehow, and everyone had laughed and said Danny and his women, and the cousin had said no, he'd been serious, he'd said she was *somebody from the old days* and he was going to help her, and then Danny had fallen off a bridge.

A woman at the cemetery. Somebody from the old days.

August sat very still. The clock said 3:14. The coffee had gone from cold to undrinkable, and she drank it anyway.

Here was the thing she couldn't stop circling, the thing that had been circling her since Dale Pruitt's face went gray over an open box: everybody in this town knew the story of Isandro Vey. Founder, benefactor, the man whose trust built the school and the library and half the riverfront, the man whose death certificate was signed, witnessed, filed, and celebrated for a hundred years. The centennial was a *festival*. There were commemorative spoons.

But a sexton had gone to his grave in the week after visiting that plot. An archivist had burned in the week after pulling probate records. An ironworker had fallen off a bridge in the week after meeting a woman at the graveside — a woman who, if you believed Danny, had no business being alive, because the only woman in Parish's history who fit *somebody from the old days* and the Vey name was a story the historical society told the way you tell children about the bogeyman: poor Ruthie, the daughter nobody ever saw, dead before she could walk, God rest her.

August had heard that story her whole life. She'd never once heard it told by somebody who'd actually seen the girl.

Three people had gone looking at that grave in the last two years. Three people had come away with something — a sight, a document, a conversation — and three people were dead, and every death had been ruled the way Parish ruled everything: sad, regrettable, nobody's fault, case closed, next.

And underneath it all, in a box that had been in the ground for a hundred years: no body. River silt, and brass polished by living hands.

She wrote at the bottom of the legal pad, in her small square handwriting, the question she'd been avoiding since the cemetery:

*If Isandro Vey never came home — who has been coming to his grave?*

The radio crackled. A fare from the hospital. She took it on autopilot, dispatched Kowalski, logged it, and the whole time her eyes kept going back to the legal pad, to the three columns, to the fourth.

There was one more thing. She'd been holding it back from herself, the way you hold back the last stair in the dark. She made herself face it.

Every one of those calls — Emmett's cancelled call, Marion's ride, Danny's ride — had come through *her* line. Her dispatch. Her log book. Three times in two years, someone connected to the Vey plot had called Parish Taxi, and three times August had been the voice on the other end. Coincidence was possible. Parish was small; there was only one cab company.

But the man at the viaduct gas station hadn't asked for a cab, not really. He'd asked *how late are your drivers out.* And the man at the chapel payphone had gone quiet after she'd quoted the fare, quiet in the way of a man listening — not to her, but to something behind her, or deciding something about her. She'd thought, at the time, that she'd heard a second set of breathing on that line. She'd told herself it was the payphone, the wind off the river, the old wires.

She hadn't told anybody about the breathing. She hadn't even told herself, not properly.

August looked at the clock: 3:41. In four days, the whole town would walk to that cemetery with flowers and speeches and commemorative spoons, and they would stand around an empty box and celebrate a death that hadn't happened, and three graves away from the ceremony would be the people who'd found out what the box was really for.

She tore the top sheet off the legal pad, folded it in quarters, and put it in her shirt pocket, over the heart, which was where she kept things she wasn't ready to say out loud.

Tomorrow, she decided, she'd go to the historical society. Marion Fessler had worked there. Marion's files had burned — but the historical society had a junior archivist now, a nervous kid named Peter Lutz who'd once told her, unprompted and at length, about the society's duplicate holdings in the county annex, the ones that hadn't burned. And the society had Cordelia Vey, who was to Parish's official memory what the church was to its official forgiveness: the keeper, the gatekeeper, and the person you had to get past.

Cordelia Vey, who signed the checks for the centennial. Cordelia Vey, whose family name was on the empty box.

August pulled her coat off the back of the chair and killed the desk lamp, and in the sudden dark the radio hissed softly, all those frequencies full of voices nobody was listening to yet. She'd learned long ago that in Parish, the truth didn't announce itself. It came in on the night line, quiet, one call at a time, waiting for somebody to write it down.

She was halfway to the door when it rang.
