## 5. Accident Number Four

The call came in at 4:17 in the morning, and August knew before the dispatcher on the other end of the line finished saying the words.

"Unit twelve's in the water," the state patrol sergeant said. "Off the Dock Street ramp. Cab went through the guardrail."

"Is he out?" she asked, though her hands were already pulling up the unit roster, already finding the name.

"They're working on it."

They worked on it for forty minutes. August sat at her console with the headset pressed to one ear, listening to the channel the way you listen to a heartbeat — waiting for it to change, knowing what it will do instead. At 5:02 they pulled Delmar Osei out of the river, and at 5:03 a voice on the frequency said the word *deceased* in the flat tone people use for weather, and August took off her headset and set it down very carefully, as if it might go off.

She had spoken to him eleven hours earlier.

---

Delmar Osei drove cab number seven for Parish Taxi & Livery, which was to say he drove the night shift nobody wanted, the graveyard run between the bus depot and the last bars closing, and he had done it for thirty-one years without so much as a fender-bender. He was a small, precise man who kept his cab cleaner than most people kept their kitchens, and he called dispatch every few weeks for no particular reason — to report a streetlight out on Corliss, to ask whether the viaduct ice had been salted, once, memorably, to complain that somebody had been leaving flowers at the cemetery gate at three in the morning and he didn't like driving past it.

August liked him. That was the whole of it. In a town where everyone she spoke to on this frequency was either drunk, frightened, or performing, Delmar was none of those things. He was just a man doing his rounds, reporting the world as he found it.

Yesterday afternoon — yesterday, she kept having to correct herself; it still felt like the same day — he'd called in at 5:40 p.m., his usual pre-shift check-in, and instead of hanging up he'd gone quiet on the line in that way older men do when they're deciding whether to say something.

"You still there, Miss Okonkwo?"

"I'm here, Delmar."

"That woman picture you asked about." He meant the photograph the junior archivist had slipped her — Isandro Vey, alive and laughing beside an unknown young woman, dated four years after his burial. August had spent two days showing it around quietly, and one of the places she'd shown it was the taxi office, because cabs remember everything. "I couldn't place her face. But I placed the coat."

"The coat," August repeated.

"Navy wool, double-breasted, with the brass buttons. She wore it the night I drove her to the depot. Must be — Lord, must be going back now. Nineteen seventy-something." A pause, the sound of him thinking. "You don't forget a fare like that. Young woman, middle of the night, paid cash, wouldn't give a destination till we were moving. Kept looking out the back window the whole ride like the road owed her money."

"Where'd you take her?"

"Depot. But that's not the thing." His voice dropped, and August had leaned toward the radio without meaning to, the way you lean toward a window when the wind changes. "The thing is where she told me to wait. She came back out before the bus left — missed it, actually, first bus — and she stood on the curb crying, and then she got back in my cab and said take me home, and gave me an address I've never forgotten because it wasn't a house. It was a number off Harrow Lane. The old poorhouse road."

"Harrow Lane," August said. "What number?"

"Eleven. There's nothing at eleven, Miss Okonkwo. There hasn't been anything at eleven since they tore the poorhouse annex down. But there was a light on in the trees back of the fence line, and she said let me out here, and she walked into the dark like she'd done it a hundred times." He'd cleared his throat. "I always figured it for one of those things you don't ask about. But you're asking about things now, aren't you? Since the coffin come up empty."

August had looked at the clock. 5:52 p.m. She had a shift starting at six.

"Delmar," she'd said, "can you meet me tomorrow? After your run?"

"Sure. Come by the office around ten, I'll have the logbook. I wrote the date in it. I write everything in it." And then, because he was Delmar: "You be careful who you show that picture to, honey. The Vey family still owns half the taxis in this town, you know. Always have."

That was the last thing he ever said to her. Not the address. Not the coat. *Be careful.* Thirty-one years of night driving had taught him exactly one piece of wisdom worth passing on, and he'd spent it on her.

At 10 a.m. today, August had been sitting in the dispatch office with the photograph squared in front of her, waiting for ten o'clock to become late enough to be rude. At 10:14, the call had come over the county band: vehicle in the water, Dock Street ramp, cab company unit.

She hadn't needed the unit number. There was only one cab on the river road at that hour, and only one driver left in Parish who'd been driving it long enough to remember a frightened girl in a navy coat.

---

The police were thorough, in the way Parish was thorough, which meant they were thorough about the parts that closed the file.

Sergeant Brannic came by the dispatch office at noon, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man delivering news he'd rehearsed. He was a decent man, Brannic. That was the trouble with decent men; they carried bad conclusions so gently you almost didn't check their work.

"Ice on the ramp," he said. "Guardrail's old, posts rotted through at the base — public works has a work order on it dating back two years. Cab went through, went down the embankment, into the shallows. Door was jammed." He turned his hat a quarter turn. "Coroner says drowning. No signs of foul play. I'm sorry, August. I know you knew him."

"He called me last night," August said. "Five-forty. Routine check-in."

Brannic nodded, sympathetic, already halfway to the door in his mind.

"Did you test the brakes?" she asked.

He stopped. "It went off the road, August. Brakes weren't the issue."

"Did you test them?"

A silence, the kind that has furniture in it. "We'll note it in the report," he said, which meant no.

"And the guardrail with a two-year-old work order — did anyone from public works mention who signed off on deferring the repair?"

"That's not how it works. Things get deferred. Budgets." He put his hat on, which in Brannic's language meant the conversation was over whether it was or not. "It was an accident. Old rail, black ice, a man who worked too many nights. I'm sorry. Genuinely."

She watched him go. Through the glass she could see the taxi office across the square, its lights off, a hand-lettered sign already taped inside the door: CLOSED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. Delmar's cab had been winched out of the river at nine that morning, and even from here she could see it sitting under a tarp in the impound lot, small and wrong, like a drowned thing laid out for viewing.

August sat for a long time after Brannic left. The dispatch office hummed around her — the tick of the recorder, the murmur of the open channel, the radiator knocking its slow arrhythmic knock. Somewhere in that hum was the recording of last night's 5:40 p.m. call, logged automatically like every call, preserved on tape in the archive room because the parish charter required dispatch logs be retained for thirty years and nobody had ever thought to change it.

She went and found the reel. She threaded it. She listened to Delmar's voice fill the little room — *you be careful who you show that picture to* — and then she listened to it again, and a third time, and on the third pass she heard something she'd missed live, something under his words, in the pause before he said *Harrow Lane*: a second sound. Faint. A click, maybe. Or the soft electronic chirp of another line picking up.

Party line. Half the old exchanges in Parish still ran on them. Anyone on the circuit could lift a receiver and listen, and unless they breathed, you'd never know.

Or — and this was the thought that arrived cold and complete, the way the worst thoughts do — someone had been patched in deliberately. Dispatch lines could be bridged. She'd seen it done during the flood of '89, when the volunteer fire company tied their line into hers. It took a man who knew the exchange. It took a man with access.

She rewound the tape again and wrote down the timestamp of the click to the second.

---

By evening she had spread it all out on the dispatch desk, the way she imagined detectives did in books, except her evidence was a photograph, a dead man's remembered address, and a two-second sound on a magnetic reel.

Three accidents. She wrote them on the back of a duty roster, because it was the paper nearest to hand and because writing them anywhere official felt suddenly dangerous.

*Wendell Croft, sexton, drowned in the baptismal font cistern, 1998. Called a cab to the cemetery six days prior.*

*Marian Ellery, parish archivist, died in the records-room fire, 2011. Cab receipt to the cemetery found in her effects.*

*Tomas Reyes, fell from the viaduct, 2019. His sister swore he'd been out that night but wouldn't say where. August had pulled the taxi company's fragmentary ledgers last week: a fare, cemetery gate, the night before he fell.*

And now Delmar Osei, whose sin was not that he'd visited the cemetery but that he remembered a fare from fifty years ago — a young woman in a navy coat, driven to the depot, driven home again to a number on a road where nothing existed. Delmar, who had been about to sit down across from her at ten o'clock and hand her a logbook with a date written in it.

He hadn't even made it to the meeting. They hadn't waited to see what he'd give her. They'd simply removed the possibility.

That was the part that made her hands go cold on the desk — not the death itself, terrible as it was, but the *arithmetic* of it. The other three had died after visiting the cemetery. Delmar died before telling her anything at all. Which meant whoever was watching wasn't cleaning up trails anymore. They were pruning possibilities. They were killing the future, not just the past.

And they were watching her closely enough to know about a ten o'clock appointment made on a party line at 5:40 the previous evening.

August got up and checked the parking lot. Her car sat alone under the sodium light, ordinary, untouched. She looked at it for a long moment anyway. Then she went back inside and locked the door behind her, which she had never once done on shift, and sat back down at the console.

The recorder ticked. The channel murmured. Out past the square, the river moved in the dark the way it always had, patient and total, taking what it was given.

She understood, sitting there, that something had ended. For three weeks — since the coffin lid had come up light and the undertaker's face had gone gray — she had been an observer. A woman with a good memory and a night job, following a pattern the way you follow weather: interested, attentive, safely outside it. She had told herself that was what she was right up until Brannic put his hat on and called it an accident, and she had let him, had said nothing, had watched him walk out, because some trained and reasonable part of her still believed the town's oldest rule: that things in Parish are what the reports say they are.

Delmar Osei had broken that rule for her. He'd spent his last words on a warning, and the warning had cost him everything, and the least she owed him was to stop pretending the reports were true.

She picked up the pen. Under the three accidents she wrote a fourth, and under it, in letters small and hard:

*Not accidents. Maintenance.*

Then, below that, the only lead that mattered, the thread the killers didn't know she held — because Delmar had given it to her on an open channel, yes, but they'd been listening for names and dates, and an address where nothing exists sounds like nothing:

*Harrow Lane, 11. The light in the trees.*

The poorhouse road. The ledger page razored out of the county book — an infant girl, surname Vey, 1962, delivered by a man paid in cash. A photograph of Isandro Vey standing beside a living young woman four years after his funeral. And now a physical place, a fence line, a light that burned in the dark behind the annex that was torn down, where a frightened girl in a navy coat had walked home like she'd done it a hundred times.

Someone had lived hidden on that road. Maybe someone still did. The town said Ruthie Vey was dead — Cordelia would say it again, August was sure, in that warm drawing-room voice that closed doors — but towns said a great many things, and August had spent twenty years on the night shift learning the difference between what people reported and what was actually on the line.

At 11:48 p.m., the phone rang. She let it ring twice, composing her voice, and answered with the standard cadence, neutral as static.

"Parish dispatch."

A pause. Then a woman's voice, low and silken and unmistakable, the voice that had deflected every question at the historical society like a fencer turning blades: "Miss Okonkwo. Cordelia Vey. I hear you've had a dreadful week, losing poor Mr. Osei. Such a devoted servant of this town." A soft pause, precisely measured. "I wonder if you might come to tea tomorrow. There are things I think you ought to understand about our history — things that might spare you further grief."

August looked at the duty roster on her desk. At the four names. At the address written beneath them in her own small, hard hand.

"How kind," she said. "What time?"
