## 1. The Retainer

The rain had been coming down on Graywater Hollow for six days straight, the kind of patient, gray rain that makes a town feel like it's being slowly dissolved. I was at my desk with a cup of tea gone cold enough to qualify as evidence when the bell above my door rang — that tarnished little brass thing I'd been meaning to oil for three years — and a man stepped in out of the weather like he'd been assembled rather than born.

That's not an exaggeration. That's an observation. Twenty-two years as coroner of this town teaches you to read bodies the way other people read newspapers, and everything about this one read *wrong*. Pale in a way that wasn't sickly so much as deliberate, like he'd decided color was optional. Tall, narrow, dressed in charcoal wool with no wrinkles anywhere, which after a walk through that rain should have been impossible. His hair was dark and cut with geometric precision, and his hands — I always look at hands first; hands tell you what a person does — were clean, uncalloused, and very still.

Nobody's hands are ever that still. Grief moves them. Nerves move them. Even liars fidget somewhere below the wrists.

"Ms. Halloran?" His voice was quiet and exact, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Depends on who's asking."

"Isandro Vey." He didn't offer a hand. Good instinct. I hadn't offered mine either. "You're the coroner."

"I'm the coroner, the tax preparer of last resort, and occasionally the only person in town willing to tell Mrs. Aldous that her cat died of old age and not malice." I set down the tea. "Which brings us to you. What can I do for you, Mr. Vey?"

He crossed the room without hurrying and sat in the chair across from my desk — the client chair, though clients were rare enough that it mostly held files waiting to be filed. He sat the way people sit when they've rehearsed it. Spine straight, ankles crossed, coat still buttoned.

"I want to retain you," he said.

"This is a coroner's office, not a law firm. I don't take retainers. I take bodies."

"You'll want to hear the request first."

I've learned over two decades that when someone says *you'll want to hear this*, they're usually wrong, and hearing it anyway costs you sleep. But pride is a funny thing. Mine has always been tied to closing cases — every death in Graywater Hollow accounted for, explained, signed off in my own careful handwriting. In twenty-two years I have never left a file open. There are worse legacies. There are also, I was about to learn, stranger ones.

"All right," I said. "Hear it I will."

He reached into his coat and produced an envelope. Not a checkbook, not a card. An envelope, thick and soft-edged, that landed on my desk with the particular heavy whisper of cash. A lot of cash.

"That's a retainer," he said. "There's more where it came from."

"There's a problem with that already," I said, not touching it. "Cash doesn't leave a trail, and things that don't leave trails usually belong to people who don't want to be found. I find people who don't want to be found tend to be found by me eventually, face-down somewhere, and I'd rather skip the middleman."

Something moved across his face then — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might have lived once, before it was demolished. "You're exactly what they said you were."

"*They* being whom?"

"Let's stay on task." He folded his hands in his lap. Stillness again. "I want to hire you to prove that a murder has not happened yet."

I waited for the punchline. It didn't come. The rain filled the silence instead, ticking against the window like a slow clock.

"A murder," I said carefully, "that hasn't happened yet."

"Yes."

"You want me to prove a negative about a future event. Do you understand what you're asking? That's not investigation, that's prophecy with paperwork."

"I understand precisely what I'm asking." He leaned forward a fraction, and for the first time something human flickered under all that pallor — urgency, maybe, or fear wearing urgency's coat. "On the night of the fourteenth — eleven days from now — a man will be found dead in Graywater Hollow. Drowned, but not by drowning. He will have ligature marks at the throat, post-mortem, made with something thin. Wire, most likely. And there will be water in his lungs that isn't quarry water."

The room got very small around me. Not because of the details themselves — details are my trade, I collect them like some people collect stamps — but because of how he delivered them. No hesitation. No checking of notes. He recited a corpse the way a priest recites a psalm: memorized, reverent, terrible.

"How do you know that?" I asked.

"Because everyone will believe I did it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have that you'll believe tonight." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. Drama I could handle. Calm men describing future corpses were another category entirely.

I looked at the envelope. I looked at him. Somewhere outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the town, and I thought about all the quiet deaths I'd catalogued in twenty-two years — heart attacks in armchairs, falls on icy steps, the slow polite failures of old age — and how in all that time, Graywater Hollow had never given me a murder. Not one. The town wore its innocence like a pressed shirt.

And here sat a stranger telling me the shirt was about to come unstitched.

"The victim," I said. "You said *a man*. Give me a name."

He gave it. I won't write it here yet — not until I understand why saying it out loud made the air change temperature — but I knew it. Everyone in Graywater Hollow would know it. It belonged to a family with deep roots and deeper silences.

"And you know the date, the method, the victim," I said, "but you can't tell me how you know."

"What I can tell you is that when it happens, the evidence will point to me. Completely. Convincingly. I will be the obvious suspect, and I will have no alibi anyone believes, because—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. "Because my circumstances are unusual."

"Unusual how?"

"In ways that would make you stop listening if I told you now."

I stood up and walked to the window. This is a habit of mine when I need my face to stop giving testimony. The street outside was empty, wet, lamplit. Somewhere a dog was complaining about the weather on behalf of the entire town.

Here's the truth about me, since we're being honest with each other: I am very good at my job, and being very good at your job in a town this small means being very invisible. Nobody invites the coroner to dinner. People see me coming and adjust their faces. I built that invisibility on purpose, brick by brick, and I guard it jealously. And every instinct I possess — instincts honed on two decades of the dead, who are the only truly honest witnesses — was telling me that the pale man in my office was a locked door with something screaming behind it.

Every instinct except one. The one that said: *he described wounds you'd find in your own report format. Ligature, post-mortem, secondary fluid analysis. That's not a confession. That's an autopsy delivered in advance.*

Only three kinds of people talk like that. Coroners. Killers. And people who've seen a coroner's report before — closely, recently, intimately.

"Mr. Vey," I said, turning back, "I'm going to ask you one question, and I want you to lie to me if you're going to lie, because I'll know either way. Have you seen a coroner's report on this before? On this specific death?"

For the first time, his stillness broke. Just his eyes — they went to the envelope on my desk, then to the window behind me, then back, and in that half-second I watched a man decide something with the weight of a man deciding whether to jump.

"No," he said. "But I've seen reports like it. All my life."

"All your life," I repeated. "And how long is that, Mr. Vey? Because I ran a thought while you were talking, and it's bothering me. Isandro Vey. I know every name in the parish registers back to the founding. I know every family, every branch, every bastard and black sheep. There is no Vey in Graywater Hollow. There never has been."

The silence stretched long enough that I could hear the rain changing its mind, softening toward mist.

"No," he agreed quietly. "There isn't."

"So either you're from somewhere else—"

"I'm not."

"—or you're lying about your name."

"I'm not lying about anything," he said, and rose, and buttoned his coat with those unnervingly still hands. "That's rather the point of all this. Keep the retainer or don't. But on the fourteenth, when they pull a body out of the water with wire marks on its throat, remember that I came to you first. Eleven days early. Asking for help."

He walked to the door, paused with his hand on the frame, and looked back at me. Up close, in the lamplight, I noticed for the first time that his eyes weren't just pale. They were gray. A particular gray. The gray of flooded quarry water.

"Prove it didn't happen, Ms. Halloran," he said. "Before it does."

Then he was gone into the mist, and the bell gave its tired little ring, and I stood alone in my office with an envelope full of money I hadn't touched and a name in my head I couldn't shake.

I should have refused him. Any sensible person would have. Instead I poured out the cold tea, sat down, pulled a fresh file from the drawer, and wrote at the top of the page, in my own careful, honest handwriting:

*Vey, Isandro — status unknown.*

Then, after a moment, because pride is a funny thing and curiosity is funnier:

*Victim: [name withheld pending verification]. Date: the 14th.*

Eleven days. I had eleven days to prove a murder hadn't happened yet.

What I didn't know — what I couldn't have known, standing there in my warm office while the town dissolved politely around me — was that the first body would surface in three.

* * *

It started, as these things do, with a records check.

I told myself it was due diligence. Client verification, nothing more. Every investigator worth their salt runs the name before taking the money, and I was worth my salt if nothing else. So the next morning, with the rain finally exhausted and the town wrung out and dripping, I sat down with the parish registers, the tax rolls, the census abstracts, and the county archives, and I looked for Isandro Vey.

Nothing.

Not *little* nothing. Not *moved-away-in-'94* nothing. Nothing-nothing. No birth record. No tax file. No school enrollment, no marriage license, no property deed, no fishing permit, no library card. In a town where the registrar once recorded the gender of a litter of kittens because Mrs. Aldous insisted they were dependents, Isandro Vey did not exist.

I sat back in my chair and regarded the blank space where a life should have been, and felt the first true cold of the season settle into the room.

A man who doesn't exist had walked into my office, paid me a fortune in untraceable cash, and described a corpse in perfect clinical detail.

And underneath the unease — I'll admit it, because honesty is the one luxury I allow myself — underneath all of it, something in me that had been asleep for years sat up and opened its eyes.

I closed the empty file. I put on my coat. And I went out into the wet morning to do the only sensible thing left.

I went to see the dead.
