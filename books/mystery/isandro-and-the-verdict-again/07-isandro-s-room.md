## 7. Isandro's Room

Odile Vane came down to breakfast that morning wearing a scarf the color of crushed plums and an expression I would come to know well over the following weeks: the look of a woman who has decided something and considers further discussion a form of rudeness.

"You'll want to see his room," she said, before I'd finished buttering my toast.

"Good morning to you too," I said.

"It is a good morning. Fog on the water, the gulls arguing about something. Pell says it means rain by evening, but Pell says that about every morning, and he's been right twice." She folded her hands atop the table with the ceremony of a judge preparing to sentence someone. "The room. Today. I've been thinking about it all night, and I've decided that if I wait until I feel ready, I will die first, and then you'll have to break in, and you'll do it badly."

"I've broken into rooms before."

"Not my brother's room you haven't." She pushed back her chair. "Come along. Before I lose my nerve, which at my age arrives and departs like a bus with no published schedule."

Sable appeared in the doorway with Odile's shawl — she seemed to materialize whenever Odile stood, the way nurses do, as though the profession installed a sixth sense — and draped it around her employer's shoulders without being asked. Odile tolerated this the way a cat tolerates being dried after rain: with dignity intact, resentment implied.

"The key," Sable said quietly.

It wasn't a question, exactly. It was more like a stone dropped into still water, and I watched the ripples cross Odile's face.

"In the drawer of my writing desk," Odile said. "Where it has always been."

Something passed between them then — brief, wordless, and not entirely comfortable. Sable's eyes went to me for half a second, and whatever she saw there made her mouth tighten before she smoothed it into professional blankness. Then she was gone, and Odile was already moving toward the stairs with her cane tapping out a rhythm like a metronome set to *determined*.

I followed. It occurred to me, somewhere around the third step, that I had just witnessed a small negotiation conducted entirely above my head, and that I had come out of it holding neither end of the conversation. This, I was learning, was simply what it felt like to work for the Vanes.

* * *

The east wing of Vane House had the particular silence of a place nobody goes. Not the silence of emptiness — emptiness echoes — but the silence of preservation, of dust lying down and staying down, of air that hasn't been asked to move in years. The wallpaper faded differently here, in long vertical bands where the sun came through gaps in the shutters, so the corridor looked striped, like something waiting to be gift-wrapped.

Odile stopped at the last door. It was painted white once; now it was the white of old bone, and there was a strip of yellowed tape across the jamb where someone, thirty years ago, had sealed it with the solemnity of a customs official.

She held out her hand. Sable, who had followed us at a distance of exactly six feet — I counted, because counting things keeps the nerves busy — placed a small iron key in her palm.

"You don't have to come in," Odile said to her.

"No," Sable agreed. "I don't."

And she stayed in the corridor, arms crossed, looking at the door as if it were a dog that had once bitten her. Which, in a way, I suppose it had.

The key turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung inward onto thirty years.

I want to describe it properly, because I have thought about that room every day since, and description is how I keep it from turning into something simpler than it was.

It was a boy's room. That was the first thing — not a dead man's room, not a museum, but a boy's room, caught mid-sentence. A bed with a quilt thrown back as though its owner had intended to return in five minutes. A desk chair pushed out at an angle. On the desk, a glass of water with a skin of dust on its surface so fine it looked like frosted glass, and beside it a paperback novel laid face-down, spine up, holding a boy's place in a story he never finished.

The curtains were half-drawn. Through the gap, the fog Odile had admired at breakfast pressed against the glass like a face.

"Nobody comes in," Odile said from the doorway. She hadn't crossed the threshold. "After the fire, Corliss wanted to pack everything away. Boxes, charity, the whole machinery of forgetting. I refused. I said we'd wait until Isandro came home to decide what belonged to him." Her voice did something complicated in the middle of the sentence. "He never came home. So we waited. And now it's been so long that waiting is all the room knows how to do."

I stepped inside. The floorboards complained under my weight, and I found myself apologizing to them, which tells you something about the state of my composure.

The clothes were in the wardrobe — school blazers, two of them, one with the cuffs let down and re-stitched by a hand that cared more about function than beauty. Sweaters folded with the particular neatness of a child who had been taught that things could be taken away. Shoes in a row, sized in ascending order like the rings of a tree, marking the years he'd grown. The largest pair had hardly been worn. Whatever night ended this room had arrived before the boy finished growing into his own feet.

On the walls: a map of the county with pins in it. A poster of a sailing race, curling at the corners. And pinned above the desk, at the height of a seated child rather than a standing one — as though he'd worked there, elbows down, for hours — a drawing.

I went to it the way you approach anything fragile, which is to say badly, with your breath held.

It was done in colored pencil on paper gone soft and gray at the edges. Five figures stood in a row on a green hill under a blue scribble of sky. They were drawn the way children draw people — stick limbs, dot eyes, smiles rendered as single confident arcs. But they weren't anonymous. Each figure had been given care. One wore a dress with actual patterned flowers on it. One had a hat. One was very tall and stood slightly apart, hands in pockets. One held the hand of the smallest figure, and the smallest figure was smiling widest of all.

Five figures. Four faces clear and cheerful.

The fifth face had been scratched out. Not erased — erased leaves a smudge, an ambiguity. This had been scratched, hard, with something pointed, over and over, until the paper had worn thin enough to see the wall through it. The body remained. The smile remained, curving up from nothing, attached to a head that was now a ragged hole.

I stood there for a long time.

"Do you know when he drew it?" I asked.

"He was nine. Maybe ten." Odile had come as far as the threshold and no farther. She gripped the doorframe with one hand and her cane with the other, and I understood suddenly that entering this room cost her something physical, some tax levied against her heart, and that she had chosen to pay it by proxy — sending me in as her eyes. "He drew everyone he loved. He was that sort of child. He'd give you a drawing of yourself and watch your face while you looked at it, and if you didn't look pleased enough he'd take it back and add something. A bird, usually. He believed drawings needed birds."

"And the fifth figure?"

For a moment I thought she wouldn't answer. The fog shifted outside the window, and the light in the room moved like something breathing.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the truth, Mr. Hartwell, and you may write it down as the only honest ignorance in this house. I found the drawing scratched out after the fire. I don't know which figure it was meant to be. I don't know who scratched it, or why, or whether it was done in anger or in grief or in fear. I've had thirty years to wonder, and wondering, unlike wine, does not improve."

"May I photograph it?"

"You may photograph everything. That's what you're for." She said it without sharpness — almost fondly, the way you'd describe a good dog's job. "But Mr. Hartwell. There's something else. On the desk. Under the blotter, or in the drawer, or wherever a boy hides things he doesn't want found. There's a letter. Half-finished. Corliss read it once, the week after the fire, and then she put it back and forbade anyone to touch it, and none of us ever spoke of it again." Her knuckles whitened on the cane. "I'm speaking of it now. Find it. Read it. I'm too much of a coward to read it myself, and too honest to pretend otherwise."

* * *

Finding a hidden letter in a boy's room turns out to be less detective work than empathy. Boys hide things where boys hide things, and the places are universal: under the mattress (nothing), inside the pillowcase (a marble collection, forty-one of them, each wrapped individually in tissue paper, which told me more about Isandro Vane than any document in the county archive), behind the loose baseboard (dust), and finally —

The desk. The top drawer stuck, and I worked it loose gently, hating myself for every sound the old wood made, as though the room might object. Inside: pencils worn to stubs, a tin of erasers, a compass rose carved into the wood by some previous occupant, and a false bottom that wasn't even false so much as lazy — a sheet of cardboard laid over a shallow space, with one corner lifted where a boy's fingers had worried it smooth.

The letter was there. Folded in thirds, addressed on the outside in a round, careful hand:

*For Odile. Only if something happens.*

My hands were not steady. I noted that with professional interest and continued anyway.

I unfolded it on the desk, using the blotter as a clean surface, and photographed it first — front, back, both sides of each fold — before reading a word. Habit. Also superstition. In my experience the two professions overlap more than either admits.

Then I read it.

*Dear Odie,*

*I am writing this because you said a person should say things out loud and I said that was embarrassing and you said embarrassment is the price of honesty and you're probably right, you're usually right, which is annoying.*

*Here is the thing I can't say out loud.*

*At night I hear him. Not ghosts, don't make the face, I don't believe in ghosts. I mean I hear him moving around downstairs when everyone else is asleep, and yesterday I found my blue sweater on the wrong hanger, and this morning someone ate the last of the jam and left the spoon in the sink and it wasn't me and it wasn't Pell because Pell licks his spoon clean like an animal.*

*Nobody believes me. Corliss says I'm dreaming. You believe me, don't you? You have to believe me because if you don't then I'm the only one who knows and that's worse than*

The letter stopped. Mid-word, mid-thought, the pen stroke trailing off as though the writer had been called away — or had heard something — or had simply run out of the particular courage that writing requires and told himself he'd finish tomorrow.

There was no tomorrow. Not for this letter, not in this hand, not in this room.

I sat down in the boy's chair — it creaked, and I flinched — and read it three more times. *I hear him.* A boy of nine or ten, hearing someone in the house at night. Someone eating jam, moving sweaters, existing in the margins of the household like a draft under a door. And the family explaining it away as dreams.

Thirty years later, a woman named Odile Vane was paying me triple my rate to prove her brother hadn't been murdered yet, on the strength of letters predicting it.

I am not a man who believes in coincidence. Coincidence is what we call patterns we haven't earned the right to see yet. But I will confess that sitting in that sealed room, with the half-written letter in front of me and the scratched-out drawing watching from the wall, I felt the floor of the case shift beneath me — not collapse, nothing so dramatic, just that settling motion a house makes at night when you realize it is older and stranger than you assumed.

Someone else had been in this house. Even then. Even before the fire.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited and stayed like an unpaid guest — someone else had been *in this room*. Recently. Because as I sat there letting my eyes adjust to the details, the way you do in any space, I began to notice what the dust was telling me.

Dust is honest. Dust doesn't rehearse. The dresser top wore its thirty years evenly, a uniform gray felt. The windowsill likewise. The books on the shelf were furred like moths. But the desk — the desk was different. The surface around the blotter showed faint clean tracks, swaths where the gray had been disturbed and resettled thinner. The drawer I'd opened had bright scratch-marks around its lip, newer than the compass rose carved inside. And on the seat of the chair, where I was sitting, the dust lay in a film interrupted by a shape — an impression, days or weeks old, of someone who had sat here before me.

Someone had searched this room. Carefully, recently, and put everything back almost perfectly.

Almost.

I photographed the letter again, and the drawing, and the dust, and the drawer, and the chair, working methodically while my mind ran ahead of my hands. When I finished, I replaced the letter beneath its cardboard, closed the drawer with the same gentle criminality I'd used opening it, and stood.

Odile was still in the doorway. She had watched me the entire time, and her face had gone very still in the way of faces that are holding themselves together through sheer administrative effort.

"Well?" she said.

"There's a letter," I said. "Half-finished. It breaks off mid-sentence."

"What does it say?"

I weighed it — the whole truth, the partial truth, the merciful truth — and chose, for the moment, the second option. "It says he trusted you. It says you were the only one who listened to him." I hesitated. "And it says that before the fire, he believed there was someone else in the house."

The color left her face in stages, like a tide going out. Behind her, in the corridor, I heard Sable shift her weight — a small sound, floorboard-soft, but in that silence it carried like a shot.

"That's impossible," Odile whispered. "The house was full. Family, staff, all of us under one roof. Who else could there possibly have—"

She stopped. I watched her stop, watched the sentence hit something inside her and turn around.

"Mr. Hartwell," she said, and her voice had changed, gone thin and careful, "the drawing. The fifth figure. Did you look closely at the others? At who was holding whose hand?"

"I did."

"Then tell me honestly. In your professional opinion." She gripped the doorframe. "When a child draws five figures and scratches out one face — is he scratching out the person he fears?"

I looked back at the drawing. At the four smiling faces and the ragged hole where the fifth should be, at the smallest figure smiling widest of all, hand-in-hand with someone whose identity I couldn't fix, and I thought about a boy hearing footsteps at night while the whole household swore the house was empty.

"Sometimes," I said carefully, "a child scratches out the face he's afraid of."

"Yes," Odile said. "And sometimes, Mr. Hartwell, he scratches out the face he's afraid *for*."

She turned and walked back down the corridor, cane tapping, spine straight, a small woman carrying thirty years of a question she'd never allowed herself to ask aloud. And as I followed her out and pulled the door shut — the lock clicking home like a period at the end of a sentence — I noticed, fresh in the dust of the hallway floor, what I should have noticed coming in.

Footprints. Small ones, narrow-soled, leading away from Isandro's door toward the east stairs.

They were not thirty years old.
