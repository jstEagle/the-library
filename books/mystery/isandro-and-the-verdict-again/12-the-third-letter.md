## 12. The Third Letter

The third letter arrived on a Tuesday, which I mention only because Tuesdays had, by that point in my acquaintance with the Vane family, acquired a certain ominous texture. Nothing good seemed to happen at Vane House on a Tuesday. The boiler chose Tuesdays to groan like a man being lowered into a grave. Pell chose Tuesdays to drink his coffee standing in the doorway, staring at the treeline as though the trees owed him money.

And now, on a Tuesday, the postman handed me a cream-colored envelope as I sat in Odile's room, pretending to read a trust document while actually watching her sleep.

"From the county?" I asked, though I already knew. I had learned the handwriting by then — the tall, narrow letters, the peculiar way the writer crossed his t's with a single impatient slash, like a man cutting a wire.

The postman shrugged and left. I turned the envelope over. No return address. Postmarked from the village, which meant the writer had walked it to the box himself, which meant the writer wanted me to know he was close, which meant — I was beginning to understand — everything about these letters was a message, including the parts that pretended to be about something else.

Odile stirred. "Is it here?"

"You were asleep."

"I was resting my eyes. There's a difference, Mr. Hartwell, and the difference is that I heard the door." She pushed herself up against her pillows with the grim competence of a woman who had been dying slowly enough to get very good at it. "Read it."

I slit the envelope. Inside, one page, same cream stock, same narrow hand.

*The third thing will happen at the estate office. Tomorrow night, between eleven and one. The guardianship file will be taken. Tell the lawyer not to bother standing watch — he will be given a reason to be elsewhere, and he will take it, because he is the kind of man who believes his cleverness is a shield. It is not. It is a door left open.*

*After it happens, look down. The ground remembers what hands forget.*

*— I.V.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading had produced a sensation in my chest I didn't care for, something between a cold draft and a hand.

"He knows about me," I said. "He's writing about me in the third person, to me."

"He's writing about you in the third person *because* it's to you," Odile said. "That's the whole point. He wants you to know he sees you. It's a courtesy, in his way. Rude people pretend they haven't noticed you. My brother was always polite." She said it flatly, without affection, the way you'd say *my brother was always tall.*

"He's not your brother."

"He signs himself so. In this family, Mr. Hartwell, we have learned to be flexible about what people sign themselves." She held out her hand for the letter, and I gave it to her, and she read it with her lips moving slightly, which she would have hated to know. "The ground remembers what hands forget," she repeated. "That's a riddle."

"It's an instruction. He's telling me what to look for after the break-in."

"Then you'll look for it."

"I'm also told not to stand watch. Which, coming from a man who has thus far been right about everything, is either excellent advice or the oldest trick in the book."

Odile smiled. It was not, I noted, a comforting smile. "Which do you think it is?"

"I think," I said, "that I'm going to stand watch, and I'm going to be elsewhere, and I'm going to try to be both at once, which is the sort of thing that gets lawyers disbarred and detectives killed."

"Then you're the right man for it," she said, and closed her eyes, and rested them, and heard everything.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I do instead of worrying, which is paperwork. I had, by then, assembled a modest file on the Vane matter: copies of the fire report, my notes on the impossible guardianship timeline, the first two letters, and a growing list of questions I had asked aloud in empty rooms, which is either madness or method depending on who's asking.

The estate office was a small brick building between the house and the carriage barn, built in some optimistic decade when the Vanes had still believed in the future of the estate. It held the household ledgers, Corliss's correspondence, the safe — an old floor model, the kind with a dial you had to be gentle with — and, in a locked cabinet, the guardianship file I had photographed two weeks earlier and then, foolishly, returned to its place. I had told myself I returned it because removing documents from a client's property without cause was legally dubious. The truth was I had returned it because I wanted to see who would come for it.

That is the kind of cleverness the third letter had already diagnosed. It is not a shield. It is a door left open. I have since had occasion to agree.

At nine o'clock I presented myself in the kitchen, where Sable was washing dishes and Pell was drinking his coffee standing in the doorway, staring at the treeline.

"I'll be in the office tonight," I said. "Working."

"Working," Pell repeated, without turning around.

"Late. On the case. Alone."

"Alone," said Sable, to her dishwater.

"Nobody should come down to the office tonight. For any reason. If you hear anything, stay in the house."

"Nothing ever happens here," Pell said, which from Pell had the weight of a weather report, and was about as reassuring.

I took my position at eleven. I will not pretend it was comfortable. The estate office had one lamp, one chair, and a draft that came up through the floorboards with the smell of a century of cold winters. I turned the lamp low, sat behind the door rather than in front of it — a small concession to the letter's warning — and waited, with a notebook on my knee and my coat still on, in case I needed to look like a man who had merely fallen asleep at his work.

Between eleven and one. I watched my watch eat the minutes.

At eleven-forty, the dogs started.

I should explain the dogs. Vane House kept two of them, ancient and ceremonial, named Biscuit and something I never learned because everyone just called him *the other one*. They had not barked at anything in living memory, including the postman, including thunder, including, on one occasion, a deer that walked through the rose garden with the confidence of a visiting duke. Corliss fed them from the table and they had, in consequence, lost all professional ambition.

They barked from the direction of the house.

It was, I want to be clear, a good bark. Alarmed, urgent, the bark of dogs who had remembered, after fifteen years, what dogs were for. And it was coming from the house — from Odile's wing — and whatever else I was, whatever else I had become in that house, I was not a man who could sit in a drafty office and listen to dogs announce trouble at a dying woman's door.

I was at the house in under three minutes. I will spare you the details of the crossing except to note that I knocked over a wheelbarrow, swore at it, and apologized to it on the way back, which tells you most of what you need to know about my character.

Odile's door stood open. She was sitting up in bed, pale but composed, and Sable was beside her with a lamp, and the dogs were in the hallway losing their minds at absolutely nothing.

"What is it?" I demanded. "What happened?"

"A noise," Odile said. "Downstairs, I thought. Sable heard it too."

"I heard something," Sable said. Her voice was steady but her hands weren't; the lamp trembled in a small, tight circle. "Maybe a door."

I stood there in the hallway, heart going like a drum in a parade, and the thought arrived with the slow, sick clarity of ice cracking underfoot.

*He will be given a reason to be elsewhere, and he will take it.*

The dogs had never barked at anything in their lives. The dogs had been barking at nothing. Or rather — the dogs had been barking at a sound someone had made for the purpose of being barked at, and the someone had counted on the dogs' fifteen years of laziness to make the bark extraordinary, and had counted on me to be exactly the kind of man who would run toward a dying woman's door at the first sign of trouble.

I had been managed. I had been managed the way you manage a large, well-meaning animal, with a stick and a whistle, and I had trotted exactly where I was meant to trot.

"Gentlemen," I said to the dogs, "you are either the finest actresses in England or you were paid in sausages," and I ran.

* * *

The estate office lamp was still burning low. The door was still shut. For one foolish half-second I thought I'd imagined the whole thing, that I was a man who had been spooked by a barking dog and a guilty conscience.

Then I smelled the cold.

The window at the back of the office — the one behind the filing cabinet, the one I had checked at eleven and found latched — stood open six inches, and the November air was pouring through it like a confession. The cabinet stood open too. And the floor safe's dial hung loose, turned to the combination, which someone had either known or watched being known.

The guardianship file was gone.

I stood in the middle of the room and did what I always do when I've been made a fool of, which is to say I took out my notebook and started working, because humiliation is fuel if you refine it properly.

The window told its story readily enough. The paint on the sill was scored where the sash had been pried — not forced, exactly, but persuaded, by someone with a thin blade and patience. The cabinet had been opened with a key or a skill; there was no damage. The safe was the interesting part. The combination to that safe was known, to my certain knowledge, by exactly three people: Corliss, Pell, and Odile. I had never been told it. I had photographed the file's contents on the day Odile opened the safe for me, and I remembered — I made a note of this with some bitterness — that she had stood slightly between me and the dial the whole time.

Either one of the three had opened it, or one of the three had talked, or — and this was the possibility I turned over last, like the last card in a hand — someone had watched the safe being opened at some point in the past thirty years, and had waited, and had chosen tonight.

I crouched. The third letter had said: *look down. The ground remembers what hands forget.*

The floorboards near the window held the ghost of mud — a half-moon of it, dried now, the print of a boot with a worn outer heel. Not Pell's boots; I had seen Pell's boots, and they were the boots of a man who walked the same ground every day of his life, worn dead center. This print was worn on the outside edge, the print of a man who walked on roads and gravel and stood around in lobbies. A town boot. A visitor's boot.

I followed the mud ghosts backward across the floor, out the window's reach, along the gravel path — and there, just where the path met the drive, half-crushed into the stones, I found it.

A cufflink.

Gold, or gold-colored — I didn't touch it at first, just crouched over it the way you'd crouch over a sleeping snake. Plain-faced, heavy-looking, and engraved. Two initials, cut deep in an old-fashioned script:

**R.M.**

I sat back on my heels in the gravel and ran the household through my head like a ledger. Corliss Vane. Pell — whose first name was Ambrose, and whose surname I had never learned but which was certainly not anything beginning with M, because I had read the payroll records. Sable — a single name, a nurse's name, chosen or given. Odile Vane. Isandro Vane, thirty years gone.

No R. No M. Not in this house, not in the fire report, not in the guardianship papers, not in the trust documents, not in the death certificate Corliss had signed with her own hand. I had spent three weeks inside the paperwork of the Vane family, and the letters R and M had never once appeared in it.

Which meant the man who had opened a window, opened a cabinet, opened a safe, taken a file, whistled up two lazy dogs, and walked away through my own personal blind spot was a stranger. A professional, or near enough — the work had been clean, the timing surgical, the dogs a touch of genuine artistry. But a stranger. And a stranger, in this affair, was a new species entirely. Until tonight, every hand I had found in the Vane matter belonged to someone inside the house. The letters. The alibis. The impossible paperwork. All of it, family.

This was the first fingerprint from outside the walls.

I wrapped the cufflink in my handkerchief, because I am a lawyer before I am anything else and evidence is evidence, and I stood there in the cold with the drive stretching away into the dark toward the village, and I asked myself the question the letter had been waiting for me to ask:

*Who does a hidden man hire?*

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. The letters came from inside — the handwriting, the knowledge, the intimacy of the grievances. But the letters could not open windows. Letters cannot pry a sash or turn a dial or throw a stick for two ancient dogs. Somewhere between the man who wrote *I.V.* at the bottom of cream-colored pages and the man with the worn outer heel, there was a transaction. Money. Instructions. A stranger brought inside just far enough to do a job.

And the job was not the file. I understood that as I stood there, and the understanding went through me like the draft through the open window. A man who wanted the guardianship file could have taken it any night for thirty years. The file had sat in that safe like a bone in a dog's yard, ignored. No — the file was a message. Taking it tonight, on the night the letter named, was the writer proving something to me. That the letters were schedules, not predictions. That when he said a thing would happen, the thing happened, and on time, and with a stranger's boots on the ground to do it.

He wasn't threatening the Vane family anymore.

He was demonstrating.

I looked down the drive, at the lamps of the village somewhere beyond the black line of the trees, and I thought about the phrase Odile had used in her flat, unloving voice: *in this family we have learned to be flexible about what people sign themselves.* Two boys, I had begun to suspect. One name. And now, perhaps, a third man — hired, paid, initials R.M. — who knew enough to be dangerous and might not know enough to be safe.

Behind me, the dogs had gone quiet. Ahead of me, somewhere in the dark, a man with a town boot and a stranger's name was carrying thirty years of buried paperwork toward whatever he'd been paid to do with it.

I put the cufflink in my breast pocket, over the heart, where I keep the things that keep me up at night, and I went back toward the house — where Sable was standing at the kitchen door with the lamp, watching me come, and where, for the first time since I'd met her, she looked less like a woman who had seen something terrible and more like a woman who was waiting to see whether I had.

"Mr. Hartwell," she said quietly. "You look like a man who found what he was looking for."

"No," I said. "I look like a man who found what somebody *left* for him. There's a difference, Sable, and the difference is going to keep me up tonight."

She held the lamp a little higher, and the light caught her face, and I saw — I am nearly sure I saw — the flicker of a woman deciding something.

"Then come inside," she said, "and I'll tell you about the winter Isandro came. Before you find out the wrong way."
