## 5. Twelve Years of Polite Silence

August Yarrow had a system for the morning after the town lost its mind.

First: open the shop at seven, as always, because routine was the only religion he'd kept. Second: make coffee strong enough to strip varnish. Third: pretend that nothing had happened in the cemetery yesterday, even though half the county was calling to ask if he'd heard, and the other half was already standing outside his window pointing at the historical society like it was a wound they couldn't stop touching.

He'd heard. Of course he'd heard. He'd been standing three feet from the hole when Tomás pried the lid and the light fell into an empty box.

The phone started at 7:40. The *Harrow's Gap Courier* wanted a quote. A man from the regional paper wanted directions to the Verne plot. Someone who identified herself only as "a concerned citizen" asked whether August believed in resurrection, which he answered with "we're closed on Sundays" before hanging up on a Tuesday.

By nine he'd retreated behind the counter with the day's arrivals — two boxes of donated paperbacks from the estate of old Mrs. Calloway, God rest her hoarding soul — and began the work that had held him together for twelve years: sorting, cataloging, shelving. Alphabetical order. Dewey decimal where it mattered, alphabetical where it didn't. A universe you could put your hands on. Every book in its place, every place known, nothing missing except the things he'd made sure stayed missing.

The bell over the door rang at 9:15.

"We're not doing interviews," he said without looking up.

"Good thing I'm not here to interview you."

He knew the voice before he knew the face, which was backwards and wrong, because he'd have known that face anywhere — it was Ruth's face, younger, rebuilt around someone else's jaw. Nadia Okafor stood in the doorway of Yarrow's Books with a canvas bag over one shoulder and a press lanyard tucked into her coat pocket, twenty-nine years old and wearing her mother's frown like an inheritance.

She'd cut her hair since the funeral. That was the detail his brain grabbed first, the small stupid one, because the large ones were too large. She'd been fourteen then. She was a woman now, and she was looking at him the way people look at a house they used to live in.

"Hello, August."

"Nadia." His hands did what his hands always did now; the tremor ran through them and he pressed them flat against the counter until it stopped. "I heard you were in town."

"You heard I was in town." She came forward slowly, reading the spines as if the shop might testify against her. "From whom?"

"The grapevine has excellent coverage in Harrow's Gap."

"Mm." She stopped at the poetry section — his poetry section, alphabetized within an inch of its life — and pulled out a slim volume, turned it over, put it back exactly right. She'd been raised by Ruth Okafor; she noticed everything. "You look good," she said, which was a lie delivered kindly. "Older."

"That's generally how it works."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you answer a real question with a technicality so we never have to be in the same conversation." She set her bag on the counter, uninvited, and perched on the stool customers weren't allowed to use. "Twelve years, August. Twelve years of Christmas cards that say 'Wishing you well' and nothing else. You know my mother is dead, right? You were there. It's not a rumor you need to verify annually."

"I know your mother is dead."

"Then say something about it."

The coffee maker gurgled in the back room like it was trying to fill the silence for him. August took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirttail, put them back on. Buying time with ritual. "You're here about the coffin," he said.

"I'm here about the coffin." Her eyes didn't move from his face. "Among other things."

*There it is.* He'd spent the night telling himself she wouldn't come, or that if she came it would be for the story — the empty grave of Elias Verne was a gift to any journalist, a hundred-year-old mystery served up with dirt still on it. But Nadia had never once come to Harrow's Gap in twelve years, not for the anniversary, not for the memorial, not for the plaque they put up with Ruth's name spelled wrong for six months before anyone corrected it. She came when there was a reason. And the reason standing between them had a shape he recognized, because he'd built it himself: a case file in a drawer, a final page torn away, a daughter who had been told *accidental fire, structural collapse, smoke inhalation* by men who'd been told to say it.

"There's coffee," he said. "If you want it."

"I want the interview."

"Then you'll want the mayor. Diana Verne-Cole loves a microphone. She practically married one."

"I've got the mayor. I've got the gravedigger's cousin, the church archivist, and a woman at the diner who claims Elias Verne's ghost bought her a lottery ticket." Nadia leaned in. "What I don't have is the one person in this town who knows more about local records than the historical society, and who happened to be standing at the front of the crowd yesterday looking like he'd seen a ghost himself. Not Verne's ghost. Something else."

"I was surprised," August said. "Anyone would be."

"Sure." She let the word sit there, unconvinced, and looked around the shop again — slower this time, deliberate. He watched her gaze travel the shelves and felt each pass like a searchlight. Poetry. Local history. The locked cabinet where he kept the expensive first editions, which also contained, third shelf from the bottom, behind a false row of sermons, a slim ledger that fit inside a coffin with room left over for a brass key.

Don't look at the cabinet. Don't look at the cabinet.

She looked at the cabinet. "Nice lock," she said.

"It keeps the valuable things valuable."

"Does it?" She smiled, and it was Ruth's smile, sharp at one corner. "Here's my problem, August. I've been a reporter for eight years, and I know what a source looks like when they're holding something back. They get tidy. They answer questions with questions. They offer you coffee." She tapped the counter twice. "You offered me coffee eleven seconds into this conversation. You hate offering people anything."

"I'm trying to be hospitable."

"You're trying to be a wall." She slid off the stool and shouldered her bag. "Fine. I'll do this the long way. I'll knock on every door in this town, and eventually I'll end up back here, because I always end up back here, and next time I won't be polite about it."

"Nadia—"

"Twelve years of polite silence," she said from the doorway, hand on the bell. "That's what you and I have had. Polite silence. My mother deserved better than polite silence from both of us." The bell rang. The door shut. She was gone down the sidewalk toward the courthouse, where the case files lived, where Ruth's file lived, minus its last page, and August stood behind his counter with his heart going like a trapped bird and understood, with total clarity, that the twelve quiet years were over.

* * *

Nadia had learned interrogation from her mother without either of them admitting it. Ruth Okafor had been the kind of cop who asked questions in a warm voice and then waited, patient as erosion, until the truth wore through. Nadia had inherited the patience and none of the warmth, or maybe the warmth was just buried deeper, under eight years of editors telling her to get the quote or lose the job.

Her first stop was the courthouse annex, where the county kept records nobody digitized because digitizing would mean somebody could read them. The clerk, a soft-spoken man named Pell who owed her a favor from a story two towns over, let her into the archive room with a warning: "Fire section's a mess. Half those boxes got water damage when the sprinklers went off in '13."

"In '13?"

"Twelve years ago. When the Okafor—" He caught himself. His ears went red. "When the fire happened. At the old police annex."

Of course. Of course the records had burned or soaked in the same disaster that killed her mother, because Harrow's Gap was a town that tidied up after itself. She found the box anyway. RUTH OKAFOR — CASE FILES — 2011–2013, and inside, folders in her mother's blocky handwriting, and inside those, the file on the Verne ledger inquiry — because that was what her mother had been working on when she died, everyone admitted that much, though no one could ever quite produce the paperwork.

The folder was thin. Too thin. Nadia spread the pages across the table: interview notes, a photograph of a bookshop interior, a memo requesting cipher analysis of an unidentified ledger recovered from — and there the page ended, mid-sentence, ragged at the edge. Torn. Not water-damaged. Torn by a hand.

She sat with the ragged edge for a long time.

Her mother had died carrying something. Everyone said so in the way people say things without saying them — the sheriff who retired rather than close the case, the deputy who'd had too many drinks at the VFW and told her, when she was nineteen, that "your mama found something she shouldn't have, sweetheart, and that's all I'll ever say." Nadia had spent twelve years building a career on other people's buried secrets while leaving her own town's biggest one in the ground, because coming home meant admitting the homecoming was overdue.

Then yesterday a hundred-year-old coffin opened in front of four hundred people and was empty, and Nadia had felt something turn over in her chest that wasn't grief and wasn't excitement but was adjacent to both: *the ground here doesn't hold what it says it holds.*

And August Yarrow — her mother's partner, her mother's friend, the man who sent Christmas cards like a man paying rent on guilt — had gone white as bone at the graveside. She'd seen it from thirty feet. She'd been watching him the whole service, because watching August was the closest thing she had to watching her mother's last case unfold.

She photographed the torn page, repacked the folder, and went to find the one witness the town hadn't thought to warn yet.

* * *

Tomás Ruiz lived in a trailer behind his brother's feed store, and he answered the door already holding a beer, though it was barely noon. He looked at her press lanyard and his face did something complicated.

"You're the Okafor girl."

"Ruth Okafor's daughter. Can I ask you about yesterday?"

"No." He started to close the door.

"Mr. Ruiz." She put her hand flat on it — not pushing, just present. "I know you were paid to keep quiet about something. I don't know what yet. But the person paying you works for a woman who just buried an empty coffin in front of the entire county, and whatever you're keeping quiet about is going to come out sideways, and when it does, it'll be better for you if it comes from you."

Tomás looked at her hand. Then past her, at the road, at the hills, at whatever a man looks at when he's deciding how much trouble he's already in. "Your mother," he said slowly, "was digging into the same thing. Twelve years back. She came out here and asked me questions, just like you."

Nadia's pulse ticked up. "What did you tell her?"

"Nothing. I didn't know anything then." He drained the beer in one motion, like medicine. "I know things now. And I'll tell you this much for free, because your mama was decent to me when nobody in this town was: whatever August Yarrow told you, double it. Whatever he didn't tell you — that's the real story."

"What didn't he tell me?"

Tomás almost smiled. Almost. "Ask him about the ledger. Ask him why he keeps it in the poetry section." He stepped back into the dimness of the trailer. "And Okafor girl — ask him about the night of the fire. Watch his hands when you do. Man's hands will tell you what his mouth won't."

The door closed. Nadia stood on the gravel a moment, heart loud, the afternoon sun flat and white on the feed store roof.

The poetry section. She'd stood in it that morning. She'd touched those spines.

She got in her car and drove back toward Main Street, toward the shop with the green awning, rehearsing questions in her mother's voice — *warm voice, patient, wait for the truth to wear through* — and telling herself the tightness in her throat was just the story, just the job, just a reporter closing in on a lead.

It wasn't. It was a daughter, twelve years late, finally knocking on the door her mother had died behind.
