## 17. What August Did That Night

The memory did not come back all at once. It came the way water comes through a failing roof—one drop at a time for years, and then, without warning, the whole ceiling gives.

August was standing in the poetry section of his own shop at eleven minutes past midnight, holding a cup of tea he had forgotten to drink, when it happened. He'd come down to check on the ledger one more time. He'd been checking on it every night since Tomás stopped coming to work, since the quarry, since the scrap of paper in Tomás's dead hands. He told himself he was guarding it. The truth was uglier: he was waiting for it to accuse him.

It had been accusing him all along. He just hadn't been able to read the language.

He set the ledger on the counter under the lamp, opened it to the last entry—the entry dated the night Ruth died—and this time, instead of looking away, he looked.

And the ceiling gave.

* * *

Twelve years ago, in October, Ruth Hollis-Yarrow had come to the bookshop after closing, the way she did every Thursday, and she had not been herself.

August remembered that much now—remembered it with terrible clarity, the way you remember the last ordinary thing before an extraordinary one. She'd stood where he was standing now, in fact, by the poetry shelves, and she'd been wet from the rain and out of breath, which was unlike her. Ruth climbed stairs like they were personal insults; she never ran anywhere unless something was wrong.

"August." She said his name the way she said *objection*, once, in a courtroom, and made a whole room go quiet. "I need you to listen to me, and I need you not to ask questions until I'm done."

"I'm listening."

She put a book on the counter between them. It was slim, cloth-bound, unmarked except for a pressed violet someone had glued inside the front cover a century ago. It looked like nothing. It looked like a hymnal or a diary or a donation nobody wanted.

"This came out of the Verne estate," she said. "The historical society was going to discard it. Diana signed off on the disposal list herself—I checked. Nobody knows what it is. Nobody but me, and now you."

"What is it?"

"That's your first question." But she answered it anyway, because Ruth always answered, because silence was a tool she saved for people who deserved it. "It's a ledger. A double-entry book. Debits on one side, credits on the other. Except the debits aren't money."

She opened it to a page near the middle and turned it toward him, and August saw columns of small, patient handwriting in a cipher he recognized without understanding—recognized because he'd seen it once before, years earlier, in a first edition of Elias Verne's sermons that had passed through his hands and gone to a collector in Spokane.

"It's debts," Ruth said. "Favors. Names. Going back to 1912 and running forward to—" her finger stopped on the last written line "—last month. August, there are entries from last month. This book is still being kept."

"By who?"

"That's my second question, and I don't have an answer yet. But I know what's in it. Land transfers that never happened. Payments to families whose names are on half the buildings in this town. And here—" she tapped a line so hard her nail clicked against the paper "—here's a payment dated three days after Elias Verne supposedly died. A payment authorized by him. In his hand. You don't authorize payments when you're dead, August. Not even in Harrow's Gap."

The rain had been loud on the old roof that night. He remembered that too—the particular drumming of it, the way the gutters over the poetry section always choked and overflowed. He remembered thinking, absurdly, that he should clear the gutters on Sunday.

"Ruth," he said carefully, "what are you going to do?"

"I'm going to finish decoding it. There's a key structure based on the sermons—it's elegant, actually, whoever built it knew their work—and then I'm taking it to the sheriff, and if the sheriff is one of the names in the book, I'm taking it to the state attorney general, and if *he's* in the book, I'll take it to a newspaper. I will take it to the moon if I have to." She closed the ledger and put both hands flat on top of it, and looked at him, and what he saw in her face then—what he had spent twelve years refusing to see—was fear. Not for herself. "But I can't keep it at the house. Nadia's there. If anyone comes looking—"

"They won't come looking."

"August." His name again, that courtroom voice, but softer now. "I found a page torn out of my mother's case file last week. My *mother's*. The file on the fire in 1912. Someone has been in the county archive, and someone does not want this history read. I need you to hide this book somewhere no one would ever think to look, and I need you to not tell me where, and I need you to promise me—if anything happens to me—you'll finish it. You'll decode it and you'll publish it. Every name. Even the ugly ones. *Especially* the ugly ones."

"Why would anything happen to you?"

"Because I asked that question out loud in a room with four people in it, and one of them left before I finished my coffee."

He should have taken her seriously. God, he should have taken her seriously. Instead he'd done what he always did with Ruth when she frightened him—he'd reached for order, for procedure, for the comfort of things that could be alphabetized.

"Fine," he'd said. "Give me the book. I'll put it somewhere safe. And you're going to go home, and you're going to eat dinner with your daughter, and on Monday we're going to sit down together and work the cipher like two reasonable adults, and in six months we'll publish a very boring article about municipal accounting fraud that nobody reads."

She'd laughed. He remembered the laugh. It was the thing that undid him later, more than the fire, more than the smoke—that she had laughed, that she had trusted him enough to laugh, that the last sound she ever made in his presence was trust.

"You're a coward about feelings and a lion about filing," she'd said. "It's why I married you." Then she'd kissed his cheek, rain-damp and cold, and gone out into the dark, and the bell above the door had rung once behind her, and August Yarrow had taken the ledger of Harrow's Gap and hidden it in the poetry section, spine-in, between Bishop and Blake, where no one in the history of the town had ever voluntarily looked.

* * *

The fire started nine days later.

He remembered the phone call now—not as a memory exactly, but as a wound reopening, the scar tissue peeling back to show him what was underneath. It was Nadia calling, fourteen years old, her voice stripped down to its wires: *the house, Dad, the house is on fire, Mom went back in.*

He drove. He didn't remember driving, but he must have, because he arrived, and arriving was the part that came back to him in full color, in smell, in heat.

The Hollis house—the house Ruth had grown up in, the house she'd gotten in the divorce, the house with Nadia's drawings taped inside every closet door—was burning from the back forward. Later, the fire marshal would say electrical, would say old wiring, would say these things happen. August had accepted that. August had needed to accept that, the way a drowning man needs air.

But standing in the street that night, watching the windows go orange, he had known. Not suspected—*known*, the way you know a chord is wrong. Ruth had called him nine days ago with a book full of living names and a fear she couldn't hide, and now her house was burning from the back, where the wiring was newest.

He got to the porch and the heat pushed him back like a hand on his chest. Firefighters were shouting. Neighbors were holding Nadia, who was fighting them, fourteen years old and feral with terror, screaming her mother's name into the smoke.

And then August saw her.

Through the kitchen window—the window over the sink, the window where she drank her coffee every morning while doing the crossword in pen—Ruth was there. On her knees. Moving. Alive. Her mouth open, and though he could hear nothing through the glass and the roar, he knew what she was saying, because he could read her lips the way he could read a spine label at forty feet.

*August.*

Not *help*. Not *Nadia*. His name. Because Ruth Hollis-Yarrow, who trusted almost no one, who had handed him a book of secrets nine days earlier and laughed and kissed his cheek, was looking for him in a burning building. Calling for the one person she believed would come.

He tried the door. The handle was hot enough to brand. He tried again anyway, and the skin of his palm stuck and tore, and he carried that scar for twelve years and told everyone who asked that it was from a box cutter in the stockroom.

A firefighter caught him around the chest and hauled him backward off the porch, and August fought him, actually fought him, a fifty-one-year-old bookseller swinging at a man in turnout gear, and lost, because he was always going to lose, because some part of him—

Some part of him.

Here was the thing the ceiling gave way to reveal. Here was the drop of water that had been falling for twelve years, the one he'd flinched from every time:

In the moment the firefighter's arms closed around him, in the moment the choice was made for him by force and physics and the failure of his own body against a stronger man—in that exact moment, August Yarrow had felt relief.

Relief. A bright, cold, unmistakable current of it, running through the horror like a wire through a wall. *Someone else is deciding. Someone else is holding me back. I don't have to go in. I can't go in. It isn't my fault.*

And beneath the relief, quieter, older, speaking in the voice of a man who had spent his whole life curating shelves because every other kind of courage was beyond him, a thought so small and so monstrous that it took twelve years to surface:

*She gave you the book. The book is safe. The book is in the poetry section, spine-in, between Bishop and Blake. Whatever happens in that house, the book survives. She would want the book to survive. She told you so herself.*

He had stood in the street and watched the kitchen window go white, and he had held the firefighter's arm not to break free but because breaking free required a decision, and August Yarrow had spent his entire life arranging his days so that no decision larger than Dewey decimal placement was ever required of him.

They pulled Ruth out at 11:47 p.m. She was already gone. Smoke inhalation, the report said. She had been found in the hallway near the front door, which meant she had been trying to get out, which meant she had been alive and moving and *calling his name* when he stood in the yard calculating whether the book was safer than she was.

Nadia did not speak to him at the funeral. She spoke to him once afterward, at the reception in the church basement, standing by the folding table of casseroles, and what she said was: "You weren't even burned." Not an accusation. Worse—an observation. A fourteen-year-old cataloguing a fact about the world the way her mother would have, the way Ruth taught her to: *note the anomaly, pursue the anomaly.* He hadn't been burned. He hadn't been inside. He had a bandaged palm and a story about a box cutter and a face that could not meet hers, and a child had looked at all of it and filed it away, and he had spent twelve years praying she'd never pull the file.

Then came the part he had buried deepest, deeper than the relief, deeper than the window, because it was the part that made everything else unforgivable.

Three days after the funeral, a man came to the shop. August remembered him perfectly now: gray coat, gray face, a voice like a form being filled out. He said he represented parties interested in the historical record of Harrow's Gap. He said there had been a fire. He said that certain materials were best preserved by being surrendered. He said the word *ledger*.

And August—who had watched the woman he loved die rather than choose her over a book—had looked this man in the eye and said: "I don't know what you're talking about."

He had lied. Easily. Fluently. The lie had cost him nothing, and that was the discovery that broke him open on the floor of his own memory: the lie had cost him *nothing*, because some chamber of his heart had already been sealed off, already declared itself a private archive, already stamped the truth RESTRICTED and shelved it where no one would look. He had protected the ledger from the gray-coat man the way he had failed to protect Ruth from the fire—passively, structurally, by being exactly the kind of man who keeps things instead of using them.

The gray-coat man had studied him for a long moment. Then he'd said, "Mrs. Verne-Cole sends her regards," and left a card August had never thrown away, tucked into the same slim gap in the poetry section where the ledger lived, because August Yarrow kept evidence of his own cowardice the way other men kept trophies of their courage.

Diana Verne-Cole had known about the ledger twelve years ago. Diana Verne-Cole had sent a man to his shop three days after Ruth's funeral. And August had hidden the book, and hidden the visit, and hidden the relief, and hidden the window, and hidden the scar, and hidden and hidden and hidden, until the hiding became a life, until the archive became a man, until there was nothing left of August Yarrow but a shelf with his name misfiled at the bottom of it.

* * *

He came back to himself on the floor of the poetry section, kneeling, the lamp still burning, the ledger open in front of him to the last entry. Dated the night Ruth died. He had assumed for twelve years that the date was coincidence—a keeper of the book noting some routine debt on an ordinary night.

Now he read the entry properly, with new eyes, with Ruth's eyes, and the letters resolved the way they always did once you stopped fighting the cipher: a debit, entered in a steady hand, for *services rendered, residence of R.H., October —, extinguished.*

Extinguished. Like a candle. Like a line item.

The book had recorded her death the night it happened. Which meant the book's keeper had known. Which meant the keeper had been close enough to know. Which meant the gray-coat man's report had gone back up the chain within hours, and someone had opened the ledger and entered the debt in the same neat hand that had been balancing Harrow's Gap's accounts since 1912.

Ruth wasn't a victim of the fire. Ruth was an entry in it.

August knelt on the cold floorboards of the shop he had built as a fortress of smallness, and he did not cry, though his whole body shook with the effort of not crying, because crying was release and release was not what tonight required. What tonight required was the thing he had fled from in a burning yard twelve years ago: a decision.

He stood up. His knees cracked. He wiped his face with the sleeve that smelled of dust and old glue, and he looked at the ledger lying open on the counter like a witness finally willing to talk.

"Okay, Ruth," he said aloud, to the empty shop, to the shelves, to the woman in the window. "You told me to finish it. Every name. Especially the ugly ones."

He got a legal pad and a pen, and he sat down at the counter, and he began to write—not the cipher this time, but the truth, in plain English, starting with the words *My name is August Yarrow, and twelve years ago I let Ruth Hollis die because I was afraid.*

He wrote until the sky outside the front windows went from black to the color of wood ash. And when he finally looked up, stiff-necked, hollowed out, lighter than he had felt in twelve years and heavier than he had ever felt in his life, he saw a figure standing across the street in the gray dawn light, motionless, watching the shop.

Watching him.

Even at that distance, even in that light, he knew the silhouette. He'd have known it in the dark, in the smoke, in his sleep.

Diana Verne-Cole had been standing outside his bookshop long enough to watch him learn the truth. And she had not knocked.
