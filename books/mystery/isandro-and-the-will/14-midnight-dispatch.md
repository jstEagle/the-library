## 14. Midnight Dispatch

The radio came alive at 11:47 p.m., the way it always did when the city decided to confess.

*Central to all units, we have a pickup at the Hotel Meridiano, two passengers, destination undisclosed.* A pause, static chewing the edges of a voice Odile had known for eleven years. *Anyone in the south quadrant?*

"South quadrant," she said into the mic, and heard the small ritual of acknowledgment ripple back — *copy, copy, ten-four, dispatch* — a litany she'd come to think of as the city's heartbeat, steady even when everything else in her life had gone arrhythmic.

She sat in the dispatch room above the Despacho Taxi garage with a paper cup of coffee gone cold an hour ago, a logbook open in front of her, and a name written at the top of a fresh page in her narrow, upright hand: **Bautista Quintero**.

Three weeks until the dinner. Nine days since she'd found the will file photographed and hidden in her own coat lining like a confession someone else had slipped into her pocket. Six days since the second letter — no, she corrected herself, there hadn't been a second letter yet. That was still coming. She could feel it the way sailors feel weather: a pressure behind the ears, a flatness in the light. Whatever Quintero was building, he wasn't finished building it.

But tonight she wasn't going to wait inside his architecture. Tonight she was going to do the thing she knew how to do, the thing that had solved three deaths that three separate police forces had been content to file under *accident*: she was going to listen to what the city said when it thought no one was keeping score.

Because here was the truth about a taxi company that nobody outside the trade understood: drivers see everything. Not dramatically — not like witnesses in films, gasping at crimes in progress. They see the way water sees. Slowly, passively, without judgment. They see who leaves which house at which hour, who cries in the back seat, who pays cash, who asks to be dropped a block from their own door. A month of a man's life leaves sediment in a taxi company's logs the way a river leaves silt, and all you have to do is know how to pan for it.

Odile had been panning since seven o'clock.

* * *

The method was simple and tedious, which was to say it suited her perfectly.

Every driver on the night rotation kept a trip sheet — time, origin, destination, fare, notes. Most notes were useless or obscene. But every few entries, something surfaced: *pax nervous, kept checking phone. Pax asked me to wait 20 min, paid extra. Big house on the hill road, guy came out carrying a briefcase like it owed him money.*

She'd pulled the sheets for the last five weeks — long enough to cover the period after Aurelio Villalobos's diagnosis, after the will was supposedly rewritten, after whatever machinery Quintero had set in motion began to turn. She cross-referenced pickups against the addresses she now knew by heart: the Villalobos estate, Quintero's office on Calle Reforma, his apartment on the north side, the courthouse, the marina.

And then there were the gaps. The trips logged as destinations that didn't quite make sense. The fares picked up at odd hours from places lawyers had no business being at one in the morning.

At 12:30, Chuy's voice crackled through: *"Oye, despachadora. You pulled my sheets from the third week of last month?"*

Odile leaned toward the mic. "I pull everyone's sheets, Chuy."

"Yeah, but you *called* me about them. Nobody calls me about anything." A pause, the sound of a cigarette lighter. "This about the lawyer?"

Her hand went still on the page. "What lawyer?"

"The one from the big house up the hill. Villalobos money. He rides with me sometimes — late, always late, always going places lawyers don't go." Another pause, longer, smoke exhaled into the mouthpiece. "You're doing your thing again, aren't you. Like with the fisherman."

Chuy had driven her out to the coast twice during that case, unprompted, unpaid, because he'd carried the drowned man's brother in his cab the night after the funeral and couldn't stop thinking about how quiet grief made people. "Like with the fisherman," she said.

"Then ask me the right questions, jefa. I got a fare to catch." And he rang off, leaving her smiling at the radio in the dark room — the closest thing to warmth she'd felt in nine days.

* * *

She asked the right questions for four hours.

The first answer came from Rosa, who drove the Wednesday-to-Saturday rotation and remembered everything because, as she put it, *my memory is the only pension I've got.*

*"Quintero? Sure. Tall one, gray at the temples, tips like he's apologizing for something. I picked him up at the estate — the Villalobos place — maybe five weeks back. Eleven-something at night. Took him to the marina."*

"The marina," Odile repeated. Her pen didn't move. Her whole body had gone very still, the stillness she'd learned in the years of listening, because movement meant breathing and breathing meant thinking and thinking too fast meant missing things.

"Marina del Norte, the commercial dock, not the pretty one. Dropped him by the fuel depot. Weird spot. No cars around. He told me to keep the meter running and came back twenty minutes later smelling like diesel and salt." Rosa's voice tightened with the particular pleasure of a witness who has just realized her testimony matters. "I remember because it was raining sideways and he didn't have an umbrella. Rich men don't stand in rain unless they're meeting somebody they don't want seen."

Five weeks back. Odile checked her own timeline, written in the margin of the logbook in pencil so it could be erased if anyone ever searched her room. Five weeks ago was the week before Aurelio Villalobos entered the hospital for the surgery that would leave him sedated while his signature dried on a forged will.

The marina. Where boats left from. Where things could be dropped into deep water and never found.

It matched nothing yet. It matched everything eventually. She wrote it down.

* * *

The second answer took longer, and it came from the least likely source: a kid named Emiliano, nineteen, three months on the job, who initially denied having ever driven anyone important anywhere.

"I drive nobodies," he said over the radio, defensive. "Drunk guys. Students."

"Drunk guys are the best witnesses," Odile said. "They remember everything except that they remember it. Walk me through your bad nights."

He walked her through six of them before she heard the click of recognition in his voice — the sound of a memory surfacing. "Okay, so, this one night — like a month ago, month and a half — I pick up this guy outside the quarry road. Out past the industrial park where there's literally nothing, right? Middle of the night. He's got dust on his shoes, white dust, like chalk, and he makes me take the long way into town, says he doesn't want to pass the weigh station."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Suit, but the suit was messed up, like he'd been crawling around or — " Emiliano stopped. "Señora. Are you saying that was the lawyer? Because I looked him up later, after I saw his picture in the paper next to some charity thing, and I thought, no way, that guy in the quarry road was sweating through his shirt."

A month and a half ago. The quarry. Odile closed her eyes and let the old file open itself in the dark behind them: Tomás Herrera, sixty-one, found at the bottom of the quarry access road eight years ago, ruled a fall, the white dust of crushed limestone on his clothes, his widow's settlement processed — expedited — by the firm of Quintero & Asociados before she'd finished grieving.

She had proven it was no fall. She had never proven who stood at the top of that road. She had told herself for eight years that the question was unanswerable.

Emiliano had just answered it for free, between fares, in a voice still cracking with adolescence.

"You did good, Emiliano."

"I did?"

"You're getting a raise."

"We get raises?"

"No," she said, "but it felt good to hear, didn't it," and he laughed, startled, and rang off, and she sat alone in the dispatch room with the rain starting against the windows and wrote *QUARRY — CONFIRMED* in letters that pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

* * *

The third answer she already knew. She'd known it since the fisherman case, though she hadn't known she knew it, and finding it now was less discovery than recognition — the sick, vertiginous click of a lock turning that had been waiting for its key for a year.

She called Chuy back at 3:40 a.m., when the radio traffic thinned to nothing and the city lay open like a patient on a table.

"The underpass," she said. "Route 9, the one they built under the rail line. Ever run a fare out there late?"

Silence. Then, quietly, all the performance gone out of his voice: "Twice."

"Tell me."

"First time was last winter. January. Cold enough to crack windshields. Guy flags me at the gas station on Route 9, wants to go to the underpass, says he's expecting a delivery." Chuy's exhale fogged the mouthpiece audibly. "There's no deliveries at the Route 9 underpass at two in the morning, jefa. There's nothing there. I drop him, he stands under the light looking at the drainage channel, and fifteen minutes later he comes back and says the delivery was cancelled. Paid me triple not to talk about it."

"And the second time?"

"Two weeks ago. Same guy. Same spot. This time he didn't get out. Just sat in my back seat looking at the channel for ten minutes, then said, 'Take me home.' Didn't say another word the whole ride." A long pause. "That was the fisherman, wasn't it. The one you figured out. That's where they found him."

"That's where they found him." Under the rail line, in the drainage channel, after the storm last winter — Rafael Ochoa, fifty-four, thirty years on the water, dead of 'exposure' a hundred meters from his own boat. She had unraveled it: the debt, the insurance policy signed three weeks prior, the witness who'd seen a car with municipal plates idling near the underpass. She had taken it as far as the evidence allowed and watched it die in a prosecutor's inbox, and the only person who'd thanked her was the widow, who'd cried and said *at least somebody knows my husband didn't just fall down drunk.*

Someone had met Bautista Quintero at that underpass in January. Someone had cancelled.

Or someone had been checking his work.

* * *

By dawn the picture hung complete on the wall of the dispatch room, assembled from trip sheets, pencil marks, and the accumulated memory of a dozen drivers who had never once been asked what they saw.

Bautista Quintero had made three late-night journeys in five weeks — to the marina, to the quarry, to the underpass. Three sites. Three of Odile's cases. The drowned fisherman. The fall from the quarry road. And the third — she made herself write it, made herself look at it — Elena Vasquez, the woman from the underpass district, the hit-and-run that wasn't, the first case Odile had ever cracked, four years ago, back when she'd still believed solving them would be enough.

He had visited the geography of his career. Or he had visited the geography of hers.

That was the thought that wouldn't leave her as the sky went gray over the garage and the day shift started clattering up the stairs. Because there were only two readings, and both of them were terrible. Either Quintero was retracing his own crimes — sentimental, arrogant, careless in the way successful men become careless — or he was retracing *hers*, walking the ground where she had dug, learning how she thought, preparing for the moment he would need to make her disappear as cleanly as he'd made three other people disappear.

*A crime that has already been planned in detail.* Isandro's anonymous letter. Her own death, described in police-report grammar, waiting somewhere in the future like a fare that never arrives.

She stood at the window with the last of the cold coffee and watched the morning buses start their routes, and felt the fear settle into her chest — not the sharp panic of the study, of the photograph taken through glass, but something heavier and more useful. Certainty. She had spent years listening for what people didn't say; now she had listened to a month of a killer's movements, and the movements said everything.

Quintero wasn't improvising. Men who improvise don't return to the scene of a four-year-old hit-and-run at two in the morning. He was working from a plan, and the plan had chapters, and she had just read enough of it to know how it ended: with a toast, a darkened room, a poisoned glass, and a convenient grieving nephew with a gun registered in his name.

Unless the plan changed. Unless someone switched the decanters.

"Jefa." Chuy's voice, soft on the radio, pulling her back. "You still there?"

"I'm here."

"You look like hell. I can tell by your voice."

She almost smiled. "Chuy. The lawyer. The night he sat in your cab at the underpass and didn't get out. Did he say anything else? Anything at all?"

Another silence, longer, and when Chuy spoke again she could hear him choosing each word like a man stepping across ice. "Yeah. One thing. He was talking to himself, I figured, half asleep, but I heard it clear. He said—" the radio crackled, held its breath— "*'She counts everything. So count on her being there.'*"

The line went quiet. Outside, the first bus of the morning hissed past below the window, and Odile stood very still in the gray light, hearing it: not a threat, exactly. An invitation. He wanted her at that dinner. He had counted on her from the beginning.

Which meant Marisol Villalobos — the cousin with the debts and the arithmetic and the dates that didn't match — was either his instrument or his loose end. And loose ends, Odile thought, reaching for her coat, were exactly the kind of thing Bautista Quintero liked to handle personally, late at night, in places where nobody was keeping score.

Except her. She was always keeping score.

She picked up the phone and dialed the number Marisol had given the catering office, and listened to it ring.
