## 1. The Retainer

The red light on the recorder was the only honest thing in the room.

Vesper Thackeray had learned to trust it more than she trusted herself — more than her memory, which edited; more than her voice, which performed; more than her instincts, which had a documented history of being wrong in interesting ways. The light didn't editorialize. It simply said: *this is happening now, and now is all you get.*

"Episode forty-seven," she said into the microphone. "The Coldest Case I Ever Covered. And I mean that literally, because this one involves a man who froze to death in a walk-in freezer at his own wedding reception, and nobody noticed for eleven hours because everyone assumed he'd stepped out for a cigarette."

She paused. In her headphones, her own breathing sounded enormous.

"Eleven hours," she repeated, softer. "Two hundred guests. A string quartet. An open bar that, according to three separate witnesses, was *excellent*. And not one person thought to check whether the groom was still among them." She let the silence sit the way she'd learned to let silences sit — long enough to become uncomfortable, short enough that listeners didn't skip ahead. "By the end of this episode, I'm going to introduce you to four people who each had a reason to leave him there. And by the end of the season, I'm going to tell you which of them is lying about the cigarettes."

The studio was actually her spare bedroom, which was actually a converted dining nook with acoustic foam wedged between framed postcards from towns where murders had happened. The foam did nothing. Her neighbor's cat could be heard through two walls on good days. But the light was red, and the light was honest, and that was enough.

She was thirty-one seconds into the second act when the doorbell rang.

Vesper froze mid-sentence — "*and what makes this case so unusual isn't the freezer, it's the*" — and stared at the light as though it might explain. Nobody rang her doorbell. Her listeners wrote letters, actual letters, which she loved and found slightly alarming. Her mother called. The occasional process server knocked. But the doorbell was a creature of the world outside the show, and she hadn't fed it in months.

She saved the file, muted the mic, and went downstairs with the particular wariness of a woman whose profession involved asking dangerous people polite questions.

The man on her porch was old the way furniture is old — not frail, but finished, made entirely of materials that had already survived everything intended to destroy them. He wore a charcoal overcoat buttoned to the throat despite the mild afternoon, and he held a leather document case against his chest the way other men held Bibles or babies. His face was long and dry and precise, like something drawn with a ruling pen.

"Ms. Thackeray," he said. Not a question. He pronounced it *Thack-er-ay*, all four syllables, with the care of a man who had spent a lifetime reading names aloud and refused to mangle one now.

"That's me. Mostly at night, though. Mornings I'm a different, worse person."

"I've listened to every episode," he said. "Twice, some of them. Your episode on the Harrow County fire was sloppy in the middle third, but your instinct about the insurance signature was correct, and I want you to know I noticed."

Vesper blinked. "Thank you? That's — most people lead with the part they liked."

"Most people are being kind. Kindness wastes time, and I have very little left, so I've stopped spending it on courtesy." He said it without self-pity, without drama, the way a man reports the weather. Then he extended a hand — cool, dry, a handshake like signing something. "Ivo Marchetti. Retired notary public. Forty-one years. I witnessed nine thousand six hundred and twelve signatures, and I would like to discuss witnessing yours."

"My — "

"May I come in? It's about a murder."

And here was the thing Vesper would replay later, at 2 a.m., staring at her ceiling: he said *it's about a murder* the way other people said *it's about a roof repair.* No weight on it. No thrill. A murder was a task on a list, somewhere below *pick up dry cleaning* and above *renew passport*, and Ivo Marchetti had clearly already organized the list.

She should have closed the door. She knew that even then, in the small professional corner of her brain that kept a running tally of red flags. Instead she heard herself say, "There's coffee. It's not good coffee, but there's a lot of it."

"I don't drink coffee," he said, stepping inside. "But I appreciate the ritual. Proceed with it anyway. It'll make you feel like this is a normal conversation, and we both need that."

* * *

He sat in the guest chair across from her desk — the chair reserved for interviewees, positioned carefully so the recorder was visible but unobtrusive — and declined the coffee with a small gesture, as if declining a country. Vesper poured herself a mug mostly to have something to do with her hands.

"You said you listen to the show," she began. "All of it?"

"Forty-six episodes. You have a habit of underplaying your best evidence and overexplaining your worst. You also apologize when you cry, which you should stop doing. Crying is data."

"It's — okay, wow. Mr. Marchetti — "

"Ivo."

"Ivo. I'm going to need you to tell me why you're here, because I have a rule about not guessing. Guessing is how podcasts get people convicted."

Something flickered across his dry face — approval, maybe, or its distant cousin. He opened the document case and removed a single sheet of paper, laid it flat on the desk between them, and turned it to face her with two fingers, squaring its edges to the desk's edge. Forty-one years of muscle memory.

It was a retainer agreement. Her name was already printed in the client field. Her address. Even her EIN, which meant he'd looked her up through the state's business registry, which meant he'd done homework, which meant the sloppiness comment had been earned.

"I'd like to retain you," he said, "for a period of one year, beginning today."

"This is very thorough, but I do true crime. Cold cases. Things that already happened. I'm not a private investigator, I don't do security work, and I definitely don't do — whatever this is shaping up to be."

"What this is," Ivo said, "is an investigation into a murder that has not yet occurred."

Vesper set down her mug. "That's not a cold case. That's a police matter. If someone's threatened you — "

"No one has threatened me."

"Then — "

"I will be murdered within the year," Ivo Marchetti said, in the tone of a man confirming a dental appointment. "I don't know the date. I know the season. Late autumn, I believe, though I could be off by weeks either way. I know it will look natural or accidental, because the people responsible are careful. And I know that when it happens, the evidence will be arranged so thoroughly, so *lovingly*, that no coroner in this state will look twice." He folded his hands on his knee. "I am hiring you to prove my murder before it happens. To build the case now, while I'm alive to help you, so that when I die, someone can stand up in a room full of grieving relatives and say: *no. Look again.*"

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, the neighbor's cat performed its daily demolition of the bookshelf. Vesper became aware that she was holding her breath and let it out slowly, buying time.

"Okay," she said. "Let's take that seriously for a second. People who predict their own murders usually fall into three categories. One: paranoid, and needs a doctor, not a podcaster. Two: suicidal, and planning to frame someone, which is its own kind of horror. Three: con artists setting up an insurance claim or a grift. Which are you?"

"None."

"That's what all three would say."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "Which is why I brought proof."

He reached into the document case again and produced a cashier's check. Vesper looked at the number and had to look twice, then a third time, because her brain kept rounding it down to something survivable.

"That's a year's fee," Ivo said. "Your published rate, plus fifty percent, because I intend to be a difficult client. Non-refundable. It clears Monday. There's a second check of equal size in escrow with my bank, payable on delivery of a written findings report, regardless of whether I'm alive to read it."

"Ivo. This is — no. Absolutely not. You can't pay me to investigate a murder that hasn't happened. There's no victim. There's no crime. There's just a very calm old man with too much money and a flair for the dramatic."

"I have never once been dramatic," he said, and the terrible thing was that she believed him. "Drama is for people who aren't sure they'll be believed. I am entirely sure. I've spent forty-one years watching liars perform, Ms. Thackeray. I know exactly what belief costs, and I've paid it."

"Then tell me who's going to kill you."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you wouldn't believe me yet, and worse — you'd warn them." He leaned forward, and for the first time something moved behind his eyes that wasn't calm. It took her a moment to identify it, because it was so out of place on that ruled, finished face. It was tenderness. Grim, unsentimental, deeply worn tenderness, like a coin rubbed smooth. "I'm not hiring you to save me, Vesper. May I call you Vesper? Thank you. Saving me is not on the table; I've reviewed the options personally. I'm hiring you to *witness* me. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire job."

"A witness to what? You haven't told me anything."

"I've told you everything that matters today. The rest arrives on schedule." He stood, buttoned his coat with two economical motions, and slid the retainer across the desk until it touched her mug. "You have seven days to decline. After that, the check clears and we begin. Read the terms. Clause nine is the important one."

Vesper looked down at clause nine. *Client acknowledges that Investigator may publish findings in any medium, at any time, including during Client's lifetime, without prior approval.*

"You're letting me broadcast this?"

"I'm *requiring* it. A secret investigation protects no one. I want them to hear you working. I want them to lie awake wondering what you've found." He collected his document case. At the door of her studio — her dining nook, her foam-wedged shrine to the dishonest dead — he paused, and for a moment the notary fell away and she saw just an old man, tired in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hope you fail. I hope in twelve months you write me an angry letter saying I wasted your time and my money and that no one ever laid a finger on me. I hope you bill me for the hour and curse my name." He smiled then, briefly, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen all year. "But I've read this ending before, Vesper. I helped write it, once, for someone else. And I know how the paperwork goes."

Then he was gone, and the door clicked shut with the soft finality of a seal pressed into wax.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night, which was normal. What wasn't normal was that she didn't sleep *working* — no timeline spreadsheets, no re-listening to episode audio, none of the usual insomniac crafts. She lay in bed thinking about the word *witness*, turning it over like a stone found on a beach.

At 3 a.m. she gave up, went downstairs, and pulled up the state notary registry. Ivo Marchetti. Commissioned 1971. Retired 2012. Clean record, no complaints, no disciplinary actions in four decades of handling other people's most consequential moments. Nine thousand six hundred and twelve signatures, and apparently not one of them disputed.

She searched news archives next. Marchetti appeared exactly three times in fifty years: once as a witness to a zoning dispute, once donating to the library, and once in 1974, in a two-inch item she had to squint to read.

*MARCHETTI MAN DIES IN LAKE FALL. Services for Edmund Marchetti, 24, of [illegible], who drowned Saturday during a family outing, were held Tuesday. Survivors include a brother, Ivo.*

She stared at it for a long time. Drowned. Family outing. Brother.

Her phone buzzed on the desk. A text from a number she didn't have saved:

*Clause nine means you can start recording whenever you like. But clause eleven means you can't record our conversations without telling me. I assume you noticed. — I.M.*

Below it, a second message, sent four minutes later:

*P.S. The Harrow County fire. You were right about the signature. Follow that instinct. It's the same hand.*

Vesper set the phone face-down, walked to the window, and watched the street where a retired notary had stood that afternoon, holding his secrets against his chest like a man carrying water in his hands across a desert.

Every instinct she owned — the ones that were wrong in interesting ways, the ones that had built a career and burned two friendships — every one of them stood up and pointed at the retainer on her desk and said the same thing:

*This case shouldn't exist. Take it.*

She got out a fresh legal pad. At the top, in block letters, she wrote: **IVO MARCHETTI — WILL BE MURDERED (ALLEGEDLY)**. Underneath, after a moment, she added: **BY WHOM? AND WHY DOES HE ALREADY KNOW THE PAPERWORK?**

Outside, dawn came up gray and unhurried, indifferent to deadlines. Six days until the check cleared. Somewhere out there, presumably, a murder was warming up in the wings, checking its watch.

Vesper Thackeray poured the world's worst coffee, hit record on nothing in particular, and started making a list of everyone who might want a careful old man dead — beginning, she suspected, with whoever had taught him to be this calm about it.
