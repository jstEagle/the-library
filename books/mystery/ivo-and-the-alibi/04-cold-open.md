## 4. Cold Open

There is a particular smell to a county records annex that no amount of institutional lemon disinfectant can kill: old paper, old glue, and the faint ghost of decades of coffee spilled by people who were also, in their way, hunting something.

Vesper Thackeray had spent enough of her life in rooms like this one that she'd stopped noticing the smell years ago. What she noticed now was the silence — the specific, attentive silence of a building that should have been closed an hour ago but wasn't, because a clerk named Doreen had taken pity on her.

"Last call was at four," Doreen had said, not unkindly, when Vesper explained she needed pre-1980 death records for the Marchetti family. "But you've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a woman who's going to sit here until they carry her out. I'll leave the lights on till six. Don't touch the microfiche machine, it bites."

So Vesper sat at a scarred oak table under fluorescent light the color of weak tea, with a box of index cards in front of her and her recorder running on the table beside her — not because she intended to use any of this audio, but because talking out loud helped her think, and because somewhere in the back of her mind lived the superstitious belief that cases liked to be narrated. Stories wanted witnesses. That was practically the thesis of her entire career.

"Marchetti," she said quietly, flipping cards. "Marchetti. Come on. Somebody died in this family before 1975, I can feel it."

She could feel it because Ivo Marchetti had told her so — or nearly. Three days after the dinner at which she'd met the heirs, he had summoned her back to his office above the shuttered notary shop on Calloway Street, poured her tea she didn't want, and said, in the tone of a man discussing weather:

"You'll want to look into 1974."

"I'll want to—"

"My brother." He had looked out the window at the parking lot below, at nothing. "Emilio. You'll find him where the records are kept. Or you won't, and that will tell you something too."

Then he'd refilled her tea without asking and moved on to a long, fond digression about the proper storage of wax seals, as though he hadn't just handed her a thread and dared her to pull it.

Vesper pulled threads. It was, professionally speaking, the whole of what she did.

* * *

The card came up at 5:22 p.m., forty minutes before Doreen's mercy expired.

**MARCHETTI, EMILIO J. — b. 1941 — d. 11 AUG 1974 — LAKE CALLOWAY — ACCIDENTAL DROWNING.**

Vesper read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you reread a sentence in a contract that has just changed your life.

Accidental drowning. Fine. People drowned. Lakes were wet; that was their whole deal. But something about the card bothered her, and it took her a moment — sitting very still, one finger resting on the corner of the card like it might bolt — to identify what.

It wasn't the cause of death. It was the *completeness* of the card.

Vesper had spent eleven years in cold cases, and if cold cases had taught her anything, it was that old paper rots unevenly. Records from the seventies were a lottery. Half the time the coroner's report was missing, misfiled, water-damaged, or eaten — genuinely eaten, once, by mice, in a case she still thought about at 3 a.m. The other half of the time the file existed but was thin as tissue: a one-page report, a scribbled certificate, done.

This card was annotated. Cross-referenced. There were little pencil marks in margins in at least three different hands, dated across decades — a 1978 correction to the spelling of a witness's name, a 1991 note referencing a *see also*, a 2009 stamp she didn't recognize from any county office currently operating. Someone had been *tending* this file the way gardeners tend roses.

Nobody tends a file for fifty years unless they're growing something in it.

"Doreen," Vesper called, keeping her voice pleasant, casual, a woman merely curious. "Can I pull the actual file? Emilio Marchetti, 1974?"

Doreen appeared around the shelving with the unhurried gait of a woman who had never once been hurried by anything, including fires. She took the card, squinted at it, and frowned.

"Huh."

"*Huh* is not my favorite word, Doreen."

"It's checked out." Doreen turned the card over, as if the answer might be hiding on the back. "Has been since — huh. Since before my time, and I've been here twenty-six years."

"Checked out by whom?"

"There'd be a card for that." Doreen checked. There was no card for that. "Well. That's not right."

"No," Vesper agreed softly. "It isn't."

Here is what Vesper knew about records, and what she suspected most people secretly knew and preferred not to think about: archives are not neutral. An archive is a story a place tells about itself, and every story has an editor. Files went missing all the time — through fire, flood, incompetence, and occasionally through the far more interesting mechanism of a person who understood exactly which drawer held the paper that could ruin them.

But files that went missing usually stayed missing. This file hadn't gone missing. This file had been *maintained*. Corrected. Stamped. Loved, almost.

You don't destroy evidence you're afraid of. You destroy evidence carelessly, or in a panic, or not at all. But you *curate* evidence the way Ivo Marchetti kept his office — everything labeled, everything in its place, everything arranged so that anyone who came looking would find precisely what was meant to be found.

Vesper sat back down and pulled the rest of the Marchetti cards. And there it was again, the pattern repeating like a signature: the parents' death records, complete and annotated. A property dispute from 1969, cross-referenced twice. Even a marriage record for a cousin she'd never heard mentioned at dinner. Every document touching the Marchetti name had been handled. Touched. Tended.

Except—

She flipped back to Emilio's card and looked at the annotations again, really looked, holding it under the lamp. The pencil notes were in different hands, yes. But two of them — the 1978 correction and a small, precise checkmark beside the word *ACCIDENTAL* — were in the same hand.

A hand she recognized.

She'd seen it three days ago, signing a retainer agreement in blue-black ink, in letters so neat they looked typeset.

*Ivo.*

Vesper set the card down very carefully, the way you set down a thing that might be listening.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the lake path, because it was a warm evening and because she needed to move her legs while her brain did the heavy lifting. Lake Calloway spread out to her left, flat and gold in the late sun, full of paddleboarders and dogs and children shrieking with the pure joy of being cold on purpose. It was, Vesper reflected, extremely difficult to imagine this lake killing anybody. It looked like a lake in a commercial for antidepressants.

And yet. August 1974. Emilio Marchetti, thirty-three years old, drowned.

Her recorder was in her bag. She didn't take it out. Some thoughts weren't ready to be spoken aloud yet; saying them too early gave them power, or worse, made them sound stupid.

Let's say, she thought, stepping around a golden retriever with ambitions toward her backpack. Let's say Ivo killed his brother. Fifty years ago. Statute of limitations on manslaughter ran out decades ago — even murder, in most jurisdictions, would be pushing it, though some places had no limit on murder at all, and she'd have to check which kind of place this was. Let's say the file was curated to protect that secret. Fine. Old crime, old man, buried history. Sad, but not her problem, and more importantly not *newsworthy*.

Now let's say the same man walks into her studio, pays a year's fee in cash, and calmly informs her he will be murdered within twelve months.

Those two facts did not fit together. That was the trouble. A man hiding a fifty-year-old crime wants *silence*. He does not hire a true-crime podcaster with a modest but real audience and a documented habit of not letting things go. Hiring Vesper Thackeray to investigate your family was like hiring a woodpecker to fix your siding.

Unless the two crimes were connected. Unless the old crime was the *reason* for the new one — motive, inheritance, the four heirs at that terrible dinner table, the grievances that had surfaced over dessert like bodies rising in a pond, and God, Vesper, stop with the pond imagery.

Or unless—

She stopped walking. A jogger muttered something at her back and swerved around her.

Unless the murder prediction was itself part of the curation. Unless Ivo Marchetti, professional keeper of documents, had decided how his story would end and was arranging the paper trail accordingly — hiring a witness, planting a mystery, feeding her 1974 like bait on a hook, so that whatever happened to him would arrive pre-explained, pre-investigated, pre-narrated. A man who curated his brother's death file for fifty years was exactly the sort of man who would curate his own.

"That's insane," Vesper said aloud, to a duck. The duck declined to comment.

But her heart was going faster than the walk justified, and there was a feeling spreading through her chest that she knew well and usually loved: the click of a hunt beginning, the world sharpening, every stranger on the path suddenly a possible source. For eleven years this feeling had been the best thing in her life. The chase. The pattern. The moment the boring town revealed its hidden wiring.

So why did it feel different this time?

Because, said the honest part of her, the part she usually negotiated with rather than obeyed — because every other cold case had been someone else's grave. This one had invited her in. This one had paid her upfront. This one had poured her tea.

* * *

Back at her apartment — a third-floor walk-up she affectionately called the Archive Annex, on account of the floor-to-ceiling shelves and the fact that guests had to excavate a chair before sitting — Vesper laid out what she had on the kitchen table, which doubled as her war room, dining room, and shame shrine to unfinished research.

One index card. Two pages of photocopies she'd grabbed from the open drawers before Doreen's six o'clock lights-out: a 1974 newspaper clipping about the drowning (*LOCAL MAN DIES IN LAKE TRAGEDY*, a headline that managed to be both bland and ominous), and a property survey of the Marchetti lakeside parcel from 1971.

One cup of tea gone cold, forgotten, which for Vesper constituted a medical event.

She started a fresh case file. She always started the same way: a manila folder, the victim's name, and a single question written on the tab. Eleven years of habit. The question was the spine of the whole investigation; get it wrong and everything built on it leaned.

She wrote: **What happened to Emilio Marchetti on 11 August 1974?**

Looked at it. Crossed it out.

Wrote instead: **Who is editing the Marchetti story — and why did they let me in?**

Better. Truer. She pinned the folder to the corkboard above the desk, where it joined the thin gray company of open cases, and stood back with her arms crossed, regarding it the way a chess player regards a board two moves before realizing she's in trouble.

The newspaper clipping was next. She read it properly this time, pen in hand.

*Local Man Dies in Lake Tragedy.* Emilio J. Marchetti, 33, of Calloway Street, drowned on the evening of August 11th during a solo swim. Survived by his brother, Ivo Marchetti. Services private. The article was four paragraphs long and contained, as far as Vesper could tell, zero actual information — a masterpiece of the genre, really, a story that existed purely so that a story existed. No witness named. No coroner quoted. No mention of whether Emilio could swim, which for a grown man drowning in a calm inland lake in August was, you'd think, paragraph two material.

Solo swim. Private services. Brother survives.

Vesper underlined *solo* twice, then felt childish about it and drew a small, dignified arrow instead.

The property survey was duller and therefore more interesting. The Marchetti parcel included lake frontage — a boathouse, according to the 1971 drawing, at the northern edge. She checked the current county assessor maps online. The boathouse was gone now; the parcel had been subdivided in 1988, sold off in pieces, the shoreline dotted today with the tasteful cottages of people who would be absolutely horrified to learn their deck chairs occupied the footprint of a drowning.

But in 1974 there had been a boathouse. And Ivo Marchetti — who had been twenty-nine that summer, who had stayed in this town his whole life, who had become a notary, a keeper and sealer of documents, a man whose entire profession was making things official — had spent fifty years making sure the paper record of his brother's death was tidy.

Tidy. Not absent. *Tidy.*

Vesper put her head down on the table, briefly, next to the cold tea.

Because here was the thing she kept circling, the thing that had turned the evening's thrill slightly sour, like milk a day from turning: the file hadn't been hidden from her. Doreen's drawer had opened easily. The cards had come up on the first flip. Ivo himself had given her the year, the name, the location — *you'll find him where the records are kept* — like a father sending a child on a treasure hunt, confident she'd find exactly the prize he'd wrapped.

Every investigator knows the difference between a locked door and an unlocked one. Locked doors mean *stop*. Unlocked doors mean *come in, we've been expecting you*.

The Marchetti file was an unlocked door in a house where every other door had been carefully, lovingly maintained.

Someone wanted her reading this file. Which meant someone knew she'd come looking. Which meant—

Which meant the case already knew her back. Had known her, possibly, before she knew it. Before the retainer. Before the dinner. Maybe before she'd ever heard the name Marchetti at all.

Vesper sat up straight. The apartment had gotten dark around her; she hadn't noticed the sunset happen. Down in the street someone laughed, ordinary and human, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

She reached for her phone, thumb hovering over Renata's number — because Renata answered questions, Renata made tea that stayed hot, Renata's kitchen was the closest thing to a hearth this case offered — and then stopped, finger in the air.

Why did she trust Renata?

She thought about it honestly, which took about four seconds. She trusted Renata because Renata was warm, and asked good questions, and remembered details, and made her feel like the most interesting person in any room. All true. Also — said the honest part again, that tenant who never paid rent but never left — those were the exact qualities Vesper herself performed for a living, on microphone, to make strangers trust *her*.

She put the phone down without calling.

Instead she opened her laptop, created a new document titled **TIMELINE — MARCHETTI**, and typed the only line she had:

*1974 — Emilio drowns. File begins to be tended. By whom?*

Then, after a moment, a second line:

*Ivo says: "You'll want to look into 1974."*

And then, because paranoia is just pattern recognition with anxiety, a third:

*Who else knows I'm looking?*

She stared at the three lines for a long time. Outside, the lake would be going dark, folding its gold away, doing whatever it did at night when nobody was watching. Somewhere in this town, presumably, Ivo Marchetti was asleep in his neat office above the shuttered shop, surrounded by fifty years of perfectly ordered paper, dreaming — if he dreamed at all — of a night in August when the water was calm and his brother went swimming alone.

Tomorrow, Vesper decided, she would go back to the annex and ask Doreen who else had requested the Marchetti files in the last year. Tomorrow she'd pull the assessor's chain of title on the boathouse parcel. Tomorrow she'd start on the witnesses named in the clipping's fine print, the ones the article didn't quote.

Tonight, she did the thing she always did when a case first showed its teeth. She hit record on her phone, held it close, and spoke into the dark of her kitchen, quiet, just for the file:

"Cold open. August 1974. A man goes swimming and doesn't come back, and his brother spends the next fifty years dusting the paperwork. Everyone calls it an accident. The statute of limitations agrees." A pause. "But somebody keeps checking the lock on that door. And I intend to find out why — starting with the man who hired me to prove a murder that hasn't happened yet, and who seems awfully eager to show me one that did."

She stopped the recording. Saved it. Labeled it.

And it was only after she'd turned off the lamp and lain down in the dark, listening to the familiar sounds of the building settling around her, that the last piece clicked into place, the small one, the one that raised every hair on her arms:

Ivo had told her to look in the records. But he'd never told her *which* records office.

And she'd driven straight to the county annex without checking whether there was another one — a private archive, a church registry, a family collection — anywhere else in the region that held 1974.

As if she'd always known where to go.

As if someone had made sure she would.
