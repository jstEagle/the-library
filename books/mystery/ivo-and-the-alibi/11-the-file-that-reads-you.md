## 11. The File That Reads You

The county archive closed at five on Fridays, which meant that by 5:40 I was back in my hotel room with a paper bag of cold noodles, two highlighters, and the entire Marchetti case file spread across the bed like a tarot deck nobody sane would want a reading from.

I had photographed every page in the deposit box, and now I'd spent three days photographing the official archive copies too — the coroner's report on Luca Marchetti, 1974, death by misadventure, drowning in the irrigation canal behind the family orchard. The file Renata's town kept so carefully. The file that had been bothering me for weeks in a way I couldn't articulate, the way a room bothers you when one picture frame is hanging a half-inch crooked and you can't find which one.

Tonight I found it.

I'd made myself a chart. This is the part of the job nobody puts in the podcast trailers — not the dramatic confrontations or the midnight graveyard visits, but a woman sitting cross-legged on a hotel duvet at nearly six o'clock on a Friday evening, drawing columns on the back of a takeout menu because she'd run out of legal pads. Left column: the official record. Right column: what I knew, and when I'd learned it.

I worked through the file page by page, dating everything.

Here's what you need to understand about old case files: they decay unevenly. Paper yellows in patches. Ink fades where a folder creased. Coffee rings happen. Staples rust and leave little orange ghosts. A fifty-year-old file should look like a fifty-year-old file — battered in random places, worn where hands happened to hold it, not where logic says they should have.

This file wore its age *strategically*.

The pages most likely to be handled — the cover sheet, the coroner's summary, the witness statements — were yellowed evenly, beautifully, like props in a period drama. But the back pages, the ones nobody would ever touch — the property diagrams, the canal maintenance records — were crisp. White. Practically smug about it.

And then there were the replacements. Once you saw them, you couldn't unsee them. Page fourteen, a statement from a farmhand named Aldo Petri, was typed on a machine whose ribbon was fresher than the one used for page thirteen. Same typewriter model, different decade of ribbon wear. Somebody had retyped Aldo's statement at some point and slipped it back in, and they'd done it well enough to fool everyone for fifty years.

Everyone except a podcaster with insomnia, a flatbed scanner, and a grudge against tidy things.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the ceiling.

"Okay," I told the ceiling, because talking to yourself is free therapy and I'm between therapists. "Somebody cleaned this file. Recently-ish. Carefully. Which means somebody knew exactly what needed cleaning."

That was creepy but survivable. Old families protect old secrets; I'd written an entire season about a shipping magnate whose descendants had laundered his reputation so thoroughly that three separate biographies read like brochures. Files get curated. It's practically a civic tradition.

What wasn't survivable — what made me put down the noodle fork and not pick it up again — came next, when I did the thing I should have done days ago.

I dated my own investigation.

Left column, right column. What the file hid, and when I'd found out.

*The coroner's photo.* The original, the one in the deposit box, showed something the archive copy didn't: a bruise on Luca's temple, photographed and then — in the official version — simply absent, the photo replaced with a cleaner print. When had I learned there *was* an original? Tuesday. Nine days ago. The deposit box opened Monday.

*The second will.* I'd confirmed its existence Wednesday, cross-referencing probate indexes at the county annex.

*The ledger page.* 'R.' Thursday night. Two days ago.

Now here is the thing that turned my blood from warm to interestingly cold: the *edits* to the archive file weren't fifty years old either.

Page fourteen — Aldo Petri's statement — mentioned, in passing, that he'd seen "a light on at the notary's office" the night Luca died. Ivo's office. The detail was small, buried mid-paragraph, easy to miss. And the retyped version had softened it: "a light may have been on." May have. The hedge of a man who never said it at all.

But here's the kicker, the thing I underlined twice and then drew a box around and then, in a state of rising hysteria, starred: the retyping ink wasn't fifty years old. Under magnification — thank you, hotel desk lamp and a jeweler's loupe I bought in a moment of optimism in Reno years ago — the ribbon impression was sharp. Recent. Within the last year, maybe two.

Someone had edited this file *while I was investigating it.*

No. Worse. I flipped through my notes with hands that wanted to shake and didn't let them, because shaking hands are for people who get to be victims, and I was working.

The edits tracked me.

The softened statement about the light appeared after my third interview with Ivo — after I'd started asking him, obliquely, about the night his brother died. The replacement of the coroner's photo in the archive index — someone had updated the finding aid to note the original as "misplaced" — was dated, in pencil, in a margin, in a hand I now recognized: the same neat archivist's script that had catalogued the deposit box contents. Dated the week *after* I first asked about photographs.

Every edit corresponded to a discovery of mine. Not before. Not after. In step. Like a dance partner who knew the choreography because they'd choreographed it.

I got up and walked around the small hotel room, which took four steps each direction, so mostly I just turned in place like a rotisserie chicken having an epiphany.

Somebody inside my circle knew exactly what I knew, and exactly when I knew it, and was quietly tidying the historical record around me like a maid fluffing pillows in a house where a murder was being planned.

Who had access?

Marcus — no. Marcus could barely access his own bank account; he'd asked me last week how to attach a document to an email, and the document was already attached. Odile had access to nothing but her own grievances, which were extensive but not archival. Tobias hadn't been in the archive since — well, since I'd started watching who went in and out of the archive, which was its own confession about my mental state.

Renata ran an inn eleven miles away and radiated warmth like a wood stove. Rhea — no, wait, I didn't know anyone named Rhea yet. Scratch that. That was a thought for a future chapter of my life I hadn't lived yet.

And Ivo himself, who was dying on schedule, serene as a man reading his own obituary and finding it flattering.

I called Ivo.

He picked up on the second ring, which he always did, as though he'd been holding the phone and waiting, which I increasingly suspected he literally was.

"Miss Thackeray."

"Ivo. Question. Has anyone else been to the county archive recently? Anyone connected to you?"

A pause. Not a guilty pause — I've heard guilty pauses, they have a specific texture, like stepping on a stair that isn't there. This pause was *sad*. "The archive is a public institution," he said gently. "Anyone may visit."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind of answer I have left to give." I could hear him smiling, that patient, terrible smile of his. "Vesper. How is your investigation going?"

"It's going fine," I lied.

"Wonderful. Keep going. You're closer than you think." And he hung up. He *hung up*, the way you hang up on a grandchild, warmly, with the confidence of a man who believes the conversation will continue because he has arranged for it to continue.

I stared at the phone. Closer than you think. Closer to *what*? I hadn't told him what I'd found. I hadn't told anyone what I'd found. So how did he know I was close?

Unless he didn't know. Unless he was saying it to everyone. Unless —

Unless someone was telling him what I knew, and when.

*

I didn't sleep much, which was fine, because sleep requires believing your hotel room contains only you, and I'd lost faith in that premise somewhere around two a.m., when I re-read my own notes and noticed that my handwriting on Tuesday's entry was pressed hard enough to imprint three pages down, and the third page down was a list of names, and I caught myself checking the imprints like a psychic reading a palm.

Wednesday morning I did the only reasonable thing, which was to make a timeline on the hotel window using dry-erase markers I'd stolen from a conference room in 2019. The maids were going to hate me. The maids were going to have questions. Let the maids have questions; I had bigger problems.

Down the left side: my discoveries, dated. Across the top: the file edits, dated. And in the middle, connecting them like a conspiracy theorist's dream board, lines. Red lines. I only had red and one dying green, so red it was. My room looked like the wall of a very organized hostage-taker.

The pattern held. Every edit within forty-eight hours of my corresponding discovery. Every single one. Eleven data points. Statistically, this was not a coincidence; statistically, this was a subscription service, and I was the content.

Which meant surveillance. Not cameras — I'd swept for those, or rather I'd paid a man named Devraj at a security shop to sweep for those, and he'd found nothing and charged me for lunch anyway. Not phone taps; I checked mine the paranoid way, unscrewing nothing, just listening for the telltale crinkle of a line that isn't yours alone.

No. This was simpler and worse. This was a person. A person who talked to me, drank coffee with me, laughed at my jokes — and I do make jokes, good ones, jokes people remember — and then went home and updated the file.

*A person who talks to me.*

I stood in front of my window timeline in yesterday's clothes and felt the specific loneliness of my profession arrive all at once, like weather. Here's the thing they don't tell you about true crime work: you spend years learning to trust patterns instead of people, and then one day the pattern tells you that the people were the trap all along. Every warm conversation recalibrates itself in your memory. Renata asking nothing about herself and answering everything. Odile's brittle precision. Tobias's nervous generosity. Even Ivo, especially Ivo, hiring me with a year's fee upfront, buying himself a witness, keeping me close the way you keep a lamp close — not because you love the lamp, but because of what it shows you.

Had any of it been real? Was I a person to these people, or a process? An instrument being read even as I read the file?

I thought about calling my sister. I thought about it for a long time, thumb hovering. Then I imagined explaining it — *so there's this file, see, and it's editing itself around me* — and heard how it sounded, and put the phone down. Isolation isn't always imposed. Sometimes you build it yourself, brick by brick, out of sentences you can't say aloud without sounding unwell.

Fine. Then I'd work alone. I'd always worked alone; the found-family stuff was new and, apparently, load-bearing, and discovering the load-bearing walls were fake was exactly the kind of structural news you don't want at seven in the morning.

One more pass through the edits. There had to be a tell. Forgers always leave one; the file had been curated by a careful person, but careful people are careful *on average*, and averages have edges.

I found it at noon, in the margin of the canal maintenance record, page thirty-one. A cataloguing stamp — date received, initials of the processing archivist. The stamp had been reapplied. Fresh ink over the ghost of older ink, slightly rotated, two degrees counterclockwise. Sloppy by archival standards. Human by every other standard.

The initials: *R.V.*

I sat with that for a while. County staff directories were online; I pulled up the archive's current personnel page and scrolled. No R.V. Former employees, then. I requested the personnel index through the public records portal, hit paywall, swore creatively, and remembered — with the small cold click of a lock turning — that Ivo had given me a key to more than a deposit box. He'd given me his professional credentials, once, offhandedly, back when I still thought he was a charming old man instead of a charming old man with a filing system for guilt. Notary access. Records retrieval rights. Fifty years of them.

By two p.m. I had the former-employee list. By 2:15 I had crossed out everyone who'd left the county before 1990, everyone who'd died, everyone whose initials didn't match.

One name remained. Hired nineteen years ago. Still employed. Initials matching the stamp. And — I felt this in my sternum, the way you feel a stair that isn't there — currently assigned to the special collections desk, Tuesdays through Saturdays.

I looked at my calendar. Today was Wednesday. Tomorrow was Thursday. Saturday was the day Ivo had named, months ago, in a calm voice over tea, as the day he expected to die.

I booked nothing, packed nothing, told no one. I wrote the name on my window timeline in the dying green marker, circled it twice, and stood back.

The file had been reading me. Tomorrow, I was going to go read it back.

My phone buzzed. A text from Renata: *Come to dinner tomorrow? Making the braised rabbit you liked. Bring your appetite and your stories. — R.*

I looked at the initial. I looked at the initials on the stamp. They matched, of course they matched, and also of course they meant nothing, because half the county probably had those initials, and also they meant everything, and I stood in my green-circled crime scene of a hotel room understanding, finally, the shape of the trap:

It wasn't that someone inside my circle was editing the file.

It was that I couldn't know *which* someone, and every hour I spent suspecting them all made me easier to manage, lonelier, louder in my own head. Whoever 'R' was, they'd built a machine where suspicion did the surveillance for them.

Tomorrow I'd dine on rabbit with one of them, and smile, and watch.

Saturday, Ivo expected to die.

I picked up the green marker and wrote, beneath the name, one word: *Tomorrow.*
