## 5. A Voice from the Archive

The archive lived in Orsen's basement the way a body lives in a quarry: submerged, preserved by cold, waiting for someone with the stomach to bring it up.

Three filing cabinets, two external drives labeled in Tomas's old handwriting — *SEASON ONE*, *SEASON TWO*, *DO NOT TOUCH* — and forty-one hours of cassette tape recorded in the autumn of 1994, when Vesterhavn still believed Mikkel Brandt had simply walked into the sea and the police still believed it too. Orsen had bought the tapes at an estate auction eleven years ago, a box lot nobody else bid on, and had listened to perhaps a third of them before the podcast pivoted to fresher tragedies, juicier ones, cases where the grief was recent enough to have a pulse.

Now the grief was theirs. Now the quarry held a man in Orsen's own coat, and every hour of tape felt less like research than like reading someone else's mail that had been addressed to them all along.

Orsen made coffee they didn't drink and set up the old deck on the workbench, threading the first unplayed cassette with hands that remembered how. The machine wheezed, caught, began to turn. Hiss filled the basement like weather.

*Interview conducted 14 October 1994. Subject: Grethe Lund, domestic employee, Halvorsen residence.*

Orsen sat down slowly on the stool by the workbench.

They knew the name. Everyone in Vesterhavn over forty knew the name, though almost no one said it anymore — Grethe Lund, the Halvorsens' housekeeper, the woman who'd given one statement to the police and then vanished from the record as completely as Mikkel Brandt himself. She'd died in 2001, alone, in a rented room above the bakery on Kirkegade. Orsen remembered the obituary because it was so small. Two lines. No photograph. The town had a gift for making people disappear without ever moving them.

On the tape, a younger voice — Inspector Dahl's, unmistakable even through thirty years of oxide and hiss, that patient gravel — asked her to state her name for the record.

*"Grethe Lund."*

*"And your position at the Halvorsen household?"*

*"Housekeeper. Eleven years."*

A pause on the tape, long enough that Orsen could hear Dahl shifting paper, could hear — faintly, absurdly — a clock ticking somewhere in whatever room they'd used. The Halvorsen kitchen, maybe. Orsen tried to picture it: the long pine table, the copper pots Einar Halvorsen liked to show off to visitors, the window over the sink looking out on the terrace where the anniversary dinner had been served the night Mikkel Brandt walked out of history.

*"Mrs. Lund, I want you to tell me again what you saw on the evening of September third. Take your time. Start wherever it's easiest."*

Another pause. When Grethe spoke, her voice was low and careful, the voice of a woman choosing each word the way she might choose which glass to set before which guest.

*"I was in the scullery. Washing the dinner things. There were many glasses — it was the anniversary, thirty years, Mr. Halvorsen likes—" she stopped herself. "Liked to mark such things properly. I heard a car come up the drive. Not their car. Their car was already in the garage; I'd heard Mr. Anders put it away not twenty minutes before. This was another engine. Heavier. Diesel, I thought, or something like it. It came slow up the drive and stopped."*

*"Stopped where?"*

*"By the gate. Not at the house. By the gate, where the turning circle is. And then there were voices. A man's voice, raised — not shouting, but raised. It carried across the gravel. I couldn't make out the words, only the... the shape of them. Angry words. Then Mr. Halvorsen's voice, lower, and then footsteps going down toward the gate, two sets, and then nothing for a while. Maybe ten minutes. Maybe fifteen. I kept washing. You keep washing when you hear things you shouldn't. That's half of what a housekeeper is for."*

Dahl's voice, gentle: *"What happened after the ten minutes? Fifteen?"*

*"One car left. Only one. It went back down the drive fast — faster than it came. And Mr. Halvorsen came back up to the terrace alone, and he called out to his daughter that everything was fine, that the guest had changed his mind about staying. And I stood in the scullery with my hands in the water and I thought: whose mind changes at half past nine at night, on a road with no buses, in the rain?"*

The tape hissed. Somewhere in the background a door closed.

*"Did you see the car itself, Mrs. Lund? Make, color, anything?"*

*"Only the shape of it, from the scullery window. Dark. Big. A sedan, older model. And the headlamps — this is a strange thing to remember, but I remember it — the left one sat higher than the right. Like the car had been in a knock sometime and never mended proper. One eye higher than the other. It looked at me coming down the drive like something with a crooked face."*

*"Did you tell anyone else this?"*

A silence on the tape that lasted four, five seconds — an eternity in interview time. Orsen found they'd stopped breathing.

*"I told the officer who took my first statement. The young one. He wrote it down. Then a few days later he came back and said the matter was settled, that Mr. Brandt had been seen at the harbor, that there was no second car, that I must have heard the wind off the water. Wind off the water." She laughed once, a dry sound with no floor under it. "Thirty years in service, Inspector. I know a diesel engine from the wind off the water."*

*"Why do you think he said that?"*

*"Because someone told him to say it," Grethe Lund said, and her voice did not waver at all. "And because whoever told him has more weight in this town than I will ever have. I've worked for rich families my whole life, Inspector. You learn what happens to servants who see things. They stop being needed. And then they stop being comfortable. And then they stop."*

The tape ran on — Dahl asking whether she'd be willing to give a formal deposition, Grethe saying yes, yes, of course, and then the recording simply ended, mid-sentence, mid-breath, as if someone had pressed stop with a purpose.

Orsen sat in the blue light of the workbench lamp and listened to the empty tape run its last seconds, the spools turning over nothing, and felt the particular vertigo of the researcher who realizes the archive has just changed size around them.

There was no second-car testimony in the official file. Reyes had let Orsen read it — grudgingly, in an interview room that smelled of cold coffee, watching them the whole time like a woman feeding something she wasn't sure wasn't a wolf. Orsen had gone through every page twice. Grethe Lund appeared exactly once: a single-sentence statement dated October 2nd, 1994. *Witness heard nothing unusual on the night in question.* Nothing unusual. Signed.

But here was Dahl, three days after that statement was signed, taking a full account from her anyway — off the record, on his own equipment, in her own voice, with her own crooked-faced car in it. A diesel sedan with one headlamp sitting higher than the other. An argument at the gate. Einar Halvorsen walking a guest down the drive and coming back alone.

Coming back alone.

Orsen pressed stop. The reels clicked. In the silence afterward the basement seemed louder than the tape had been — the tick of the cooling deck, the drip of the tap upstairs, their own pulse in their ears.

They got up and pulled the case file copy they'd transcribed, flipping to the witness index with fingers that weren't quite steady. Grethe Lund. One line. *Heard nothing unusual.* And beneath it, in the column for follow-up interviews: a dash. No follow-up. No deposition. No mention anywhere in three hundred pages of a dark sedan with mismatched lamps idling at the Halvorsen gate on the last night anyone saw Mikkel Brandt alive.

A voice had spoken into a microphone in October of 1994 and then been folded out of existence like a letter returned unopened.

Orsen thought about Grethe's obituary. Two lines. Above the bakery on Kirkegade. Died in 2001, seven years after she saw a car with a crooked face come down a drive, seven years of being told she'd heard the wind off the water. What kind of life is that, Orsen wondered, knowing what you know and having everyone agree, politely, permanently, that you don't know it?

It was the same kind of life, they realized with a cold settling in the chest, as the one Orsen had been living for the past month — since the quarry, since the jacket, since the whole town started looking at them with that soft, pitying, watchful expression, the look people give someone who has begun saying things that are inconvenient to agree with.

* * *

Orsen didn't sleep. They made a second pot of coffee and drank it standing at the counter while the tapes played on, cassette after cassette, Dahl's gravel moving through the town like a dredge. Most of it was dross — neighbors repeating rumors, harbor men repeating the harbor men — but Orsen catalogued everything now with new eyes, listening for the gaps, for the statements that sounded rehearsed, for the places where the record had been ironed flat.

At four in the morning they found the second thing.

It was on a tape marked only *MISC / OCT-NOV*, unlabeled beyond that, and it was not an interview at all. It was Dahl talking to himself — or rather, dictating. Case notes, spoken aloud into the recorder in his car, the way old investigators did before laptops, his voice close and tired against the windshield-rain sound behind him.

*"October the nineteenth. Follow-up on Lund. She confirms the vehicle again, same details, same description. Says the young constable who took her first statement was Rask — Rask now works private security for Halvorsen Shipping, which is a coincidence I don't believe in. Requested formal deposition be scheduled. Denied by the prosecutor's office this morning. Reason given: insufficient corroboration. Insufficient corroboration." A short laugh with nothing warm in it. "She corroborates herself. Twice. Same car, same lamps, same voices. That's not insufficient, that's inconvenient. Somebody above me has decided what happened at that gate, and it isn't what a housekeeper heard with her hands in the dishwater."*

Rain on glass. A wiper squeaking once.

*"Next step: pull registration records for diesel sedans registered in the district, '88 to '94, prior damage to front-left assembly. That'll take weeks through the DMV channels, but there's no other way. If Halvorsen's name comes up on that list, God help me, I'll need to be very sure before I say it out loud."*

The tape ended there. No follow-up cassette in the box. No list. Either Dahl never finished the search, or he finished it and the result went somewhere Orsen very much wanted to find.

They sat down on the kitchen floor, actually sat, back against the cabinets, because standing suddenly seemed like too much architecture. Outside, the sky was doing the thing it does before dawn in Vesterhavn — going the color of the inside of a shell, the harbor lights dissolving one by one.

A diesel sedan. Front-left damage. Registered in the district between 1988 and 1994. It was a needle, but it was a needle in a drawer, not a haystack, and Orsen had spent a decade learning how archives work, how records leak, how the past leaves receipts even when it burns the letters.

And yet.

The suspicion arrived the way damp arrives in old houses — not all at once but everywhere at once, in every wall at the same time. Orsen turned it over and made themselves look at it straight.

Who owned these tapes? Orsen did — legally, auction-stamped, eleven years in the basement. But who had *recorded* them? Dahl. On his own equipment, off the record, including a session with a witness whose testimony the prosecution had buried. These cassettes should have been in an evidence locker or a shredder. Instead they'd surfaced at an estate sale, boxed and lot-numbered, priced to move, and Orsen — of all the people in Vesterhavn, the podcaster, the one with the microphone and the audience and the fresh corpse wearing his coat — had been the only bidder.

Eleven years ago, Orsen had thought it luck. A bargain. Forty-one hours of true-crime gold for the price of a dinner out.

Now, sitting on the cold kitchen tiles with dawn coming up gray over the rooftops, they counted the chain again and found every link suspicious. Someone had kept these tapes safe through Dahl's forced retirement, through his disappearance from town, through Grethe Lund's quiet death, through a decade and a half of curated silence — and then had let them go, at the exact moment, into the exact hands, that would guarantee they'd eventually be heard.

Someone wanted this tape found. The thought arrived fully formed, wearing its own evidence. But wanting a buried voice heard could mean two opposite things, and both of them had teeth. It could mean an ally — someone inside the old machinery, someone like Reyes, slipping truth out through the cracks the way Dahl's address book had slipped into Orsen's pocket. Or it could mean a director. Someone staging the discovery the way the quarry had been staged, dressing the past in clothes tailored to fit, feeding Orsen truth and lies in equal measure until they could no longer tell which hand was feeding.

Grethe Lund's voice was real. Orsen believed her the way you believe a person describing a smell — completely, bodily. The crooked-faced car had come up that drive. Einar Halvorsen had walked a guest down to it and come back alone. Those facts rang like struck metal.

But facts, Orsen had learned this season, were also bait.

They got up off the floor, washed the untouched coffee down the sink, and pulled a legal pad toward them. At the top they wrote: *DIESEL SEDANS, DISTRICT REGISTRY, 1988–1994, FRONT-LEFT DAMAGE.* Underneath: *RASK — constable → Halvorsen security.* Underneath that, after a moment: *WHO SOLD ME THE TAPES?*

That last question had an answer, probably. Estate auctions kept records. Sellers signed manifests. Eleven years was a long time, but paper was patient — paper was the most patient thing in Vesterhavn, more patient than the sea, more patient than the Halvorsens' smiles.

Orsen was reaching for their jacket — their other jacket, the replacement, the one that didn't have a dead man's story sewn into its lining — when the phone buzzed on the counter. Unknown number. Local prefix.

They answered, and a voice they knew said their name.

It was Liv Halvorsen, and she sounded, for the first time in all the polished decades of her public composure, afraid.

"You've been listening to the old tapes," she said. It wasn't a question. "I need to know what you heard. And Orsen — I need you to tell me before my father finds out you have them. Because the last person who heard my father at that gate" — a breath, thin as wire — "didn't die in the sea. And neither, if you're careless, will you."

The line went dead, and Orsen stood in the gray morning light holding the phone, understanding that the archive had just stopped being the past.
