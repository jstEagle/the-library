## 10. Anders Breaks Ranks

The rain had been falling on Vesterhavn for three days, and by the third day the town had begun to smell of it — wet stone and cold salt and the particular mineral tang that rose from the flooded quarry when the water table climbed too high. Anders Halvorsen noticed these things because noticing was all he had left. The drinking had taken everything else: his appetite, his sleep, his talent for looking at himself in mirrors without flinching.

He sat in the back booth of the Skipper's Rest at four in the afternoon, which was early even by his standards, with a glass of aquavit he had ordered an hour ago and had not touched. The glass sat there like a small accusation. Behind the bar, old Preben polished glasses that were already clean, and the radio murmured its afternoon programming — until it didn't, until the voice coming out of it was Orsen Iversen's voice, clipped and rearranged, saying words Anders knew had been cut from their original body like organs from a corpse.

*—Liv Halvorsen has questions to answer about the night Mikkel Brandt—*

Preben reached over and snapped the radio off. The silence afterward was enormous. Two fishermen at the corner table looked at each other and then, carefully, at nothing.

Anders drank the aquavit in one swallow. It burned going down, but not enough. Nothing was enough anymore. He thought about calling his sister, and did not. He thought about calling his father, and did not do that either, because calling Einar Halvorsen meant performing, and Anders had been performing for thirty years and his throat was raw from it.

He thought about the quarry.

He always ended up thinking about the quarry.

* * *

It was full dark when he left the Skipper's Rest, though the dark came early now, the autumn pressing down on the town like a lid. He walked instead of driving, which was a decision his legs made without consulting him, and found himself on Havnegade with the harbor lights smearing across the wet cobbles, and then — because Vesterhavn was a town with only two directions, toward the water or away from it — he found himself outside the guesthouse where Orsen Iversen was staying.

He stood across the street for a long time. Rain dripped off the eaves above him in a slow percussion. Through the window of the ground-floor room he could see a lamp burning, and a figure bent over a table covered in paper, and he understood suddenly, with the terrible clarity that only comes to men who have drunk past steadiness and into something else, that this person across the street was doing what no one in his family had ever dared to do.

Looking. Actually looking.

His father looked away. His sister looked through. The whole town had perfected the art of the averted gaze — thirty years of eyes sliding off the quarry like water off oilcloth. But this stranger, this podcaster with the borrowed clothes and the borrowed name-recognition, was standing in the middle of Vesterhavn pointing a light into the deepest room of the house, and everyone was furious about it, and Anders — God help him — was grateful.

He crossed the street before he could talk himself out of it. His boots splashed. The sound seemed very loud.

He knocked. There was a long moment during which he imagined the door never opening, during which he rehearsed retreats, and then the lock turned and Orsen Iversen stood in the doorway with a pen still behind their ear and an expression that moved from surprise to wariness in the space of a breath.

"Mr. Halvorsen," they said.

"Anders." His voice came out wrong, gravelly, too loud for the narrow doorway. "Just Anders is fine. May I—" He gestured vaguely at the room behind them, at the papers, at the whole apparatus of excavation. "Five minutes. I need five minutes and then you'll never see me again."

Orsen studied him. He could feel the study happening, feel himself being read the way he'd seen his father read contracts — looking for the clause hidden in the margins. Whatever they found, they stepped back and let him in.

* * *

The room smelled of coffee gone cold and old paper. There were documents everywhere, laid out in careful rows, and Anders made himself not look at them, because he knew some of them would be photographs and he was not sure what his face would do. Orsen cleared a chair without comment. He sat. His knees cracked. Somewhere outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the roof.

"You've been drinking," Orsen said. Not an accusation. An observation, almost gentle.

"Since lunch." He laughed, and hated the sound. "Since before lunch, if we're honest. Since the radio, really. Someone played me your voice today saying things you didn't say. Did you know that's possible? That a man can be dismantled by an edit?"

"I know exactly how that's possible." Something moved behind Orsen's eyes — anger, old and banked. "Your family heard it too."

"My father heard it at breakfast and said nothing. That's how you know it landed." Anders put his hands flat on his knees to stop them moving. "I didn't come here about the radio."

"No?"

"No." He breathed. The room was very warm and very small and the papers on the table seemed to lean toward him, listening. "I came here because I'm tired. Thirty years tired. And because you're going to keep digging whether I talk to you or not, and there are two ways this ends. One where you find things out from strangers and archives and dead women's tapes, and one where—" He stopped. Started again. "I never believed him."

Orsen went very still. "Never believed who?"

"My father. About that night." The words came out of him like teeth being pulled, one at a time, each with its little root of blood. "The story we told. The story we *all* told — the dinner running long, Mikkel leaving angry, the roads home dark, nobody seeing anything. I was twenty-two years old and my father sat us down the next morning, Liv and me, at the kitchen table before the police came, and he told us what happened. Not asked. *Told.* And then he told it again to Inspector Dahl, and again to the second policeman, and by the third telling it was smooth as river stone, and I remember thinking — I was drunk then too, younger kind of drunk, scared kind of drunk — I remember thinking, *he's had practice. Nobody tells a story that well the first time.*"

"And you said nothing. For thirty years."

The question hung there, and Anders let it, because it deserved to hang. He looked at his hands. His father's hands, reproduced faithfully, the same heavy knuckles, the same signet ring — no. Not the same ring. His own ring sat in a drawer at home; he hadn't worn it since the body came up out of the water wearing a dead man's history under its ribs. He'd read about the ring. Everyone who mattered had read about the ring.

"I said nothing," he agreed quietly, "because my father told me what saying something would cost. Not in those words. My father doesn't threaten. He *prices* things. He sat at that table and he talked about the business, about the yards, about the forty families whose Christmas dinners come out of Halvorsen Marine, and about our name, which in Vesterhavn is worth more than the business, and he did all of it without once saying *be quiet*, and both Liv and I understood perfectly." He looked up. "You want to know the worst part? It worked. It worked so well that after a few years I couldn't tell anymore whether I believed him or just needed to. Memory is loyal, Mr.— Orsen. Memory takes its master's side."

The rain intensified briefly against the window, a fistful of gravel thrown at the glass, then eased.

"What changed?" Orsen asked. "Why now?"

Anders almost laughed. What changed. Where to begin. The jacket on the corpse. The autopsy report circulating through the town's bloodstream like poison. The way his father had gone from granite to something harder and more brittle, something that might crack rather than bend. The way Liv had started looking at him across dinner tables with an expression he couldn't parse — warning, or permission, or plea.

"A man came out of a quarry wearing a mystery," he said. "Thirty years we lived with an empty grave and a closed file, and it was almost — I won't say comfortable, but it was *shaped*. We knew its edges. And now someone has taken the shape apart, and underneath it there's a body, and the body is wearing—" He stopped. "You know what it's wearing. Everyone knows what it's wearing. Half the town thinks you killed him yourself for the ratings."

"I'm aware."

"The other half thinks my father did. And the terrible thing, the thing I can't say at my own table—" His voice dropped, and he leaned forward, and the alcohol and the fear and the thirty years all came up together in him like floodwater. "The terrible thing is that when I heard he was dead, Mikkel Brandt, found after all this time — my first feeling wasn't horror. It was *relief*. Because a body means an ending. A body means the story finally gets told by someone other than my father." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "What kind of son feels relief?"

"The kind who's been carrying something that isn't his."

Anders lowered his hands. Orsen was watching him with an expression he couldn't fully read — not pity, he'd have resented pity, but something adjacent to it, something patient.

"If I talk," Anders said, "really talk, on the record, in whatever medium you use to take people apart — I lose everything. You understand that? Not risk losing. *Lose.* My father will disown me inside the hour. The board will follow him — half those men owe their seats to his favors. The business goes, the house goes, the name goes. In Vesterhavn the name is the house is the business is the man. I'd be a stranger in the town I was born in, at fifty-two, with nothing but the truth, which pays no dividends." He shook his head slowly. "I've done the arithmetic a thousand times since I saw your light on. Truth costs everything I have. Silence has cost me everything I am. And I can't decide anymore which of those is the poorer estate."

The clock on the nightstand ticked. Down in the street a car passed, tires hissing on wet stone, and its lights swept the ceiling and were gone.

"There's something else," Anders said. "A reason I came tonight specifically, and not last week or next week. And I need you to understand that I don't know what it means. I only know it frightened me."

Orsen waited.

"Liv," he said. "My sister. Three nights ago she came to my house after midnight, soaked through, no coat, wouldn't come inside — stood on my step in the rain like a ghost. And she asked me one question. She asked me whether I remembered what Mikkel Brandt said to Father at the dinner, before he left. Whether I remembered *the exact words*." He swallowed. "I don't. I was at the far end of the table, the band was playing, I caught maybe a sentence. But Liv asked like a woman checking a lock. Like she needed to know whether anyone else had heard what she heard. And then she said, *Don't ever repeat this, but Father followed him out. Father followed him out and was gone forty minutes, and when he came back his cufflink was missing and his hands were shaking, and he told the band to play louder.*"

The room had gone very quiet. Even the rain seemed to have drawn back to listen.

"She never told the police that," Orsen said. It wasn't a question.

"She never told *anyone* that. Until three nights ago, at my door, in the rain, when the whole machinery started moving again." Anders stood, unsteadily, and Orsen rose with him. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and turned back. "I haven't decided anything tonight. I want you to know that. I'm a coward in good weather and worse in bad, and tomorrow morning the fear will be back and I'll probably regret every word. But you should have this much from someone inside the walls: my father's story has held for thirty years not because it's true but because it's *load-bearing*. Pull it out and the whole house comes down — the business, the family, the town's whole idea of itself. And there are people, powerful people, who will do a great deal to keep that story standing. Watch your brother, Orsen. Watch whoever feeds you. In my experience, the most dangerous thing in Vesterhavn isn't a lie. It's a truth given to you at exactly the moment someone wants you to have it."

He stepped out into the rain. It fell on him kindly, cold and clean, washing the taste of the aquavit and the confession together out of his mouth. He did not look back at the lit window. He walked home the long way, along the harbor, past the dark hump of the quarry road, and at his own door he stood a moment in the wet dark, listening to the water somewhere below the town holding its dead and its secrets in equal suspension.

Inside, he took the signet ring out of the drawer and set it on the table where he could see it from bed.

He slept, eventually. Badly. And dreamed of a dinner table thirty years long, at the far end of which a chair stood empty, and at the near end of which his father raised a glass and said, *to thirty untroubled years* — while beneath the floorboards, soft as tide, something knocked.
