## 7. The Witness Who Chose to Vanish

The caravan park at Gullwrack Point had the particular sadness of places built for holidays that never quite arrived. Forty-odd white caravans in rows, grass gone brown at the edges, a chip van that opened only on Saturdays according to a sign faded past legibility. The sea beyond the sea wall was the colour of old pewter, and it made the same sound it had made twelve years ago, which was no sound at all, just a long grey breathing.

Astrid walked the rows with his collar up and the fire's smell still faintly in his hair, though he'd washed twice. The investigators had found the button by noon — brass, stamped with a crest, from the estate livery — and by one o'clock Astrid had understood two things: the Verdicts had stopped being patient, and whatever he did next had to be done before they remembered how to be.

The third thing he understood came when he saw the caravan at the end of row six.

It was nothing. That was the point of it. A boxy white unit with a rusted awning and a plastic flamingo by the step, identical to every other caravan except for one detail Astrid had spent thirty years learning to notice: the curtains were drawn against a morning with no sun to keep out. People who wanted light kept their curtains open. People who wanted to see who was coming kept them closed.

He knocked anyway. Then he stood back, hands visible, the way you do when you've spent your life making people open doors and know exactly what a hand near a pocket looks like.

Nothing. A gull screamed somewhere over the wall like a bad omen with good timing.

"Tomas," Astrid said to the door. "I'm not police anymore. I'm not anything anymore. My shop burned down last night because someone thinks I know where you are." He paused. "Which means they think you're worth burning for. You should hear the rest."

The lock turned. Not all the way — a chain, an inch of gap, one eye.

Tomas Reeve had been twenty-four the last time Astrid saw him, a wiry groundskeeper's boy with a stutter and a habit of looking at your shoes. The man in the gap was fifty pounds heavier, grey at the temples, with the reddened nose of a man who drank to make the mornings quieter. But the eyes hadn't changed. They were still doing arithmetic — calculating exits, weighing odds, arriving always at the same losing sum.

"You're the detective," Reeve said. His voice was roughened but the stutter was gone; twelve years of talking only to himself had sanded it smooth. "The one who quit."

"I didn't quit. I retired badly." Astrid held up the envelope. "You wrote me a letter once. Or someone wrote it for you. *The witness is still breathing.* Was that you?"

A long silence. The chain didn't move.

"It was a mistake," Reeve said finally. "I'd had a few. I posted it and then I sat up all night waiting for a car to come." He almost smiled. "It never did. Twelve years, and they don't even watch anymore. That's the worst part, Mr. Eastwick. Being important enough to hide and not important enough to remember."

"Then let me remember you properly." Astrid kept his voice level, unhurried, the voice he'd used on frightened witnesses for three decades. "Come outside. Or let me in. Or talk through the gap — I don't care. But Ivo Verdict has been dead since he was eleven years old, and everyone in this county has agreed to pretend it was an accident, and last night someone burned my bookshop to keep that pretense alive. I am done being polite about it."

The eye studied him. Then the door closed, the chain rattled, and it opened again.

* * *

Inside, the caravan was painfully tidy. Dishes washed and racked. Bed made with hospital corners. On the small table, a half-finished jigsaw of a lighthouse, five hundred pieces, maybe forty placed. A man arranging a life into small controllable tasks, one edge piece at a time.

Reeve poured tea from a thermos without asking, and his hands were steady until they weren't, a fine tremor starting at the third cup.

"You want to know why I ran," he said.

"I know why you ran. I want to know what you saw."

Reeve laughed — a short, broken sound with no humour in it. "Everyone always wants the seeing. Nobody asks about the carrying." He sat down across from the jigsaw, wrapped both hands around his cup as if the tea might testify on his behalf. "All right. All right. It was the fourteenth of June. Hot — proper hot, the kind we get twice a decade. I was mending fence along the river bend below the estate, the stretch behind the boathouse."

Astrid said nothing. He had learned long ago that silence was a chair you offered people, and most of them, eventually, sat down in it.

"The boy was there. Ivo. And the other one — Callum." Reeve's mouth worked around the name like it was a stone. "Edmund's son. They were down at the water with a canoe they weren't supposed to take out. I heard them arguing before I saw them. Ivo wanted to go back in. Callum wouldn't let him have the paddle." He stared into his tea. "They were kids. Eleven, twelve. It should have been nothing. Kids fight over paddles."

"But?"

"But Callum pushed him." The words came out flat, rehearsed by twelve years of private repetition, worn smooth as sea glass. "Not hard. That's the thing nobody understands. It wasn't violence. It was a shove — the kind brothers give each other. And Ivo slipped on the wet boards and went in sideways, and he hit his head on the pontoon, and he didn't come up." Reeve's breath shuddered out of him. "And Callum stood there. For I'd say ninety seconds. Just standing. Looking at the water. And then he started screaming for help, and by the time anyone got there, I'd already gone in after the boy."

"And you pulled him out."

"Too late to matter." Reeve's eyes came up, wet and furious at himself. "I did CPR. I broke one of his ribs doing it. I know what a drowned boy feels like in your hands, Mr. Eastwick. I've felt it every night for twelve years. So yes. I saw it. And I told the first constable on scene exactly what I told you, word for word, and I watched him write it down."

Astrid felt the cold arrive in his chest, the old familiar cold, the one that meant a case had stopped being paper. "And then?"

"And then Edmund Verdict shook my hand at the funeral." Reeve set the cup down with exaggerated care, as if sudden movement might shatter something other than crockery. "Shook my hand. Thanked me for my efforts. And two days later a man came to my flat — not police, worse than police, a solicitor — and laid out photographs on my table. Photographs of my sister. Ellen. She was nineteen. Working summers at the Verdict estate hotel, saving for nursing school." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Photographs of her coming off night shifts. Getting into her car. Walking to the bus stop. Dozens of them. And the solicitor said, very politely, that young women driving alone on dark roads had such bad luck in this county. Car trouble. Weather. And that it would be a tragedy if Ellen's luck turned, so soon after the family had suffered theirs."

The gulls cried outside. The sea breathed its grey breath.

"They didn't threaten me," Reeve said. "Threats you can report. Threats you can fight. They threatened *her*, and they did it with photographs and a smile, and I understood that I had a choice between my conscience and my sister's spine, and I chose—" He stopped. Started again. "I moved. Changed my name. Got work under the table, then above it, then taxes under an alias because some clerk somewhere decided to keep me alive on paper — I never knew who, I swear to God I never knew who. And every year on the fourteenth of June I drink too much and I look at that water—" he nodded toward the sea wall, absurdly, as if the river were in the room— "and I tell myself I did the right thing. And every year I believe it a little less."

"Cowardice," Astrid said quietly.

"Yes." Reeve didn't flinch. "Say it. Everyone's entitled to one honest sentence about themselves, and that's mine. I was a coward. But here's what I need you to understand, Detective, because it's the only thing I have left to give: cowardice isn't the absence of love. Sometimes it's love with nowhere else to go. I would do it again tomorrow. That's the damnation of it. Put my sister in those photographs and I'd stay silent another twelve years, and I'll hate myself in a new way every single morning, and I will still choose her." He looked up, and his eyes were clear now, scoured clean. "Understand me. Don't forgive me. There's a difference, and I'd rather you understood."

Astrid understood. That was the terrible part. He thought of a case file he'd closed twelve years ago with a signature and a shrug, because the ruling was accident and the family was powerful and his own retirement was eighteen months away and the water was cold and the boy was already dead and what good could one stubborn man do? He had chosen comfort over courage with a fountain pen, in triplicate, and called it procedure.

"I'm not forgiving you," Astrid said. "But I need your statement. Sworn. Written. Signed. Because there's a missing page in Ivo's autopsy — toxicology — and a coroner's assistant who never signed the final report, and a constable named Nell Brandt who just lost her badge for following the money, and a confession letter sitting in my coat pocket written by a girl who waited twelve years for someone brave enough to read it aloud." He leaned forward. "Ellen is safe, Tomas. She's a nurse in Ashcombe. Married, two children. I checked before I drove here. The photographs stopped mattering the day she stopped working for them — and the day I put your statement in front of a crowd, they'll stop mattering entirely, because men like Edmund Verdict can burn shops and bury pages, but they cannot un-hear ten thousand witnesses."

Reeve was silent for a long time. He looked at the jigsaw — the lighthouse barely begun, the sky pieces all the same, the task designed to fill years and failing at it.

"If I sign," he said, "there's no going back to this."

"No. There isn't."

"And if it fails — if your reading aloud doesn't work, if the town closes ranks the way it always closes ranks —"

"Then I fail with you," Astrid said. "Publicly. Loudly. In front of everyone. I've spent twelve years failing privately and calling it peace. I'd like to try the other kind."

Reeve laughed again, and this time there was something in it — not joy, nothing so generous, but the first crack of daylight through a long-sealed room. He went to a drawer, took out a pad of paper and a pen, and sat down at the table among the scattered lighthouse pieces.

"Start anywhere," Astrid said. "Start with the paddle."

Reeve bent his head and began to write, and the pen scratched in the little caravan like something hatching.

Outside, far down the row, a black car slowed at the park gate. It didn't turn in. It idled there for a full minute, engine ticking, and then it pulled away — but Astrid had seen the glint of glass, the shape of a man watching, and he understood that the choice had already been taken out of all their hands.

By tonight, Edmund Verdict would know his witness had spoken.

There was one gala left on the town calendar. One room. One microphone.

Astrid picked up his coat.
