## 1. The Retainer

The trouble with dusting a bookshop is that the books resent it.

I'd learned this over four years of losing money at Yarrow's on Wren Lane, where the stock had settled into the particular melancholy of volumes nobody wants: three shelves of local histories no tourist cared to read, a wall of mid-century thrillers with cracked spines and yellowed heroes, and an entire crate — God help me — of self-published memoirs from a man who had walked the length of the Severn estuary twice and needed everyone to know about it both times. I ran a cloth along their spines and they gave me the silent treatment, which was fair. I'd done nothing for them either.

Outside, October was doing its annual impression of a town holding its breath. Salt Hollow in autumn is a place where the light comes down at an angle, as if even the sun can't quite commit. The harbour sat low and grey, the gulls argued about something that would never be resolved, and up on the ridge road you could just see, if you squinted past the church spire, the pale scar of the old quarry where the town's better angels went to be mined and its worse ones went to fall.

I wasn't thinking about the quarry. I want that on the record. I was thinking about margins, and how a bookshop's are thinner than the paper they're printed on, and whether I could justify turning the heating on before November or whether I'd simply wear my coat indoors like a woman who has given up on dignity but not on warmth.

The bell over the door did its small bright thing at eleven minutes past ten, and a man came in sideways, the way people do when they're not sure they want to be anywhere.

He was sixty-ish, silver at the temples, wearing a coat that cost more than my quarterly rent and carrying a briefcase he held slightly away from his body, like evidence. He looked around the shop with the expression of a man checking a room for exits, found two — door and window, both inadequate — and then found me.

"Ms. Yarrow." Not a question. He knew. That was the first wrong note. People don't come to a failing bookshop knowing the name of the failing bookseller unless they've come for something other than books.

"We're mostly fiction," I said. "Some poetry. The memoirs are non-negotiable."

"Emrys Yarrow," he said again, as if repetition were a form of flattery. "Formerly of the Salt Hollow constabulary. Twenty-two years. You closed the Halloway case. And the one at the cannery, the year after."

"And now I sell books," I said. "Badly. You have the advantage of me."

"Halloran. Peter Halloran." He said his own name the way men say it when they expect it to mean something. In this town it did — Halloran & Prentice, solicitors, three generations of handling other people's money and griefs and occasionally, if the gossip held, making the two interchangeable. "Might we speak privately?"

"You're speaking privately now. This is the most private a bookshop gets on a Tuesday."

His eyes flicked to the window, to the empty street, back to me. "Perhaps somewhere without glass."

There it was. I set down my cloth. Four years retired, and some part of me — the part I kept telling myself had been amputated cleanly, no complications, minimal scarring — sat up like a dog hearing its name.

* * *

We took the two chairs by the radiator, which ticked and knocked because I hadn't bled it, another item for the list of things I was failing at. Halloran perched rather than sat, briefcase flat across his knees like a shield held backwards.

"I'll be direct," he said.

"That would be novel. Go on."

"My client requires certain assurances regarding an event scheduled for the seventeenth of November."

"What event?"

"A dinner."

"A dinner," I repeated. "You've retained a detective — or whatever it is I'm meant to be these days — to attend a dinner?"

"To prevent a murder."

The radiator knocked. Somewhere out front a lorry changed gears on the ridge road, a sound like the town clearing its throat.

"Whose murder?" I asked, because there is always a right question and it's usually the obvious one.

"Aldous Vane's."

I let that sit. Everyone in Salt Hollow knew Aldous Vane the way coastal towns know their lighthouses: as a fixed point, a benefaction, a name on the new wing of the cottage hospital and the restored bandstand and the scholarship fund that had sent half a generation of clever kids off to universities they couldn't otherwise have touched. Vane House above the east cliff, the anniversary gala every November that the *Courier* covered like a coronation. Forty years married to his first wife, God rest her, twelve to the second. A man so thoroughly beloved that disliking him felt less like an opinion than a symptom.

"Aldous Vane," I said carefully, "is going to be murdered. At his own anniversary dinner. By someone. For reasons unknown. And your client knows this how?"

"My client doesn't know it. My client fears it."

"Fears it on what basis?"

Halloran opened the briefcase. His hands were steady; his jaw wasn't. There's a distinction worth learning — hands lie all the time, trained into stillness by boardrooms and courtrooms, but the jaw tells the truth if you watch it long enough. His was working at something.

He drew out a document in a stiff cream folder and laid it on the little table between us without letting go of it, the way you might set down a bird you weren't sure was dead.

"I drew up a new will last month," he said. "For Mr. Vane. Under instruction."

"Solicitors do."

"The will supersedes all previous instruments. It redistributes certain — significant — portions of the estate."

"To whom?"

And here he did something interesting. He looked at the window again. At the door. At the crate of Severn memoirs, as though hoping one of them might walk the estuary a third time and carry this conversation away on its back.

"To a beneficiary," he said, "whose identity I am not at liberty to disclose."

"You drew up a will naming a beneficiary you won't name, for a client whose fears you won't explain, and you've come to a woman who sells second-hand books to prevent a murder that hasn't been threatened, planned, or evidenced. Have I got the shape of it?"

"You have the shape of it," he said, with something horribly like relief, as though I'd summarised his life.

"Mr. Halloran. Why me? You know what I was. You must also know why I stopped being it. There are firms in Ashcombe that do security assessments. There's the police, such as they are. There's any number of ex-servicemen who'd take a retainer to stand in a corner looking large at a dinner party."

"There are," he agreed. "None of them are you."

"Flattering and uninformative. My favourite combination."

He leaned forward. Up close I could see the tiredness under the tailoring, the particular grey that lives in the faces of men who keep secrets professionally and have recently been handed one too heavy for the shelf. "Ms. Yarrow. I cannot tell you what I know, because privilege binds me. But I can tell you what I don't know, which is nearly everything, and which frightens me more. I have practised law in this town for thirty-one years. I have drawn wills for the living and probates for the dead and I have never once — never — put a name on a beneficiary line that I could not verify. Last month I did. And the name—" his voice did something small and involuntary, a hitch, a swallowed syllable— "the name is a name I know to be impossible."

"Impossible how?"

"Dead," he said. "Eleven years dead. No grave. No estate. No record anyone will confirm. And Aldous Vane, who is not a sentimental man, who has never in forty years of philanthropy given a penny without a plaque attached, has left the better part of himself to a ghost." He straightened his cuffs. They didn't need it. "My client believes that when this becomes known — and it will become known, Ms. Yarrow, documents have a way of surfacing — someone close to him will decide that murder is cheaper than exposure. I believe my client may be right. I believe the dinner is where it will happen, because that is where everyone will be, and everyone will be watching each other, and no one will be watching him."

The radiator knocked again, patient as a metronome. I became aware that I had stopped listening to my own scepticism somewhere around *no grave*, and that the rest of me was leaning forward, and that leaning forward is a thing the body remembers even when the mind has filed the whole profession under *closed*.

"Why not go to the police?" I said. "You know them. Better than I do, these days."

Something crossed his face then — fast, gone before I could name it, but I caught the shape of it, and the shape was fear, and the fear had a direction. It wasn't pointed at the murderer-who-didn't-exist. It was pointed inward. Toward the police. Toward the town. Toward, possibly, me.

"The police," he said mildly, "are a public institution. What my client requires is discretion."

"What my client requires," I said, "is a psychiatrist and a decent locksmith. What *you* require, Mr. Halloran, I genuinely cannot tell."

He opened the briefcase again and produced an envelope. Not a cheque — cash, banded, thick enough to make its own argument. He set it on the table between us next to the cream folder, and the two objects sat together like a question and its bribe.

"Six months' fees," he said. "In advance. Whatever you conclude — whether you stand guard at that dinner or tell me by Friday that my client is a foolish old man frightened of his own paperwork — the money is yours. I only ask that you look. Look properly, the way you used to look. Before the seventeenth of November."

Five weeks. I looked at the envelope. I thought about the heating, and the rent, and the crate of memoirs, and the fact that pride is the one luxury a failing bookshop can't stock but apparently couldn't stop me buying either.

"This is absurd," I told him.

"It is entirely absurd," Halloran agreed, standing, gathering his coat around him like a man re-armouring. "That is rather the point. Absurd things are the only ones that frighten me anymore. The sensible ones I can bill for."

At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and didn't turn around.

"One more thing, since you'll find it anyway and I'd rather you heard it from me. The beneficiary line. The impossible name." A breath. "It isn't initials. It isn't a cipher. It's a full legal name, witnessed, sworn, signed by a man who knew exactly what he was writing."

"Which is?"

"Mara Quill," he said, and the bell over the door announced his exit like a verdict, and the cold came in off the harbour behind him and stayed.

* * *

I stood in my shop for a long time after, holding the envelope, not counting it. Counting would have made it real, and I wanted a few more minutes of unreality, the pleasant fog where a solicitor says a name and it means nothing, where a word is just a word, four letters and a sound, and does not reach down through four years of careful, deliberate, expensive forgetting and pull.

Mara Quill.

Eleven years.

The same eleven years since Daniel fell from the quarry ridge on a Tuesday in June, on a path we'd walked a hundred times, in weather so mild the coroner had remarked on it — *a fine day for walking*, he'd said, and everyone had nodded, and I had nodded, and the file had closed with a neatness I had personally verified, because I was good at my job, because verifying was what I did, because the alternative was a question I could not afford to ask while the ground was still soft over him.

I had sealed that door myself. I'd stood in this very shop with a crowbar of routine and shut it, shelf by shelf, invoice by invoice, until the hinges rusted and I could almost pretend the room behind it was empty.

Now a nervous solicitor had walked in sideways and hung a name on the outside of it.

I put the envelope in the till without counting it, which was a lie I told myself about pride. Then I turned the sign to CLOSED, which I almost never did before noon, and went upstairs to find the box I had promised myself I would never open, telling myself all the way up the stairs that I was only going to look, that looking was not opening, that a detective could look at anything and remain untouched.

The lie held for exactly as long as it took to cut the tape.

Inside, under the certificates and the commendation letters and the photograph of Daniel squinting into harbour light with his hair doing what it always did, lay my copy of the closed file on the death of Daniel Yarrow — and I already knew, with the cold certainty that arrives ahead of its own reasons, that I was going to read every page, and that one of them was going to ruin me.
