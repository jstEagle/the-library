## 2. A Will Without a Grave

The archive room at the back of Halloran & Prentice smelled the way all solicitors' archive rooms smell: of cold paper, warm radiators, and money that had been sitting still so long it had developed opinions. A green-shaded lamp. A table scarred by a century of fountain pens. Two chairs, one of which was clearly meant for clients and one of which was clearly not.

Halloran gestured me into the client chair with the courtesy of a man who had rehearsed this. He'd changed his tie since yesterday — navy instead of grey, as if formality were a dial he could turn up when the situation required gravity. It did nothing for him. He still looked like a man who slept badly in a good bed.

"Tea?" he offered.

"I'd rather have the will."

"Yes." He said it the way people say *yes* to dentistry. "I thought you might."

He unlocked a drawer — an actual key, an actual lock, in an age when most documents lived in clouds and could be stolen by anyone with a grudge and a password — and laid a single folder on the table between us. Cream card, tied with tape. The tape was new. Everything else about it looked old, which told me the age was deliberate. Documents, like alibis, are dressed for the occasion.

"The revised testament of Aldous Vane," Halloran said. "Executed on the fourteenth of March. I drew it. I witnessed it. My clerk witnessed it. It is, I assure you, entirely valid."

"And entirely secret."

"He insisted on secrecy. Mr. Vane is a private man about certain matters."

"Mr. Vane is a public man about everything else. There's a wing at the hospital with his name on it. There's a bench by the war memorial with his name on it. There's probably a urinal somewhere with his name on it." I untied the tape. "A man like that doesn't rewrite his will in secret unless what's in it would embarrass him or frighten someone. Which is it?"

Halloran didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer.

I opened the folder.

I've read a lot of wills. Retired detectives read wills the way retired doctors read obituaries — professionally, hungrily, checking for the thing everyone else missed. Most wills are tedious instruments of love and grievance: the house to the son who visited, the resentment distributed in equal shares among the children who didn't. This one began tediously enough. Odile Vane, the second wife, secured for life. Casper, the son, provided for — though the drafting had a careful, lawyerly chill to it, the kind of provision that says *here is your money, now go away* without ever using those words. Bequests to charities whose letterheads I recognized from the town's every gala and raffle. Mrs. Ansel, the housekeeper, received a sum generous enough to be either gratitude or hush money; with servants, those are often the same document.

And then, near the end, in the clause that governs the residue — everything left over, the true weight of a fortune built over fifty years — a name I didn't recognize.

*Mara Quill.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because my eyes had decided they were mistaken and I wanted to give them a chance to correct themselves before something worse happened.

"Mara Quill," I said. "Who is she?"

"That," said Halloran, "is rather the question."

"You drafted this. You know who she is."

"I drafted it under instruction." His voice had gone carefully flat, the way voices do when they're carrying something heavy and don't want to spill it. "My client's instructions were precise. Complete. I was given the name, the address for service, the terms. I was not given — nor did I ask for — the story behind them. Solicitors who ask too many questions about their clients' affairs tend to find their clients' affairs asking questions about them."

"So you're telling me you took instructions from Aldous Vane, wrote a dead stranger into the most valuable clause of his will, and never once wondered why."

"I wondered." For the first time since he'd walked into my bookshop, some of the polish came off him. What was underneath looked older, and frightened, and tired. "That's rather why you're here, Ms. Yarrow."

I set the folder down. Not slammed — I want that on the record, because what I wanted to do was slam it, and the fact that I didn't should count for something in whatever ledger is kept on me. "Explain to me how a name in a will constitutes a threat to anyone's life. Because from where I'm sitting, this looks like an old man's sentimentality. Mistresses get written into wills all the time. So do estranged daughters, loyal gardeners, and cats. None of it requires a detective."

"The beneficiary clause isn't the problem," Halloran said. "The date is."

"The date?"

"The will was executed on the fourteenth of March." He leaned forward and turned the folder toward me, tapping one long finger against the execution page, beside the signatures. "Look at the attestation clause. Read it aloud, if you like. I'll wait."

I read it. I am a slow reader of legal documents by training and temperament; speed is where mistakes hide. And there, in the boilerplate, in the same ink, in the same hand as everything else:

*Signed and acknowledged by the Testator in our presence, this fourteenth day of March, at Ashcombe House —*

"Ashcombe House," I said. "Vane's estate. Fine. Old men are allowed to sign things at home."

"Ms. Yarrow." Halloran's finger moved from the attestation clause down to the signature block itself, where Aldous Vane's name stood in a strong, slanted, confident hand — the signature of a man who knew exactly what he was signing and had signed things like it a thousand times. "Mr. Vane has been in Lisbon since the second of February. He returns next week. He has not been at Ashcombe House since Christmas."

The room got very quiet. The radiator ticked. Somewhere upstairs a phone rang twice and stopped, as if someone had answered it and then thought better of speaking.

"That's impossible," I said. "If the signature's forged, the will's worthless, and worthless wills don't need protecting—"

"The signature is not forged." Halloran said it with the weary certainty of a man repeating himself to himself. "I watched him sign it. I sat across the table from Aldous Vane on the fourteenth of March and watched him take the pen in his own hand. I am not mistaken about the man. I've known him thirty years. I drew up his first marriage settlement. I would know."

"Then he was here."

"He was in Lisbon. I have seen the photographs. His wife sends them to half the town."

"Then someone who looks like him—"

"Ms. Yarrow." He closed his eyes briefly, the way men do when they're about to say the thing they've been avoiding saying since they walked through your door. "Either Aldous Vane was in two places at once, or someone wanted very badly for this will to exist exactly as it exists — valid, witnessed, dated, unimpeachable — and arranged for it to be so. And whichever of those is true, that person believes the will matters enough to commit fraud over it. People who commit fraud over paper will, in my experience, escalate."

"To murder."

"To murder," he agreed quietly. "At the anniversary dinner. Twenty-six guests, a marquee, a string quartet, and a man who will stand up in front of all of them and announce — I don't know what he'll announce. That's the point. I don't know. But the will names a beneficiary no one has ever heard of, executed under circumstances that cannot be explained, and Mr. Vane intends to make some kind of declaration that night. Something he's been building toward. You may draw your own conclusions about the connection. I have drawn mine, and mine keep me awake."

I looked down at the name again. *Mara Quill.* Even the shape of it on the page was strange — two small blunt words sitting in the middle of all that legal architecture like a sparrow in a cathedral.

"Who is she?" I asked again. "You must have an address for service. Everyone in a will gets one."

Halloran hesitated. Then he slid a second sheet across the table — a covering memorandum, typed, unsigned. An address in a coastal town forty miles north. A cottage name. *Harbourlight.*

I wrote it down in my notebook because writing things down is a habit older than my grief, and then I asked the question I should have asked first, the one any competent detective asks before anything else:

"When did Mara Quill die?"

And Halloran, who had spent our entire acquaintance being careful, was suddenly, terribly precise.

"Eleven years ago," he said. "June. Drowned. It was in the local paper. Small piece. They never found the body."

* * *

I want to describe what happened inside me in the next few seconds, and I'm going to fail, because the English language was invented by people who had never had the experience. But I'll try.

There is a filing system in every investigator's head. Mine is unusually rigorous — I built it myself, out of necessity, back when my memory was evidence and sloppiness could cost someone their liberty. Cases go in drawers. Drawers have labels. Labels are final. *Solved*, *closed*, *not mine*. Eleven years ago I had put a drawer away so hard I'd welded it shut, and I had not opened it since, and I had built a whole life — a shop, a routine, a careful small existence with the sharp edges sanded off — specifically so that I would never have to.

Eleven years ago, in June, my husband Daniel went out for a walk along the quarry ridge above Harrow Bay and did not come home. They found him at the bottom three days later. The fall was ruled accidental. The file was closed in five weeks. I signed off on the closing summary myself — I was still serving then, still trusted, still the detective everyone brought the awkward cases to — and I signed it because the evidence supported it and because the alternative was a word I couldn't afford to think yet.

*Accident.* June. Eleven years ago.

Mara Quill. June. Eleven years ago.

Two deaths in the same month, in the same year, in the same stretch of coastline, and one of them wearing my husband's face.

"Cold in here," I said, because my mouth needed to say something while the rest of me caught up. "Is there a window?"

"There's a radiator—"

"Is there a *window*."

There was. I opened it onto a lightwell that smelled of rain and old stone, and I breathed until my hands stopped doing the thing hands do when the body knows something before the mind consents to know it.

Behind me, Halloran said, gently, "Ms. Yarrow? Are you quite—"

"Give me a moment."

I gave myself several moments. I counted the bricks in the opposite wall of the lightwell. Forty-one. I catalogued the sounds: traffic, a gull, the radiator, Halloran's breathing, which had gone careful and shallow, the breathing of a man watching someone stand near an edge.

Here is what I understood, standing at that window with the cold air on my face: the coincidence was already too large. One drowned woman, eleven years dead, surfacing in the will of the richest man in the county, in the same month my husband fell — that was not coincidence. Coincidence is a load-bearing wall in detective work; you trust it more than you'd like and it holds more often than you'd expect. But this wasn't coincidence. This was architecture. Someone had built these two deaths into the same structure, deliberately, and I had just been handed the floor plan.

And underneath the understanding, colder than the air from the lightwell, was the other thing. The thing I had come here to avoid knowing and now knew.

This was not a job.

Jobs have boundaries. Jobs end. You take the retainer, you work the problem, you deliver the report, you invoice for expenses, and you go home to your unsold stock and your single mug and your life with the edges sanded off. That is what I had told myself yesterday, taking Halloran's absurd envelope of cash out of pride, telling myself it was six months' rent and nothing more.

But pride is just grief wearing a suit, and I had known it even then. Some part of me — the part that had read the name *Yarrow* on the cheque and felt the hook go in — had known exactly what this was. A door. A door I had sealed shut eleven years ago with a signature on a closing summary, and doors like that don't stay sealed. They wait. They wait for a nervous solicitor with a retainer and a name on a page, and they open inward, and everything you buried comes walking through with its shoes on.

"Mara Quill," I said to the lightwell. "Tell me about her."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean everything. Age, occupation, last known address, next of kin, the newspaper piece, the inquest — was there an inquest? There's always an inquest for an unidentified body. Who identified her? Where's she buried?" I turned around. "You're a solicitor. Death is your business. You know how this works."

"Missing at sea," Halloran said. "Presumed drowned. No body recovered, so — presumption of death order, seven years later, obtained quietly. I believe her sister applied. There was no estate to speak of. She was—" he consulted some internal index, "—an accountant, I think. Or a bookkeeper. She worked in town, briefly. Three years, perhaps four. Kept herself to herself. The paper called her 'a well-liked newcomer.'"

"A well-liked newcomer," I repeated. "Drowned in June. Presumed. No body." I came back to the table and sat down, because my legs had expressed a preference. "And eleven years later she turns up in Aldous Vane's will, in the residuary clause, with an address for service at a cottage called Harbourlight, and you want me to believe this is the first time anyone has connected her to anything."

"I'm not asking you to believe anything," Halloran said. "I'm paying you not to believe things. That's rather the nature of the engagement. Doubt everything, including me. Especially me, if it helps."

"Why especially you?"

"Because I am the one who drew the document," he said simply, "and therefore I am the one with the most obvious motive to lie about it. A good investigator starts with the messenger. Start with me. I'd be disappointed in you if you didn't."

For a moment — the first moment — I almost liked him.

I closed the folder. Tied the tape. My hands were steady now; they always are once the decision's made, whatever the decision is. That's the part nobody tells you about detection, the part the novels leave out: the fear doesn't come before the choice. It comes after, and it stays.

"Terms," I said. "Same as quoted. Daily rate, expenses, and one condition of mine."

"Name it."

"I work it my way. If I decide the trail touches something I need to pull, I pull it, and I don't clear it with you first. Including," I said, looking at him steadily, "if it touches you."

"Including me," Halloran agreed. "Though I'd ask — as a personal favour, unpaid — that you start with the sister."

"Nell Quill," I said. "Yes. She's on my list."

Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice. Surprise, maybe. Or relief. Or the particular expression of a man who has just discovered that the person he hired might be more dangerous than the problem he hired her for.

"You know the sister's name," he said.

"I know everyone's name," I said, standing, putting on my coat. "It's the living I have trouble with."

* * *

The walk back to the bookshop took eleven minutes and I spent all of them not thinking about Daniel, which is to say I spent all of them thinking about nothing else.

It's a discipline, not-thinking-about-Daniel. I've had eleven years of practice. There's a route through the town that avoids the ridge road, a bakery I stopped using because the smell of the bread was his mother's recipe, a whole cartography of avoidance I mapped without ever admitting I was mapping it. And now a solicitor had dropped a name into the middle of the map like a stone into a pond, and the ripples were crossing streets I'd sealed years ago.

*Mara Quill.* I turned the name over as I walked. Quill. A writer's name, a feather's name, a name that belonged in a novel rather than a coroner's report. Had I ever heard it before? I searched backward through eleven years the way you search a house after a burglary — systematically, room by room, touching everything. The inquest file. The witness list. The quarry, the tide tables, the three days before they found him. Daniel's colleagues. Our friends. The constable who took my statement, young and kind and useless, saying *we'll find whoever did this* before correcting himself, mortified, to *we'll establish the circumstances*.

Nothing. The name rang no bells, which worried me more than if it had rung all of them. Because a name that appears nowhere in your memory but sits at the centre of your husband's death month is not an absence. It's a removal. And I was beginning to suspect — with the cold, unwelcome clarity that arrives at the worst possible moments, usually on street corners — that I had personally read a document in which that name appeared, and that some part of me had agreed, eleven years ago, to forget it.

The bell over my shop door announced me to the empty shop, and I stood a moment in the familiar dust-and-paper smell, letting it settle me. Then I went through to the back room, past the till that hadn't rung today, past the shelf of local history that sells four copies a year, to the locked cabinet under the stairs.

Inside the cabinet, in a box file I had not opened in eleven years, was my copy of the file on the death of Daniel Yarrow.

Every officer keeps copies. It's not regulation — it's superstition. We tell ourselves it's for reference, for appeals, for the day the case reopens. Really it's because we can't bear to let go, and because we learned somewhere that files have a way of shrinking, and we want witnesses to the original size.

I carried the box to the table. I made tea I didn't drink. I sat with my hands flat on the cardboard lid for a long time, listening to the clock in the shop tick, feeling the door under my hands — the sealed door, the welded drawer, the whole careful architecture of my last eleven years — and understanding that once I lifted this lid, none of it was going to hold.

Six months' fees, Halloran had paid. Upfront. For a job with no body, no threat, no evidence.

No. That wasn't right anymore, was it. There was a body. There had always been a body. It was just that nobody — least of all me — had ever been allowed to look at it properly.

I opened the box.

The file smelled of 2014 and of the person I had been when I closed it: efficient, grieving, eager to be finished. I turned to the index first, because indexes don't lie — pages get removed, but the index remembers them. Witness statements, scene photographs, pathologist's report, tide data, the closing summary with my signature at the bottom like a period at the end of a sentence.

And there, between *Statement — R. Ferro, DS* and *Scene photographs 1–14*, an entry in the original typewritten list:

*Witness statement — M. Quill, taken at Harrow Bay, 19 June.*

Page forty-one.

I turned to page forty-one.

It wasn't there.

The staple holes were present. The ghost of the page was present — a faint offset shadow of typing on the sheet behind it, transferred by eleven years of pressure, legible in fragments if you tilted it to the lamp. I tilted it. I read what the ghost had to say.

*...states she was walking the cliff path... observed the deceased approximately...*

And then the shadow failed, and the rest was blank paper, and outside the window the afternoon was going grey over the rooftops of a town that had believed a comfortable story for eleven years because I was the one who told it.

I sat there a long while. The tea went cold. The clock ticked. Somewhere in the walls of the shop, a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, the way pipes do, the way houses do, the way the dead do when they finally decide to speak.

Mara Quill had witnessed my husband's death.

Mara Quill had been erased from my file.

And tomorrow morning, I was going to find her sister, and I was going to start pulling threads until either the whole garment unravelled — or I finally learned what I had helped to sew.
