## 18. Who Swore It Closed

The dining room at Vane House smelled of beeswax and blood, and by morning it smelled mostly of the second thing.

I stood where the paramedics had stood, in the doorway, because the constable on duty — a boy named Prewitt who looked like he'd been issued his uniform before he'd finished growing into it — had been told to admit no one. He admitted me. That was the first fact worth writing down: the police cordon around Aldous Vane's death had a hole in it shaped exactly like me. Someone had called ahead. Someone wanted me inside.

"Mrs. Yarrow." Prewitt shifted his weight. "DS Ferro said you'd come. Said you were to have the run of the room."

"Ferro said that."

"Yes, ma'am. Before he was—" The boy's throat worked. "Before they took him in for questioning."

* * *

Let me set the room down properly, because a locked room is only ever a sentence, and sentences can be parsed.

Aldous Vane died between the toast and the coffee. Ninety seconds of darkness — the fuse box in the service corridor, tripped clean, no scorch marks, which meant hands that knew what they were doing rather than an old house failing. When the emergency lamps came up, Vane was on the floor beneath his own portrait with a carving knife from the sideboard standing in him like a flag planted on conquered ground, and Mara Quill was kneeling beside him with her fingers wrapped around the handle, screaming words none of us could later agree upon.

Forty-one guests. Two exits. One knife that lived in the kitchen's inventory and had been carried through a door watched by Mrs. Ansel, who swore — dying or not, she swore with the precision of a woman settling accounts — that no one had passed her tray all evening except the wine steward, twice.

The wine steward who did not exist on the catering company's roster.

That was the sentence I kept coming back to. Everyone else was looking at Mara, whose fingerprints were on the knife because she'd pulled it out — she said — trying to save him. Everyone else was looking at Casper, drunk enough to weep and sober enough to lie about it. Everyone else was looking at Odile, whose hand had shaken through the toast and whose phone had gone silent as a held breath ever since.

I was looking at the wine steward.

Because here is what forty-one witnesses will give you: forty-one descriptions of a man nobody actually saw. Tall, someone said. Average, said another. Grey jacket, said three people independently, and when I checked the catering company's linens that morning — I checked them before breakfast, because grief has never once improved my sleep — every grey jacket in their stock was accounted for, hanging in a steamy back room smelling of starch. Every one but one, and the missing one was a size fifty-two, long in the sleeve.

Ferro wears a fifty-two. Ferro has always worn a fifty-two; I spent eleven years standing next to him at crime scenes, close enough to know the way his cuffs rode his wrists.

And Ferro had been the one to insist, weeks ago, that the Quill file stay shut. Ferro had been the one with access to the evidence lockup. Ferro had been the name in Casper Vane's drunken mouth. And Ferro had told Prewitt to let me into this room this morning, which could be read two ways: trust, or a man building himself an alibi out of my reputation, knowing I'd find whatever he needed found.

The evidence pointed at Ferro the way water points downhill. Effortlessly. Naturally.

Too naturally. Water always finds the channel someone cut for it.

* * *

I went home and laid it all out on the bookshop counter, between the till and a stack of unsold memoirs, the way I used to lay things out on my desk at the station: photographs of the dining room, the seating chart, the timeline, the inventory sheet from the caterers, and — weighted under a paperweight shaped like a lighthouse that Daniel bought me in Whitby, God help me — a photocopy of the reconstructed page.

The page I'd rebuilt from memory and a court transcription. The page that had gone missing from Daniel's file eleven years ago. The page bearing one line in a clerk's careful hand:

*Witness statement taken: M. Quill, 14 March.*

Eleven years ago, that page existed in three places. The original file, which I signed off on. The court transcription, which sat in the public record until someone paid to have the inquest papers sealed — and I now knew who had the standing to request that seal, because only next-of-kin and legal counsel could do it, and Daniel's next of kin was me, and I never asked.

Which left legal counsel.

My counsel. The solicitor who had handled Daniel's estate, who had drawn up my own will with such patient kindness, who had appeared in my life eleven years ago at the worst hour of it and never entirely left. Halloran had been there for the funeral. Halloran had brought the sandwiches. Halloran had been the one who told me, gently, that the file was closed and closure was a mercy, and I had been too gutted to ask why a man who wasn't family was so invested in my mercy.

And then, six months ago, the same man walked into my bookshop with six months' fees in cash and a story about a murdered philanthropist who hadn't been murdered yet.

I sat down on my own step stool and let that settle over me like weather.

He'd hired me. That was the part that made my hands go cold, colder than the morning Daniel died. Halloran hadn't just known about the anniversary plot — he'd *commissioned the investigation*. He'd put me on the scent of Mara Quill, alive, hidden, preparing to walk into that dining room and testify. He'd funded the very digging that would unearth everything he'd spent eleven years burying.

Unless.

Unless he hadn't hired me despite the plan. Unless he'd hired me *because* of it. Because a detective of my particular reputation, officially retained to prevent a murder, would make the perfect instrument. If Mara surfaced, I'd be the one who found her — and whoever silenced her afterward would have a chain of custody running straight through my hands. If she didn't surface, I'd be the fool who took the money and failed. Either way, Emrys Yarrow, the woman who solved three impossible accidents after retirement, would stand in court someday and swear she'd been hired to *prevent* the death of Aldous Vane.

You cannot frame a frame without a framer. But you can hire one.

* * *

Ferro came to the shop at nine that night, released without charge, looking like a man who'd been run through a mangle. His grey suit jacket hung wrong on him — borrowed, I noticed, from the duty locker, because his own had been seized along with everything else he owned.

"They found lint," he said, without preamble. "Grey wool, in the service corridor by the fuse box. Matched my jacket."

"I know."

"You know." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Emrys, I was in the loo when the lights went. Two other men can swear to it. But the lint—"

"The lint was planted. Your jacket went missing from your locker at the station four days ago. You reported it."

His head came up. "How did you—"

"Because I checked, Ferro. Because somebody wanted you wearing that jacket in that corridor, and they couldn't risk you leaving it safely at home." I poured him the bad whisky I keep for emergencies; this qualified. "Who knew about the locker?"

"Anyone at the station. Half the town. Emrys—" He drank, grimaced, drank again. "They've got me suspended pending inquiry. My career is over unless they find the real—"

"They won't find the real anything," I said. "Not unless we do it first. Sit down. I need to think out loud, and you're going to listen, and then you're going to tell me if I'm wrong."

He sat. I laid out the counter the way I'd laid it out that morning, and I walked him through it: the wine steward who wasn't, the grey jacket, the lint, the fuse box tripped by a hand that knew houses like Vane House the way staff know them — not the way police do.

"It's not you," I said. "It was never going to be you. You're the channel. Somebody cut it years ago and let the water find its level tonight."

"Then who?"

I put the photocopy of the reconstructed page on the counter between us, and I watched his face while he read the single line that had cost Daniel Yarrow his life.

"M. Quill, witness statement, fourteenth of March." I tapped it. "This page vanished from a sealed file in a police archive. To take it, you need archive access. To know it mattered, you need to know what Mara Quill knew. To have it sealed at the inquest, you need standing — kin or counsel. Eleven years ago, Ferro, who had all three?"

He was quiet for a long time. The shop clock ticked like something being counted down.

"Halloran," he said finally, and the word came out of him like a tooth. "Halloran had counsel standing. Halloran visited the archive — I remember now, God help me, I drove him there myself once, he said he was collecting estate papers. And Halloran—" He stopped. Started again. "Halloran's practice. Emrys, half his practice is Vane estate work. Trusts, the foundation, the charity filings. The estate doesn't just fund him. The estate *is* him. Without Vane's business, he's a two-room office above a chemist's."

"And Vane was about to be publicly destroyed by the woman he'd hidden for eleven years," I said. "Along with everyone who helped hide her. Including, I'd wager, a solicitor who drew up a will naming her beneficiary — a will that only makes sense if you already know she's alive, which means Halloran knew, which means when he learned she planned to surface at the dinner, he had exactly one move left."

"Kill Vane first. Frame the witness."

"Kill Vane first," I agreed, "and let the dead man's guilt die with him, and let the living witness hang for it. Two birds, one blackout. And then hire the widow of the first murder to investigate the second, so that when it all comes apart, the great Emrys Yarrow swears on oath she tried to stop it." I picked up my glass. My hand was steady, which surprised me. "He didn't pay me a retainer, Ferro. He paid me a character witness."

The clock ticked. Somewhere outside, rain had started, soft against the window like fingers testing the glass.

"He'll have covered himself," Ferro said. "He always covers himself. There'll be nothing to touch him."

"There's one thing." I reached under the counter and brought out the small recorder I'd bought three days ago, after Odile's phone call cut her confession short and I understood that in this town, the truth only survived if you caught it mid-air. "He's coming here tomorrow. Ten o'clock. To express his condolences and discuss the estate's affairs — my husband's estate, he'll say, unfinished business, he'll want to comfort me. He's been comforting me for eleven years, Ferro. It's how he hunts."

Ferro stared at the recorder, then at me, and I watched him understand what I was asking and what it would cost. Evidence obtained this way would be fought. A serving officer sitting in the next room while a retired detective extracted a confession privately — it wouldn't survive a defense barrister's first sneer.

"You're asking me to help you hang him outside the law," he said.

"I'm asking you to help me get him inside it any way it will take him. The law had eleven years, Ferro. It filed Daniel under *accident* and Mara Quill under *missing* and it shook Halloran's hand at the funeral." I turned the recorder over in my fingers. "Tomorrow at ten, he walks in here wearing sympathy like that grey jacket. And I'm going to talk him right out of it."

* * *

He arrived at ten o'clock precisely, because guilty men are always punctual — innocence dawdles, guilt keeps appointments. I heard his umbrella close on the step, heard Ferro shift his weight in the back room among the unsold poetry, and I unlocked the front door before Halloran could knock.

"Emrys." He stepped in, bringing cold air and the smell of wet wool, and his face arranged itself into the expression I had trusted for eleven years — sorrow, professionally tailored, one size fits all bereavement. "I came as soon as I could. What a terrible business. Terrible. And poor Miss Quill — though I suppose you warned us all, didn't you? You did your best."

"I did my best," I agreed, and poured the coffee I'd made strong enough to strip varnish, and set the cup in his hands, and sat down across from him the way I used to sit across from men who thought they'd gotten away with it. "Aldous, though. Such a shock. You'd known him thirty years, hadn't you?"

"Since before the foundation. Since—" A flicker, quickly smoothed. "A long time. We were friends."

"Were you." I opened the ledger — Ruth Ibarra's ledger, the true one, the one with the standing payments and the quiet names — and turned it toward him, and watched his eyes go to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. "Because I've been reading his friendship. Page fourteen is interesting. So is the entry marked *H., quarterly*, going back eleven years, stopping last spring."

The cup trembled, just slightly, in his well-kept hands.

"Last spring," I said softly, "is when Mara Quill contacted you about rewriting the will. Isn't it, Gerald?"

And Halloran set down his coffee with great care, the way a man does when his hands can no longer be trusted with anything fragile, and looked at me with the friendly face sliding off at last like a mask in the rain — and said:

"You should have taken the fee and dusted your books, Emrys."
