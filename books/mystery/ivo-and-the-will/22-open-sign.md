## 22. Open Sign

The bell over the door of Yarrow's Books had been broken for two years, and in that time I had come to think of it as a colleague. It said nothing when I wanted silence, and when it finally spoke, it meant something.

It spoke on a Tuesday morning in late October, three weeks after the anniversary dinner, and the man who came through the door behind it was a stranger carrying a book like a wounded bird — cradled against his chest, both hands, as though it might bolt.

"Are you open?" he asked, which was a fair question, because the sign in the window had spent most of the autumn reading CLOSED in letters so faded they were less an instruction than an apology.

I looked at him. He looked at me. Somewhere behind him, out on Wexford Street, the town went about its business with the particular self-consciousness of a town that has recently been in the newspapers. Every second passerby glanced at my window. A few of them glanced at me through it, the way you glance at a house where something happened.

"Not yet," I said, and turned the card in the window to OPEN before I could talk myself out of it. The cardboard was soft with age. It moved anyway. Most things do, if you push them.

The man exhaled as though I'd granted him a visa.

* * *

Three weeks is not long enough for a town to digest what happened at Vane House, but it is long enough for it to develop opinions. The anniversary dinner had entered local legend already, retold in the bakery queue and the post office with the details shifting like weather: the lights failing, the toast going cold in raised glasses, Aldous Vane standing up out of his chair with an expression no one could quite name — relief, some said, which was the detail I kept coming back to myself — and then the dark, and then the terrible ordinary sound of a body finding the floor.

Mara Quill had walked into that room eleven years after the town agreed she'd never existed, and she had stood in the wreckage holding a knife she hadn't touched, and she had said, over and over, to Ferro's officers, to the coroner, to anyone who would listen: *I never touched it. You have to believe me. I came here to tell the truth.*

The town believed her eventually. Towns are slow but they are not stupid. What the town had trouble believing was everything else: the ledger, the payments, the quiet machinery of a philanthropy that had been laundering fear for two decades. That a beloved man had built a monument to generosity on top of a grave. That there were two graves, actually, though only one of them had a stone.

Daniel's had a stone. I'd visited it. But we'll get there.

Halloran was in custody awaiting trial, and his solicitor — hired by his own firm, which told you everything about how fast loyalty evaporates when the fees stop flowing — had entered a plea of not guilty, because solicitors must. The confession I'd recorded in my back room sat in an evidence locker wearing a chain-of-custody tag that was, technically, a fiction. Ferro had resigned rather than explain where it came from. His last act as a serving officer was to hand the recording to the Crown Prosecution with a straight face and a resignation letter already typed. I owed him for that. I intended to pay the debt the only way he'd accept: badly, over bad coffee, at irregular intervals, forever.

Ruth Ibarra had dissolved the partnership's charitable arm and was cooperating with investigators with the serene efficiency of a woman who had always known this day would come and had dressed for it. Odile Vane had left town. Casper Vane had stayed, oddly — sober now, working at the marina, telling anyone who'd listen that his father had been a monster and that he'd loved him anyway, which was the most honest thing any Vane had ever said in public.

And Mara Quill had given her testimony, all of it, in a courtroom that hung on every word, and then she had gone home to Nell's spare room and started, slowly, to exist again. Eleven years of being nobody takes longer than eleven weeks to undo. But she was trying. She'd bought a coat. That sounds like nothing. It isn't nothing. The dead don't buy coats.

The estate was unravelling the way estates do — lawyers, creditors, the slow public undressing of accounts — and the town watched it come apart with the expression of people watching a beautiful building be demolished and discovering, underneath, exactly the rot they'd always half suspected.

And me? I dusted stock. I made tea. I waited on the four customers a day who still came in, and I found that the number was creeping upward, five, then seven, then one grey afternoon eleven, which I counted twice because I didn't trust it.

Grief, it turns out, doesn't leave you. It just changes shape. For eleven years mine had been a locked room with a false floor. Now it was a room with the windows open — colder, yes, but you could breathe in it, and you could see the street.

* * *

The man with the wounded book set it down on my counter with the reverence people usually reserve for infants and explosives.

"It belonged to my mother," he said. "I think someone's tampered with it."

I looked at the book. It was a fat Victorian novel, cloth boards gone furry at the corners, spine sun-bleached to the colour of weak tea. Perfectly ordinary. Perfectly beloved.

"Tampered," I repeated.

"There's a hollow." He opened the back cover and showed me where the endpaper had been lifted and relaid — neatly, professionally, but paper remembers, the way wood remembers, the way towns remember. Beneath it, a shallow cavity, empty. "There was something in there. Photographs, I think. Or letters. My mother died in August. I found the hollow last week. Everything else in her house was exactly as she left it. Except this."

"And you brought it to a bookshop."

"I brought it to you." He said it plainly, without flattery, which is the only kind of plainness I trust. "My wife says I should go to the police. But the police—" He stopped, recalibrated. Everyone in this town recalibrated around the police now; it was like navigating around roadworks. "The police would want to know why my mother hid things in books. And I'd have to tell them about my father. And I'm not ready to tell anyone about my father."

I poured us both tea, because it was that hour and that kind of conversation, and because my kettle had been feeling underemployed for years.

"Tell me about your mother instead," I said.

He talked for forty minutes. She had been a schoolteacher, meticulous, private, fond of margins — she wrote in the margins of everything, including, he now realised, possibly the margins of her life. She had died suddenly and gently, and she had left him a house full of ordered rooms and one book with a hole in it, and he could not shake the conviction that the hole was a message. Not to the world. To him.

"She knew you'd find it," I said. "People who hide things in hollowed books aren't hiding them from their children. They're leaving them somewhere the children will eventually look."

He stared at me. "Then where is it?"

"That," I said, "is the question. And questions are the only stock in this shop that never goes out of print."

I took the book. I told him I'd look at it properly — bindings, hinges, the other endpapers, the spine lining, the places a careful woman might build a second hollow if she suspected the first would be found. I told him it might take a week. I told him my fee, which I had raised, modestly, since the autumn, on the theory that desperation should cost something even when it's sympathetic.

At the door he paused. "They say you solved the Vane business," he said. "They say you did it outside the law."

"They say a lot of things."

"Is it true?"

I thought about Halloran's voice on that recording, calm and reasonable right up until it wasn't. I thought about Ferro's face when he took the envelope. I thought about Daniel, and about the word *accident*, and about how many times I'd signed that word without looking under it.

"The law is a tool," I said. "Like a hammer. Wonderful for building. Useless when what you need is a key. Sometimes you have to pick the lock yourself and accept that the lock-picking will be mentioned later."

He nodded slowly, the way people nod when they've decided you're either exactly what they need or slightly mad, and in my experience those categories overlap more than the profession admits.

* * *

That evening Ferro came round, uninvited, carrying chips and a bottle of wine that was better than either of us deserved. He'd grown a beard in retirement, presumably to disguise the fact that he was thirty-eight and unemployed, and it wasn't working.

"You turned the sign over," he said, dropping onto the shop's one decent chair and surveying the shelves with the air of a man auditing a crime scene. "Marjorie at the bakery says you've got customers again. Actual ones. Buying actual books."

"Marjorie should get a hobby."

"She has one. It's you." He handed me the wine. "So. Is it true you're taking cases again?"

"One case. A hollow book and a dead schoolteacher who may or may not have left her son a message."

Ferro winced. "Emrys. You've been free for three weeks."

"I know. It's dreadful. I keep catching myself being content. It's like a rash."

He laughed, and then stopped laughing, and looked at me across the tea-and-book smell of my shop with the expression he used to wear across interview tables, the one that meant he was about to say the true thing instead of the safe one.

"You could just sell books," he said. "You could just be a woman who sells books. Nobody would blame you. After everything — after Daniel, after Halloran, after giving up your career twice, once when they pushed you and once when you jumped — nobody on this earth would blame you for closing the door."

"No," I agreed. "Nobody would."

"So why open it?"

I got up and straightened a shelf that didn't need straightening, because some answers require a prop. "Because I spent eleven years believing a lie I helped write," I said finally. "Because there's a woman in Nell Quill's spare room learning how to be alive again, and she can only do that because somebody refused to let a closed file stay closed. Because the law is a hammer, Tom, and the world is full of locks." I turned the book over in my hands — the schoolteacher's book, the hollow one, the next one. "And because I'm still angry. I'm always going to be angry. Anger's not a flaw. It's fuel. You just have to aim it."

Ferro looked at me for a long moment. Then he raised his glass. "To aiming it," he said.

"To the lock-pickers," I said, and we drank to ourselves, which is the only toast two retired cops can make with a straight face.

* * *

After he left I stood in the doorway a while, watching Wexford Street put itself to bed. The lamplighters of commerce: the bakery shuttering, the pub warming, a couple arguing amiably about a parking space. Ordinary life, resuming. The town had survived its truth. Truths are like that — they feel unsurvivable right up until everyone's surviving them.

Somewhere out there, a dead schoolteacher had left her son a message in the margins of a hollowed book, and I intended to find it. I didn't know yet that the message would lead me up the coast to a lighthouse that had been decommissioned for thirty years and was somehow still paying an electricity bill. I didn't know that the name written inside the second hollow — the one hidden beneath the first, because careful women build in redundancy — would be a name I knew, a name from a file I'd burned, a name that would make me sit down very suddenly on the floor of my own shop at two in the morning with the taste of old grief in my mouth.

All I knew that night was this: the bell above my door was broken, the sign in my window said OPEN, and tomorrow morning I would get up and choose it again. Because that's what choosing is. Not once, grandly, at the quarry ridge or the graveside or the moment the lights fail at a dinner party. Every morning. Small, stubborn, unglamorous. Turning the card over in a window that needs cleaning, for a town that's watching, with hands that aren't steady and never were.

I am older than I was. I am lighter than I was, though not light — I don't think I'll ever be light; I carry Daniel everywhere, and Mara carries her eleven years, and between us we've learned that the weight is survivable if you stop pretending it isn't there. I am unburdened in the way a ship is unburdened once you've thrown the ballast overboard: faster, freer, and riding dangerously high in the water.

Still angry at the world. Still fond of it, in spite of everything, which is worse.

I reached up and rang the broken bell once, for luck, for practice, for the client who would walk through that door tomorrow with an impossible request and a wounded book and no idea what he was starting.

It didn't ring. It never does.

But I heard it anyway.
