## 8. The Committee

The municipal building in Skarvik smelled of floor wax and old radiators, the particular scent of a place where nothing urgent had happened in decades. Ada stood in the lobby for a full minute before the receptionist noticed her, and when she did, the woman's smile arrived a half-second late, like a tide that had forgotten its schedule.

"Can I help you?"

"I'm here for the review committee. Nine o'clock."

"You're the podcast lady." It wasn't a question. The smile stayed fixed but something behind it recalibrated — Ada had learned to read that flicker over three years of knocking on doors that didn't want to open. "Room two. Down the hall, past the notice board."

The notice board was worth the walk. Community supper dates, a lost cat named Sultan, a faded poster for the 1994 herring festival — the year Marta Voss walked out of her classroom on a Thursday afternoon and into the water, according to everyone who'd been paid or frightened enough to say so. Ada photographed it anyway. You never knew which piece of wallpaper would turn out to be load-bearing.

* * *

Room two held a long table, seven chairs behind it, and one chair facing them — hers. The asymmetry was the message. She took her seat and arranged her notebook, her folder of documents, and her recorder, which she placed openly on the table because hiding it would have been worse than showing it.

Eirik Lund arrived last, and he arrived the way weather arrives: gradually, then all at once. He was perhaps sixty-five, silver at the temples in a way that looked earned rather than purchased, wearing a sweater that suggested he owned boats. He shook hands with two of the committee members, clapped a third on the shoulder, and only then turned to Ada with an expression of genuine, unguarded delight.

"You must be Ms. Iversen." His voice was warm gravel. "I've listened to your program. All of it, I'm afraid — my wife plays it while she cooks, and now I know more about cold cases than any man my age should." He sat down at the center chair as naturally as a man sitting down to his own kitchen table. "I want you to know we're all grateful someone still cares about Marta Voss. This town has carried that weight a long time."

Ada had interviewed hundreds of people. She knew charm when she heard it — knew its textures, its grain, the way real warmth ran deep and performed warmth ran along the surface like oil on water. Lund's was flawless. That was the first thing she wrote in her notebook, under the date: *Flawless.*

"Thank you," she said. "I'll keep this brief."

"Please, take your time." He gestured at her papers like a host offering bread.

She laid it out plainly. The disappearance of Marta Voss, March 1994, ruled accidental drowning within eleven days. No body recovered for six weeks, and then only by a fisherman who wasn't asked to make a statement. Witness statements taken from four residents, none of whom had been re-interviewed when inconsistencies emerged. A harbor office logbook with a missing page. And a statute of limitations that would bar prosecution in five months and nine days.

"The request is simple," Ada said. "Reopen the case. Formally. Before the window closes."

She felt the room listen. Not the committee — the room itself, the walls, the radiator ticking in the corner, the town pressing its ear against the plaster. Somewhere behind her, she suspected, were people who had come just to watch through the door.

Lund let the silence sit for exactly the right length. Then he folded his hands and said, gently, "May I ask you a question first? Off the record, so to speak." He nodded at her recorder without hostility. "Though of course, record whatever you like. I've nothing to hide from anyone."

It was beautifully done. *Nothing to hide* delivered as an invitation rather than a defense.

"Ask."

"When you say reopen — what outcome are you hoping for? Truly. If I could give you any answer this morning, what would it be?"

"That Marta Voss gets her name back," Ada said. "That the file stops saying *accident* when at least four people in this town know otherwise."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt, she thought. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a familiar piece of music played slightly wrong. "That's a good answer," he said softly. "I believe you mean it."

Then he turned to the committee and destroyed her motion in ninety seconds flat.

* * *

He never raised his voice. He never said no. What he did was procedural jiu-jitsu, executed with such courtesy that each hold looked like a helping hand.

The petition, he noted regretfully, had been filed under the wrong form — a Form 12 rather than a Form 9, an error any layperson could be forgiven for, and he forgave it immediately, warmly, while making clear the committee could not act on it. The correct form required a sponsoring member, and the sponsoring member required ten days' written notice, which could not be waived except by quorum, and two members were away — one fishing, one ill, both, Ada noticed, conveniently absent. The case file itself, he explained with genuine sympathy, had been transferred to county storage during the 2009 records consolidation, and retrieval requests ran six to eight weeks.

"Six to eight weeks," Ada repeated.

"Sometimes faster," Lund said. "I'll make a call myself. Personally." He smiled at her, and the smile contained, folded inside it like a letter inside an envelope, the knowledge that five months minus eight weeks left very little room. "You see the difficulty. It isn't that anyone here lacks the will. It's that the machinery has its own pace, and we mustn't pretend we can outrun it. That way lies bad process, and bad process helps no one — least of all Marta."

He said her name like a man who had known her. Ada catalogued that too.

One committee member, a nervous woman named Bergliot who taught piano, ventured that perhaps an exception could be made given the circumstances. Lund thanked her for her compassion, agreed entirely with her sentiment, and observed that exceptions made under public pressure were precisely how institutions lost the public's trust — a sentence that managed to make the obstruction sound like integrity. Bergliot subsided. The vote, when it came, was deferred pending the correct paperwork, which in the grammar of small-town committees meant *no*, spoken politely enough to be deniable.

"Ms. Iversen." Lund stood when she did, and walked her to the door himself, hand briefly at her elbow — a touch light as a moth landing. "For what it's worth, I hope you get your answers. This town needs them, even if it doesn't know how to ask." A pause, calibrated. "And do give my regards to Halvard Brandt, if you're still seeing him. Old colleagues ought to stay in touch. It's a small harbor. We all end up in the same water eventually."

She kept her face pleasant and her pulse from showing. He knew about Halvard. Which meant Halvard's warning-off hadn't been private — or hadn't stayed that way.

Outside, the morning was bright and cold, the harbor glittering like something innocent. Ada walked to her car, sat down, and let her hands shake for exactly thirty seconds. Then she opened her notebook and wrote:

*He didn't kill the motion. He buried it — with honors. Six to eight weeks for the file. Ten days' notice. Wrong form. Every obstacle reasonable. Every reason a wall.*

Underneath: *He wanted me to see the wall. That was the point.*

* * *

Nell was waiting at the guesthouse with coffee and the particular expression of a sister who has spent the morning not asking questions and is now done with it.

"Well?" she said.

"I got buried politely."

"Is that better or worse than rudely?"

"Worse. Rudely you can quote." Ada dropped into the chair across from her. Nell had claimed the window seat in the guesthouse common room as her own territory now, spreading her mother's letters across it like a card sharp — though she still hadn't shown Ada the ones she'd found, and Ada hadn't pushed, and the not-pushing was starting to ache like a tooth.

"You should have seen your face when you came in," Nell said. "You look like you lost a fight with a librarian."

"I did. He used procedure as a weapon. It was almost elegant." Ada wrapped her hands around the mug Nell pushed toward her. "Five months, Nell. And he just spent forty minutes explaining why none of them can possibly move before spring."

"And you believe he's the one."

"I believe he's the center of gravity. Everything falls toward him." She drank. The coffee was terrible; the guesthouse owner bought it in tins and defended it like family. "But belief isn't evidence. He knows that better than anyone. That's what today was — him showing me the distance between what I know and what I can prove, and letting me measure it."

Nell studied her for a moment. "You're not discouraged."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because a man with nothing to hide doesn't learn my sources' names." Ada set down the mug. "He mentioned Halvard. Nobody saw me meet Halvard. So either Halvard told him — possible, but Halvard's too scared of him for that — or someone's watching me and reporting. Which means I'm not chasing anymore, Nell. Something's chasing back."

Nell absorbed this, then reached across and straightened the collar of Ada's jacket, a gesture so abruptly maternal that Ada blinked. "Then stop walking predictable routes," Nell said. "Starting with coming home before dark like a schoolgirl. Go be unpredictable somewhere."

"I'm going to review my notes."

"Nerd."

"Sister."

They almost smiled at each other. It was, Ada reflected, the most progress they'd made since Nell's arrival — friction softening into something with a shape she might eventually be allowed to call family. Their mother hung between them still, unspoken, the paid silence Nell was carrying around like a stone in her pocket. But stones could wait. Stones were patient. That was practically their job description.

* * *

The letter was waiting on the guesthouse front step when she came down after dark to lock up.

Not in the mailbox — on the step, weighted under a smooth gray stone, placed there while the house was full of people, while Nell sat ten feet from the door, while the owner watched television with the volume up. Ada stood looking at it for a long moment before she touched it, and even then she picked it up by the corner, the way June had taught her to handle archival paper.

Same heavy cream stock. Same careful, upright hand. Her name on the front, spelled correctly, no address needed — as if the whole town were simply an extension of her mailbox.

Inside, one page.

*Dear Ms. Iversen,*

*I watched you this morning. You were composed, thorough, and generous to people who did not deserve it — especially the piano teacher, who tried to help you and will pay a small price for her kindness. I mention this so you understand that everything has a cost here, including decency.*

*Your performance was excellent. His was better. Did you notice he never once denied anything? Denial is for men with something to lose. Eirik loses nothing by your suspicion; suspicion without proof is just weather to him. You brought rain. He owns the roof.*

*You asked the committee for the file. Stop asking committees. The file you need was never in the municipal building. Ask yourself instead: who moved it, when, and what did they carry out of the harbor office on the night of March 17th that weighed enough to need two hands?*

*You are closer than you think and further than you fear. Continue in order.*

*— A friend of the truth, if not of the people who killed it*

Ada read it twice, standing under the guesthouse porch light with moths ticking against the bulb above her head. Then she did what she always did with evidence: she photographed it, bagged it, logged the time, temperature, and placement of the stone.

Then she allowed herself one less professional reaction, which was to laugh — a short, startled sound, alone in the dark, half terror and half something dangerously close to delight.

*"Performance,"* she said aloud to the empty street. "He called it a performance."

Because that was the thing about the letter — the thing that would keep her awake tonight, notebook open, pen moving. The writer had been in that room. Not metaphorically. The details were too precise: Bergliot's intervention, Lund's non-denials, the exact texture of the meeting. Either the writer had been standing behind that door with the other watchers —

Or the writer had been sitting at the table.

Seven chairs behind a long desk. One absent fishing. One absent ill.

Ada looked down the street toward the harbor, black water holding the last gray light, and thought: *five months minus eight weeks minus ten days.* Lund had counted it out for her himself, smiling, certain the arithmetic protected him.

Her correspondent was counting too. Counting faster.

She went inside to write it all down, and to wonder — for the first time with real, cold clarity — whether her invisible ally and her visible enemy might be sitting in the same room every second Tuesday, shaking the same hands, wearing the same calm.

And whether the friend of the truth had just, deliberately, pointed her at the harbor office door.
