## 13. The Middle of It

The wall in the guesthouse sitting room had been beige and blameless for thirty years, according to Mrs. Aune, who owned the place and pretended not to listen at doors. By nine o'clock on Thursday morning it was neither.

Ada stood back with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and looked at what they'd done to it. Index cards in three colors. Photographs pinned at crooked angles. Red string — Nell had insisted on red string, "because if we're going to be people with a murder wall, we should commit" — connecting Marta Voss's last known day to the harbor office, the cove, the boathouse, and a small cluster of cards that represented everything no one had ever been charged for.

"You've made a spiderweb of grief," Halvard said from the armchair, where he sat with his old case notebook open on his knee like a hymnal.

"I've made a *system*," Ada said. "The spiderweb is incidental."

June was already on a step stool, adjusting a card so its top edge aligned with the others. She'd brought her own ruler. Of course she had. "If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. Chronology left to right, geography bottom to top. Otherwise it's just decoration."

"It's a bit of both," Nell said. She was on the floor, cross-legged, surrounded by the contents of three shoeboxes she'd carried over from her room — their mother's letters, the ones she still hadn't shown Ada. Ada had noticed the boxes. Ada noticed everything; it was the job. But Nell had stacked them under the window with a kind of deliberate casualness that said *not yet*, and Ada had decided, in the middle of the night, staring at the ceiling of the room where Marta Voss had once slept, that she would let her sister come to it in her own time.

That was new for her. She filed the feeling away to examine later, the way she filed everything.

* * *

They had not exactly agreed to be a team. It had happened the way weather happens.

After Halvard's confession — the car near the dock, the powerful driver, the thirty years of swallowing it — he had tried, twice, to hand Ada his notebook and announce he was done. "I'm an old man who kept his mouth shut when it mattered," he'd said. "You don't want me. I'm ballast."

The second time, June had looked up from her tea and said, "Halvard, you knew every dock worker by name in 1994. You know which of them are still alive and which ones lie. If you quit now, you're not ballast, you're a lifeboat cutting itself loose out of guilt." Then she'd gone slightly pink, as though surprised by her own ferocity, and hidden behind the teapot.

Nell had said nothing at all. She'd simply moved Halvard's chair closer to the table before he came down for breakfast, and set his notebook on it, squared to the table edge, waiting. He'd sat in it. That was how Halvard joined: by sitting where someone had saved him a place.

And Nell herself — Nell had never asked to join. She'd simply started showing up with coffee, then with questions, then with a red pen and opinions about Ada's card layout ("your handwriting slants when you're angry, did you know that?"). By Wednesday night she was arguing with June about whether the print shop receipt belonged in the evidence column or the timeline column, and Ada had realized her estranged half-sister had quietly become load-bearing.

Now, Thursday morning, Ada tapped the center of the wall, where Marta's photograph hung — a school portrait, early thirties, dark hair cut bluntly, an expression that managed to be patient and unimpressed at once. The expression of a woman auditing a harbor trust's books while everyone smiled at her.

"All right," Ada said. "Let's say it out loud, all of it, start to finish. Not because any of us doesn't know it. Because saying it in order is how you find the hole."

* * *

It took two hours. June narrated the paper trail: Marta's audit notes, reconstructed from fragments, showing discrepancies in the harbor trust accounts dating back years — sums moved, invoices duplicated, a maintenance company that existed mostly on letterhead. Eirik Lund chaired the trust. Sander Koll ran the dock. Torvald Ness signed things without reading them, apparently as a way of life.

Halvard filled in the night itself, or the shape of it: Marta seen leaving the school at four; her bicycle found by the dock; the water in the harbor office; Koll's car — Lund's car? he still couldn't swear to the make, only the silhouette of power behind the wheel — near the dock after dark; the body in the cove two days later; the ruling of accidental drowning, ordered from above, tidy as a folded flag.

"And then nothing," Nell said. "Thirty years of nothing."

"Not nothing," Ada said. "Torvald." She crossed to the cards tracking the letters. "He wrote to me. Two letters first, then more. And here's what I keep snagging on."

She took down the letters themselves — she'd begun keeping them in a document sleeve, handled like evidence, which made Nell raise an eyebrow the first time and say nothing since — and laid them on the table in order.

"The confession came first. Then the promise of 'the rest' if I investigated publicly. Then the letter inside my locked car, with details nobody public ever had — the order of interviews, the sequence. Then the praise after the committee meeting. Each one arrived at exactly the right moment to move me along. Like—" she searched for it— "like stage directions."

"Like a case file," Halvard said quietly.

Everyone turned to look at him. He'd gone very still, the way he did when something surfaced from thirty years down.

"Say that again," Ada said.

"When I worked the case — before Lund shut it — I interviewed witnesses in an order. Standard practice. Start with the last confirmed sighting, work outward. School, then the road, then the dock, then the harbor office, then the cove." His finger traced the air above the table, left to right. "I wrote it up that way. My report went to Lund in that order."

Ada felt the cold walk up her arms. She pulled the third letter — the one from the locked car — and read its instructions aloud. Interview the ferry clerk. Then the school caretaker. Then the woman who'd seen lights at the cove.

"Ferry clerk," she said. "Caretaker. Lights at the cove."

"Halvard's report," June breathed, already halfway off her chair, moving toward the wall. "In what order did your interviews go?"

Halvard closed his eyes. Ada watched him retrieve it, this old man reaching into a drawer he'd nailed shut for three decades. "Ferry clerk first — she saw Marta cross on the morning boat. Caretaker second. Then the dock workers. Then—" he opened his eyes— "then the cove. Lights. I never got further. Lund pulled me off it before I could interview anyone past the cove."

June had the original witness list — Halvard's notebook pages, photocopied — spread against the letters. Her finger moved down both columns. "One, two, three. One, two, three. It's not similar. It's *identical*."

* * *

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Ada had heard silences like it before, in archives and courtrooms and once in a hospital corridor: the silence of a thing becoming true.

"So Torvald read the file," Nell said finally. "So what? He worked with Marta. He could have gotten hold of it."

"No," Ada said. "Listen. Halvard's report was never the official file. Lund buried it. The official file is the accident ruling — thin, clean, no witness sequence at all. The witness order exists in exactly two places." She held up fingers. "Halvard's original report, which went to Lund. And Halvard's personal notebook, which stayed in Halvard's house until three weeks ago."

"Until I gave it to you," Halvard said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice and listening to it talk.

"Which means either Torvald saw Lund's copy of the report—" June began.

"—or Torvald has access to this room," Nell finished.

Nobody looked at the door. Everybody thought about the door.

"He was in my locked car," Ada said. "He knows what's on this wall, or he predicted it. He knew about the committee meeting the same day it happened. Either Torvald Ness is being fed information by someone very close to the case—" she let it hang— "or the man who wrote me those letters isn't working alone. Or isn't who he says he is."

"Or," Halvard said slowly, "he read the report *in 1994*. Before it disappeared into Lund's drawer. He was Marta's colleague; police talked to him early. Maybe he saw it lying on a desk." He rubbed his jaw. "But that doesn't explain the committee letter. He knew what was said in that room, Ada. Who told him?"

"Eirik Lund," Ada said, "is the answer to most of our questions lately, and I'm tired of him being the answer instead of the evidence."

* * *

Mrs. Aune appeared at noon with a tray — open-faced sandwiches, coffee, a plate of cinnamon buns that had no business being as good as they were — and surveyed the wall with the expression of a woman deciding whether to object to the pins.

"My mother taught Marta Voss to swim," she said, setting down the tray. "In the shallows at Buvika, summer of '68. Marta was frightened of the deep water for years after. Funny, everyone saying she drowned." She adjusted one sandwich a quarter-inch, as though the tray were also a system requiring alignment, and left.

No one spoke until the stairs stopped creaking.

"She's known the whole time," Nell said softly. "This whole town has known the whole time."

"That's why the letters chose me," Ada said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud, but there it was. "Not because of the podcast. Well — partly. But a stranger can ask questions here. A local can't. Everyone in Skarvik has spent thirty years being polite at each other across a grave. They needed someone with no history to carry it." She looked at the wall. "Someone filed the truth better than anyone. That's what he wrote. Whoever wrote it."

"Your confessor," Halvard said, "has a strange relationship with the truth. Confessing to a murder he swears he didn't commit, in order to expose a murder he won't name."

"Cowards are rarely consistent," June said, with a gentleness that took the edge off. "Fear makes its own logic. He wanted it known and he wanted himself safe, and those two wants have been fighting each other for thirty years. We're just the battlefield."

Ada looked around the table. June, ink-smudged, fierce in the quiet way of people who guard other people's records. Halvard, upright in his saved chair, carrying his shame like a rucksack he'd finally set down but couldn't take off. Nell, who had boxes of their mother's secrets under the window and hadn't run yet.

Three years ago Ada's family had been a microphone and an alphabetized filing cabinet. Now there were four chairs at this table and all of them occupied, and the terrifying part — the part she'd examine later, in the dark, with the discipline she wore like armor — was how much she didn't want to lose it.

"We should eat," she said, because someone had to, and because Halvard was looking at the buns with the restraint of a man who believed hunger was a moral failing. "Then we map the holes."

* * *

The holes, it turned out, were instructive.

By late afternoon the wall had grown a fourth color of card — yellow, for questions — and the yellow outnumbered everything else. Where was the original audit ledger now? What exactly had Lund's copy of Halvard's report contained? Who else had keys to the archive wing, and had the fire investigation even begun properly? Why had their mother been paid, and by whom, and—

Ada caught Nell looking at the shoeboxes. Caught herself looking too. Said nothing. Filed it.

It was June who found the crack, near five o'clock, standing at the wall with her head tilted like a bird's.

"The letters stop," she said.

"What?"

"Look at the dates against the investigation sequence. Confession. Promise. Instructions. Praise. Four letters, four stages — matching Halvard's report, matching the original inquiry, step for step." She turned around, and there was something almost apologetic in her face, the archivist's distress at a gap in a record. "But Halvard's report didn't end at the cove. There was a fifth interview planned. He told us this morning — Lund pulled him off before he could do it. The fifth person on his list."

Ada's eyes went to the notebook page photocopied and pinned beneath Marta's photograph. Five names. The fifth was circled in Halvard's old blue ink, annotated in his cramped hand: *not reached.*

The name beside it was **Eirik Lund**.

In 1994, before he was committee chair, before he was charming and helpful and dangerous at dinner parties — Eirik Lund had been the harbor trust's treasurer. The last person Marta Voss was known to have met.

The room went very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed over the gray water, indifferent as arithmetic.

"He never sent the fifth letter," Halvard said. "Because the fifth interview never happened. The writer isn't following the case file, Ada. He's following *my report* — the version that stops at the cove. Which means he's seen it. Recently enough to know it word for word."

"Or," Nell said, and her voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they step over a threshold, "he *wrote* it. Once. Thirty years ago. From the other side of the desk."

Ada looked at the fifth name on the list, and at the red string running from it to the harbor office, and understood with perfect clarity that somewhere in this town, someone had just realized what they'd realized — that the letters had a shape, and the shape pointed uphill.

Her phone buzzed on the table. An unknown number. A single line of text:

*You're faster than he expected. Be careful with the fifth name.*

Ada stared at it, then held the screen up so the others could see, and watched three faces go pale in the lamplight, and thought: *the middle of it is where it starts moving.*
