## 22. After the Thaw

The ice went out of the fjord in the third week of April, and Skarvik came down to watch it go.

It was a thing Ada had never seen before — a whole town standing at the rail of the harbor in their good coats, drinking coffee from thermoses, waiting for the sound. When it came it was less dramatic than she'd expected: a long groan under the water, like an old man turning over in bed, and then the plates of ice beginning to shift and grind against one another, drifting out toward the open sea in slow, glittering procession. Nell stood beside her with her hands wrapped around a paper cup, and June had climbed up onto the bollard where she could see better, and Halvard — who had lived here his entire life and claimed he'd watched this ceremony sixty-eight times and would watch it sixty-eight more if the fjord let him — stood slightly apart, hat in his hands, not watching the ice at all but looking up the hill toward the churchyard.

"He does that every year," June said quietly, following Ada's gaze. "Since January."

"I know," Ada said.

They all knew. They knew everything about each other now, the way people do after they've spent a winter mapping a crime on a guesthouse wall together. There was no longer any part of Halvard's guilt, or June's stubbornness, or Nell's guarded heart, or Ada's own terrible talent for keeping grief at arm's length that the others hadn't seen laid out flat under lamplight. It was, Ada thought, watching the ice go, the strangest kind of wealth. Three years of cataloguing other people's losses had never taught her that this was what she'd been looking for.

The last plate of ice caught the morning sun as it slid past the breakwater, flashed white-gold once, and was gone.

"There," said Halvard, to no one in particular. "That's Marta's year done properly."

* * *

The reburial was held on a Tuesday, because Marta Voss had been a schoolteacher and everyone agreed she would have found a Tuesday fitting — no fuss, no weekend crowds, just the ordinary machinery of a working day set briefly aside.

The town turned out anyway. Half of Skarvik must have been there, standing among the older stones with their hats off, and Ada noticed — because noticing was her profession — which families stood together and which stood carefully apart, and how old Mrs. Aune, who for thirty years had been the first to say "accident" whenever Marta's name came up, now wept openly into a handkerchief that had clearly been pressed into service many times since February.

The new stone read:

**MARTA VOSS**
*Teacher. 1958–1994.*
*She asked the right questions.*

June had chosen the epitaph. She'd presented three options to the group over dinner one night, deadpan, and when Nell suggested *She asked the right questions* June had written it down immediately and refused all further discussion. It was, Ada thought, exactly correct. The whole case had turned on questions — Marta's, about the harbor trust's books; Torvald's, finally, after thirty years of cowardice; Ada's own, asked aloud into a microphone to strangers. The wrong people had spent decades making sure nobody answered them. The stone would keep asking now, in granite, indefinitely.

Eirik Lund was not present. He was in custody awaiting trial, and would be for some months; Koll had testified, Torvald had testified, and the audit notes recovered from the boathouse box — scorched at the corners but legible, Marta's neat handwriting marching across every page — had done the rest. The harbor trust was dissolved by municipal order in March. Its remaining assets were frozen pending civil claims, and its handsome offices by the dock were already being discussed as a site for, of all things, a public library annex. June had opinions about the shelving. June had opinions about everything, delivered in her flat archivist's voice while she ate enormous quantities of whatever was put in front of her, and Ada had come to find this one of the great comforts of her life.

After the service, while the crowd thinned, Halvard stayed behind. He did it every week now — everyone knew it, nobody remarked on it. He brought a trowel and a bucket and a pair of shears, and he tended the grave the way he'd once tended his old case notebook: thoroughly, precisely, without any expectation of thanks.

Ada lingered at the gate, meaning to give him privacy, but he looked up and beckoned.

"You may as well make yourself useful," he said. "There are dandelions. I'm too old to fight them alone."

So she knelt in the April grass beside him and pulled weeds, and neither of them spoke for a long time, and it was — she would remember this later, would file it away in the small warm drawer she kept now for things that didn't need analyzing — it was enough.

"She'd have liked you," Halvard said eventually. "Marta. Stubborn about facts. Terrible at letting things lie."

"People keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true." He sat back on his heels, surveyed his work with the satisfaction of a man closing a file properly for the first time in thirty years. "You know what I figured out? All those years I thought my sin was silence. But it wasn't. My sin was believing silence was the same as peace." He tapped the earth gently with the trowel, twice, like knocking on a door. "It isn't. Peace is this. Getting your hands dirty in April."

Ada looked at the stone. *She asked the right questions.*

"Halvard," she said. "When this trial's done — would you record something with me? For the last episode. Both of us, on tape, saying what actually happened. Not for the story. For the record."

He considered her for a moment, and then he smiled — the slow, creased smile that had taken all winter to come back to his face.

"For the record," he agreed. "Yes. I'd like that very much."

* * *

Nell found the lease renewal on the kitchen table of the guesthouse, propped against the salt cellar, with a pen beside it and a note in the landlady's round hand: *Same rate as winter, since you kept the pipes from freezing by being home so much.*

Home. Ada stared at the word for a full minute.

She had meant to stay six weeks. Then eight. Then through the committee hearing, through the fire, through the trap, through the arrest, through the trial preparations — and somewhere in there the six weeks had become seven months, and the suitcase had migrated from the floor to the closet shelf, and she had begun, without deciding to, buying the good coffee instead of the travel-sized kind.

"You're doing the face," Nell said from the doorway. She was eating toast, standing up, in the manner of someone who had lived somewhere long enough to stop pretending otherwise. "The spreadsheet face. You're calculating whether staying is statistically defensible."

"I don't have a spreadsheet face."

"Nell," Nell said, in a fair imitation of Ada's recording voice, "the data suggests a strong correlation between proximity to the subject and quality of outcome."

"That is not my voice."

"It's exactly your voice." Nell set down her toast and picked up the pen and held it out. "Sign it. You can always leave in the autumn. Or you can do what I did, which is sign things and then discover you're glad."

Ada took the pen. Their mother had died owing money to men like Eirik Lund, and Nell had carried that knowledge alone for months rather than wound her sister with it, and they were still — honestly — repairing from that. But repair, Ada had learned, was mostly a matter of showing up repeatedly in the same room until the room stopped being dangerous. Nell had moved into the room next door in November. Neither of them had mentioned it since.

"Why did you stay?" Ada asked, signing. "Originally. After Mother's letters. You got your answers. You could have gone back to Bergen."

Nell looked out the window at the harbor, where the water was doing its bright spring business, boats going in and out, gulls conducting their eternal grievances.

"Because Mom spent thirty years being afraid of this town," she said, "and I wanted to know it as something else. And because—" she shrugged, embarrassed, "—because you were here. And I'd spent ten years only having you in December. Turns out I didn't like the arrangement."

Ada signed the lease. Her hand was steady.

"The wall map comes down today," she said. "I've been putting it off."

"I know. I've been watching you put it off for two weeks." Nell retrieved her toast. "I'll get the screwdriver."

* * *

Taking down the wall map took most of the afternoon, and it became, without anyone planning it, a small ceremony.

The map itself was corkboard and string and forty-odd index cards, and beneath it the wallpaper bore faint pinholes in the shape of everything they'd learned: the harbor office, the cove, the print shop, the ferry dock, the archive wing (burned, rebuilt, reopened with new locks June had personally researched), the committee chamber where Lund had smiled his way through five months of procedure before Friday night undid him.

They worked through it card by card, because it seemed wrong to just pull them down. Each one got read aloud, and each one got a verdict.

*"Witness statement, T. Ness, 1996."* — "Testifying in May," Ada said. "Terrified, but testifying." She unpinned it.

*"Harbor trust ledger, 1994, pages missing."* — "Reconstructed," said June, who had reconstructed them from shipping manifests out of pure archival spite. "In triplicate." Unpinned.

*"Koll, Sander — moves body, orders from L."* — "Serving his sentence. Writes me letters," Halvard said. "Long ones. Very sorry. Sorry takes up a lot of paper with some men." Unpinned.

*"Lund, Eirik — chair."* Ada held that one a moment. On it, in her own handwriting from October, was a single line: *Charming. Helpful. Watch.* She thought about the dinner, the veiled threat about her mother's debts, the certainty she'd walked out with. She thought about Friday night, the boathouse, the match in his hand, Halvard stepping out of the dark, June's phone camera steady as a metronome.

"Guilty," she said simply, and unpinned it, and Nell took it from her and tore it in half and then in half again, and nobody needed to say anything about that.

The last card was the oldest one, the first thing she'd ever pinned up: *"Voss, M. — disappeared 11 Oct 1994. Ruled accidental drowning."*

Ada read it twice. Then she crossed out the second sentence with the pen from the lease, drew a line clean through *ruled accidental drowning*, and wrote underneath, in careful block letters: **MURDERED. CONVICTION PENDING. NAME RESTORED.**

"Now it can come down," she said.

They rolled the cards into a shoebox — June insisted on archival storage, obviously — and Ada wrote on the lid: *Cold Ledger, Case Closed,* and for the first time in three years the words felt like an ending instead of a wound.

The empty wall looked strange. Bare and pale, with its constellation of pinholes, like a sky with the stars removed. Nell stood back and studied it with her head tipped.

"We should hang something there," she said. "Something that isn't about death."

"Marta's class photo," June said at once. "1979. I found it in the school archive. She's third from the left, making the funniest face I have ever seen on an adult human being."

"Yes," said Ada. "Exactly that."

* * *

May came in mild, and with it came the last mail.

Not letters — those had ended in February, when Torvald Ness, in a holding cell, had finally explained himself to Ada through the glass: *I couldn't say it out loud for thirty years. But you filed the truth better than anyone alive. I thought if I gave it to you in pieces, in order, you'd build it into something that couldn't be ignored. I was a coward. I used your courage instead of finding my own.* She had forgiven him, mostly, in the way you forgive a man who handed you a lit torch and also the fire that burned your friends' lives down around it. Time would finish the job or it wouldn't.

No — the May mail was ordinary. Listener letters, dozens of them, arriving since the news broke: prosecutors filing charges, headlines reading *SKARVIK CASE REOPENED AND CLOSED IN THE SAME WINTER*. People wrote to say they'd listened to episode forty-one on a night shift and cried. A woman in Tromsø wrote to say she had reported her brother-in-law after twenty years of silence, and signed it, *For the record.* A man wrote to complain that episode twelve had gotten a ferry timetable wrong, and Halvard laughed so hard at that one he had to sit down.

And one envelope, postmarked Skarvik, containing a photograph.

It was the 1979 class photo. On the back, in a hand Ada didn't recognize, someone had written: *Third from the left. She'd have wanted to be on your wall. — the fifth-grade class of 1987, all grown up now, and grateful.*

Ada carried it upstairs and hung it herself, hammering the nail into the exact center of the bare patch, stepping back, adjusting it a degree, stepping back again. Marta Voss, thirty years old and immortal, pulling a face at a camera, surrounded by children who had never forgotten her.

Below it, the guesthouse kitchen table waited with four chairs around it, because four chairs had become the number without anyone discussing it. Halvard came Tuesdays and Thursdays and pretended it was for the coffee. June came whenever the archive closed and ate like a stevedore. Nell had simply never left.

Ada put the kettle on and stood at the window while it heated, watching the harbor do its evening shining, and let herself feel the thing she had spent three years and one whole winter learning to feel without flinching: that the case was over, and she was not.

Her career-defining exclusive had gone to the prosecution instead of the airwaves, and her download numbers had fallen, and a rival podcast had run a lazy summary episode that got three details wrong, and none of it — genuinely, measurably, none of it — stung the way she'd once assumed it would. There would be other cases. There might even be other podcasts. But there would not be another winter like this one, and she had chosen it, chosen them, with her eyes open, and the choice had turned out to be the best reporting decision of her life: she had finally gotten the story right, including the part about what mattered.

The kettle sang. She made four cups, out of habit, out of hope, out of the certain knowledge that at least two of them would be drunk.

Then she carried hers to the little table by the window — the one where the mail landed, where two envelopes had once arrived side by side and rearranged her entire existence — and sat down in the chair that faced the door.

The chair across from her was empty. The light was going gold on the water. Somewhere up the hill, a bell rang the hour, and downstairs, unhurried, familiar footsteps started up toward the kitchen — more than one set — and Ada smiled, and waited, and was not lonely, and the empty chair where the letters used to arrive was just a chair, and it was almost time for dinner.
