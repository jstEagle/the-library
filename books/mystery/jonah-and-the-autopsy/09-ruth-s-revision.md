## 9. Ruth's Revision

Ruth Ellery called me at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, which told me everything before she said a word. Ruth was a woman of habits so fixed they could have been used to calibrate instruments. She called the pharmacy at nine. She called the diner to complain about the coffee at noon. She did not, under any recorded circumstance, call anyone before eight, because eight was when her program ended and God went back to minding His own business.

"Miss Lockridge." Her voice was a whisper with gravel in it. "I need to amend my statement."

"Good morning to you too, Ruth."

"I need to amend it *now*. Before church. Before—" A breath, shaky as a first attempt at riding a bike. "Before anyone else hears what I have to say."

I sat up in bed, and the gray light through my curtains did its usual gray light things, and I thought: here it comes. Three days since the man in my coat had come off my table and into the county's cold storage, three days since I'd lied to Dane Whitlock with a straight face about the coat's provenance, and the town's oldest nervous system had decided to short-circuit.

"Come to the office at ten," I said. "We'll do it properly. Recorded."

"No." The word cracked like ice on a pond. "Not the office. Not anywhere with—there are people in the office, Miss Lockridge. People who write things down." A pause. "Your house. Can I come to your house? Nobody watches your house."

*You'd be surprised*, I thought, and then wished I hadn't, because the thought had a shape to it now, a shape I'd been refusing to look at directly, the way you refuse to look at a mole until the doctor insists.

"Ten o'clock," I said. "Coffee's terrible. You've said so publicly."

* * *

Ruth Ellery arrived at 9:52 with a casserole dish she did not offer to me and would not set down for the entire visit. It contained, as far as I could tell through the glass, a layer of green beans and beneath that, judging by the way she gripped it, the full weight of her conscience.

She sat on the edge of my sofa like it might bill her. She was seventy-one and had taught fourth grade for thirty-eight years and had buried a husband and a son, and I had watched her at funerals—everyone in Marrow Creek had watched Ruth Ellery at funerals, because she was the town's designated weeper, the woman whose grief gave everyone else permission. I had never seen her frightened. Grief she could carry. This was different. This sat on her like weather.

"Tell me," I said.

"I saw him." She looked at the casserole dish. "The drowned man. I told Sheriff Whitlock I saw him last Thursday, by the feed store, wearing your gray coat, and I told him he looked lost, and that part was true, he did look lost, like a man reading directions to a place he'd already been." She swallowed. "But that's not what I told the sheriff, and I need to fix it before it dries."

"Fix what part?"

"The part where I said I'd never seen him before." Her eyes came up and fixed on mine, and they were wet, but not funeral-wet. "Miss Lockridge, I'd seen him before. I've seen him a dozen times. He used to come to the library on Saturdays. He'd sit in the periodicals and read the *Gazette* from back to front, obituaries first, and he always—" her voice did something complicated— "he always sat facing the window. Facing the street. Facing your office across the square. I thought he was a lonely man. I used to bring him coffee. He never drank it. He just held it, like it was something he was keeping safe for somebody."

The room got very quiet. My refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside a dog was failing to catch something.

"Ruth," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to, "when did these Saturdays start?"

"Last spring. March, maybe. He had a way of—" She stopped. Started again. "You know how some people you remember by their hands? His hands were always clean. Always. A workingman's coat and hands like a surgeon. And he knew things. He'd mention things. Little things. He knew the diner changed its pie supplier in February. He knew old Mr. Havel had a second reading of his will. He knew—" and here she set the casserole dish down on my coffee table with a click that sounded, in that quiet, like a lock turning— "he knew you'd had the flu in April. He said, 'I hope the coroner's feeling better,' and I never told him you'd been sick, and I never told him your name, because everybody here just calls you Miss Sable or the coroner, and he said *coroner* like it was a title he was proud of you for holding."

I want to tell you that I felt dread. I want to tell you the floor tilted. What I actually felt was worse and stranger: I felt *recognized*. Somewhere across three years of careful, orderly, anonymous living, someone had been watching me the way I watched bodies—attentively, respectfully, noting every mark—and some traitorous cellar of my heart had found that less frightening than flattering.

I hate that about myself. I've had time to sit with it since, and I still hate it.

"Ruth. Why recant? Why now?"

She looked at the window. My curtains were drawn, but the light came through them the way light does, gray and total, and she looked at the light like it might be taking notes.

"Because yesterday someone left something in my mailbox," she said. "And it wasn't mail."

* * *

She wouldn't say what it was. She said it was at home, and she said she hadn't touched it since, and she said—this was the part that made the back of my neck go cold—"I told Sheriff Whitlock my first statement was true and I'm sticking to it, and he wrote that down, and I signed it, because I thought that would make it stop. Making things official. That's what you do to make things stop. Isn't it?"

"Sometimes," I said, "it's what you do to make things worse."

She left the casserole. I let her. Green beans and conscience, going stale together on my coffee table.

I drove her home. She wanted me to. She didn't say so, but she sat in my passenger seat with her hands folded like a child on a church pew, and when I pulled up in front of her little house on Corliss Street—the house with the wind chimes made of spoons, because Ruth Ellery had always been exactly that kind of woman—she looked at the mailbox the way you look at a dog that bit you once.

"I'll look at it," I said.

"You won't touch it?"

"Ruth. I'm the coroner. Touching things is the whole job."

That got half a laugh out of her, which I counted as a medical outcome.

* * *

The mailbox was the old-fashioned kind, a rounded tin box on a wooden post, painted the same faded teal as her shutters. The little door was shut. The little red flag was up, which Ruth said she hadn't raised.

I put on gloves. Not because of procedure. Because of what I was afraid I already knew.

Inside the box was a single envelope. No stamp. No address. Her name on the front in block letters, *RUTH*, and the letters were wrong in a way I couldn't name yet, drawn rather than written, like someone disguising a hand they were ashamed of.

I opened it standing there on Ruth Ellery's walk with the spoon chimes turning slowly in the wind behind me.

One index card. The kind I used for morgue inventory, back when the county's budget still allowed index cards. On it, in handwriting I would have known at the bottom of the ocean, at the bottom of a well, in the dark, in my sleep:

*Ruth — You were kind to me. That's why you're safe. Stop talking. Not a threat. A promise. — J*

The *J* was the killer. Not the word. The letter. Jonah wrote his J's the way our father taught him, with a crossbar, old-fashioned, a little flourish at the top like the letter was taking a bow. I'd spent my whole childhood watching that J appear on the tops of my homework pages, checking my math, correcting my spelling. *Sable, you spelled "separate" wrong again. There's *a rat* in separate.*

My brother had been in Marrow Creek since March. Sitting in the library with his back to the obituaries and his face to my window. Holding coffee he never drank. Learning me.

And now he had been to Ruth's mailbox, and Ruth had already gone to the sheriff and signed a statement saying her first lie was true, and Jonah knew she'd gone to the sheriff, because Jonah knew everything, because Jonah had made it his life's work to know everything, and the note said *you're safe* the way our father used to say *this is for your own good* right before it wasn't.

"Sable?" Ruth's voice from the porch. "Is it bad?"

I looked at the card a long time. Then I put it in an evidence bag from my coat pocket—yes, I carry them, don't you?—and walked up to the porch and sat down next to her on the bench her husband had built, and I did the hardest thing I have done in a life that has since gotten much harder: I told the truth to one person.

"Ruth," I said, "do you remember the Lockridge boy? The one who drowned in 2016?"

Her mouth opened. Closed. Thirty-eight years of fourth graders had taught her every expression a human face could make, and the one she made now was the one for a student who has finally, finally understood the lesson.

"That was him," she said. "In the library. In your coat." Not a question. "Oh, Miss Sable. Oh, honey." Her hand found mine, dry and warm and trembling slightly. "He's been *watching* you."

"Since March," I said. "Maybe longer."

"What does he want?"

I thought about the coat on the dead man. The scarf gone from my drawer. The watch gone from my nightstand. The way the victims were dressed—dressed *by* someone, dressed *as* someone. I thought about a boy who used to play coroner with me in our father's funeral home, who used to let me be the doctor because he liked being the body, who used to say, *when we grow up, we'll work together, you'll find out how everyone died and I'll keep the records.*

"I don't know," I said, which was a lie, because I was starting to know, and the knowing was a door I did not want to open, because on the other side of it was a room I'd been keeping shut since I was nineteen years old.

* * *

I drove back to my house and sat in the driveway for twenty minutes with the engine off and the evidence bag on the passenger seat, and I had a long, honest argument with myself, and I lost.

Here's the argument. Here's the version I'd give a jury, if it ever came to that, and God forgive me, it would: *I didn't go to the sheriff with the note because the note wasn't evidence of a crime. It was a threat, technically, but it said 'you're safe,' and threats that promise safety don't hold up, and Ruth had already recanted, and there was nothing to investigate.* That's the version. Here's the truth: I didn't take the note to Dane Whitlock because the note was in my brother's handwriting, and if Dane looked at my brother's handwriting, Dane would ask why a dead man's handwriting was familiar to the coroner, and I had already lied to Dane once about a coat, and lies are like bodies—they get heavier the longer you carry them, and I could not carry two.

One lie was a mistake. Two was a policy.

I went inside and locked the door and stood in my kitchen and did the thing I'd been not-doing for three days. I said it out loud, to the empty room, in the voice you use when the prayer has to be specific enough to count:

"Jonah is alive."

The refrigerator hummed. The gray light did its gray light things. Nothing in the universe rearranged itself to acknowledge that I had finally caught up to what my own closet had been telling me.

And then—because I am my father's daughter and my brother's sister and a woman who has spent three years believing that order is the same thing as safety—I did the only thing I knew how to do with a truth that size.

I sat down at my kitchen table, and I took out a legal pad, and I started making a list.

Not of suspects. Not of evidence. A list of my files. Every case file in the Marrow Creek morgue, every report I'd filed in three years, every drawer number, every date. Because if Jonah had been here since March, if he'd been reading the *Gazette* obituaries back to front and watching my window, then there was one place in this town where my whole life was written down in my own hand, organized, alphabetized, waiting—

—and I suddenly, with the full force of a body hitting water, needed to know whether my files had been read.

The list ran to forty-one entries. At entry thirty-one, my pen stopped.

I had filed that one myself. I remembered the date, the drawer, the rain on the loading dock. I remembered writing the name.

The name on my list was not the name in my memory.

Someone had corrected it.
