## 11. Interrogation by Firelight

There are two ways to interrogate a person, and I've tried both enough times to have opinions.

The first way is the room with the metal table and the fluorescent hum and the tape recorder blinking its little red eye of judgment. That works fine on men who stole outboard motors and teenagers who egged the church van. It works terribly on anyone who has spent years around death, because people who work with death develop a relationship with silence that most interrogators can't compete with. You sit them down under bad light and they simply wait you out, calm as a pond, while you realize halfway through your third question that you're the one sweating.

The second way is dinner.

I learned that from my mother, who could extract a confession from any of her five children using nothing but pot roast and the strategic deployment of *so, anything you want to tell me?* delivered at exactly the moment the gravy was poured. My mother believed—and I've come to agree—that people lie better when they're performing for an audience and worse when they're comfortable. Comfort is the trap. You feed a person, you warm their hands, you let the fire do the softening, and then you ask the question you've been carrying around all day like a stone in your boot.

Which is why Sable Lockridge was sitting across from me at my kitchen table on a Thursday night in October, watching me ladle venison stew into bowls, and why she had no idea—none whatsoever, I'd have bet my badge on it—that she was already caught.

Or so I thought. We'll get to that.

* * *

Let me back up, because fairness matters even in a story where I come out looking like half a fool.

I'd known Sable for three years, which in Marrow Creek terms is practically a marriage. She arrived in the fall of '19 with a state certification, a rental house on Delaney Street, and a way of talking about corpses that made the whole grim business sound like filing taxes. The town took to her the way towns like ours take to anyone competent and uncurious about our business—which is to say warmly, and without ever quite letting her inside. She came to the pancake breakfast. She did not come to anybody's kitchen. She visited graves on Sundays like she was keeping an appointment, and I knew because I'd seen her there twice, standing over stones with no flowers in her hands, just standing, like a woman reading a document only she could see.

I liked her. I want that on the record before anything else I say about this. I liked her dryness, the way she never performed grief at funerals but always stayed until the last car left, the way she called the dead "the guests" as if the morgue were a hotel and she ran a tight ship. In three years she'd given me no reason to doubt her beyond the ordinary human allotment.

Then a drowned man washed up at Miller's Bend wearing her coat.

I didn't know it was her coat at first. What I knew was this: the victim had no ID, no wallet, no phone, and fingertips wrinkled past the point of printing at the scene, though the lab got partials eventually. His clothes were good quality, clean, wrong for swimming. And when I'd mentioned the gray wool coat to Sable in passing—*nice coat on our floater, somebody's missing a hundred bucks*—she had looked at it for one second too long. Not a flinch. Sable doesn't flinch. It was more like a librarian seeing a book shelved in the wrong section: recognition, plus a small private recalculation.

I filed that away. I'm a sheriff in a town of four thousand; filing things away is most of the job.

Then Ruth Ellery told Deputy Cho she'd seen the man alive the week before, walking up Route 9 in "that coroner's coat," and the story had holes in it you could drive the hearse through—she said Tuesday, then Thursday; she said he was alone, then that he was talking to someone in a truck she couldn't describe except that it was dark-colored, which in Marrow Creek describes roughly sixty percent of vehicles. But the core of it stuck. The dead man had been wearing the coroner's coat while alive, and now the coroner wasn't saying much about it.

And then there was the thing I hadn't told anyone, including myself, in full sentences: the case file. I'd gone back through it one night, the way I do when I can't sleep, and I noticed that the intake notes contained a detail about the victim's boots—the particular resoling, the stitch pattern—that nobody at the scene had mentioned to me. When I asked Cho where it came from, he shrugged and said Dr. Lockridge must have added it after her exam. Fine. Normal. Except the phrasing of the note matched nothing else she'd ever written. Three years of her reports sit in my records room, and I know her sentences the way you know a friend's knock on your door. This sentence knocked wrong.

So: dinner.

* * *

She arrived at seven with a bottle of wine and an apology for being late that was fifteen minutes more accurate than her actual lateness, which is to say she'd sat in her car outside my house for ten minutes deciding whether to come in. I know because I watched her from the window like a creep, and because I've done the same thing outside other people's houses, and we recognize our own.

"Something smells illegal," she said, handing me the bottle. A cheap red, the kind you bring when you're not trying to make a statement.

"That's just my cooking."

"You hunted this deer yourself?"

"I hunted it. The deer lost."

"Good." She sat down at my table like she'd sat there a hundred times, which she had, maybe six times, and looked at the fire I'd built in the hearth with an expression I couldn't read. "You don't usually burn wood in October."

"Chilly week." It was fifty-five degrees. I'd built the fire because firelight makes people honest. My mother again. The woman should have run the FBI.

We ate. I did the small talk properly—her garden dying for the season, the roof repair on the courthouse, Deputy Cho's disastrous attempt to grow a mustache. She laughed in the right places. She ate like a woman with an appetite, which I noted and disliked noting, because appetite can be performed too, and once you start cataloguing a friend's chewing you've already left decent country behind.

Halfway through the stew I said, casual as passing the salt, "Ruth Ellery came by the office today."

Sable's spoon continued its arc from bowl to mouth without deviation. "How is Ruth?"

"Confused, mostly. She's telling a different story every day about our floater. Saw him Tuesday. No, Thursday. Alone. No, with a truck." I shrugged. "You know how witnesses are. The truth comes out of them like thread off a spool—pull it wrong and it knots."

"Mm." She tore a piece of bread. "Did she see him or not?"

"That's what I keep asking her." I let a beat pass. "She keeps saying the coat. Gray wool, brass buttons. Says she'd know it anywhere because—" and here I watched Sable's face the way you watch a door you suspect is going to open—"because she sees you wear it all winter. Church, mostly. The hardware store."

"She has a good eye," Sable said. "For coats."

"For coats," I agreed.

Silence, but a working silence, both of us doing arithmetic. I refilled her wine. Outside, wind moved through the pines along my property line with that long ocean sound they make, and the fire popped, and I understood suddenly why my mother had loved this method so much—it wasn't the comfort, exactly. It was that in firelight everyone looks guilty, so the guilty have nowhere to hide and the innocent have nothing to prove. Level ground. All you have to do is talk long enough for the real person to walk out from behind the polite one.

"Sable." I set my spoon down. "Where's your coat?"

And here is the thing I will think about for the rest of my life, however long the county lets me keep having one: she lied to me immediately, fluently, and badly.

"Gave it away," she said. "End of last season. There's a donation bin behind the Methodist church."

"The one that got padlocked in August because kids kept sleeping in it?"

"They fixed it."

"They didn't. I signed the work order myself." I kept my voice pleasant, almost apologetic, the voice you use for delivering bad news about weather. "Also, Ruth says she saw the man in the coat last week. If you gave it away months ago, either Ruth's lying, the church bin has a very active afterlife, or somebody pulled it out of wherever it went and put it on a drowning man."

Sable looked at her wine. Then at the fire. Then at me, and for a moment—not long, a second, maybe two—I saw something underneath the coroner's composure that made the hair on my arms stand up under my flannel. It wasn't guilt. I've seen guilt; guilt is shifty and wet. This was older and sadder than guilt. It was the look of a person standing in a burning house trying to decide which photograph to save.

"Dane," she said, "do you think I killed a man and dressed him in my own coat?"

"No," I said, and meant it, and hated how fast I meant it. "I think you know something about that coat you're not saying. Those are different crimes."

"Are they?" Something almost like humor crossed her face, thin as ice on a puddle. "In this town they might be the same."

"Try me anyway."

She was quiet for a while. The fire worked. I'll give the fire credit; it earned its keep. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed register—lower, more careful, the voice she used for describing wounds to grieving families.

"I noticed the coat at the autopsy. I recognized it. I own—or owned—a coat exactly like it. Same cut, same buttons. I bought it in—" a pause, smooth as a road repaved overnight, "—in Rochester, before I moved here. I assumed it was mine. I checked my closet afterward and mine was still there. Two identical coats. Coincidence. I didn't report it because reporting it would have sounded insane, and because there was nothing to report."

It was a good answer. Structurally sound. Emotionally calibrated. And it was a lie, or at least a lie wearing a true answer's clothes, because I had spent twenty minutes in her donation history and her trash pickup schedule—God forgive me—and there is no record of any coat leaving her house, and because when she said *mine was still there* her eyes went not to me but to the middle distance, to some interior closet I couldn't see, doing inventory.

"Two identical coats," I repeated.

"People buy the same coat twice, Dane. You own four identical flannels."

"These are work shirts. That's different."

"It's exactly the same."

"It is not—" I stopped, because we were arguing about flannels, which meant she was winning, which meant I'd let her steer, which meant I was worse at this than my mother had been at forty percent of the effort. I leaned back. "Okay. Say I believe you. Two coats. Explain the boots."

"What about the boots?"

"The intake note. Resoled with a double row of stitching, cobbler in Halloway. Nobody at the scene saw that. It showed up in the file in your handwriting."

"My handwriting," she said slowly, "is on every page of that file. I wrote the report."

"Not that line. That line came in later. Different pen pressure. I've been reading your reports for three years, Sable. I know how you hold a pen on good days and bad days."

That landed. I watched it land. Whatever armor she'd assembled over the stew developed a crack right down the middle, and through the crack I glimpsed not a killer but something more frightening: a tired woman carrying a weight she'd decided, for reasons of her own, that I couldn't be trusted to help carry.

"Dane." She set her napkin down and folded her hands, and for a moment she was so completely the professional—calm, precise, kind in the clinical way—that I almost stood down. Almost. "If I tell you that I'm handling this, would you believe me?"

"Handling what?"

"This. The case. There are things I need to check before they go in a file. Things that—if I'm wrong—would hurt people for no reason. If I'm right, they'd hurt people anyway, but for a reason." She held my eyes. "Can you give me a week?"

Every instinct I have as a lawman said *no*. Every instinct I have as a human being—who has his own locked drawer, thank you, everyone does, and mine has things in it from 2016 that I think about at 3 a.m.—said *yes*, and worse, said it with relief.

"A week," I said. "Then you come to me with whatever it is, or I stop asking nicely."

"Fair." She picked up her wine and drank, and her hand was steady, and I remember thinking: *steady hands, steady hands, God, she's good.*

We finished dinner talking about Cho's mustache. She helped me wash dishes, standing hip to hip at the sink, drying bowls while I washed, and it was so domestic and so easy that I felt sick with it, because underneath the dish soap and the firelight I was building a case against my friend and she knew it, probably, and was drying my grandmother's bowl anyway.

At the door, she paused with her hand on the frame.

"Dane. Whatever you think is happening—" she turned, and the porch light caught her face, and she looked, for one unguarded instant, exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with the hour, "—don't decide about me yet. Promise me that. Just don't decide yet."

"I haven't decided anything," I said, which was true, and also the single worst lie I told that night, and I'd heard several good ones over the stew.

She walked to her car. I watched until the taillights cleared the oaks. Then I went inside, banked the fire, and sat down at my kitchen table with the case file and a legal pad, and started making a list of everything Sable Lockridge had ever told me that I hadn't checked.

The list was short. That was the problem. For three years I'd trusted her so completely that I had never once checked anything, and now the checking was going to take a while, and somewhere in the middle of that list, between *the coat* and *the boots* and *the week she needs*, I wrote a fourth item and stared at it for a long time:

*2016. Ask her about 2016.*

Because here's what I haven't said yet, what I'd been circling all evening like a moth around the lamp: the drowned man's partial prints had come back that afternoon, flagged, run twice, confirmed by the state lab. They belonged to Jonah Lockridge. Sable's brother. A boy who drowned off Halloway Point seven years ago, whose funeral I attended, whose father shook my hand at the reception with a grip like a vise and eyes like a man paying off a debt.

A boy whose grave I drive past every Sunday on my way to church.

I sat with that until the fire burned down to orange ribs. Then I picked up the phone to call the state lab and confirm what I already knew, and the phone rang in my hand before I could dial.

Dispatch. Cho's voice, tight and young. "Sheriff—you up? We got a call. Body found behind the old elementary school on Hollow Creek Road. Female. Retired teacher, they think—Marge Ostrander." A pause, and I heard him swallow. "Sheriff, she's dressed weird. Like a costume or something. Old-fashioned dress. You're gonna want to see this one yourself."

I looked at the legal pad. At the word *2016*.

"Yeah," I said, reaching for my keys. "Yeah, I'm gonna want to see it."
