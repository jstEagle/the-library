## 12. The Third Dressing

The call came at 5:47 on a Tuesday morning, which in my experience is the hour reserved by God for delivering news that ruins the rest of the week.

"Sable." Sheriff Dane Whitlock's voice was doing something I'd never heard it do before. It was careful. Dane had many registers—bored, irritated, the particular flatness he used when he had to tell a mother something no one should ever have to say—but never careful. Careful meant he'd rehearsed. "We've got a body out past the old tannery road. You should probably come see this one yourself."

"You always say that."

"This time I mean it like a man means things."

I hung up and stood in my kitchen holding a coffee mug I hadn't drunk from, listening to the house settle around me. Three years I'd lived in Marrow Creek, and the house still felt like a rental I was housesitting for a stranger named Sable Lockridge. Lately it had started feeling like a stage set, too—like someone came through when I wasn't home and rearranged the props to mean something.

I checked my closet before I left. Habit now, third day running. Gray wool coat: present. Blue scarf: present. Father's watch, which was technically mine by inheritance and technically missing by theft: still gone. Everything else hung in its rows like patient witnesses waiting to be called.

Everything else, I would learn an hour later, was not the point.

* * *

The old tannery road runs along the creek where the water goes slow and brown and apologetic, past foundations of buildings that gave up decades ago. The county keeps meaning to pave it and the county keeps not doing it, which is Marrow Creek in a sentence. A deputy's cruiser sat angled at the shoulder with its lights going, painting the fog red and blue in slow alternation, and beyond it three figures stood in a loose triangle around something on the ground.

Dane was one of the figures. He turned when he heard my boots on the gravel, and his face did a thing I want to describe accurately because it mattered: it didn't flinch. It *prepared*. Like a man watching weather come in that he'd already decided not to mention.

"Morning," he said.

"Who is she?"

"Nobody knows yet. That's part of it." He stepped aside. "Look at her hands first."

I crouched over the body—a woman, mid-forties maybe, lying on her back in the weeds with her arms arranged at her sides, palms up, in a posture of almost comical surrender. She'd been dead twelve hours or so. Cause of death wasn't immediately obvious, which already told me it wouldn't be drowning, because Jonah—or whoever I was still pretending this might be, though by then the pretense was mostly scaffolding—had done drowning.

Then I looked at what she was wearing, and the world performed its small quiet trick of removing the floor.

Boots. Brown leather ankle boots with a scar across the left toe cap where I'd caught them on a morgue drawer two winters ago. Jeans, dark wash, frayed at the right hem because I kept meaning to fix them and kept not fixing them. The charcoal henley I owned two of. And over it all, unbuttoned, hanging open—

My gray wool coat.

Not a coat like it. Not a coat from the same store. My coat, with the burn mark on the inner pocket lining from a cigarette I'd never admit to anyone, including myself, that I'd smoked behind the funeral home during my mother's wake.

"That's—" Deputy Ruiz started.

"I know whose coat it is," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. Somewhere under the level voice there was a scream being filed away for later, alphabetized, put in a drawer. "It's mine."

"It's yours," Dane repeated. Not a question. A man laying a brick down carefully so nobody hears it land.

"From my closet."

"Uh-huh." He looked at me then, fully, and I understood that whatever we had been to each other—friends, colleagues, the two people in town who ate lunch together without talking—we were standing on opposite sides of something now, and the something was widening by the minute. "Sable. Whose clothes are those on you right now?"

I looked down at myself. Charcoal henley. Dark jeans. Boots—the other pair, the black ones, but still. I had dressed in the dark, as I do most mornings, pulling on the uniform of myself without looking, and now the uniform was evidence.

"Mine," I said. "Obviously."

"Obviously," Dane said, and wrote nothing down, which was somehow worse than if he'd written it down.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about seeing your own clothes on a corpse: it isn't horror, exactly. Horror has a temperature, a rush, a body-response. This was colder than that. It was recognition—the specific vertigo of catching your reflection in a window and having it not move when you do. The woman on the ground wore me the way a mannequin wears a display, faithfully and completely wrong. Whoever had dressed her knew the outfit. Knew the order of layers. Knew about the coat's burn mark, which meant they'd been close enough to study it, which meant—

Which meant stop. Which meant later. Which meant do your job.

"ID?" I asked, because work is the only prayer I know.

"Purse had a library card. Ruth Ann Voss. Teaches—taught—fourth grade over in Halloway, commutes through here." Dane handed me a plastic sleeve. The card photo showed a round-faced woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair, smiling the guarded smile of people who don't love cameras. "Retired last spring. Lives alone."

Lived alone. Tense is such a brutal little machine.

"I'll need her medical history, next of kin, the works," I said. "And I need the scene processed before we move her. Full documentation. Every seam of every garment."

"You're going to document the clothes."

"I'm going to document everything. That's the job."

"The clothes are *you*, Sable."

"And if I skip them, everyone gets to say the coroner hid the evidence that incriminated her." I heard my own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "Let them say it anyway. But let the file be clean."

Dane held my gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded once, slow, and turned to give Ruiz instructions, and I knelt back down beside Ruth Ann Voss and began the work of turning a person into a case, which is either the most merciful thing my profession does or the most monstrous, depending on the day.

Her fingernails were clean. No defensive wounds. Ligature marks faint on the throat—strangulation, probably manual, probably by someone strong and unhurried. Her face, under the early light, held an expression I've seen on too many of the dead: not terror, just surprise, the look of someone interrupted mid-thought by the end of thought.

And pinned inside the collar of my coat, where only someone processing the garment would find it, a slip of paper, folded twice.

I should have called Dane over. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this file, if anyone ever does: I *should* have.

I unfolded it instead.

No words. Just a single character written in blue ink in a hand I would have known blindfolded, upside down, dissolved in grief or drunk on sleep: a neat, schoolroom-perfect **J**, followed by a period. As if signing a test. As if saying *present*.

As if saying: *see? Still keeping score.*

I folded it back along its creases and slid it into an evidence bag, and my hand did not shake, and I have never been more frightened of my own steadiness.

* * *

By noon, the town had done what towns do, which is metabolize a stranger's death into material.

I stopped at the Gas-N-Go for coffee I didn't want, because I needed to hear what was being said, and Marrow Creek's gas station between eight and noon is its true seat of government. The bell over the door announced me and the conversation did the thing conversations do—the sudden drop, the too-loud restart about nothing, feed prices, the weather, anything—and then Deena Marsh, who runs the register and the town's actual intelligence apparatus, looked at me over the lottery tickets and said, with terrible kindness:

"Heard you got called out to the tannery road early."

"Heard right."

"Awful thing." A pause calibrated to the millimeter. "They saying much about... how she was found?"

"They're saying she was found."

"Mm." Deena rang up my coffee with the solemnity of a notary. "Folks are saying she was dressed funny. Dressed up like somebody." Her eyes came up, mild as milk. "Like somebody particular."

"Deena."

"I'm not saying anything, honey. I'm asking if there's anything to say." She slid my change across. "Three bodies in two months, though. And the second one in that poor teacher's mother's dress, and now this. Folks notice patterns. It's not personal."

But it was personal. That was the whole architecture of it—it had been personal from the first drowned man in my coat, and the town could smell the personal-ness the way animals smell weather. I carried my coffee out past the bulletin board where someone had already pinned a printout of the tannery road scene—someone had *photographed* it before the tape went up, because privacy died quietly sometime while I was busy elsewhere—and beneath it, in ballpoint on a sheet torn from a legal pad, someone had written:

**WHO IS SHE SUPPOSED TO BE THIS TIME?**

Underneath, in different handwriting: **ask the coroner.**

* * *

Ruth Ann Voss's daughter drove in from Halloway that afternoon to identify the body formally, a formality that exists mostly to make the living feel the machinery cares. She was maybe twenty-five, wearing grief like a borrowed coat—one size too big, sleeves hanging past her hands. She stood at the viewing window and made a sound I will not transcribe, and then she gripped the sill and said, "Why was she dressed like that? The deputy said—somebody said she was dressed like somebody. Who?"

"There's an investigation ongoing," I said, from the far side of the glass, wearing the original of everything her mother was wearing.

The girl looked at me. Really looked. And I watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes—not accusation, not yet, just the raw material of it, the sum beginning to assemble itself. Her mother, dressed as the coroner. The coroner, standing here, alive, in the same clothes.

"Did you know her?" the girl asked.

"No."

"She taught fourth grade for thirty years. She kept every class photo on the wall of her classroom. Hundreds of kids." The girl wiped her nose with her sleeve. "Somebody took all that and put it in a costume."

Yes, I wanted to say. Yes, exactly, you understand it faster than the sheriff does. Someone is dressing the dead in the archive of my life, page by page, and I don't know why, and I can't say any of that out loud because every word of it sounds insane and every word of it also sounds guilty.

"What I can tell you," I said instead, "is that she went quickly, and she wasn't afraid long, and I will handle her with care. That's a promise I can keep."

She nodded and left, and I stood alone in the observation room looking through the glass at my own coat rising and falling on no breath at all, and I thought: *he's getting better at this.* The first body had been approximate. The second, precise. This one was fluent—seams aligned, hems even, the boots laced the way I lace them, left over right, because of a childhood habit I'd never told a living soul.

Except one.

* * *

That evening Dane came to the morgue without calling ahead, which he knows I hate, which means he wanted me off balance. He stood in the doorway of my office holding a folder against his chest like a shield and didn't sit.

"Autopsy findings," I said, to fill the space.

"Give 'em to me plain."

"Strangulation. Manual. Time of death roughly eleven p.m. to two a.m. No sexual assault. No struggle. Toxicology pending, but I'm guessing clean—she trusted whoever was close enough to touch her throat."

"Trusted." He set the folder on my desk but didn't let go of it. "You know what's eating at me, Sable?"

"Tell me."

"The dress on the second victim. Mrs. Halloran's dress. You identified it in under a minute. Called it a nineteen-eighties Talbots, said your mother bought it for Easter service the year before she got sick." His jaw worked. "I ran that detail past the Halloran boy. He says nobody outside the family could've known the provenance of that dress. Nobody."

"My mother talked about her clothes the way some people talk about scripture. Half the town heard that story at one funeral lunch or another."

"Maybe." He finally released the folder and sat, heavily, the way men sit when sitting is a concession. "Maybe. And maybe I'm asking the wrong questions and I'll regret it. But I've known you six years, and I watched you lie to me twice this month—small lies, dumb lies, the kind you tell when the truth costs something—and I keep thinking about the fact that every single piece of evidence in these cases points at one person, and that person keeps finding the evidence first."

The fluorescent light buzzed. Down the hall, a compressor cycled on, the building breathing its mechanical breath.

"If I were framing you," I said quietly, "would I use my own coat?"

"If you were smart? Sure. Nobody frames themselves. It's the oldest misdirection there is." He rubbed his eyes. "Or if you weren't smart—if you were sick, or scared, or covering for somebody—"

"Covering for who, Dane? Name one person I'd burn my life down for."

He looked at me for a long time, and something moved behind his face—something old, something he'd been carrying longer than this case, maybe longer than our friendship—and he didn't answer. He picked his folder back up, stood, and paused at the door.

"State's sending somebody," he said. "An agent. Bureau. Should be here end of the week."

"A federal agent. For a town of nine hundred."

"For a town of nine hundred with three staged bodies and a coroner the whole diner's voting on." He half-turned. "For what it's worth—and I know what it's worth, which is approximately nothing—I voted for you too. So far."

"So far," I repeated.

"So far," he said, and left me alone with the hum of the lights and the file on my desk and, three rooms away, my own name lying cold on a steel table, wearing my face's absence, waiting for someone to ask it who it really was.

I locked up late. On my way out, I passed the intake bay, and the night attendant mentioned, offhand, that a man had come by around dusk asking whether the coroner worked evenings.

"Did you get a name?" I asked.

"Nah. Kept his collar up. Funny thing, though—he thanked me. Real polite." The attendant shrugged. "Said to tell you he's proud of how thorough I am."

"How thorough *you* are."

"That's what I said too." He laughed. "Town's full of weirdos."

I walked to my car in the dark, keys between my fingers, and did not run, because running would have meant admitting what I already knew: that somewhere out past the edge of the lot, in the dark that had been watching me for months, my brother was grading my performance.

And tomorrow, a stranger from the state would arrive to grade it too.
