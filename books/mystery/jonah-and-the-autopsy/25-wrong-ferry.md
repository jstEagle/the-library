## 25. Wrong Ferry

I've been a lawman long enough to know that most stakeouts are theater. You sit in a dark car drinking bad coffee, watching a door that will never open, and you tell yourself the waiting is the work. Ninety percent of policing is just being present when the world decides to misbehave. The other ten percent is paperwork about it afterward.

But this stakeout had a different flavor. This one had hope in it, and hope is the worst thing you can bring to an ambush.

The ferry dock at Marrow Creek is a sad little appendage of a town that stopped needing ferries sometime in the nineties. The state kept one running out of stubbornness or budget-line inertia—a flat-bottomed thing that crossed to the far shore twice a day and carried mostly commuters, occasionally a truck full of feed, and once, memorably, a man with a llama. At eleven p.m. on a Thursday it carried nobody at all, which was exactly why we'd picked it.

The plan was Priya's, and it was good. That's what I kept telling myself. *The plan was good.* Sable would pack her car in plain view—duffel bags, a gas can, the whole amateur-fleeing-the-country production—and a "leaked" itinerary would place her on the 11:40 ferry. Jonah Lockridge had spent seven years reading his sister like a case file. He would come to intercept her. We would be there first.

Simple. Clean. The kind of plan that works in a report.

"You're doing the jaw thing," Priya said from the passenger seat. She had a thermos of coffee she'd been nursing for three hours without appearing to drink any of it, which I suspected was some kind of federal skill they taught at Quantico between surveillance law and how to make locals feel stupid.

"What jaw thing."

"The clenching. Like you're chewing through a problem." She glanced over. "You've been doing it since we staged the car."

"I'm fine."

"You're chewing."

I unclenched. Somewhere down the dock line, in the treeline east of the parking apron, Deputy Alvarez sat in a second vehicle with the engine off and the windows cracked, freezing with the particular dignity of a man who'd volunteered for the cold post because the alternative was listening to me and Priya not-talk for another four hours.

Sable's car sat in the lot where we'd left it, positioned for maximum visibility. Packed. Duffels visible through the rear window. A scarf draped over the passenger seat like a set designer's idea of haste. She'd argued against the scarf—said Jonah would know she never left scarves lying around loose—but Priya had wanted the detail, and Sable had gone quiet in that way she went quiet, the way of a woman who had learned somewhere along the line that her instincts were worth less than other people's plans.

That should have been my first warning. I'd seen her do it before. Every time Sable Lockridge folded up her own judgment and put it away, something downstream went wrong. I was the sheriff. Noticing things was nominally my job.

But here's what I'll confess, since confession seems to be the theme of my late middle age: part of me wasn't watching the treeline that night. Part of me was watching the memory of Sable's face when she'd handed over her house keys so we could stage the packing. There'd been a look on it—not fear, exactly. Something more like an accountant reviewing a ledger and finding a column that didn't add. She'd known something was off about our own trap, and she'd let us run it anyway, because the alternative was telling us everything, and telling us everything meant admitting how long she'd known her brother was alive and said nothing.

Guilt makes people cooperative. It doesn't make them honest.

* * *

At 11:15, the fog came in off the water.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. Fog on the creek isn't weather; it's a decision the river makes. One minute the dock lights are sharp little coins in the dark, and the next they're smudged, halved, and the treeline goes from *trees* to *the idea of trees*. Radio reception dies by degrees. Your own headlights become two pale suggestions pointing nowhere.

Priya keyed her mic. "Alvarez. Visibility check."

Static. Then: "Can't see the water. Can see maybe thirty feet of dock. Nothing moving."

"Copy. Hold positions."

We held. That's the comedy of it—we were so disciplined. Two vehicles, three officers, a federal agent with a profile she believed in, all holding position in a fog bank while the man we were hunting presumably watched us hold it. Because that's what he did, we learned later. He'd been there since dusk. He'd watched Priya and me walk the dock at nine, tap the pilings, discuss sight lines. He'd watched Alvarez park. He'd probably watched us drink the coffee. Jonah Lockridge didn't defeat our trap. He attended it. Sat in the back row. Took notes.

At 11:38, two minutes before the ferry's arrival, my radio crackled.

"Sheriff." Alvarez, voice pitched low and wrong. "I've got movement at the north end. Someone at the ticket booth."

"On my way. Priya—"

But Priya was already out of the car, moving toward the dock with her weapon drawn and her flashlight off, because federal agents are trained to close distance and small-town sheriffs are trained to think about who else might be moving while everyone's looking at the movement. Old instinct, the kind you don't choose. I looked at the fog behind us instead of ahead.

Empty road. Empty lot except Sable's staged car.

And then I made the mistake. I'll own it plainly: I got out of the vehicle.

Not to chase anything. Just to reposition—to get eyes on both approaches, mine and Alvarez's, because the fog had eaten my sight lines and sitting blind in a parked car felt like waiting in a coffin with the lid ajar. Three steps from the door. Maybe four. Close enough that when the voice came out of the white air beside me, calm and almost apologetic, it was already too late to matter.

"Sheriff Whitlock."

I turned with my hand on my sidearm and the world went white at the edges—not from fear but from the stun of a flashlight beam straight into the eyes, professional grade, held steady by someone who knew exactly what he was doing. I got my gun half-clear of the holster. Half. I remember the weight of it swinging, the leather dragging, and then the smell—that's the detail that stays, God help me, the *smell*—camphor and old wool and underneath it something faintly chemical, like a room that had been closed for years.

Then nothing. Or not nothing—the dark has a texture, if you're unlucky enough to visit. I remember falling the way you remember a story someone told you about yourself.

* * *

Here is what I know about the hours I lost, assembled later from evidence and other people's testimony, because my own contribution to the record begins and ends with a smell:

The 11:40 ferry came and went empty. Alvarez found no one at the ticket booth—he'd been given a false movement, a coat on a stick or a shadow arranged just so, enough to pull his eyes north while the real business happened south, twenty feet from the sheriff's own car. Priya reached the booth, found nothing, keyed her mic for me, and got static. She walked back through the fog calling my name in a voice that, by her own later admission, she hated hearing herself use.

They found my vehicle door open. My sidearm on the gravel, unfired. Blood—not much, a scalp wound, deliberate, the work of someone controlling force rather than surrendering to it. And tire tracks leading away on the service road, from a vehicle nobody saw, in a fog nobody could argue with.

What they didn't find, for six hours, was me.

* * *

Waking is a thing I've done roughly nineteen thousand times, so I flatter myself I know the mechanics. Usually there's a gradient—dark to gray to the ceiling of your bedroom, the body reporting in piece by piece, feet first, like a company coming back from lunch.

This time there was no gradient. There was a light.

A single bare bulb hanging above me, and beneath it a smell I knew in my bones before my brain caught up: camphor, wool, dust, and under everything the sweet-chemical ghost of formaldehyde. I was lying on something hard and slightly tilted. My head throbbed in a way that suggested a scalp laceration, competently cleaned—I could feel the sting of antiseptic and the tug of butterfly closures.

I tried to sit up. Straps at my wrists. Not tight—resting there, the way you'd restrain someone you didn't actually expect to struggle. That was somehow worse than tight.

"Easy," said a voice from beyond the light. Gentle. Almost warm. "You took a knock. Give it a minute."

I knew the voice. God forgive me, I'd known it since the boy was nine years old, since he and his sister used to ride their bikes past my office window on summer evenings, and I'd wave, and they'd wave, and the whole town had seemed then like a place where children grew up into decent lives. I'd signed the death certificate paperwork on that boy seven years ago. Metaphorically. The actual certificate had been Sable's to process, though of course none of us knew yet what a rehearsal that had been.

"Jonah," I said. My voice came out gravelled. "Son, whatever you think this is—"

"It's not an interrogation." He stepped into the light, and I got my first real look at him as a grown man, and the worst part—the part I still can't shake—is how ordinary he was. Mid-thirties, average build, a face you'd forget across a diner table. Kind eyes. That was the family curse, I realized. The Lockridges all had kind eyes, right down to the father, and the father had been the worst man I ever took money from.

"I need you to look down," Jonah said. "I need you to see it. Then we can talk."

Against every instinct a career officer owns, I looked down.

He'd dressed me in a suit.

Charcoal gray, double-breasted, cut in a style that had gone out of fashion sometime around the turn of the century. It fit me badly—not badly the way cheap clothes fit, but badly the way clothes fit when they were tailored for a different man's shoulders, a broader chest, a different posture. The lapels were wide. The tie was silk, knotted in a full Windsor done by someone who'd practiced it many times alone in a mirror. On the breast pocket, a white square of fabric folded into three precise points.

I knew the suit. That was the obscenity of it. I'd stood next to that suit at a funeral, twenty-some years ago, and shaken the hand inside its sleeve, and helped that hand load a boat onto a trailer one bright June morning, and accepted an envelope from it in a parking lot with my eyes carefully elsewhere. Marcus Lockridge's Sunday suit. His funeral suit. The suit he'd been buried in—or supposedly buried in, because nothing about that family stayed buried where you put it.

"The hunter becomes the costume," Jonah said softly, watching my face with an expression I can only call scholarly. "That's how I thought of it. I'm sorry you had to hear it out loud."

"Why am I alive?" It came out steadier than I deserved.

"Because the ledger doesn't close on you tonight." He crouched so we were eye level, and up close the kindness in his face resolved into something more complicated—devotion, maybe, the fierce and terrible kind, the kind that burns down houses to keep one person warm. "You're a supporting character, Sheriff. Important. But the list has an order, and you're not at the end of it. You're near it. Do you understand the difference?"

I thought about Ruth Ellery. About the retired teacher in the dead woman's dress. About the third body wearing Sable's boots, her very walk reproduced in costume. Each victim a witness. Each victim a page. And now the sheriff of Marrow Creek, dressed as the villain of the family romance, laid out under a bulb like an exhibit prepared for trial.

"You're re-enacting him," I said. "Your father. In reverse."

Something moved behind his eyes—pleasure, quick and involuntary, the flash of a student whose answer gets confirmed. "She told you."

"She figured it out. She's better at this than either of us ever gave her credit for."

"Yes," he said, and the word had such naked love in it that I had to look away. "Yes, she is. That was always the tragedy. Dad couldn't see it. He looked at her and saw a daughter, something to file under *handled*. I looked at her and saw—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. When he spoke again the warmth was banked, controlled, worse. "You took his money, Sheriff. Twice that I have documentation for. You buried the incident report in '16. I'm not going to hurt you for it. I want you to understand that I *know*, and that knowing is the punishment. The suit is the punishment. Waking up every morning for the rest of your life as a man who wore this suit and did nothing—that's the sentence. I'm just the clerk who files it."

"My people are looking for me."

"They found your blood on the gravel an hour ago. By now Agent Nair has a theory, and it's a good one, because she's good. But theories point at the ferry. I don't work at the ferry anymore." He smiled, and it was Sable's smile—I'd seen it on her face a hundred times in three years, usually over case files, usually rueful—and seeing it on him was like watching a photograph move. "One more thing, and then I'll leave you where they'll find you. Comfortably. Warm. Alive, Sheriff, I promise you that. I keep my promises. Ask anyone I haven't killed."

"That's not the reassurance you think it is."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

He leaned close, and I smelled the camphor again, the closed rooms, the years. "Tell her the locket still opens. Tell her exactly that, word for word. She'll understand. And tell her—" for the first time his voice cracked, a hairline fracture running through all that composure, "—tell her I did the Windsor knot the way Dad taught me. She'll know what that means too. She always knows what things mean. That's why I have to finish before she does."

He stood, gathered his flashlight, and paused at the edge of the light like a man checking his reflection in a doorway.

"For what it's worth," Jonah Lockridge said, "you were the only adult in this town I ever believed was trying. That's why you get to live. Try harder."

The bulb clicked off. I heard a door, a lock, footsteps receding on concrete—counted them, out of habit, out of the animal discipline that was all I had left—eleven steps, then silence, then after a long while, distantly, an engine.

* * *

They found me at dawn, in the old maintenance shed behind the shuttered funeral home, strapped to a mortuary preparation table that hadn't held a body in fifteen years. Priya Nair arrived first, ahead of the paramedics, ahead of protocol, and stood over me with an expression I'll take to my grave: not relief, not triumph. Recalculation. She was already revising her profile around the fact that the killer had taken a hostage and returned him breathing, groomed, and costumed—had, in effect, mailed the department a message written in the sheriff's own skin.

"Whitlock. Can you talk?"

"Give me a minute to remember which decade it is."

"Whoever it was—did you see—"

"He wants you to know he was at the dock since sundown." I pushed myself upright against the straps until someone finally cut them. My hands shook and I let them; hiding it cost more than showing it. "He watched us set the trap, Priya. He graded it."

Her jaw tightened. "The suit. Describe the suit."

"Charcoal. Double-breasted. Marcus Lockridge's burial suit." I looked down at myself—at the wide lapels, the silk tie, the pocket square folded in threes—and felt the full architecture of it settle over me like a second skeleton. "There's a message for Sable. Word for word, she said it matters. Tell her *the locket still opens*."

Priya wrote it down. Her pen stopped halfway through the last word.

"And the knot," I added. "He tied the Windsor the way their father taught him. He said she'd know what that means."

Outside, the fog was burning off, and the first gray light found the funeral home's dead windows, and somewhere in that building or another one like it, a man in ordinary clothes was walking calmly toward the last name on his list. I sat on the edge of the table where bodies used to be, wearing a dead man's suit, and understood—with the clarity that only comes to men who have been somebody's prop—that we had never been hunting Jonah Lockridge at all.

We'd been running alongside him. And he was nearly home.
