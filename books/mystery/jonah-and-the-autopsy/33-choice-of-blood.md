## 33. Choice of Blood

The scalpel was heavier than I remembered.

That's a lie, of course. A #22 blade on a stainless handle weighs almost nothing—four ounces, maybe less, the weight of a letter you regret sending. But my hand knew the difference between holding a tool and holding a verdict, and the verdict had mass. It settled into my wrist like wet sand.

Jonah stood across the autopsy table from me, our father's exhumed body between us under the lights, and he was smiling the way he used to smile at age eleven when we'd finished a puzzle before Mom got home from her shift—the smile of a boy presenting evidence that he was good, that he was worth loving, that the afternoon had been well spent.

"See?" he said. "It's just like when we were kids. You always did the cutting. I did the reading."

"You did the reading," I agreed. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. The wire taped to my sternum was a small cold coin against my skin, and somewhere outside—across the parking lot, in a van full of people who were trusting me not to die stupidly—Priya Nair was listening to every word. "You read everything, Jonah. That was always the problem."

"The problem." He turned the word over like a stone he'd found in his shoe. He was wearing gloves—my gloves, the ones with the split seam in the left thumb I'd never gotten around to replacing—and beneath them his hands were steady. Seven years dead and his hands hadn't shaken once. "Sable. Look at him. Really look."

I looked. I'd been avoiding it since he rolled the drawer open, but avoidance is a luxury for people with time, and I could hear the clock now, the one in my own chest, ticking toward whatever Priya's patience limit was. So I looked.

Our father lay on the table the way all my patients lay: surrendered, democratic, stripped of rank by death the way no court had ever stripped it from him alive. The exhumation had not been gentle. Someone—Jonah, alone, with a shovel and a flashlight and God knows how many nights—had dug through six feet of Marrow Creek clay to get to him. The casket lid was propped against the wall by the sink. Dirt still clung to the suit's shoulders. Harold Lockridge, funeral director, embezzler, architect of the lie that had eaten our family whole, looked smaller than I remembered. Dead men usually do. The big ones especially.

"He's already autopsied," I said quietly. "By me. Three years ago, unofficially, when I first found the ledger numbers. Cause of death: heart failure. Complicated by cowardice. I didn't write that last part down."

Jonah laughed. It was a real laugh—I hated that it was a real laugh—and for a second the morgue felt like our kitchen, 1998, some dumb joke about the embalming fluid smelling like the punch at church socials. Then the laugh ended and what was underneath it came up, and what was underneath was not a boy at all.

"I need you to finish it," he said. "Officially. On the record. Everything he did—the accounts, the bodies he moved through the home, the insurance fraud, what he made me sign. You put it in a report with your name on it and then—" his voice caught, actually caught, and I understood with a lurch that he believed this, that somewhere in the papered room of my life downstairs in his head this was the final page, the correction, the grade he'd been waiting seven years to give me— "and then we're clean. Both of us. The ledger closes and nobody owns us anymore."

"And the four people you killed?"

"They weren't killed." His jaw tightened. "They were *filed*. Mr. Aldous was the bank manager who looked away. Mrs. Vann was the secretary who typed the false invoices. Ruth Ellery saw Dad's car at the boat launch that night. And the first man—" he paused, and something flickered across his face that might have been grief if grief had been raised by our father, "—the first man was the one who helped him move the money offshore. They were entries, Sable. Entries get closed."

"They were *people*." I set my palms flat on the cold steel edge of the table, on either side of our father's ruined hands. "Ruth Ellery taught me to drive stick in the Kmart parking lot. She failed her first three attempts at parallel parking and laughed about it until she cried. She wore my graduation gown because I asked her to stand in for Mom at the ceremony, because Mom couldn't get out of bed that day, and she cried harder than I did when they called my name." My voice cracked on that and I let it. Let him hear it. "That's what an entry looks like when you hold it up to the light, Jonah. It has a laugh. It has bad knees and a cat named Duchess and a handwriting that slants left. You didn't close a ledger. You murdered a woman who was kind to a lonely girl."

He flinched. Good. Somewhere under all that archival precision there was still nerve tissue.

"She testified wrong," he said, quieter. "She would have buried you with him. Her statement put *you* at the boat launch. Don't you understand? Every one of them was a thread tied around your neck. I was pulling them out."

"You were tying new ones." I straightened. "And here's what you've never understood, in all your years of reading my files, all your months of correcting my margins: I don't need protecting from the truth. I never did. That was Dad's religion—protect the family, protect the name, bury anything that bleeds. You learned it at his knee and you practiced it in six graves. I'm the only one in this room who ever broke with the church."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Recognition, maybe, arriving too late to be useful, the way recognition does.

Then he said, gently, terribly: "Pick up the knife, Sable."

And I realized the moment had arrived without announcing itself, the way these moments do. He wasn't asking anymore. The locket was out—he'd taken it from his pocket sometime during the speech, our mother's locket, the one she was buried wearing until Jonah apparently decided she wouldn't miss it—and he held it up between us on its thin gold chain, turning slowly, catching the surgical light.

"This is us," he said. "Before him. Before any of it. You cut, I'll read, and when it's done we walk out of here and drive north and nobody in Marrow Creek ever says the name Lockridge again except as a ghost story. Pick it up. Finish the family's work the right way, for once. Prove you're mine."

*Prove you're mine.* There it was. Seven years of letters never sent, of photographs taken through my windows, of reports annotated in a dead man's hand—all of it converging on four words. He didn't want an accomplice. He wanted confirmation. He wanted the little sister back, the one who did the cutting while he did the reading, the one whose competence he'd curated like a museum, and he wanted her to choose him with her own hand so the choice could never be undone.

I picked up the scalpel.

I heard him exhale. I heard, very faintly, through the walls and across the lot, nothing at all—which meant Priya was holding her team exactly where I'd begged her to hold them, giving me the sixty seconds I'd promised I'd need. Sixty seconds to end a seven-year war. I'd asked for ninety. She'd given me sixty and told me not to make her regret being generous.

"Sable," Jonah whispered, and held out the locket. "Take it. Take her back. Take *us* back."

I took one step forward. Then another. The table was between us and our father, and I went around it the long way, past the sink, past the cabinet where we used to play hide-and-seek among the empty drawers when the building belonged to the living, and Jonah turned to keep me in view, smiling, waiting, certain.

"Yes," he breathed. "Yes. That's it. That's my girl."

"My name," I said, "is Sable Marie Lockridge."

His smile dimmed a degree. "I know—"

"And I'm the coroner of Marrow Creek." Another step. Close enough now to smell him—dirt and pine and something chemical, formalin maybe, weeks of sleeping near his work. "Not yours. Not Dad's. Not the town's. Mine. I spent three years hiding inside this job because I thought the job could hold what I couldn't carry. It can't. Nothing holds it. You just carry it, and you say it out loud, and you keep walking. That's the whole secret. You could have learned it too, if you'd ever once come through the front door instead of the file cabinets."

"Sable—"

"So here's what happens next." I raised the scalpel, and he leaned toward it, offering himself, closing his eyes like a man receiving communion—

—and I cut the cord.

The chain parted above his fingers with a sound too small for what it ended. The locket dropped, hit the tile, spun, and lay still between our feet: two halves of a mother's face, hinged open, empty.

Jonah stared at it. Then at me. The certainty drained out of his expression the way blood drains from tissue—top down, unevenly, pooling in the places that bend.

"What did you do," he said. Not a question. A man reading his own autopsy report.

"I'm choosing duty over blood," I told him, and my voice did not shake, and I want it recorded somewhere, in some margin, in someone's hand: *it did not shake*. "You are Jonah Elias Lockridge. You faked your death in August 2016. You have murdered four people—Aldous, Vann, Ellery, and the man in my gray coat whose name I will learn and speak at your trial. You desecrated our parents' graves. You stalked me for seven years. And I am naming you. Out loud. In this room, where I say true things about the dead, I am saying the truest thing I know: my brother is a killer, and I love him, and those are both facts, and only one of them goes in the report."

"You're going to let them take me." His voice had gone small. Eleven years old again, caught breaking something. "After everything. After I kept you safe. After I—"

"You kept me in a *box*, Jonah. Labeled. Alphabetized. You loved me the way Dad loved the funeral home—as property that reflected well on the owner." I set the scalpel down on the table, next to our father's shoulder, blade facing away from both of us. Deliberately. Let the wire catch the gesture; let Priya know it was over, that I was stepping back, that the man in front of her could be taken. "I'm not property. I never was. That's the lesson. That's the whole education. Class dismissed."

For one long second I thought he might rush me. I catalogued it the way I catalogue everything—his weight, the distance, the steel table at his back, the drawer handles he could pull—and I found I wasn't afraid. That surprised me most of all. Seven years of dread, three years of paperwork armor, one night of standing alone in a morgue with a ghost, and at the actual moment of contact there was no fear in me at all. Just a terrible, clarifying calm, like water gone still enough to see the bottom.

Jonah didn't rush me. He knelt. He picked up the locket with both hands, cupped it like a wounded bird, and began, very softly, to cry—not the way men cry in interrogation rooms, performing damage, but the way children cry when the game ends and the dark comes back and the hiding place turns out to have been a coffin all along.

"I just wanted us to be clean," he said into his hands.

"I know," I said. "That's why it worked for so long. Monsters don't frighten me, Jonah. Wishes do."

The doors burst open on the word *wishes*—steel and boots and white light, Priya's voice cutting the room into sectors, *hands, show me hands, DOWN*—and Jonah rose, obedient, empty, letting them take his arms. As they turned him toward the door he looked back at me over his shoulder, dirt still on his sleeves, our mother's locket confiscated in an evidence bag already, and he asked it the way he'd asked everything all night: gently. Lovingly. Like a boy finishing a puzzle before Mom gets home.

"Will you do mine someday?" he said. "When it's my turn on the table. Promise me it'll be your hands."

And God forgive me, because I am my father's daughter after all, born into a family that answered hard questions with soft lies—I opened my mouth to give him mercy.

But I'd cut the cord. All of them. So instead I heard myself say, clear and level, in front of Agent Nair and four deputies and the listening dead:

"No, Jonah. Somebody else's hands. Mine are done carrying you."

The door swung shut behind him, and the morgue went quiet, and I stood alone with our father's body and my own hammering heart, and I thought: *that's the first true thing I've ever said to a member of my family.*

Then my legs remembered they were legs, and gave out, and Priya Nair caught me before the floor could file its report.
