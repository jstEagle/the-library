## 1. The Night Book

The town of Calloway had two clocks that mattered after midnight: the one on the water tower, which ran four minutes fast and had for as long as anyone could remember, and the one on the wall behind the dispatch desk at Calloway Cabs, which ran four minutes slow. Mara Ishikawa had never corrected either. Between them, in that eight-minute seam, lived everything the town didn't say out loud.

She sat at the desk with a mug of tea gone cold beside her elbow and the night book open in front of her — a ledger bound in green cloth, its spine cracked soft as an old shoe. The radio hissed on the low channel. Outside, Main Street lay under a skin of frost, the streetlights burning orange halos into the dark, and the only movement was a dog crossing the intersection with the confidence of a pedestrian who owned property nearby.

"Unit two, you out there?" she said into the mic, mostly for the pleasure of hearing a voice.

"Out here," came Ray Okafor's drawl, tinny and half-asleep. "Nothing moving but me and the frost."

"Copy. Come in at two, I'll have coffee on."

"You'll have the dregs on."

"The dregs are coffee, Ray. That's the whole philosophy of this business."

She heard his laugh crackle out and then the channel went quiet again, and Mara settled back into the silence the way other people settled into a chair. Ten years of nights. She knew this room the way a priest knows a confessional — the scuffed floor, the corkboard with its faded permits and a photograph of the 1998 company picnic, the smell of toner and old upholstery and, underneath it all, the faint mineral tang of the radiator that nobody had ever fixed because Denny Calloway believed a radiator that worked was a radiator you'd soon have to replace.

The night book was her father's invention. Kenji Ishikawa had run dispatch here for twenty-two years before the heart attack took him in the parking lot, mid-shift, keys still in his hand, and in all those years he had kept two sets of books. The official log — times, addresses, fares, the dry skeleton of every ride — went to the state board and the tax man. The other book, the green one, was his alone. In it he wrote what people said in the back seats. Not everything. He'd had a code for that, a shorthand of his own devising, small marks in the margins like bird tracks: a circle for a confession, a slash for a lie, a star for something that would matter later. Mara had found the code in a shoebox after the funeral, along with forty-one green ledgers and a note in his careful hand: *Someone should know what this town says when it thinks no one's listening. That someone is you now, kiddo. Sorry about that.*

She'd been angry about it for a year. Then she'd started reading. Then she'd started writing.

Because that was the thing nobody understood about a cab company in a town of eleven thousand people: it wasn't transportation. It was the confessional with wheels. The church got the polished sins; Calloway Cabs got the real ones.

At 11:40 the door opened and Walt Prentiss came in the way he always came in on Fridays, which was to say sideways, holding onto the frame, smelling of the Anchor Bar's well whiskey and grief. Walt had been coming in drunk every Friday for three years, ever since his wife Carol died, and every Friday he delivered a portion of the same monologue to the only person in town paid to listen at that hour.

"Evening, Mara."

"Evening, Walt. Ray's two minutes out."

"He's a good boy, Ray." Walt lowered himself onto the bench by the door, which existed for exactly this purpose. "You know what Carol said to me? Last thing she said that made any sense? She said, *Walt, you never listen to the ends of stories.*" He shook his head at the corkboard. "And she was right. I never did. I'd walk out of the room right at the good part."

"What was the end of the story, Walt?"

He looked at her then, surprised, as if no one had ever asked. "I don't know. That's the whole damn tragedy of it." He laughed, wet-eyed, and Mara wrote in the margin of the night book, in her father's shorthand, the small circle that meant *confession, harmless, recurring.* Walt Prentiss, circle, Friday. The town's grief filed in its proper place.

Ray pulled up at 11:58 by the wall clock, 12:02 by the tower, and Walt was loaded into the back seat with the tenderness of long practice. Through the window Mara watched the cab's taillights diminish down Main Street, carrying Walt home to the house where Carol's coats still hung in the hall, and she felt the particular loneliness of her position settle over her like the frost outside. She was the keeper of everyone's endings. She had no one to bring her the ends of her own stories. Her mother was eleven years gone; her father, nine. Her friends from high school had children and day shifts and group chats she'd been quietly removed from for lack of participation. What she had was a radio, a green book, and the whole sleeping town murmuring in her ear.

She wouldn't have traded it. That was the honest truth, and she'd have admitted it to no one.

* * *

The small hours came in their usual order. At 1:15 a call from the Anchor Bar for a ride home — a bachelorette party, six girls, one of whom wept in the back seat the whole way and told the driver, who told Mara, that she wasn't sure she loved the man she was marrying, and Mara logged it with a circle and a question mark. At 1:50 a fare from the hospital: a man whose father had died at 1:37, riding home alone to an empty kitchen, who said to Ray, "He waited for me to leave the room. They do that. They wait until you go get the coffee." Slash — not a lie, her father's code said, but not the whole truth either; the man's father had waited because he'd wanted to spare him, and someday the man would know that, and the ride home would mean something different.

At 2:30, Mrs. Halloran called the dispatch line directly, as she did every night, though she had not left her house in four years.

"Mara, dear. Is that you?"

"Every night, Mrs. Halloran."

"I saw a light on at the Fenwick place. Is somebody living there now?"

The Fenwick place had been empty for six years. "Could be a raccoon in the attic," Mara said. "I'll have Ray drive past."

"You're a good girl. Your father was a good man. Did I ever tell you he drove me to the hospital when my Thomas was born? Snow up to the axles. He carried me across the yard in his arms like I was the baby."

"Every night, Mrs. Halloran."

"Well. It bears repeating." And she hung up, satisfied, and Mara wrote it down — not the Fenwick place, not the raccoon, but the snow, the yard, the arms — because that was the job. The town said a thing once, and if nobody wrote it down, the town got to pretend it hadn't happened, and the pretending was how places like Calloway stayed small and quiet and full of unexploded ordnance.

She knew more about this town than the newspaper, the sheriff, and the three churches combined. She knew which marriages were performances, which businesses laundered more than money, which deacon had a second phone. None of it had ever been worth anything. That was the strange part. A decade of secrets, and the secrets were all small — small enough to keep, small enough to be kept *for* people. Her father had believed that was the service. The fare got you home; the margin got you off the record. *Everybody needs one place where it didn't happen,* he'd written in the first ledger, and Mara had underlined it twice, and had built her whole quiet life inside that sentence.

At 3 a.m. by the wall clock, 2:56 by the tower, the radio said nothing, the phone said nothing, and Mara got up to make the second pot of coffee, which was the hour's only appointment.

That was when the stranger walked in.

She heard the door before she saw him — the bell over it had a cracked note, a flat B, and it cut through the radiator's hum like a knife through paper. She came around the corner with the coffee pot in her hand and found a man standing just inside the entrance, shaking frost off the shoulders of a coat that cost more than her car. He was tall, mid-forties, gray at the temples, with the kind of face you forget in the moment you look away from it. He looked at her the way a man looks at a locked door he'd been told would be open.

"You're open," he said. Not a question, but close to one.

"We're always open. That's the whole idea." She set the coffee pot down. "You need a cab?"

"No." He crossed the room slowly, reading things — the corkboard, the permits, the photograph of the 1998 picnic — as if checking them against a list he'd memorized. "I need the dispatcher. They told me at the Anchor you're the one who's here. Every night. Ten years."

"Who told you at the Anchor?"

"A man at the bar." He smiled without warmth. "This town tells you everything if you buy the first round."

"That's the other thing this town is known for." She stayed behind the desk. The night book lay open in front of her; she turned it, casually, face down. "If you're not a fare, I'm going to need to know what you are."

He reached into his coat and set an envelope on the desk. Not cash — a cashier's check, and she could see the amount from where she stood, and the number had a comma in it that made her sit down without meaning to.

"That's a year," the man said. "Prepaid. Every fare this company runs for the next twelve months, settled in advance, no questions from your bookkeeper."

"Nobody prepays a year of cab rides. Nobody *needs* a year of cab rides. Ray drives the same four drunks in a circle."

"Then the money's not for the rides." He took the room's only other chair, uninvited, and sat with his hands folded, and now that he was still she could see the thing underneath his composure, the wire under the plaster: a man running a fever of purpose. "It's for the person who keeps the books. I'm told you keep *all* the books. The real ones."

The radiator ticked. The radio hissed. Somewhere out on Route 9 a truck ground through its gears, the only proof the world was still turning.

"My father's dead," Mara said.

"I know," the man said. "I'm sorry. I'm also not here about him, exactly." He leaned forward, and the flat B in the bell seemed to still be ringing somewhere behind his words. "My name is Elias Vann. And I need you to prove something for me, Ms. Ishikawa. I need you to prove that a man will not be murdered — four weeks from now, at an anniversary dinner he's throwing for himself. The man's name is Aldous Reeve."

The name went through her like cold water through a pipe. Everyone in Calloway knew the name Aldous Reeve; everyone knew the other one too, the one that came attached, the way a wound comes attached to a scar. Lena Reeve, drowned, ten years ago this spring. Closed case. Closed book.

Mara looked at the check with its obscene comma, then at the man who had walked out of the frost at 3 a.m. with her father's name already in his mouth and a dead woman's husband on his tongue.

"Start from the beginning," she said, and reached for a fresh pen.
