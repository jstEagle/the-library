## 5. The Judge's Garden Party

The invitation arrived on cream cardstock, the kind of paper that announces its own expense before you read a word of it. *The Honorable Alden Crane requests the pleasure of your company at the Meridian Historical Society's Annual Garden Benefit.* Below that, in smaller type: *A celebration of thirty years of service to the courts and citizens of Halloway County.*

Silas read it twice, standing behind her register with the afternoon light falling through the front window in long amber bars that caught the dust she never quite defeated. Thirty years. She had known him for twenty-nine of them. He'd been a young prosecutor then, all elbows and certainty, and she'd been a patrol officer with a notebook full of observations nobody wanted. They had climbed together — he up the judicial ladder, she through homicide and into detective's gold shield — and somewhere along the way the climbing had become the friendship, two people who understood each other because they had both learned what it cost.

She almost didn't go. The shop was bleeding money; Saturdays were when she could least afford to close early. But there was the other thing too, the thing she hadn't said aloud to anyone: Jonah Reyes's file sitting beside Elias Brandt's in the county archive like two books shelved by mistake, and the way Reyes had looked at her — not surprised, not afraid, *expecting* — had lodged under her skin like a splinter she couldn't find with her fingers.

And Crane would know about the archive. Crane knew everything about this town's paper skeleton.

She went.

* * *

The Meridian Historical Society occupied a Greek Revival mansion on Calloway Street, four white columns and a lawn that rolled down toward the river like green silk someone had shaken out and left to settle. By five o'clock the garden was full of the county's respectable machinery: councilmen in seersucker, lawyers' wives comparing orthodontists, a string quartet sawing through Vivaldi near the rose arbor where nobody listened. Waiters moved through the crowd with trays of champagne in fluted glasses, and the smell of cut grass mixed with sunscreen and lilies until the whole afternoon tasted faintly of funerals.

Silas stood at the edge of it with a glass she wasn't drinking, wearing the one good blazer she owned and feeling the way she always felt at these things — like a crow that had wandered into an aviary of doves. She was scanning the crowd without meaning to, an old habit, cataloguing exits and faces, when she heard her name spoken in a voice she would have known underwater.

"Silas Voss. As I live and breathe."

Alden Crane came toward her across the lawn with his hands already open, and the crowd parted around him the way water parts around a keel. He was seventy-one now and wore it like a tailored coat — silver hair swept back, a tan earned honestly on golf courses, eyes still quick and blue and amused. People turned to watch him pass. They always had. There was something in the way he moved through a room, some gravitational courtesy, that made everyone feel briefly chosen.

"Alden." She let him take her hands. His grip was warm, dry, firm. "You look ridiculous. Nobody retires looking that healthy."

"I cheat." He laughed, and the laugh was exactly as she remembered it, low and rolling, the laugh of a man who had never once doubted that the world would eventually agree with him. "Sunrise walks. Fish oil. And the occasional glass of something criminal." He nodded at her untouched champagne. "You're not drinking."

"I'm working."

"You're always working. That was always the trouble with you." But he said it fondly, steering her gently by the elbow away from the quartet, toward the shade of a magnolia whose blossoms had gone brown at the edges and dropped waxy petals across the grass like shed skins. "How is the shop? I drove past last month. That corner of Ashbury Lane needs you. It needs light in the window."

"The shop survives."

"Survives." He repeated the word softly, tasting it, and something in his tone made her look at him more carefully. "You know, I've got some discretionary money sitting idle. Charitable remainder trust, the accountant keeps nagging me about it. A small business loan — nothing formal, just between friends —"

"Alden."

"— at terms no bank would offer you," he finished smoothly, as though she hadn't spoken. "Consider it. That's all I ask. Consider it."

She considered instead how strange it was, this sudden generosity, arriving in the same season that a frightened woman had walked into her shop with triple payment and a prophecy. The thought passed through her like a draft through a poorly sealed house, and she shut the door on it before it could become anything.

"Tell me about the archive," she said.

His eyebrows rose, mild and interested. "The county archive? What about it?"

"I've been doing research. An old case."

"Which old case?" He smiled as he asked it, sipping his own drink, and the smile was easy, unhurried, the smile of a man with nothing behind his teeth but enamel. Around them the party murmured on, glasses chiming, the quartet sawing, a child shrieking happily somewhere near the fountain.

"Halloway," Silas said. "1994."

For a moment — and she would turn this moment over for weeks afterward, examining it from every angle like a jeweler checking for flaws — for a moment Alden Crane's face did something very small. Not a flinch. Not a freeze. Something more like a page turning: the expression he'd been wearing reached its end and a new one began, identical to the eye, printed on different paper.

"Halloway," he said. "God. That takes me back. You made your bones on that one." He touched his glass lightly to hers. "We both did."

"We did."

"Elias Brandt." He shook his head with genuine-seeming sorrow. "That poor devil. I signed off on the appeal denial myself, you know. One of the hardest signatures I ever put on paper. I went back and reread the transcript twice before I could do it." His voice dropped, confiding, warm. "He confessed, Silas. Whatever doubts people like to manufacture afterward — he stood up in that courtroom and told the world what he did. Sometimes the system works. Ugly, but it works."

"He recanted," Silas said. "Before he died. You know that."

"I know he wrote some letters." Crane waved a hand, dismissive and gentle at once, a man brushing away a fly without harming it. "Prison does that to men. Gives them religion, or grievance, or both. It doesn't change what happened." Then his eyes came back to hers, and they were steady and blue and kind, and he said, "Why the interest? After all these years?"

And here was the strange part, the part she kept returning to later like a tongue to a broken tooth: she lied to him. Automatically, without deciding to, the way her hand still went to where her badge used to hang. "A bookseller thing," she said. "True crime sells. I'm thinking of stocking local titles."

Something eased in his shoulders — so slightly that anyone who didn't know him for twenty-nine years would have missed it entirely. "Smart," he said. "Grim, but smart. Well." He drained his glass and set it on a passing tray with a soft click. "Come by the house this week. Eleanor would love to see you. We'll dig out the old photographs — God, the ones from your promotion. Your hair, Silas. The eighties should apologize to you personally."

She laughed, and meant it, and hated how easily the laugh came, how the sound of his voice could reach back through three decades and take her hand like they were still walking the same corridor.

He kissed her cheek — cologne and something underneath it, soap or starch — and moved back into the crowd, and the crowd closed around him, and a councilman's wife caught his arm and tilted her head back to laugh at whatever he said, and Silas stood alone under the browning magnolia with her untouched champagne and felt, for the first time in twenty-nine years, that she had just watched her oldest friend lie to her.

Not proven. Not even probable. A page turning. That was all.

But she had spent thirty years reading faces in interrogation rooms, and pages did not turn in Alden Crane's face unless something underneath them needed covering.

* * *

She stayed another hour, which surprised her. Partly it was professional habit — these gatherings were where a town told on itself, if you stood still long enough and listened. Partly it was the archive question, gnawing at her. She found the historical society's president, a brisk widow named Mrs. Okafor who wore her authority like a brooch, and asked, casually, who maintained the society's relationship with the county records office.

"Oh, Jonah Reyes handles most of our retrievals," Mrs. Okafor said, adjusting her glasses. "Wonderful man. Meticulous. He knows those stacks better than the building knows itself. Why, last year he found us the original deed to this house, tucked inside a box of tax ledgers from 1911. A genius with paper, that one."

"Does he ever mention his family?"

Mrs. Okafor's mouth pursed thoughtfully. "He keeps himself. Quiet man. Devoted to the work." She leaned in slightly, lowering her voice with the relish of a woman dispensing sanctioned gossip. "There was talk, some years ago, of an uncle. Mother's side. In trouble with the law, back before any of our time. Jonah doesn't speak of it. One respects that."

"One does," Silas said, and her heart was beating oddly, a slow heavy percussion against her ribs. *An uncle. In trouble with the law.*

Elias Brandt had never married. But he'd had a sister — she remembered now, dredging it up from the sediment of the case file, a sister named Ruth who'd sat in the second row of the gallery every day of the trial with her hands folded like a woman praying against evidence. Ruth Brandt. If Ruth had married, if she'd had a son —

If Jonah Reyes was Elias Brandt's nephew, then everything rearranged itself. The calm. The expectation. The file shelved beside Brandt's like a bookmark left in place for thirty years. This wasn't a man waiting to be murdered. This was a man conducting a vigil.

She thanked Mrs. Okafor and drifted back toward the garden, and that was when she saw Crane again — across the lawn, near the rose arbor, standing apart from his admirers for just a moment, alone with his phone held to his ear. He was not laughing now. His back was half-turned to the party, his shoulders squared, and even at forty yards she could read the posture: it was the posture he took when delivering bad news, when sentencing, when telling a widow what the forensics had found. Commanding. Controlled. And beneath it, in the set of his jaw, something tight.

He ended the call. Stood still a moment longer, looking out past the hedge toward the street, toward the town beyond it. Then he turned back, and the warmth came up over his face like stage lighting, and he was laughing again before he'd taken three steps.

Silas set her glass down on a waiter's tray and left without saying goodbye.

* * *

The drive home took her past the county archive, because the route home had always taken her past the county archive and she had never once thought about it until today. The building sat squat and limestone-gray behind its row of young oaks, lit at the entrance, dark everywhere else. A single car in the lot. Through the ground-floor windows, a wedge of fluorescent light fell across the floor of the reading room, and in that light, at a table, a figure sat bent over papers.

She pulled over. She told herself it was to check the hours posted in the door — she'd need to come back Monday, request the Brandt file properly, see whether it had been touched, thinned, annotated. That was the story she gave herself, idling at the curb with her blinker ticking like a slow heartbeat.

Through the glass, the figure straightened, stretched, gathered his papers. Jonah Reyes. Working late on a Saturday night while the county's great and good drank champagne on a lawn. She watched him carry his folder to a cabinet against the far wall — watched him open a drawer, place the folder inside, and then, before closing it, pause with his hand flat on the drawer's face. Just for a second. The way a man pauses at a grave.

Then the drawer shut, the lights went off section by section, and Jonah Reyes came out the front door, locked it, and walked to his car without once looking toward the street.

Without once looking toward her car, Silas corrected herself. Because she could not shake the feeling — irrational, unprofessional, cold at the base of her skull — that he had known she was there the entire time.

She drove home through streets going blue with dusk, past the courthouse with its dome catching the last light, past Crane's neighborhood with its hedged and sleeping mansions, and by the time she unlocked the shop's back door and stood in the dark among her books, she had assembled the day into a single sentence that she spoke aloud to no one:

*Alden Crane offered me money the same week a stranger paid me triple to prevent a murder that hasn't happened yet.*

The shop smelled of paper and dust and the ghost of coffee. She did not turn on the lights. She stood in the dark listening to the building settle, and above her, in the apartment upstairs, the pipes knocked once as Mara Quill — who had, after the break-in scare at the shop, taken Silas up on the offer of the spare room and now slept twelve feet above her head — turned over in bed.

Silas climbed the stairs quietly, lay down without undressing, and stared at the ceiling until the streetlight through the blinds laid its pale ladder across the plaster.

Three days ago she had believed she was investigating a threat to Jonah Reyes's life.

Tonight, lying awake in the dark, she understood with a certainty that arrived whole, like a verdict, that she had gotten it backwards. Jonah Reyes was not the man in danger.

Jonah Reyes was the danger — and he had been aimed, with terrible patience, at everyone who had ever touched the Halloway case.

Including her. Including, she thought, with the first true cold of the season moving through her chest, including Alden Crane.

She slept eventually, badly, and dreamed of a drawer that would not stay shut.
