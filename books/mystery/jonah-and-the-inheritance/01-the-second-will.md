## 1. The Second Will

The courier arrived at 4:47 on a Thursday afternoon, which was the first thing wrong with him.

I say that with the benefit of hindsight, of course. At the time he was simply a young man in a gray uniform with a clipboard and a padded envelope, standing in the doorway of my office while rain came down behind him in long silver threads, and I was thinking about dinner. But later—much later, when I had learned to read that day backward like a page in a ledger—I would understand that everything about his arrival had been slightly off. Couriers delivering legal documents to my office did not come at quarter to five on a Thursday. They came at ten in the morning, or they didn't come at all, because anything worth delivering to Ansel Lockridge was worth delivering during business hours, when witnesses were plentiful and coffee was fresh.

"Package for Lockridge Probate Services," he said, though my name was painted on the glass door in letters six inches tall. "Signature required."

I signed. He left. The rain closed behind him like a curtain drawn across a stage, and I stood there holding an envelope heavier than it should have been, and I remember thinking—not yet with alarm, but with the mild professional irritation of a man whose evening is being rearranged—that the return address was a post office box in a town I'd never heard of.

My office occupies the second floor of a converted bank building on Calloway Street, three windows facing east, a safe older than I am bolted into the floor of the back room. There is a photograph on the wall beside the safe: a woman with silver hair and reading glasses pushed up into it, her arm around a younger version of me, both of us squinting into summer light outside the county courthouse. Vera Halloway. My mentor. Dead eleven years this past March, and still, on difficult days, the voice in my head that says *check it twice, Ansel, then check it again.*

I mention her now because she is where this story truly begins, though I didn't know it yet. I mention her because when I finally opened that envelope, hers was the face I saw.

* * *

The envelope contained two things: a letter of transmittal, typed, unsigned except by initials—*E.M.*—and a document.

The document was a will.

I have handled perhaps four hundred wills in my career. I know their anatomy the way a doctor knows a body: the preamble, the revocation clause, the appointment of executor, the disposition of estate, the attestation clause with its careful liturgy of witnesses and signatures. A will is a formula dressed in solemnity, and most of them are as predictable as weather in a valley. This one was not.

It was dated March 3rd of this year.

Edmund Voss died September 14th, thirty years ago.

I want you to sit with that arithmetic for a moment, because I sat with it for a long time, standing at my window with the paper in my hand while the rain kept up its patient work against the glass. A will dated six months after the testator's death. Not misdated. Not forged in the obvious sense—the paper stock was right, the ink had aged into the fibers the way old ink does, the typewriter face was consistent with machines from the correct era, and I checked all of it, because checking is what I do. The document was old. It had been executed—or at least prepared—decades ago, and dated with a date that could not exist.

Unless, of course, Edmund Voss had been alive on March 3rd of this year.

Which was impossible, because Edmund Voss was the founder of Harrow's Bend, the timber and shipping magnate whose name was carved over the library doors and cast into the bell in the courthouse tower, whose funeral had been the largest in the town's history, attended by two governors and a senator. I knew all this the way everyone in the county knows it, which is to say vaguely, the way you know the shape of a mountain you've never climbed. Voss was dead. Voss had been dead since before I passed the bar. His estate had been probated, settled, distributed, and closed before I ever hung out my shingle. It was, in the language I use with clients, a closable case—and it had been closed for thirty years.

And here was a document claiming otherwise, delivered anonymously, unsigned by its sender, on a rainy Thursday at 4:47 in the afternoon.

I did what any sensible lawyer would do. I set it down, made tea, and told myself I would look at it again in the morning with fresh eyes.

Then I picked it up again before the kettle boiled.

* * *

Here is what the will said, stripped to its bones:

*I, Edmund Asher Voss, being of sound mind and disposing memory, do hereby revoke all prior testamentary instruments and declare this to be my last will and testament.*

Standard. Every will opens that way; the revocation clause is boilerplate, the legal equivalent of clearing your throat. But in this case the throat-clearing was a knife. If the document was genuine, it didn't merely add to the settled estate—it erased thirty years of probate history with one sentence. It unmade every distribution, every deed, every inheritance that had flowed from the original will. Julian Voss's fortune, the family trust, the endowments bearing the Voss name—all of it, revoked by a dead man with a live signature.

The dispositions themselves were brief. Small bequests: to the Harrow's Bend public library, five thousand dollars—"for books, not plaques," which made me smile despite myself; to Ruth Ashford, housekeeper, the sum of ten thousand dollars and "my gratitude, which cannot be inventoried." And then the residuary clause, the one that matters, the one that carries the whole weight:

*All the rest, residue, and remainder of my estate, of whatever nature and wheresoever situated, I give, devise, and bequeath to my daughter, Maren Ashford.*

Daughter. Maren Ashford. The same surname as the housekeeper, which was either a coincidence or the most interesting non-coincidence I had encountered in twenty years of practice. No address. No identifying details beyond the name. An heir who might be anywhere, or nowhere, or—my mind supplied helpfully—invented whole cloth by whoever had gone to the considerable trouble of aging a fraudulent document and mailing it to a stranger.

Because that was the rational explanation, and I clung to it the way a drowning man clings to driftwood. Someone was running a scam. Estates attract predators the way shipwrecks attract sharks; I had seen fabricated heirs before, seen DNA tests and birth certificates manufactured with impressive skill. The anonymous delivery, the unsigned transmittal letter, the impossible date—all of it fit the profile of a probe. Someone testing whether the Voss estate had a watchdog, or whether the dog had died of old age along with everything else.

Except.

Except for the execution formalities. Two witnesses, signatures legible: *D. Okafor* and *L. Merritt*, addresses in Harrow's Bend. I looked them up that evening in the county directory. Daniel Okafor had been a schoolteacher, retired, deceased four years. Lillian Merritt had worked at the county records office; she was alive, elderly, and according to the directory still living on Fenwick Lane. Two real people, or names lifted from real people by someone with local knowledge. Either way, the fraud—if fraud it was—had roots in Harrow's Bend.

And then there was the stamp.

* * *

Every will I've ever validated has carried the notary's seal, pressed or embossed near the attestation clause, and this one did too—a circular impression, faded but legible, reading: *Vera L. Halloway, Notary Public, County of Harrow's Bend, Commission Expires 1998.*

I stood very still for a long time after I read it.

You have to understand what that stamp meant. Vera Halloway had practiced law in this county for forty years. She had taught me everything—how to read a title chain, how to question a hostile witness without making an enemy, how to close a case so cleanly that no one ever came back to reopen it. She used to say that a lawyer's reputation was built out of endings: *anyone can start a fight, Ansel; the work is finishing things.* When she died, half the county came to her funeral, and the other half sent flowers. Her commission expired with her life. No one else could legally use that seal. No one else would dare.

And yet there it was, embossed in old paper, in a will dated six months after a man's death, naming an heir nobody could find.

Three possibilities presented themselves, and I laid them out on my desk like cards.

One: forgery. Someone had obtained a document Vera had once stamped—some routine affidavit, some power of attorney—and altered it, or had somehow impressed her seal onto fresh paper. Difficult but not impossible; seals get stolen, desks get sold, estates get scattered. This was the comfortable theory. I held onto it for most of an hour.

Two: Vera had notarized a fraudulent will herself, knowingly or not, sometime before her death. This required believing that Vera—the most scrupulous person I have ever known—had put her seal to a document dated after a man's burial without noticing the date, or worse, noticing it and proceeding anyway. I rejected this theory quickly and almost angrily, the way you reject an insult to someone who can no longer defend herself.

Three: the will was exactly what it appeared to be. Executed in March of this year, witnessed by a man dead four years ago and a woman alive on Fenwick Lane, sealed by a notary whose commission had lapsed with her heartbeat—executed, in other words, by a dead man, in the presence of the dead, under the authority of the dead.

The third possibility was absurd. I wrote it down anyway, in the margin of my legal pad, because thirty years in probate has taught me that the absurd is frequently just the true wearing a bad disguise. Wills are documents about death; they are the one genre of writing in which the dead retain the power to speak. But they speak through paper, not through pulse. A will dated after a death is not a legal instrument. It is a message. And messages, unlike instruments, are addressed to someone.

To me, apparently.

* * *

I did not sleep well, which is unusual for me; I am a man who sleeps the way he works, thoroughly and without sentimentality. I lay awake cataloguing what I knew, which was nearly nothing, and what I felt, which was considerably more than nothing and therefore more dangerous. Because beneath the professional curiosity—the clean, cold thrill of an anomaly, a puzzle with actual teeth—I detected something less welcome. Unease. The specific unease of a man who realizes the ground under his feet has a hollow sound to it.

Vera's stamp. Of all the notaries in the state, in all the decades, the document had arrived carrying *her* seal. Coincidence was possible. I am a lawyer; I believe in coincidence the way I believe in lightning—it happens, it's random, and mostly it misses you. But if it wasn't coincidence, then someone had chosen me deliberately. Someone who knew my history, who knew Vera had trained me, who understood that I of all lawyers in this county would recognize that embossed circle and be unable—*unable*—to walk away from it.

That was the trap of it, if trap it was. Any other attorney would have filed the thing away, called it a crank letter, maybe forwarded it to the bar association and forgotten it by Monday. I couldn't. Because if the will was genuine, then Vera's seal had been used on it, and until I knew how and why, her name sat under a cloud, and I discovered—sitting there in the dark with the rain still working at my windows—that I would burn down a great many comfortable assumptions before I let that cloud stand.

Professional pride, you could call it. I prefer to call it what it was: the beginning of the case becoming personal, though I didn't yet know how personal, or at what cost.

In the morning I did three things. I photographed every page of the will under raking light, front and back, and locked the original in the old bank safe. I wrote a letter to Lillian Merritt on Fenwick Lane, Harrow's Bend, requesting a conversation regarding a matter of estate administration—deliberately vague, deliberately unalarming. And I pulled the file on the Estate of Edmund A. Voss from the county records, or tried to.

The clerk found the index card. Thirty years old, typed, precise: *Estate of Edmund Asher Voss, deceased. Filed September 21st [year]. Probate granted October 2nd. Estate closed April 11th, following year.* Everything orderly. Everything final.

But the file itself—the box, the folder, the ream of paper that a manor-sized estate generates even in a tidy administration—was missing. Checked out, the card said. No date of checkout recorded. No borrower's name.

"Sometimes the old ones just wander," the clerk said, shrugging. "People take them home for genealogy projects and forget to bring them back."

"Thirty years ago?" I said.

She shrugged again, apologetic, already turning to the next person in line.

I stood in the marble corridor of the records building with my coat dripping and thought about a will dated six months after a funeral, sealed by my dead mentor, naming a daughter no one had ever heard of, and an estate file that had wandered off into the dark sometime in the last three decades and never come home.

Closable cases. That had been my pride, my brand, my quiet boast: give Ansel Lockridge a mess, and receive back a finished thing. I had closed four hundred files and never once reopened one.

I went back to my office, took the will out of the safe, and read it again from the top. And this time, at the bottom, beneath the witness signatures and the notary's seal, I noticed what I should have noticed the night before—a line so small I had taken it for a stray mark, a flaw in the paper.

Handwritten, in faded pencil, in a script cramped and old-fashioned, at the very foot of the last page:

*If you are reading this, ask Ruth why the flowers keep coming.*

I did not know who Ruth was. I did not know what flowers. But I knew, with the certainty that arrives ahead of evidence and is usually right, that somewhere in Harrow's Bend a grave was lying about what it held—and that I had just been asked, by a dead man, to open it.
