## 11. Mayor Aldous Pell's Version

The Pell house sat on the highest ground in Harbor Falls, a white clapboard relic from the town's founding century, its porch columns painted so many times they had gone soft-edged, like candles left burning too long. From that porch you could see the whole town laid out below—the millpond, the church spire, the cemetery where Edmund Voss had not been lying for thirty years—and I understood, standing on the welcome mat with Finch's death still raw in my chest, that the view was the point. Whoever lived here had spent a lifetime looking down.

Aldous Pell opened the door himself. No housekeeper, no assistant. He was ninety-one years old and moved like a man who had never once been in a hurry, because hurry was for people who could be made to wait.

"Mr. Lockridge." His voice was dry and pleasant, the voice of a man reading a grocery list. "I wondered when you'd come. Come in before the wind does."

* * *

The parlor smelled of bergamot and old paper. Every surface held something deliberate: a ship model under glass, a framed photograph of Pell shaking hands with three governors, a mantel clock that ticked with the patience of geology. He poured tea himself, steadying the pot with both hands, and I noticed he'd set out two cups before I arrived. He'd known I would come the way he'd known everything else for half a century—by simply assuming the world would behave as he required.

"Harold Finch," he said, before I'd even sat. "Terrible thing. Stairs are treacherous at our age. I've asked my doctor twice about having rails installed." He smiled over the rim of his cup. "You don't take sugar. I remember. You clerked for Whitfield, didn't you? The summer of '89. You sat where you're sitting now and took notes while I explained the harbor zoning to him."

I hadn't mentioned Whitfield to anyone in this town. The notary stamp on the second will—my dead mentor's stamp—was the thread I'd been pulling alone.

"You have a good memory, Mayor."

"I have a complete one," Pell said. "That's different. Memory is what happens to you. A complete memory is what you do with it." He set down his cup and folded his hands, and I saw that his fingers were spotted and knotted with age but perfectly steady. "You've been busy, Counselor. The coroner's ledger. Harold's calendar. And now here. You work fast for a man who prides himself on closing things properly."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know why you *think* you're here." He leaned back into his chair, and the light through the lace curtains fell across his face in stripes, so that his expression seemed to assemble and disassemble behind them. "And I'm going to give you the truth. All of it. Because unlike the people you've been talking to—a drunk, a frightened old woman, a boy playing at inheritance—I have nothing to fear from the truth. The truth is on my side. It always has been."

* * *

What he told me, he told beautifully.

Edmund Voss, he said, had been dying for years before he died—heart, lungs, the slow collapse of a man who had built an empire out of granite and stubbornness and paid for it in tissue. In the spring of his last year, he'd gone abroad. Lisbon, then Zurich, then a clinic outside Lucerne whose name Pell delivered with a faint Swiss precision, as though he'd checked the letterhead personally.

"He died there. I have the clinic's correspondence somewhere still, if it matters—which it won't, because you'll understand in a moment why no document survives that needs to." He sipped his tea. "Edmund's great terror was not death. It was dying *small*. Dying in a bed in another country, hooked to machines, while the town he built read about it in a wire dispatch. He wanted to come home whole or not at all."

"So the coffin," I said.

"So the coffin." Pell nodded, granting me the point like a chess move. "He asked us. Not told—asked. Three men he trusted: his coroner, his mayor, his banker. Bring me home quietly, he said. Bury a closed casket, say I died in my sleep at Voss House, spare the town the ugly epilogue. He was very persuasive. He was also, by then, very ill, and we loved him. Any of us would have done more than that for him."

It was good. I'll say that for it. It accounted for the empty coffin, for the three signatures, for the secrecy. It even accounted, with a small adjustment, for Ruth Ashford—Pell supplied her without prompting, which should have warned me. *There was a woman, of course. A housekeeper who accompanied him abroad. She came back alone and grieving and asked only to be left in peace.* He said it gently, generously, folding Ruth into the story as a supporting detail rather than a life.

"And the photographs?" I asked. "The ones Mayor—you showed to Coroner Brandt. The ones that bought his silence."

Something crossed Pell's face then, quick as a fish under ice. "Photographs of Edmund abroad, yes. Proof of life, proof of illness. Brandt needed to see that Edmund was real and dying, or he'd never have certified anything. Sentimental man, Brandt. Needed to feel he was doing right."

"The photographs showed him alive," I said carefully. "Years past the death date. Embracing someone."

"Embracing a nurse, I expect. Clinics have nurses." He refilled my cup without asking, and his hand did not tremble, and I understood that this was the performance of a lifetime—not the story, but the pouring. "You're hunting a conspiracy, Mr. Lockridge, because conspiracies are exciting and grief is not. But what you're actually holding is a mercy, thirty years old, performed by men who loved their founder. The second will is a forgery—someone's cruel joke, or someone's greed. The exhumation was vandalism. And Harold Finch died the way old men die, on stairs, alone." He looked at me over the cup. "Go home. Close your file. Some cases are better left unclosed."

*Almost*, I thought. *Almost believable.*

That was the horror of Aldous Pell. He wasn't lying the way guilty men lie—defensively, sweating, overexplaining. He lied the way a historian writes: selecting, smoothing, arranging facts around a conclusion until the conclusion felt inevitable. If I hadn't already stood over an open coffin full of sandbags, if I hadn't held a cufflink monogrammed *E.V.* in soil that had been disturbed long after the burial date, I might have walked out of that parlor believing every word.

But there was one seam. One thread he couldn't see because he'd been telling this story so long he'd forgotten which parts were fabric.

* * *

I let him talk another twenty minutes. About the town, mostly—about how Harbor Falls had been Edmund's body long before it was his grave, how a founder's legacy was a load-bearing wall and you did not pull it out to satisfy curiosity. He spoke of the councilmen who'd served under him with fond contempt. He spoke of Julian Voss with genuine distaste—"that boy wants the empire without the weight of it"—and of Ruth Ashford with a softness that didn't fit anywhere in his architecture, that hung off the story like a coat on the wrong hook.

"She was devoted to him," Pell said. "Ruth. Devoted beyond her station, some said. Beyond sense." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "When she came back from Europe alone, I offered her whatever she needed. Money, position, references. She refused all of it. Said she had what she needed." His eyes came up to mine, striped by the curtain light. "Do you know what she meant by that, Counselor?"

"No," I said. "Do you?"

"I imagine she meant the cottage." He said it lightly. Too lightly. "She kept a little place out by the marsh. Her mother's, originally. People need somewhere to put their grief."

My skin went cold, and I kept my face still, because I understood in that instant exactly what he was doing. He wasn't slipping. He was *showing me*. Telling me he knew about the cottage, knew about the marsh, knew where I'd been and where I was going. The courtesy was the menace. The tea was the threat. Every sentence he'd spoken had been a hand resting briefly on my shoulder, reminding me how easily hands can tighten.

"There's one more thing," I said, and I took out my notebook, though I didn't need it. "The photographs. The woman in them. Her face was turned away in every frame—Brandt said so. You never saw her face either. Correct?"

"Correct," Pell said pleasantly.

"But you knew Edmund went abroad with a companion. Ruth Ashford accompanied him. So when the photographs surfaced, you assumed—"

"I assumed nothing," Pell said. "I saw what was necessary to see."

"Then help me close a loose end." I clicked my pen, a small domestic sound in that ticking room. "For the file. The woman in the photographs—the companion who traveled with him, who came back alone. Give me her name. As you understand it."

And Aldous Pell, ninety-one years old, untouchable for thirty years, looked at me across his perfect tea service and said:

"Maren."

The clock ticked. The wind worried the shutters. Somewhere below us, the town went about its afternoon, ignorant of the name hanging in the air between us like smoke.

"Maren Ashford," Pell repeated, calm as scripture. "The daughter. That was the gossip, anyway—that the housekeeper brought the girl along, that Edmund doted on her. Children soften old men. It's well documented." He smiled, and the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was the most frightening thing I had seen in this entire case. "Why do you ask, Mr. Lockridge?"

Because Brandt had told me the truth about those photographs two nights ago with whiskey shaking in his voice: *the woman's face was turned away, but she was young, and she wasn't the mother, and Pell never asked me a single question about who she was, because Pell already knew.* Because the name Maren appeared nowhere in any record from that year—no travel manifest, no witness statement, no gossip column—because Maren Ashford had left this town at eighteen, a decade before Edmund Voss died, and had not come back until last month.

Because the woman in the photographs was Ruth. Ruth, whom Pell had just described as grief-stricken and alone. Ruth, whose name he had handled all afternoon like a man handling a live wire with gloves. And the name he'd given me instead—the name he'd volunteered with the smoothness of long rehearsal—belonged to the heir. To the girl whose name was written in the second will. To the person Pell had the greatest possible interest in connecting to Edmund Voss *before* any court did, so that when the truth surfaced, it would look like the conspiracy's invention rather than its discovery.

He hadn't slipped. He'd planted.

Except—

Except that a man rehearsing a lie chooses the safe answer. *The housekeeper,* he should have said. *Ruth, presumably.* Naming Maren was not safe. Naming Maren was specific, and specificity is where liars bleed. He knew the woman in those photographs, and the knowledge had been sitting in him so long, worn so smooth, that when I pressed the bruise, the wrong name rose up first—the name that mattered, the name he feared, dressed up as gossip.

"That's very helpful," I said, closing the notebook. "Thank you for the tea, Mayor."

"Thank you for coming." He walked me to the door himself, unhurried, and on the threshold he paused with his hand on the frame—an old man seeing off a guest, or a warden checking a lock. "You clerked for Whitfield," he said again. "Bright boy. He died with his files in perfect order, you know. Everything cross-referenced. Everything *closed*." His eyes were kind and bottomless. "Do be careful on the stairs, Counselor. They're treacherous at any age."

* * *

I sat in my car at the bottom of Pell's hill for a long time, engine off, watching his windows in the rearview mirror. Behind the lace curtains, a shape moved once and went still. He was watching me leave. He would watch until I was out of sight, and then he would pick up a telephone, or he wouldn't—because maybe there was no one left to call except the fixer who had already visited Harold Finch's staircase, and maybe the call had already been made, the moment I parked at the bottom of the hill.

One name. One wrong, precise, poisoned name, offered like a gift.

Maren Ashford was in danger—not because Pell believed she was the woman in the photographs, but because he needed everyone else to believe it, and because people who stop being useful to Aldous Pell start being problems, and problems get stairs.

I started the engine and reached for my phone, and my hand was steady until I saw the screen: three missed calls, all from an unknown number, all within the last hour. No voicemail. No text.

Except one message, sent four minutes ago, from the same unknown number, six words long:

*They know where the cottage is.*
