## 10. The Second Letter

The envelope was waiting for him on the mat when Vesper Coldharbour came down the stairs at six in the morning, and he knew before he touched it that it was the same hand.

He stood over it a long moment, bare feet cold on the boards, the house still dark around him except for the gray seam of light under the door. Outside, the gulls were already working the harbor, their cries thin and insistent, like something demanding to be let in. He had learned, in twenty years of coroner's work, to read the weight of an envelope by its shadow. This one sat flat against the floorboards with the particular density of paper that had been written on heavily, both sides, by someone who had run out of room and refused to stop.

He crouched. Picked it up. Turned it over.

No return address. Postmarked Coldharbour — again, always Coldharbour, as though the writer never left, as though they were standing behind him at this very moment watching him hold it. His own name in that same careful, upright script: *Mr. V. Coldharbour, Coroner.* Not Doctor. Not even his first name spelled out. As if the writer wanted the title and nothing else — wanted to remind him what he was, what he did, whose deaths passed through his hands.

Vesper carried it into the kitchen without opening it. He made coffee first. This was not calm; it was discipline. Ruth Ellery's handwriting — his predecessor's handwriting, impossible, two years dead — had already taught him that whatever came out of these envelopes would rearrange the furniture of his life. He would not read it standing up, shaking, unprepared. He would sit. He would drink his coffee. He would be a coroner about it.

Then he opened it with a knife, along the top edge, preserving the fold.

*The tide gives up what it took,* the letter began, *but only on its own schedule. Three days from now — Thursday, the seventeenth — the water will go out farther than it has gone in forty-one years. Go to the point below the old Wren pier at low tide. Bring nothing you are not prepared to lose.*

There was more. Three pages of it, written small and dense, front and back, and Vesper read them twice before the coffee finished brewing, and then a third time while it went cold in the cup.

The letter did not confess again. It instructed. It named the wreck of a boat — the *Maren Lee*, a name Vesper did not know but wrote down anyway because the writer clearly expected him to know it, or to find out. It described the position of the hull with the precision of someone who had been there: *forty yards off the point, under the sand shelf, where the kelp thins.* It said that remains were aboard. It said that documents were aboard. And then, near the end, in handwriting that pressed harder into the page, hard enough to emboss the paper:

*I have been dying slowly for two years and I am done being patient. I chose you, Mr. Coldharbour, because you sign the papers. Because you look at the dead when no one else will. Do not make me wrong about you.*

Two years.

Ruth Ellery had been dead two years.

Vesper set the letter down on the table and looked out the window at the harbor, at the water lying flat and pewter-colored under the early light, keeping everything it had ever taken folded inside itself. Forty-one years. He did the arithmetic without wanting to. Forty-one years ago was 1968. The year of the girl in grave thirteen. The year his father's boat went out and did not come back, though his father himself had come back, walking up the beach alone, and had never once told anyone what happened out there.

Three days.

* * *

Ivy Wren had not slept either, which was why she heard the postman's bicycle rattle past the gate at half past seven and was down the drive before he'd reached the neighbor's fence.

"You've got something for me," she said. "You just skipped my house."

The postman — a boy of maybe nineteen who had delivered to the estate since he was fifteen and had never once met Ivy's eyes — looked at her the way people in Coldharbour had looked at her since she'd come back for the funeral: as if she were a photograph that had started moving. "Nothing today, miss."

"There's always something. My grandfather got mail every day of his life, and half of it was threats and the other half was begging." She held out her hand. "Check again."

He checked again, theatrically, patting the satchel. Nothing. Ivy watched him pedal away and stood a moment in the middle of the road, gravel biting through her shoes, feeling the shape of the morning. Something was wrong with it. Not the absence of mail — the presence of everything else. Her mother Eleanora had been awake since five; Ivy had heard the parlor clock and then footsteps, unhurried, deliberate, going nowhere in particular, the way her mother walked when she was waiting for something she couldn't name. Marcus hadn't come home at all. And the tide —

She'd grown up on this coast. She knew its moods the way other children knew their mothers'. For three nights now the water had been pulling back a little further each evening, baring stones that hadn't seen air in years, and the fishermen at the dock had begun to talk about it in low voices, the way they talked about weather that killed people. A big draw-down coming. An extreme tide. Her grandfather would have known exactly how many years since the last one.

Her grandfather. Who was eleven days dead and had died, according to everyone official, of his heart, in his sleep, with a glass of brandy on the nightstand and a book face-down on the blanket.

Ivy turned and walked back up the drive, and halfway to the house she stopped, because the thought arrived whole, the way the important ones always did: *the tide goes out farthest on Thursday.*

She didn't know why that mattered. But she knew who might.

* * *

He was in the office, of course. She'd understood that about Vesper Coldharbour within a week of knowing him: the man lived inside his work the way a hermit crab lives inside a shell, and the shell was the only place he could be found. The light was on above the door of the converted boathouse on Marsh Lane even at eight in the morning, and when she knocked, there was a long silence — the silence of a man deciding whether to pretend he wasn't home — and then the bolt drew back.

"Ivy."

"Good morning to you too." She slipped past him into the warmth, smelling coffee and formalin and old paper, and stopped when she saw the table. Three pages lay spread across it, weighted at the corners with a scalpel case, a brass paperweight, a stone. Even upside down she recognized the handwriting, because she'd spent a week of her childhood trying to copy it out of one of her grandfather's ledgers, fancying herself a forger. "That's the same hand," she said quietly. "As the first letter."

Vesper closed the door and shot the bolt again. "How do you know that?"

"Because I told you I found a page. Years ago. In the boathouse at the estate." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "I've had forty years of Wren handwriting drilled into me. That's the same pen pressure, the same way the letters stand up straight like soldiers. Whoever writes those letters learned to write the way my grandfather's generation learned — slates and rulers and fear." She looked up at him. "What does the new one say?"

He hesitated. She watched him do it — watch him weigh her, measure her, decide how much truth he could afford — and she realized she'd started cataloguing his tells the way he probably catalogued hers, and that this was what trust looked like between two people who had none to spare: a slow, grudging disarmament, one tell at a time.

"Thursday," he said finally. "Low tide. There's a boat under the sand off the old Wren point. The *Maren Lee*. He says there are remains aboard, and documents." He turned the pages toward her. "And he says he's been dying for two years."

Ivy read. The kitchen-warm office seemed to cool around her as she moved through the lines, and when she reached the sentence about choosing him — *because you sign the papers* — she felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet. She read the last line twice. *Do not make me wrong about you.*

"He knows things," she said. "Not just about the family. About you." She tapped the page. "*You sign the papers. You look at the dead when no one else will.* That's not research. That's intimacy. That's someone who has watched you for years."

"And yours." Vesper came around the table, and up close she could see he hadn't slept — the skin under his eyes bruised-looking, the stubble a day past acceptable. "Read this part." He pointed to the second page, a paragraph she'd skimmed past in her hurry. "'Tell Miss Wren that the page she took from the boathouse in the summer she was fourteen is safe, and that she was right to keep it hidden in the lining of her winter coat, and that her grandmother knew and said nothing.'"

Ivy sat down. Not dramatically — her knees simply made the decision for her.

Nobody knew about the coat. Nobody alive. She had sewn that page into the lining herself, badly, with dental floss, at fourteen, certain her grandmother would find it and burn it, and had kept the coat through three moves and two countries and her mother's increasingly pointed suggestions about charity shops. It hung in her closet in London right now, thirty pounds heavier than any coat needed to be, and she had never told a living soul.

"She's dead," Ivy said. "My grandmother. Dead four years."

"Yes."

"So either your confessor is a ghost—"

"—or someone she told," Vesper finished. "Someone she trusted enough to tell that detail. Someone who was close to her. Close to the house." He rubbed his eyes. "Eleanora is still alive, Ivy. And lucid in ways nobody gives her credit for. I've seen it myself."

"My mother." Ivy said it flatly, testing the weight of it. Then shook her head. "No. My mother can't write a grocery list without losing the thread. You've seen her."

"I've seen her perform," Vesper said. "There's a difference. I've spent twenty years watching people pretend to be less than they are. Grief, madness, confusion — they're all shelters. I should know. I built mine out of minding my own business." He paused. "But it doesn't have to be her. It could be anyone your grandmother trusted. Anyone who was in that house."

"The housekeeper? Mrs. Tull? She's ninety."

"A nurse. A priest. A friend." He spread the three pages flat again, and Ivy saw for the first time that he'd annotated them in pencil, tiny numbers in the margins, cross-references, the methodical mind working underneath the exhaustion. "Whoever it is, they know the family from the inside, they know me from the outside, and they know the tide tables better than the harbormaster. And they're running out of time. Look at the pressure of the writing. First letter, measured. Second letter—" he touched the embossed final line, the grooves deep enough to catch a fingernail, "—this is a person writing against a deadline. Pain, or fear, or both. They don't think they'll get a third chance."

Ivy stared at the words *do not make me wrong about you* until they blurred.

"Thursday," she said. "The tide goes out farthest Thursday. Everyone at the docks is talking about it. Forty-one years since the last draw like it." She looked up. "Forty-one years. What happened forty-one years ago?"

Vesper was quiet for so long she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"A girl drowned," he said at last. "1968. Grave thirteen in the churchyard. Ruled accidental. And my father's boat went out the same season and came back short one crewman, and my father came back a different man, and never said why." His voice didn't break; it did something worse, it flattened, going carefully level the way a man walks carefully across ice. "The ledger entries start that year. I'm sure of it now. Whatever's under that sand has been down there since the beginning."

"Then we go Thursday," Ivy said.

"We?" 

"You got a letter addressed to *Miss Wren*. That makes me a recipient. Besides—" she stood, buttoning her coat, and felt the old anger come up in her, the good kind, the kind with a direction, "—whoever wrote this knew about a page I hid when I was fourteen. Which means they've been protecting me a long time. Or watching me a long time. Either way, I'm done being a secret somebody else keeps."

Vesper studied her, and she let him, and after a moment something in his face eased — not trust, exactly, but the decision to proceed without it.

"Thursday, then. Low tide is 4:40 in the afternoon. We go separately, we park separately, and if anyone asks, we've never discussed anything but condolences." He walked her to the door, unbolted it, then stopped with his hand on the wood. "One more thing. The letter says bring nothing you're not prepared to lose. I'd take that seriously. If Halloway hears about a crowd on that point Thursday—"

"Halloway." Ivy turned back. "His name's in the ledger. You told me."

"It's in the ledger. And this morning, driving in, I noticed a cruiser parked on Marsh Lane with nobody in it." He said it evenly, but his eyes moved past her shoulder, out toward the street, and she followed the look and saw it: the white-and-black sitting fifty yards down, engine off, windows fogged at the corners as though someone had been sitting in it a long while.

"Since when do they patrol Marsh Lane?" she asked.

"They don't," Vesper said. "They never have."

He closed the door gently behind her, and Ivy walked down the lane with her spine straight and her heart going hard, aware of the parked car's fogged windows at her back the entire way, and aware of something else too — a certainty settling into her like cold water into a hull. Whoever had written those letters had chosen them both, deliberately, the way you choose tools. And whoever was watching Marsh Lane had chosen them too.

Three days. The tide was already drawing back.
