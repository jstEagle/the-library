## 14. The Confession's Handwriting

The rain came in off the water around midnight, the kind of slow gray rain that made Coldharbour feel like a town underwater, and Vesper sat in his office with both confession letters spread flat on his desk beneath the green lamp, and for a long time he did nothing but look at them.

He had read them a dozen times each. He knew their contents the way he knew the contents of a stomach report—by heart, by rote, by the grim intimacy of his trade. *I killed Aldous Wren.* The first letter, postmarked the day after the funeral. The second, naming a date three days hence, warning that *the tide gives up what it took*. Two letters from a murderer—or a confessor, or a ghost—and Vesper had spent every waking hour since the second one arrived trying to think of them as words rather than as objects.

But they were objects. Paper and ink. And paper and ink could be read in more ways than one.

He had been avoiding this particular way of reading them for two days, and he knew it, and he was honest enough with himself to admit why. Because there was a discipline in forensic document examination that belonged to the living world—the world of state labs and sworn experts—and Vesper Coldharbour had spent forty years making himself small enough that no one would ever ask him to testify about anything. To compare hands was to prepare evidence. To prepare evidence was to become visible. And visibility, he had learned at his father's funeral, standing in a black coat while men whose names appeared nowhere shook his hand and called him *son*, was how people in this town ended up in the water.

But Ivy Wren had stood in his office three nights ago with a page of her grandfather's ledger in her coat pocket, and she had said, *You're the only person in Coldharbour I can't imagine lying to me,* and something in him had cracked along an old seam. He had already been seen walking the shoreline. Already been watched. His office had been searched; his decoded pages stolen. Invisibility, he understood now, was not a shelter. It had only ever been a delay.

So he stopped delaying.

He cleared the desk except for the two letters, then went to the filing room at the back of the building—the cold room, the deputies called it, though it held no bodies anymore, just forty years of the dead in manila folders. The heating vent ticked. The light buzzed. He pulled the drawer marked E–H and found what he was looking for without searching, because he had known it would be there, because some part of him had known since the second letter arrived and had simply refused to say so out loud.

Ruth Ellery's files. 1979 to 2019. Forty years of Coldharbour's dead signed out by the same steady hand.

* * *

She had been coroner when Vesper was still a boy hauling crab pots on his father's boat, and she had been coroner when he came back with a mortuary science degree and a plan to be nobody, and she had been the one who hired him, trained him, and—though she never said it in so many words—taught him that the dead deserved better than efficiency. She was a tall woman gone stooped, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and a habit of talking to corpses as if they were difficult relatives who'd finally stopped interrupting. *All right, Mr. Pell, let's see what you've got to say for yourself now.* She had worked until the winter she turned eighty-one, and then she had died in her sleep in the house on Lantern Row, and Vesper had signed her death certificate himself, and it remained the hardest signature of his career—not because the cause was unclear but because it wasn't. Natural causes. An old heart, finished. He had written it in his own careful hand and hated every letter.

Two years ago this March.

He carried her files back to the desk and set them beside the letters, and for a moment he simply rested his palm flat on the top folder, the way you might touch a grave marker before you read the name carved into it.

Then he opened the oldest file and took out a death certificate from 1981—a drowning, a fisherman named Corliss, ruled accidental—and laid it beside the second confession letter, and turned the lamp brighter, and looked.

The vertigo did not come all at once. It came the way the tide comes, in pulls, each one taking more ground than you thought you had to lose.

First he noticed the general architecture of the script, which any eye could see: the tall, narrow ascenders, the letters leaning slightly left as if the writer were always half-turning away; the peculiar crossed *t*s, struck twice, quick and impatient, like someone canceling something out. Ruth's hand. He had seen it ten thousand times across four decades of certificates, reports, and margin notes in files he inherited wholesale when she retired. You did not forget a hand you had countersigned for twenty years.

Then he noticed the specific things, the things only someone who had studied her writing the way he had would catch. The way she formed her lowercase *e* with a flat crossbar instead of a loop, a small horizontal shelf inside the curve. Present in the certificate. Present in the confession. The *y* that descended below the line and hooked back under the preceding word, as though the letter were trying to return to safety. Present. Present. The ink pressure—he tilted the page under the lamp, saw the grooves the pen had pressed into the paper, deep and even, the pressure of a hand that wrote steadily no matter what it was saying. Ruth had written death certificates the way other people drove nails: clean, certain, unhesitating.

*I killed Aldous Wren.*

Written in Ruth Ellery's hand.

Vesper sat back. The chair creaked. Somewhere outside, the rain thickened against the window glass, and the harbor bell buoy rang its dull note through the dark, and he became aware of his own breathing the way you become aware of a sound only when it threatens to stop.

He told himself the obvious things first, because that was the discipline, that was what Ruth herself would have done: consider the ordinary explanations before the extraordinary ones. Forgery. Someone imitating her hand. But forgery was a craft, and craft leaves seams, and he found none. He spent an hour finding none. He compared the letters against six more files spanning thirty years—the flat-shelf *e*, the double-struck *t*, the returning *y*, the spacing, the slant, the pressure—all of it consistent, all of it hers, down to the small idiosyncrasy of her numeral *4*, which she wrote open-topped like a sail catching wind. The date on the second letter contained a *3* and a *7* and no *4*, and he caught himself almost disappointed, wanting the confirmation, needing it, and then he found a *4* in the postmark code and it was open-topped and he put the magnifier down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

There was another explanation, less ordinary. A sample-based imitation, traced or practiced over years by someone who had access to her files—which meant someone with access to this office, this room, these drawers. That was possible. That was, in fact, the most reasonable remaining theory, and it should have comforted him, and instead it frightened him worse, because it meant the confessor had sat where he was sitting, in the dark, going through the dead like drawers of clothing, learning Ruth's hand stroke by stroke.

And there was a third explanation. The one that required no theory at all, only arithmetic, and therefore was the cruelest.

Ruth Ellery had died two years ago. These letters were weeks old. The dead do not write.

Except that in Coldharbour, Vesper thought, lowering his hands and looking at the lamplight swimming on the wet window, the dead had never much respected the rules. They surfaced. They washed up. They waited forty years in the tide and came ashore on a receding sea in front of half the town. If anything in this drowned place had learned patience, it was them.

* * *

He made coffee he didn't drink and worked until three in the morning, because grief and fear both wanted him idle and he refused to give either one the satisfaction. He built a timeline. Ruth retired in 2019, spring. She died in March 2021. The first confession letter was postmarked eleven months ago—no. He corrected himself, checked the postmark again, felt the floor tilt. The first letter was postmarked the day after Aldous Wren's funeral, which was eight weeks ago. Eight weeks. Not eleven months. He was losing time the way the eroding bluff behind the cannery lost soil—one night you noticed, and the whole edge had moved.

Eight weeks ago, Ruth Ellery had been two years dead, and her hand had addressed an envelope at the Coldharbour post office.

Unless she hadn't died.

The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, wearing its own clothes. He examined it anyway, because he was his father's son and his father had taught him that you check the trap even when you're sure it's empty. Death certificate, signed by Vesper Coldharbour, cause natural. Body viewed? No—there had been no viewing. Ruth had left instructions, he remembered now, remembering with a strange swimming sensation: cremation, no service, ashes to the sea. Her lawyer had handled it. Vesper had signed the certificate based on the attending physician's report—Dr. Amos Prine, who was himself dead now, who had died the following year, quietly, of the same category of quiet failure. So the chain ran: Prine's word, Vesper's signature, a sealed urn, an ocean. No autopsy. No reason for one. Nobody in Coldharbour autopsied anybody the town had agreed to lose.

His own handwriting on her certificate suddenly looked to him like a co-conspirator's.

He got up and walked the length of the office to shake the feeling loose, past the steel table where he'd prepared Aldous Wren's body and noted the details that didn't match the story, past the window where the rain drew long silver lines down the glass. The town was black beyond it, one or two lamps burning on Harbor Street, the Wren house dark on its hill above the water like a headstone with windows.

Someone had searched this office nine days ago. Amateurish, frightened, he'd thought—drawers rifled, files reshelved wrong, his decoded pages taken. He had assumed Halloway, or someone doing Halloway's errands. But now a different image assembled itself, unbidden: someone in this room at night, practicing. Filling page after page with Ruth Ellery's *e*s and *t*s and *y*s, tearing them up, starting again. Learning to speak with a dead woman's voice. Why go to such lengths—unless the message needed to come from her specifically? Unless the authority of the letter depended not on what it said but on whose hand said it?

Or unless the writer believed, in whatever broken place such a belief lives, that the hand was not borrowed at all.

Vesper stood at the window until the glass fogged with his breath, and he admitted the last thing, the thing underneath everything, the thing he'd been carrying since the shoreline and the stone marker with its scratched numbers: that some part of him had recognized the possibility from the beginning and had flinched from it, because if Ruth Ellery was alive—if she had faked her death, or let it be faked—then she had let him sign the certificate. She had let him carry her. She had chosen him for that too, the way the ledger had been chosen for him, the way the instructions in her boxes—*to whoever signs my cases next*—had been waiting for him before he ever knew he was being waited for.

Anger came, briefly, hot and clarifying. Good. Anger was better than vertigo. Anger kept your feet under you.

* * *

Near dawn the rain quit, and the light came up the color of oyster shells, and Vesper did the thing he should have done two days ago: he went back into the cold room and pulled every file Ruth had touched in her final year of practice, looking not for handwriting but for hesitation. For the places where the steady hand faltered.

He found them in the Wren files.

Aldous Wren's preparation—his own work, six months ago—but in the margins of the intake sheet, in pencil, faint, a notation he had never noticed because he had never gone back to look: a string of numbers in Ruth's hand. The same figure-set style as the stone marker on the shore. He stared at it for a long moment, heart knocking, then flipped through the rest of the folder. On the back of the personal effects inventory, folded small, a sheet of Ruth's official stationery, blank except for a single line near the bottom, written and then heavily struck through—struck twice, those impatient crosses—but legible beneath the strikes:

*If you are reading this, you are the one I chose, and I am sorry for what it costs you.*

No date. No signature. Just the hand. Her hand. The flat-shelf *e* in *reading*, the double-struck cancellation through the sentence like a tide pulling the words back under.

Vesper sat down slowly on the steel stool beside the empty table, in the room where he had spent his life among the dead, and felt the vertigo crest and break over him at last—not horror, exactly, though horror was in it, but something larger and stranger: the sensation of a door opening in a wall he had lived against for forty years, believing it was solid.

The dead were writing to him. One of them had been writing to him for months. And the note in his own case file, hidden in his own office, struck through but not destroyed—kept, deliberately, the way you keep a letter you mean to send someday—meant that Ruth Ellery had expected him to find it. Had trusted him to look where no one else would look. Had known that the man she trained to be invisible would one day be forced to read everything.

Three days. The second letter had given the town three days, and one of them was already drowning in yesterday's rain.

He folded the note into his breast pocket, over his heart, where it lay like a coal, and he picked up the phone to call Ivy Wren, and his hand stopped above the receiver—because a new thought had surfaced, quiet and terrible, the way the worst ones always do:

If Ruth had left him a message in the Wren file, she had left messages elsewhere. And if she had gone to the trouble of faking her own death—or letting the town believe in it—then somewhere in Coldharbour, behind one of those dark windows, an eighty-three-year-old woman was watching to see whether the man she chose would be worth the cost.

The line hummed in his ear. Outside, the tide was turning, and far down the shore, he could almost believe he heard it giving something back.
