## 14. Colter's Confession, Almost

The call came in at 11:47 on a Tuesday night, and it was the kind of call I'd taken a thousand times: a pickup at the Rusty Anchor, no destination given. Drunks never gave destinations. They gave coordinates of despair and let the driver figure out the rest.

I should have sent Amos. Amos was retired now, but his nephew ran the late shift, and the nephew owed me a favor for covering his daughter's hospital run back in March. But something about the address snagged in me like a fishhook. The Rusty Anchor sat four blocks from the dispatch office, which meant whoever called it had walked past my window to get to a phone. And the voice on the line, slurred though it was, had that flat parish cadence I'd been hearing in my sleep for two weeks—the cadence from the pier-night tape.

"Send somebody," the voice said. "Or don't. Doesn't matter."

Then it hung up.

I pulled the log. The number matched the payphone outside the Anchor, but the account it billed to belonged to the Sheriff's Department discretionary fund. Dane Colter had called a cab with county money and then told the cab he didn't need it.

I sat with that for a long moment, headset around my neck like a yoke, listening to the empty channel breathe. Then I did something I'd never done in nine years of dispatching. I left the desk.

Ruthie Marchand from the day shift owed me double for the funeral I'd covered in June. She came in yawning and suspicious, and I told her my mother was sick, which was true in a way—my mother had been dead eleven years, and some nights that sickness felt fresh enough to build a life on. She waved me off. I took my own cab, because a dispatcher's perk is a fleet, and drove the four blocks to the Anchor with my heart going like a bad alternator.

* * *

Dane Colter was not hard to find. He was the only man in the bar sitting with his back to the door, which in Parish is either a death wish or a declaration that you've stopped caring who walks in behind you. He wore his uniform shirt untucked over jeans, badge still clipped to the belt, and there were three empties lined up in front of him like little soldiers awaiting orders he couldn't give.

I took the stool beside him. He didn't look over.

"Cab's outside," I said.

"I canceled."

"You called one first."

That got him. He turned his head slowly, the way big men do when they're managing their own equilibrium, and looked at me with eyes that were red-rimmed and, underneath the whiskey, terrified. Not angry. Terrified. It was the same look I'd seen on his face at the exhumation, when the river stones came up out of Augustin Voss's coffin and the whole town gasped and Dane went white as the limestone they carve founders out of.

Recognition, I'd thought then. I thought it again now.

"Joubert." He said my name like he was reading it off a warrant. "The dispatcher."

"Somebody has to know where everybody's going."

He laughed at that—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it—and signaled the bartender for another without asking if I wanted one. The bartender, a woman named Odile who'd known me since I was small enough to sit on my father's shoulders during the Blessing of the Fleet, set down two glasses anyway. Parish courtesy. Or Parish surveillance. In this town the two had always shared a border.

"You've been busy," Dane said.

"Slow season."

"Mm." He drank half his glass in one motion. "Celeste Voss says you've been to her house twice. Says you asked her about river stones." His voice dropped on the last two words, dropped like a stone itself into deep water. "Says you've been pulling old tapes."

"Old tapes are public record."

"They're company property."

"So is this conversation," I said, "if I decide to have it."

He stared at me. And here is the thing I want to get right, because it matters: I was afraid. Not of him exactly—he was drunk and alone and wearing his ruin like a wet coat—but of what he represented, which was the law of Parish, and the law of Parish had five years of practice at making things disappear. Mara Voss. Her brother. A coffin full of rocks. The town didn't hide its secrets; it buried them with honors and held a centennial.

But under the fear, steady as ballast, was the recorder.

It was in my inside pocket, a little cassette job I'd bought at the drugstore three days after Ruth Ellery's hymn had spelled out a dead woman's name in changing keys. I'd carried it every day since, waiting. A dispatcher learns patience the way a priest learns prayer: by repetition, alone, in the dark.

"Dane," I said, "why did you call a cab?"

He looked at his glass. "Wanted to go somewhere."

"Where?"

"The pier." He said it before he could stop himself, and I watched the word land on him like a slap. His hand shook when he raised the glass again. "Old pier. Cannery pier. Habit."

"Habit from when?"

"Joubert." He set the glass down hard enough to slop whiskey across the bar top. "You're a careful man. I've watched you. Nine years behind that headset and nobody in this parish knows your middle name, what you drive, whether you vote. You built yourself into a ghost on purpose. I respect it. It's the smartest thing anybody in this town ever did." He leaned closer, and his breath was a wall of bourbon. "So be smart now. Whatever you think you found in those ledgers, whatever you heard on those tapes—it's older than you. Bigger than you. It started before either of us could walk, and it'll be running when we're both in the ground, and the only question—the only one—is whether you're standing in front of it or beside it."

"Is that an offer?"

"It's arithmetic."

* * *

I want to record here, in whatever part of me keeps the ledger, that for about ninety seconds I hated him less than I'd hated anyone in years.

Because I knew what he was describing. I lived it. The founding families weren't men; they were weather. You didn't fight them any more than you fought rain. You built your roof, you kept your head down, you dispatched your cabs and catalogued your fares and told yourself invisibility was a kind of safety. Dane Colter had just said aloud the bargain I'd made with myself at twenty-three, and hearing it in another man's mouth, wearing a badge, soaked in whiskey and self-loathing—I understood that he wasn't threatening me so much as pleading with me to take the deal he'd taken, so he wouldn't be the only one in the cage.

"Pier-night," I said quietly. "Five years ago. There was a fare logged from the cemetery gate. Driver waited forty minutes at the cannery pier on someone's instruction. That instruction came over the radio in a voice I've now heard maybe two hundred times, because I listen to it every morning giving the road report." I let that sit. "Young deputy. Polite. Very calm. Said, 'Keep the engine running and don't ask the passenger anything.'"

Dane closed his eyes.

"She was dead," he said. "Officially. Three days drowned. You understand? She was already dead. Nobody was taking her anywhere she hadn't already been."

"Except away."

"Except away." He opened his eyes and they were wet, and I honestly could not tell you if it was the whiskey or the water. "You know what I did before this? Before the badge? I was seventeen and I went through the ice off Gull Point, and a girl pulled me out. Sixteen years old, skinny thing, arms like wire, and she hauled me out of black water that had already decided to keep me. Held me on the bank till I coughed my lungs right side out. Never told a soul. Never wanted anything for it." He laughed, that ugly laugh again, aimed at himself this time. "Five years ago the water came back for a debt, and I paid it wrong. I paid it to the wrong people."

"What happened at the pier, Dane?"

And he turned to me, fully turned, and for one suspended second I believed he was going to say it—all of it, the drowning, the staging, Celeste's signature on the death certificate, the whole rotten architecture—and I pressed my hand flat against my chest where the recorder ticked against my ribs like a second heart.

What he said was: "She asked me to help her disappear. And I did. Everything else—" His jaw worked. "Everything else was already done before I got there. I just drove the getaway."

"From what?"

"From Parish." He drained the glass. "From her own family. From a name that eats its young. You think Augustin Voss died natural? You think that box was robbed?" He shook his head slowly, and something in the motion frightened me more than any threat had, because it was the motion of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it. "Nobody robbed that coffin, Joubert. Somebody emptied it on purpose, and when you find out why, you're going to wish you'd stayed behind your headset."

My blood went cold and stayed there. Because he hadn't denied the stones. He'd explained them.

"Dane—"

"My wife used to say I talk too much." He fumbled for his wallet, dropped a bill on the bar that covered both our drinks and then some. "Go home, dispatcher. Take the arithmetic. Front of it or beside it. Those are the doors."

The phone behind the bar rang. Odile answered, listened, and her eyes came up and found Dane with a precision that told me everything before she spoke.

"Sheriff. Mrs. Voss for you. Says it's urgent."

Something shut in Dane Colter's face like a cell door. He stood, swayed once, caught himself on the bar rail, and tucked his shirt in with drunken, terrible dignity—badge, belt, the whole costume of the law going back on over the man.

"Tell her I'm coming," he said.

Then he leaned down close to me, close enough that I could see the fear swimming under the bourbon, and said, almost gently:

"You never heard any of this. And Joubert—if you go looking for the man who drove that fare, don't. Amos Petit doesn't deserve what happens to people who talk to you."

The bell over the door clanged. He was gone into the dark, and I sat very still with my hand over my chest, feeling the recorder's spools turning, turning, holding every slurred word of it—*she asked me to help her disappear, nobody robbed that coffin, somebody emptied it on purpose*—while Odile pretended to wipe the bar and the jukebox played something about a river.

* * *

I sat in my cab outside the Anchor for twenty minutes, letting the tape run back through my head until it wore grooves.

Amos Petit. He'd said the name himself, unprompted, the way guilty men hand you the knife and watch whether you cut. Which meant one of two things: either Dane was drunk enough to lose control of the one secret that mattered, or Dane was sober enough, even now, to feed me a name and count on me walking straight into whatever trap was built around it.

But here's what Dane didn't know, lying in his bed of arithmetic: I'd already found Amos Petit. Two weeks ago, in the oldest ledger, the one with the water-stained cover—fare 4471, cemetery gate to cannery pier, driver: A. Petit, retired the following spring with money nobody could explain. I'd been sitting on that name like an egg, afraid of what would hatch.

Now Dane had confirmed it was warm.

I drove home through streets slick with river fog, past the darkened historical society, past the church where Ruth Ellery slept above her organ pipes with a dead woman's hands. At a red light that hung green for no one at that hour, I played the tape back once, low, just to hear it was real. Dane's voice filled the cab: *everything else was already done before I got there.*

Before he got there. Which meant the drowning, the staging, the death certificate—all of it had a shape before the young deputy arrived at the pier with his radio and his calm voice. Someone had designed it. Someone had signed it.

Celeste Voss had called him away at midnight, urgent, and I knew—I knew the way I knew a fare was going to skip before the passenger knew it themselves—that somewhere across town, the widow was moving pieces, and the piece she was moving tonight was a dying old cabbie named Amos Petit.

I turned the cab toward the bay side of town and put my foot down.
