## 20. Broadcast

The dispatch console had never been built for what I was about to do to it.

That was the thing about the Parish Taxi & Livery board—installed in 1974, upgraded once, grudgingly, in 1998. A wall of toggle switches, a patch bay with hand-labeled jacks, and the old emergency relay coupler that every dispatcher before me had treated like a museum piece: the line that could, in theory, feed audio straight into the parish's civil emergency frequency. Hurricane protocol. Flood protocol. The kind of thing you tested twice a year with a tone and never once used for real.

I had tested it myself, four years ago, out of the same boredom that had made me alphabetize five years of fares. It worked. Nobody had noticed. Nobody ever noticed me.

That had been the whole architecture of my life, and tonight I was going to burn it down with it.

* * *

It was 6:40 in the evening. The centennial broadcast went out at seven from the parish hall stage—WPTN-AM, the little station old Henri Boudreaux had run from a cinderblock building behind the feed store since before I was born. Every radio in the parish would be tuned to it. Every porch, every truck cab, every kitchen. The founding families had bought the hour themselves—a full hour of ceremony, speeches, the reading of Augustin Voss's founding proclamation, Celeste Voss's closing address. Henri had told me about it on Tuesday, proud as a man announcing his own funeral arrangements.

"They want the whole hour," he'd said. "Can you believe it. Celeste Voss, live, uninterrupted."

I'd said something polite. I'd been planning this since Wednesday.

The plan had three pieces, and they sat on the console in front of me now, laid out like surgical instruments.

Piece one: the pier-night tape. Five years and three weeks old, pulled from the archive reel I'd hidden inside the housing of the water heater in the dispatch office bathroom—the one place Dane Colter's men had already searched once and found nothing but rust. I'd dubbed it onto a cassette at the library's listening booth, then dubbed it again at Amos Petit's kitchen table while the old man listened to his own younger voice come back at him and wept without any sound at all.

Piece two: Amos's statement. Recorded two nights ago, his oxygen tank hissing in the background like a second narrator, his voice thin but level. *They paid me four hundred dollars to forget a fare. I forgot it for five years. I can't forget it no more, not with my lungs the way they are.* He named the names. He named the amount. He named the pier.

Piece three: the ledger. Forty-one pages of the historical society's private accounts, photographed on my phone in the dark of the archive room, transcribed in my own careful hand onto legal pads because I didn't trust anything digital anymore. The cannery closure fund. The "hazard remediation" payments—eleven thousand dollars, the year my ledgers said a man named Étienne Voss-Beaumont, dockhand, age twenty-four, went into the water off the cannery pier and never came out. The quarterly disbursements to a law firm in Baton Rouge. The signature at the bottom of every page: A. Voss, then, after his death, C. Voss.

Celeste had been signing the hush money for five years. Her husband's coffin had barely cooled before her pen started moving.

* * *

At 6:52, my phone buzzed. Dane.

I let it ring. Then it buzzed again. I picked up, because I wanted to hear his voice one last time before it was worth nothing.

"You're not at the office," he said. No greeting. He'd been calling every six hours since the suspension, and every call had the same texture—a man patting his pockets for something he'd already lost.

"Where would I be, Sheriff?"

"Don't." A breath. Static on the line, or maybe it was him breathing through his teeth. "Cyrus. I'm telling you as a friend, which is more than I've got left to be. Whatever you think you're doing tonight—don't. Celeste's people are already at the tide-flats. She knows. About Mara. I don't know how, but she knows, and she sent men, and if you go out there—"

"I'm not going out there."

A pause. He hadn't expected that. "Then where—"

"Henri's broadcast starts in eight minutes," I said. "You should be at the parish hall. I hear Celeste gives a hell of a speech."

The silence that came back down the line was the longest we'd ever shared, and I understood it completely. He was doing the arithmetic. The emergency frequency. The dispatch console. The man who had spent five years invisible, holding every thread the parish's secrets were woven from.

"Cyrus," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle like river ice in March. "Mara will never be safe again. Not if you do this. They'll come for her."

"They've been coming for her for five years, Dane. You just called it keeping her alive." I looked at the clock. 6:55. "There's one difference between us. You protected her by burying the truth. I'm protecting her by digging it up. Only one of those holds when the next tide comes."

"You don't know what they'll do."

"I know exactly what they'll do. It's all in the ledger."

I hung up before he could answer, and my hands were shaking, and I let them shake. Fear was fine. Fear was fuel. What I couldn't afford was the other thing—the loneliness, the little voice that said *you could still put it all back, alphabetize it, log it, file the night away like every other night.* I put that voice in a box and I set the box on fire.

* * *

At 6:58 I keyed the console.

The procedure was stupid-simple, which was why it had survived forty years unexamined: pull the patch cord from the incoming emergency line, cross-patch the auxiliary input to the relay coupler, open the channel. The coupler didn't broadcast on WPTN itself. It fed the parish's civil alert tone system—the same speakers bolted to the volunteer fire hall, the church steeple, the cannery water tower, the four corners of the tide-flats road. Henri's station signal went out over the airwaves; my feed would go out over the ground. Every alert speaker in the parish, plus every scanner, plus every radio in every cruiser and every cab, carried the emergency channel as a standing monitor. You couldn't turn it off without a wrench. The founding families had built it that way, back when they still believed infrastructure was a form of love.

They had built the mouth. I was only going to put words in it.

At 7:00 exactly, I heard Henri Boudreaux's voice come up on the monitor feed—warm, ceremonial, a little awed: *"Good evening, Parish, and welcome to the centennial hour. One hundred years ago tonight, Augustin Voss drove the first piling for the cannery that built this town—"*

I let him run for four minutes. I wanted the whole parish settled in. Porches. Kitchens. The parish hall itself, where I knew from the setup crew that Henri had wired the stage speakers to carry both the AM feed and, because Henri was Henri and never met a system he didn't bridge, the emergency monitor as a backup. Whatever I sent down the wire would land in that hall, over Celeste Voss's head, while she stood in her black dress waiting for her hour.

At 7:04, I pulled the patch cord and opened the channel.

I had rehearsed the words in the cab, parked at the boat launch, forty minutes earlier. I said them now, plain, into the microphone that had only ever carried addresses and fare confirmations and the small liturgy of the night shift.

*"This is Cyrus Joubert, dispatcher, Parish Taxi and Livery. What you are hearing is the parish emergency channel. It is not an emergency. Or it is, but not the kind with sirens. For the next forty minutes I am going to play you the truth about Augustin Voss's empty coffin, about the cannery pier, and about a woman this parish buried alive five years ago. The founding families paid to keep it quiet. I'm not charging anything. Listen."*

Then I pressed play on the pier-night tape.

* * *

You have to understand what the parish heard.

First, the hiss of five-year-old archive reel. Then my own younger voice, flat, procedural, reading back the fare: *"Pickup, cemetery gate, 11:47 PM. Passenger: M. Ellery. Destination: cannery pier."* A pause. Then a second voice—young, calm, wearing the particular authority of a man who has never once been contradicted. Deputy Dane Colter, five years younger, directing the driver: *"You'll wait at the gate. However long it takes. Nobody comes down that road tonight. That's not a request, it's the parish."*

Then the driver's voice—Amos Petit, younger, uncertain: *"Deputy, that woman's supposed to be dead. They buried her Tuesday."*

And Dane, calm as still water: *"That's right, Amos. And if you ever want to be alive yourself, that's what she'll stay."*

I let it run. I let the whole tape run—the long silence of the wait, the return of the cab at 1:22 AM, the passenger's voice, barely, through the window, one sentence that I had listened to perhaps two hundred times: *"Tell my brother I'm sorry I couldn't save him twice."*

Then I stopped the tape, and in the silence I could hear—through the monitor, through the open window of the dispatch office, through the very walls of the parish—something I had never heard before: the town, all at once, making a single sound. Not a scream. A low, collective intake, like the sea before it decides.

* * *

I played Amos next.

His voice came through the speakers thin and oxygen-rattled and absolutely unbending. He told them about the four hundred dollars. He told them about the envelope with the fleur-de-lis seal he'd found under his wiper blade every quarter for five years, like a subscription to silence. He named the men who'd delivered it: Raymond Thibodeaux. Paul Arceneaux. And once—only once, the first time—Celeste Voss herself, in her black Mercedes, who had looked at him through the glass and mouthed, slowly, so there could be no misunderstanding: *thank you for your discretion.*

Then I read the ledger. Forty-one pages. I read every entry, every date, every signature. The parish heard the number Étienne Voss-Beaumont's life had been assigned: eleven thousand dollars, entered as *hazard remediation, pier structure, 20XX.* They heard the quarterly payments. They heard the law firm's name. They heard Celeste's signature, five years of it, neat as embroidery.

And then, because the ledger alone was paper and the tape alone was a ghost, I told them the part that made it flesh.

*"Étienne Voss-Beaumont was a dockhand at the cannery. He was twenty-four years old. On the night of the closure, he found the founding families' account books in the pier office—found out the closure wasn't bankruptcy, it was theft, that Augustin Voss and his partners had stripped the cannery's pension fund and were going to blame the tide. He said he'd go to the state. Three men walked him out onto the pier to change his mind. He went into the water instead. His sister, Mara Voss, saw it from the shore. She was going to testify. So they killed her too—on paper. They buried an empty coffin next to her father-in-law's empty coffin, and the parish stood at the graveside and wept for two people who were never in the ground. One of them is alive. She has been playing the organ at St. Brigid's for five years under the name Ruth Ellery. She has done nothing wrong except survive. The people who did do something wrong have names, and I've just read you all of them."*

I paused. The monitor was alive with sound now—Henri's broadcast still running underneath, oblivious, the founding proclamation being read in a schoolteacher's careful voice, and over it, bleeding through from the parish hall itself, a murmur like a hive opening.

*"One more thing,"* I said. *"Celeste Voss sent men to the tide-flats an hour ago, looking for Mara Voss and the records she's kept. If you're one of those men, the whole parish just heard your employer confess. Go home. If you're the parish—the tide-flats road is where she is. She's been dead five years. It's time somebody came for her alive."*

* * *

I cut the channel at 7:31 and sat in the sudden quiet of the dispatch office, and the quiet was enormous, like the inside of a bell after it's been struck.

My hands were still on the console. I realized I was listening for sirens—for Celeste's men, for Dane, for the state police, for anything. What came instead was stranger. Headlights. Dozens of them, moving up the bayou road toward the tide-flats, not fast, not angry—steady, deliberate, the whole parish getting into its trucks at once the way it does for a flood or a funeral. Somebody had turned on a porch light. Then another. Then, down the whole length of Water Street, all of them, every porch on the row, lit up like a runway.

I had spent five years learning the parish one fare at a time, one secret at a time, alone behind a headset. It had never once occurred to me that the town was listening too. That everyone had been keeping their own ledgers. That all it took was one voice on the wire to make the arithmetic public.

The phone rang. I let it ring. It rang again, and again—Celeste's number, I'd have bet the cab, and then numbers I didn't know, and then the dispatch line itself, lighting up like Christmas. I didn't answer any of them. There was nothing left to say into a handset. Everything that needed saying was already outside, moving up the bayou road in a river of headlights.

I took the master tape, the ledger transcription, and Amos's cassette, and I put them in a plastic bag, and I put the bag inside my coat, and I walked out to my cab.

The tide was coming in. Mara was on the flats. And for the first time in the history of the Parish, the whole town was driving toward the truth instead of away from it—I just had to get there before the water did.
