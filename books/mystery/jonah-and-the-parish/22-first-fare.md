## 22. First Fare

The cab was his now—had been for four months, since the company folded under the weight of what everyone had heard on the emergency frequency that June night. Cyrus Joubert had bought it at auction for less than the radio in its dashboard was worth, and he'd spent a week of evenings pulling the dispatch console out and then, on the seventh night, putting it back. Some instruments you don't sell even when the orchestra has scattered.

He still kept the ledger. That hadn't changed either.

The town had changed, though. Parish in October wore its scandal like a coat two sizes too big—everyone swimming in it, shoulders shrugging against the fabric, nobody quite able to take it off. The cannery's skeleton had come down in August, and the pier was being rebuilt with state money and shame money in equal measure, new pilings driven into the same old mud. They were calling it the Centennial Pier now, which struck Cyrus as the kind of naming that towns do when they want to bury something alive and give it a headstone at the same time.

Celeste Voss was awaiting trial. Amos Petit had died in September, peaceful, in his sleep, with a lawyer's letter on his nightstand absolving him of nothing and everything. Dane Colter lived forty miles east now, working security at a grain terminal, and sent Cyrus a postcard once a month that never said anything except the weather.

And Mara—

Mara Voss, née Ruth Ellery, née a third name Cyrus had learned only last month over coffee, a name from before all of it—waited at the end of things the way she always had: quietly, in plain sight, until you knew to look.

* * *

The call came in at 4:47 on a Tuesday afternoon, which was the hour the light went long and gold across the tide-flats and made even the mud look like something worth photographing.

"Dispatch." He said it out of habit, though there was no one to dispatch to but himself.

"I need a fare." Her voice, low and even, with that faint organ-note underneath it that he'd spent five years not hearing and three months unable to stop hearing. "Corner of Delacroix and Main. Going to the pier."

"You know I charge by the mile," Cyrus said. "Not by the secret."

"That's why I called you."

He wrote it in the ledger anyway, because the ledger was the one true thing he owned: *4:47 p.m. — One passenger. Delacroix & Main → Centennial Pier. Fare: settled long ago.*

The cab smelled like coffee and old vinyl and, faintly, of the sea salt that got into everything in Parish and never left, the way grief gets into a house. He drove the six blocks with his hands easy on the wheel and his heart doing something undignified against his ribs.

She was waiting on the corner in a gray coat, her hair down for once instead of pinned back like a woman sitting vigil over her own life. She got in without hurrying, the way people get into cabs when the driver is someone they've already decided to trust, or decided to stop being afraid of, which amounts to the same decision made in a different order.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm exactly on time. You're early."

"One of us should be."

The ride took nine minutes. Neither of them filled it with talk. That was another thing he'd learned about her—that silence between them wasn't a wall but a room, and they could both stand in it comfortably, looking at different walls. The flats opened up on their left, silvered and enormous, and the new pier stuck out into the bay like a sentence someone had started and hadn't finished.

"They're done with the reconstruction," she said as he pulled onto the gravel lot. "Finished Friday. There's a ceremony Saturday. Ribbon, speeches, a bishop."

"Will you play?"

"No." She looked out at the water. "I'm retired from playing for other people's memories."

He parked. She didn't move to get out, and neither did he. The meter read $6.50. He reached over and clicked it off.

"The tide's low," she said. "Really low. Spring tide. It's giving things back."

* * *

They walked out together on the new planking, which smelled of cut lumber and creosote, past the ribbon stanchions not yet unwound, to where the old pier's bones still showed at the waterline—the stubbed-off pilings of the original structure, black with a century of salt, standing in the drained mud like the teeth of something that had died with its mouth open.

"There," Mara said.

Down in the muck, half-exposed, lay a shape that had been part of the cannery's foundation—a collapsed section of the old loading platform, timber and rusted iron, and caught in its lattice, glinting dully, objects that had no business surviving five years of tides.

Cyrus climbed down. The mud took his shoes to the ankle and held them with the patient grip of a thing that had been holding secrets longer than either of them had been alive. He worked the first object free with both hands.

A lunch pail. Tin, dented, the paint worn to bare metal in the shape of a hand that had carried it ten thousand times. Inside, when he pried the clasp, a thermos gone to rust and a folded work shirt stiff as cardboard.

"It's his," Mara said from above him. Her voice didn't break. It had done all its breaking years ago; what was left was something harder and quieter. "That's Étienne's. He ate off that pail every shift for eleven years. I used to pack it."

Cyrus passed it up to her, then went back for the rest. A boot, sole separated, floating up out of the mud like a confession. A canvas tool roll, tied with wire, containing chisels worn to nubs. And last, wrapped in oilcloth that some careful dead man's hands had knotted against the water, a tin box, and inside the tin box—

The watch.

No. Not *the* watch. A second watch. Étienne's everyday watch, the cheap one, the one he'd worn to work because he wouldn't risk the good one. Its crystal was fogged from the inside with five years of brine, but the caseback, when Cyrus turned it over, was engraved clean as the day the jeweler's tool had touched it:

*É.V. — For my sister, who keeps time better than any of us. — M.V., Christmas 1979*

"He gave me the good one," Mara said. Cyrus climbed back up beside her, mud to his knees, holding the tin box like an offering. "The one I gave you. This one stayed in his locker. The police never searched the lockers—they closed the case in nine days. Nine days, Cyrus. My brother drowned on a Tuesday and by the following Thursday they'd decided he'd been drinking and slipped."

"And he didn't drink."

"He didn't drink." She said it steadily, but her thumb moved over the fogged crystal in small circles, wiping at a fog that would never lift. "He was meeting Augustin that night. He had the ledger pages—the real ones, the ones showing what the families skimmed off the workers' fund after the fire. He was going to give Augustin one chance to make it right before he went to the state. And Augustin—" She stopped. Started again. "Augustin brought my uncle. And the pier railing was old, and my brother was tired, and the water was cold, and it took ninety seconds. I've timed it. Ninety seconds is how long it takes to drown a man who can swim."

The tide-flats stretched out around them, empty and shining, holding nothing back anymore. Out past the channel markers a shrimp boat dragged its wake across the gold light.

"Why here?" Cyrus asked. "Why tonight?"

"Because the construction crew found the old platform collapsed last week and the foreman called the historical society, and the historical society called me." She almost smiled. "Apparently the parish organist is who you call about old things now. My reputation has undergone certain revisions."

"And you wanted to see it first. Before anyone else."

"I wanted *him* seen first." She looked down at the pail, the boot, the watch. "Five years he was down there under everything they built on top of him. Under the settlement, under the silence, under my funeral. Everyone in this town walked over my brother's grave every day and called it progress." She closed the tin box. "So yes. Before the bishop. Before the ribbon. Before Celeste Voss's lawyers start filing motions about desecration or whatever word they'll use. Him first."

* * *

They sat on the edge of the new pier with their legs hanging over the water, which was something neither of them had ever done here before, because the old pier had been a place where things happened to people, and the new pier was too young to have earned fear yet.

The sun was going down behind the cannery's absence, and the sky did what Parish skies did at that hour—went soft and enormous, rose and copper, forgiving everything for approximately eleven minutes.

"My lawyer called today," Mara said. "The state's formally declined to pursue anything against me. Falsified death certificate, alias, all of it. The prosecutor's exact words were 'the court lacks jurisdiction over a deceased person.'" She shook her head. "Dane's testimony bought that. His whole career, cashed in for one lie told under oath: *I believed she was dead.* Which was true, in a way. We all believed it. That was the entire architecture of the thing—everyone believing it, including me, most days."

"Do you?" Cyrus asked. "Believe it?"

She considered that with the seriousness she gave everything, turning the question over like the watch in her hands.

"I believe Ruth Ellery is dead," she said finally. "Ruth Ellery sat at that organ for five years and played for people who thought she was a quiet woman with no history, and she let them think it, and it nearly hollowed her out. But Mara—" She looked at him sideways, and the last of the light caught her face. "Mara just came back from the dead twice in one year. Once when you broadcast those tapes, and once tonight, when the tide gave my brother back. So I'm going to find out who she is. For the first time. At thirty-four years old."

"Late blooming," Cyrus said. "Some of us take longer."

"What about you? Who are you, now that the whole town knows your name?"

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he'd had months to practice the answer.

"Lighter," he said. "Turns out carrying everybody's secrets was heavier than I knew until I put them down. I miss parts of it. Being invisible. It was a good place to live." He watched the water. "But it turns out invisible isn't the same as unseen. You saw me. Back at the beginning, before any of it—you saw the dispatcher, not the furniture. I keep thinking about that. How you looked right through five years of my ledgers and found the one line that mattered."

"Page 312," she said. "October 14th. Cemetery gate to cannery pier, one passenger, fare paid cash, driver: A. Petit." She recited it like scripture, and maybe it was. "I've had a long time to memorize it too."

The tide had begun to turn. They could hear it before they could see it—the far-off hiss of the channel filling, the flats starting to gleam and shiver as the water came back for its nightly reclaiming. In an hour the mud would be sea again, and whatever remained down there would stay down there, and that would be all right. Some things belonged to the water. Her brother's bones. Augustin Voss's name, which was coming off the courthouse frieze next spring per court order. The version of themselves they'd each been when this started—one man behind a headset, one woman behind a hymnal, both of them ghosts in a town full of living liars.

"Cyrus." She said his name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "I'm leaving Parish."

He'd known. He'd known for weeks, the way he'd known things for fifteen years—from small data, from patterns, from the shape of silences. Still, hearing it landed somewhere below the ribs.

"When?"

"Sunday. There's a position in Lafayette. St. Brigid's. Small parish, bad organ, kind pastor who doesn't ask questions—or doesn't ask many." She smiled a little. "I told him I was a widow. Technically true. I've buried more identities than most morticians."

"Where does that leave the fare?"

She turned to face him fully then, and the tide hissed in the dark below them, and she reached into her coat pocket and brought out a pen.

"It leaves it unpaid," she said. "Which means you have to collect."

* * *

He drove her back into town because she asked him to, and because the ledger had one blank line left and he wanted to see what she'd do with it.

At the corner of Delacroix and Main, under the streetlight where five years ago a woman everyone believed dead had stepped out of the dark into Amos Petit's cab, she opened the ledger across her knees and held the pen above the page for a long moment.

"Not Mara Voss," she said. "That name belongs to the trial now. Not Ruth Ellery—she served her time." She glanced up at him. "You get to witness it. That's the fare. You wanted to matter, Cyrus Joubert. Congratulations. You're the only person in Louisiana who will know this name exists."

She wrote. He watched the letters form, slow and deliberate, a hand that had practiced being no one learning to be someone in real time.

Then she closed the book, handed it back, and kissed him—briefly, firmly, on the mouth, the kind of kiss that is a signature on a document, binding and witnessed—and got out of the cab.

"Lafayette is ninety minutes," she said through the open door. "Bad roads, worse radio stations. Terrible fare for a taxi driver."

"I don't drive for the fare," Cyrus said.

"I know," said Mara—whoever she was now; he'd read the name; he'd carry it. "Neither do I."

She walked up her street and into her building, and the door closed behind her, and Cyrus Joubert sat alone in his cab in the dark with the engine ticking and the ledger warm on the seat beside him.

He opened it to the last page and read what she'd written, and laughed once, out loud, at the audacity of it—at her, at all of it, at the whole improbable machinery of a town that had lied for a century finally telling the truth because a taxi dispatcher couldn't sleep.

Then he started the engine, turned the page to a fresh line, and wrote the only entry that mattered:

*8:52 p.m. — One passenger. Parish → Lafayette. Fare: everything I've got.*

He pulled away from the curb. Behind him the tide came in over the flats, covering the old pier's bones, keeping what was left to keep, giving back everything else.
