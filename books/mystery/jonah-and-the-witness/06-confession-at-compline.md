## 6. Confession at Compline

The last service of the day emptied out of the cathedral like water finding its level, and Jonah Reyes stood in the shadow of the north transept and watched Lena Underhill do the thing she did every evening: she touched the stone.

Not a caress. Nothing so sentimental. It was more like a customs officer's inspection — her palm flat against the twelfth-century pillar, fingers spread, as if checking the building for contraband. Then she moved to the next one, and the next, working her way down the aisle in the blue dusk while the vergers snuffed candles behind her. She was saying goodnight to the cathedral the way other people said goodnight to a spouse they no longer loved but could not imagine leaving.

He had hired that woman to save his life. He had not known he was hiring a witness.

*Come back after Compline,* she'd said on the phone, and her voice had been careful in a way that frightened him more than shouting would have. *There are things I need to ask you where the walls can't hear.*

Now the walls couldn't hear, or so they both pretended, and Jonah stood with his hands jammed in his coat pockets watching her cross the empty nave toward him, her footsteps ringing off the flagstones like small deliberate bells.

"You came," she said.

"I considered running instead." He tried for lightness and heard it crack. "I got as far as the station car park."

"What stopped you?"

"The thought of you reading my file afterward." He meant it as a joke. It didn't land as one. Her face in the candle-glow was grave and patient, an archivist's face, the face of someone who had spent her life among records of people who were sure they had time. "Ms. Underhill—Lena—I owe you an explanation. The real one. Not the version I rehearsed in your office."

"Then we should sit," she said. "Confessions go better sitting down. It's why they built all these pews."

* * *

She chose a bench halfway down the nave, beneath a window whose saints had been reassembled after the war by glaziers working from memory, so that one figure held a chalice where his sword should be and another wore a bishop's mitre over what was unmistakably a soldier's tunic. Jonah had noticed the window on his first visit and found it oddly comforting: even holiness, repaired, was a patchwork of honest mistakes.

Lena sat at the far end of the pew, angled toward him, her notebook closed on her knee. Waiting. She was very good at waiting. He understood now that this was the whole of her craft — not deduction, not brilliance, just the discipline of letting silence do its work until people handed over the truth like something broken they wanted fixed.

"My name isn't entirely Jonah Reyes," he began, and then stopped, because that sounded mad. "No. That's dramatic. My name is Jonah Reyes. But the man who walked into your office four days ago was performing a role, and you deserve to know who wrote the script."

"Go on."

"I'm an accountant." He laughed, short and bitter. "You've already guessed that, haven't you? You looked at my hands."

"I looked at your cufflinks," she said. "Engraved initials. People who wear monogrammed cufflinks to hide from the world usually work in numbers or law. Law lies better."

"Accounting," he said. "For eleven years. The last three of them for the Meridian Trust — a charitable foundation. Restoration grants, heritage endowments, that sort of thing. Respectable money moving through respectable channels." He turned his cufflink slowly under his thumb. "Except that eighteen months ago I was doing a routine reconciliation and I found a mortuary roll."

Her breath caught — a small sound, quickly mastered, but he heard it.

"A medieval document," she said carefully. "In a modern charity's accounts?"

"In a scanned archive attached to a grant file. St. Aldhelm's had applied for funding through Meridian — roof repairs, the crypt conservation project. The supporting documentation included images of historical records. And one of them wasn't right." His mouth had gone dry; he pressed on. "A mortuary roll is a list of the dead, compiled so that monasteries would pray for their souls. This one had been added to. Names in different hands, different centuries — and three names written in ballpoint pen. Modern names. Living men, when they were entered. Men who died within the year. Accidents. Falls, fires, a drowning."

"Thomas Ellery," Lena said quietly. "Michael Sorrell. David Anwyl."

Jonah stared at her. "You know them."

"I know their files." Her knuckles had gone white around the notebook. "I spent two years telling anyone who would listen that those accidents weren't accidents. No one listened. So tell me how their names came to be in a thirteenth-century prayer roll, Mr. Reyes, and take your time, because I have waited a long time for this sentence to finish."

So he told her. How he had photographed the page and started pulling threads — shell companies, restoration invoices for work never done, payments routed through the Meridian Trust to a consultancy called Hallow & Vane, and behind Hallow & Vane, always, the same signature authority. The Dean of St. Aldhelm's. Wycliffe.

How he had done the stupidest thing of his life: he had emailed a query. A polite, professional, devastating query, cc'd to the wrong person — a man named Vane himself, the fixer, whose name he hadn't yet understood was not a coincidence but a warning.

"Forty minutes later," Jonah said, "I got a reply. One line. *Some ledgers record deaths before they occur. Yours has been updated.* And then my flat was searched — professionally, nothing taken, everything moved two inches. And I understood that I hadn't found a fraud. I'd found a filing system. They don't hide their murders, Ms. Underhill. They archive them."

The candles had burned lower. Somewhere above them a door boomed shut, and the sound rolled down the nave like a held breath released.

"So I ran," Jonah said. "And then I stopped running, because running is what the others did, and the others are in the ground. Running gives them privacy. I needed witnesses. I needed—" He made himself look at her. "I needed the murder to be impossible to commit quietly. So I made noise. I hired an archivist with a reputation for seeing through staged deaths. I told her exactly when and where I would die, in detail, dated, witnessed, documented. Because a predicted murder has an audience, and an audience is the one thing men like Wycliffe cannot archive."

Lena was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was strange — not angry, which he had braced for, but wondering, almost wounded.

"You didn't hire me to investigate."

"No."

"You hired me to be seen hiring you. I'm not your investigator, Jonah. I'm your alibi. Your shield. If anything happens to you, there's a paper trail leading straight from your death to my inquiry to the crypt to the ledger. I'm the reason it can't be quiet." She looked down at her hands. "You used me."

"Yes." He didn't flinch from it, though everything in him wanted to. "And I'd do it again, because the alternative is a shallow grave behind a founder's tomb and a line of ballpoint pen in a book of prayers. But I'll tell you what I didn't expect." His voice dropped. "I didn't expect you to be brave. I read about your cases — the inquest files, the letters you wrote, the way they treated you. A woman who kept knocking on doors that had been locked against her for years. I expected a professional. I didn't expect—" He stopped himself.

"Don't," Lena said softly. But she had shifted along the pew without seeming to notice she'd done it. Six feet of medieval oak between them had become three.

"They suspended DS Marsh today," she said. "Fabricated charges. Which means they're clearing the board, Jonah. Friday isn't a threat anymore. It's a schedule."

"I know."

"And you sat here tonight anyway. In their cathedral. Beneath their window."

"Where else would I confess?" He smiled, and it hurt. "Besides — you said the walls can't hear. I'm starting to believe you keep this place's secrets better than its masters do."

She turned to look up at the patched window, the saint with the chalice and the sword, and something in her face eased and sharpened at once. When she spoke again it was barely above a whisper, and it was not forgiveness, not yet, but it was the beginning of the road that led there.

"Friday night," she said. "Compline ends at nine. After that, the chapter has the crypt booked for a private conservation inspection. Your name will be on that list, won't it? As the donor's representative."

"Yes."

"Then here is what happens now." She opened her notebook at last, and took out a pen — a plain ballpoint, and they both looked at it, and something passed between them that was half grief and half vow. "You will write nothing else in any ledger. From tonight, if a record is going to be made of what happens in that crypt, I will make it myself. In my own hand. While I'm standing there."

"Lena." He reached out — stopped — let his hand rest on the pew between them, palm up, an offering rather than a claim. "If they come for me Friday, they'll come for whoever stands with me."

"I know," said the archivist of St. Aldhelm's, closing her hand over his. "That's why I'll be the one holding the pen."

Above them, in the dark gallery, a door that should have been locked clicked softly shut.
