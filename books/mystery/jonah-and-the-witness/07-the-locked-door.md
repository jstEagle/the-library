## 7. The Locked Door

Friday came the way weather comes to the coast — not all at once, but in a slow darkening that no one else seemed to notice.

Lena woke before dawn in her flat above the archive, and for a long moment she lay still beneath the wool blanket, listening to the cathedral breathe. It was an old habit. Stone expanded and contracted with the temperature; timbers groaned; the great building exhaled at night and drew breath again at first light, and if you had lived beside it long enough — inside it, really, because she had never quite left it since the day she'd been hired eleven years ago — you could read its moods like a face. This morning it said nothing comforting. This morning it creaked like a house holding a secret behind its teeth.

She dressed in dark clothes she would not have chosen on any other day. Grey jumper, black trousers, soft-soled shoes that made no sound on flagstone. She packed a small bag: torch, water, the decoded photographs of the mortuary roll copied onto two memory cards, one of which she had already posted to herself at her old address in Wells, the other tucked against her ribs in an inner pocket. A paper copy of everything sat in Ivo Marsh's desk drawer at the station, or had until Tuesday, when they had taken his warrant card and his keys and told him not to come back.

*Conduct unbecoming,* the letter had said. *An unauthorised inquiry into chapter business.* As though the chapter were a country with borders. As though asking who had ordered fresh mortar laid in a sealed crypt were a crime.

Ivo had called her from a phone box outside the chippy on Fore Street, of all places, his voice flat with a humiliation he was trying to make sound like anger. "They're not even pretending it's about procedure, Lena. Wycliffe signed the complaint himself. He shook my hand at the harvest supper."

"I know," she'd said.

"They'll come for you next."

"Let them." And she had meant it, which frightened her more than anything else that week.

* * *

By eight o'clock the cathedral was filling with the ordinary machinery of a Friday morning. Vergers unlocking doors. The flower team arguing gently over lilies. A school group assembling in the south transept, thirty children in high-visibility vests, their voices ricocheting off the Norman pillars like birds trapped in a barn.

Lena moved through it all carrying a conservation crate — a genuine one, borrowed legitimately from her own stores, because whatever else happened today she intended to be able to account for every object in her care. Inside the crate, beneath a layer of acid-free tissue and a convincing arrangement of document folders, were the things she actually needed: a thermos, a cushion folded flat, a wind-up travel clock, and the small brass key to the crypt's outer door, which she had signed out three weeks ago under cover of a routine environmental survey and never signed back in.

The notice had gone up yesterday, laminated and official:

**CRYPT CLOSED FRIDAY FOR PRIVATE CONSERVATION INSPECTION BY APPOINTMENT OF THE CHAPTER. NO ACCESS WITHOUT WRITTEN AUTHORISATION.**

Private. That word had been doing a great deal of work all week. Private inspection, private contractor, private meeting of the Fabric Advisory Committee — rescheduled twice, then cancelled, then quietly struck from the online calendar by someone with access to the communications office. Everything about Friday had been arranged to leave no witness.

So Lena had made herself into one instead.

It was not a plan so much as an argument she had won against herself at four in the morning. She could not stop what was coming; she had understood that by Wednesday, when Jonah had stopped answering his phone and his rented room above the antiquarian bookshop had been found neatly vacated, rent paid through the month, no forwarding address. Either he had run — and some cold, sensible part of her hoped he had, hoped he was already on a train to somewhere with sea in it — or he was exactly where he had always said he would be on Friday, because a man who stages his own predicted death cannot simply decline to attend it. The prophecy was the trap. The trap needed bait. And Jonah Reyes had spent months making himself irresistible bait.

But if she could not prevent it, she could *witness* it. There was a difference between a thing happening in the dark and a thing happening to someone who had seen it, who could name it, who had already scattered copies of the evidence beyond anyone's reach. An archivist knew better than most that records outlive intentions. She intended to be the record.

* * *

At half past nine she went down.

The stair to the crypt descended from behind the choir, a spiral of worn steps lit by a single bulb that buzzed like a trapped insect. At the bottom, the outer door — oak, iron-banded, older than the parish registers — stood where it always stood, and her key turned in it as it always turned, and then she was inside, in the low vaulted dark that smelled of cold stone and candle grease and, faintly, of lime mortar.

The inner chamber was the problem. Its door had been relocked after their discovery, and she did not have that key. But an archivist knows buildings the way a doctor knows bodies, and she knew that this door had been hung in 1740 by a carpenter named Elias Prynne whose workmanship, however honest, had settled with three centuries of floor movement. The frame had dropped a sixteenth of an inch at the top right corner. The door could be lifted, with effort, on its hinges' slack — if one had a lever, patience, and no audience.

She had brought a flat pry bar disguised among conservation tools. It took her eleven minutes and cost her a scraped knuckle and a moment of pure animal panic when something shifted overhead — only a pigeon, resettling in the triforium, but her heart slammed as though the whole cathedral had leaned down to look at her.

Then the inner chamber stood open, and she went in, and pulled the door almost shut behind her.

* * *

She chose her place the way she would have chosen shelving for a fragile volume: away from damp, out of the sightline of the doorway, with her back to something solid. Behind the founder's tomb there was a shallow recess where the medieval masons had stored their tools while they worked, a pocket of shadow barely four feet deep, floored with rubble. She spread the folded cushion, sat down with her back against the cold curve of the wall, and arranged the crate in front of her knees so that from the doorway the recess would read as nothing — mason's junk, centuries of it.

Then she waited.

Waiting, she discovered, was a discipline she had been training for all her life without knowing it. She had spent years alone in stacks and cellars, hours passing like water through a sieve, content in the company of things that could not speak. But this waiting had teeth. Every sound arrived doubled: the drip of condensation, and *the drip of condensation*; the far-off thunder of the organ being tested for Sunday, and *the organ, played by living hands, while men plan murder below*. Terror, she found, could be transposed like music — played in a lower key, at a slower tempo, until it became something almost bearable: stillness. Attention. Witness.

She thought about Jonah's hands. She had noticed them the first day, how they moved when he talked about numbers, conducting invisible columns, precise and nervous. She thought about the way he had looked at her when he confessed at Compline — not pleading, exactly. Asking to be seen. *You're not my investigator,* he'd said. *You're my alibi. My shield.* And she had wanted to be angry and hadn't managed it, because the truth underneath his terrible scheme was simple and human: he had shouted for help in every legitimate way available, and every door had opened onto the same locked corridor, and so he had built a fire in the corridor instead, and prayed someone would come with water rather than watch him burn.

She had come with water. She had also, possibly, walked into the fire.

Her travel clock ticked against her palm. Eleven forty. Above her, faintly, the noon service would gather; she heard footsteps cross the crossing overhead, the murmur of a congregation settling, the first notes of a hymn — and she thought, absurdly, of the school children in their high-visibility vests, of the flower arrangers and their lilies, of the whole bright surface of the cathedral going about its morning directly above the cavity behind the tomb where someone had mixed fresh mortar and waited for a body.

The hymn ended. The service proceeded. And then, at ten past twelve, as the last amen faded into stone, she heard the outer door.

Two sets of footsteps. No voices at first — just the careful, professional quiet of men who had done this before. Then a low voice, unhurried, almost bored: "Chapter says the inspection runs till two. We've got time to do it properly."

And a second voice, younger, tighter: "He's supposed to be here already."

"He'll be here. They always come. It's the ones who don't come that make mess." A pause; the scrape of something heavy being set down near the tomb. "Get the light. And mind the mortar — it's only been curing a fortnight."

A torch clicked on. Through the gap between the tomb and the wall, Lena watched the beam swing across the ceiling vaults, across the founder's effigy, across the hairline seam of new mortar she and Ivo had found — and settle, at last, on the inner door.

On the inner door, which stood open less than an inch.

The beam stopped.

Lena did not move. Her heart was a fist knocking on a door no one would answer. In the dark behind the tomb, in the smell of lime and old stone, the archivist of St. Aldhelm's held perfectly still, and listened, and witnessed — as the light crept toward her, inch by inch, along the floor.
