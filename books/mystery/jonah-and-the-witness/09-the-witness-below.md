## 9. The Witness Below

The dark of the crypt was not empty. Lena had learned that in thirty years among archives: darkness is only another kind of shelf, and everything shelved waits to be read.

She stood in the angle where the Norman pilaster met the wall, behind a stack of conservation crates the chapter had obligingly delivered that morning — *for the inspection*, the work order said, in Dean Wycliffe's own tidy hand. Her torch was off. Her phone was silenced. In her coat pocket, pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, lay the mortuary roll, wrapped in acid-free tissue as though it were already evidence. Which, God willing, it would be by midnight.

Above her, muffled through nine feet of stone and floor, Compline had begun. The voices of the small evening congregation came down thin and changed, less like singing than like weather — a warmth moving over a cold country. She let it steady her. She had hidden in this crypt before, as a girl, during a fire drill she'd been too shy to join on the green; she had hidden here as a young archivist, weeping once over a letter she could not bear to catalogue. The dark knew her. She was asking it now to know Jonah too.

He was somewhere near the tomb of Aldhelm the founder, keeping to the shadows on the far side, exactly where they'd agreed. She could not see him. She could hear him breathing when the wind through the ventilation shaft went still — a quick, careful breath, the breath of a man trying to make himself small. She wanted to call out something foolish and kind. She kept her silence the way one keeps a hand flat on an open book: so nothing turns the page before its time.

At 8:47, the door at the head of the stair unlocked.

Not forced — unlocked, with a key turning easy as Vespers, and that ease told her more than any confession could have. Only three keys existed. Hers, the chapter steward's, and the Dean's.

Two sets of footsteps descended. The first was unhurried, almost liturgical, each step placed like a candle on an altar rail. The second dragged slightly on the left — she had read that gait in the maintenance logs without knowing she was reading it: the man who signed himself *R. Callow, Conservation Services* had limped since the scaffolding fall of 2019, and every invoice he'd ever submitted had been countersigned by Wycliffe.

A flashlight beam swept the chamber, lazy, proprietary.

"After you," said Callow, and his voice was mild, conversational, the voice of a man discussing mortar mixes. "Mr. Reyes. We know you're here. You booked the crypt for your little vigil — did you think we wouldn't read our own diary?"

Lena's blood went cold and then, strangely, calm. *Our own diary.* The ledger disguised as a mortuary roll; the deaths recorded among the donors; the whole cathedral keeping accounts of its living as meticulously as its dead. They had never hidden the record. They had simply trusted that no one would read it closely enough.

Jonah stepped out of the shadow by the founder's tomb.

She saw him in the spill of the flashlight: narrow-shouldered, glasses fogged with the crypt's cold, his hands raised slightly as though in surrender or in blessing — with Jonah it had always been hard to tell the difference. He had dressed for it, she realized absurdly, in the good grey suit from their third meeting, the one he'd worn to look trustworthy. He looked trustworthy now. That was the tragedy of him.

"I came to pray," Jonah said. His voice cracked on the second word and steadied on the third. "For Mr. Halloway. For Ms. Okafor. For Mr. Brandt." The names of the dead — the names from the roll, the names the coroner had called accidents. He said them like a lesson he had finally memorized. "I thought someone ought to say them out loud down here. Since no one else has."

"Sentimental," said the second man, and Lena's stomach turned, because she knew that voice too — not from logs but from life: Prebendary Acheson, Wycliffe's gentle, silver-haired fixer of awkward meetings, the man who had once brought her tea when she worked late and asked, kindly, whether she ever found the archive *lonely*.

"It isn't sentiment," Jonah said. "It's arithmetic. Three whistle-blowers, three accidents, one prepared grave behind the founder's tomb, and fresh mortar laid last month. I'm an accountant, gentlemen. I don't believe in prophecy. I believe in ledgers. And yours balances."

The flashlight swung toward the cavity behind the tomb — toward the space they had opened and resealed, the grave built in advance, waiting with the patience of all things archival. And in that moment of redirected light, Lena made her choice.

She stepped out of the dark.

* * *

Later — in statements, in dreams, in the long retellings she would give Ivo over bad coffee — she would try to explain why she had done it. Not waited. Not filmed it from hiding, the safe archival thing, the witness-in-the-stack thing. But words were her trade, and she understood at last what Ivo had tried to tell her weeks ago, standing humiliated in her reading room: that a record no one hears is only paper. Testimony requires a voice. It requires a witness willing to stand in the light and be counted among the living.

So she stood between Jonah and the two men, and she spoke.

"My name is Lena Underhill," she said, and her voice rang against the vaulting the way a struck bell rings, clean and owned. "I am the archivist of this cathedral, and I am here under the terms of a private retainer to establish that no murder will occur in this place on Friday the fourteenth. I regret to inform the parties present that they are making my job very difficult."

Callow laughed — a short, startled bark. Acheson did not laugh. Acheson's flashlight found her face and held there, and she made herself meet it.

"Miss Underhill." His voice was warm as ever, and that warmth was the most frightening thing in the room. "This is a conservation matter. Chapter business. Whatever you imagine you've found—"

"A mortuary roll," she said. "Fourteenth century, ostensibly. A record of the dead, commissioned for the soul of Aldhelm's benefactors. Except the scribe recorded the living among them — coded, but coded carelessly, because who audits a prayer? Thomas Halloway, entered eleven days before he fell from the triforium. Grace Okafor, entered while she was still sending emails to the Charity Commission. Daniel Brandt, entered the week before his car left the road at Deadman's Bend. And beneath each name, in the same cipher, a sum. Donations. Restoration funds. Laundered through a decade of beautiful, necessary stonework." She drew a breath. "And on the final leaf, in ink barely a month old, a new name. Jonah Reyes."

"You can't prove—"

"I don't have to prove it," Lena said. "I only have to say it. That's what a witness is, Prebendary. Someone who says what she has read, out loud, in front of everyone, and cannot unsay it. Every word I've spoken tonight is already transcribed, timestamped, and in the hands of people who will read it whether I walk out of here or not."

It was mostly true. Ivo had the photographs; a solicitor named Ferro had the affidavit; the rest was nerve. But nerve, she was learning, is also a document. It bears witness.

Acheson moved then — not toward her, which she had braced for, but past her, toward Jonah, and she understood her error a half-second too late: she had spent her minutes on words, and words buy time only until the listener stops believing them.

"Take him," Acheson said quietly, to Callow. "The archivist can watch. She wants to witness. Let her witness."

Callow was stronger than his limp suggested. Jonah fought — she would remember that always, the accountant in his good grey suit twisting and grabbing at the tomb's edge, knocking loose a fragment of twelfth-century carving that would grieve her later — but the cavity behind the founder's tomb was low and close and made for receiving bodies, and Callow had him half over the lip of it, one arm pinning his chest, before Lena reached them.

There was a steel conservation trolley beside the crates. She did not decide to lift it. Thirty years of hauling folio boxes had put the strength in her arms without consulting her, and it came up and around and caught Callow across the shoulder and skull with a sound like a mis-shelved volume falling from a high stack.

He went down. Jonah went down with him, into the cavity, into the grave dug for him in advance — but alive, alive, scrambling on his elbows in the dust of centuries, and Lena dropped to her knees at the edge and gripped his wrists.

"I've got you," she said. "Look at me. I've got you."

His glasses were gone. His eyes behind the blur were the widest she had ever seen them, and full of a terror that broke her heart and fixed it simultaneously, the way a broken bone heals stronger at the fracture line. "Lena—"

"Don't talk. Climb."

Then Acheson's hand closed in her hair.

* * *

Pain whited the world. He wrenched her back from the cavity's edge, and Jonah's fingers slid from hers, and she heard him fall back against the tomb slab below with a sound that was not silence, thank God, not silence. She twisted, scratched, got her teeth into the wool of Acheson's sleeve like some feral thing out of her own medieval margins, and he struck her — open-handed, then not open-handed — across the ear and cheekbone.

The blow meant for Jonah, she understood dimly, was being given to her instead, with interest. Fine. She had taken worse from a collapsing shelf in '09, and that shelf hadn't mattered to her at all.

"The Dean," Acheson was saying, breathless now, above her, to someone at the stair — "Dean Wycliffe, sir, we're nearly—"

And she heard, descending the stairs, slow and heavy and unhurried as a funeral bell, a fourth set of footsteps. Footsteps she had heard every weekday for eleven years in the cloister. Footsteps that had once paused, kindly, to ask if the archive was lonely.

Dean Wycliffe came into the flashlight light with his hands folded, a man arriving to inspect work. He looked at Callow, groaning on the flags. He looked at the cavity, and at Jonah cringing in it. He looked at Lena, bleeding from the ear onto eight hundred years of stone, and his expression was sorrowful, gently reproachful, the expression of a shepherd disappointed in a lamb.

"Oh, Miss Underhill," he said. "You were the best of us."

"No," Lena said, and her voice came out broken and did not break. "I was the best of you. That's why you should have known better than to write me into your ledger."

Wycliffe sighed and nodded to Acheson — *finish it*, the nod said, finish it quickly, before the bells—

And the bells began.

Not Compline's end. Something wrong, urgent, all nine of the great bells at once, an alarm rolling down through the vaulting like judgment itself come home to roost — and with it, boots on the stairs, many boots, and a voice she knew better than her own heartbeat, hoarse with running and fury:

"POLICE! DS Marsh — nobody moves — UNDERHILL! Where are you?"

Torchlight exploded across the crypt, blue and white, and Ivo Marsh came down the last steps with his suspension letter still, absurdly, folded in his breast pocket, six officers flooding past him, and his eyes found her on the floor, bloody and upright, and something in his face came apart and reassembled into resolve.

Wycliffe turned toward the stair, toward the dark of the passage beyond, toward the one door in the cathedral that might still be open—

Lena, on her knees in the dust, reached out and caught the hem of his cassock.
