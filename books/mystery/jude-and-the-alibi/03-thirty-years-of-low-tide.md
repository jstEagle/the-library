## 3. Thirty Years of Low Tide

The letter stayed in the cash drawer all night, beneath the tray where the twenties and fives lived, and Xanthe dreamed about it anyway.

Not the words—the words were burned into them already, legible on the inside of their eyelids—but the paper itself. The way it had felt between finger and thumb. Cheap stock, slightly cockled, as if it had once gotten wet and dried badly. A letter that had been to sea, or near it. Xanthe woke at four with salt in their mouth and lay listening to the harbor bell counting out the fog until it was late enough to put the kettle on and pretend the day was ordinary.

It was not ordinary. It was the day after the day after the funeral, and the tide tables said low water would come at 11:14 a.m., which meant that by midmorning the flats below the lifeboat station would be bared again—mud and bladderwrack and old rope, the sea's ledger opened for anyone patient enough to read it. Thirty years ago Xanthe had learned to read those flats the hard way, kneeling in cold muck with a trowel while gulls screamed overhead, looking for a boy who was not there.

Danny Voss had never been there. That was the thing no one in Saltmere liked to say aloud: thirty years of searching, dredging, dowsing, praying, and the sea had never once given him back. Until now it hadn't even given up his bones. And yet yesterday they had buried him—or buried the idea of him, an empty coffin dressed in flowers, because his mother, before she died, had asked for a grave to visit and the town had decided kindness mattered more than accuracy.

Xanthe poured the tea and did not drink it. The shop smelled of dust and old glue and, faintly, of Marisol's clove cigarettes from the night before, and none of it was enough to keep the past where it belonged, which was underwater.

* * *

In 1994 the Founders' Regatta had been the biggest thing Saltmere did all summer, and everyone who was anyone was on the water or watching it.

Xanthe remembered it in fragments now, the way you remember any day that turned out to be the hinge of your whole life—not as a narrative but as a series of photographs left too long in the sun. The bunting strung along the seawall, red and white gone pinkish in the glare. The smell of frying onions and diesel. Hettie Marsh—young then, already formidable—in a yellow dress at the head table of the regatta dinner tent, laughing at something her father said. Sheriff Amos Marsh, big hands, bigger voice, a man who shook yours like he was testing your grip for weakness. Father Anselm Cole in his collar, sweating through the July heat, holding a paper cup of lemonade like it was communion wine he didn't trust.

And Danny Voss, fourteen years old, last seen at 4:40 in the afternoon by half the town, walking along the harbor arm with his fishing rod over his shoulder like a soldier's rifle, whistling something nobody could later agree on.

Xanthe had been twenty-six. Three years on the force, still green enough to be flattered when Ray Delacroix handed them tasks that mattered. *Verify the statements, Xan. Check the times. You're good with details.* Ray had been forty then, broad and easygoing, with a laugh that made witnesses relax and a habit of tapping his pen twice against his notebook before he wrote anything down—a tic Xanthe had forgotten for decades and now could not stop seeing, there behind their eyes, tap-tap, tap-tap.

The three statements. Xanthe had carried them home that first night in a folder that seemed heavier than paper had any right to be.

Bram Voss, nineteen, Danny's older brother: at the yacht club bar from four o'clock onward, buying a round for the crew of the *Meridian*, seen by a dozen people, none of whom were drunk enough to be wrong. He'd been angry at Danny that week—everyone knew it, the brothers had come to shouting outside the chandlery on Thursday—but anger wasn't evidence, and Bram's alibi was a wall built of friendly faces.

Hettie Marsh: hosting the regatta dinner, moving between forty-odd guests from five until midnight, a woman so visible she might as well have been lit from within. Her father vouched for her personally, though he hardly needed to; the whole town had watched her work the room.

Father Anselm Cole: hearing confessions in the church vestry from four-thirty to seven, the door closed, the red lamp burning. No witness inside, obviously—but the schedule was posted, two penitents had come forward to confirm their appointments, and priests, everyone agreed, did not lie.

Three people. Three alibis. Each one solid on its own, and each one, Xanthe had thought at the time, confirmed by their own careful checking of tide times, road distances, sight lines. They had walked the harbor arm with a stopwatch. They had called the yacht club steward. They had sat in the vestry doorway and noted how the light fell through the frosted glass.

They had signed all three verification forms in their own careful hand, and Ray Delacroix had countersigned beneath, tap-tap, and Sheriff Marsh had closed the file with a speech about how sometimes the sea takes what it wants and we must accept God's will, and the town had needed so badly to believe him that it did.

The case broke six months later, quietly, the way things break in small towns—no explosion, just a slow subsidence. A fisherman swore he'd seen a boat idling off the point that evening. A reporter from the county paper started asking why no proper search of the seabed had ever been done. And then the evidence locker was found open one Monday morning, and Danny's rod—the only physical item ever logged, recovered from the rocks below the harbor arm—was gone. Not stolen in any way that showed. Simply absent, its logbook line initialed by someone whose initials might have been anyone's, the ink smudged, the chain of custody snapped clean as a bone.

No one was ever charged. Xanthe was never accused of anything except, eventually, of caring too much—of being the officer who wouldn't let it go, who kept pulling threads in a sweater the town wanted to keep wearing. But reputations in Saltmere are tidal. They rise and fall on currents nobody controls, and Xanthe's went out one winter and never really came back. There had been a transfer offered, a quiet suggestion that maybe the city suited them better, and instead they had done the unforgivable thing: they had stayed. Stayed and shrunk, year by year, from detective to clerk, from the harbor arm to the bookshop counter, until the woman who'd once walked a crime scene at dawn with a stopwatch was selling secondhand Agatha Christies to tourists and calling it a life.

Thirty years. Long enough for the guilt to settle into the sediment, layered over with daily smallness, compressed, seemingly inert.

The letter had loosed it. Guilt, Xanthe was learning, behaves like something buoyant finally cut free from the bottom—it doesn't rise fast, doesn't breach dramatically. It comes up slow, wobbling, trailing weed, and it arrives at the surface looking smaller than it felt down there and somehow heavier all the same.

* * *

At eleven, Xanthe walked down to the flats.

They told themselves it was habit, nothing more—thirty years of low tides had built the walk into their body the way a scar builds itself into skin. Down Harbor Street past the chandlery, past the yacht club where the regatta flags still hung limp in the windless air, down the slipway onto the mud that sucked at their boots with a sound like slow applause.

The lifeboat station stood above the tideline on its concrete apron, whitewashed, shuttered, decommissioned since before Marisol was born. From here, thirty years ago, the searchers had worked: lines of volunteers in wellingtons, rakes and poles, the sheriff's megaphone voice telling them to check the wrack line, check the wrack line. Xanthe had knelt right about there, where a channel of leftover water gleamed like spilled mercury, and sifted mud until their fingers went numb, and found nothing but a child's plastic bucket, blue, unbroken, belonging to no one.

They stood now where they had knelt then, and let the memory do what memory does.

Because here was the thing the letter had dragged up, the thing Xanthe had spent three decades not quite thinking: the alibis had fit together. Not merely each standing alone—each standing *on* the others. Bram's dozen witnesses at the yacht club included two crew members of the *Meridian*, and the *Meridian*'s crew had also been at the regatta dinner, where Hettie Marsh had served them canapés, and the dinner's guest list had been compiled by the parish council, of which Anselm Cole was secretary, and Cole's confession schedule had been printed in the same bulletin that advertised the dinner. A closed loop. A wreath. At twenty-six, Xanthe had found this satisfying—everything corroborating everything, a case sewn shut with neat little stitches.

At fifty-six, standing in cold mud under a white sky, Xanthe found it horrifying.

Because real alibis don't interlock. Real memories are ragged; real witnesses contradict each other about whether it was four-forty or five-fifteen, whether the man wore a hat. Real life is loose thread everywhere. Only stories interlock perfectly—and someone, in the summer of 1994, had told a story so complete that a young officer with a stopwatch and a hunger to do right had helped stitch it shut without ever noticing they were sewing a shroud.

*I know what happened to Danny.* The letter's opening line. *I know because I helped make sure no one ever found out. So did you.*

So did you.

Xanthe crouched and pressed two fingers into the mud, an old reflex, testing the ground the way you'd test a pulse. Cold. Alive with worms and lugworm casts and the small busy machinery of the tideline. Somewhere out under that gray water—if the letter was true, if any of it was true—was a boy who had been fourteen for thirty years.

"Looking for something?"

The voice came from the slipway. Gus, in his oilskin coat, hands in pockets, weathered face unreadable as driftwood. The old ferryman moved like a man who had spent his life reading water and trusted nothing else.

"Tide's out," Xanthe said. "Habit."

Gus looked at the flats a long moment. "I crewed the search boats, first week," he said. "You remember. Every boat in the harbor, dragging grids. Old man Marsh directing from the point like he was conducting an orchestra." He paused. "Funny thing. He always knew where to tell us to look next. Like he had a map."

The words dropped into Xanthe's chest like a stone into a well—down and down, no bottom sound.

"You've been odd since the funeral," Gus went on, not unkindly. "Marisol says you're sleeping in the back room again. Whatever it is—" he shrugged, a gesture that took in the sea, the sky, the whole drowned weight of the town "—water gives things up eventually. Always has. Question is whether you'll be standing here when it does."

He turned and crunched back up the slipway, leaving Xanthe alone with the mercury-bright channel and the rising water beginning, patiently, to erase their bootprints.

* * *

That afternoon Xanthe unlocked the drawer, took out the letter, and read it a third time.

*I know what happened to Danny. I know because I helped make sure no one ever found out. So did you. Three names, detective. You built their alibis yourself—I watched you do it. Come find me before the anniversary gala, or I'll come find you. Bring the truth. It's the only thing I'll trade for.*

No signature. Postmarked the day after the funeral—which meant it had been written, addressed, and mailed either in the raw hours after the service or, more likely, long before, timed to land like a hook set in a fish's jaw.

Xanthe laid the page flat on the counter, beside the register, in the lamplight, and did what they had trained themselves never to do with evidence: they touched it, traced the ink with one fingertip, and let themselves feel the full vertiginous weight of the sentence underneath all the others.

*You built their alibis yourself.*

There had been a fourth name on those verification forms. Not as suspect—as certifier. Young Officer X. Eriksen, signature neat, conviction absolute, checking boxes for a town that wanted its tragedy tidy. If the alibis were woven, then Xanthe's own hands had held the shuttle. And whoever wrote this letter had known that—had counted on it—had mailed a confession not to the police, not to the papers, but to the one person in Saltmere whose guilt was load-bearing.

Outside, the light was going coppery on the harbor. Marisol's key rattled in the back door; Gus's laugh boomed somewhere near the history shelves. The shop filled with its ordinary noises, its ordinary warmth, its fragile ordinary family, and Xanthe folded the letter away and smiled at all of them and said nothing, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing overhead, that the next thing they had to do was go and look Bram Voss in the face—because of the three names on the page, his was the one whose grief had looked, at the funeral, almost exactly like relief.
