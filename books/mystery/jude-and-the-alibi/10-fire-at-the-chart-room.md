## 10. Fire at the Chart Room

Gus had never trusted a quiet harbor.

Forty-one years on the water taught him that silence was just the sea holding its breath, and the sea only held its breath before it did something cruel. So when he stepped out of Eriksen Books at half past eleven that night—out for his walk, the one Xanthe pretended not to know about and he pretended not to need—the stillness over the harbor sat wrong in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The fog was coming in off the sound in long gray ropes, unwinding down the streets, pooling in the gutters. The streetlamps wore halos of wet light. Somewhere a halyard clinked against an empty mast, that small idiot bell that never stops ringing no matter what the world is doing. Gus pulled his coat tighter and walked toward the water because that was where his feet went when his head got loud, which it had been ever since Xanthe started leaving the shop early with that look on their face—the look of a man carrying something breakable in both hands and pretending it was nothing.

The chart room stood at the landward end of the harbor master's pier, a low brick building with a green door and a slate roof gone soft with moss. It had been the yacht club's records office once, back when the Founders' Regatta meant something more than a tent and a raffle. Now it mostly held dust, old trophies nobody polished, and the paper history of this town: race logs, mooring ledgers, membership rolls going back to before the war. Gus knew the building the way you know a bad knee. He'd ferried half the signatures in those ledgers across the sound himself, back when the ferry ran and his hands were steady and his wife was alive and none of it hurt yet.

He smelled the smoke before he saw anything else.

It came to him the way smoke always comes—underneath everything, patient, wrong. Not woodsmoke from a hearth, not diesel off a boat engine. Something chemical underneath it, something sweet and oily, the smell of a thing being eaten on purpose. Gus stopped on the cobbles near the fish market and turned his face into the wind coming off the water, and there it was again, faint as a lie told quietly.

Then the fog above the chart room glowed.

Not orange yet. Just a dull amber bloom inside the mist, like a coal wrapped in wool. Gus stood very still for three seconds—he counted them, an old habit from emergencies, give yourself three seconds to be afraid and then spend the rest moving—and then he was running.

His knees were seventy-two years old and they let him know about it, every step a negotiation, but he ran anyway, down the ramp onto the pier, boards drumming under his boots, past the moored dinghies knocking their little wooden knocks against each other as if applauding or warning him, he couldn't tell which. The green door was hot. He could feel the heat coming off it from two feet away, a wall of it, and through the frosted pane of glass in its upper half the light inside had gone from amber to a living, breathing orange.

"Hello!" he shouted, hammering the door with the flat of his hand. "Anyone in there!"

Nothing. Then—a cough. A thin, wet, human cough, from somewhere deep inside.

Gus's heart did something complicated. The night watchman. Old Piet Vandersloot, who took the watch three nights a week for beer money and talked to the gulls like they were shareholders. Piet, who was eighty if he was a day and slept more than he watched.

The door was locked. Of course it was locked; Piet locked everything, it was the whole of his employment. Gus looked around wildly, saw the iron mooring bollard, and put his shoulder to the door twice without effect, and on the third try he didn't think about his shoulder at all, he thought about Piet coughing in the smoke, and the frame gave with a crack like ice going out in spring.

Heat rolled over him like a wave breaking. The front office was already burning—the old trophy case, the roll of honor with all those faded photographs of regatta winners squinting into the sun, flames walking up the wallpaper in bright quick fingers. The smoke hung at head height, black and greasy, and beneath it the room glowed like the inside of a kiln.

"Piet!" Gus dropped low, where the air was still breathable, and followed the coughing. "Piet, boy, make a noise."

A groan from the back, from the archive room, the room where the actual records lived—the race logs, the ledgers, thirty years of regatta paperwork including, though Gus only thought of this later, much later, standing in the cold with soot in his lungs, *the year Danny Voss went into the water.*

Gus crawled. There was no dignity in it and he didn't care. His palms found broken glass, hot floorboards, the leg of a desk. The archive room door was open and the fire hadn't fully taken it yet—it was eating the front first, working its way back like a tide coming in, and Gus thought absurdly, *tide, always tide, even fire keeps a harbor schedule*—and there was Piet, sprawled behind the filing cabinets where he'd evidently crawled himself before the smoke finished him, one hand outstretched toward the window that was painted shut in 1987.

Gus got him under the arms. Piet weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks in a canvas jacket, and his face was gray as the fog outside. "Gus," Piet whispered, which was remarkable, because Gus would have sworn the man didn't know his name. "Fire," Piet said, helpfully, and coughed, and Gus hauled him backward through the doorway, through the front room where the heat pressed on his face like a great hot palm trying to turn him away, and out the broken door into the blessed freezing night.

They made it maybe ten feet down the pier before Gus's legs quit and he lowered Piet onto the boards, both of them gasping, soot and spit and salt. Behind them the windows of the chart room lit up full orange, and the fire found its voice—that low roar, deeper than any engine, the sound of something enormous feeding.

"Sirens," Gus rasped. "Somebody's called—"

"I called." Piet coughed. "Called it in before I went down. Smoke in the vents. Went back for the logbooks." His eyes filled—not with smoke, Gus realized, but with shame. "Couldn't carry 'em. Too heavy. All them years of records."

"It's paper, Piet. Paper burns. You don't." Gus said it gruffly, but he put his hand on the old man's chest and left it there until the breathing steadied.

That was when he saw the figure.

Across the pier, at the shoreward end where the fog was thickest, someone stood watching the fire. Not running toward it, the way people do, drawn helpless to catastrophe. Not running away. Just—watching. Standing very still with their weight on one hip, the posture of someone waiting for a bus, and the firelight reached them in pulses, lighting them up and letting them go, lighting them up and letting them go.

And in the pulse of light, Gus saw the bag.

A tote bag, slung crosswise over the shoulder. Canvas, faded navy, with white lettering worn soft by washing. Gus knew that bag the way he knew his own hands. Marisol carried it every day at the shop, slung exactly that way, and the lettering said ERIKSEN BOOKS — EST. 1961 in the font Xanthe's mother had chosen forty years before Marisol was born. Xanthe kept a stack of them behind the register, gave them away with purchases over fifty dollars, and Marisol had claimed hers within a week of arriving, ragged runaway girl with sharp eyes, wearing the shop's name like armor.

The figure turned. Just slightly. Just enough.

Fog swallowed the rest. By the time Gus was on his feet shouting—"Hey! HEY!"—there was nothing there but the pier and the fog and the fire roaring behind him, and the sirens finally threading their way down through the town, red light smearing across the wet cobblestones.

* * *

They wouldn't let him back past the tape. Gus stood with the growing crowd of neighbors in their coats over pajamas, the whole town assembling itself the way towns do at fires, everyone talking at once, and Gus said nothing. He watched the hoses throw silver arcs into the orange throat of the building. He watched the roof of the chart room sag, hold, sag again, and finally go in with a rush of sparks that rose straight up into the fog like a slow-motion fireworks show, embers climbing and dying, climbing and dying.

Piet was in the ambulance with oxygen, waving weakly. Gus had given his statement to a young firefighter with a clipboard: heard smoke, smelled fire, broke door, pulled man out. He had not mentioned the figure. He had not mentioned the bag.

He told himself it was because he wasn't sure. Fog, firelight, an old man's eyes. Any tote bag looks like every other tote bag. Half the town owned one; Xanthe gave them away like communion.

He told himself that, and then he walked home the long way, past the fish market, past the dark windows of the chandlery, and the whole way the question walked beside him like a dog he couldn't shake off: *who else would be at the chart room at midnight, alone, calm, watching?*

Who else had reason?

Because here was the thing Gus had been chewing on for days now, the thing he'd said nothing about, because saying things is how you lose people: Xanthe had been asking questions. Quiet ones, clumsy ones, badly disguised ones. Xanthe, who hadn't asked a question bigger than *paperback or hardback* in fifteen years, had suddenly developed a hunger, and Gus had watched it happen with the particular dread of a man who has seen a retired dog catch a scent it should have been too old for. And wherever Xanthe's questions pointed—to Bram Voss drinking alone at the Anchor, to Councilwoman Marsh's too-generous smile, to Father Cole's careful silences—one person was always nearby, listening, watching, carrying that bag.

Marisol.

Who had come to the shop eighteen months ago with no last name she'd offer and no history she'd tell, whom Xanthe had hired on the spot because, Gus suspected, Xanthe recognized something. Who loved the shop with a ferocity that frightened people who weren't paying attention. Who had started, lately, to watch Xanthe right back—with worry, Gus had thought. With loyalty.

Or with something else.

"No," Gus said aloud, to the empty street, and a cat on a fence stared at him with total lack of sympathy. Because that was the other shape of it, the shape he liked even less: if it wasn't Marisol, then someone had been walking around this town wearing her bag, her name, the shop's name, doing whatever this was—burning records, sending letters, pulling threads—and that meant the shop wasn't the hunter's den anymore. It was bait. It was cover. Somebody was using the family as camouflage, or somebody was hunting the family, and either way the walls Gus had leaned on for fifteen years—the coffee at four, the crossword arguments, Odile forgiving late fees with theatrical sighs, Marisol humming while she shelved—all of it was suddenly a house of cards sitting on a fault line.

The fire crews were damping down when he finally came back around to the harbor road, and he stood a while watching steam rise off the blackened brick, ghost-smoke where real smoke had been. The chart room was gutted. Whatever paper hadn't burned had drowned in hose water, ink running like wounds. Thirty years of regatta records, dissolving into gray slurry between the cobblestones.

Thirty years. The number had teeth now. Everyone in town was saying it since the funeral—thirty years since the Voss boy vanished, thirty years since the case that ended the great detective's career, though nobody said that part to Xanthe's face. Thirty years of low tide, and now, the same month, a confession letter arrives (Xanthe thought nobody knew; Gus had seen the envelope, had seen Xanthe's face) and a fire eats the one building in town that held the paper trail.

Coincidence was a word sailors used before the weather turned.

Gus walked home to the room behind the shop—he'd been sleeping there more nights than not lately, though neither he nor Xanthe had ever said why out loud—and let himself in through the back door as quietly as he could. The shop breathed around him in the dark: paper and dust and the lemon polish Marisol used on the counter, the smell of safety, the smell of home, and he stood in it a long moment with his soot-blackened hands hanging at his sides like tools he no longer knew how to use.

On the hook by the door, where she always left it, hung Marisol's spare cardigan.

And on the shelf beside it, stacked neat as scripture, a row of the navy tote bags. Empty. Waiting. Identical.

Gus counted them twice. He'd have sworn there were eight yesterday. There were seven tonight.

He stood in the dark of the warm shop, smelling of smoke, and listened to the building settle, and somewhere upstairs Xanthe turned over in bed, and somewhere across town Marisol slept or didn't sleep, and out in the harbor the tide was turning, going out, going out, exposing things.

In the morning, he decided. In the morning he would say something. To Xanthe, or to Marisol, or to whoever deserved it least.

But when morning came, and the seven of them gathered in the shop among the smell of coffee and burnt brick drifting in from the harbor, it was Marisol who spoke first—set down the kettle, looked at Gus with those sharp unblinking eyes, and said:

"You were out last night. Your boots are covered in pier grit, and you smell like the chart room fire." A pause. The kettle ticked as it cooled. "So before you ask me where I was—I think we'd better all sit down."
