## 16. The Anniversary Gala

### Xanthe

The Founders' Hall had been built to outlast the town that paid for it, and for a hundred and fifty years it had done exactly that—granite shoulders, a slate roof green with lichen, windows tall enough to let the sea in as light. Tonight it wore its centennial best: strings of amber bulbs along the rafters, a quartet tuning in the corner where the coat check used to be, round tables dressed in white linen so crisp they looked like sailcloth waiting for wind.

Xanthe stood at the edge of it all in the only good jacket they owned, feeling like a splinter worked into old wood.

Thirty years ago tonight, Danny Voss had stepped off the *Meridian's* tender at the Founders' Regatta, waved to someone on the dock, and walked into the particular darkness of a town that would spend three decades pretending not to notice what was missing. The anniversary gala had always been held on this date. Xanthe had never understood how anyone could celebrate anything on it. Now, watching Hettie Marsh glide between tables in oyster silk, laughing at something Councilman Peale said, Xanthe understood perfectly: the celebration was the point. A hundred and fifty candles lit over one boy's unmarked grave.

"You look like you're about to arrest the punch bowl," Gus muttered at their elbow. He'd shaved for the occasion, which somehow made him look more disreputable, like a pirate doing community service. His knuckles were still scabbed from hauling the night watchman out of the chart room fire two weeks ago. "Marisol's by the door. Odile's got the flask. We're all here."

"That's what worries me," Xanthe said.

They had argued about this—Xanthe and Marisol, mostly, the night before, among the smoke-stained paperbacks and the smell of char that still lived in the shop's back wall. Marisol had wanted to come armed with everything: the notebook, the lifeboat station photographs, the corroded chain link wrapped in a handkerchief like a relic. Xanthe had said no. Not yet. Tonight was not for evidence. Tonight was for forcing a reaction. You didn't show your hand; you showed your face, and watched whose eyes went where.

"Everyone I need is in this room," Xanthe had told her. "Bram. Hettie. Anselm. And whoever wrote me that letter, because they'll want to see if their bait worked." They had touched the letter's crease through their breast pocket, where it rode against their heart like a second set of ribs. "I just have to say one word loud enough."

*Reopened.*

Now the quartet finished tuning. Mayor Peale took the small stage beside the portrait of the founding families—Marsh, Voss, Cole, Delacroix, painted in oils so dark they seemed to be standing in their own funerals—and began his remarks. Something about resilience. Something about the sea giving and the sea taking. Xanthe barely heard it. They were watching the room the way they hadn't watched a room in fifteen years, and hating how good it felt, the cold bright machinery of attention clicking back into gear.

Hettie Marsh sat at the head table beneath her own great-grandfather's portrait. She was magnificent, Xanthe granted her that. Seventy-one years old and straight-backed as a mast, silver hair pinned in a coil, hands folded on the tablecloth like she was presiding over communion. Her alibi for the night Danny vanished had been this same hall, this same date, thirty years ago: hosting the regatta dinner, witnessed by forty people who had no reason to lie and every reason to be certain.

Forty witnesses. Xanthe had verified them personally, at twenty-six years old, eager and trusting, signing whatever Ray Delacroix put in front of them.

Bram Voss sat three tables away, alone, in a suit that fit him the way grief fits everyone—badly, borrowed, too heavy at the shoulders. He hadn't wanted to come. Xanthe had gone to the house on Channel Street yesterday and told him he had to. "You buried your mother last month and your brother thirty years ago," Bram had said, hollow-eyed. "What do you want from me now?" And Xanthe had said, "Your presence. That's all. Just stand where I can see you." Bram had agreed the way drowning men agree to hold a rope.

Father Anselm Cole was near the back, as priests are, saying grace over canapés, his collar white as a gull's breast. He had not returned Xanthe's calls since the day he confessed—hearing a confession, rather—that the sin named to him thirty years ago was not killing but burying. He could not break the seal. But he had looked at Xanthe across the confessional grille with an anguish so naked it was nearly a confession itself, and he had said, "Come to the gala, Detective. Whatever you intend to do. Come, and let God watch too."

The mayor yielded the podium to applause. Then Hettie Marsh rose, because of course she did, to give the toast she gave every year. She lifted her glass toward the portraits.

"To the founders," she said, voice carrying like a bell across water. "To the families who built this town on granite and stubbornness. To those we've lost—" a pause, measured, practiced, her gaze sweeping the room and landing nowhere in particular, "—and to those who keep faith with them. May the tide bring home what belongs to us."

Applause. Glasses raised. Somewhere near the window, a woman dabbed her eye.

Xanthe moved.

It wasn't a dramatic walk. That was the trick of it—they'd learned this from Ray, of all people, back when Ray still taught instead of concealed. You didn't storm a room. You crossed it like weather, inevitable and unhurried, until everyone noticed you without knowing when they'd started noticing. Xanthe passed the head table. Passed Bram, whose fork stopped halfway to his mouth. Passed Father Cole, who closed his eyes as if in prayer or surrender.

At the podium, Xanthe did not take the microphone. They simply stood beside it, in plain clothes, in a bookshop jacket, and waited for the noise to die on its own. It took eleven seconds. Xanthe counted.

"Good evening," they said, and their voice carried anyway; fifteen years behind a counter teaches you projection. "For those who don't know me, I'm Xanthe Eriksen. I used to be a police officer in this town. I was one of the officers who investigated the disappearance of Daniel Voss, thirty years ago tonight."

The silence changed texture. It went from polite to taut, like rigging before a squall. At the head table, Hettie Marsh remained seated, glass still raised, smile still fixed—but Xanthe saw her thumb whiten against the stem.

"The case was never closed," Xanthe continued. "It was never solved. And as of this week, it has been formally reopened. There will be questions. There will be interviews. Some of you will be asked to account again for where you were on this night, thirty years ago." A beat. "Some of you already know who you are."

No one breathed. The quartet, bless their professional instincts, played nothing.

"I'll tell you why I'm saying this here, in front of everyone, instead of knocking on doors." Xanthe turned slowly, letting the whole hall feel included in the turn. "Because thirty years ago, this case was handled quietly. Quietly is how evidence goes missing. Quietly is how a preliminary autopsy request gets quashed. Quietly is how a boy walks off a dock and becomes a rumor, then a shame, then nothing at all. So no more quiet. If anyone in this room knows anything about Danny Voss—the letter writer who contacted me knows I'm speaking plainly now—I'm asking you to come forward while coming forward is still your own idea."

There it was. The sentence under the sentence. Xanthe watched faces the way a shore watches the waterline for the pull before the wave.

Hettie Marsh set down her glass. The click of crystal on linen was very small and very loud. Her composure held—mostly. But her smile had gone from hosted to fixed, a painting of a smile, and when she spoke her voice was warm honey poured over a blade. "Well," she said, rising, drawing every eye as naturally as gravity draws rain. "How wonderful. After thirty years, some diligence at last. The Founders' Committee stands ready to assist in any way, Detective. Anything the investigation needs." Her eyes found Xanthe's and held them, and there was something in them Xanthe had seen once before, in a suspect's kitchen, decades ago—not guilt exactly, but arithmetic. Calculation running fast and cold behind warmth. "Anything at all."

"Thank you, Councilwoman," Xanthe said. "You'll be hearing from me."

Across the room, Father Anselm Cole was already standing. He left his seat with his head bowed, threaded between tables without meeting anyone's eyes, and pushed through the brass doors into the night. Mid-speech, mid-toast, mid-everything. The doors swung shut behind him with a sound like a held breath released.

And Bram Voss—Bram sat very still, both hands flat on the tablecloth, staring at the portrait of his family above the head table as though the painted faces might finally tell him something true.

### Marisol

Marisol had never been inside the Founders' Hall before tonight, and she spent the first hour cataloguing exits the way other people catalogued appetizers. Two front doors, brass-handled. One service door by the kitchens, propped open for catering, breathing out garlic and steam. Windows along the seaward wall, tall as church windows, latched with iron hooks—she checked one casually on her way past with a plate of nothing. Latched, but the hooks were old and the paint around them cracked. In her notebook, in handwriting she kept small enough to hide under her thumb, she wrote: *4 windows, seaward. Old locks. Kitchen door open.*

She'd been keeping the notebook since the stranger bought the tide tables. Since she'd realized the stranger was Bram Voss wearing a tote bag stolen from behind the register, and that following him had led her to the lifeboat station and the smell of fresh-turned earth. Since she'd understood, with a clarity that still frightened her a little, that the bookshop was not just where she worked. It was hers. Theirs. The first thing she had ever been unwilling to lose.

So she watched. She watched Hettie Marsh work the room after her toast, moving with the smooth inevitability of a woman who had never once entered a space she didn't consider hers. She watched the councilwoman pause at the table where the Delacroix family's elderly cousins sat, and lean down, and speak briefly to a man in a wheelchair, and Marisol saw the man's face go gray. She watched Father Cole leave like a man fleeing a fire only he could smell. She watched Bram not eat.

And she watched Xanthe, up at the podium, being a detective again. It was strange, seeing it. Like watching a bird that had lived in the shop for years suddenly remember it had wings. Marisol felt proud and afraid in equal measure, the way you feel watching someone you love do something dangerous on purpose.

When Xanthe finally stepped down, the room exhaled and the quartet cautiously resumed, and the gala tried to become a party again, the way a body tries to stop bleeding. Marisol drifted to Xanthe's side with two glasses of cider, handing one over, murmuring, "Kitchen door's open, four seaward windows with bad latches, and Hettie just talked to the Delacroix cousins and scared the hell out of the old man."

"Good," Xanthe said absently, scanning the crowd. "Keep watching."

"Always."

It was maybe twenty minutes later, during the dessert course, that Marisol noticed the woman. Gray-haired, sensible coat despite the heat of the hall, standing near the coat check with the posture of someone waiting for a bus that isn't coming. She wasn't eating. She wasn't talking. She was watching Xanthe the way Marisol herself watched people—with purpose. When Xanthe moved toward the hallway by the restrooms, the woman moved too, unhurried, and passed close behind them.

Marisol lost sight of both of them for less than a minute. When she caught up, the woman was gone—out the service door, probably, swallowed by the kitchen steam—and Xanthe was standing very still by the coat check, one hand in their jacket pocket, holding something small.

"What is it?" Marisol asked.

Xanthe's face, in the amber light, looked older than it had an hour ago. They unfolded the thing carefully, a slip of heavy cream paper, hotel stationery maybe, and angled it so only Marisol could read.

Block capitals, pressed hard enough to emboss:

**STOP, OR THE SHOP BURNS NEXT.**

Marisol's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the granite, down into the cold harbor mud where a boy had waited thirty years to be found. Around them the gala went on—laughter, music, the clink of glasses, Hettie Marsh's laugh ringing out bright and unbothered from the head table.

"They were in this room," Marisol whispered. "Just now. They walked right up to you."

"Yes." Xanthe refolded the note with steady hands, but Marisol had been living with them long enough to see the tremor underneath, the fury banked so deep it burned blue. "Yes, they did."

"What do we do?"

Xanthe looked out across the hall—at Hettie, serene beneath her ancestors; at the empty chair where Father Cole had been; at Bram, still staring at the paintings; at Gus and Odile by the window, oblivious, guarding the flask between them like a sacrament. The found family, scattered through a room full of people who had spent thirty years building alibis.

"We finish our cider," Xanthe said quietly, "and we go home, and tonight nobody sleeps at the shop alone. Because that note?" They slipped it into their pocket, next to the confession letter, two documents pressed together like opposing pages of the same book. "That note means we've got them scared. And scared people with matches make mistakes."

Outside the tall seaward windows, far below the terrace, the winter tide was coming in over the dunes by the old lifeboat station—dark, patient, carrying something toward the light.

But none of them knew that yet.
