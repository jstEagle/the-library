## 17. Arson, Attempted

Gus had never been a man who slept well in other people's houses. Forty years on the ferry had trained his body to wake at the smallest wrongness—a shift in engine pitch, a change in the water's voice against the hull—and even now, retired and half-crippled and sleeping on a cot in the back room of Eriksen Books because he'd decided the shop needed a night watchman whether Xanthe agreed or not, that training held. He woke at 2:57 by the wind-up clock on the shelf of maritime histories, though he couldn't have said what woke him. The foghorn. A car. Nothing.

Then he could say.

Smoke has a sound before it has a smell. It is a hush, a softening, like snow falling on water—the sound of air being eaten. Gus lay still on the cot with his bad hip complaining and listened to the hush, and his heart went cold and quick the way it used to go when a passenger fell overboard at night, when every second was arithmetic you did with your whole body.

He was up without remembering standing. The cot was in the stockroom, behind the biography section, and through the gap under the door he could see it: not light, not yet, but a color where no color should be. An orange breathing under the door seam, faint as a coal wrapped in cloth.

The smell arrived then—old paper burning, which is a smell Gus knew from a lifetime ago, from a church fire in his boyhood, from the ferry's chart locker going up in '89. Paper burns sweet. It burns like something being apologized for.

"Marisol," he said, too loud for three in the morning, and then again, crossing the stockroom in four strides, hammering the flat of his hand on her door. "Marisol! Fire!"

* * *

Marisol had been dreaming about the harbor, which she almost never did anymore. In the dream she was small again, six or seven, and her mother was holding her hand on the pier at Puerto Vallarta, and the boats were all lit up like lanterns strung on black water, and everything was fine, everything was so fine that she should have known it was a dream, because nothing in her actual life had ever been that fine—

Gus's fist on the door tore the dream open.

She came out barefoot in Xanthe's old cardigan, which she'd been wearing to bed for a month now because it smelled like the shop, like dust and cedar and the pipe tobacco Xanthe swore they'd given up, and the first thing she understood—not smoke, not heat, but this—was that the cardigan smelled like the shop, and the shop was burning, and if the shop burned she would never smell it again as long as she lived.

"Gus." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Where?"

"Front. Near the front."

"The register?" Because the register was where Xanthe kept the letter now, framed behind the counter glass, the confession letter that had started everything, and some part of Marisol's mind—quick, clerkish, cataloguing—had already filed the arsonist's probable target list, and the letter was at the top of it.

"Doesn't matter where," Gus growled. "Out. Both of us. Out first, phone after."

But she was already moving past him into the dark of the store, because she knew the shop the way she'd once known the streets of a city four thousand miles away—in her feet, in her spine, blind. She could walk Eriksen Books with her eyes shut and she did it now, one hand trailing the spines, past Biography, past Local Interest, past the children's corner with its crooked little chair, and the smoke was thicker here, lying along the ceiling in a grey river, and near the front window the darkness had a pulse to it. Orange light stuttered up from somewhere below the front display table—the new arrivals table, the one Marisol dressed every Tuesday with her own hands, fanned covers like wings.

A bottle. She saw it then, or its aftermath: a green glass neck protruding from the wreckage of the display, flames crawling out of it in slow, greedy tongues, licking up into the table, into the books, into thirty years of a life stacked spine by spine.

She didn't think. Later she would understand that she hadn't thought at all, that thinking would have sent her out the back door like a sensible person, and instead she grabbed the ceramic pitcher off the windowsill—the one Odile had brought back from Portugal, ugly and enormous, meant for flowers—and filled it from the tap in the little kitchenette, and threw the whole thing at the base of the fire.

Steam screamed. The flames flinched, shrank, regrouped.

"MARISOL." Gus's hand closed on her shoulder from behind, iron-hard, and she realized he'd followed her, this seventy-year-old man with his ruined hip, and that he was holding the shop's fire extinguisher, the red one nobody had checked since the Clinton administration. He shoved it into her arms. "You aim at the base. Not the flames. The base. I'll get the rest."

"What rest?"

"The back door's got a hose bib outside," he said, already turning. "Go."

* * *

What happened next took eleven minutes, though neither of them would ever agree on how long it felt. Marisol emptied the extinguisher in a white roaring cloud that turned the front of the shop into a blizzard, coughing, eyes streaming, aiming at the base the way Gus said, at the drowned black heart of the fire where the bottle lay shattered among the ruins of the new arrivals. Gus came back through the rear door dragging fifty feet of garden hose, swearing steadily in the French of his Acadian grandmother, words Marisol didn't know but understood completely, and together they fought the fire the way you fight anything that means to take your family from you: badly, furiously, with whatever is at hand.

The extinguisher died with a wheeze. The hose held. And the fire, which had wanted very much to live, found itself standing in a soaked ruin of a bookshop with nothing dry left to eat, and it sulked, and it guttered, and it died.

When the volunteer fire truck finally arrived—six minutes after Marisol's call, sirens whooping down the empty coastal road—the firefighters found two figures sitting on the curb in the alley behind the shop, side by side, black-faced and dripping, sharing a blanket from the stockroom like two survivors of a shipwreck. Which, Marisol thought, was exactly what they were.

The shop stood. Barely. The front window was starred with heat cracks. The new arrivals table was a charred island in a lake of dirty water. The smell of wet ash hung over everything, over the whole street, over the whole town maybe, and the fire chief—a broad, kind woman named Ferro whom Marisol had sold a cookbook to in October—crouched in the wreckage and came out holding something delicately between two gloved fingers.

"You want to see this," Ferro said. "Both of you. Before I bag it."

It was a matchbook. Cardboard, warped by water, but legible. Marisol read it upside down in the truck's headlights and felt the cold come into her hands before her head caught up.

THE SALTBANK YACHT CLUB — FOUNDED 1931 — MEMBERS AND GUESTS ONLY

The Saltbank Yacht Club. Where the Founders' Regatta launched every August. Where Danny Voss had stepped off the face of the earth thirty years ago. Where, in nine days, the anniversary gala would fill the clubhouse with every founding family in the county, including Councilwoman Hettie Marsh, whose composure had cracked at the gala announcement like ice under a boot heel.

"They came back," Marisol said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "After the chart room. They came for the shop."

Ferro looked at her. "Chart room fire wasn't accidental either. We're saying that officially now. Accelerant both scenes." She sealed the matchbook into an evidence bag. "Somebody's working through a list."

* * *

Gus sat on the curb long after the trucks pulled away, long after Ferro told them twice to get checked out for smoke inhalation, and watched the dawn come up grey and salt-stained over the harbor. His hands were shaking. They hadn't shaken like this since the '89 fire, since he'd stood on the deck of the *Merrilee B* and watched forty years of charts go up, and he was too old and too tired to pretend otherwise. So he let them shake.

Beside him, Marisol hadn't moved in twenty minutes. She was staring at the shop's darkened front window, at the OPEN sign that still glowed faintly in the glass because nobody had thought to switch it off, absurd and stubborn, a little neon heart beating in a dead storefront.

"I know what you're going to say," she said finally.

"Do you."

"You're going to say we should tell Xanthe tonight. First thing. That this changes things, that whoever's doing this isn't playing anymore, that—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "That I shouldn't have run toward it. That I could've died in there."

"That last part's true," Gus said. "Rest of it, I don't know yet."

Marisol turned to look at him. In the grey light her face was streaked with soot and her eyes were red-rimmed and there was a burn blister rising on the heel of her palm where the extinguisher's nozzle had scalded her, and Gus thought: Xanthe found this kid half-starved in the doorway two winters ago and gave her a job and never asked a single question, and look at her now. Look at what the shop made.

"My mother burned our apartment down when I was nine," Marisol said. It came out flat, rehearsed, like something she'd carried a long way. "Not on purpose. She fell asleep with a cigarette. We lost everything. I remember standing in the street watching the window glow and thinking—that's it, that's the whole world ending, right there, that orange square." She looked back at the shop. "Tonight I ran *toward* it. Did you notice? I didn't even decide to. My feet just went."

"I noticed," Gus said.

"So here's what I figure." She pulled her knees up under the blanket, chin down, and her voice steadied the way a compass needle steadies, finding north. "Whoever set that fire thinks we're scared. Thinks we're just—the help. The clerk and the old ferryman. Furniture. They think if they scare the furniture enough, Xanthe will sit down and shut up and let Hettie Marsh buy her way clean before the gala." She lifted her chin. "They're wrong. You don't get to burn my family's house and have me be furniture."

Gus laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound, smoke-roughened. "Your family's house."

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean." He worked his bad hip around, wincing, and put his hand flat on the cold pavement between them, palm down, the way he used to press his palm to the deck of the ferry in heavy weather, feeling for what the sea intended. "Here's what I know about fires, girl. I've fought two and been chased by a third. A fire tells you everything about the person who set it. How much they hate you. How much they're afraid of you. That bottle through the front window at three in the morning—that's not confidence. That's panic wearing a mask. Somebody out there is scared enough of a bookshop to try to burn it down, and scared people make mistakes."

"Like leaving a matchbook."

"Like leaving a matchbook." He pushed himself upright, joints crackling like the embers inside, and stood looking at the ruined front of the shop with his fists on his hips, an old man silhouetted against his own smoke. "Saltbank Yacht Club. Members and guests only." He said it slowly, tasting it. "I ferried half those members out to their pretty boats for forty years. I know whose guest passes live in whose desk drawers. And I know one more thing, Marisol Voss—"

"I'm not a Voss."

"—that Xanthe is going to walk through that door in four hours, see what's left of the front room, and go quiet in that particular way, the dangerous way, the way the sea goes quiet before it turns." He spat, an old habit, precise as punctuation. "So we've got four hours. Four hours to decide what we tell them, and what we don't, and whether we stand in front of this shop like a couple of fools until they get here."

Marisol stood up beside him. The blister on her palm throbbed. Behind them, the OPEN sign hummed its small stubborn note in the broken window, and down the alley, where the dumpster stood against the brick, something else waited that neither of them had seen yet—something the fire crew had missed in the trampled dark of the scene.

A second matchbook, unburned, tucked neatly into the gap between two bricks at knee height. Placed, not dropped. Facing outward, so that anyone walking the alley would find it.

Inside the cover, written in ballpoint in a small, controlled, unhurried hand, were five words:

*Nine days. Then the shop.*

And beneath that, in the same hand, a single initial that made Marisol's blood stop moving when she found it an hour later, alone, while Gus dozed in the stockroom:

*H.*

She stood in the alley with the matchbook in her scorched hand and the dawn coming up hard and gold behind her, and she did not scream, and she did not cry, and she did not put it back. She photographed it with her phone—front, back, the bricks, the alley, the angle of the morning light—and then she zipped it into the inner pocket of Xanthe's old cardigan, over her heart, where she kept the notebook now, and walked back inside to wait for her family to wake up.
