## 10. Letters to No One

Jude had learned to read Mara the way sailors read weather. The set of her shoulders when she came down the stairs in the morning. How long she stood at the register before opening the till. Whether she hummed — she almost never hummed, but when she did, it meant something had loosened, some knot worked free overnight.

Lately there was no humming. There was the confession letter folded into a manila folder that lived now in the drawer beneath the register, and there was Mara's face at odd moments, going somewhere else entirely, like a woman checking a wound through her clothes. And there were the names. Jude heard them the way you hear a song stuck in someone else's head: Corliss. Foye. Halloran. Anselm. Saltglass. Spoken half-aloud over coffee, muttered at the shelf of local history while Mara re-alphabetized it for no reason.

It was Anselm's name that snagged.

Jude knew it from the shop itself — *Defrocked: The Fall of Father Anselm Quinn*, one of three copies of a true-crime paperback that had sat unsold on the endcap since before Jude arrived. A priest who'd been pulled out of the pulpit twenty years ago over what the book called "financial irregularities" and what everyone in town called "the money," full stop, because nobody wanted to say more about a man who still said Mass in his own front room every Sunday for whoever cared to come. He lived up past the shingle beach in a narrow house with a blue door, and he was on Mara's list of the Saltglass Circle, which meant he had been at the hotel the night Tomás Vane drowned, which meant —

Which meant he might know something about the hotel. About the nineties. About who had passed through its corridors with their heads down and their buckets in hand.

Jude told themselves it was research. That was the word they used, carefully, the way you step onto ice. Research. For the shop. Mara wanted to build the local history section; Jude was helping; it made sense to talk to people who remembered things. It even sounded true when Jude rehearsed it on the walk up from the pier, past the shuttered chandlery, along the sea wall where the wind came off the water flat and hard enough to make your eyes run.

The lie underneath it stayed underwater, where lies do their best work. Because Jude had seen the photograph. Had held it two nights ago in the back room of the bookshop, alone, while Mara was upstairs asleep: a faded snapshot of a woman in a gray uniform smock standing on the steps of a grand, rotting hotel, squinting into sun. On the back, in a careful, school-taught hand: *Mum, Saltglass Hotel, summer of '94.* And beside her, cropped at the shoulder, a tall man laughing, his face turned away.

Jude's mother had cleaned rooms at the Saltglass Hotel the year Tomás Vane went into the water.

Jude hadn't told Mara. That was the arithmetic Jude could not stop running: tell her, and become part of the case; become part of the case, and become a question instead of a person; become a question, and be sent back to the system that had chewed through four placements before spitting Jude out onto this pier. Mara had signed nothing yet. Nothing was permanent. Everything was permanent. Both things were true, and Jude carried them both like stones in each pocket, walking up the hill to the blue door.

* * *

Father Anselm opened the door before Jude finished knocking, as if he'd been standing on the other side listening to the sea and had simply decided the sea was done talking.

He was smaller than the photograph on the paperback suggested, and older — a stooped man in a cardigan the color of oatmeal, white stubble on his chin, eyes a pale washed-out blue that nonetheless fixed on Jude with an attention that felt less like looking and more like being weighed. Behind him the hallway smelled of candle wax and boiled tea and something faintly medicinal.

"You're not selling anything," he said. Not a question.

"No, Father."

"Then you're not from the council either. They knock twice and leave pamphlets." He stepped back. "You'll come in, because you've walked all the way up here in weather that would turn back a postman, and because you're young, and the young only walk that far when they're carrying something heavy."

The front room was small and fiercely tidy. A prie-dieu knelt in the corner beneath a paper crucifix. Books everywhere — not arranged so much as settled, like sediment. On the mantel, between two chipped saints, stood a framed photograph of six young people on a hotel lawn, arms slung around shoulders, laughing at something outside the frame. The Saltglass Circle, thirty years younger. Jude recognized the faces from Mara's mutterings: a boy who would become the magistrate, grinning like he already owned the courthouse. A broad-shouldered youth with Declan Foye's jaw. A girl with kind eyes — Halloran. A thin intense boy whose face Jude didn't know until the logic assembled itself: Elias Vane. And at the center, arm around the laughing tall man, a woman with dark hair and a smile that seemed to cost her something.

Vera Saltglass.

And Tomás. The tall man. The one cropped out of Mum's photograph.

"Tea?" Anselm asked, already pouring from a pot that had clearly been waiting. "I'm told I make it too strong. I'm told a great many things." He handed Jude a cup with a chip in the saucer and lowered himself into an armchair with the care of a man negotiating with his own knees. "Now. You've come about the hotel."

It wasn't a question either, and Jude's prepared speech — the shop, the local history section, the anniversary of the town's founding, anything — died somewhere behind the teeth.

"How did you—"

"Because everyone comes about the hotel eventually. They just take different roads to my door." He sipped his tea. "Some come as journalists. Some come as children of the people involved. Some come as detectives." His pale eyes didn't flicker. "One came as a friend, once, and that was the hardest visit of all. So. Which are you?"

"Research," Jude said, and hated how thin it sounded against the weight of that room. "For a bookshop. We're doing a section on local history. The Saltglass burned down in—" 

"Nineteen ninety-eight. Arson, never solved, though everyone has a theory and most of the theories are wrong." He set his cup down. "What's your name?"

"Jude."

"Just Jude?"

"For now."

Something moved across the old priest's face — not suspicion exactly. Recognition of a certain kind of answer, the kind given by people who have practiced having no surname. He studied Jude for a long moment, and then he did something unexpected: he laughed, softly, without much humor in it.

"I spent twenty years hearing confessions. You develop an ear for the pause before a lie. Yours is very good. Better than most adults'. But it's still a pause." He leaned forward. "You want to know about the night Tomás Vane drowned. Everyone does. So let me save us both the performance. Ask me what you actually came to ask."

Jude's hands were tight around the chipped cup. The tea was too strong and too hot and Jude drank it anyway, buying seconds.

"My mum worked there," Jude said finally. The words came out low, as if they might not carry past the table. "In ninety-four. She was a cleaner. I found a photograph. She's on the steps in her uniform, and there's a man next to her, cut off at the edge, and I think—" The breath caught. "I think it's him. Tomás. And I think she saw something that night, or knew something, because after that summer she changed. She stopped sleeping with the window open. She got jumpy around water. And she never, ever talked about that job, not once, not even drunk, and my mum talked when she was drunk, she talked plenty, but never about the Saltglass."

The room was very quiet. Outside, the wind worried the gutters.

Anselm had gone still in the way old men can go still — completely, deliberately, like a stone deciding to be a stone. When he spoke, his voice had dropped its dryness. What remained was gentle, and that gentleness was worse than any sharpness would have been.

"What was her name?"

Jude told him.

He closed his eyes. Just for a moment — a second, maybe two — but Jude watched the name land on him like weather, watched something behind his face rearrange itself around it. When he opened his eyes again, they were wet at the rims, and he did not trouble to hide it.

"She used to sing in the corridors," he said. "Early mornings. The guests complained, and Vera told them to leave her be, that the rooms got cleaner when she sang, and Vera was right." He looked at his hands. "She had a voice like a gull over water. You couldn't mistake it. Half the staff set their clocks by her."

"You knew her."

"I knew everyone at the Saltglass. It was my parish, such as it was. The rich and the staff and the drifters, all under one leaking roof." He rose, slowly, and went to the window, where the gray light made him look carved out of the same material as the sea wall below. "And yes. She was working the morning after. The morning they pulled him out."

Jude's heart was a fist. "What did she see?"

"That is precisely the question you must never ask anyone else." He turned. The gentleness was still there, but now it had edges. "Child, listen to me, because I am an old man who has buried most of the people in that photograph on my mantel, and I will bury the rest soon, and I have had thirty years to learn what silence costs and what it protects, and I am telling you: your mother saw enough to frighten her for the rest of her life, and she chose — wisely, bravely — to carry it alone rather than let it touch her child. She built her whole life around keeping that night away from you. Do you understand? Every silence was a wall she laid brick by brick. And you are standing here asking me to hand you the key."

"She's dead," Jude said. It came out harder than intended. "She died two years ago. Whatever wall she was building, nobody's behind it anymore. It's just me. And somebody sent a confession letter to my— to the woman I live with, and somebody else is dead already, the marina man, and the police are circling, and if my mum's name gets pulled into it by someone *else*, by a journalist or a detective or God knows who, I'd rather it came from me. I'd rather I knew first."

Anselm came back to his chair but did not sit. He stood over Jude, and for a moment the defrocked priest and the parish priest were the same man, and the room smelled more strongly of candle wax, of decades of small fires lit against the dark.

"You live with Mara Quintana," he said quietly.

Jude said nothing. There was nothing left to deny with.

"Of course you do. The bookshop on the pier. She took you in." He exhaled through his nose, something that wasn't quite a laugh. "That woman doubted the verdict in ninety-four. Twenty-two years old, a rookie, and she stood in my churchyard — not my church, I'd lost that by then, but she found me anyway — and she asked me point-blank whether I believed Tomás Vane drowned by accident, and I lied to her face, and she knew I lied, and she was young enough to be wounded by it. She's carried that too. We all carry pieces. That's what the Saltglass made: not friends. Custodians. We hold the pieces apart so they never make a whole."

"And if they did? If someone put them together?"

"Then someone would have to decide what the truth is worth." He sat down at last, heavily, and looked at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "Thirty years ago five people agreed to say the same thing at the same time, and they have said it so long they believe it, mostly, except in the hour before sleep, which is when the truth collects its rent. Your mother was not one of the five. She owed them nothing. But she saw the shape of the lie from the outside, from the servants' stairs, and she understood that the shape of a lie is the same whether you're inside it or out." He lifted his eyes to Jude. "So she kept her piece. And now you've inherited it, and you're asking me to help you spend it."

"Is that wrong?"

"It is the most dangerous thing you could do," Anselm said, "and the most understandable, and I will not pretend those are different categories." He reached across the table and covered Jude's hand with his own — dry, warm, trembling slightly. "But hear an old man's warning, and hear it as blessing and curse together. Some inheritances are better buried. Not because the truth is shameful — shame is the Circle's disease, not yours — but because of *who is still alive*. The people holding those pieces are not all old and sorry like me. Some of them are powerful, and frightened, and frightened powerful people have spent thirty years paying whatever the silence costs. If you start turning over bricks, they will not ask whether you're a child. They will ask what you know. And then they will ask how to make sure you never tell."

The wind slammed something loose against the side of the house. Jude didn't flinch, but barely.

"Was my mum threatened?"

Anselm was quiet a long time. The clock on the mantel ticked with a sound like a slow heart.

"She came to see me," he said at last, "in the autumn of ninety-four. A few months after. She sat where you're sitting and she asked me whether God required her to tell what she'd seen, and I —" his voice caught, steadied — "I told her that God required her to protect her child first. I told her some truths do more damage arriving than staying buried. I gave her absolution for a silence that may not have needed it. And she went home and never spoke of it again, and neither did I, and I have said that particular penance over to myself every week for thirty years wondering if I gave her peace or just company in the dark."

"What did she see?" Jude asked again, softer now. Not demanding. Asking.

Anselm withdrew his hand. He looked at the photograph on the mantel — at Vera, at Tomás, at the whole laughing circle of them — and Jude watched him arrive at a decision the way you watch a tide turn: slowly, and then all at once, and then irreversibly.

"I will tell you one thing," he said. "One, because you deserve one true thing from the people who failed your mother, and one is all I can give without breaking faith with the dying and the dead. Are you listening?"

Jude nodded.

"Your mother did not see the drowning. Nobody saw the drowning — that is the whole architecture of the lie, that no one saw. But late that night, coming down the service stairs with her bucket, she saw a light moving on the water. A torch. And she saw a figure on the rocks below the seawall, standing very still, watching the water. Not calling for help. Not running for help. Watching." His voice dropped to nearly nothing. "And the figure turned, and the torch swung, and for one second the light crossed her face in the dark stairwell, and she dropped behind the wall, and the light went away, and she never told me whose face it was. She only told me it was a face she knew. Someone from the hotel. Someone from *that photograph*."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts. Jude gripped the edge of the table.

"And you don't know who?"

"I know who she suspected," Anselm said. "And I know why she never said the name aloud, even to me, even to God in her own words. Because the name was too big. Because saying it would have put a cleaning woman's word against—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "No. That is the wall, and I will not hand you the key today. Today I will hand you something better and worse: the knowledge that your mother was brave, and that her silence was armor, not cowardice, and that she loved you enough to carry a fear alone for twenty years so it would never once cross your doorstep."

"But it crossed anyway," Jude whispered. "It's sitting in a drawer under the register."

"Yes," Anselm said. "Someone has started mailing the pieces. And that, child, is why you must go home and say nothing to anyone — not to your detective, not to your priest, not in your sleep. Because whoever is mailing the letters knows the story needs new mouths, and they will find yours." He stood, and the standing seemed to cost him something beyond his knees. "Go on, now. It'll be dark soon, and the path along the shingle is treacherous after rain."

Jude rose on legs that felt borrowed. At the door, hand on the latch, Jude turned.

"Father. If I figure out whose face it was — if I figure it out myself — will you tell me if I'm right?"

Anselm looked at Jude for a long, long moment. The light behind him had gone blue with the coming evening, and the photograph of the Circle seemed to watch from the mantel, six young people frozen on a lawn that no longer existed.

"If you figure it out yourself," he said, "then it was always yours to know. And I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

* * *

The walk back down was the longest of Jude's life, and Jude had walked away from four homes and one funeral home and one police station, so the competition was stiff.

The shingle crunched and shifted underfoot, giving back nothing solid, and the sea kept pace on the left like something walking alongside. Jude's mother's voice ran through the whole descent unbidden — not any particular words, just the sound of it, the gull-over-water sound Anselm had named, singing in corridors Jude would never stand in, in a hotel that was ash, in a summer before Jude existed.

*A face she knew.*

Jude turned it over the whole way down, the way you worry a loose tooth. Someone from the photograph. Someone from the hotel. Standing on the rocks watching a man drown and doing nothing — or standing there because they'd already done everything, which was a different thing entirely, and Jude's stomach clenched around the difference.

By the time the pier lights came into view, Jude had done the thing Jude was best at, the survival skill honed across four placements and two years of sleeping in places with locks that didn't work: Jude had closed the box and put it on the highest shelf.

But boxes don't stay shut. Jude knew that too.

Through the bookshop window, warm gold against the blue-black evening, Mara stood at the register with the manila folder open in front of her, one hand pressed flat against her mouth, reading something again — the letter, or her partner's old correspondence, or whatever fragment of the past she'd unearthed that day. She looked up as the bell over the door chimed, and her face did the thing it always did when Jude came home: the loosening, the relief she thought she hid.

"You're soaked," she said. "Where were you?"

"Walking," Jude said. "Thinking about the local history section."

It was a good pause before the lie. Almost no pause at all.

Mara smiled and believed it, or believed enough of it, and turned to make tea, and Jude hung up the wet coat with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and upstairs in the room that was almost theirs, almost permanent, Jude lay awake in the dark listening to the tide come in, carrying the one true thing like a coal cupped in bare palms.

A face she knew. From the photograph.

And tomorrow, Jude thought, staring at the ceiling while the sea breathed against the pilings below — tomorrow Jude would have to look at that photograph on the mantel again, at the six laughing faces, and try to see which one of them had stood on the rocks in the dark and watched.

Because Jude's mother had kept a box. And Jude had a key to a linen room, and a page torn from a ledger, and a memory of a snapshot with a man cropped out at the shoulder — and somewhere in all of it, in the things Mum had saved without knowing why, the face was waiting.

Jude just had to open the box alone.
