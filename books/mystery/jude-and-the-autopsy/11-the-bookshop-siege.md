## 11. The Bookshop Siege

Mara knew something was wrong before she reached the door.

She had opened the Salt & Page six mornings a week for eleven years, and the shop spoke to her the way a house speaks to its owner — in small voices. The creak of the third floorboard. The particular dark of the front windows at 6:40, when the pier lamps still burned and the sea beyond them was only a rumor. This morning the lamps were on inside.

She never left the lamp on. Jude teased her about it — *you'd unplug the till if you thought it dreamed* — and it was true. Electricity cost money the shop didn't have, and Mara Quintana did not leave lights burning in empty rooms. Not since the house on Corvus Street, twenty years ago, when she'd come home to a door standing open and learned what a stranger's hands could do to a life in forty minutes.

The lamp was on. And beneath the salt-and-paper smell of the shop there was something else, faint but unmistakable: the cold mineral breath of the sea, dragged in on someone's coat.

She stood on the threshold with her key still in the lock and let herself feel it — that old animal lift along the spine, the body remembering what the mind had spent a decade teaching it to forget. Then she went in.

The fiction section was first, and it told her everything. Books pulled from the shelves not by the spine but by the block, whole runs of them lifted out and set down again slightly wrong, the way a searcher works who doesn't care about order, only about what might be hidden behind or between. Someone had gone through the paperbacks shelf by shelf, squeezing each row forward to check for an envelope, a photograph, a folded page. They had worked fast and they had worked angry — a copy of *Rebecca* lay face-down on the floor with its spine cracked, and someone had stepped on it.

Mara crouched and touched the cover. Dry. Hours ago, then. Before the rain stopped.

"Jude," she said quietly, to no one, and the name made the fear real.

She moved through the shop like wading through wreckage. The poetry alcove: disturbed. Local interest: gutted, every volume on coastal history and maritime law pulled down and riffled. The locked drawer under the register — the one where she kept the day's takings and, lately, the two letters — hung open, its cheap lock sprung with something flat and patient, a knife blade maybe, worked until the wood gave. The cash tray sat untouched. Three hundred and twelve pounds, she knew without counting, because she'd counted last night. Whoever had done this hadn't wanted money.

They had wanted the letters.

And on the counter, squared neatly against the grain of the wood as if placed there by a careful hand rather than dropped by a fleeing one, lay the envelope.

Mara didn't touch it for a long moment. She looked at it the way she'd once looked at things through glass in evidence bags — the postmark ghosting up gray against the cream paper, and even from three feet away she could read it, because she had read it a dozen times in the last two weeks, had held it to the window light, had run her thumb over the ink as if the letters might confess their sender by texture alone.

The postmark said ST. BRENDAN'S HOSPICE.

Not the town sorting office. Not the pier box fifty yards from her own door. The hospice, up on the headland, where Bridget Halloran had worked for thirty years among the dying, praying for strangers.

Someone had taken the confession letter out of her drawer, carried it away, and brought it back. Or — the colder thought, arriving a half-second later — someone had kept a copy all along, and this envelope was a message: *I know what you have. I know where you keep it. I can reach it whenever I choose.*

Her sanctuary. Eleven years of secondhand spines and bad coffee and Jude's handwriting on the staff whiteboard — *TODAY'S SPECIAL: ANY TWO PAPERBACKS, ONE ACT OF KINDNESS* — and someone had walked through it in the night with their hands in everything.

Mara picked up the phone and called Reyes.

* * *

Detective Imani Reyes arrived in nineteen minutes, which meant she'd driven like a person who hadn't yet learned the departmental wisdom of arriving slowly at anything involving Mara Quintana. She came in with a scene kit and a look that was equal parts concern and professional hunger.

"You didn't touch anything?"

"The envelope. I had to."

"Of course you did." Reyes set down the kit and surveyed the room with the flat sweep of someone cataloguing damage. "You called me and not the local desk."

"I called the one detective in forty miles who'll tell me the truth." Mara wrapped the envelope in a fresh evidence bag Reyes handed her without being asked. "Look at the postmark."

Reyes read it. Her jaw tightened. "St. Brendan's. That's Halloran's—"

"That's Halloran's hospice, yes. Or it's the nearest franking machine to whoever wants us thinking of Bridget Halloran." Mara set the bag down. "Postmarks are costumes, Imani. Anyone can wear one."

"But someone did wear this one. Which means they wanted us looking at the hospice, or they're careless, and nobody in this case has been careless in thirty years." Reyes was already gloved, moving along the shelves, reading the search pattern the way Mara had taught her to read tide lines — what the water took, what it left. "They went through the local history twice. Everything else once. Whatever they're after, they think it connects to the drowning."

"It does connect. It's the whole case in a drawer." Mara heard her own voice go low and hard. "Except they didn't find it, because it wasn't here."

"Where is it?"

"Somewhere they'll never think to look." She wasn't going to say more, not even to Reyes. The letters — Elias's confession and the promise of a second death — had moved three days ago into a deposit box under a name that wasn't hers, in a bank in a town where nobody knew her face. Paranoia, Jude had called it, affectionately. Last night it had been prudence.

Reyes paused at the back door. The bolt was intact; the frame showed no bruising. "Front lock?"

"Picked or a key. No force anywhere. They were let in, or they waited for a key." Mara's stomach turned over slowly, like something shifting in deep water. "Imani. Who else has keys?"

"You. The girl. Anyone else?"

"No one. I never even gave Hester Bloom one, and Hester practically lives here." But even as she said it, the arithmetic was running itself, cold and automatic, the way it used to run in the old days. A key could be copied in ten minutes by anyone who borrowed it overnight. A key could be taken from a jacket. A key could be asked for sweetly by someone a teenager trusted.

Jude had been closing alone three nights a week.

"When did the girl last close?" Reyes asked, and Mara saw that the same arithmetic had reached her too.

"Tuesday. Two nights ago." Mara pulled her phone and dialed, and the four rings before Jude answered were the longest of her year.

"Mara?" Jude's voice, sleep-rough, annoyed, alive. "It's seven in the morning. I don't start shelving till ten."

"Where are you right now?"

A pause. "...In bed? Are you okay? You sound weird."

"I'm fine. Stay where you are. Don't come to the shop until I call you. I mean it, Jude. Don't walk down that pier alone today."

Another pause, longer, and when Jude spoke again the sleepiness was gone. "What happened?"

"Later. I promise. Stay put." She hung up and found Reyes watching her with an expression she recognized from mirrors — the look of a cop doing math they hate.

"If they had a key," Reyes said carefully, "they might have had it for a while. They might have come any night. Including a night your kid was here closing up."

"They weren't here when Jude closed. Jude would have noticed—" Mara stopped. Would they have? A drawer hanging open, books reshelved wrong? Jude alphabetized the true crime by case number and noticed when a single paperback was a centimeter out of line. Yes. Jude would have noticed. But that was Tuesday, and the search felt fresher than two days, and between Tuesday morning and now there was a gap of nearly forty-eight hours in which anyone with a copied key could have stood in this room in the dark, going through her life shelf by shelf.

Or — and the thought arrived with the weight of a hand on her shoulder — Jude had noticed something. Had seen a book out of place, a drawer ajar, and said nothing, because saying something meant explaining why they'd gone quiet these past weeks, why they flinched at the name Saltglass, why there was a box under their bed they wouldn't let Mara near.

*Everyone in this family keeps secrets now,* she thought, and hated how naturally the word *family* fit, and hated more that it fit precisely because everyone in it was afraid of losing the others.

"Scene team will want the counter and the drawer," Reyes said. "I'll walk it myself. Mara — whoever did this wanted you to find the envelope. Squared up on the counter like that? That's theater. That's a note."

"I know."

"So what's the note say?"

Mara looked at the evidence bag. Through the plastic, the hospice postmark seemed almost gentle, almost clerical — the stamp of a place where people went to be forgiven at the end.

"It says they think the letters convict them," she said. "Which means the letters are closer to the truth than even I knew. And it says they're willing to break into a bookshop to get at paper." She met Reyes's eyes. "Paper is the least of what I'm willing to protect."

* * *

By noon the scene team had come and gone, leaving chalk-dust fingerprints and a receipt for the evidence log, and Mara had made herself do the thing she least wanted to do: stand in the wrecked shop and put it back together, shelf by shelf, because the alternative was to stand in it and shake.

She was rehoming the local history — the gutted section, the twice-searched section — when she found it. Tucked into the gap where *Tides of the Bay: A Maritime History* should have been, wedged flat against the shelf back as if someone had hidden it in haste or desperation: a page torn from a notebook, folded once.

Her heart performed a slow, terrible maneuver. She unfolded it with fingers that refused to be steady.

It was Jude's handwriting. She knew it instantly — the tall ascenders, the way Jude crossed their t's like little flags. Grocery lists, the whiteboard, the margins of returned paperbacks. She'd know it anywhere.

*If they come while I'm here — don't fight. Phone under the till. Say I'm alone. Say I don't know anything.*

Below it, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*I won't let them use me to get to you.*

Mara stood very still in the middle of her ruined shop, holding her child's fear in both hands.

So Jude had known. Had felt it coming — or had imagined it, which was somehow worse — and had written instructions to themselves like a soldier taping a note inside a helmet, and had hidden the note where no searcher would think to look, inside the one section they cared about most. And had said nothing. Had smiled across the counter and argued about music and eaten her toast, carrying this.

The bell over the door rang.

Jude stood in the doorway, rain-wet, breathing hard, having clearly ignored the instruction to stay put with the speed and thoroughness Jude ignored all instructions. Their eyes went from Mara's face to the empty shelf gaps to the police tape still knotted around the register, and Mara watched the color leave their face in real time.

"You said stay away," Jude whispered.

"And you came anyway." Mara crossed the floor and did the thing she had never once done in public, in eleven years, in front of God and the fiction section: she pulled Jude into her arms and held on. Jude went rigid with surprise, then gripped her back hard enough to hurt.

"They searched the shop," Mara said into their hair. "Last night or the night before. With a key, or a pick. They went through everything, and they left the confession letter's envelope on my counter like a calling card, postmarked from the hospice." She felt Jude's breath catch. Felt the small tremor that ran through them, and understood — finally, completely — that this was not the first time Jude had been afraid in this room.

"Mara—"

"Don't." She pulled back and held Jude's face in her hands, the way she'd wanted to since the night the letters arrived and her hands shook and she'd pretended they hadn't. "Listen to me. I found your note."

The silence between them stretched thin as wire.

"I'm not angry," Mara said, and found, to her surprise, that it was true. "I'm terrified. There's a difference, and the difference is that terror makes people honest. So here's mine: someone got into our shop in the night, and I don't know how, and I don't know who, and the only thing keeping me sane is knowing you weren't here when they did it. Here's yours now. Whatever you've been carrying — the hotel, your mother, the box under your bed, all of it — it stops being a secret today. Not because I'm demanding it. Because secrets are how they got in. Every lie in this town for thirty years has been a door left unlocked, and I am done leaving doors unlocked in my own house."

Jude's eyes were wet and furious and young. "You'll send me away."

"I will bury anyone who tries," Mara said, "under the true crime section, spine-inward, where no one will ever shelve them correctly again."

A wet, broken laugh escaped Jude despite everything — and then, like a dam going, the words came: the photograph in their mother's things, the pay stub, the linen-room key, the ledger page with the scratched-out name. All of it, tumbling out in the gutted local history aisle, while the rain started again against the windows and the sea kept its own counsel beyond the glass.

When Jude finished, Mara was quiet for a long moment. Then she took the evidence bag containing the envelope off the counter and set it down between them like a chess piece.

"Hospice postmark," she said. "Bridget Halloran's hospice. And tonight we're going to open your mother's box together, every item, no matter what it costs us. Because whoever searched this shop thinks those letters point at them — and if my dead partner's confession and your mother's ledger page are pointing at the same person, Jude, then the scratched-out name isn't just a name anymore."

She looked toward the window, toward the gray water and the headland where the hospice stood above the tide line, its windows catching the last of the light.

"It's a target. And so are we."
