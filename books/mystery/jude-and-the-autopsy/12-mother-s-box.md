## 12. Mother's Box

The box had lived under Jude's bed for three years, through four couches and two towns and one long cold winter on the pier, and in all that time Jude had never once opened it.

It wasn't much to look at. A shoebox, actually — some brand that hadn't existed since before Jude was born, the cardboard gone soft at the corners, held shut with a rubber band that had dried to the texture of an old scar. Their mother's handwriting on the lid in ballpoint, faded almost to nothing: *JUDE — WHEN OLD ENOUGH.* Four words. She'd been twenty-six when she wrote them. She'd been thirty-one when she stopped being anything at all.

Jude sat on the floor of their room above the bookshop with the box between their knees and the rain coming down hard against the window, and told themselves, for maybe the hundredth time, that tonight was not the night.

But tonight was the night. It had been the night ever since Jude had stood in the true-crime aisle three days ago with a photograph of the Saltglass Hotel trembling in their hand — the same hotel from the picture tucked in their mother's wallet, the same crooked sign, the same dark windows looking out over the flats like a face trying not to watch something. The same hotel where their mother had worked. The same hotel where a man named Tomás Vane had drowned the year before Jude was born.

The same hotel that was now, somehow, at the center of whatever was happening to Mara — the letters, the shaking hands, the long silences at the register while she stared through the front glass at the weather like it was speaking to her.

Jude had hidden the photograph back in the stockroom. Had said nothing. Had smiled at Mara over the till and made tea and shelved returns and lied, every single day, with the fluency of someone who'd been lying about small things so long they'd forgotten how heavy even small lies got.

The rubber band came off with a sound like a small bone cracking.

* * *

Inside: not much. That was the first thing. Three years of dread, and the whole of their mother's life fit in a shoebox with room left over.

A birth certificate — Jude's, creased into quarters. A hospital bracelet, tiny, yellowed. A photograph of their mother on the beach, laughing at whoever held the camera, sunburned across the shoulders, younger than Jude was now. Jude put that one aside fast, face-down, because looking at it too long did something to their chest that they didn't have words for.

Under the photographs: paper. A social security card. A bus ticket, unused, dated eleven days after Jude was born — she'd meant to go somewhere, Jude thought, and hadn't. A Christmas card signed *Auntie B* with no envelope.

And then, near the bottom, wrapped in a page of newsprint gone brittle as moth wing:

A pay stub. *SALtglASS HOTEL & PIER — HOUSEKEEPING,* the letters half worn away, but legible enough. *R. OKONKWO. WEEK ENDING 14 SEPT 1994.* Their mother's name, printed there in some dead clerk's typewriter font, proof that she had stood in that building, carried linens down those stairs, existed inside the story Mara was chasing. Dated September 1994. The drowning had been in September 1994. Jude knew that now. They knew far too much now.

Beneath the stub, a key. Small, brass, unremarkable except that a strip of masking tape clung to its bow with a single word in their mother's hand: *LINEN.*

Jude turned it over twice, three times, feeling its weight, and thought: why would you keep this? Why would you keep a key to a room in a hotel you no longer worked at, carry it across years and towns, seal it in a box marked *when old enough* — unless the key wasn't for a room. Unless the key was for a door you might need to walk back through someday. Or prove you'd once walked through.

Their hands were sweating. They wiped them on their jeans.

At the very bottom of the box, folded once, then once more, was a single sheet of heavy hotel stationery, cream-colored, water-marked with a crest — a gull over waves, the Saltglass mark. It had been torn roughly along the left edge, ripped out of something bound. A ledger. Jude didn't know how they knew that; they just did, the way you know a page has been torn from a book by the ragged ghost of its stitching.

Columns ran across it in faded ink. Dates. Room numbers. Names.

The date at the top of the page read **17 SEPTEMBER 1994**.

Jude's mouth went dry. Below the date, the names — guests, a dozen of them, written in a confident looping hand. And partway down, one entry defaced: a name scratched out so violently the pen had torn the paper, gouged through to the desk beneath, a black storm of ink where a name used to be. Beside the scratched name, in the margin, in different ink — bluer, newer, their mother's handwriting, Jude knew it instantly, had known it their whole life from birthday cards and grocery lists —

— a single word: ***why?***

That was all. One word. One question their mother had asked on paper and never answered aloud, folded away with a key and a pay stub and a baby's hospital bracelet, waiting under her child's bed for thirteen years.

Jude sat very still in the lamplight and listened to the rain and understood, with terrible clarity, that they were holding the reason their mother had never slept properly, the reason she'd jumped at knocking, the reason she'd moved them twice in one year without explanation, the reason — maybe — she was dead. And that they could not carry it alone anymore.

Downstairs, light still showed under the shop door. Mara closed late these days. Mara stayed late most nights now, doing whatever it was she did with the letters and the files and the past.

Jude put the box back together — photographs, bracelet, stub, key, ledger page — and carried it downstairs like something explosive.

* * *

Mara was at the big table in the back, the one buried under stock that never sold, and she didn't look up when the floorboards announced Jude on the stairs. She was writing. She'd been writing a lot lately, filling notebooks in her cramped slanted hand, and there were photocopies spread around her — Jude caught a glimpse of a marina, a coroner's report, a list of five names — before Mara, without being asked, slid a folder over the top of them. Not hiding. Just... containing.

"Shouldn't you be asleep?" Mara said. "School tomorrow."

"It's Saturday."

"Huh." She kept writing. "Time's gotten slippery."

Jude stood there with the shoebox against their chest and found that the speech they'd rehearsed on the stairs — *I have to tell you something, don't be mad, don't send me away* — had evaporated entirely. What came out instead was smaller and worse:

"You're investigating the Saltglass Hotel."

Mara's pen stopped.

She looked up slowly, and Jude watched her do the thing she did — the detective thing, the thing Jude had seen a hundred times without understanding until recently — where her whole face went quiet and her eyes went to work. Reading. Weighing. Deciding what the person in front of her needed versus what the truth required.

"How do you know that name?" Mara asked. Carefully. Like handling wire.

Jude set the shoebox on the table. The sound it made was nothing — cardboard on wood — and it was the loudest thing Jude had ever heard.

"I need you to promise something first."

"No," Mara said. "I'm done making promises I can't keep. Ask me something else."

Jude's throat hurt. "Don't send me away."

Something moved behind Mara's eyes — something old and bruised — and she pushed back her chair and came around the table and did the last thing Jude expected, which was to sit down on the floor beside the table's leg, right there on the old rug, and pat the space next to her. Like: *if we're doing this, we're doing it at eye level.*

"Sit down, kid," she said. "Nobody's going anywhere. That's a promise I can keep."

So Jude sat down on the rug of a closed bookshop at nine o'clock on a rainy Saturday, opened a shoebox, and told the truth for the first time in weeks.

* * *

They started with the photograph — the one from the stockroom, retrieved now, laid beside the one from the wallet. Same hotel. Same sign. Then the pay stub, and Mara read their mother's name aloud — *Ruth Okonkwo* — softly, like a prayer for a stranger, and something in her expression shifted in a way Jude couldn't parse yet.

Then the key. Mara held it up to the lamp, turned it, read the tape. Her jaw tightened.

Then the ledger page.

She went very still. Jude had seen Mara angry, tired, amused, afraid — had seen her hands shake over two envelopes in the storm — but they had never seen her go *still* like this, stillness spreading through her like frost across a window. She smoothed the page flat with both palms, careful as if it were a wounded bird, and bent close to the date.

"Seventeenth of September," she murmured. "The night he drowned."

"I know," Jude said. "I looked it up. After I saw the photo in your — in the stuff you've been reading. I know I shouldn't have. I know you told me to stay out of it."

"I did."

"And I did anyway. Because it's *my*—" Jude's voice cracked and they hated it and pushed through anyway. "Because my mother was *there*, Mara. She cleaned that hotel. She was working the week that man died. And she tore this page out and she wrote *why?* next to somebody's name and she kept it her whole life and she never told anyone, and then she died, and I got the box, and I was too scared to open it because everything she ever left me felt like it would fall apart if I touched it, and now—" They stopped. Breathed. "Now it's falling apart anyway. Just slower."

Mara said nothing for a long moment. Rain ticked against the dark windows. Somewhere down the pier, a mooring line creaked against a cleat, rhythmic, patient, like the sea counting.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Mara finally asked. No anger in it. Worse — gentleness. "Three days ago. When you found the photograph."

"Because everyone who gets close to this case ends up dead or ruined," Jude said. "Because you took me in when nobody else wanted me, and I thought — if I'm connected to it, if my mum is connected to it, you'll look at me different. You'll think I brought it here. You'll think I'm a liability. People send back liabilities." They stared at the floorboards. "I've been sent back a lot."

The silence after that was long enough that Jude risked looking up — and found Mara watching them with an expression that wasn't pity, which was the only thing Jude couldn't have survived. It was recognition. Like Jude had said something Mara had once said to someone else, in another life, in another voice.

"Listen to me," Mara said. "You are not a liability. You are the only person in this entire case who's been honest with me tonight, and that includes every cop, magistrate, and priest in this county." She tapped the ledger page. "Your mother saw something. This page says she saw something. And she was so frightened of it that she hid it from everyone — including you — and it ate her alive. That's what secrets do. I've watched them do it my whole career." A pause. "I've got a few eating me right now."

"What do we do?"

"*We*," Mara repeated, and almost smiled. "First, we find out whose name that is."

Jude looked down at the scratched-out entry. Up close, under the lamp, the damage was total — ink driven through the fibers, the paper nearly perforated, the name annihilated with a fury that scared Jude more than any neat concealment would have. Whoever had scratched it out hadn't been hiding it. They'd been *killing* it.

Except.

Mara tilted the page toward the lamp again, angling the light low across the surface, and frowned. "Paper remembers," she said, half to herself. "Old trick. You press hard enough tearing through, you emboss what's underneath." She reached for her notebook, tore out a blank page, laid it over the scratched name, and took a pencil — a soft one, 4B, from the cup by the till — and began to shade, gently, in long diagonal strokes.

Graphite bloomed across the blank sheet. Most of it was noise — the texture of the gouged paper, gray static. But rising out of the static, stroke by stroke, like something surfacing through dark water:

A capital letter. Tall, narrow, with a peculiar forward lean.

Then a second letter. And a third.

Mara's shading hand slowed. Stopped.

Jude watched her face do something complicated — grief and disbelief arriving at the same intersection from opposite directions and colliding.

"Mara?"

She didn't answer. She was staring at the half-formed letters as though they were a face she knew. As though they were a face she'd trusted.

Because Jude, leaning closer, could see them too now, emerging letter by letter from the graphite fog — and the second letter had a crossbar struck through its middle in a way Jude had already seen once before, in a photocopied letter on this very table, in handwriting belonging to a man ten years dead.

The tall narrow capitals kept coming. Enough of the name to be unmistakable to anyone who'd spent thirty years loving it or mourning it or both.

Mara set down the pencil with a hand that was not quite steady, and said, very quietly, to the room, to the rain, to the dead:

"Oh, Elias. What did you *do?*"
