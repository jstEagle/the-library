## 18. Foye's Last Booking

The marina office smelled of diesel and old coffee, and of death only faintly — the police tape across the door had kept out the curious but not the smell of the sea itself, which had gotten into everything the way it got into everything on this coast.

Detective Imani Reyes stood at Declan Foye's desk with her sleeves rolled and her notebook open, and when Mara came in she didn't ask what she was doing there. That was the arrangement now: Reyes didn't ask, and Mara didn't pretend to be anything other than what she was — a retired detective haunting a case like a woman returning to a house she'd been evicted from.

"You're not supposed to be past the tape," Reyes said anyway, for the record.

"I'm donating books to the courthouse library," Mara said. "I got lost."

"Twice this week."

"I have no sense of direction." She set her bag down on the counter beside a rack of laminated tide charts and looked at the desk. "Tell me about his last booking."

Reyes turned the ledger around so Mara could see it. Foye had run his marina the old way, pen and paper first, computer second — a fat ledger with columns ruled in blue, each slip and each rental logged in a hand that had grown shakier over the years but never illegible. The last page was dated the night he died.

"Three rentals that evening," Reyes said. "A couple from inland took the skiff out at four, back by six. A charter captain topped off his fuel tank at seven-forty. And then—" she tapped the line, "—a visitor's mooring, logged at eight-fifteen. No name. Foye wrote *walk-in* in the name column and drew a little arrow to the fee box. Cash."

"Walk-ins pay cash," Mara said. "That's not unusual."

"No. But look at the next column. Remarks." Reyes angled the ledger so the lamplight caught the faded ink. Foye had written two words: *knows boats.*

Mara stared at them. It was nothing. It was everything. A man who ran a marina for forty years noting, without thinking, that this particular stranger handled himself well on water — that he tied off a cleat without being shown, that he stepped from dock to deck without a wobble. Foye hadn't known he was writing evidence. He'd just been doing what he'd always done: reading people by their hands.

"Whoever moored here that night knew boats," Mara said quietly. "And Foye died in the tidal flats, staged as a boating accident, with a watch set to the wrong hour — the same staging signature as 1994. The killer didn't learn boats for this. He grew up with them."

"Or she," Reyes said.

"Or she." But Mara was already shaking her head. "The Circle's women are accounted for that night — badly, but accounted for. And none of them would stage a drowning. They've spent thirty years pretending the first one never happened. You don't repeat the thing you're trying to bury. You repeat the thing you can't stop rehearsing."

Reyes closed the ledger gently, the way you close a book whose author has recently died. "There's something else. Uniforms found a card in Foye's wallet. Not a business card — a handwritten one. A name and a phone number, and on the back, in the same hand: *invoice paid in full, report follows.*"

"A name I'd know?"

"You already know it." Reyes held up her phone. A photograph of the card, enlarged. *Dr. Amara Osei, genealogical researcher.*

Mara felt the floor of the case shift under her feet, the way the pier shifted when a big swell came through — nothing visible moving, everything repositioned.

"He hired a genealogist," she said.

"Six weeks ago. I called her this morning. She talked to me for an hour once I told him Foye was dead — she liked him, said he was the rare client who wanted the truth even when it cost extra." Reyes scrolled to a photograph of a typed report, its header stamped DRAFT — NOT FOR DELIVERY. "Foye commissioned a full family history. He told Osei it was for his retirement — he wanted to write up the marina's story, the families who'd used it. But halfway through, he narrowed the scope. One family. One question."

"Which question?"

Reyes looked at her for a moment before answering, and Mara understood that the young detective had already made the leap herself and wanted to see if Mara would land in the same place.

"Whether Daniel Murch was Tomás Vane's son."

* * *

Osei's office was above a bakery in town, and the smell of warm bread rose through the floorboards while she laid out her working papers on a table scarred by thirty years of other people's revelations. She was younger than Mara expected, precise, kind-eyed, with the particular patience of someone whose profession was telling strangers who they really were.

"Mr. Foye came to me in the spring," she said. "He started broad — marina families, founding stock, that sort of thing. Then one afternoon he called and asked me a strange question. He asked whether I could establish paternity retroactively. From documents. Records. He said DNA wasn't possible — the father was long dead — but he wanted documentary proof, the kind that would hold up."

"Hold up where?"

"That's what I asked." Osei folded her hands. "He went quiet, and then he said: *hold up in front of a boy who deserves to know who his father was.* Those were almost his exact words. I remember because most clients want proof to take something — an inheritance, a claim, a name. Mr. Foye wanted proof to give something."

Mara sat very still. Outside, gulls were arguing over the bakery bins, their cries coming thin through the glass.

"What did you find?"

Osei turned her papers around. Birth registers. Baptismal records. A hospital admission card from 1989 under Vera Saltglass's married name, and beneath it, in a chain of documents assembled with a genealogist's quiet ruthlessness: the dates, the gaps, the nine months counted backward to a summer when a certain man had lived year-round in the flat above the Saltglass Hotel boathouse, when Vera's husband had been working a season in Portsmouth, when the hotel's own guest ledger showed one Tomás Vane registered for six consecutive weeks.

"Vera gave birth in March 1990," Osei said. "The child was adopted out quietly — private arrangement, the paperwork sealed but not unfindable. The adoptive parents' surname was Murch." She tapped the final document, a copy of the adoption order. "Daniel Murch. Born March 1990. Father listed as unknown. Mother listed, per the arrangement, as *a resident of the district.*"

"And Foye — how did he react when you told him?"

Osei hesitated. "He cried," she said simply. "He apologized for crying. Then he said, *thirty-five years, and nobody told the lad.* He said he'd known Tomás Vane since they were boys. He said—" she consulted her notes, though she plainly didn't need to, "—he said, *Tomás never knew either. Died before he could. That's the cruelest part, that the boy grew up thinking his father walked out on him, when his father never even knew he existed.*"

The room was very quiet. The bread smell had become unbearable, domestic and warm, absurd against the cold arithmetic on the table.

"Did Foye say what he intended to do with the report?"

"He said he was going to tell Daniel himself. Man to man. He said the boy — he still called him a boy, though he must be thirty-five — deserved to hear it from someone who'd loved Tomás, not from a lawyer or a records office." Osei's voice dropped. "He booked an appointment with me for the week after he died. To collect the final draft. When he didn't come, I assumed he'd changed his mind. Clients do." She looked up at Mara, and her kindness sharpened into something harder. "He didn't change his mind, did he. Someone made sure he couldn't tell."

Mara didn't answer. There was no answer that wasn't yes.

* * *

They walked the tidal flats at dusk, Reyes and Mara, because Reyes wanted to see it again and Mara understood why. Some scenes you had to revisit with your body, not your file. The flats lay exposed and gleaming under a sky going bruise-colored, channels of leftover seawater threading the mud like veins. Somewhere out there, three weeks ago, Declan Foye had knelt in the silt with a watch on his wrist set to an hour he never lived.

"The promise letter," Mara said. Her breath fogged. The equinox was behind them now; the light was leaving earlier every day, the year tilting toward dark. "Say it out loud with me. All of it. I want to hear how it sounds."

Reyes blew out a breath. "Someone mails a confession to a thirty-year-old drowning in the handwriting of a dead detective. Same day, same postmark window, a second letter arrives promising another death before the equinox. Everyone assumes both letters come from the same hand. The confession is genuine — Elias Vane's, confirmed. So the promise letter gets treated as Elias's too. Posthumous, unhinged, irrelevant to any living suspect." She kicked a shell fragment out of her path. "But it isn't his. Anselm said someone edited the material. The promise letter is a forgery riding shotgun on a real confession."

"Riding shotgun," Mara repeated. "That's exactly right. Whoever mailed it needed the confession to be believed — needed the Circle to feel the past opening under their feet. So he bound the two letters together, one true and one false, and let everyone's assumption do the work. And then he waited to see which of them would crack."

"Foye cracked first."

"Foye cracked first — or rather, Foye moved first. Because Foye wasn't afraid of the letters. Foye was *moved* by them." Mara stopped walking. The mud sucked faintly at her boots. "Think about it. He reads a confession saying Tomás Vane's drowning wasn't an accident, that the Circle covered it. What does a man do who loved Tomás, who's carried thirty years of unease about that verdict? He doesn't run. He starts digging. He hires a genealogist. He finds out Tomás left a son nobody told. And he decides — this is the part that kills me, Imani — he decides to *repair* things. To give Daniel Murch his father. The letter meant to terrify the Circle set one honest man on a road that ended in the tidal flats."

"Because Murch couldn't let him tell."

"Could he?" Mara turned to face her. "Put yourself inside it. You are Daniel Murch. Your whole life, your father is a blank, and the blanks fill themselves in the way blanks do — with abandonment, with shame. You grow up, you get work, and by some iron joke of fate you end up as clerk to Magistrate Corliss, taking minutes for the very men who stood on the shore the night your father drowned, listening to their alibis polished smooth as sea glass, year after year, meeting after meeting. You know. Maybe you've always half-known. And then the letters come, and you understand that the truth about your father exists, written down, in the world — and that these five people will spend whatever's left of their lives keeping it sealed. And then Declan Foye, decent old Declan, finds the last piece and comes toward you with open hands, meaning to give you the one thing you ever wanted."

Reyes was silent. Out on the flats, a heron stood in a channel, motionless, hunting.

"So he killed him," she said at last. "The one man trying to give him the truth."

"He killed him *and* staged it as a drowning," Mara said. "Do you see the shape of that? He didn't hide Foye. He put him in the water, put a watch on him set to the wrong hour — the same wrong hour, the signature from 1994 — because the staging wasn't concealment. It was *accusation*. He was showing anyone who could read it: this is what they did to my father. This is the lie, performed again, so you'll finally look at it. He's not destroying evidence. He's submitting it. Thirty years of a verdict nobody would reopen, and he's forcing the reopening with bodies."

"He wants it declared murder."

"He wants it declared murder *by us*," Mara said. "By the institution that closed the file. By me, maybe — the rookie who doubted and shut up. Every piece of this has been addressed to someone. The confession to me. The deaths to the Circle. The staging to the record." She hugged her coat tighter against the wind coming off the water. "It's legible now, Imani. That's what frightens me. A killer whose logic you can read is a killer who thinks he's nearly finished."

* * *

Back at the bookshop, past ten, Mara spread it all out on the counter between the till and the stack of unshelved paperbacks: photocopies of the ledger line, Osei's document chain, the timeline Bridget Halloran had given her, the two letters in their evidence sleeves. Jude sat cross-legged on the stepladder with a mug of tea going cold, watching her work the way Jude watched everything now — closely, guiltily, carrying some weight Mara hadn't yet been allowed to lift.

"It fits," Jude said finally. "All of it. Murch had access — he's Corliss's clerk, he'd know every alibi, every gap. He had the letters — Anselm mailed them, but Murch could've gotten to Elias's papers, or guided Anselm's hand. He had the motive." Jude's voice caught slightly on the word. "And he had the watch. He'd have grown up hearing the story somehow. Even distorted, even wrong — especially wrong. He built the whole thing out of a lie he was raised on."

"Yes." Mara didn't look up. She was staring at the ledger photocopy, at Foye's small sure notation. *Knows boats.* "There's one thing that doesn't fit yet."

"What?"

"How he knew about the letters at all. Anselm mailed them quietly. If Murch edited the promise letter in, he had to have seen the confession before it was posted — which means he was inside Elias's effects, or inside Anselm's confidence, or both. And there's a second thing." She finally lifted her head. "Bridget Halloran told me Tomás was alive when the others left. Which means someone else was there at the end. Elias saw him struggling. But Elias arrived late. Somebody was with Tomás before Elias ever came down to the water."

Jude had gone very pale. Mara noticed, filed it, felt the old ache of things her found family wouldn't yet tell her — and let it go, because the shop phone was ringing, and at ten at night the shop phone never rang with anything good.

She picked it up. A voice she didn't know, elderly, wavering, with the careful diction of someone reading from a prepared sentence.

"Miss Quintana? This is Fairhaven House, Mrs. Saltglass's care home. I'm sorry to ring so late. Mrs. Saltglass is having one of her clear evenings, and she keeps asking for you — and for the boy who visited her. She says—" the voice faltered, apologetic, "—she says her son came back to finish the letters, and that he's coming for the rest of them before the tide turns. She says you'll know what it means. Do you— should we be worried, Miss Quintana?"

Mara looked at the counter, at the timeline, at the last unexplained hour of a night thirty years gone.

"No," she said, and heard her own voice go flat and certain. "But don't let anyone else visit her tonight. No one at all. I'm on my way."
