## 4. Jude and the Confession

The Pemberton ballroom smelled like money trying to remember it was once new. Eighty candles on a cake the size of a coffin lid, a string quartet sawing through Vivaldi, and somewhere in that glittering crowd, Corwin Voss in his charcoal suit, holding a glass of champagne he never drank from. I'd watched him for an hour. He didn't sip. He just held it, like a prop, like a man who needed his hands occupied so they wouldn't do what they wanted to do.

Aldous Pemberton sat at the head table looking bewildered by his own party, which struck me as about right. The man had been managed for forty years — by Voss, by the trust, by the careful architecture of a fortune built partly on a dead woman nobody talked about anymore. Marion Pemberton. Drowned in her own garden pond in 1987. Ruled accidental. Ruled closed.

Ruled, as of three weeks ago, possibly murdered — if a certain unsigned confession was real.

I had it in my breast pocket. Or rather, I had *a* copy in my breast pocket, and this was the part of the evening that required me to be a good liar, because the copy in my pocket was worthless. A decoy. The real one was already uploaded, timestamped, and sitting with two attorneys and one very patient detective sergeant who had agreed to attend a birthday party off the clock.

All I had to do was read it aloud.

"Mr. Pemberton." Voss materialized at my elbow the way he always did, silent as a bill. "You're not family. May I ask how you got on the guest list?"

"I'm press," I said. "Sort of. True crime podcast. I'm doing a piece on Aldous's eightieth. Human interest."

"Is that so." His eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had spent decades watching other people's clocks. "And is the piece going well?"

"It's going to have a hell of an ending."

Something flickered behind his face — not fear exactly. Calculation. He'd already searched my studio; he'd already taken everything except the confession, which meant he believed the confession was the only thing left that could hurt him, which meant he would come for it tonight, here, where the statute on Marion Pemberton's death expired at midnight and took his liability with it.

He just needed to know where I kept it.

"You seem nervous, Mr. Voss," I said pleasantly.

"I never get nervous. It's bad for billing." He smiled with all the warmth of a signed retainer agreement and drifted back into the crowd.

I checked my phone. One text from the number I'd been given three weeks ago, from the woman who hired me: *Do it at 11:40. Trust me.*

Jude had been missing for six days. Her apartment stripped, her car abandoned at a park-and-ride, her journal — the one she'd left in my studio mail slot, the one full of entries about her uncle's death foretold — sitting on my desk like a dare. Everyone assumed she'd run. I'd assumed she'd run, right up until I realized that everything she'd done — the overpayment, the knowledge of my methods, the journal planted where I'd find it — had been choreography. She hadn't hired me to stop a murder.

She'd hired me to be a witness.

* * *

At 11:38, I climbed onto the small stage beside the quartet and tapped a glass. Two hundred faces turned toward me, including Aldous's, watery and expectant, and Voss's, which had gone very still.

"Ladies and gentlemen. Before we cut the cake, I've been asked to share a document. A historical one. From the estate archives." I unfolded the pages slowly, letting the room lean in. "It concerns Marion Pemberton."

The name landed in the ballroom like a dropped glass. I heard someone's breath catch near the dessert table.

"This is a confession," I said. "Unsigned. Undated. But detailed — dates, times, the location of a garden pond, the fact that a woman who couldn't swim was found face-down in it with no water in her lungs." I let that sit. "It names the person who arranged her death. And that person has spent the last thirty-seven years managing this family's money, and tonight, at midnight, the statute of limitations on Marion's murder expires forever."

Voss was already moving. That was the thing about men like him — they can't help themselves. Thirty-seven years of control, and I'd just lit it on fire in front of two hundred witnesses. He came up the steps onto the stage with his champagne glass still in hand and his voice pitched low and reasonable, a lawyer's voice, a voice built for closing doors.

"That document is a forgery," he announced to the room. "This man is a fraud. If everyone will simply remain calm—"

"Then you won't mind if I read the name," I said.

And I read it. *Corwin Voss.* Loud enough for the microphones. Loud enough for the phone in my pocket streaming live to a server in Virginia. Loud enough for the two off-duty officers by the coat check to stop pretending to eat canapés.

What happened next happened fast. Voss's hand shot out — not for the pages, but for me, gripping my lapel, and then the champagne flute was falling and there was a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life: glass shattering on parquet, and Voss saying, quietly, only to me, "Where is the original?"

"Everywhere," I said.

His other hand went into his jacket, and I want to tell you I was brave, but mostly I was lucky — the officers reached him before whatever was in that jacket came out. They took him down against the head table, the eighty candles guttering, Aldous Pemberton rising out of his chair saying *what is this, what is the meaning of this* in a voice cracking down the middle, and above it all the string quartet had stopped, and the silence was enormous.

That's when the lights in the service corridor behind the stage clicked on, and Jude walked out.

* * *

She looked terrible. Six days of something had carved hollows under her eyes, and her hair was pinned up wrong, and she was wearing a caterer's apron over her dress, which told me most of what I needed to know about where she'd been hiding. In plain sight. In his house. Serving champagne to the man she'd predicted would kill her uncle.

"You're supposed to be missing," I said.

"I was never missing. I was backstage." She watched the officers walk Voss past us, his composure finally cracked open, his mouth working around words like *slander* and *entrapment* and *call my firm*. "You read it beautifully, Bram. Better than I hoped."

"Hoped." I laughed, and it came out ragged. "Jude, I thought you were dead. I thought *I* was the target. I set myself up as bait in your house without knowing whose trap I was walking into."

"You knew enough. You always knew enough — that's why I picked you." She pulled the apron off and folded it, precise as a signature. "Listen to me. The confession is forged. You knew that too. I wrote it — every word, from Marion's actual autopsy file, which Voss buried in 1987. It's fake, Bram. It proves nothing legally."

"Then why—"

"Because it doesn't have to prove anything. It just has to make him move." She nodded toward the officers, toward Voss's ruined face. "He's spent thirty-seven years making sure nothing about Marion ever surfaced. Tonight, in front of two hundred guests and a live stream, he assaulted a man on stage while demanding the location of a document. The forgery flushed him. The recording catches him. And the detective sergeant has been sitting on the real autopsy discrepancy — no water in her lungs — since Tuesday. Midnight doesn't save him anymore. The clock ran out on his secret, not on the crime."

I stood there in the wreckage of the party, the cake uncut, the candles burning down, and understood the shape of it at last. She hadn't paid me too much because she was nervous. She'd paid me exactly what a witness costs.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why not go to the police yourself?"

"Because nobody believes a niece with a feeling. And nobody listens to a podcast about a crime that hasn't happened yet." She smiled, tired and fierce. "But everybody listens to a scandal. I needed the truth to have a witness with an audience. Happy birthday, Uncle Aldous."

Across the ballroom, Aldous Pemberton stood alone among his flowers and his family, asking anyone who would tell him what year Marion died, and why no one had ever said.

Jude touched my sleeve. "There's one more thing you should know," she said. "About who really wrote the first confession. The one Voss kept in the archive all these years. It wasn't forged at all."

She nodded toward the old man at the head table, staring at his own hands.

"And he's ready to talk."
