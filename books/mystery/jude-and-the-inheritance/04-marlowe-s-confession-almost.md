## 4. Marlowe's Confession, Almost

The brandy was a mistake. Marlowe Vane knew it the way a drowning man knows the water is cold — distantly, academically, without any intention of doing anything about it.

He had started at four o'clock, which was early even by his standards, because Dr. Amara had come that morning and used the word *weeks* in the flat, practiced voice doctors reserve for sentences they have said too many times to other people. Weeks. Not months. The word had sat in his chest all afternoon like a stone swallowed whole, and by four o'clock he had decided that if his body intended to betray him on a schedule, he would at least choose what he did with the hours remaining. He had chosen the good bottle — the one he'd been saving for a celebration that never came, that would never come now — and he had carried it to the library himself, refusing Odile's help with a wave of his hand that he intended to look imperious and knew looked unsteady.

The library at Vane House still smelled of leather and pipe smoke and, beneath both, of dust — the particular dust of rooms where no one reads anymore but no one has the heart to close up. Lark's books were still here. He could see her spines from where he sat: the poetry she'd loved and pretended not to, the field guides to birds she'd never had time to watch, the law texts from the one semester she'd spent at university before Cassian Roth had begun pruning her life down to something small enough to control. Her handwriting was inside some of them. He had checked, once, years ago, drunk in exactly this way, and had found her name inscribed on a flyleaf in that slanted, impatient script — *Lark Vane, property of nobody* — and had closed the book so fast he'd cracked the spine.

He poured another two fingers. His hand shook. It had been shaking for months, though he'd told Tamsin Eriksen it was only age, and he suspected the lawyer hadn't believed him. Good. He didn't want to be believed. Belief was for people who deserved it.

Seven o'clock. He had called the Eriksen woman's office that morning — before the doctor, before the word *weeks*, when he'd still been pretending this was a matter of patience rather than urgency — and left instructions: come tonight, seven sharp, the house, tell no one. He'd heard the pause on the line, the fractional hesitation of a professional being asked to do something irregular, and then the smooth acceptance: *Of course, Mr. Vane. Seven o'clock.*

That was why he'd hired her. That pause, and then the acceptance. Everyone else either refused outright or agreed too eagerly. Tamsin Eriksen weighed things first. Weighed them, and then took them anyway, which was the only kind of person who could be trusted with what he was about to do — or rather, with what he was about to almost do, because there were limits, there had to be limits, and the condition stood: she must never learn why. She could turn over every stone in the county except the one he was sitting on.

At ten past seven, he heard the car on the gravel. He knew the sound of every vehicle that came up Coldwater Hill — the milk truck's rattle, Odile's ancient hatchback, the occasional lost tourist turning around in the drive — and this one was new to him, a quiet engine, a careful driver. He imagined Tamsin Eriksen climbing out into the blue evening light, looking up at the house the way everyone looked at it: with that mixture of admiration and pity people reserved for beautiful things that were clearly dying. Let them. The house and he had an understanding.

Odile answered the door. He heard the low exchange in the hall, the lawyer's voice level and unhurried, and something in his chest tightened unpleasantly. He had summoned her here to give her a key and a deadline and as much of the truth as he could afford, and now that the moment had arrived he found he wanted to delay it, the way a man delays reading the last letter of someone he loves.

*Someone I love.* There it was. Seven years of calling her *my sister*, *the deceased*, *Lark* — clinical, legal, arm's-length — and the brandy had stripped all of it away in an afternoon. Someone I love. Whom I may have killed. Whom I definitely failed.

"Mr. Vane." Odile stood in the library doorway, and behind her, Tamsin Eriksen — dark coat, dark eyes, the kind of face built for not being remembered. "Shall I bring coffee?"

"Bring the bottle," Marlowe said, "and two glasses, and then leave us. Whatever you hear through this door, you haven't heard."

Odile's gaze flicked to the lawyer and back. Something passed over her face — fear, or its well-practiced cousin — and then she nodded once and withdrew. She was loyal, Odile. Loyalty like hers was a debt he could never repay, and he had stopped trying. Some debts you simply carry until the carrying is done.

Tamsin Eriksen entered the room the way water enters a crack: quietly, completely, taking in everything. He watched the eyes move — the books, the cold fireplace, the portrait above the mantel that he had never allowed anyone to remove, the one of Lark at nineteen with wind in her hair and the harbor behind her, painted the summer before everything. Most visitors pretended not to notice it. The lawyer looked at it directly, held it for three full seconds, and then turned to him with a face that gave away nothing except the fact that it was choosing to give away nothing.

"Mr. Vane. You wanted to see me."

"I wanted to see you drunk," he said, lifting the glass. "You'll forgive me if I don't extend the courtesy. I find that one of us should be sober, and it has never once been me."

* * *

They talked for an hour, or the shape of an hour — he lost track of the clock, the way he lost track of most things now, time going soft at the edges like wet paper. He talked about the estate, the trust structure, the tax exposure, all the dry machinery of death that lawyers pretend is the substance of dying. Tamsin Eriksen listened the way good listeners listen: not waiting for a turn, but actually listening, occasionally asking a question so precise it felt less like a question than a scalpel.

And somewhere in the middle of it — the third glass, maybe the fourth — the machinery slipped, and the truth began leaking through the seams.

"You want to know why triple fees," he said. It wasn't a question. "Everyone wants to know. Your firm knows. Your partners know. You've all got theories. Blackmail. Insanity. Guilt." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, like something breaking. "Guilt. Yes. Let's talk about guilt, Ms. Eriksen. Let's talk about what a man does with it when he can't drink enough of it away and can't confess it to anyone because the confession itself would be the cruelest thing he's ever done."

The lawyer didn't move. Didn't lean forward, didn't take notes. Just waited. God, the waiting. It was unbearable and it was the only thing keeping him upright.

"The night my sister died," he said, "it was storming. You know that. Everyone knows that — it's in the file, it's in the verdict, it's in the story the county tells itself. Storm on the water, boat gone missing, sister gone missing, brother inherits. Simple. Neat." He swirled the brandy and watched it climb the glass. "Do you know what else happened that night? Do you know what isn't in any file?"

He had meant to stop there. He had rehearsed stopping there, standing in front of the mirror that morning like an actor with a bad script: *say the line, then close the door.* But the brandy had loosened the hinge, and the door swung wider than he'd built it to go.

"She came to me first," he heard himself say. His voice had dropped, gone rough. "Before the water. Before any of it. She came to me at eleven o'clock at night, soaked to the bone from just walking in the rain because she couldn't stand to be inside that marriage one more hour, and she sat where you're sitting, and she said—" 

His throat closed. Seven years, and the words were still exactly where she'd left them, preserved in amber, in brandy, in guilt.

"She said, *Marlowe, if anything happens to me, promise me you won't look for very long.*"

The fire had burned down to embers. Somewhere in the walls the old house ticked and settled, the way it always did at night, like a body cooling. Tamsin Eriksen sat absolutely still, and in the stillness Marlowe saw something flicker across the lawyer's face — not shock, not pity, but recognition. The look of someone who understood that a sentence like that one was a door, and that doors, once opened, could not be politely re-closed.

"What did you promise her?" the lawyer asked. Quietly. Almost gently.

"That's not— " He set the glass down harder than he meant to. Brandy jumped the rim and spread across the mahogany like a dark stain, like blood, like all the metaphors he refused. "That's not the question you were hired to ask."

"No," Tamsin Eriksen agreed. "But I'm asking it anyway."

"And I'm paying you not to." He pushed himself up from the chair, and the room tilted, and he caught the mantel beneath Lark's portrait and hung on, breathing, furious — at the brandy, at the weakness, at the woman in the painting who had made him complicit in her own erasure and never told him how the trick was done. "You were hired to prove a murder didn't happen. Full stop. The why is mine. The why stays buried with me, and I will be buried soon enough, so you needn't wait long for the secret to die of natural causes."

"Mr. Vane—"

"Do you know what they'll say about me?" He turned on her, and he let the arrogance come up like armor, like a man putting on a coat of mail one plate at a time. "After I'm gone. They'll say Marlowe Vane, the last of the Vanes, drank himself to death in a rotting house on a hill, alone, mad, muttering about his dead sister. They'll say he probably did it, killed her for the money, and the guilt finally ate him. It's a tidy story. People love tidy stories. And I will let them have it, Ms. Eriksen, gladly, gratefully, because the untidy version would hurt someone I—" 

*Love,* said the brandy.

"—someone I owed," he finished, "more than anyone alive can calculate."

Silence. The embers ticked. Outside, the wind had come up off the water, moving through the cedars with that long, sighing sound the house had taught him to hear as a child — the sound of the place breathing.

Then the door opened.

* * *

Odile stood in the doorway with a tray she did not need to be carrying — coffee, untouched, a formality, an excuse — and her eyes went from Marlowe to the lawyer and back, and Marlowe saw in her face the particular terror of a woman who has spent seven years listening through doors and hearing exactly the wrong things.

"Mr. Vane." Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; the cups chattered faintly against their saucers. "Your medication. It's past nine."

"It's past nine," he echoed, and laughed again, softer this time. "Past nine, and past everything else. Thank you, Odile. Set it down."

She set it down. But she didn't leave immediately, and in that hesitation — two seconds, three — Marlowe watched her look at Tamsin Eriksen with an expression he had never quite seen on her before. Not suspicion. Something closer to assessment. A woman deciding, after seven years of silence, whether the person in the room might finally be safe to break it.

Then it was gone, shuttered away, and she withdrew, and the door clicked shut, and the three of them — the two living and the one in the portrait — were alone again.

Marlowe crossed to the desk. His legs were less reliable than they'd been an hour ago; he noticed this the way he noticed everything now, cataloguing his own decay with a scholar's detachment. From the locked drawer — the second lock, the one no one knew about, not even Odile — he took a key. Old brass, small, worn smooth. He had kept it in his pocket for seven years, carried it through hospitals and boardrooms and sleepless nights, a stone in his shoe he'd refused to remove because removing it would mean admitting he'd been walking on it at all.

"This opens the study at the end of the east hall," he said. "Lark's study. Sealed since she died. Odile cleans the hall outside it, nothing more. No one has been inside." A lie, half a lie — he had been inside twice, and both times had lasted less than a minute before the air of the place drove him out, the air of a room that still belonged to someone. "Whatever your investigation requires, start there. Paperwork, correspondence, her effects. If there's anything in this house that proves my sister wasn't murdered, it will be in that room. She was always the clever one. I was only ever the one who paid for things."

He held out the key. For a moment his hand wouldn't open — the fingers curled around the brass as if it were warm, as if it were hers — and then he forced them apart and let it go, and the small weight passed from his palm to the lawyer's, and something in his chest tore loose and did not settle back.

"One more term," he said. "Non-negotiable. Listen carefully, because I'll only say it once, and I'm told my heart keeps its own calendar now." He met Tamsin Eriksen's eyes, and he let the mask drop — just for a moment, just long enough — and showed what was underneath: not arrogance, not the imperious dying patriarch, but a tired man holding a secret the size of a coffin. "This case closes before I do. Whatever you find, whatever you prove, whatever you suspect — I want it finished while I'm alive to hear it. I have spent seven years not knowing, Ms. Eriksen, and I am done. Done. If you cannot promise me an answer before my heart gives out, take your fee and walk out of this house tonight and I will hire someone hungrier."

The wind pressed against the windows. In the portrait above the mantel, Lark at nineteen looked out over the harbor forever, wind in her hair, unaware of everything that came after — or perhaps, Marlowe thought suddenly, wildly, aware of all of it, painted with that half-smile because she had already known, even then, exactly how her story would have to end in order to begin.

Tamsin Eriksen closed their hand around the key.

"You'll have your answer," the lawyer said.

It was, Marlowe thought, the first time in seven years that anyone had promised him the truth instead of the file. He raised the last of the brandy to the portrait, to the storm, to the promise he'd made at eleven o'clock on a night of rain — *I won't look for very long, Lark. I swear it* — and wondered, as the lawyer's footsteps faded down the east hall toward the sealed door, whether breaking a promise to a ghost counted as betrayal, or as the only kind of faithfulness left.

Down the corridor, metal met brass. The lock, after seven years, turned on the first try.

As if it had been waiting.
