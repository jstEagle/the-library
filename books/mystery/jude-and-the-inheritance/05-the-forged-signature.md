## 5. The Forged Signature

The graphologist's office sat above a shuttered bookbindery on Pell Street, in a building that smelled of old glue and colder money. Tamsin had chosen him the way they chose everything — by elimination. Three names had come up in the professional directories; one had testified for the defense in a case Tamsin had once gutted on cross-examination, and one had a website with a photograph of himself in front of an American flag. The third was a man named Aurelio Senn, seventy-something, who answered his own door and kept his magnifying lamps cleaner than most surgeons kept their instruments.

"You're the one who closes things," Senn said, before Tamsin had fully crossed the threshold. "I read the trade journals. You've never lost a probate challenge."

"I've never taken one I couldn't close," Tamsin said, which was true, and which was also the sentence that had begun to taste like ash in their mouth over the past two weeks.

Senn's workspace was a single long table under north-facing windows, the light coming in flat and honest, the kind of light that flattered nothing. He worked under three lamps with articulated arms, and when he spread the documents across the blotter, he did it with cotton gloves and a pair of bone folders, handling each page the way a priest handles relics — not because they were sacred, but because they had been touched by the dead, and the dead deserved a certain gravity.

The documents themselves were modest. That was the thing that had bothered Tamsin from the beginning, the thing they had turned over on the train, in the bath, at three in the morning with the ceiling staring back: how modest they were. A letter of instruction regarding the disposition of Lark Vane's personal effects, signed eleven days before her disappearance. A codicil to the family trust, signed four days before. A bank mandate authorizing a transfer of signing authority, signed the same week. Three signatures, three dates, all in the final month of a woman's life — the ordinary administrative debris of a person putting her affairs in order before dying.

Except she hadn't put them in order. Tamsin knew that now. She had put them in order the way a stagehand sets a scene — deliberately, with someone else's hands.

"Tell me what you see," Tamsin said.

Senn didn't answer immediately. He adjusted the angle of the first document under the lamp — the codicil — and the light raked across the paper at a low slant, and for a moment Tamsin saw what the light saw: the surface of the ink, not the words it made. Words are for readers. Light at ten degrees is for liars.

"Sit," Senn said. "This will take some telling."

Tamsin sat. Outside, a delivery truck idled at the curb, and somewhere below, the bookbinder's old sign creaked on its brackets in a wind that had been blowing off the river all week.

"First, the good news," Senn said. "Whoever wrote these signatures could write. That's rarer than you'd think. Most forgers are caught by competence, not by cleverness — they hesitate, they patch, they draw instead of write. This person didn't draw. This person *wrote*. Look at the terminal stroke on the *k* in Vane." He turned the page toward Tamsin and touched it with a gloved fingertip, not quite touching the ink. "It kicks up and then falls away. Confident. A little theatrical, even. That's her hand. I've been given three authenticated samples of Lark Vane's writing from her correspondence — your courier was thorough — and this matches them. Line weight, slant, letter formation, the way she crosses her *t*s with a slight leftward drag. If you put this in front of a bank clerk, a notary, a court, they'd pass it without a second look."

"And if you put it in front of you?"

Senn smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man about to ruin someone's month.

"Put it in front of me," he said, "and I'll tell you that every signature on these three documents was traced."

The word landed in the room and stayed there.

"Traced," Tamsin repeated.

"Traced. Not copied from memory, not freehand imitated — *traced*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." He slid the codicil under the strongest lamp and picked up a loupe, holding it to his eye. "When a person writes naturally, the pen moves at varying speeds. Fast on the connecting strokes, slower at the starts and finishes. Speed leaves a signature of its own in the ink — pooling at the points where the pen hesitates, thinning where it flies. Natural writing is full of these little accidents. It's a heartbeat made visible." He straightened. "This has no heartbeat. The ink deposition is *uniform*. Every stroke, the same flow, the same pressure, the same speed, from the first letter to the last. No one writes that way. Not when they mean what they're writing. Not when the words are their own."

"Slow writing can look uniform," Tamsin said. "A careful signer, an elderly hand—"

"An elderly hand trembles. A careful signer slows at the *ends* of words, not through them. And look here—" He rotated the page again, and now Tamsin saw it, and once seen it could not be unseen: along the baseline of the signature, faint as breath on glass, a second line. A groove. "Indentation. She — or he, or whoever held the pen — was writing over something. A model. An original placed beneath this page, and the pen following it like a needle following a groove in a record. The pressure of the pen transferred the outline of the true signature onto the page below, and then our forger went over the track. Twice, in places. You can see the double-strike in the descender of the *L*. The pen went down the same path twice because the first pass wasn't dark enough, and when you're tracing, you know exactly where the path is, because it's *right there under your hand*."

Tamsin looked at the three documents laid out in their neat row and felt the particular vertigo that comes when a proof arrives too clean. This was the feeling they lived for — the anomaly resolving, the knot giving up its structure — and it had never, in fifteen years, felt like this. Like standing on ice and hearing it speak.

"Three documents," Tamsin said slowly. "All traced from models. Which means whoever forged them had the originals."

"Had access to them," Senn corrected. "There's a distinction, and it matters. To trace a signature, you need the model *under your hand*, in real time, in the same room. This wasn't done from a photograph or a memory. This was done sitting at a desk, with Lark Vane's actual letters and actual signed papers spread out, one at a time, under a working lamp." He began to stack the documents, squaring their edges with the bone folder. "And here's the second thing. The model signatures — the ones underneath — weren't all from the same period. The ink chemistry of the indentations varies. The letter of instruction was traced over a model roughly two years old. The bank mandate over one perhaps six months old. Whoever did this had a *file*. Years of her correspondence, organized, kept, available. Not a lawyer's file — a lawyer keeps documents, but a lawyer doesn't keep a woman's personal letters going back half a decade. This is a *domestic* archive. Someone in her daily life collected her. Kept her hand the way you'd keep a pressed flower."

The room was very quiet. The delivery truck had gone. Tamsin could hear the wind working at the sign below, and their own pulse, which had no business being audible and was.

"Could she have traced them herself?" Tamsin asked. "Deliberately. Slowly. For some purpose of her own."

"I considered it. It's the obvious alternative." Senn pulled a sheet of his own analysis across the table — pages of close-up photographs, magnified to the texture of moonscapes. "But no. Look at the start strokes. When a person traces their own signature, even slowly, even carefully, there's a micro-hesitation at the point of entry — the pen hovers, orients, commits. It's invisible to the eye but not to the ink. There's no hesitation here. The pen arrived already moving, already certain of the path, because the path was under it and someone else had chosen it." He looked up. "Whoever wrote these signatures was writing a stranger's name. fluently. Someone who had practiced her hand — or someone whose hand was so controlled that practice hardly mattered."

"Which is it?"

Senn took off his gloves, finger by finger, and Tamsin understood that the gloves coming off meant the professional portion of the afternoon was over and the human portion was beginning.

"I can't tell you whose hand it was," he said. "Handwriting identification across a forgery is a fool's errand and I don't do fools' errands; you didn't pay for one. But I can tell you what kind of person traces like this." He folded the gloves and set them down. "Someone patient. Someone who had done it before — there's a fluency in the pen control that doesn't come from a first attempt. Someone meticulous, unhurried, working in a place where they would not be interrupted for the hour or two each document required. And someone who had access to her papers over a long period, and to her *routines*, because—" he tapped the codicil — "this is dated four days before her disappearance, and it was witnessed by the housekeeper. Odile. You told me that. A forger doesn't produce a document to be witnessed by a stranger. They produce it to be witnessed by someone who will sign without reading closely, because they trust the room. Because the room is *theirs*."

Tamsin sat with that.

The house. The word arrived in their mind not as a deduction but as a physical sensation, cold at the back of the neck. Not a lawyer's office. Not a notary's back room. Not a bank. The documents had been born inside the house on Coldwater Hill — in the lamplight of some room where Lark Vane's letters lived in a drawer, where a desk lamp could burn for two quiet hours while a pen learned to be a dead woman's hand. Odile had witnessed the codicil. Odile, who kept fresh flowers in a dead woman's room. Odile, whose loyalty Tamsin had not yet been able to price.

And one more person had been in that house. One more person had the run of its desks and drawers, had grown up beside Lark Vane, had inherited everything she left behind — everything, Tamsin now understood, that had been *made* to be left behind.

Marlowe.

The name settled over the thought like a sheet over furniture.

"Mr. Senn," Tamsin said. "If I brought you a fourth sample — a known hand, someone living — could you tell me if it was the tracing hand? Not for court. Just for me."

Senn looked at them for a long moment over the lamps.

"For court, no. The literature won't support it and neither will I." He began packing the documents into their portfolio, careful, square, final. "But between us? Tracing leaves a rhythm. A forger's own hand leaks into the spaces between the letters — the connectors they have to invent, the joins the model doesn't supply. Bring me the fourth sample and I'll tell you whether the silences match." He held out the portfolio. "Whoever you're thinking of, Mr. — Ms. — Eriksen. Think carefully. People who trace signatures like this don't usually stop at signatures."

* * *

Tamsin walked. It was a long way from Pell Street to the office and they took all of it, because the train would have put them in a seat facing their own reflection and they were not ready for that conversation.

The city did what cities do — poured its noise over them, indifferent — and Tamsin moved through it turning the proof over and over like a stone with something alive under it. This was the moment that was supposed to feel like victory. Fifteen years of practice had been built on this exact feeling: the anomaly that resolves, the document that confesses, the small perfect machine of evidence clicking into alignment. Other lawyers got their satisfaction in courtrooms, in front of audiences. Tamsin had always gotten it here, alone, in the space between a question and its answer, and had guarded that privacy the way other people guarded reputations. Invisibility was not the absence of ambition. It was a kind of ambition — the ambition to be the last person anyone suspected of mattering.

And now the proof sat in the portfolio under their arm, and it did not feel like victory. It felt like a door they had opened onto a room where someone was still standing.

Because here was the shape of it, and the shape was terrible in its simplicity. The documents were forged inside the house. Forged with a domestic archive of Lark Vane's letters — years of them, kept, curated. Forged to be witnessed by the housekeeper, who signed without reading closely because she trusted the room. And the only will that emerged from that house — the secret will, the one probated in whispers by a notary who was dead within the month — had carried these signatures into legal reality. The forgery wasn't a detail of the case. It was the case's spine. Someone had built Lark Vane's death out of paper, patiently, in lamplight, and the paper had held for seven years, and it would have held forever if a dying man with too much money and too much guilt hadn't hired the one lawyer in the city who couldn't leave a neat file alone.

*Neat,* Tamsin thought, crossing the river bridge against the wind. *The file was neat. Rehearsed. I wrote that in my notes in the first week and didn't know why it frightened me.* Because neatness, in Tamsin's long experience, was never innocence. Innocence was messy. Innocence lost receipts and misdated letters and forgot which notary it used. Only guilt was neat. Guilt rehearsed.

And if the documents were forged in the house — then the question that had been *who killed Lark Vane* quietly changed its clothes. Because a forger needed her alive to build her death. You cannot trace the hand of a woman who isn't there to leave her hand behind. The letters in the drawer had accumulated over years, but the forger had worked in her final month, and the models were recent, and that meant — Tamsin stopped at the middle of the bridge, hands on the cold rail, wind shoving at their coat — that meant the last signatures in the archive had been gathered while she lived. The forger had been collecting her, right up to the end. Saving her hand for the work it would do after she was gone.

Or after she was *gone*. The distinction arrived uninvited and Tamsin stood with it, staring down at the grey water, letting it finish itself: there was a version of this story in which Lark Vane had never been murdered at all — in which she had walked out of that house alive, and someone in it had sat down at a desk that same week and begun the patient, loving, monstrous work of making her dead on paper. Building a corpse out of her own handwriting. And that version of the story had a different killer entirely, or perhaps no killer at all, only an architect — and the architect had inherited everything, and had spent seven years keeping fresh flowers in her room, and had hired Tamsin to un-prove a murder that would, if un-proven, point directly back through the forged ink to his own front door.

*Why,* Tamsin thought, *would a man pay triple fees to prove his own guilt?*

Unless the guilt wasn't what the file said it was. Unless there was a version of the story where Marlowe Vane had done the one unforgivable merciful thing, and had spent seven years dying of it, and wanted, before his heart gave out, to hear a professional say the words *she was never murdered* — not for the estate, not for the law, but because a man can only carry a grave that isn't real for so long before he needs someone to dig it up and show him it's empty.

Or unless it was exactly what it looked like, and the dying man was the forger, and Tamsin was the instrument he'd hired to dismantle his own crime before the law could use it against his memory.

Both stories fit the ink. That was the horror of it. The graphology didn't choose between them. It only said: *someone in that house did this, someone patient, someone practiced, someone she trusted enough to leave her letters within reach.*

The wind came up the river and Tamsin pulled their coat tighter and thought of the house on Coldwater Hill — the dust sheets, the clocks, the room with the flowers. *An absence that feels inhabited.* They had written that in their notes too, and it had seemed like atmosphere. Now it seemed like testimony. The house had been keeping her. The house had been kept *for* her. And somewhere in it was a desk drawer with years of a woman's handwriting filed like specimens, and a lamp with a low-slanting bulb, and the ghost of a pen moving twice along the same groove.

Tamsin's phone buzzed against their chest. A message from the courier service: the additional records Tamsin had ordered — the trust's internal correspondence, the notary's surviving appointment book, the household accounts — had been delivered to the office. All of it, at last, the paper sediment of the Vane household, layer by layer.

And one line in the courier's confirmation made them stop on the bridge with the wind in their ears:

*Delivered 4:12 p.m. — received at your office. Note: package was opened and resealed prior to final delivery; contents verified complete.*

Opened and resealed.

Tamsin stood very still. Somewhere behind them, in the city they had spent a career moving through unseen, someone had held Tamsin's documents in their hands and put them back in the envelope and sent them on, the way you'd return a borrowed book — politely, carefully, wanting you to read it, wanting you to know, perhaps, that you were being read.

The portfolio under their arm suddenly weighed what it was: proof that the dead woman's signature had no heartbeat, and the growing suspicion that Tamsin's own case had acquired one.

They turned toward the office, walking faster now, and the wind followed them the whole way, and behind the wind — faint, patient, in a hand that was almost hers — something was still writing.
