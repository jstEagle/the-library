## 19. The Choice

### Tamsin

The Vane estate looked different at dusk — or perhaps it was Tamsin who had changed since the first visit, and the house had simply been honest all along.

They came up the drive in Lark's borrowed car, headlights off for the last quarter mile, because Lark insisted. "Cassian watches the road," she'd said on the ferry over, her voice flat as slate. "He's watched it for seven years. He just never had anything worth stopping."

Now the house rose out of the fog the way it must have risen out of every one of Lark's nightmares: gabled and gray, its windows lit in only three places, like a face that had forgotten how to smile with its whole mouth. The ivy had climbed higher since October. The gutters sagged. Somewhere inside, a dying man was waiting to learn whether his sister was a ghost.

Tamsin parked behind the boathouse, where the gravel wouldn't show tire tracks, and sat for a moment with both hands on the wheel. In the leather satchel between them lay the second will — Lark's will, dated eleven days after her own murder, leaving everything to a name that had turned out to be a shell, a shield, a door out of a burning marriage. It was real. It was valid. And it was the only thing standing between Cassian Roth and everything he had spent seven years strangling into silence.

"You can still turn around," Lark said. She wasn't looking at Tamsin. She was looking at the house, at the third-floor window where a lamp burned behind curtains Odile kept washed and pressed for a woman everyone believed was under the lake. "I've done this before. Walked away from a building full of people I loved because staying meant they'd die too. I'm good at it. You don't have to be."

"I'm not walking away." Tamsin heard their own voice and barely recognized it — no lawyer's smoothness, no practiced calm. Just a person, tired and certain. "Marlowe paid me to un-prove a murder. That's what I'm here to do. Everything else is —"

"Everything else is why you're here," Lark said quietly. "You could have mailed him the truth. You didn't. Don't dress it up as duty, Eriksen. I spent seven years dressing things up as duty. It never once worked."

Tamsin almost smiled. Almost. Then the porch light went out — all of them, at once, the whole house dropping into darkness except that single lamp on the third floor — and there was no more time for almost anything.

"He's here," Lark breathed.

"No," Tamsin said, watching the dark windows. "He was already here. He's been here since before we left Greyhaven."

* * *

### Lark

Seven years of hiding taught you to read houses the way other people read faces, and Lark read this one like an open wound.

The front door stood unlocked. That was the first wrong thing. Odile locked it against the wind, against the mailman, against the memory of men in wet suits dragging the lake. Tonight it swung open at Lark's touch without a sound, oiled recently, and the hallway beyond smelled of beeswax and something else — something chemical and faintly sweet, like the air after lightning.

The second wrong thing was the grandfather clock. It had stopped at 9:14. Lark remembered it ticking through every childhood Christmas, every funeral, every night she'd crept down for bread and cheese and the illusion of being alone. Marlowe would sooner stop his own heart than let that clock run down.

"Cassian stopped it," she whispered. "He does that. Little proofs of ownership. He used to turn my photographs face-down when he wanted me to know he'd been in the room."

Tamsin's hand found hers in the dark. Lark hadn't asked for it. Hadn't wanted it. But she held on anyway, briefly, the way you hold a railing on a staircase you're falling down, and then she let go and led them toward the study, where the light under the door burned steady and yellow and wrong.

She heard her brother before she saw him. Not words — breath. The wet, laboring pull of lungs filling with fluid, the sound she'd listened to through their mother's hospital door at nineteen and sworn never to hear again. He was alive. He was alive, and he was asking for her without knowing she existed, and Lark stood outside the door of the study where they'd built forts out of law books and felt seven years collapse into nothing.

Through the gap in the door she saw him: Marlowe, vast once and now shrunken, propped in the wingback chair by the cold fireplace, an oxygen line running from his nose to a tank that hummed like a hive. His hands were enormous and translucent on the arms of the chair. His eyes were closed.

And standing at the window with his back to the room, silhouetted against the last gray light, was Cassian Roth.

He hadn't changed either. That was the obscenity of it. Seven years should have hollowed him the way they'd hollowed her; instead he stood straight and silver-templed and immaculate, adjusting his cufflink, a man who had aged like a portrait while everyone around him rotted.

"You're late," Cassian said, without turning. "Though I suppose punctuality was always my department."

Lark pushed the door open.

### Tamsin

What struck Tamsin, in the first frozen seconds, was how completely the room belonged to Cassian. Not physically — physically it was still Marlowe's study, still crowded with the drowned-library smell of old paper and pipe smoke. But Cassian stood in it the way water stands in a hull: having found the lowest point, occupying it entirely.

Marlowe's eyes opened. They found Tamsin first — professional habit, looking to the lawyer — and then they moved past, and Tamsin watched a dying man watch a ghost.

"Larkie," he said.

It was nothing. A syllable, a child's name, air shaped by ruined lungs. But Marlowe Vane's whole body broke around it — the great shoulders folding forward, the translucent hands rising as if to catch something falling from a great height — and Tamsin understood, with a violence that felt physical, why he had paid triple fees to un-prove a murder. Not for the estate. Not for absolution. For this. For one hour of being a boy again in a house where his sister was alive.

"Don't get up," Lark said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "Don't you dare get up."

"I wasn't going to." A ghost of his old arrogance flickered across his face. "Doctor says I'm not allowed to be dramatic. As if I ever needed permission."

"Hello, Lark." Cassian turned from the window at last, and Tamsin saw the gun.

It was small, black, unhurried — held low against his thigh the way a man holds a pen he isn't using yet. He didn't point it. He didn't need to. The room already knew.

"Tamsin Eriksen." Cassian nodded pleasantly, as though they were meeting at a bar association dinner. "You look well. Prison agrees with some people; I imagine unemployment will agree with you. May I see the will?"

"The will is with my co-counsel," Tamsin said. "In a place you'll never find."

"There is no co-counsel." Cassian's smile was gentle, almost fond. "There's a safe-deposit box in Bellhaven under the name Ruth Calloway, which is clever — I did enjoy that — and there is a woman named Odile Marchand, who has kept your sister's secrets beautifully for seven years and whose nephew has a gambling problem I've been subsidizing since Tuesday. So." He extended his free hand, palm up. "The will. Please. Before this evening becomes something none of us wants."

Marlowe made a sound — not a word, a refusal, a whole argument compressed into one wet rasp — and tried to rise. The oxygen tube pulled taut. Lark was at his side instantly, pressing him back down, her forehead nearly touching his, and Tamsin heard her say, rapid and low, the litany of two siblings' private language: *the clock, the boat, the blue coat, I know, I know, I know.*

Tamsin stepped between the chair and the gun.

It was not bravery. Tamsin examined the feeling honestly, the way they examined everything, and found it was simpler and worse than bravery: it was Chapter Thirteen. It was the client destroyed by a file closed too neatly. It was every case since, closed fast and clean, invisibility maintained, questions left unasked. Duty had gotten them here — duty, and the fee, and the reputation. And duty now said: give him the paper. Preserve the record. Let the machine do what machines do.

But Lark's hand was on her brother's chest, counting his heartbeat like a woman counting coins she doesn't have enough of, and Tamsin heard themselves say:

"No."

Cassian sighed. "Ms. Eriksen."

"You want the will because the will ends you. Lark alive means the trust dissolves, the marriage is void ab initio, and every document you notarized as her husband becomes evidence. You're not here to protect an estate. You're here to bury a witness." Tamsin's voice stayed level, but their heart was slamming so hard they could feel it in their teeth. "So here's my counteroffer. There is no will in this room. There never was. What there is —" they drew out their phone, screen glowing, recording light pulsing red — "is a confession. Yours. Every word since we walked in. The subsidized nephew. The stopped clock. The gun."

Something moved behind Cassian's eyes — the first genuine expression Tamsin had ever seen on him, and it was fear, quickly murdered.

"You'd burn your own career to save a dead woman's paperwork."

"She's not dead." Tamsin glanced back at the chair, at the two heads bent together, silver and dark. "That was rather the entire point."

* * *

### Lark

Lark heard the phone, heard the recording, and thought: *he'll kill them.* Not might. Will. She knew the arithmetic of Cassian Roth better than anyone breathing — knew that he killed the way other men filed taxes, quarterly, without passion, because the alternative was disorder. Two witnesses. One gun. A house full of dark.

But then Marlowe's hand closed around hers, and his grip — God, where did a dying man find that grip — and he said, clearly enough to reach every corner of the room:

"Cassian. Sit down."

Cassian didn't move. "Marlowe—"

"You heard me." Her brother lifted his head, and for a moment the ruin fell away and Lark saw the man who had rowed her across the lake at midnight, who had forged a death certificate with shaking hands because she asked him to, who had spent seven years drinking himself toward the grave out of grief for a lie he himself had built. "You are in my house, holding a weapon, threatening my sister and my lawyer, and I am going to tell you exactly how the rest of your evening goes. Sit. Down."

Cassian sat. Perched, really, on the edge of the leather ottoman, gun still loose in his hand, and Lark realized with a lurch that he was stalling — that somewhere below them, or above them, or outside in the fog, there was a second person, and Cassian was keeping them all in this room until —

The lamp on the desk flickered.

Then the lights died altogether, all of them, even the humming oxygen concentrator, and the house went silent in the way houses go silent when someone cuts the power, and in that silence Lark heard footsteps on the stairs. Unhurried. Familiar.

Odile's voice came out of the dark, steady as a metronome: "Monsieur Roth. Your car is blocking the gate. And the police are four minutes behind me."

"You're bluffing," Cassian said softly.

"I have been paid anonymously for seven years to keep a dead woman's room ready," Odile answered, and Lark heard the smile in it, fierce and terrible. "Do you truly believe I don't know how to make a telephone call?"

A phone screen bloomed white in the darkness — Cassian's, checking, calculating — and in that sliver of light Lark moved. Seven years of moving quietly through other people's houses, and she crossed her brother's study without a sound, and her hand closed over the gun's barrel and pushed it toward the floor, and Cassian, startled, actually let go.

Tamsin's flashlight beam snapped on, pinning him — kneeling half-risen, empty-handed, forty-seven years old and suddenly very ordinary.

"Chapter Twenty," Tamsin said quietly, to no one, to everyone. "The truth, even the ugly parts."

Headlights swept the drive below. Sirens, still distant, threading through the fog like wire through cloth.

Marlowe laughed. It cost him everything — Lark felt the laugh tear through his chest under her hand, felt the wet rattle of it — but he laughed, long and helpless, a boy again at the bottom of some magnificent disaster.

"Larkie," he gasped. "Tell me you brought the will. Tell me I get to sign something. One last time. It's the only part of the job I ever loved."

Lark looked at Tamsin across the ruined room — at the invisible lawyer who had chosen, tonight, to be seen — and Tamsin reached into the satchel and set the second will on the desk beside the stopped clock.

"It's dated eleven days after your death," Tamsin said. "Legally, it supersedes everything. But there's a form for that now. A new instrument, properly witnessed, revoking all prior wills." They set down a pen. Their hand, Lark noticed, was finally, perfectly steady. "All you have to do is tell the truth. Even the ugly parts."

Marlowe took the pen. Outside, the sirens turned onto Coldwater Hill.

"Then somebody fetch me a witness," he said, "before my heart remembers it's broken."
