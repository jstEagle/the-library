## 3. Inventory of a Borrowed Life

The cottage smelled of lavender and cold ash, the way houses smell when someone has died in winter and the fire simply stopped being fed. Ivo stood in the doorway of Rose Cottage for a long moment before crossing the threshold, key still in hand, as if the door might object to being opened by a stranger. By law, Ivo was not a stranger. By every other measure, they were.

Executor's duties began with an inventory. Ivo had drafted such clauses a thousand times—*an inventory and valuation of the chattels of the deceased to be prepared within six months*—and had never once imagined standing on the receiving end of the language, standing inside someone's life while it was sorted into columns.

They set up at the kitchen table, which was scrubbed pine worn pale at the edges, and opened a fresh legal pad. The pen hovered. Then, because there was no other way to do this, Ivo began.

*One cottage, two bedrooms, contents as follows.*

* * *

The sitting room held forty years of a life folded small. A sofa with a knitted throw, its colors faded into agreement with one another. A wireless on the mantel, dial worn smooth. Bookshelves: mostly novels with library sale stickers, a complete set of Trollope, and—this stopped Ivo's pen mid-stroke—a shelf of hymnals. Not one or two, but eleven, different editions, different decades, some water-swollen, some nearly new.

Ivo opened the nearest one. In the margin beside *Abide With Me*, in a small, upright hand, someone had written: *He never liked this verse. I sang it anyway.*

Beside another hymn: *Third Sunday after the fire. Harold did not look at me once.*

Ivo sat down slowly on the dead woman's sofa and read marginalia for an hour, forgetting entirely to catalog. The notes were not devotional. They were dated. They were addressed—to no one visible, to no one named, but composed with the care of letters that expected eventually to be read. *You would have laughed at Mr. Pell's hat today.* *The apples came in well this year; I left a bag on the churchyard wall like always.* *Forgive me the thing I cannot write here.*

*Forgive me the thing I cannot write here.* That phrase appeared four times across three different hymnals, years apart.

Ivo wrote in the inventory column: *Hymnals (11), annotated.* And beneath it, in smaller handwriting, as though the legal pad might be subpoenaed: *She was writing to somebody.*

* * *

The photographs were in a biscuit tin on top of the wardrobe in the back bedroom, wrapped in tissue paper as if they were the good china. Ivo unwrapped the first one standing, then had to sit on the edge of the bed, which was neatly made, hospital-cornered, unused-looking.

A child on a beach, maybe four years old, squinting into sun, holding up a crab with both hands like an offering. The child wore a striped shirt Ivo knew—not remembered, *knew*, the way you know a face in a dream—with a missing button replaced by thread of the wrong color. Ivo had seen that shirt in their own sealed adoption file, photograph number three of seven, the only artifact from Before. The file said the shirt was all the birth mother left.

Except she hadn't left it. She'd kept a copy of the child. Here. In a tin. For forty-odd years.

There were more. The same child older, gap-toothed, on a church step. The child perhaps nine, scowling beautifully at a piano recital program. No photographs past ten. Whatever window Edith Marsh had been permitted to look through onto this child's life, it had closed early—and she had kept the curtains drawn but never taken them down.

Ivo's hands were not quite steady when they wrote: *Photographs (14), tin, biscuit, blue.*

Then, because grief for a stranger is an absurdity and must be managed like any other irregularity, Ivo made tea in the dead woman's kitchen and drank it from the dead woman's cup and did not cry, mostly.

* * *

The ledger was in the bottom drawer of the dresser under a false folder labeled ELECTRICITY. Leather-bound, spine cracked white at the hinges, brass corners gone green. It looked official—the kind of volume a parish keeps for minutes, for registers, for the long bookkeeping of souls. But when Ivo opened it, there were no names at all.

Columns. Dense, ruled columns of figures, page after page, decades deep. Dates ran down the left margins in that same upright hand—Thursdays, Ivo noticed, almost without exception Thursdays—but the entries themselves were numbers. Groups of them. 114 / 22 / 7. 31 / 3 / 190. Page references, maybe. Or accounts. Or nothing at all.

No names where names should be. A parish ledger with the people removed.

Ivo turned pages until the light went, and the numbers did not resolve, and the sense of standing outside a locked door grew so acute that Ivo finally closed the book, pressed both palms flat against its cover, and said aloud, to the empty kitchen, "What are you?"

The house declined to answer. But somewhere outside, in the garden, something moved—a soft metallic clank, then silence, then the deliberate sound of a latch being lifted by someone who believed themselves unheard.

* * *

Ivo took the torch from the hall and went out the back door into a garden gone pleasantly feral. Frost was coming down, silvering the bean poles and the collapsed cold frame. At the bottom, past an apple tree heavy with frozen windfalls, stood a shed. Its window glowed.

Not glowed—flickered. Candlelight.

Ivo should have announced themselves. A solicitor would have. Instead Ivo crossed the grass quietly, out of some instinct older than profession, and knocked once on the shed door.

The candle went out instantly.

"I know you're in there," Ivo said. "The latch just moved."

Silence, long enough to have texture. Then, muffled, defensive, young: "It's empty. Go away."

"The shed belongs to the estate of Edith Marsh. I'm the executor." A pause. "Which means technically you're squatting in my inventory."

Another silence, and then, with the weary bravado of someone who has been cornered before and survived it: "Edith said I could stay."

The name landed strangely—Ivo couldn't have said why—in the frost-dark air. "Did she."

"She fed me. Since September. Left stuff on the wall." A rustle; the door opened six inches, on a chain that had clearly been installed by the occupant. One eye appeared, dark, ringed, unimpressed. "You're not the police?"

"No."

"You're not from social services?"

"No."

"You're the lawyer." The eye narrowed. "Edith said a lawyer would come. She said—" the voice faltered, caught itself, hardened—"she said whoever came shouldn't be trusted with the shed."

Despite everything, despite the day of photographs and marginalia and numbers without names, Ivo felt something in their chest come loose by a single degree. "And yet here I am, asking about the shed instead of evicting you. My name is Ivo Villalobos."

"Wren." The door opened another inch. The occupant resolved into outline: sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and defiance, wearing two coats at once and fingerless gloves, hair cut badly by someone with scissors and courage. "Just Wren. Don't ask what it's short for, it isn't short for anything."

"May I see where you're living, Wren?"

"Why?"

"Because it's below freezing, and because I'm going to be in this house for weeks doing an inventory, and because pretending we can't see each other will get exhausting very quickly."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a border negotiation. Then the chain came off.

Inside, the shed was a miracle of small survival: a camp bed, a sleeping bag rated for weather far kinder than this, a crate of books, a camping stove with one dented kettle, and—on a nail by the window—a canvas shopping bag, still containing a loaf of bread, a jar of honey, two tins of soup. All dated recently. All, Ivo noted, from the village shop, paid for in cash by a woman who had apparently been feeding a runaway for months and telling no one.

"She didn't tell me to keep it secret," Wren said, following Ivo's gaze to the bag. Defensive again. "She just said some things work better quiet."

"Some things do," Ivo said, and heard how much of their own life fit inside that sentence.

They stood in the cold together, executor and squatter, neither quite willing to be the first to soften. Then Wren jerked their chin at the torch beam still leaking from the cottage's back door, at the shape of the ledger visible where Ivo had left it open on the kitchen table.

"That her book?" Wren asked. "With the numbers?"

Ivo went very still. "You've seen it."

"Once. She was reading it on the wall bench last summer. I asked what it was." Wren shrugged, pulling the second coat tighter. "She said it was the true register. Said the real one had too many lies in it to be useful."

The frost ticked in the silence. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"The true register," Ivo repeated.

"That's what she called it." Wren's eyes, in the dying candlelight, were sharp and unromantic and entirely awake. "So. Are you throwing me out or not?"

Ivo looked at the sleeping bag, the honey, the badly cut hair, the girl who had been loved in secret by a woman who wrote letters in hymn margins to a child she'd lost—and felt tenderness arrive the way weather arrives, uninvited, undeniable, changing everything it touches.

"No," Ivo said. "But we should talk about the terms."

* * *

Later, back at the kitchen table, Ivo picked up the ledger again and read the first entry by lamplight. The date was the fourth of October, 1984. Three days after a fire Ivo had not yet heard about, in a parish whose records had too many lies in them to be useful.

The entry was short. Just five groups of numbers, and beneath them, in the margin, the only words in the entire book:

*I did it. God forgive me.*
