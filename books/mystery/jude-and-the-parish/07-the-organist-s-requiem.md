## 7. The Organist's Requiem

Marguerite Pell had played the organ at St. Aldhelm's for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one thing above every other: the dead do not leave. They settle into a building the way damp settles into stone—slowly, invisibly, until one day you notice the walls are weeping and you cannot say when it began.

Edith Marsh had been dead eleven days, and the church was already full of her.

Marguerite felt it most at the organ. The bench still held the impression of Edith's visits—she had never been a musician, not properly, but she'd sit beside Marguerite on Thursday evenings after the ledger room closed, hands folded in her lap like a schoolgirl, and listen to Marguerite run through the psalms. *Play the minor one,* she'd say. *The one that sounds like rain.* And Marguerite would play the one that sounded like rain, and Edith would close her eyes, and for a few minutes the weight she carried—whatever it was, and Marguerite had her suspicions, had always had her suspicions—would lift just slightly, like a curtain stirred by wind.

Now Marguerite sat at the bench alone, and the weight seemed to have transferred itself into the very pipes.

It was Wednesday evening. Evensong had ended an hour ago. The verger had gone home; the cleaner wouldn't come until morning. The light through the east window had gone from amber to bruise, and the candles Dennis had left burning for Edith threw their small trembling shadows up the pillar of the north aisle. Marguerite had told herself she was only going to run through Sunday's music. Instead, her hands found something else.

The requiem.

She hadn't planned it. Her fingers simply went where they always went when grief got ahead of her—into the piece Edith had taught her, note by note, over four winters of Thursday evenings. It had no name. It was not on any service list, had never been on any service list, and Marguerite had never played it for a congregation. It lived only in a folder of loose manuscript paper she kept in the organ bench's underside, beneath a false bottom that had once, she suspected, held someone's cigarettes.

She drew the folder out now. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was her reputation. *Marguerite Pell could play through a hurricane,* Dennis liked to say, and she had, once, in 1990, when a storm had taken a slate off the roof and dropped it through the vestry ceiling mid-anthem, and she had not so much as glanced up.

She opened the folder. The manuscript was in Edith's hand—not her everyday hand, the small tidy script of the ledger, but a larger, looser scrawl, the writing of a woman who had learned music late and loved it without technique. Marguerite had copied it out fair years ago, but she kept the original, because there were things in the margins that were not music.

She set it on the stand. She flexed her fingers. And she began to play.

* * *

The requiem began in D minor, which was not unusual. What was unusual was its architecture. It was built in seven sections, and each section was built of phrases whose lengths obeyed a pattern Marguerite had never found in any textbook: 3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2. Then the pattern repeated, transposed, the numbers governing not only the phrase lengths but the intervals between the sections' key centers, the number of bars of silence between movements, even—this had taken Marguerite two years to notice—the number of notes held in the pedal point beneath each section's final cadence.

She had asked Edith about it once, early on, in the third winter. *It's a beautiful structure, but where's it from? Fauré? Duruflé?*

Edith had looked at her with an expression Marguerite could still summon perfectly: a kind of weary amusement, the look of a woman watching someone admire the wrapping paper on a bomb.

*It's from a book,* Edith said.

*Which book?*

*One you haven't read, dear. One you shouldn't.* And she had patted Marguerite's wrist, and asked her to play the rain piece instead, and Marguerite—coward that she was, then and now—had played it.

It was only after Edith's death, only four days ago, sitting in the cold cottage with Ivo Villalobos's card in her pocket and the executor's letter in her handbag, that Marguerite had understood. She had gone home and taken down her old parish registers—the organist kept her own copies, duplicates, a habit from a dispute with a former vicar over who controlled the records—and she had opened the register of births to the years Edith's numbers had always circled around, and she had seen it.

The numbers in the music were the numbers in the register. Page numbers. Line numbers. Entry numbers. The requiem was not a requiem at all. It was a key. Or rather—it was the same key that opened something else, something Edith had kept locked for forty years, and Edith had taught it to her the way you teach a child a nursery rhyme that is also, secretly, a prayer: so that it would survive her.

Edith had hidden the confession in the one place no one would think to look. In beauty. In the thing she loved.

Marguerite's foot found the swell pedal. The music rose toward the fourth section—the one in five, the one that always made her think of someone climbing stairs they didn't want to climb—and she played it as she had never played it before, with the whole knowledge of what it was, and the church filled up with it, and the candles bent their flames, and somewhere in the tower a jackdaw shifted on its nest and complained.

She was still holding the final pedal note—nine beats, she counted them now, *nine*, of course it was nine—when she heard the door.

* * *

She knew it was him before she turned. She had heard the way he moved that first day at the funeral: a city walk, heel first, a man accustomed to corridors rather than flagstones. Ivo Villalobos stood at the west end of the nave, half in shadow, coat still buttoned, as though they had not decided whether they were staying.

"You locked it," Marguerite said, without turning around. "The north door. I heard it."

"I did. Then I heard the organ." A pause. "It's not on the service list. I checked."

Of course they had checked. Marguerite turned on the bench and looked at the executor of Edith Marsh's estate: forty-something, grey at the temples, a face that had been handsome once and had since decided to become useful instead. There was something in that face that unsettled her—not the sharpness she'd expected from a lawyer, but the opposite. A kind of rawness. The look of someone who had come to a parish to file papers and had instead begun to come apart.

"That's because it's not for services," Marguerite said.

"What is it for?"

Marguerite looked at the manuscript on the stand. She could lie. She had been lying, in her way, for thirty years—lying by omission, by the small daily cowardices of a woman who had chosen to be safe rather than brave. But Edith was dead. Edith was dead and the cottage was full of hymnals with writing in the margins, and Ivo Villalobos was standing in the nave with the look of someone who would not stop, and Marguerite had learned enough about people like that—Edith had been one, once, before the world taught her better—to know that you could not stand in the way of them. You could only choose whether to be swept along or trampled.

"It's Edith's," she said. "She wrote it. She taught it to me, over four winters. Thursday evenings, after she'd finished with the ledger room."

"The ledger room." Ivo came down the aisle slowly, their footsteps sounding against the stone. "Dennis mentioned she went every Thursday. Forty years."

"Forty years, yes. And then she'd come sit there—" Marguerite nodded at the empty half of the bench— "and listen. And one winter she asked me if I'd learn something she'd written. She said she wanted it *kept*. Not performed. Kept. She said—" Marguerite stopped. Her throat had gone tight, which infuriated her; she was not a woman who wept at benches. "She said she wanted it somewhere that would outlast paper. Paper burns, she said. She said it very specifically. Paper burns."

Ivo had reached the organ. Up close, Marguerite could see the sleeplessness on them, the faint tremor in the hand that came to rest on the case's carved rail. "Paper burns," they repeated. "She said that."

"She said a great many things. That was the trouble with Edith. She said everything, if you knew how to hear it, and nothing, if you didn't." Marguerite closed the folder, then—why?—opened it again. "You've been reading her papers."

"I'm the executor. It's my job."

"It's your job to inventory them. Not to *read* them. There's a difference, Mr. Villalobos, and I suspect you crossed it the first night." She said it gently, and watched it land, and saw that she was right, and hated herself a little for the skill.

"The ledger," Ivo said. "The numbers in it. Do you know what they are?"

The candles guttered. Somewhere in the dark of the nave, the building held its breath—the way old buildings do, Marguerite thought, the way they lean in.

"I know what they're *like*," she said. "They're like the music."

Ivo went very still. "Say that again."

"Seven sections. The phrase lengths run three, one, four, one, five, nine, two. The intervals between sections, the bars of silence, the pedal notes—all of it numbered, all of it patterned. I thought for years it was some system she'd invented for her own pleasure. Then after the funeral I sat down with the registers, and I found the same numbers circling the same years. Page and line and entry. Your ledger and her requiem are built on the same skeleton, Mr. Villalobos. She wrote her confession twice. Once in a code that could be burned, and once in a code that could only be forgotten." Marguerite's voice, steady, steady, steady. "And I am asking you to forget it."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Ivo's hand had closed on the organ rail hard enough that the knuckles showed white.

"You're asking me to walk away."

"I'm asking you to *stop*."

"They're not the same thing."

"No," Marguerite agreed. "They're not. Walking away is what I did, for thirty years. Stopping is what I'm asking you to do now that it's too late for either of us to have walked." She rose from the bench. Her knees ached; she was sixty-eight and the cold of the church got into them by November, and it was only October, and she was tired in a way that had nothing to do with her knees. "Sit down, Mr. Villalobos. Please. If I'm to beg you, I'd rather not do it standing."

Ivo did not sit. But they did not leave either, and Marguerite supposed that was something.

"Harold Finch came to see me," she said. "Two days after the funeral. Before you'd even arrived, or just after—I don't remember which; the days have been strange. He was very kind. He's always very kind. He asked whether Edith had left any papers with me. He said the family—" and here Marguerite smiled, a thin thing— "the *family*, as though the parish were his family, as though he were Edith's next of kin. He said it would be a kindness to the parish to keep her private matters private. He said she'd been a very private woman and would have wanted it that way."

"And what did you say?"

"I said Edith had never left anything with me in her life except a folder of music, and that I intended to keep playing it." Marguerite looked at her hands. "That much was true. The rest—" She exhaled. "I have known Harold Finch for thirty-one years, Mr. Villalobos. I played at his wife's funeral. I played at his golden anniversary. He is generous, and punctual, and he has given this parish more money than the diocese has in half a century. And every time he walks past that vestry door, he looks at it. Did you know that? Thirty-one years, and he still looks at it. The way you look at a room where something happened. The way you look at a grave you're not sure is deep enough."

Ivo's breath had gone shallow. Marguerite could hear it in the quiet.

"You've read the newspapers," they said. "The fire. 1984."

"I was here for the fire. I was twenty-eight years old and I had just taken this post, and I stood on the green at two in the morning—" Marguerite stopped. Closed her eyes. Opened them. "At *two in the morning*, Mr. Villalobos. The newspapers said morning. They said Edith raised the alarm at dawn. But I was on the green at two, because I'd forgotten my keys and gone back for them, and the vestry was already glowing. Already *glowing*. And there was a car behind it that I never told the police about, because when I opened my mouth to say so, I saw who was standing at the edge of the crowd, and I closed it again." She looked up at Ivo, and let them see, for the first time in thirty years, everything she had kept behind her face. "I was a young woman in my first post, and his family owned half this parish, and I told myself the car might have belonged to anyone. I have been telling myself that for forty years. It is the great accomplishment of my life, that lie. I have maintained it the way you maintain an organ. Daily. With care. And it has kept everyone safe. Edith. Harold. Me. Even you, Mr. Villalobos, though you didn't exist to be kept yet."

"So Edith's confession—"

"Is a *wall*," Marguerite said, and her voice cracked at last, one clean fracture down the middle of all that steadiness. "It is a wall she built with her own body, and everyone she loved has lived forty years in its shelter, and you want to pull it down to find out what's true. Well, I'll tell you what's true. What's true is that a man died in that fire, and Edith Marsh spent her whole life paying for it, and if you dig up *why*—if you pull one stone loose—everything she built comes down on everyone still standing under it. Harold. Dennis. That child in the shed you think nobody knows about. *You.*" She stepped closer, and took Ivo's hand—cold, cold, the executor's hand was freezing—and held it the way she had once held Edith's, on a Thursday, in the rain piece. "Some confessions keep people safe. That's what they're *for*. Not every sin wants finding out. Some sins want *carrying*. Edith carried hers so that the rest of us wouldn't have to, and the only kindness left to any of us is to let her put it down."

Ivo did not pull their hand away. That was the worst of it. They stood there and let her hold it, and their eyes were wet, and they said, very quietly:

"She was my mother."

Marguerite closed her eyes. "I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the funeral. You have her hands. You sat in the third pew and you folded them in your lap exactly the way she folded hers on that bench, every Thursday, for thirty years." Marguerite released the hand and stepped back, and gathered herself, and became once more the steady woman, the hurricane organist, the keeper of lies. "Which is why I'm begging you. Not as her friend. As someone who has watched what this parish does to the people who tell the truth here. Ask Dennis about the last person who tried. Ask him why the Thorne family left and never came back. Ask him—" she hesitated, and then, because she had already gone too far to stop, "—ask him what happened to the page from the birth register. The one for 1978. Ask him who tore it out, and when, and whether it was before Edith died or after."

Ivo had gone the colour of candle wax. "The register's missing a page?"

"Go home, Mr. Villalobos." Marguerite closed the organ bench. The false bottom shut over the folder with a soft, final sound, like a coffin lid in miniature. "Lock the north door behind you. And tomorrow, when you wake up and decide you can't let it go—because you won't, you have her stubbornness along with her hands—then at least do one thing for me. Before you dig up what Edith buried, find out who else is looking for it. Because you are not the only one who received a letter this month. And Harold Finch's solicitor telephoned the church office this afternoon to ask—" her smile was terrible in the candlelight— "exactly when the cottage is scheduled to go to market."

She blew out the candles one by one, and the dark came down over the nave like a held breath, and somewhere out in the churchyard, gravel shifted under a foot that was very quiet, and then was quieter.
