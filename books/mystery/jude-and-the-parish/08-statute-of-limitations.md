## 8. Statute of Limitations

The phone rang four times before Aurelio picked up, and Ivo spent those four rings rehearsing a question that had no casual version. You could not ask a man to commit a small act of professional treason against his own retirement and lead into it with *how's the weather in Bath*.

"Villalobos." Aurelio's voice came through gravelled and unhurried, the voice of a man who had billed by the six-minute increment for forty years and had never once been rushed. "You're calling on a Tuesday. Either someone died or you've finally read the arbitration clause."

"Someone died," Ivo said.

"Ah." A pause, the sound of a chair creaking. "Condolences. Professionally or personally?"

Ivo looked out the cottage window at the garden, where Wren was pretending not to watch the house from the shed doorway, a shape in a hooded sweatshirt, motionless as a heron. "That's the thing I'm calling about. It may be both."

He told it badly at first—the way you tell a story you haven't finished understanding yourself. A will. A parish. An executorship that made no sense. A dead woman whose name matched the one on documents Ivo had spent thirty years not reading, and then had read, in one white-knuckled evening, in a solicitor's office in Dorchester while the rain came down like a verdict.

Aurelio let him run out. He was good at that. He'd trained Ivo in it, actually—the silence as instrument, the held line as a room you let the client walk around in until they found what they'd been avoiding.

"Edith Marsh," Aurelio said at last, tasting the name. "And you believe she was your mother."

"I believe the paperwork believes it. I believe she kept a photograph of me on her mantelpiece, aged seven, in a jumper I remember. I believe—" Ivo stopped, started again. "I believe she wrote letters to me in the margins of hymnals and never posted them."

Another silence. When Aurelio spoke again, the gravel had softened. "You were never an easy one to unsettle, Villalobos. It's unsettling to hear you unsettled."

"There's more."

"There's always more. That's why they pay us."

Ivo carried the phone into the kitchen, set it on the counter, and laid the ledger open beside the kettle. The columns of numbers marched down the page in Edith's small, upright hand—service times, collections, candle counts, forty years of a parish's arithmetic. And threaded through them, in the same hand, the cipher. *I did it. God forgive me.* Dated the third of October, 1984. Three days after the vestry fire that had killed Vicar Ambrose Thorne.

"Tell me about arson," Ivo said. "Statutes of limitations. If someone confessed, in writing, to a fire that killed a man—thirty-nine years ago—what's the clock say?"

The creak of the chair stopped. He could hear Aurelio thinking, that particular quality of silence, like a held breath in a large room.

"England," Aurelio said. "No general limitation period for indictable offences—arson with intent to endanger life, manslaughter, they can technically be charged at any time. But." The word came down like a gavel. "You know the practicalities. Evidence rots. Witnesses die, forget, or marry each other. The Crown Prosecution Service looks at a thirty-nine-year-old case with one dead confessor and asks itself what a jury is meant to do with it. In practice, Villalobos, the clock is not legal. It's human. And it's very nearly run out."

"Then say the confession were false."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the lane a tractor changed gears.

"Say it were false," Aurelio repeated. "You're saying the dead woman didn't do it. That she took the blame for someone."

"I'm saying the arithmetic in this ledger doesn't add up, and the person it doesn't add up for is still alive, still walks to church on Sundays, and still shakes my hand."

"Who?"

"The churchwarden."

Aurelio made a sound—not quite a laugh. "Of course it's the churchwarden. It's always the churchwarden, or the organist, or the man who runs the flower rota. Thirty years of estate law, and the murderers are always the ones who count the collection." He paused. "You said *still alive*. Villalobos, what is it you actually want? Justice? Because a confession from a dead woman and a hunch about a churchwarden is not a prosecution. It's a novel."

"I want to know if the confession being false changes anything legally. For the estate."

"Ah." The chair creaked again. "Now we're back on solid ground. Read me the codicil."

Ivo had it on the counter already, the second document in the leather folder, the one the Dorchester solicitor had summarised in a tone of pleasant finality. He read it aloud: the residue of the estate—such as it was, the cottage, its contents, the modest account—passed to Ivo as executor. But the cottage itself, the freehold, was bequeathed by codicil to the PCC of the Parish of St. Aldhelm's, *to be sold and the proceeds applied to the restoration fund*, upon the executor's certification of the estate accounts.

"Certification," Aurelio said. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning I sign a statement that the estate is as the will describes. That the documents are what they appear to be."

"And if the documents are not what they appear to be. If the estate contains a ledger that is, in fact, a confession to a crime—and that confession is false—then the estate is not as the will describes, and you cannot in good conscience certify it." Aurelio let that sit. "You see the trap she built you, whoever built it. You can't certify and you can't refuse without asking why. And the moment you ask why, the codicil is in play."

"Voidable?"

"If the codicil was procured by fraud—or if its purpose is frustrated by a fundamental falsehood in the testatrix's intent—yes, it's attackable. A codicil that gifts the cottage to the parish *on the strength of a false confession the testatrix believed true*—if you can prove she believed falsely, that the gift rests on a lie—chancery would look at it. Slowly. Expensively. But they'd look." A pause. "Why does it matter who ends up with a cottage in a parish you'd never heard of six weeks ago?"

Ivo turned toward the window again. Wren had moved—was crossing the garden now with the exaggerated casualness of the young, hands in pockets, stopping at the shed's open door to examine something that did not need examining. Watching the cottage. Watching Ivo on the phone.

"Because someone else wants the cottage sold," Ivo said. "Badly. The churchwarden. He's offered to handle the estate paperwork himself. He's offered twice. He talks about the restoration fund the way other men talk about their grandchildren. And this morning I found a letter in Edith's papers from a firm of commercial agents in Poole—a valuation enquiry. Dated three weeks before she died. The parish, it seems, had already had the cottage appraised."

"Appraised by whom?"

"The enquiry came through the parish office. The churchwarden runs the parish office."

Aurelio was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, the professional crispness was back, and beneath it something Ivo remembered from twenty years ago—the low, quick voice he'd used when a case turned from paperwork into blood. "Walk it through for me. Slowly. I'm an old man and I like the scenic route."

"The codicil leaves the cottage to the parish for sale. The churchwarden controls the parish. He's been pushing the sale since before Edith died. Fine. Ordinary. Parishes sell things. But now say the ledger exists—say the confession exists—and say the churchwarden has known for forty years that Edith kept a record of that night. Every Thursday, forty years, she sat in the ledger room. Everyone knew her habit. Say he's spent forty years assuming the record was harmless—numbers, service times, an old woman's piety—and then, this year, something made him check."

"What would make him check?"

Ivo looked at the hymnal on the windowsill, the marginalia in its margins, the small looping hand that wrote *I carry someone else's sin so you won't have to* over and over like a rosary. "She started putting her affairs in order. She made a will naming a stranger as executor. She wrote two letters and had them scheduled for posting after her death. She was a woman dismantling a bomb she'd sat on for thirty-nine years, and she was doing it in a village where nothing is ever secret. If he checked—if he read enough to understand what she'd written—he couldn't destroy it, because she'd named an outside executor. A stranger. Someone he couldn't reach into a filing cabinet for."

"So he needs the ledger discredited or the executor managed."

"He needs the estate settled *fast*," Ivo said. "Before anyone reads the ledger properly. Before anyone asks why a woman who confessed to manslaughter in code would also write, in the same code, that she did no such thing. The codicil gets the cottage into the parish's hands, the sale goes through, the proceeds vanish into a restoration fund with forty years of deferred maintenance to absorb them, and the one document that could hang him is buried under a pile of sold-and-dusted household effects. And the statute—" Ivo stopped. "You said there's no legal clock. But there's a practical one. And he's been counting down longer than I have."

The line hissed. Aurelio breathing, or the sea, or both.

"Villalobos," he said finally. "I'm going to say something and you're going to want to argue, so let me get all the way through it. If what you're describing is true—if this man killed a vicar and let your mother carry it for thirty-nine years—then the law will not save you. The CPS will not charge on a cipher and a hunch. A court will not void a codicil on a theory. What you have is a document that proves *she* confessed and nothing that proves *he* did. You are holding the wrong half of the evidence, and the half you're holding is the half that convicts a dead woman of a crime she didn't commit." A breath. "Unless you find the other half. And you have—" he paused, doing the arithmetic Ivo had already done, "—call it five weeks. Six. Before any prosecutor who might have cared stops caring, and before a man who's waited forty years decides the garden is finally clean."

"Five weeks," Ivo said. "That's what I came to hear."

"No. You came to hear that you're wrong. That it's a cottage and a codicil and a sad old woman who wrote strange things in her old age. I can't sell you that. What I can sell you is this: don't sign the certification. Not yet. Not until you know what you're certifying. The moment you sign, the estate becomes what the will says it is, the codicil executes, and the only leverage you have—your refusal, your doubt, your *this document is not what it appears*—evaporates. Your signature is the clock, Villalobos. Not the statute. You."

Ivo looked at the ledger. At the columns. At the small, patient, terrible arithmetic of a life spent writing down everything because no one would listen to the words.

"There's one more thing," Ivo said. "The valuation. The commercial agents in Poole. If Finch requested it before Edith died—if he was arranging the sale while she was still alive—"

"Then he wasn't waiting for her death," Aurelio said. "He was preparing for it." The old man's voice dropped. "Villalobos. Be careful. Men who prepare for a death and then benefit from it are not, as a class, squeamish about a second one. Especially not when the second one is a stranger with a lawyer's eye and a ledger in a suitcase."

"I don't have a suitcase."

"You have a mother's confession. That's heavier."

Ivo laughed—short, surprised at itself. "Thank you, Aurelio."

"Don't thank me. Send me the codicil. I want to read her exact language before I tell you how attackable it is. And Villalobos—" the pause stretched, and when he resumed, the gravel was gone entirely, and what was underneath was just an old friend, "—whoever she was, whoever she was to you. She named you. Out of everyone in that parish, out of everyone in her life, she named you to finish it. That's not a legal appointment. That's a message. Read it that way."

The call ended. The kitchen was very quiet.

Ivo stood at the counter a long while, the ledger open, the codicil beside it, the two documents like two halves of a sentence that only made sense read together. *To be sold and the proceeds applied to the restoration fund.* Edith's hand in the cipher. Edith's hand in the margins. Edith, everywhere, writing to a child she'd given up and a truth she'd swallowed, and the parish—Harold Finch's parish—waiting with an appraisal and a smile to turn her whole life into liquid assets.

Money. It came down to money, the way it always did, the way it had in every estate Ivo had ever settled. Not passion, not madness. A roof, a fund, a comfortable margin. A man who'd killed once to protect his name would kill again—had killed again, in the slow way, in the way of letting a woman carry his crime until it bent her spine—to protect the same name and the same comfortable parish where he counted the collection every Sunday.

Outside, Wren had given up pretending. They were standing at the garden gate, arms folded, watching the cottage with the flat, assessing stare of someone deciding whether a building could be trusted.

Ivo opened the door.

"You were on the phone a long time," Wren said.

"I was."

"You look like someone died." A beat. "Worse than before, I mean."

"Someone did. A while ago." Ivo held the door wider. "There's tea. And I need to show you something in the ledger—the last pages, the ones I can't crack. Fresh eyes. You said you were good at puzzles."

Wren's eyes narrowed. "What's in it for me?"

"Food. A locked door. And—" Ivo weighed it, then said it, because Aurelio was right and the clock was Ivo's own signature and the clock was running, "—because in about five weeks, nobody will be able to punish the man who did this. And I'd rather not let him win on a technicality of time."

Wren considered that with the seriousness of a judge. Then they came up the path, past the roses Edith had planted for a child she never named, and stood in the doorway of the cottage they'd been squatting beside for a month, and said:

"Fine. But I get the good chair."

* * *

They worked at the kitchen table through the afternoon, the light going from white to amber, Wren in Edith's good chair with their boots off the cushions and their finger tracing columns of numbers while Ivo read out the register dates, the key text, the small machinery of the cipher grinding through its gears. Most of the late entries were mundane—roofing estimates, choir rosters, a long run of numbers that resolved to *repaired the east wall, paid in cash, do not tell the diocese*, which made Wren snort. But the final entry—the last page, dated nine days before Edith's death—would not resolve. The numbers were wrong. Too many. Out of range for the register.

"It's a different key," Wren said at last, sitting back. "Same hand, different book."

Ivo stared at the page. Different book. Edith had keyed her confession to the parish register of births—the book that held Ivo's own missing line, the torn page, the year 1978. But the last entry pointed elsewhere. Somewhere else in the church. Somewhere else in her life.

"Everything in that church is numbered," Ivo said slowly. "Pews. Hymns. Graves. The registers. The—" and stopped, because the thought arrived whole, the way the important ones did, cold down the back of the neck.

The churchyard wall. The stones. Every stone in an English churchyard bears a number in the sexton's book. Edith had walked that churchyard every day of her life.

The last entry wasn't a confession. It was an address.

Ivo looked up. Wren was watching, and for once there was nothing guarded in the kid's face—just the naked, hungry interest of someone who had finally been let inside something.

"Tomorrow," Ivo said. "First light. Before the parish office opens."

Wren grinned, and it was not entirely a nice grin. "I'll bring the torch."

That night Ivo did not sign the certification. The folder stayed shut on the counter, and the codicil slept inside it, and somewhere across the village Harold Finch slept too—or didn't—in a house that owned half the parish, five weeks from safety and not knowing yet that the clock had started running the other way.
