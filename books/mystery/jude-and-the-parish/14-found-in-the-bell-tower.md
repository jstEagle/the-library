## 14. Found in the Bell Tower

### Ivo

The search had lasted nine hours, and by the fourth I had stopped pretending it was a legal obligation.

I had walked Marsh Lane twice, the canal path once, checked the bus shelter with its torn timetable, the chip shop where the owner shook his head before I finished my sentence, the playground where the swings moved in wind that wasn't there. I had shown Wren's photograph—a blurry thing I'd taken through the kitchen window weeks ago, a shape at the shed door holding a chipped mug—to three strangers and one police community support officer who asked questions I couldn't answer without answering everything else. *And how do you know this child, sir? And why is she missing from your property?*

*She isn't mine,* I'd wanted to say. *That's rather the point of all of this.*

By two in the morning the frost had come down hard, silvering the parked cars along Vicarage Row, and my breath hung around me like something I was shedding. My phone showed four missed calls from Dennis Okafor, one from Marguerite Pell, and nothing from anyone whose voice I wanted. I stood in the churchyard with my collar up and understood, with the clarity exhaustion grants, that I was not looking for a runaway.

I was looking for the person I had failed to keep.

The church rose out of the dark ahead of me, St. Aldhelm's squat tower black against a sky just beginning to consider grey. I had searched the cottage—Wren's corner of it, the blanket on the sofa, the mug washed and set upside down on the rack, which hurt worse than mess would have. Tidied up. Put right. As if leaving were a chore to be completed properly.

I let myself in through the south porch with Edith's key, which was now my key, which still felt like borrowing. The nave was a cavern of cold air and faint candle-smell. I did not turn on the lights. I knew where I would look; I think I had known since midnight, since the moment I'd realized the ledger page was gone from my folder—the final cipher entry, the one we hadn't yet cracked, the coordinates or whatever they were, the last thing Edith Marsh had written in numbers. Wren had taken it as collateral. Insurance against being handed over.

A child who had been given up by every adult system designed to hold her had stolen the only proof she mattered enough to bargain with.

I crossed the nave, past the font, past the brass plate for Ambrose Thorne that I now passed the way you pass a grave you've stopped fearing, and pushed open the tower door at the base of the stairs.

The cold inside the tower was older than the cold outside. It came off the stone in centuries. The spiral steps went up into darkness, and somewhere above them, faint but unmistakable, came the small sounds of someone trying not to exist: the shift of a body against wood, the whisper of a jacket zipper being drawn closed like a held breath.

"Wren," I said. Not loudly. In a tower, loud is cruelty. "It's Ivo."

Nothing. The silence above me had the specific texture of a person listening.

"I'm not calling anyone," I said. "Not the social worker, not the police. Nobody. I just want to talk. You can stay up there as long as you like."

Still nothing. But the silence changed weight, the way a room changes when someone decides to hear you out.

I started up the stairs.

They were narrow, worn concave in the middle by four hundred years of feet, and my knees registered their opinion at every turn. Halfway up there was a slit window and I paused there, because my legs demanded it, and looked out at the parish going pale—rooftops, the canal, the allotments with their frozen canes standing like a tiny abandoned orchestra. Somewhere down there was a cottage with a spare bedroom full of boxes and a table set for one. Somewhere down there was a life I had been executing instead of living.

I climbed the rest of the way.

The ringing chamber opened around me: ropes hanging slack in their sally covers, the frame of the bells overhead humming faintly even in stillness, as if the tower remembered sound the way bodies remember grief. And in the corner, wedged between the wall and an old bench, wrapped in her own arms and a thin denim jacket entirely inadequate to January—

Wren.

Her face was blotchy with cold and crying. Her eyes found mine and went flat and hard, the way a cat goes flat when it's cornered and has decided fighting is better than hoping.

"You followed me," she said. Voice cracked down the middle.

"Nobody followed you. I guessed." I stayed by the stair door. Distance, I had learned, was a language she understood. "You left the mug upside down. You always leave things exactly where they were. So I thought about where you'd go that felt—" I searched for the word. "Safe. High up. Stone. Somewhere Edith's."

Her jaw worked. "I don't feel safe anywhere."

"No," I said. "I know."

"I took the page." She said it fast, defiant, chin up, hands shaking. "I'm not sorry. You were going to figure it all out and then what? Probate finishes, social services finish, everyone gets what's theirs and I go back to Maple Court with my stuff in a bin bag. Again. At least with the page I've got something. Something somebody wants. That's more than I've ever had."

The words landed in me like stones dropped down a well, each one striking deeper than the last.

"You're wrong about one thing," I said.

"Yeah?"

"I wasn't going to hand you over. I was going to ask you to stay."

### Wren

He said it like it was easy. Like people just *said* things like that.

Ivo stood there in the doorway with frost melting on his shoulders and his face all hollowed out from walking around all night—I'd seen him from the slit window at some point, a small dark figure crossing the churchyard, stopping, standing still so long I thought he'd frozen—and he said he was going to ask me to stay, and my whole chest did something stupid.

I didn't cry. I'd done enough crying. I was twelve hours past crying and into the part where you get mean because mean is easier.

"Stay where," I said. "Your boxes? On your sofa till the council finds out and you get done for harbouring? No thanks. I've done foster houses. I've done the bit where they're nice for six weeks and then the real kid acts up and suddenly it's all *maybe this isn't working out.*"

"This isn't a foster house."

"It's a cottage you've owned for like five minutes."

"It's Edith's cottage," Ivo said. "Which makes it complicated. Which makes it—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands, and for the first time since I'd known him he looked less like a lawyer and more like a person, which was somehow scarier. "Can I sit down? My legs have filed a formal complaint."

He didn't wait for permission, just lowered himself onto the bottom step of the next flight up, and I hated how relieved I was that he wasn't looming. The tower creaked above us. One of the bells shifted its weight in the frame like something turning over in sleep.

"Edith fed you," he said. "For months. Before I ever got here."

My throat went tight. "How d'you know that?"

"Dennis told me. Sausage rolls from the bakery on Thursdays. Left on the shed step. She told him she was feeding birds." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "She was. Technically."

"She never came in," I said. The words came out before I could stop them, and once they started they kept coming, the whole night's worth. "Never once knocked on the shed door. Just left food and went. And I used to watch her walk back across the yard and I thought, she knows I'm here, she's not stupid, and she's letting me be here anyway, and that was—nobody does that. Nobody lets you just *be.* Everyone wants paperwork. Everyone wants to fix you or report you or save you. She just left sausage rolls."

"That was her whole method," Ivo said quietly. "Leaving things where people could find them."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing. Everything. Ask me again in a week." He looked up at the bells. "Wren. I need to say some things and you're going to want to argue, and I'd take it as a personal favour if you let me finish first."

I pulled my jacket tighter. "Fine."

"The codicil leaves the cottage to the parish. Harold Finch wants to sell it. If I prove Edith's confession false—which I will—the codicil voids and the cottage comes to me. And here's the part you'll want to argue with." He turned and looked at me straight on, no lawyer-voice, just tired eyes in a cold room. "I don't want to sell it either. I want to live in it. And I want you to live in it. Properly. A room with a door that locks—your terms, remember. Food in the fridge. Your name on something, somewhere, officially, as soon as I can make that happen legally, which I can, because I am extremely annoying about law."

"That's mental," I said. "You've known me like two months."

"I've known you since you stole my biscuits and reorganized my filing by colour."

"That was *helping.*"

"It was. It was genuinely better organized." He reached into his coat pocket, slow, deliberate, the way you move around a frightened animal, and I watched his hand the whole time. What came out was small and brass-coloured and caught the grey light from the window.

A key.

"Spare executor's key," he said. "Cut Tuesday. Before any of this happened. I was going to give it to you Sunday, with breakfast, like a normal person. You've ruined the timing completely."

I didn't move. Keys are promises and promises are traps and I have been trapped by nicer-looking keys than that. "What's it open?"

"The front door. The back door. The shed, if you still want the shed, though God knows why, it has spiders." He set it down on the stone step between us. Didn't slide it toward me. Just put it down, in the middle, neutral territory, and took his hand away. "It doesn't expire. It doesn't come with conditions. If you take it and then change your mind, that's allowed too. Doors work both ways."

The tower hummed above us. Down in the churchyard a blackbird started up, one experimental phrase, testing whether the world was ready for morning yet.

"Why?" I said. And my voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Why me? You didn't even want to be here. You came here for a will. You didn't want a—"

"A what?" Ivo said softly. "Family?"

I shrugged like it was nothing. My eyes were burning. Traitors.

"I came here," he said, "to execute a stranger's will. I have spent thirty years drafting other people's endings, Wren. Contracts. Estates. Closure, professionally administered. And I have learned every possible way a life can be divided up and handed out on paper, and not one single form exists for—" He stopped. Started again. "Edith gave up a baby in 1978. There's a torn page in the register. Nine months before I was born."

The cold went through me all over again, different cold.

"You're joking."

"I have never been less joking in my life."

"So she's your—" I couldn't say it. Saying it made it real and real things get taken away.

"My mother," Ivo said. "Yes. Under another name. In another parish. For sixty-some years." A pause. "She left food on a step for a stray too, apparently. It seems to run in the family."

I laughed. It came out wet and horrible, half sob, but I laughed, and Ivo didn't look at me like I was broken, just waited, patient as stone, while I got myself back together with the heel of my hand.

Then I did the hardest thing. Harder than running, harder than the canal in February, harder than Maple Court ever was.

I picked up the ledger page from inside my jacket—warm from my body, creased where I'd gripped it all night—and held it out.

"She wrote it in numbers," I said. "The last one. I stared at it for hours. I thought maybe if I understood it, I'd understand why she—" My voice went. I pushed through. "Why she kept everyone. Why she fed strays. Take it. Finish it. Whatever's in that box or whatever it says—it should be yours. You're hers."

Ivo took the page with both hands, careful, like it was a bird.

"We're hers," he corrected. "Both of us. Possibly Dennis. Definitely the organist, though she'd deny it under oath."

"Marguerite's terrifying."

"Yes. Family usually is."

### Ivo

We came down the tower together as the sky went from pewter to pearl, Wren two steps ahead of me the entire way—not because she feared I'd grab her, I understood, but because leading was the only way she knew how to follow. At the tower door she stopped, silhouetted against the brightening churchyard, and looked back at me with an expression I will carry to my own ending: suspicion and hope in equal measure, a face deciding, minute by minute, to risk it.

"If this is a trick," she said, "I'll take the key and the page and disappear so thoroughly you'll need Interpol."

"Noted. I'll alert no one."

"And I'm not doing homework at the kitchen table like some advert."

"The table is available for homework, card games, silent brooding, and eating. All uses welcome."

She almost smiled. Then she walked out into the frost with her shoulders squared like a soldier, and I followed, and the blackbird above us finally committed to the whole song.

At the lychgate she paused. "Ivo?"

"Mm?"

"The letter that came after the funeral. The one saying the confession's a lie." She was looking at the vestry window, dark glass in a brightening wall. "Who do you reckon sent it?"

I had wondered the same thing through nine hours of streets. Someone who knew the confession was false. Someone close enough to the cottage to know its secrets. Someone who wanted me digging—and wanted me digging *fast*, before the statute ran out.

Someone, I thought, watching my breath hang in the cold, who was running out of time to be heard.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I intend to find out. Today, if I can. Starting with whatever's in that last entry."

Wren nodded slowly. Then she dug in her pocket and produced something I hadn't seen her take: a second piece of paper, folded small, soft with handling.

"Found this tucked in the shed wall," she said. "Been sitting on it. Seemed like a good day to stop."
