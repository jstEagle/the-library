## 17. Confrontation at the Organ

Marguerite Pell had been playing the organ for forty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one immutable law of St. Aldhelm's: the building told you things after dark that it never said aloud on a Sunday morning.

Evensong had ended twenty minutes ago. The last of the congregation—six souls, seven counting the dog that always sat by the font—had shuffled out into the blue February dusk, and now she was alone with the instrument, running her hands over the keys without pressing them, the way you might touch the face of someone sleeping. The console lamps made two small amber islands in the dark of the choir loft. Below her, the nave stretched out in shadow, the pillars going up into blackness like the legs of some patient animal.

She was not alone, of course. She had known that since the third hymn.

Ivo Villalobos had come to evensong for the first time tonight, and they had not come to pray. Marguerite had watched them from the loft—the lawyer's stillness of them, the way their eyes moved over the congregation like fingers over a ledger, counting, weighing, filing. They had sat in Edith's pew. That was either an accident or a message, and Marguerite did not believe Ivo Villalobos did anything by accident.

Now she could hear them on the stairs to the loft, taking each step slowly, letting the wood announce them. Courteous even in ambush. Edith would have liked that about them. *Edith,* she thought, and her chest did the thing it had been doing all week, the folding-in, the ache with no name that was not quite grief because grief required permission and Marguerite had spent thirty years refusing to give herself any.

"You have a gift," Ivo said from the top of the stairs, "for choosing music that sounds like a question."

Marguerite did not turn around. Her hands stayed in her lap, folded over the black skirt she wore to every service, the skirt that had outlasted three vicars and one fire. "It's how I pray," she said. "Badly, but loudly."

"Was tonight's requiem a prayer?"

"It was a requiem."

"For Edith Marsh. It wasn't on the service list."

"No." Marguerite finally turned. Ivo stood at the edge of the lamplight, coat still on, a folder under one arm—the folder, she was almost certain, that held the ledger, or photographs of it, or whatever fragment of Edith's forty years of Thursdays they had managed to crack open so far. Their face in the amber light was careful, courteous, and underneath the care something moved like water under ice. "Service lists are for the living," Marguerite said. "Edith knew the piece. She helped me write it."

"I know." Ivo came forward and sat—not beside her at the console but one bench down, angled toward her, close enough to be intimate and far enough to be deniable. A negotiator's distance. "The structure mirrors the ledger columns. Phrase lengths keyed to the numbers. I've been listening to it for three days, Miss Pell. It's a cipher set to music. She taught you a confession and asked you to play it once a month where no one would think to read it."

The organ breathed around them—the blower's low hum, the faint tick of cooling pipes. Marguerite looked at her own hands. Seventy-one years old and they still looked, to her, like her mother's hands, which was its own small horror.

"You've been talking to Dennis," she said.

"Dennis talks to everyone. It's his gift too."

"And the boy? Wren?" The name came out before she could stop it, and she saw Ivo register it, saw them file it away. Too late to be casual. "He sits in the gallery sometimes. Thursdays. He thinks no one sees him."

"He's safe," Ivo said. "That's not why I'm here."

"Why *are* you here, Mr.—" she stopped. "Villalobos. What a strange name. Do you know what Edith called you, when she talked about you? Not your name. She didn't have it. She called you *the winter child.* Because you'd been born in January, and because—" Marguerite's voice caught on a splinter of old anger. "Because that was all she was ever allowed to keep of you. A season."

Ivo had gone very still. Stillness, Marguerite thought, was this one's native language; they could hold it the way other people held their breath. But she had spent four decades watching faces across a console, reading the ones who wept at the Kyrie and the ones who wept at the coffee hour, and she could see the ice moving now, cracking along lines they hadn't chosen.

"She talked about me," Ivo said. It wasn't a question, but it wanted an answer.

"Every Thursday for years, she climbed those stairs while I practiced, and she sat where you're sitting, and she talked about everything except the thing she'd come to say. And then near the end she said it anyway." Marguerite rose, went to the console, and put one finger on a key without sounding it—a habit, touching the note and withholding it, a whole life in a single gesture. "You want me to break, Mr. Villalobos. You've been circling this parish for weeks with your ledger and your letters and your polite questions, and everyone keeps telling you to stop, and you won't, because you're your mother's child whether you know it or not—she never stopped digging at anything either, God help her. So yes. I'll break. But you'll hear it in order, because the order is the only dignity left in it."

She sounded the key after all—one low note, deep enough to feel in the floorboards—and let it die.

"In 1984 I was twenty-eight years old. I'd been organist here three years. Harold Finch was already churchwarden, the way he'd been churchwarden since the Flood; his family built half this parish and mortgaged the other half. Ambrose Thorne was vicar—young, difficult, honest in the way that makes enemies. He'd found out something about the Finch accounts. Restorations funds, roof appeal money. He told the PCC he intended to audit the books himself, personally, and he told Harold he intended to do it publicly, at the annual meeting, because, and I quote him exactly, 'sunlight is the only disinfectant this parish has ever trusted.'"

Ivo's breath had gone shallow. Marguerite could hear it.

"The night of the fire, I was here late. Practicing. I always practiced Thursday nights—that's when the building was empty, that's when *she* started coming to the ledger room, though I didn't know that yet. Around eleven I heard a car in the lane behind the vestry. Engines carry in this churchyard; everything does. Then voices. Ambrose's voice, and another one, low. I couldn't make out words, only the shape of them—an argument with a shape I recognized, the shape of a man being cornered. Then quiet. Then I smelled smoke."

"What did you do?"

"What anyone does. I ran down, I shouted, I got the door open—Ambrose was already past helping, the vestry ceiling had gone up like paper, and there was a man standing in the churchyard watching it burn." Marguerite heard her own voice flatten, go far away, the voice of testimony rather than conversation. "Not running. Not shouting. Watching. With his arms folded. Like a man checking work he'd paid for."

"Finch."

"I didn't see his face. The light was behind him. But I saw his car, and I saw the way he stood, and when the police came I told them both things, and do you know what happened, Mr. Villalobos? The inspector wrote down *organist, hysterical, unreliable.* His word. Hysterical. I was twenty-eight and my vicar had just burned to death ten feet in front of me and I was *hysterical.* And Harold Finch stood at my shoulder through the whole interview, so kind, so helpful, telling the constable I'd been upset lately, that I'd lost my father that spring, that grief made me imagine things. And the fire was ruled accidental. An electrical fault. In a vestry where the wiring had been inspected and passed six weeks earlier—by a firm that subcontracted to Finch Holdings."

The silence afterward had weight. Somewhere above them a roosting pigeon shifted, and the sound fell down through the dark like a dropped coin.

"Edith found out," Ivo said quietly. "Years later."

"Edith found out *that same year.*" Marguerite turned fully now, and let them see her face, let them see what thirty years of carrying this had done to it. "She was the parish secretary then. She kept the books—real books, not the cipher. Ambrose had shown her what he'd found, two days before he died. She had copies. She went to the police after the fire and they smiled at her the way they'd smiled at me. She went to the diocese and the diocese thanked her for her concern. She went to a solicitor in Salisbury who took her money and told her that a cleaning woman's word against the Finch family was worth exactly nothing, and that continuing might be worth a libel action against *her.*"

"So she confessed instead."

"So she confessed." Marguerite's voice cracked at last, and she let it. "Don't you understand yet? Don't you see what she did? She couldn't get justice, so she bought the next best thing—she made herself the story. If the truth ever surfaced, if Finch ever needed a shield, there would be one waiting: Edith Marsh, the woman who raised the alarm, the woman whose ledger said *I did it, God forgive me.* A confession dated three days after the fire. She built him an alibi out of her own guilt and she hid it in code so obscure that only someone who loved her enough to spend months on it would ever find it—and she hid it in the one place Finch would never look, because Finch never once in his life believed a woman's private arithmetic mattered."

"But the anonymous letter," Ivo said. "Postmarked the day after her funeral. It says the confession is false. If she built it as a shield, why send a letter tearing it down?"

"Because shields expire." Marguerite came back to the bench and sat, suddenly tired, suddenly seventy-one in every joint. "Statutes close. Witnesses die. She was dying when she wrote that letter—she knew, she'd known since the summer. And she told me, the last Thursday, sitting right there: *When I'm gone, the confession becomes just a confession. A lie with no purpose. Someone will find it and believe it, and I'll have lied to them from the grave, and worse—I'll have let Harold win forever.* So she scheduled the letter. Standing instruction with her solicitor, mailed the day after the funeral. It was a hand reaching out of the ground, Mr. Villalobos. Hers. Saying: *look again.*"

Ivo was silent a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had lost its lawyer's polish entirely. "She let the whole parish think she killed a man. For thirty years. Dennis said she never took communion."

"She took communion," Marguerite said. "She just wouldn't take it from Harold's hands. He served the cup at Easter, always, family privilege. She'd wait for the second chalice. Every year. Thirty Easters." She laughed, or something adjacent to laughter, wet and brief. "And everyone thought it was penance. It was contempt. The purest, most patient contempt I have ever witnessed."

"Why tell me now?" Ivo asked. "Marguerite—why now, when you begged me to stop? *Some confessions keep people safe,* you said."

"Because keeping people safe has a cost, and I've been paying it since 1984, and I am so tired." She reached out and closed the fallboard over the keys, gently, the way you close a coffin. "I warned you off because warning people off is what I do. It's reflex. Forty years of *hysterical, unreliable, imagine things.* You learn to swallow evidence like a pill you don't chew. But then you sat in her pew tonight, and you listened to her requiem like it was a letter addressed to you—which it was—and I realized Edith planned all of this. The ledger. The letters. Even you. She couldn't have known you'd find her, but she hoped, and her hope had better paperwork than most people's faith." Marguerite looked at Ivo directly. "She said Harold lit it. She said his family owned half the parish, and she asked me—who would believe her? Well. I'm answering her now, thirty years late. *We will.* Me, and Dennis, and a runaway boy, and a lawyer with her eyes. That's who believes her."

Ivo's composure broke then—not dramatically, nothing so useful as tears, just a fine tremor around the mouth, the look of someone hearing a door open after decades locked. "There's a codicil," they said. "The cottage to the parish, contingent on the confession standing. Finch wants to sell. And the statute—my mentor says weeks. Days, maybe."

"Then you'd best find what she left you to find," Marguerite said. "She always kept one more thing back. One more Thursday. Ask yourself what a woman hides when she wants to be believed, not merely pitied."

Ivo stood, paused at the stairhead. "The last entry in the ledger. We haven't cracked it. The cipher shifts—it doesn't match the register anymore."

Marguerite smiled, and it was the smile she saved for the final verse of certain hymns, the ones that end not in triumph but in something quieter and harder. "Try the requiem," she said. "She said you'd know when. She said—" and here Marguerite's voice finally gave out altogether, and she finished in a whisper, "—she said, *play it for my child, and they'll hear where I put the rest.*"

Ivo went down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and the church door opened and shut behind them, and Marguerite Pell sat alone in the amber lamplight with her hands in her lap.

Then she lifted the fallboard, and began, softly, to play the requiem again—this time not as a question, but as directions.
