## 18. Decoding the Last Entry

The last entry in Edith Marsh's ledger was different from all the others.

I had noticed it before—how could I not? It sat alone on the final ruled page, longer than any entry that came before it, and written not in the neat columnar figures that had taken Wren and me three weeks to crack, but in a slightly looser hand, as though she'd been tired, or hurried, or both. The numbers ran on for lines and lines without pause, a river of digits that had defeated every key we'd tried against it.

The parish register of births had unlocked the rest of the book. Every cipher group resolved to a page number, a line number, a word number—a book code as old as ciphers themselves, hidden in the most boring document in any church in England. *I did it. God forgive me.* Forty years of Thursdays distilled into borrowed words from a register of christenings. But the last entry refused. We'd tried the register. We'd tried the hymnal. We'd tried the prayer book, the burial roll, even Marguerite's old service lists, which Dennis produced from a cupboard smelling of candle wax and mouse.

Nothing.

"It's not a book code," Wren said, on the third night, their chin propped on their fists, the ledger open between us under the cottage kitchen lamp. "Or it's not *only* a book code."

"What makes you say that?"

Wren tapped the page with one chipped fingernail. "Look at the shape of it. All the other entries are groups of three or four numbers. Page, line, word. This is—" they counted under their breath, lips moving, "—pairs. Mostly pairs. Some threes at the start, then pairs all the way down."

I looked. They were right. I had spent so long staring at the content that I'd stopped seeing the form. That was the trouble with expertise; it taught you to look for what you expected to find. Wren had no expectations. Wren had only eyes.

"Pairs," I said. "Page and line?"

"Maybe. Or—" Wren hesitated, and I watched them wrestle with something, some instinct they didn't quite trust yet. "Or coordinates."

"Coordinates."

"Like a map." They said it carefully, watching my face. "My dad used to do orienteering. Grid references. Eastings and northings. Six-figure references, two numbers each." A shadow crossed their face at the mention of the father—they never said much about him—but they pushed through it, which was more than they'd managed in November. "You'd need a map."

"We don't have a map."

"We have a churchyard," Wren said.

* * *

Dennis Okafor let us into the church at half past seven on a Thursday morning, before the cleaners came, before the light was fully up. He had his keys on a ring worn smooth as river stone, and he moved through the nave the way a man moves through his own kitchen, not looking, knowing.

"You're saying she hid something in the churchyard," he said. It wasn't quite a question. He'd been saying versions of it to himself since I'd telephoned him the night before, and I think by now he'd made his peace with it, or as much peace as a verger could make with the idea that the woman whose brasses he polished every week had spent forty years carrying a secret heavy enough to bend her back.

"Not hidden," I said. "Recorded. There's a difference. She wanted it found—eventually. By someone patient enough to read her properly."

"And you think you're that someone."

"I think I'm the someone she addressed it to."

He didn't ask what I meant by that. He knew. Everyone at St. Aldhelm's knew by now—or believed they knew—that the executor was Edith Marsh's child, given away in 1978, come home too late. Dennis had absorbed the news the way he absorbed everything: quietly, thoroughly, and with an immediate practical follow-up. In his case, the follow-up had been to unlock the ledger room without being asked and leave the radiator on.

The churchyard in early December was silvered with frost, the yews black against a sky the color of dishwater. Dennis walked us along the north wall, where the ground rose toward the lane and the older stones leaned together like conspirators. The wall itself was old—older than the church, Dennis claimed, parts of it Saxon—and built of coursed flint and limestone, patched over centuries with brick, with render, with whatever came to hand.

"Grid reference," Wren murmured, walking the wall's length with their hands jammed in their sleeves, counting. "If the reference starts at a corner, you'd pace it out. Eastings first, then northings."

"The cipher's first numbers were larger than the register has pages," I said. "That's why I thought it wasn't a book code at all. But if they're paces—"

"How many?"

I had the transcription in my coat pocket, copied out fair in my own hand because I couldn't bear to carry hers. I read the opening groups aloud. Wren counted on their fingers, then started again from the northeast corner, pacing heel-to-toe along the wall, muttering numbers, while Dennis and I stood in the frost like mourners at a graveside, which in a sense we were.

Wren stopped. Counted backward. Stopped again.

"Here," they said. "Give or take a stone."

We were standing before a section of wall where the flint gave way to a panel of dressed limestone—deliberate, squared, older-looking than the patches around it. And set into the limestone, low, at knee height, was a stone that didn't match its neighbors. Smaller. Squarer. With a socket in its face that might once have held an iron ring, or might have been nothing at all.

"A memorial stone," Dennis said slowly. "For ashes. There are dozens like it. Families buy the plot, inter the urn behind the stone, and there's an end to it."

"Whose name is on it?" I asked.

Dennis bent, brushed the frost from the face of the stone with his sleeve, and went very still.

There was no name. There was no inscription at all. Just the blank, weathered limestone, and the small square socket, and beneath the socket, so faint you'd take it for mason's scoring if you weren't looking, a single incised line: a cross, no taller than a thumb.

"There's no record of this plot," Dennis said. His voice had changed—the verger's voice giving way to the parishioner's, the man who had known Edith Marsh for thirty years and was only now understanding how little knowing had gotten him. "I keep the interment register. I've kept it since 1991. There's no ash interment here. No purchase. Nothing."

"She couldn't put her name on it," I said. "She couldn't put anything on it. But she could put it *in* the ledger, coded, where only someone who read her would ever find it. She trusted the wall to keep it and the cipher to explain it."

Wren crouched, worked their fingers into the socket, and pulled.

The stone didn't move. Wren pulled harder, boots slipping on frosted grass, and swore with a fluency that would have gotten them extra prayers at any other church.

"It's mortared," Dennis said. "They all are. You'd need—"

He stopped. Because I had already seen it, and I think he saw a half-second after I did: the mortar around the false stone was newer than the mortar around everything else. Not new-new. Decades old, weathered gray, matched cunningly to the rest. But a hairline seam ran around it, clean as a scribe line, and at the bottom edge, almost invisible, a tiny channel had been cut to let rainwater out—which meant letting something else in.

A crowbar would have been honest. We had a multi-tool from Wren's rucksack, a church key, and a teaspoon Dennis produced from his pocket with the unembarrassed dignity of a man who had been a verger long enough to know that teaspoons solved more problems than prayer did.

It took forty minutes. Wren levered with the multi-tool while I worked the teaspoon into the seam and Dennis stood lookout on the path, ostensibly watching for dog-walkers, actually just unable to watch. The mortar crumbled in places, held in others; twice the tool skidded and gashed my knuckle, and the blood on the frost looked very bright. Neither Wren nor I mentioned the cold, which had gotten into our hands an hour ago and stayed.

And then the stone shifted, tilted, and came free with a sound like a tooth leaving its socket, and there was the cavity behind it—not an urn niche but a void, dry, lined with what looked like lead sheeting, and sitting in the middle of it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with tarred string, a tin box the size of a loaf of bread.

Nobody moved for a moment. The frost ticked in the grass. Somewhere beyond the yews, a wood pigeon went through its five-note complaint.

"You do it," Wren said quietly. "It's yours. Hers. Whatever."

I knelt—I was kneeling already—and lifted the box out of the wall. It was heavier than it looked. The oilcloth was stiff with age but sound; whoever had wrapped it had done the job properly, the way people wrap things they intend to survive them. My hands shook getting the string undone, and Wren reached over without a word and picked the knot apart, because their hands didn't shake, because they were sixteen and hadn't learned yet that some knots are forty years of a woman's life.

Inside the tin: a plastic bag containing a notebook, a fountain pen, and a folded newspaper clipping. Beneath those, flat and official-looking, a buff folder with a bank's crest on it, and beneath that—

Beneath that was the thing itself.

An insurance claim form. Mercantile & General Fire Assurance. Dated the eleventh of March, 1984. Signed in blue ink, in a hand I now knew better than my own—confident, slanted, the capital H with a flourish like a bird taking wing.

Harold Finch.

Claiming, on the eleventh of March 1984, for the total loss by fire of the contents of the vestry of St. Aldhelm's Church—including, itemized in his own neat print, a Victorian oak vestment case, two sets of choir robes, and a locked deed box belonging to the parish—for which he was, the form stated, the custodian.

The fire had been reported on the morning of the twelfth of March. The newspapers said so. The coroner's inquest said so. The blaze had been discovered at dawn by Edith Marsh, passing the church on her way to milk, who raised the alarm too late to save Vicar Ambrose Thorne, asleep—he had drunk, the inquest found—in the room above.

This form was signed the day *before*. Filed, stamped by the bank's receiving office, dated, witnessed. Harold Finch had insured himself against a fire that had not yet happened, and then collected on it, and the woman who died last month in a cottage full of hymnals had carried the proof of it in a hole in a wall for forty years, visiting it every Thursday, telling no one, confessing in borrowed words to a crime she hadn't committed so that no one would ever look closely enough to find the proof that would hang the man she loved.

"He filed before the fire," Dennis said. He had come up behind us without either of us hearing, and he was reading over my shoulder, and his voice was the flattest I had ever heard it. "Before. Before the vicar—" He couldn't finish it.

"Before Ambrose Thorne died in it," I said, because someone had to.

Wren said nothing at all. They were looking at the notebook in the plastic bag, and when I turned it over I understood why. On the flyleaf, in Edith Marsh's hand, in ink gone brown with age:

*For whoever is meant to find this. If no one is meant to find it, then it will keep.*

Below that, a date. The fourth of February this year. Eleven days before she died.

She had known. She had gone into the churchyard in the winter cold, eleven days before her death, and checked on her secret one last time—or added to it. The fountain pen was new; the notebook was new. She had written something in the last weeks of her life, and I turned to the first page with a care that felt like touching a wound.

One line. One only.

*I am sorry I won't be there to explain. I expect you'll be angry with me. Be angry, then. But finish it.*

* * *

We sat in the ledger room afterward, the three of us, with the box open on the table between the tea things and the radiators, and nobody spoke for a long time. Outside, the frost was burning off the graves in slow white ribbons. Somewhere overhead Marguerite Pell was playing—she hadn't seen us arrive, or had pretended not to—and the music came down through the floorboards in fragments, something slow and minor that I suspected was not on any service list either.

Triumph, I thought, should feel like something. I had imagined this moment, in the weeks of cipher work, in the nights of cross-referencing: the click of the lock, the lifting of the weight, justice arriving like a train on schedule. What I felt instead was the specific grief of reading a letter written to you by someone who knew they wouldn't live to see you open it. Edith Marsh had solved her own mystery, sealed it, addressed it, and posted it into a wall, and she had died not knowing whether anyone would ever collect.

Forty years of Thursdays. Forty years of polishing brass above a secret and sweeping gravel over a lie, and the whole time the truth sat six feet from the porch where she'd stood every Sunday singing hymns beside the man who lit the fire.

"She protected him," Dennis said at last. He was holding his tea without drinking it. "All that time. Even at the inquest—even when they ruled it accidental and closed it in a fortnight—she could have said a word. She raised the alarm; they'd have listened to her. She said nothing."

"She said 'I did it,'" Wren said. "Just not out loud."

"And the codicil," I said. "The cottage to the parish. To Finch's parish, controlled by Finch's churchwardens, sold the moment probate clears." I looked at the claim form, at the blue-ink flourish. "He was going to be paid twice. Once in 1984 and once this spring. Her death was going to settle his accounts for good."

"Unless the confession's proven false," Wren said. "Which voids the codicil. Which you told us. Weeks ago." They frowned. "So what's the problem? We've got it. Signed, stamped, dated. Hand it over."

"The statute," I said. "Arson causing death. The limitation window closes in—" I did the arithmetic I had been doing in my head for ten days, the arithmetic that woke me at four in the morning. "Nine days. And a forty-year-old insurance form isn't evidence of arson. It's evidence of opportunism, maybe—of a man who insured property and collected. His solicitors will call it coincidence, clerical error, a backdated filing. They'll bury it in expert reports until the window shuts."

"So we need more."

"We need it to be undeniable. Fast." I touched the newspaper clipping in the plastic bag—the local paper's account of the fire, yellowed to the color of weak tea—and froze.

Because there was a photograph. I'd seen the archive photos in Dennis's boxes; I knew the one of young Finch's car behind the vestry at 2 a.m. This was a different photograph, one I'd never seen, tucked inside the clipping where no archivist would file it. A snapshot, amateur, the kind taken at a parish fete. Four people squinting into summer sun outside the vestry door. Young Dennis, unmistakable even in blur. A younger Marguerite. Vicar Thorne, laughing.

And Edith Marsh—younger than I had ever seen her, dark-haired, almost pretty in a stubborn way—with her hand resting on the arm of a tall man in a pale suit, and both of them smiling at the camera with the particular ease of people who have no secrets left between them.

On the back, in pencil, in a hand I recognized: *St. Aldhelm's fete, Aug. 1983. E.M., H.F., D.O., M.P., + Fr. T.*

"They knew each other," Wren said, reading over my shoulder. "Well. Obviously."

"No." My mouth had gone dry. "Look at the date. August 1983. Seven months before the fire. Look at where they're standing."

They looked. Dennis looked. The photograph trembled slightly in my hand, and it took me a moment to understand that the trembling was mine.

They were standing outside the vestry door. Behind them, half-cropped by the frame, was the vestry window—and through the glass, faint but unmistakable, the corner of a locked deed box on a shelf. The same deed box, itemized in Finch's own print, that he had claimed for loss by fire seven months later.

Edith hadn't just carried the proof. She had built it. Piece by piece, for forty years, she had assembled a case in a hole in a wall—claim form, clipping, photograph, notebook—waiting for a reader. And the last thing she had written, eleven days before she died, was an instruction:

*Finish it.*

Marguerite's music stopped mid-phrase. Through the floorboards came the sound of the organ bench creaking, footsteps crossing the gallery, and then, descending the tower stair, unhurried and deliberate, the tap of a cane I had never heard her use before.

Dennis looked at the ceiling. Then at me.

"That's odd," he said. "Marguerite hasn't been down those stairs on a Thursday morning in twenty years. She says the tower stairs hurt her knees."

The footsteps reached the landing. Paused.

"But then," Dennis said quietly, "she also told us she never knew about the fire until she read the papers. And I'm beginning to wonder what else she's been careful about."

The door handle turned.
