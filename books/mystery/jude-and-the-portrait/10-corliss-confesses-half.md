## 10. Corliss Confesses Half

Corliss Dunmore had spent sixty-one years learning that the safest place to keep a secret was inside a silence so complete it looked like nothing at all. A blank face. A closed door. A life lived in a town you never visited, a family you never called, a niece-or-nephew — Quentin had never settled the question and neither, pointedly, had she — you never wrote to.

Twelve years of silence, in fact. Twelve years since the funeral, since she'd stood at the back of the church in her good black coat and left before the first hymn ended, and Quentin had watched her go with that look — the look that was the whole family inheritance, passed down like the china nobody used: *I see you. I will never say so.*

Now she was sitting in the Marlowe Grand's back parlor with a snifter of brandy she hadn't touched, and the child she'd abandoned to other people's care was sitting across from her with the same look, and the silence had finally gotten too heavy to carry.

"You're not drinking," Quentin said.

"I'm deciding," Corliss said, "how much of the truth you can survive."

"That's not your decision anymore."

"No," she agreed. "It stopped being anyone's decision about a week ago, when they pulled Edmund Vale out of the ground and found a box of river stones." She lifted the glass at last, let the amber catch the lamplight. "I told you to stop asking about the Vales. I want it noted, for whatever record you're keeping in that beautiful orderly head of yours, that I told you."

"Noted. I ignored you."

"You always did. Even when you were nine and I told you not to go into the flooded quarry." She smiled despite herself, and hated that she smiled. "You came back soaked to the collar with a frog in your pocket and lied about it with a straight face. I knew then you'd either end up a lawyer or a detective."

"Concierge."

"Same profession. You manage other people's arrivals and departures and you never let them see what you've noticed." She drank. The brandy was excellent, of course; Quentin ran a hotel the way a surgeon runs a theater, everything in its place, everything the best. "All right. Ask."

Quentin didn't ask. That was the maddening thing about them. They simply waited, hands folded, the lamplight doing its work on that careful face, and Corliss understood she was going to have to do this the hard way — voluntarily.

"The papers," she said. "My mother's papers. You want to know what's in them."

"I want to know why you checked into my hotel under the name Mrs. E. Fenwick, when you've been Corliss Dunmore your whole life and Dunmore is the name on the deed to a house in Cedar Falls you haven't lived in since 1989."

"Old habits."

"Whose?"

"Mine." Corliss set the glass down. Her hand was steadier than she'd expected. "And my mother's. And her mother's before her. Fenwick was the name my grandmother took when she left this town the first time. It's a name that doesn't open any doors here. That's what it's for."

"Opens doors everywhere else, I'd think. Fenwick. That's a money name."

"It's a *quiet* name. There's a difference." Corliss reached into her handbag — the old leather one, the one she'd carried to every funeral and every wedding and every one of the twelve Christmases she hadn't come home for — and brought out an envelope. Not the papers themselves. She wasn't ready for that. But the envelope, heavy, sealed, the wax stamp her grandfather had used cracked down the middle from being opened and resealed so many times across three generations.

She put it on the table between them.

Quentin looked at it the way Quentin looked at everything: as if cataloguing it. Weight, dimensions, provenance, condition. Corliss wanted to shake them.

"It's not going to bite," she said.

"It might," Quentin said. "Everything in this town bites lately. A man checked in under a dead founder's name. A coffin came up full of gravel. The archivist smiles at me like I'm a promising student. And my aunt — my aunt who hasn't spoken to me since the funeral — appears in my lobby under a false name carrying a family relic in a wax-sealed envelope." They looked up. "So yes, Aunt Corliss. I think it might bite."

*There it is.* The word *aunt*, laid down like a card. Not angry. Worse — precise. Quentin had always fought with precision. Corliss had fought with distance, which was just precision with a longer fuse.

"I owe you an apology," she said.

"For which decade?"

"All of them." She heard her own voice go rough and was surprised. She'd rehearsed this in the car on the drive up — the apology, the explanation, the whole architecture of it — and in rehearsal it had come out clean. In the room it came out like something broken. "Your mother should have been the one to tell you all of this. My sister never kept a secret in her life; it's why I loved her and why she died not speaking to me. But she's gone, and you're here, and the papers are in your lap whether I hand them over or not, because you've already gone digging. Miriam Halloway showed me the scratch marks on your curiosity the moment you walked into my parlor. You have her eyes when you're working. My mother's. The whole family's. We're a line of people who *notice*, God help us, and noticing is what got the last three generations of us into this."

"Into what?"

Corliss looked at the envelope. Then at the door. Then at the envelope again.

"Into the business of the Vale twins," she said.

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red murmur. Corliss had moved to the chair nearer the hearth, an old woman's instinct, and Quentin had not moved at all, which was a young person's instinct, or perhaps just Quentin's: planted, patient, immovable as furniture.

"Tobias," Corliss said. "Say it. You've already said it to yourself. I can see it."

"Tobias Vale," Quentin said quietly. "The hands in the portrait."

"You noticed the hands." She laughed, short and bitter. "Of course you did. A hundred and forty years of this town looking at that painting and one Dunmore looks up and says, *those are workingman's hands.* Edmund Vale never carried anything heavier than a fountain pen in his life. But Tobias — Tobias hauled stone. Cut it, too. There are quarry walls outside of town with his tool marks still in them, if you know where to look, and almost nobody knows where to look, because knowing where to look was the first thing they erased."

"They."

"The town. The family. Take your pick; by the end they were the same animal." Corliss turned her glass slowly on its base. "You've read the official story. Edmund Vale, sole founder, visionary, benefactor, the man on the monument, the man in the lobby, the man whose coffin they dug up on Sunday. Here's the story underneath. Edmund had a twin brother. Born eleven minutes apart, and the midwife — my great-great-grandmother, as it happens, though that's a coincidence I've only recently stopped believing in — swore they didn't even look alike. Edmund was fine-boned, quick, clever with figures. Tobias was built like the quarry he'd end up working. And when the land was bought and the town was plotted, the deed — the *original* deed, the one in the archive vault that Miriam Halloway guards like a dragon guards gold — the original deed names *both* of them."

Quentin's breath changed. Small. But Corliss had spent a lifetime reading the small changes in people; it was the only intimacy she'd ever permitted herself.

"Both," Quentin repeated.

"Edmund Vale and Tobias Vale, joint founders, equal shares. Tobias surveyed half the original plots himself. He laid the first foundation stone of the Marlowe Grand — your hotel, Quentin, the floor you've stood on for twelve years — with his own hands. And then Edmund died, and everything changed."

"Edmund died first?"

"Edmund died *young*. Thirty-eight. Fever, they said. And here is where my mother's papers stop being genealogy and start being evidence." Corliss leaned forward. The firelight made the wax seal on the envelope glow like a wound. "The deed named both brothers. But a deed is a document, and documents have keepers, and the keepers in this town have always been the same handful of families — the ones who married into the Vale line, the ones who sat on the first town council, the ones whose names are on the heritage plaques. When Edmund died, those keepers made a decision. A tidy one. They decided the town would be simpler with one founder. One name on the monument. One face on the portrait. One lineage to protect, because half of them had married into it and the other half owed it everything."

"And Tobias?"

"Tobias lived another twenty-two years." Corliss's voice had gone flat, the flatness she used for the worst facts. "He worked the quarry. He watched his brother's name go up on the monument — his own face, Quentin, they used *Tobias's* likeness for the portrait, because Edmund died before the commission was finished and Tobias sat for it as a favor, and then they hung it and labeled it Edmund and he had to walk past it every day of his life. He watched his name get chiseled off the plinth in the cemetery — you've seen the second plinth, don't pretend you haven't, I raised you, I know how you walk through graveyards. And he lived above the hotel. In the attic. Because it was the one piece of the town that was legally, undeniably, *his*, and he would not give it up, and they let him keep it because a mad old twin in an attic was easier than a lawsuit."

"The sealed attic room," Quentin said. Not a question. Corliss watched them file it away — *attic, sealed, second portrait* — and felt the familiar cold: every fact she gave them was a fact they'd act on, and acting on facts in this town had a body count that had just recently reopened.

"Quentin." She said their name the way you say *stop* to someone walking toward a ledge. "Before you go up those stairs. Hear the rest."

"Which is?"

"Which is that the erasure didn't end with Tobias. It *continued*. Every generation, someone has kept the truth alive and someone has kept it buried, and the two jobs have been passed down like heirlooms. My mother kept the papers. Her mother before her. The Fenwick name, the false names, the moving around — we weren't hiding from the town, we were hiding *in* it, in plain sight, the way you hide anything valuable: behind a lock everyone's forgotten the location of." She tapped the envelope once. "These papers name Tobias Vale as co-founder. They include a copy of the original deed, made in 1904 by a town clerk with a conscience. They include letters. Tobias's own hand. And they include a list — my mother's list, added in her writing — of everyone alive who knows the truth."

Quentin went very still. "A list."

"Seven names when my mother made it. Four when I inherited it." Corliss met their eyes. "Two as of Sunday."

* * *

The silence that followed had texture. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them the hotel breathed its night sounds — pipes, settling wood, the elevator's distant sigh — and Corliss realized she had missed this building more than she had ever missed any person in it. That was its own confession, and she wasn't going to make it.

"Who's on the list," Quentin said.

"No."

"Aunt Corliss—"

"*No.*" It came out harder than she intended, and she let it stand, hard, because softness would be a door and Quentin walked through doors. "You think I'm being cruel. I'm being *arithmetic*. Every person who knows is a person who can be reached, and someone in this town has spent the past week demonstrating exactly how they reach people. The gravedigger. The caretaker. Whoever was in Room 214 under a dead man's name. You think those are accidents? You think a coffin fills itself with river stones?"

"Then tell me the names and let me protect them."

"Protect them *how*? With your ledger?" Corliss laughed, and it hurt. "Oh, sweetheart. I love you. I have loved you badly and from a distance and I will answer for that to your mother's ghost for the rest of my life, but do not stand in my parlor and tell me a guest register is a shield. Your register is how they found the gravedigger. Your beautiful alphabet is a *map*, and maps are for hunters."

Quentin flinched. Actually flinched — the first unguarded thing Corliss had seen them do all night — and immediately she regretted it, because she'd spent twelve years not being the person who made this child flinch, and here she was, doing it with the accuracy of all the family's terrible noticing.

"That's not fair," Quentin said quietly.

"No. Very little of this is fair. That's rather the theme." Corliss rose, joints announcing themselves, and went to the window. The square was empty under the streetlamps. Across it, dark against the dark, the archive building stood with its one lit window on the top floor — the reading room, burning late, as it had burned late every night this week. "I'll tell you what I can. I'll tell you the shape of it. The papers name Tobias. The papers prove the portrait is him. The papers prove the plinth in the cemetery had his name on it, and the chisel that took it off belonged to a town council that included the great-grandfather of the woman who now runs the archive." She turned. "That's half. That's the half that's already public property the moment I hand that envelope to a lawyer or a reporter, and I will, when it's safe. The other half — the names, the *who else knows* — stays with me."

"Because you don't trust me."

"Because I don't trust *anyone*," Corliss said, "and because I love you, and those are the same sentence. The people on that list are alive because the list is quiet. The moment it isn't quiet — the moment it goes into your head, your ledger, your case, your *detective's* case — it starts killing people. You've seen it start. I'm asking you to help me keep it from finishing."

Quentin stood. Crossed to the table. Looked down at the envelope for a long moment — and Corliss watched them decide, watched the whole visible machinery of that careful mind weigh trust against duty, love against evidence, and she braced for the hand reaching out, because Quentin had never in their life left a locked box unopened.

Quentin didn't touch it.

"Keep your half," they said. "For now." And then, with the terrible gentleness that was the family weapon turned inward: "But you should know, Aunt Corliss, that someone already knows you're here. You checked in under Fenwick, and it was clever, and it almost worked. But the brandy you're drinking was sent up an hour before you asked for it. With a note."

Corliss's stomach dropped. "What note?"

Quentin took a card from their jacket pocket and laid it on the table beside the envelope. Cream stock. Archive letterhead.

It read, in a small, immaculate, cataloguer's hand:

*Welcome home, Mrs. Fenwick. The reading room is open late, whenever you're ready to make a donation. — M.H.*

And beneath it, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, as if the whole town were already filed and waiting: a call number.

Corliss stared at it, and the brandy turned to ice in her chest, because she recognized the classification scheme — her mother had taught it to her at this very table, thirty years ago — and the call number was not for a book.

It was the number her mother had always used, in the old family index, for one thing only.

*Inheritance papers, Dunmore line.*

Miriam Halloway knew exactly what was in the envelope. And she was inviting Corliss to bring it to her.
