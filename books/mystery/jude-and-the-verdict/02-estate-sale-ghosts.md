## 2. Estate Sale Ghosts

The return address on the package was a lie, but the postmark wasn't. Ashford County, North Carolina. A town I'd never heard of, which in my line of work usually means a town that has worked very hard at never being heard of.

I sat on my kitchen floor at two in the morning with the diary open on my knees and the shipping label propped against the fruit bowl like evidence at trial. The paper had that soft, gone-to-ground texture of things kept in attics for decades. The handwriting inside was small and slanted left, as if the writer were leaning away from her own words even as she set them down.

*Jude Calloway.* I said the name out loud to the empty apartment, the way you'd test an ice cube to see if it would hold your weight.

Here's what I knew, and I want to be honest about how little it was: Jude Calloway, seventeen years old, reported missing from Hollow Creek on October 14th, 1979. Never found. No body, no note, no credible sighting after the first seventy-two hours. Her file had been opened and closed so many times it had the consistency of a folded map — creased soft in all the same places until the paper itself gave up. It was, by every measure anyone had ever bothered to apply, the coldest missing-person case on record. Forty-six years of nothing.

And now a diary, mailed to me anonymously, with entries dated three weeks *after* she vanished.

I did what any reasonable person would do at 2 a.m. I made coffee I didn't need and started pulling threads. The estate-sale listing was still live on one of those aggregator sites, half-scraped, the photographs already rotting into gray placeholders. *Estate of E. Marsh, Hollow Creek.* Household goods, books, "assorted papers and ephemera." The sale had been three weeks ago. Somebody had bought a diary at an estate sale in Hollow Creek and then mailed it to me — to me specifically, to my P.O. box, with no note — three weeks later.

Which meant somebody wanted me to have it. Or wanted me to *go* there.

Either way, I was going. I've never abandoned a case. People say that like it's a virtue. Mostly it's just a way of being haunted on purpose.

* * *

Hollow Creek announced itself the way small towns do when they don't want company: a sagging welcome sign, a gas station with one working pump, and a main street that seemed to be holding its breath. I drove through it once without stopping, the way you circle a house before knocking, and counted what there was to count. A diner called Sam's, windows fogged with steam and life. A sheriff's department with two cruisers parked out front, both clean, both too new for a town this sleepy. A courthouse with a clock that had stopped at some point in the last century and nobody had minded enough to fix it.

The Marsh place was at the end of a gravel road, a farmhouse with good bones and bad paint. The estate sale was long over, but a hand-lettered sign still hung on the porch rail: EVERYTHING MUST GO — MAKE AN OFFER. Underneath, someone had added in different ink: *except family items.*

There was a woman on the porch.

She was maybe thirty-five, sitting in a rocking chair with a paperback face-down on her knee, and she watched my rental car come up the drive with the unhurried attention of someone who had been watching the road for a while. Not nervous. *Wary.* There's a difference. Nervous people look away. Wary people look *through* you.

"You're the second one," she said, before I'd even gotten both feet on the porch.

"Second one what?"

"Second person this month asking about Aunt Ev's things." She closed the paperback around her finger and stood. Up close she was taller than she'd looked, with dark hair pulled back in a knot that had given up on elegance sometime around breakfast. "The sale was weeks ago. Everything's boxed or gone. So either you're lost, or you're here about something specific."

"I'm Marguerite." I offered a hand. She looked at it for a second longer than politeness required, then shook it. Her grip was dry and firm, a woman who'd done actual work with her hands. "Marguerite Eriksen."

"Delia." No last name offered. Fair. I hadn't offered mine either, technically — well, I had, but I noticed she didn't trade. "And?"

"And I'm hoping you can help me with a provenance question." I'd rehearsed this in the car, the gentle version, the version that didn't say *your dead aunt may have been sitting on the answer to a forty-six-year-old disappearance.* "A book came out of this sale. A diary. 1979. I'm trying to trace who owned it."

Something moved behind Delia's eyes. Not surprise exactly. More like a door opening onto a room she'd already swept.

"Aunt Evaline was the county archivist," she said. "Thirty-eight years. If a piece of paper existed in this county, she knew where it slept." She tilted her head. "You're not from a university."

"No."

"You're not from the historical society — they sent a man in June, he sneezed the whole time."

"No."

"So what are you from?"

It was a fair question and I gave it the honest non-answer it deserved. "I make a podcast. True crime. I investigate old cases."

Delia rocked back on her heels. "Old cases," she repeated, tasting it. "Plural or singular?"

"Singular," I admitted. There was no point lying to a woman who could smell a lie the way I could smell a cover-up — professionally, and with a certain grim respect. "One case."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something I didn't expect: she laughed. One short, hard syllable with no humor in it whatsoever.

"Of course," she said. "Forty-six years, and now they come in pairs."

* * *

She made tea because she said her aunt would have insisted, and because, I suspected, it gave her something to do with her hands while she decided how much to tell me. The kitchen was the kind of room that gets frozen mid-life when its owner dies — a dish towel over the oven handle, a wall calendar still turned to March, a jar of pens by the window each one dried out past resurrection.

"The man who came before you," Delia said, setting a mug in front of me. "He was maybe fifty. Good coat, expensive shoes, wrong shoes for gravel roads. He said he was a collector of regional ephemera."

"Was he?"

"He was a liar." She said it flatly, without heat. "Aunt Ev taught me to read people the way she read documents. He asked about the diaries — plural. He asked whether anything from 1979 had come up in the sorting. He used the word 'acquire.' Nobody says 'acquire' unless they're buying something they don't want traced." She sat across from me. "I told him the household papers had been distributed. Which was true. What I didn't tell him is that I'd already gone through them."

My coffee went very still in my chest. "And what did you find?"

Delia got up, went to the pantry, and came back with a shoebox. Not the diary — I would have known, would have felt it, the way you feel weather changing. Just a box of odds: receipts, a funeral program, a library card.

"This was in the bottom of a filing cabinet marked PERSONAL," she said. "Aunt Ev labeled everything. Thirty-eight years of archiving, and the only box she ever marked personal was this one." She lifted out a single sheet, yellowed, folded in quarters. "A receipt from the Hollow Creek Public Library. Dated November 4th, 1979. Three weeks after Jude Calloway disappeared."

I looked at the receipt without touching it. "What does that prove?"

"Nothing," Delia said. "That's what kept me up nights. It proves nothing. Except—" she tapped the slip, "—it's signed. Evaline Marsh checked out three books that day, and the librarian who stamped it wrote a note on the back. My aunt kept notes the way other people keep grudges."

She turned it over. The handwriting was tiny, precise, archival:

*E.M. researching C. family records. Asked twice if any Calloway materials remained. Seemed upset when told the girl's school file was destroyed in the '81 flood. Said, quote, "she'll want to know it's gone."*

"She'll want to know it's gone," I repeated.

"That's the part that won't leave me alone," Delia said quietly. "Not *I* want to know. *She'll* want to know. Like my aunt was keeping a promise to somebody. Like she was holding a place in line for a person who couldn't come pick up their own paperwork."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a truck went past on the gravel road, slow, and I caught myself listening to it until the sound faded — counting, without meaning to, how long it took.

"Delia," I said. "Who bought the diary? Do you know? Did your aunt sell it at the sale, or—"

"It wasn't sold." She wrapped both hands around her mug. "It was donated. To the sale, at the last minute, by an anonymous donor. Aunt Ev was already dead by then — heart, in her sleep, very tidy. But the donation came with a card. Typed. No signature." She recited it the way you recite something you've read too many times: "*'For the one who keeps asking. She'll know what to do.'*"

"For the one who keeps asking," I said.

"My aunt spent her whole life answering questions other people were afraid to ask," Delia said. "I think somebody knew that. I think somebody knew exactly which questions she'd been sitting on." She looked at me, and for the first time since I'd arrived, the wariness in her face cracked into something rawer. "So here's my question for you, Marguerite-who-makes-a-podcast. Who else is asking? Because that man in the good coat came back yesterday. Parked at the end of the road for twenty minutes. Didn't come up. Just sat there."

The chill walked up my spine on quiet feet, the professional kind, the kind I'd learned to trust more than any source.

"Did you get his plate?"

"Partial. Mud on the back end." She tore a page from a spiral notebook and slid it across the table. "And Marguerite? When you find whoever's asking these questions alongside you — find them fast. Because in my experience, people who sit at the end of a driveway aren't deciding whether to knock."

"They're deciding whether they need to."

Delia nodded once, and picked her paperback back up, and that was clearly the end of the interview.

I drove back down the gravel road with the partial plate number burning in my pocket and the receipt's ghost-line repeating in my head. *She'll want to know it's gone.*

Somebody had been keeping Jude Calloway's secrets for forty-six years. Somebody had mailed me the diary. And somebody else — somebody with expensive shoes and mud on his bumper — was a week ahead of me and closing fast.

I checked my mirrors the whole way into town. Twice, I thought I saw headlights hold back a little too patiently behind me.

Both times, when I turned, the road behind me was empty.

That was somehow worse.
