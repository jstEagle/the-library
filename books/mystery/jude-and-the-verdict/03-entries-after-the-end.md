## 3. Entries After the End

I want to tell you that I did the responsible thing. That I finished my coffee, filed the diary in a fireproof box, and called a forensic document examiner with a clean license and no imagination.

What I actually did was sit on my kitchen floor for six hours with cold takeout beside me and read a dead girl's handwriting until sunrise.

* * *

The diary itself is unremarkable — a drugstore five-subject notebook, spiral-bound, the cardboard cover soft as cloth from handling. The kind of thing every teenager in 1979 owned and threw away by 1981. Except this one didn't get thrown away. This one got hidden, then archived, then boxed, then sold to a stranger for eleven dollars at an estate sale where I also bought a fondue set I will never use.

The early entries are what you'd expect. Jude Calloway, seventeen, writing about a boy named Danny who chewed his pencils, about a chemistry teacher who hated her, about wanting out of Hollow Creek "the way water wants downhill." Her voice was already there, even then — dry, watchful, funny in a way that made you suspect she'd been paying attention to adults for a long time.

> *March 4, 1979 — Mr. Pruitt says I have "an attitude of insubordination." I said thank you. He didn't like that either. You can't win with men who need you smaller than they are.*

> *April 12, 1979 — Mama's new boyfriend fixed the porch step and now acts like he built the house. Danny says I'm too suspicious. Danny is wrong. Being suspicious is just paying attention with your guard up.*

Then April 30th. Then nothing.

That was the entry the world knew about, if anyone knew about it at all. The last entry before Jude Calloway walked out of her mother's house on May 3rd, 1979, and became a photograph on a milk carton, a name in a file, a wound the town learned to walk around. Sheriff Eriksen — no relation, though God knows I checked — closed the case as a runaway in 1981. No body. No witnesses. No Jude.

Except.

Except that three pages after April 30th, in the same blue ink, in the same slanted hand, there was a new date.

**May 24th, 1979.**

Three weeks after she vanished.

* * *

Here is what I know about my own brain: it does not panic first. It panics second. First it goes very quiet and very precise, like a jeweler deciding whether the stone is real. So when I saw that date, I didn't scream or drop the notebook. I turned the page very slowly, the way you'd approach a dog that might be hurt or might be rabid.

> *May 24, 1979 — They think distance is the same as safety. It isn't. Distance is just a longer leash. Wrote the first page of the ledger tonight. If anything happens to me, the ledger speaks.*

No greeting. No explanation of where she was. As if the diary had simply picked up mid-sentence, mid-life, as if disappearing were something you could do between one entry and the next without breaking stride.

I told myself it was a forgery. I want to be honest about that, because everyone always asks, and because I asked myself first. Someone faking a dead girl's diary would fake closure — a goodbye, a confession, something dramatic. Nobody fakes boredom. And the next entries were boring in the most devastating way possible:

> *June 2, 1979 — Eggs again. The woman upstairs doesn't ask questions, which costs extra but is worth it.*

> *June 19, 1979 — Saw a paper with my face on it. Strange feeling, seeing yourself described by people who never looked. "Last seen wearing." I remember exactly what I was wearing. They got it wrong.*

She was alive. She was alive and she was *writing*, and whoever hid this diary — whoever kept it for forty years — had known it too.

* * *

I did eventually call someone. My producer, Renata, who has talked me off more ledges than I've climbed, and who answered on the second ring at eleven p.m. because that's the job.

"Marguerite. Please tell me you're not calling about a hunch."

"I'm calling about a diary."

"A diary."

"Jude Calloway's diary. With entries written after her disappearance."

The silence on the line had texture. I could hear her setting down whatever she was drinking. "Entries after — how long after?"

"That's the thing," I said. "They don't stop."

Because they didn't. I'd gone through the whole notebook twice by then, and the dates ran on like a pulse. June of '79 became September. September became a gap, then March of 1980. Then 1981. Then a long silence, then a burst of entries in 1986. The last dated entry was from **1994** — fifteen years after the girl in the photograph stopped existing.

Fifteen years of a life nobody knew about. Fifteen years of a missing person being, privately, found.

* * *

The problem — and I want to be clear that this is a good problem, the best problem I've ever had, and it still made my hands shake — is that most of those later entries aren't readable. Not physically; the ink's fine, the paper's fine. Readable in the sense that they're written in code.

Not a cipher with a key taped inside the cover, which would've been considerate. Jude Calloway wrote her private business in a system of her own invention: numbers standing in for names, dates shifted by some rule I couldn't find, and recurring phrases that clearly meant more than they said. *The ledger.* *The ones who signed.* *The gavel page.* Over and over, like a refrain.

> *Sept. 8, 1979 — Started the third section of the ledger today. Four names now. Four signatures. I keep thinking they'll feel guilty enough to come find me themselves. They won't. Guilt is a luxury for people who aren't afraid.*

> *Nov. 30, 1979 — The ones who signed think I'm gone. Let them. A witness nobody believes is safer than a witness everybody's watching. I'll write it all down instead. Paper keeps its nerve better than people do.*

> *Feb. 14, 1980 — Valentine's Day and I spent it cross-referencing. Happy Valentine's Day to me. The gavel page is nearly complete. When it's done, the ledger will say everything I can't.*

The ledger. The ones who signed. A gavel page.

I sat back on my kitchen floor — I had migrated there sometime around two a.m., apparently under the belief that proximity to linoleum aids cognition — and said it out loud to my empty apartment.

"She wasn't running from something. She was building a case."

* * *

You have to understand what that did to me. Nine cases. Nine stories where I found the truth and handed it to the light, and in every single one, the victim was a victim — a person things happened *to*, a shape other people's choices were cut around. I've built a career on giving victims back their voices, and I believed, right down to my bones, that's what this would be. Another voice recovered. Another silence filled.

But Jude Calloway hadn't waited for anyone to give her a voice. She'd taken one. She'd disappeared — the most silenced thing a person can become — and turned her own disappearance into an archive. She wasn't the subject of a true-crime story.

She was the author of one. She'd been writing mine before I was born.

I felt vertigo, actual vertigo, the kitchen tilting a few degrees off true. Because if she lived until 1994 — if she was alive, somewhere, keeping records, naming names — then the question of this case wasn't *what happened to Jude Calloway?*

It was *what did Jude Calloway know?*

And the uglier question underneath it, the one I circled all night and finally wrote in the margin of my notes in capital letters:

**WHO WAS SHE HIDING IT FROM?**

* * *

The coded entries got denser over the years. Early on she mixed plain text with cipher, almost careless — a girl testing a lock she'd just built. Later, after the gaps, she came back stricter, more disciplined, like someone who'd learned the hard way what loose paper costs.

> *Aug. 17, 1986 — Back at it. Eleven years. The ledger survived, which is more than I expected of anything. Added the fifth signature tonight. It cost me something to write it, but not as much as it would cost to leave it out.*

> *Oct. 3, 1986 — Some days I want to burn it all and be nobody forever. But nobody is what they made me. The ledger is what I made myself. Keep going.*

> *June 21, 1994 — Last entry in this book. The ledger is finished. All the names. All the signatures. Everything they did and everything they paid and everything they thought they buried. It's done. It's safe. It's closer than they'll ever look.*

And then, below that, in slightly larger letters, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

> **If you're reading this, you're either the one I trusted or the one I warned. Figure out which.**

I read that line maybe forty times. I'm reading it now, sitting in my kitchen with the notebook open and the morning news murmuring about nothing, and I still can't tell you which one I am.

* * *

Renata called back at noon. "Okay. I've been thinking about it all night, which means you owe me a spa day. Tell me the bad news."

"The diary came from an estate sale," I said. "In Hollow Creek."

"You went back to the town?"

"On paper. The sale listing was from an estate liquidator — the deceased was a man named Walter Pruett. Local historian type. Boxes of county records, personal papers, that kind of thing."

"And the diary was in his stuff."

"The diary was in a box labeled *school ephemera,*" I said, "which is either carelessness or camouflage, and given everything else, I'm betting camouflage."

Renata was quiet a moment. "So this archivist had a missing girl's diary, with proof she lived for fifteen years, and he just... sat on it?"

"Or protected it," I said. "There's a difference. People who sit on evidence are hiding it from everyone. People who protect it are hiding it from *someone specific.*"

"You think he knew who she was hiding from."

"I think," I said, looking at the last entry again, at that hard-pressed final line, "*closer than they'll ever look* — I think Jude told him exactly where to keep it, and he kept it for thirty years, and now he's dead and it's in my kitchen."

"So we go public. We announce it. Marguerite Eriksen cracks the coldest file in the state—"

"No."

The word came out of me flat and fast, faster than my own reasoning, and it surprised both of us. I covered by taking a sip of coffee that had gone cold hours ago.

"No?" Renata said. "This is the story of your career. This is the story of *anyone's* career."

"I know what it is." I looked at the window. Somebody's kid was shrieking happily in the courtyard below, and the sound seemed to come from another century. "Renata, someone signed things. Plural. Signatures imply authority, and authority in a town like Hollow Creek in 1979 implies — judges, sheriffs, families with their names on buildings. Some of those people might still be alive. Their *children* are definitely alive. And the last person who held this diary just died quietly in a house full of boxes."

"You think it's connected."

"I think I don't believe in coincidences that convenient. An archivist dies, his estate gets sold, and within weeks the one item that could blow open a forty-year-old conspiracy lands in the hands of a podcaster known for digging up old bones." I turned the notebook toward the window light, though there was nothing more to see in it. "Either the universe is generous, or somebody wanted this diary found. And I haven't decided which possibility scares me more."

* * *

That night I started on the cipher in earnest, and I want to describe what that was like, because people imagine codebreaking as drama — lightning strikes, sudden revelations. Mostly it's tedium with occasional religious experiences.

Jude's system used recurring symbols in the margins — small marks, easy to dismiss as doodles. Stars, mostly. Little crosses. And one symbol that appeared at the head of every encrypted passage, drawn with unusual care: a small rectangle atop a short stem.

A gavel.

Every coded section began with a gavel. Every mention of *the ones who signed* clustered near one. Whatever the key was, it lived in that little drawing — and I stared at it so long Tuesday night that when I closed my eyes I saw gavels on the inside of my eyelids, a courtroom of them, rapping for order.

By Wednesday I had fragments. Numbers that resolved into years. A phrase that decoded, partially, beautifully, to *HE SIGNED TWICE* — though whether *he* was a judge, a sheriff, or someone else entirely, I couldn't yet say. And one full sentence, cracked near midnight, that I copied onto a fresh index card and propped against my salt shaker like a trophy:

**THE LEDGER NAMES EVERYONE WHO MADE ME DISAPPEAR.**

Everyone. Not *something*. Not *circumstances*. Everyone.

Jude Calloway hadn't run away in 1979. She'd been erased — deliberately, by multiple hands, by people with the power to sign things — and she had spent the rest of her hidden life assembling the receipt.

I photographed every page twice, backed it up in three places, and put the original in the fireproof box. Then I booked a flight.

Because here's the thing about a ledger: it's only half a document. A ledger records debts, but somebody has to collect them. And every clue I had — the estate, the archivist, the box labeled *school ephemera*, the phrase *closer than they'll ever look* — pointed to the same small town that had smiled at its own mystery for forty years.

Hollow Creek had kept Jude's secret for four decades.

I intended to find out whether it had kept it willingly.

My flight left Friday morning. On Thursday night, an envelope arrived in my mailbox with no return address. Inside was a single sheet of paper, and printed on it in ordinary black type were the words:

**SOME ARCHIVES BURN. ASK DELIA BEFORE YOU FLY.**

I stood in my doorway holding it for a long time, listening to the courtyard below, wondering how anyone knew I was flying at all.

Then I looked up the name. Delia. Listed in the estate sale records as the niece of the deceased — the one family member who'd handled the sale, the one who'd signed the inventory.

Signed.

I packed lighter than I meant to, and I slept worse than I planned, and in the morning I flew toward a town that had already started sending me mail.
