## 8. A Friend in the Dark

The diary again that night, third cup of tea gone cold at her elbow, Sable Halloran sat cross-legged on her sofa with the water-stained book open across her knees like something fragile and alive.

She had learned to read it in order now. The early entries were a girl's entries—complaints about the Wi-Fi at Vale House, which Juniper described as "possessed by the ghost of dial-up," and affectionate exasperation about her grandmother's habit of hiding butterscotch candies in the lining of her handbag "so I'll find them after she's gone, which she says casually, like she's talking about weather." There were jokes. There was a running tally of how many times the gardener said "in my day" per week (the record was eleven). There was a boy from the feed store whom Juniper liked and refused to admit liking, on the grounds that "wanting things is how they get taken away," which Sable had read three times before understanding it wasn't a joke at all.

Then came the entry dated the fourteenth of March, and the diary changed key.

*Grandmother asked me to catalogue the east wing while she still remembers where everything is. She calls it "putting the house to bed." Today I found the room behind the linen closet—the one Father pretends is a plumbing chase. It isn't plumbing.*

Sable turned the page slowly, as if the paper might startle.

*It's ledgers. Dozens of them. Burial records going back to 1919, all in the same careful hand, generation after generation. I thought Grandfather kept them because he was sentimental. Then I noticed the columns. Dates of death that don't match any stone I've ever walked past. Amounts. Initials. It's not a record of who died. It's a record of what dying was worth.*

There was a smudge there, water or tears, impossible to tell.

*I took photographs. I know I shouldn't have. My hands wouldn't stop shaking so half of them are blurry, but I got the important pages. And then I did the stupidest, most human thing imaginable: I told someone.*

Sable set down the diary and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

The next entry was dated two days later, and its handwriting had already begun its slow deterioration—the letters leaning, the ink pressed harder, as though the girl writing was trying to anchor herself to the page.

*Ruthie. Of course it was Ruthie. Who else? We've been friends since we were six years old and she bit Danny Pruitt for pulling my hair. I told her everything, and she didn't laugh, and she didn't tell me I was imagining it, which is why I love her. She said one word over and over: run. Run, run, run. She said, "Junie, you don't owe that house your life. You don't owe anybody anything. Get out while you can still get out."*

*I said I couldn't leave Grandma. Ruthie said, "You can't help her if you're dead."*

*She's right. That's the worst part. She's right and I hate it.*

Sable read the name twice. *Ruthie.*

She knew exactly one Ruthie in Hollow Creek. Everyone did. Ruthie Calloway ran the town library the way some people run lighthouses—steadily, quietly, with the fixed conviction that drowning was preventable if you just kept the light on. Ruthie had stamped out Sable's library card personally, twenty-two years ago, when Sable was nine and checking out a book on forensic pathology that made her mother deeply uneasy. Ruthie had looked at the small grim girl and said, "Good choice. Somebody has to ask the questions."

Somebody has to ask the questions. And here was the answer to every question Sable had been carrying for a week, sitting behind a circulation desk four blocks from the morgue, wearing cardigans and recommending mysteries to tourists.

Unless the diary was lying. Unless the whole thing was an elaborate fiction planted in a flooded crypt by someone with a grudge and a fountain pen.

But the handwriting matched the medical files. Sable had verified that herself, late one night, with the particular cold satisfaction of a woman whose skepticism had been confirmed into something much worse than doubt.

Which meant Juniper Vale had told her oldest friend she was going to run—and then vanished—and Ruthie Calloway had spent a year shelving books and saying nothing.

Sable closed the diary. Outside, rain had started again, soft against the windows, the sound Hollow Creek made when it wanted to pretend it was innocent.

"Okay, Juniper," she said to the empty room. "Let's go see your friend."

* * *

The Hollow Creek Public Library occupied a converted grange hall with good bones and bad heating, and it smelled the way all the best libraries smell: paper, dust, wood polish, and the faint ghost of decades of children's glue. Ruthie Calloway presided over the front desk with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair, stamping returns with the unhurried rhythm of a woman who had never once been rushed by anything, including time itself.

"Sable Halloran." Ruthie's face opened into genuine warmth. "Twice in one year. People will talk."

"People should. It'd be the most interesting gossip this town's had since the Hendersons' divorce."

"The Hendersons' divorce was forty years ago and we're still not done with it." Ruthie slid a stack of returns aside. "What brings the coroner among the living?"

"I need the county archive access. The microfilm reader's been down at the courthouse since—" Sable waved vaguely. "Since the Nixon administration, I think."

"It's been down since Tuesday. The courthouse calls that maintenance." Ruthie was already reaching for a sign-in sheet. "Historical census? Land records? I can pull most of it myself if you tell me what you're after."

"Land records. The flood parcels. Nineteen nineteen."

Something happened to Ruthie's face. It was very small, and Sable would have missed it entirely if she hadn't spent thirty years watching faces for a living—a flicker, a settling, like a house adjusting on its foundation. It lasted less than a second, and then Ruthie was smiling again, warm as the radiator that didn't work.

"Nineteen nineteen. That's a grim subject for a Thursday."

"Grim subjects are my whole personality."

"Mm." Ruthie wrote something on the sheet. Her pen didn't waver. "I'll have the reels pulled by Monday. Anything else?"

Yes, thought Sable. Yes, actually. I'd like to know why a woman who has known me since I was nine years old is suddenly handling me like a stranger at the counter. I'd like to know why your hand went to the desk drawer when I said *flood*, and why it came back empty. I'd like to know if you helped a girl disappear, and whether you've spent twelve months being brave or twelve months being afraid, and whether those turned out to be the same thing.

"No, that's all," Sable said. "Thanks, Ruthie."

"Anytime." And then, as Sable reached the door, in a voice pitched perfectly casual, perfectly light: "Oh—Sable? How's the Vale matter coming? The missing person file. People ask."

Sable turned back. Ruthie stood behind the desk with her hands folded, the picture of neighborly interest, and behind the glasses her eyes were doing something complicated.

"Cold," Sable said. "Going colder."

"That's a shame." A pause, measured to the millimeter. "She was such a sweet girl. So fond of books. She used to come in every Saturday, her and that friend of hers—Ruthie, I mean. Me." She laughed at herself, a small self-deprecating sound. "Old habit, referring to myself in the third person. Juniper and I spent many Saturdays right there in the history section. She loved local history. Cemeteries especially. Did you know that?"

"I didn't," Sable said.

"Oh, yes. She said graves were the only honest records a town keeps." Ruthie smiled. "Sweet girl. Well. Monday, then. I'll have those reels waiting."

Sable walked out into the rain and stood under the awning for a long moment, getting thoroughly wet, thinking.

*Graves are the only honest records a town keeps.* Juniper had written almost exactly that in the diary—Sable could nearly quote it: *I walk the cemetery and read the stones like a ledger, because that's what they are.* Either the two women shared a private language, built over years of Saturdays—which they plainly had—or Ruthie was quoting the diary back at her deliberately, a message wrapped in nostalgia, testing what Sable knew.

Or warning her. Or asking her, in the only way a woman under watch could ask, to look closer.

Or none of the above, said the tired voice of professional discipline, and you're seeing conspiracies in a librarian's small talk because you haven't slept properly in six days.

Sable got in her car and sat with the engine off, watching the library windows through the rain. Ruthie moved behind them, reshelving, steady as a metronome. At one point she paused, looked toward the door—at Sable's car, unmistakably, through the fogged glass—and stood there for three full seconds before turning away.

Three seconds. Long enough to be nothing. Long enough to be everything.

* * *

That night, the diary gave her more.

Sable had stopped pretending she could read it in doses. It had become a nightly appointment, the way other people watched the news—except the news didn't make your chest hurt.

*March 21st.* The handwriting was worse now. *Ruthie came to the house today, first time in months. She brought a book she thought I'd like and inside the dust jacket was a bus schedule and four hundred dollars in twenties. She didn't say a word about it. Just handed me the book and asked about Grandma. We talked about Grandma for forty minutes and the whole time the money sat between us like a third person in the room.*

*I tried to give it back. She wouldn't take it. She said, "You'll need it more than the library needs its bake sale fund," and then she cried in the car before she drove off, and I stood at the window watching until the taillights went, and I thought: this is what it costs to be loved by someone honest. They won't let you lie to yourself.*

*She's scared for me. Not of me—for me. There's a difference. I keep having to explain that to myself lately, because everyone else in my life seems to have gotten the two confused.*

Sable lowered the book.

So Ruthie had known. Had begged her to run, had funded the running, had wept in a parked car—and had then spent a year answering questions about Juniper Vale with a smoothness that Sable now recognized, with a fresh and unwelcome sting, as rehearsed. Because Sable herself had interviewed Ruthie Calloway eleven months ago, official procedure, missing persons case, everyone in the deceased girl's orbit gets a turn. And Ruthie had sat in the morgue's visitor chair with her cardigan buttoned to the throat and said, *I hadn't seen Juniper in weeks before she disappeared. We'd drifted, the way adult friendships do. I couldn't imagine why she'd run off; she adored her grandmother.*

Drifted. Four hundred dollars in a dust jacket. A bus schedule. Forty minutes discussing a dying woman while escape money sat between them.

Sable had believed her. Had written *no significant findings* in her notes and moved on, because Ruthie Calloway had stamped out her first library card and taught her that questions mattered, and you didn't suspect people like that. You didn't suspect the lighthouse.

 betrayal, she discovered, didn't feel like anger. It felt like this: sitting alone at midnight with a dead girl's diary, realizing that the one grown-up in your childhood who told you somebody has to ask the questions had looked you in the eye and declined to answer any of them.

And underneath the sting, quieter and more persistent, another thought. One Sable kept trying to shelve and it kept falling back off the shelf:

Ruthie had lied to protect Juniper. Which meant Ruthie might know—might *still* know—where Juniper was. Which meant the girl might be alive, and the only person alive who could confirm it was currently recommending large-print westerns to retirees four blocks away.

* * *

She went back the next morning. Saturday, because the diary said Saturdays belonged to the two of them, and because Sable had learned that people tell the truth in their rituals even when they lie everywhere else.

The library was quiet, rain still working the roof. Ruthie was in the history section, on a step stool, returning a volume to a high shelf. She heard the footsteps and spoke without turning around.

"You came back for the land records. I said Monday."

"I came back for something else."

Ruthie descended the stool slowly, the book hugged to her chest like a shield, and when she turned, her face had put itself carefully back together—but Sable had seen the seam.

"And what would that be?" Ruthie asked.

Sable held up the diary. Water-stained, swollen, unmistakable.

The book in Ruthie's arms slipped. She caught it against the shelf, hard, and for a moment the librarian of Hollow Creek stood absolutely still in the aisle of local history, surrounded by a century of the town's tidy official memory, and looked at the diary the way people look at graves.

"Where," Ruthie said, and her voice had lost all its warmth, all its polish, "did you get that."

"Flooded crypt. Vale plot. Someone hid it there and the spring thaw gave it up." Sable stepped closer. "Entries continue nineteen days past the date she disappeared, Ruthie. Nineteen days. Written in a hand I've verified against her medical files. The last entry mentions the anniversary dinner. And in between, there's a woman named Ruthie who stuffed four hundred dollars into a dust jacket and told her best friend to run."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the building, a radiator knocked twice and gave up, as it always did.

"You interviewed me," Ruthie said finally. Quietly. "Eleven months ago. You sat right there in that awful green chair and you asked me if I'd seen her, and I said no."

"I remember."

"Why do you suppose I said no, Sable?" And there it was—not guilt, not quite defiance, but something rawer, older, the voice of a woman who had bitten Danny Pruitt once and had never entirely regretted it. "Think hard. You're supposed to be the one who asks questions. Why would I lie to the coroner of Hollow Creek about the whereabouts of Edmund Vale's granddaughter?"

"Because you're loyal."

"Because I'm *alive*," Ruthie said. "Loyal is what I call it at night so I can sleep. But yes. Both." She set the fallen book back on the shelf with exaggerated care, aligning its spine, buying herself a second. "You've read the diary. You've seen what that family does to records. What makes you think they treat people any differently?"

"So you know where she is."

"I know that she's safe." Something flickered across Ruthie's face—regret, immediately caged. "I know that she's safer than she'd be in any room containing her father. And I know that I have kept one secret for one year, and I will keep it one more day, one more week, however long it takes, because the alternative is a headstone with her name on it that nobody ordered and nobody can remove."

"One more day until what?"

Ruthie looked at her then, fully, and Sable saw the exhaustion underneath—the year of it, carried alone behind a circulation desk.

"Until it doesn't matter anymore," Ruthie said. "Ask me again after the anniversary dinner. If you still want to know. If you're still—" she hesitated on the word, chose it anyway— "*safe* to ask."

"I'm always safe to ask. I'm the safest person in this town."

"Yes," Ruthie said softly. "That's what she said too. That's why the diary exists at all, Sable. Why do you think it ended up in *your* morgue?" She picked up her cart of returns and began to push it down the aisle, away from her, and paused only once, without turning around. "Monday. The land reels. Pull the thread. But do it fast—because I'm not the only one who knows what you've got, and the people who buried those records in 1919 didn't stop keeping secrets when the floodwater dried."

The cart wheels squeaked away into the stacks, and Sable stood alone in the history section with the diary in her hands, understanding at last that the friend in the dark had not betrayed her at all.

She had been pointing her toward the light the entire time—and warning her, in the only language left to her, that she was not the only one following.
