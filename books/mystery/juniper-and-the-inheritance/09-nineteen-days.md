## 9. Nineteen Days

*From the recovered diary of Juniper Vale. Entries transcribed in full; spelling and emphasis preserved.*

* * *

**Day One — the day I died, according to everyone but me**

If you are reading this, then either Ruthie kept her promise or I am a very poor judge of character, which would be embarrassing, because judging character is supposed to be my one inherited talent. The Vales are famous for it. We can smell a lie at forty paces. It's how we got rich.

I am writing this in the east wing linen closet, by flashlight, sitting on a stack of monogrammed hand towels that cost more than Mara makes in a month. I checked the invoice. I do the invoices now. That's the joke of it — Edmund Vale's granddaughter, cataloguing his silver like a hired girl, and he can't even be bothered to learn my name when he barks for coffee.

Here is what I found, so that it exists somewhere outside my own head, because inside my own head it has started to feel like something I dreamed:

There is a ledger hidden in the burial records of St. Ambrose Cemetery. Not beside them. *Inside* them. The gravestones are the arithmetic.

I found it Tuesday, cataloguing Grandmother's papers for the estate inventory. A folder marked FLOOD RELIEF, 1923, which sounded charitable until I noticed the relief went entirely to one family, and the family was us. Forty-one people drowned when the Vale dam failed. Forty-one graves went into the ground fast and cheap. And forty-one entries in the burial register that don't add up — dates of death recorded on days the register itself says no bodies arrived. Birth years and death years on the stones, read as columns, form account numbers. I checked three against the charity filings in the library's back room. They're real accounts. They've been real for a hundred years.

My family launders money through dead children, and the town mows the grass over it every summer and calls it heritage.

I have not told anyone. I have told no one. I am writing it here because Dr. Okafor says — said, before she moved away — that a fear spoken aloud is half its size. This is me speaking. This is me, at two in the morning, on towels worth four hundred dollars, trying to make a thing smaller.

Tomorrow I will decide what to do. Tonight I will sit here until my legs stop shaking, because if I walk past the portrait of Great-Grandfather Ambrose looking like this, he'll know. He has that kind of face.

* * *

**Day Three**

I told Ruthie.

I want to record, for posterity, exactly how this happened, because I did not plan it. I had planned to tell no one. But Ruthie came by with the new library acquisitions list — she still brings me things to read, as though I'm a patron and not a ghost who dusts — and she found me in the study with the flood folder open, and she looked at my face and said, "Juniper Vale, you look like you've seen your own obituary."

"Close," I said. "I've seen the accounting."

She sat down. She read every page twice. She didn't cry, which I expected, and she didn't disbelieve me, which I didn't. When she finished she was quiet for a long moment and then she said, in the voice she uses for story hour, gentle and terrible: "Your grandfather killed those people."

"The dam was old. Negligent, maybe—"

"Forty-one people, Juniper. And then they billed God for the funeral." She tapped the ledger page. "This isn't negligence. This is a business model."

That is the thing about Ruthie Calloway. Everyone thinks librarians are soft. Librarians are archivists of everything humanity has ever done to itself. She has read the whole menu.

We argued for an hour about what to do. She wanted the state attorney general. I wanted — I don't know what I wanted. To unfind it. To put the folder back and go back to being a girl whose worst problem was that Edmund called her "the boy" when he was angry.

Then she asked the question that ended the argument: "Who does Edmund trust?"

"Nobody."

"Wrong. He trusts the sheriff, because he owns him. He trusts the bank, because he owns it. Who else?"

I thought about it. And the honest answer, the answer that made my stomach drop through the floorboards, was: *he trusts that nobody would dare.* He trusts the size of his name. Which means the moment anyone — anyone at all — hands a folder to the wrong person, I stop being a Vale and start being a problem, and Vales solve problems the way their dam solved the river.

"He'd have to know it was me," I said.

"Would he? Or would any missing granddaughter do?" Ruthie said it very quietly, the way you say the true awful things. "Juniper. If you stay in this house holding this folder, you are already gone. The only choice you get is whether you get to keep breathing somewhere he isn't."

So we're going to do it. God help me. We're going to make him right.

* * *

**Day Six**

Ruthie's plan, which she laid out on the study floor using chess pieces and a salt shaker to represent the sheriff (Edmund got the black queen, obviously; I got a pawn, which felt pointed until she explained the pawn is the only piece that gets to become something else):

One. I copy everything. Originals stay hidden where Edmund would never look — she wouldn't say where, and I've learned not to ask Ruthie questions she's decided not to answer. It's like asking a cat where it goes at night.

Two. I mail fragments to myself, poste restante, under a name we haven't picked yet. If anything happens to me, the paper trail exists outside Hollow Creek, dated, postmarked, undeniable.

Three. On the nineteenth — the night of the harvest dinner, when the whole house will be drunk on Edmund's sherry and its own reflection — I walk out the garden door and stop existing.

"You understand," Ruthie said, "that once you're gone, you stay gone. No calling your grandmother. No waving at Mara from a car window. Grief is a door that only swings one way."

"I've been practicing," I said, and she laughed, and then we both stopped laughing, because it was true. I have been practicing being dead all week. Eating less at dinner so no one notices my absence later. Speaking more softly, so the silence afterward won't seem strange. Yesterday I stood in front of the hall mirror and tried to imagine the house without me in it, and do you know what I saw? Nothing changed. The mirror didn't even fog. That should have frightened me. Instead it made me furious, and fury, I am learning, is a much better fuel than fear. Fear makes you hide under things. Fury makes you build things.

Also, small note for whoever finds this: the good scissors are in the third drawer of the map cabinet, and I have taken them, and I am not sorry. Edmund, if you're somehow reading this: the scissors were mine by rights of labor. I've reorganized your entire archive and you never once said thank you.

* * *

**Day Nine**

Copied the last pages tonight. My hand hurts. There is something almost funny about it — a hundred years of Vales signing their names to other people's ruin in beautiful fountain-pen script, and here's the fourth generation, hunched over a copying pencil in a cold study, forging their ancestors' arithmetic into evidence.

I mailed the first packet today from the post office in Millbrook, twenty miles out, wearing Ruthie's gray coat and a scarf up to my eyes. The clerk didn't look at me twice. I have decided to take this as a compliment rather than an insult regarding my natural charisma.

The name I sent it under: **J. Marsh**. For the marsh at the bottom of the Vale property, where the floodwater pooled in 1923 before it took the town. Edmund thinks the marsh is wasted land. Let it hold my mail.

On the drive back I passed the cemetery. I made Ruthie stop. I walked the rows of the flood graves — forty-one stones, most so weathered you can barely read them, and now I know why they were cut shallow, why the dates sit in neat little columns, why the mason was paid double per stone according to the estate ledgers. They weren't memorials. They were filing cabinets.

I stood there in the wind and I apologized to them. All forty-one. Out loud, like a lunatic. "You were people," I said. "You were people and they turned you into bookkeeping."

And then — this is the part I'll deny if anyone asks — I promised them I'd fix it. Me, a pawn, armed with stolen scissors and a fake name. It felt absurd and it felt holy and I cried the whole way home, and Ruthie pretended to need the windshield wipers on a dry day, which is the kindest lie anyone has ever told me.

* * *

**Day Twelve**

Complication. Of course there's a complication; there's always a complication; life is a novel written by someone being paid by the word.

Mara caught me.

Not with the ledger — thank God, the ledger lives in the map cabinet behind the survey maps of land nobody surveys. She caught me at three in the morning coming out of the linen closet with a flashlight in my mouth like a burglar raccoon. I stood there in my nightgown with towel-creases printed on my cheek and prepared to invent the world's worst excuse, and Mara looked at me for a long moment and said:

"You sleep down here some nights. I've known since October."

"You— what?"

"I straighten the towels." She took the flashlight out of my mouth, turned it off, and handed it back with the dignity of a woman returning a sword. "Whatever you're doing, Miss Juniper, you're doing it badly. Eat more at dinner. You've lost weight, and thin girls get talked about in this house."

And then she left. Just — left. Back down the corridor, footsteps even as a metronome.

I don't know what she knows. I don't know what she suspects. But here is what I noticed, standing in the dark with my heart going like a woodpecker: she said *Miss Juniper*. Not "the girl." Not "the boy." My actual name, in her actual mouth, like it had been waiting there the whole time.

I think Mara might be the first person in this house to see me in years. Isn't that something. It takes planning your own funeral to finally get introduced.

* * *

**Day Fifteen**

Rehearsed the disappearance tonight with Ruthie. We walked the route from the garden door to the tree line to the drainage culvert to the road where her car waits, twice, once in the dark. In the dark it took eleven minutes. Eleven minutes between being Juniper Vale and being nobody at all. I timed it on Edmund's own pocket watch, which felt appropriately vindictive.

At the culvert I asked her the question I've been carrying around like a stone: "What if I'm bad at being gone? What if I leave and I'm just — sad, forever, in a rented room somewhere?"

Ruthie considered this with the seriousness she gives to everything, including whether to shelve Steinbeck under S or St. Then she said: "You won't be sad forever. You'll be angry for a while, and then useful, and then maybe happy. Sadness is just anger that hasn't found its paperwork yet."

"That's not a real quote."

"It is now. I'm a librarian. I canonize things."

We laughed until we had to lean on each other in the culvert, two shadows giggling over a storm drain, and for one whole minute I wasn't afraid. I'm writing that down so it stays true.

* * *

**Day Seventeen**

Left the diary's first section where it will be found.

This was Ruthie's idea and it is the part I argued against hardest, because it means letting people grieve me. It means Grandmother — no. I can't write about Grandmother yet. Ask me again when I'm someone else.

But Ruthie was right, the way she's usually right, which is infuriating. "A girl who vanishes is a scandal," she said. "A girl who leaves a diary is a mystery, and mysteries get *investigated*. You need someone to pull the thread after you're gone — someone stubborn, someone honest, someone who checks things. Not the sheriff. Not the paper. Someone who reads closely."

"Like who?"

"Like the coroner," she said, and shrugged, as if she hadn't just rearranged my entire future. "Sable Halloran signed off on paperwork for our family once — refused to fudge a date, back when your uncle wanted a death recorded early for the insurance. Edmund hates her for it. Do you know how rare it is, in this town, for Edmund to hate someone for being *honest*? That's not an enemy, Juniper. That's an instrument. Leave the diary where the water will find it, and let the water give it to her."

So tomorrow night, the diary goes into the flooded crypt in its oilcloth, weighted, positioned to surface. And the entries continue past the end — past the date I'm supposed to die — because Ruthie says a coroner will check the handwriting against records, and a coroner who checks will wonder, and a coroner who wonders will come looking, and by then I'll have left her a trail of bread crumbs made of arithmetic.

I am going to be unfindable, and findable, both at once. Gone from every room, present on every page. Edmund taught me one thing after all: how to hide things in plain sight. He just never imagined I'd use it on him.

* * *

**Day Nineteen — the last entry I will write as myself**

It's nearly midnight. The dinner is Saturday. In two days I walk out the garden door and the world loses a Vale and gains, somewhere far from here, a tired young woman named J. Marsh who is very good at archives and better at grudges.

Inventory of what I carry out with me: one coat (gray, borrowed), one envelope of cash (Ruthie's, to be repaid with interest, she can invoice me), one copy of the complete ledger sewn into the lining, one photograph of Grandmother that I will not describe further because I will cry on the ink.

Inventory of what I leave behind: my name, my bed, my grandmother's hand in mine at breakfast pretending not to tremble, and this book — this book, which has heard everything, which is going to float up out of the dark and speak for me when I can't.

To whoever reads this: check the stones. Count the columns. Ask the coroner — she's the one who couldn't be bought, so she's the one who'll listen.

And to Edmund, if it ever comes to this: you built your fortune on forty-one graves and thought the water would keep your secrets. But water is patient, Grandfather. Water always comes back up.

Saturday night, the garden door. Eleven minutes to nobody.

Watch me disappear.

* * *

The final entry ends there, mid-page, the handwriting steady to the last line — steadier, Sable Halloran would later note, than in any entry before it. As if the girl had finally stopped being afraid.

As if she had somewhere to be.
