## 16. Theo's Bargain

Theo Vale had rehearsed this conversation in the shower, in the car, and once—unforgivably—in front of the bathroom mirror, where he'd watched his own face attempt the expression of a man who was not about to do something shameful.

The mirror had not believed him. He could only hope Sable Halloran would be less perceptive than a piece of glass.

She wasn't coming to him, of course. That was the problem with coroners: they had offices, and morgues, and a professional disinclination to be summoned anywhere by anyone named Vale. So he'd engineered the meeting the way his father had taught him to engineer everything—indirectly, expensively, and with plausible deniability. A county records request that required her signature in person. A courier who "happened" to deliver it at four forty-five on a Friday, when the courthouse emptied and the light over Hollow Creek went the color of weak tea.

Which is how he came to be standing in the parking lot behind the county annex, holding an envelope that weighed far more than paper should, watching Sable Halloran walk toward him with the unhurried gait of a woman who had never once been late and never once hurried on anyone else's account.

"Mr. Vale." She stopped a polite six feet away. Rain had threatened all afternoon and hadn't committed; her hair carried the gray frizz of weather that couldn't make up its mind. "Your office said there were documents."

"There are." He held up the envelope. "Though I'll confess the records request was mostly a pretext."

"I know," she said. "The parcel number you cited belongs to a gravel pit that flooded in 1987. Nobody's requested those records since the flood."

*Damn.* He'd spent twenty minutes on that detail. "You checked."

"It's my job to check things. It's going very well for me lately, actually. Busy season." Her eyes were steady and dark and entirely without warmth, but also without cruelty. She looked like a woman reading a chart. He supposed, to her, he was one.

"Sable," he said, and then winced, because she hadn't invited the first name. "Dr. Halloran. Can we—do you have somewhere to be?"

"The morgue."

"Is someone... in it?"

"Not yet. But it's Friday, and this town has a relationship with liquor and lake water that I've learned to respect." She glanced at the envelope. "Say what you came to say, Mr. Vale. The light's dying and I don't like standing in parking lots with men from your family. Nothing personal. Recent history."

He laughed before he could stop himself—a short, startled bark—and saw something flicker across her face that might have been approval. Or indigestion. With Halloran it was genuinely difficult to tell.

* * *

They ended up, absurdly, at Dottie's Diner, because it was the only place open past five that wasn't a bar, and because Theo had calculated that a public place would look innocent. He realized within ninety seconds that he had miscalculated: Dottie's at dinnertime meant half the town drifting past their booth, doing the small double-take that said *Vale boy and the coroner*, and by Monday there would be three versions of why, none of them accurate.

Sable ordered coffee and a slice of cherry pie. "For dinner," she said, when he raised an eyebrow. "I'm a grown woman with a dangerous job. I eat pie when I want."

"That's fair."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *ordering*," Theo said, and ordered the meatloaf, because it seemed like the kind of thing an honest man would order, and then hated himself for thinking in those terms.

When the food came and the waitress retreated, he set the envelope on the table between them, square, like a chess move.

"Do you know what my father is going to do at the anniversary dinner?" he asked.

"I have some theories."

"He's going to toast the dead," Theo said. "All of them. The forty-one names nobody says out loud anymore. And he's going to do it smiling, because that's what he does—he launders everything through ceremony. Grief into prestige. Crime into heritage. He's been doing it for fifty years and this town claps every time." He turned the envelope over once, twice, unable to leave it still. "And if you stand up at that dinner with whatever you've found—and I know you've found things, Dr. Halloran, you have the face of a woman carrying a filing cabinet—it won't just end him. It'll end Juniper."

The fork paused halfway to her mouth.

"Go on," she said quietly.

"She's alive." He said it barely above the clatter of the diner, leaning in. "You know it. I've known it since March. And my father doesn't. Not for certain—he suspects, which is worse, because suspicion makes him careful. If she surfaces now, before the estate transfer clears, he doesn't call the sheriff. Sheriff Dawes keeps his boat running on Vale fuel. He calls people whose names don't appear on any paperwork. People who are good at making problems become missing persons." He swallowed. "Juniper has been hiding inside my own house for months. Sweeping floors. Wearing her hair up. My father walks past her every day and doesn't see her, because he's never once looked at a servant in his life. It's the safest place in the county for her, and it stays safe exactly as long as nothing happens until the transfer. After the transfer, the estate is mine. Mine means she can't be touched. Mine means I can put her name back where it belongs—with lawyers, with the state, with daylight."

He pushed the envelope across the table. "Original ledger pages. The ones my grandfather kept, not the cemetery copies. Plus a wire transfer that would make your retirement extremely comfortable, if you're the retiring type, which I suspect you're not. In exchange: you wait. Two weeks. Let the dinner happen. Let the ink dry. Then burn everything down with my blessing and my testimony."

Sable didn't touch the envelope. She looked at it the way she probably looked at an unexplained contusion—cataloguing, measuring, unimpressed.

"And during those two weeks," she said, "your father moves Juniper's grandmother to a facility two states away. You knew about that?"

Theo's stomach dropped. "What?"

"Don't play surprised, it doesn't suit you. You have your mother's face and your father's instincts, and right now the instincts are losing." She set down her fork. "You want me to believe this is protection. Maybe some of it is—I'll grant you look like a man who hasn't slept since March, and guilt does that. But here's what I know about bargains, Mr. Vale. I've sat with a lot of people at the end, and the ones who made quiet deals always told themselves the same story you're telling me. *Just a little longer. Just until it's safe. Just until I'm in control.* And the story is even true, most times. That's the trick of it. Good intentions are the best accomplices crime ever had. They hold the door open and apologize while the bad thing walks through."

"I'm not my father."

"No," she agreed. "You're worse in one specific way. He knows what he is. You still get to feel clean." She picked up her coffee, drank, and set it down with a small ceramic click that sounded, to Theo, like a verdict being entered. "Tell me about the pages. How did you get them?"

He hesitated. Lied would have been easier; the truth came out sideways, dragged. "My grandfather's study. There's a false bottom in the desk drawer—the same desk my father uses now, though he's never found it, because he thinks he already knows everything Grandfather owned. I found it when I was nineteen. I've been sitting on those pages for eleven years."

"Eleven years." She let that sit between them like a body on a slab. "Eleven years, and you moved the moment it became inconvenient to your inheritance."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "That's partly true. I'll give you partly true. I was a coward for a long time. And then Juniper disappeared and I understood that cowardice has a body count, and I started moving as fast as I could, which apparently is a speed that looks like standing still to everyone else." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated the diner and the meatloaf and the envelope and the whole gilded machinery of his family that had trained him to negotiate instead of simply *say things*. "I am trying to save her, Dr. Halloran. I swear to you, whatever else is tangled up in me, that part is clean."

Sable studied him for a long moment. Outside, the rain finally committed, drumming soft and steady on the window, blurring the parking lot into watercolor.

"I believe that you believe it," she said at last. "That's the most I can offer anyone."

"And the deal?"

She slid the envelope back across the table without opening it. Her hand stayed flat on top of it.

"Here's my counteroffer, and it isn't one, so don't negotiate. I'm not waiting two weeks. I'm not taking money—that's not modesty, it's arithmetic; anything I take from a Vale becomes evidence against me the moment your father finds it, and he will find it, because finding things is what your family *does*. And I'm not letting you decide when the truth comes out, because you've had eleven years to decide and you decided to keep a false-bottomed drawer." She stood, dropped bills beside her untouched pie—enough for both meals, he noticed, enough to make sure he owed her nothing—and pulled her coat on. "But I'll tell you what I will do, Theo. I'll tell you that in eleven days, at a dinner full of candles and crystal, your father is going to raise a glass to the dead. And when he does, somebody in that room is going to answer him. Whether it's me or whether it's her—" she shrugged into her collar, "—that part may not be mine to choose anymore. You might ask yourself why a woman who's hidden successfully for a year would pick that night to stop hiding. Somebody gave her a reason to trust the timing. Think hard about who."

She walked out into the rain without looking back.

Theo sat alone in the booth for a long time, the envelope under his hands gone damp at the edges, the meatloaf congealing into a metaphor he refused to examine. Through the fogged window he watched her cross the lot to her car—an old sedan with a crack in the windshield—and pause before getting in, head tilted, studying something on the pavement or perhaps on the roofline of the building across the street. Then she got in and drove away, taillights dissolving down Chapel Road into the gray.

He should go home. He should burn the pages or turn them over to the state himself, tonight, tonight, *tonight*—

His phone buzzed. A text from his father, four words, no punctuation, the way Edmund Vale communicated everything: *Dinner moved up. Tuesday.*

Theo stared at it. Eleven days became seven. Seven became—his mind did the arithmetic of his father's habits, the man's love of surprise, of controlling the clock—and landed somewhere cold.

Two days. Maybe less.

And Sable Halloran had just driven off alone into the rain, carrying everything, toward a road where somebody had already run her off the pavement once.

He threw cash on the table and ran for his keys.
