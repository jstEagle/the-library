## 6. Iris

Mabel Chen had spent forty years among the archives of Ashcombe County, and in that time she had learned one immutable law of small towns: the louder the silence, the more expensive the thing being buried.

She was thinking about this law on a Tuesday morning in October, standing in her kitchen in a cardigan the color of weak tea, holding a photograph she had not looked at in thirty years.

The photograph showed a christening. St. Aldric's, 1989. The pews crowded with the founding families in their autumn finery, and there, in the second row, a baby in a white gown being passed from one set of arms to another like a very precious, very fragile parcel. Edmund Voss held her with the stiff terror of a man who had never held anything he couldn't put down again. And beside him, laughing at his terror, stood a young woman with Margaret Dunmore's exact smile.

Mabel had been the one who took the photograph. She'd had a good camera then, a Pentax, and a bad habit of photographing everything, on the theory that someday someone would want to know. Everyone had laughed at her for it. *Mabel and her archives.* Forty years later, she was the only person in Ashcombe who could produce a christening, a fire inspection report, and a train schedule for the same week in 1994, and she had come to understand that this was not a hobby. It was a vocation. Somebody had to remember, and the town had voted, loudly and by silence, that it would not be them.

She turned the photograph over. On the back, in her younger handwriting: *Voss christening — Iris, 6 months.*

Iris. The flower, the Greek goddess of the rainbow, the messenger between worlds. Edmund Voss had named his daughter after a messenger between worlds, and then he had sent her to one.

"Oh, you clever, frightened man," Mabel said to the photograph, and put the kettle on.

* * *

She had not slept well. This was Beatrix Dunmore's fault, though Mabel held no grudge about it. The young lawyer — *young*, though Mabel was old enough now that anyone under sixty qualified — had come to her office three days ago with the careful, courteous manner of someone asking for directions to a place they'd been told didn't exist. *The Harrow Mill fire. The child. Was the body ever identified?*

And Mabel had given the town's answer, the rehearsed one, the one she'd been giving since 1994: *Records from that period were damaged in a flood.* She had watched Beatrix's face while she said it. She had watched the lawyer's eyes do something she recognized from forty years of watching people lie in reading rooms — not the flicker of guilt, but the flicker of *recognition*. The look of a person who has just heard their mother's voice coming out of a stranger's mouth.

That night Mabel had gone home and opened the box she had promised herself she'd never open. Not the flood-damaged records box — the *other* one. The one she'd labeled, in a fit of archivist's honesty she'd later regretted, *DO NOT DESTROY, NO MATTER WHO ASKS.*

The kettle shrieked. She made the tea and carried it and the photograph to the kitchen table, where she'd laid out the contents of the box like a tarot spread.

Item: a birth announcement from the *Ashcombe Courier*, March 1989. *To Edmund and Lillian Voss, a daughter, Iris Wren.*

Item: a hospital discharge record, October 1994, Harrow Mill fire, one casualty, female child, age five. *Remains released to next of kin. No identification required — visual confirmation by father, E. Voss.*

Visual confirmation. Mabel had underlined that phrase in 1994 with a librarian's red pencil and a librarian's suspicion. Edmund Voss had identified his own daughter's body by sight, from a fire that had burned hot enough to fuse the mill's iron gears. There had been almost nothing left to confirm. The coroner had noted it. The coroner had also, two years later, retired to Sarasota on a pension that everyone agreed was remarkably comfortable for a county man, and Mabel had noted *that* too, in the same red pencil, in a margin she'd later blacked out herself, during the years when it seemed wise to.

Item: a train timetable. The last evening service out of Ashcombe, the night of the fire, 11:47 p.m., northbound. And stapled to it — this was the piece that had kept her awake — a carbon copy of a ticket purchase record. Two tickets. One adult, one child. Paid in cash, sold at the station window by a clerk named Denny Pruitt, who had told Mabel in 1996, over two beers and a great deal of nervous laughter, that the woman buying them had been in such a hurry she'd left her gloves on the counter. *Nice gloves. Kid had a coat over her head, like she was sleeping. Man said don't wake her.*

The woman's description, as Denny had given it: dark hair, young, upset. Mabel had asked, gently, *Could it have been Margaret Dunmore?* and Denny had gone very still and said, *I never said that, Mabel, and you never asked,* and they had finished their beers in a silence that had lasted, in effect, twenty-eight years.

But here was the thing. Here was the thing Mabel had been turning over all night like a stone with something alive under it.

Margaret Dunmore had sworn — in a deposition, in notarized statements, at a dinner party in 1997 where Mabel had been within earshot — that she was *in Ashcombe* on the night of the fire. That she had seen the Halbrook boys across the square at ten o'clock, clean and laughing, nowhere near the mill. Her alibi had held like poured concrete. It had held for Gerald Halbrook and his cousin Peter and old Cornelius Halbrook himself, all of them, all night, all corroborated by one witness.

And yet someone matching Margaret's description had bought two train tickets at 11:15 that same night, with a sleeping child whose face was hidden.

Mabel sipped her tea. The photograph of the christening lay in the middle of the table, the baby Iris smiling her toothless smile at the ceiling of a church that had since been deconsecrated and turned into a yoga studio, because Ashcombe could repurpose a church but not a memory.

*Either Margaret Dunmore lied,* Mabel thought, *or Margaret Dunmore was two places at once, which even for a Dunmore is a stretch.*

*Or* — and here the stone lifted, and the thing underneath it was cold and smooth and terrible — *or the woman at the train window wasn't Margaret at all. Or Margaret was there, and the alibi was arranged in advance, and the child on the train was not going anywhere dangerous. The child was being rescued.*

Edmund Voss had not lost a daughter in the fire. Edmund Voss had *filed* a daughter in the fire. He had taken a living girl, five years old, wrapped her in a coat, and walked her out of the story of Ashcombe — and he had needed a witness to say where the Halbrooks weren't, and he had needed a body, or the idea of one, and he had needed the town to do what Ashcombe did best, which was to look at a closed door and agree it was a wall.

Why? Mabel had turned that question over too. Why would a man erase his own child?

Because of the Halbrooks. That was the shape of it, when she let the pieces sit next to each other. Lillian Voss — dead two years before the fire, and Mabel had always found *that* timing interesting as well — Lillian had been a Halbrook. A cousin, twice removed, but blood was blood in Ashcombe, and blood came with claims. If Lillian died, Iris inherited through her. And the Halbrooks, who by 1994 were leveraged to the rafters and hungry, had been circling Edmund's mill like gulls around a trawler. Mabel had seen the letters in the probate files — polite, lawyerly letters, the kind that are the opening moves of a long game of taking everything a man has.

So Edmund had done the only thing left. He had made his daughter uninheritable. He had made her dead. *You cannot take from me what I do not have,* the erasure said. *And she cannot be taken from me, because she is no one.*

And the confession letter — Beatrix had told her about the letters, in that careful lawyer way, showing her nothing but asking everything — the confession letter made sense now too. It wasn't remorse, or not only. It was *insurance*. A man who has done a monstrous thing for a loving reason keeps the receipt. *If anything happens to me, if the Halbrooks come for her anyway, here is the truth, here is what I did and why, here is where the bodies are buried, and one of them is breathing.*

Mabel looked at the christening photograph for a long time.

Then she looked at the last item in the box, the one she had saved for last the way you save the last bite of something you're not sure you want to finish.

It was an envelope. Unsealed. Postmarked Ashcombe, June of this year. No return address. Inside, a single sheet of cream paper, and on it, in a small, upright, unhurried hand:

*Dear Miss Chen,*

*You kept the box. I have always known you would. You were the only one who photographed the christening, which means you were the only one who understood that we were real.*

*I will come to you when I am ready. Please do not look for me before then. Please, please do not tell the Dunmore lawyer about this note. She is honest, which is a fine quality in a lawyer and a dangerous one in an ally. I need to know what she is before I decide what she is to me.*

*Thank you for remembering us. It kept us alive longer than you know.*

*Iris*

Mabel set the letter down with hands that were not entirely steady, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. Thirty years. Thirty years of the town scrubbing plaques and sealing records and looking through her as though she were the furniture, and somewhere out there, a girl who was officially ash had been reading the same silence she had, and writing to her. *Thank you for remembering us.*

Then the smile faded, because Mabel Chen was old, but she was not foolish, and she had spent her life reading documents, and a document is never only what it says. *I need to know what she is before I decide what she is to me.* That was not the sentence of a grateful orphan. That was the sentence of a chess player, sizing up the board, deciding which pieces were hers, which were the enemy's, and which could be *sacrificed*.

Edmund Voss had raised this girl, or someone had, in the dark, off the record, with a dead name and a forged death and a father's confession in a safe. You did not come out of that childhood soft. You came out of it *precise*.

And the second letter — the unsigned one, the one promising *more* to whoever found it first. Mabel had assumed it was Edmund's. A second insurance policy. But Edmund was dead, properly dead this time, and the letter had been placed in the safe recently; Beatrix's description of the paper had said as much, between the lines.

Which meant the safe had been opened. Which meant *Iris* had been in the house. Which meant the second letter was not a dead man's insurance at all.

It was bait. It was a door, left deliberately unlocked, with a light on behind it, and Beatrix Dunmore had walked through it like a moth with a briefcase.

The phone rang. Mabel looked at it for three rings, her tea going cold, the photograph of baby Iris smiling up at her from the table.

She answered.

"Miss Chen." The voice was a woman's, low and even and completely unfamiliar, and it did not waste a syllable. "You received my note in June. You've been very patient, and I'm grateful. But I'm told you spoke to the Dunmore lawyer yesterday, and that she asked about the body." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "So I think it's time we all met. Tonight, if you can. And Miss Chen — please don't bring the lawyer. Not yet."

Mabel closed her eyes. *She is honest, which is a fine quality in a lawyer and a dangerous one in an ally.*

"I'll come alone," Mabel said. "But I'm bringing the christening photograph. You should see who was there."

The silence on the line lasted four seconds, and in it Mabel heard — distinctly, unmistakably — a breath that might have been a laugh, or might have been the beginning of tears.

"Row three," the voice said at last. "Second from the aisle. Bring that. And Miss Chen — wear the tea-colored cardigan. I've seen you in it. I've been watching this town a very long time."
