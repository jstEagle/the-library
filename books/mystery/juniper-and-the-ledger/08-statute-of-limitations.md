## 8. Statute of Limitations

Gerald Halbrook had not slept properly in nine days, and he had begun to develop a relationship with the ceiling of his bedroom that could only be described as codependent. He lay beneath it at three in the morning, hands folded on his chest like a man in a casket doing a dry run, and did arithmetic.

Fourteen days. That was what remained before the statute of limitations closed on the Harrow Mill fire like a door, forever, and everything his family had buried under forty years of garden parties and charitable galas became legally untouchable. Fourteen days, unless someone found the letters. Unless someone found the letters and *used* them.

And the letters were in the office of one Beatrix Dunmore.

He had met the estate lawyer exactly twice, and both times had come away with the unsettling sensation of having been X-rayed. Beatrix Dunmore was not a large person, but they had a way of looking at Gerald that made him feel like a document being read for errors. They wore their competence the way Gerald wore his father's old signet ring — constantly, and with the vague anxiety of someone who might be asked to prove it fit.

The first time, at the preliminary reading, Dunmore had said, "Mr. Halbrook, you've stated you were at the Ashcombe Inn on the night of October ninth, 1994," in a tone so flat it could have been used to level a shelf.

"That's correct."

"And who corroborates that?"

"Margaret Dunmore." He had watched, with the helpless fascination of a man watching his own car roll toward a cliff edge, for any flicker of recognition at the shared surname. There had been none. Either the lawyer was a superb actor, or — and this was the thought that had kept Gerald company at three a.m. ever since — they genuinely didn't know. Which meant the letters might not mention her. Which meant there might still be time to make them not exist.

Which brought him, at half past four on a Thursday morning, to the fire escape behind the Corvus Building, wearing a fleece he was later to describe to himself as "discreet" and which was, in fact, the exact grey of regret.

* * *

The window of Beatrix Dunmore's office had been latched, not locked. Gerald took this as providence. Later he would understand it as bait.

He came through it with the grace of a man who had once moved a piano and never fully recovered his confidence in his own body, and stood in the dark office breathing like a kettle. Rain ticked against the glass. The city beyond it was the color of wet slate.

The filing cabinets were along the east wall. He had memorized the layout from a single visit — he was not, whatever his family said, a stupid man; he was merely a man who had spent fifty-two years being told that thinking was a service other people could be hired to perform. Estate files, alphabetized. V.

The Voss file was thick. He pulled it with both hands, the way you'd pull a tooth, and fanned the contents across the desk by the light of his phone.

Deeds. Correspondence. A codicil. A codicil. Another codicil — Edmund Voss had apparently amended his will the way other men changed socks. Tax assessments. A letter from the Ashcombe Historical Society declining to comment.

No letters. No confession. No second envelope promising *more*.

He went through the file twice, then the desk drawers, then — hating himself, and doing it anyway — the potted fern, which contained no letters but did contain a great deal of soil, which now contained a great deal of him. His hands were shaking. He sat down in Beatrix Dunmore's chair to steady himself, which was the single worst decision available to him, because sitting down meant he was still there when the desk lamp clicked on.

"Mr. Halbrook," said Beatrix Dunmore, from the doorway, in a cardigan and pajama trousers, holding a mug that read WORLD'S ADEQUATE LAWYER. "You're sitting in my chair. There's fern soil on your hands. And you've been reading my codicils, which I'll note are the most exciting thing in this room, which says something about both of us."

Gerald stood up so fast the chair skidded. "I can explain."

"I genuinely hope so. I've been sitting in my car across the street for two hours waiting to see if you'd actually do it, and I'd hate for the payoff to be 'I can explain.'"

"You— *what?*"

"I was told someone might come for the letters." Beatrix set the mug down on the desk, next to the fanned papers, with the calm of a person racking a billiard cue. "I was told by someone who knows a great deal about this estate. Someone who, I've come to realize, knows things about my office that only someone *inside* the estate could know. So before you explain, Mr. Halbrook, consider that you are not the only person who has been watching this room. And that the letters you're looking for are not here."

"They're not—"

"Copies are. You may have noticed. The real ones are somewhere you will never find them, held by someone who is not me." Beatrix picked up the codicils and squared their edges, tap tap tap, a small metronome of composure. "Fourteen days, isn't it? Until the statute closes. You've been doing the arithmetic. I can tell — you have the face of a man who has been doing arithmetic against a ceiling."

Gerald's mouth opened. Closed. What came out was: "My father was a good man."

"No," Beatrix said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "I don't think he was. I think he was a frightened man who stayed frightened for thirty years, and frightened men make excellent arsonists and terrible fathers. Sit down, Mr. Halbrook. Not in that chair. The client chair. It's lower. It's deliberate."

Gerald sat. The chair was, indeed, lower.

"Here's what I know," Beatrix continued, pacing now, mug in hand. "Someone has been feeding information to a party outside this estate. That party knows my schedule, my filing system, and apparently my taste in break-in weather, because they predicted you'd come on a rainy Thursday when the fire escape cameras would be fogged. They were right, by the way. They're fogged."

"Then how—"

"Because I turned them off. I wanted to see who'd come through the window." A pause, and then, with something dangerously close to gentle humor: "You came through the window, Mr. Halbrook. In a fleece. I want you to know that when I report this — and I haven't decided if I will — the fleece will be in the report."

"You can't report this." Gerald heard his own voice go thin and high, the voice of a boy called to the headmaster's study. "If there's an investigation, if the letters come out before the fourteenth — my family — the Halbrook name—"

"Is built on a fire," Beatrix said. "On a child who burned in it, or didn't — I no longer know which, and I suspect you don't either, which is interesting, isn't it? You're terrified of a crime you can't even describe to me. You were nine years old in 1994. You've been guarding a secret you were never told the shape of."

The rain filled the silence. Gerald found that his eyes were stinging, which was absurd, and that he was too tired to be ashamed of it.

"I found a letter once," he said. "When I was nineteen. In my father's desk. It said *Harrow* and it said *forgive us* and it said a name I didn't recognize. I asked him about it and he took the letter and he burned it in the study fireplace and he looked at me and he said, 'Some doors are load-bearing, Gerald. You don't take them out to see what's behind them.'" He laughed, a small broken sound. "I've spent my whole life not opening doors. And now the door is opening itself, and it turns out I'm standing behind it."

Beatrix had stopped pacing. They were looking at him with an expression Gerald couldn't parse — not pity, not quite. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who had recently found their own name in a place it should never have been.

"Margaret Dunmore," Beatrix said quietly. "The alibi witness. She was my mother."

The floor did not move, but Gerald had the sensation of it considering the option. "I didn't know," he said. "I swear it."

"I know. That's what frightens me. Everyone swears they didn't know, and yet everyone knew *something*, and the somethings all fit together like a jigsaw nobody would admit to owning." Beatrix drained the mug and set it down. "Here's what happens now. You're going to go back through that window — the door is for clients — and you're going to go home, and you are going to stop doing arithmetic and start doing something useful. Because in fourteen days this closes, and I have come to believe that the person who wants it closed is not you, and is not me, and is very much not finished."

"Who, then?"

"I don't know yet. But they knew you'd come tonight. They wanted me to catch you in this office. Which means either they wanted you discredited—" Beatrix glanced at the window, the rain, the fogged city beyond it, and their voice dropped to nearly nothing. "—or they wanted to know whether I'd protect the letters or protect *you*. And either way, Mr. Halbrook, they were watching us both just now, and I would very much like to know from where."

Gerald looked at the window. Beyond it, across the wet street, the upper floors of the old Ashcombe Trust Building stood dark against the rain — all of them dark, that is, except one, on the fourth floor, where a light that had not been burning when he'd climbed the fire escape now glowed steady and yellow, like an eye that had been open the whole time.

He pointed. Beatrix followed his finger.

The light went out.

"Well," said Beatrix Dunmore, in the tone of a person adding a line to a ledger they were beginning to suspect had no bottom. "I believe we've just been read."
