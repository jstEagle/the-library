## 8. The Granddaughter

Camille Parish lived in the old Whitcomb house on Larkspur Row, the one with the widow's walk that had never once, in a hundred and forty years, been walked by an actual widow. Rafael had probated the estate that put her there — had watched her sign the closing papers with a fountain pen she'd brought herself, had noted even then the way her hand moved: not with ceremony but with efficiency, as if signatures were tolls to be paid and she resented every one.

She answered the door before Rafael knocked twice. As though she'd been standing in the hall all morning, waiting.

"Mr. Nakamura." She said it the way people read a headline they'd already guessed at. "You look terrible."

"I've been told." Rafael stepped past her into the foyer without being invited, which was a thing fifteen years of keeping Juniper's secrets entitled a person to do, or so Rafael told themselves on the walk over. The house smelled of cold coffee and lemon polish and, beneath it, something older — the particular dust of rooms where no one had grieved in living memory.

* * *

They sat in the parlor, which Camille had clearly arranged for company and just as clearly never used. The furniture wore its slipcovers like bandages. On the mantel stood a photograph of Edmund Parish in his founding-days prime, jaw like a cliff face, eyes that looked slightly past the camera as though it were keeping him from something more interesting. The same photograph hung in the bank lobby, the library, the historical society. In Juniper, Edmund Parish's face was less a portrait than a municipal utility.

"You demanded the exhumation," Rafael said.

"I petitioned for it. Legally. Through counsel." Camille sat with her ankles crossed and her hands folded, a woman built entirely of right angles. "There's a difference you'd appreciate."

"The court doesn't grant exhumations because someone finds them legally tidy. It grants them when there's a question about the death itself. You gave Judge Alvarado a question. Where did you get it?"

For a long moment Camille said nothing. A truck went by outside, gears grinding down the hill toward the ferry dock, and in the silence Rafael became aware of the house's other sound — a faint ticking from somewhere upstairs, a clock nobody wound properly, marking time it didn't believe in.

"I found the annuity," she said finally. "Three years ago, going through my grandmother's papers after she died. A disbursement schedule. Thirty years of payments out of the church restoration fund to a payee listed only as *E.W., care of Juniper Trust.* No invoice. No purpose. Just a signature line, initialed — I assume by your office."

Rafael felt the floor tilt a degree, the way it had the day the coffin came up empty. Those initials were theirs. R.N. Neat, small, unremarkable. Fifteen years of initials on fifteen years of paper, each one a door Rafael had walked past without opening.

"It was buried inside a consolidated fund report," Camille went on. "Page forty-something of a document nobody reads. But I read everything. That's what everyone forgets about me — they think I'm just the heir, the woman who came back to collect. I'm an auditor, Mr. Nakamura. Numbers are the only honest language anyone speaks, because numbers can't look you in the eye and lie."

"And when you found the payments?"

"I traced them backward. Then forward. Then I did the arithmetic any reasonable person would do." Her voice stayed level, but something moved behind her eyes — a light behind frosted glass. "A man dies in a fire. Three people die with him. The town buries him with full honors and names streets after him. And then, starting six months later, someone begins paying twelve hundred dollars a month to a set of initials, forever, out of a fund meant for God's roof. You don't have to be an auditor to know what that is. You only have to be willing to say it."

"So you said it. To a judge."

"I said there was reason to doubt the interment. Which was true. I believed he wasn't in that coffin." She uncrossed her ankles and recrossed them, the only tell she permitted herself. "I assumed he'd been cremated secretly, or never buried at all — that the town had staged a funeral for a man who died somewhere else, to protect his name. Towns do that. This town especially."

"But you didn't think he was alive."

"No." And here, for the first time, Camille Parish looked away from Rafael, out the window toward the cemetery hills, where the grass was already going gold at the edges of summer. "I did the math on that too. He'd be ninety-one. I assumed whatever was left of him was ash in some urn or bones under some other name, and that the money was hush payment to whoever helped move him. I wanted the coffin opened because an empty coffin would void the condition."

"The condition."

"On the inheritance." She turned back, and now her voice took on the flat clarity of a ledger line. "My grandfather's will — the real one, the 1994 one, which you also probated — left everything in trust to his descendants, contingent on one clause. *Provided the family maintains discretion regarding the circumstances of the deceased's passing.* Discretion. A legal word for silence. My father understood it. He never spoke of Edmund except in the past-perfect tense, like a man describing weather that had happened to someone else. When I asked him once why we couldn't sell the timber land, he said, *Because Grandfather bought quiet with it, and quiet isn't ours to sell.*"

The ticking upstairs seemed louder. Rafael realized their own hands had gone still in their lap, the way they went still in court — the body remembering, before the mind admitted it, that something was being taken down for permanent record.

"So you broke the silence," Rafael said, "to free the money."

"I broke the silence to find out what the silence was *for*." Camille leaned forward. "You want me to be the villain of this. I've watched you decide that since the graveside — you, and half the council, and Maren Okafor, who writes about me like I'm a season finale. But ask yourself, Mr. Nakamura: who kept this town fed on that annuity? Who signed those disbursements year after year without asking? Not me. I was in Chicago learning to count other people's money. The silence wasn't mine. I inherited it like the house, like the name, like the photograph on that mantel — and I am the first Parish in three generations who refused to dust it."

* * *

Rafael stood, walked to the window. From here you could see the cemetery, the green slope of it, the small figure — even at this distance, recognizable by posture alone — moving between the stones with a rake. Elias Wrenn. Edmund Parish. A man who had been tending the graves of a town that thought it had buried him, including, presumably, the grave that was supposed to hold himself.

"He's alive," Rafael said. Not a question. They had known since the ledger, since the letter in the storage unit, since the ferry-dock witness with her thirty-year-old certainty. But saying it aloud in this house, under that photograph, made it real in a way the documents hadn't managed.

"Yes." Camille said it simply, without triumph. "I learned it four days ago. After the exhumation. I hired a firm to trace the E.W. payments to their terminal address, same as you're doing — don't look surprised, you're not the only person in this town capable of following money. The trail ends at a PO box registered to the diocesan maintenance office. The maintenance office employs one groundskeeper for the Juniper cemetery." She paused. "I haven't spoken to him. I want that on the record, since you seem to be keeping one. Whatever he says to you, he hasn't said to me."

"Why not? He's your grandfather."

"Is he?" Something flickered across her face then — not softness exactly, but the place where softness would go if she'd ever been issued any. "I met Edmund Parish four times before I was ten. He taught me to skip stones off the reservoir and told me the fire was an accident and that grown-ups would handle it. Then he was dead, and we were rich, and quiet. The man raking that cemetery is a stranger who happens to share my blood and my inheritance conditions. I didn't come back to Juniper for family, Mr. Nakamura. I came back because someone was spending my legacy on a lie, and I wanted the lie itemized."

"And now it is. Eleven days until the statute runs. If the truth comes out before then, the estate's condition fails, the trust dissolves, and everything reverts—"

"To the town. Yes. I know how my own trust reads." She smiled, thin and precise as a paper cut. "I ran those numbers too. Do you want to know the strange part? When I imagined the coffin coming up empty, I imagined vindication. What I got instead was—" she searched for the word, found it, delivered it without flinching— "*complicity*. Because the moment that lid lifted, every dollar I'm worth became blood money in public view, and the whole town started doing arithmetic of their own. About me. About you. About who knew what, and for how long, and who got paid to keep knowing it."

Outside, far down the hill, the small figure with the rake had stopped moving. It stood very still among the headstones, facing up the slope — facing, Rafael could not help noticing, toward Larkspur Row. Toward this window.

Toward them.

"He knows," Rafael said quietly.

"He's always known," Camille said. "That's rather the point of him." She rose, straightened a slipcover that didn't need straightening, and fixed Rafael with a look that was almost — almost — an appeal. "Whatever you're going to do, Mr. Nakamura, do it soon. Because I've read the ledgers, and I've read the man, and men who wait thirty years to be caught are not waiting to be forgiven. They're waiting for the date."

Rafael was already at the door when she added, softly, almost to herself:

"He used to skip stones better than anyone I ever saw. Left-handed. Ask him about his hands sometime — the ones everyone says are too soft for the work. Ask him what else they've been too soft for."

The door closed behind Rafael with the sound of a file drawer shutting.

Down the hill, the figure with the rake had begun to move again — unhurried, methodical, row by row by row — as though the graves would keep, as indeed they always had.

But between Rafael and the cemetery gate, parked crooked in the shade of the yew hedge, sat a car Rafael had seen once before, photographed in a newspaper clipping fifteen years old: dark green, dented at the rear fender, the kind of car a man drives when he never intends to be followed — and never has been.

Until now.
