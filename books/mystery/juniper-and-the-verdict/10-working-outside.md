## 10. Working Outside

The suspension letter came on a Tuesday, typed on county letterhead so thin Juniper could read the shape of the next page through it. *Pending review of procedural irregularities in the Verne exhumation.* Irregularities. As if the irregularity were the empty coffin and not the thirty years of lies packed around it like grave dirt.

Juniper read it twice at the kitchen table, then folded it into quarters and set it under the salt cellar, where it would stay. The house was quiet in the way it always was before dawn—the water heater ticking, the maples scratching the gutters, the town below the hill still dark and dreaming its founding-family dreams. Juniper poured coffee that smelled like scorched earth and sat with it until the window went from black to the gray of old dishwater.

The badge was still on the hook by the door. Juniper looked at it for a long time. Taking it off the hook would be theft now, technically. Wearing it would be impersonation. Leaving it there felt like surrender, and surrender had a smell Juniper knew from the morgue—the sweet, patient rot of things that had stopped resisting.

So Juniper did neither. Juniper left the badge hanging and took instead the thing no one could suspend: the notebook. Forty years of coroner's notebooks lined the shelf above the desk, spines cracked, covers furred with age, and somewhere in the middle of them lived every name this town had ever tried to forget.

Juniper dressed in the dark. Drove the county car to the county lot, walked past it, and took the bus. If they were going to be watched—and they were; the sedan had been parked across from the office since Friday, a gray Buick with a driver who read the same page of the same newspaper for an hour at a stretch—then let them watch an empty parking space. Let them wonder.

The first stop was the county records annex on Delaney Street, a brick building that had once been a schoolhouse and still smelled faintly of chalk dust and children's fear. The clerk there, a woman named Odette with reading glasses on a chain and a memory like a land registry, owed Juniper for the matter of her brother's death certificate—how Juniper had written *accidental fall* when a kinder, less careful coroner might have written something that would have followed the family for generations. Debts like that didn't expire. Debts like that were the only currency still worth anything in Harlow County.

"I can't let you in the vault," Odette said, not unkindly, not looking up from her stamp. "Not with your name in the paper."

"I'm not asking you to let me in the vault."

"No," Odette said. "You're asking me to remember things." She stamped a form with a thump that echoed down the empty hallway. "The Verne trust disbursements. Quarterly. You want to know where the money went after it left the county."

"I want to know if it ever *was* county money."

Odette finally looked up. Her eyes behind the glasses were the pale, patient blue of a winter sky. "It was never county money. It was private trust—Verne Memorial Trust, established 1994, same month as the funeral, if you follow me. Four signatories. The disbursements went out through a disbursement agent, and the agent's records are public because the agent is a bank, and banks file with the state." She pulled a slip of paper from a drawer and slid it across the counter the way a dealer slides a card they've already decided to lose. "First National of Carrow. Their filings are online, but the older ones are microfiche, and the microfiche is in the basement of the public library, and the librarian there is my sister, and she closes at five."

Juniper took the slip of paper. It was warm from the drawer, as if it had been waiting.

"Odette."

"Don't thank me," Odette said, stamping again. "And don't come back here. If anyone asks, you came for a copy of your own birth certificate and I told you we don't do those anymore."

* * *

Ruth Crane had been awake since four, which was nothing new. Grief kept her hours now the way a night job would—punching in when the house went silent, working until the light came up gray and useless. She sat at her father's kitchen table in the house on Alder Street, in his chair, which still held the shape of him the way a glove holds a hand, and she went through his papers for the fourth time.

Elias Crane had been an accountant for forty years, and an accountant's papers are a kind of autobiography. Ruth had learned this the way you learn a language—slowly, badly, then all at once. The early boxes were orderly: receipts in rubber-banded bundles, tax returns by decade, a shoebox of pencils sharpened to needles. The later boxes were orderly too, but a different kind of orderly. Careful. Guarded. In the last two years of his files, her father had begun keeping a separate ledger—not the official one, but a shadow of it, columns in his small precise hand tracking sums that matched nothing he'd ever filed.

The sums went out quarterly. The sums went to a name she didn't recognize: *Hollis & Vane, Property Management, Carrow.*

Her father had been killed for this paper. She was almost sure of it now, in the way you're sure of weather. Someone had put him in the ground under a dead man's empty coffin with a tenderness that made her sick—his coat buttoned, his glasses folded in his breast pocket, someone's hands arranging him like a sleeping guest. Whoever buried him had loved him or owed him, and Ruth couldn't decide which was worse.

At seven she called the number on the coroner's card, the one Juniper had pressed into her hand at the funeral home, and got a disconnected-line recording. She sat with that for a moment, cold spreading through her chest. Then she remembered the newspaper piece—*coroner suspended pending review*—and dialed the house number she'd found in the phone book, because in Harlow County everyone was still in the phone book.

Juniper answered on the second ring, voice rough with coffee.

"It's Ruth Crane. They took your office."

"They took my *title*," Juniper said. "The office is just a room. Are you all right?"

"I found something." Ruth looked down at the ledger, at her father's handwriting, at the name. "And I think you should see it. But not at your house. And not at mine."

There was a pause on the line. Ruth could hear the town in it—the far-off school bell, a dog, the ordinary morning of a place that had been lying to itself for thirty years.

"The diner on Route 9," Juniper said. "The one with the broken sign. Nine o'clock. Sit with your back to the wall and don't order anything until I get there."

* * *

The diner's sign had lost its first three letters sometime in the previous century, so it read only **E'S**, glowing pink and half-dead over a gravel lot. Juniper arrived at nine exactly and found Ruth Crane in the last booth, back to the wall, a cup of coffee untouched in front of her and her father's ledger open under her hands like a patient on a table.

She looked terrible in the way that people look terrible when they've stopped pretending otherwise—eyes rimmed red, hair pulled back with a rubber band, her father's cardigan over her shoulders despite the warmth of the diner. She looked, Juniper thought, like someone who had decided that being careful with herself was a luxury the dead couldn't afford.

"You look like hell," Ruth said.

"You look worse."

"Good. We match." She turned the ledger around. "Tell me what this is."

Juniper put on reading glasses and studied the columns. The hand was small, ruled, exact—an accountant's hand, a hand that had spent a lifetime making other people's chaos look like sense. Quarterly sums, thirty years of them, each annotated with a date and a single letter: *P.* Then, six months ago, the letters changed. The sums continued, but the annotation became *P—held*, then *P—held*, then nothing at all. And in the margin of the final entry, in the same precise hand, her father had written one word, underlined twice:

*Hollis.*

"Hollis & Vane," Juniper said. "Property management. Out of Carrow."

"You know them."

"I know the name from the trust filings. The Verne Memorial Trust paid a disbursement agent for thirty years. I assumed the agent was a lawyer. Lawyers don't usually manage property." Juniper looked up. "Property management means real estate. Real estate means a deed. And deeds, Ruth Crane, are the one thing in this state you cannot buy your way around, because they're recorded in triplicate and the recorder's office is run by a woman named Faye who has hated the founding families since 1987."

Something moved across Ruth's face that was almost a smile. It was strange, seeing grief and appetite in the same expression. "So we go to the recorder's office."

"*We*," Juniper repeated.

Ruth met the look without flinching. "You're suspended. I'm nobody. Neither of us has any authority, which means neither of us can be ordered to stop. That's not a weakness, Coroner. That's the whole trick."

Juniper took off the glasses and folded them slowly. There was a version of this where Juniper said no—sent her home, kept her out of it, did the dangerous work alone like a responsible officer of a court that no longer existed. And there was the version where Ruth Crane was right, and the only people in Harlow County with clean hands were the people with no hands at all.

"Recorder's office closes at four," Juniper said. "Faye takes her break at two and leaves the front desk to her assistant, who is seventeen and terrified of everyone. We go at two."

* * *

The deed, when Faye's terrified assistant finally located it in the wrong drawer and brought it up wincing like an apology, was a thing of bureaucratic beauty.

*Parcel 44-C, Shoreline District, Harlow County. Structure: single dwelling, outbuildings. Grantee: Hollis & Vane Property Management, as agent for an undisclosed principal. Recorded: March 1995.*

March 1995. Seven months after Augustus Verne went into the lake and came out as a closed casket and a headstone. Juniper read the entry three times while Ruth read over their shoulder, her breath audible, her father's ledger open on the counter between them.

"An undisclosed principal," Ruth whispered. "That's him. That's my— that's *him*, isn't it. The trust pays a property manager, the property manager holds a cabin, and the principal is a dead man."

"Or a dead man's ghost," Juniper said. "Or a dead man's keeper." But they both knew. The dates were a confession. Nobody buys a lake cabin for a ghost seven months after a funeral unless the funeral was furniture.

Parcel 44-C. Ruth copied the parcel number onto her hand in pen, then onto a receipt, then made Juniper copy it too, into the notebook, in ink. "In case one of us loses it," she said. "Or in case one of us *loses it*," she added, and didn't smile this time.

The lake was forty minutes north, up a county road that climbed out of the valley's fog into thin hard sunlight. Juniper drove their own car, the personal one, a twelve-year-old sedan that had never once been associated with the county. Ruth sat in the passenger seat with the ledger on her knees and the window cracked, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The trees ran alongside them like a fence, black-trunked and dripping, and the air through the gap smelled of cold water and pine rot and, faintly, of the season turning—the green smell dying out of the world, the brown smell coming in.

"You should know," Ruth said finally, "that I'm not doing this for justice. I want to be honest with you, because you seem like someone who keeps books in your head, and I'd rather be a true number than a flattering one."

"What are you doing it for, then?"

"My father was buried under a stranger's coffin with his glasses folded on his chest. Somebody did that gently. Somebody *cared* for him at the end, and then put him in the dark under a lie, and I—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it, the way you'd master a dog on a short leash. "I want to know which it was. Love or debt. Because if it was love, then somebody out there is grieving him the way I am, and they might help me. And if it was debt—" she looked out the window at the trees, "—then my father sold something to somebody, and I want to know what it cost him, because he never spent a dime on himself in forty years and he died in a cardigan with a hole in the pocket."

Juniper drove. After a while they said, "For what it's worth, I've done this job twenty-two years, and I've never once seen a body buried with care by someone who hated them. Hate buries shallow. Hate leaves them where the dogs can find them. Whoever put your father in that grave took their time."

"Then why is he *dead*?"

"That," Juniper said, as the trees opened and the lake came into view below them, flat and pewter-colored and holding the sky the way a held breath holds words, "is the question we're about to stop being able to unask."

* * *

Parcel 44-C sat at the end of a gravel track that the county had stopped grading sometime during the previous administration. The cabin stood back from the water among birches, gray-weathered, with a stone chimney and a porch whose boards had gone silver. A boathouse leaned over the water on pilings. Everything about it said *empty*. Everything about it said *nobody's been here since the Reagan administration*.

Except the tire tracks. Two sets, fresh, pressed into the mud of the track after the last rain—which the coroner in Juniper dated, from the crispness of the edges, at less than a week.

They sat in the car at the mouth of the track for a long ten minutes, engine off, watching the cabin the way Juniper had learned to watch houses that had recently held death: not for movement, but for the absence of it. No smoke. No sound. A crow worked the boathouse roof and flew off unconcerned.

"Fresh tracks," Ruth said. Her voice had gone very steady, the steadiness of someone holding something heavy at arm's length. "Somebody's been here recently."

"Somebody drives a truck with off-road tires. Recent rain was nine days ago." Nine days ago was before Elias Crane's body had gone into the ground. Juniper didn't say that part out loud.

They got out anyway. Later, Juniper would try to reconstruct the decision and find only this: the ground had lied to them once already, and there is a particular kind of anger that doesn't ask permission.

The porch boards gave underfoot with a groan that seemed to carry across the whole lake. The front door was locked, but the lock was old, a deadbolt older than the doorframe's pride, and it gave to a flathead screwdriver from the coroner's kit with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Juniper noted, and made Ruth note, that they were now committing burglary—breaking and entering, on a suspended coroner's record, with a witness. Ruth wrote it down in the margin of her father's ledger, under the word *Hollis*: *We broke in. Both of us. Willingly.* "There," she said. "Now it's in the books."

Inside, the cabin smelled of dust and cold ash and, beneath it, faintly, of coffee.

Not old coffee. Not the ghost of coffee. Coffee, recent, the tannic smell that lives in a room for a day or two after a pot is made. Juniper stood in the doorway of the main room and felt the hair rise on their forearms.

The furniture was shrouded in sheets, ghost-gray in the light through the curtained windows. But one sheet had been pulled back from a chair by the cold fireplace—a reading chair, worn corduroy, angled toward the light. On the table beside it: a pair of drugstore reading glasses, folded. A hardcover book with a receipt as a bookmark. A tin of pipe tobacco, half full. And a mug, upside down on a saucer, rinsed clean.

Someone lived here. Someone lived here *recently*. Someone drank coffee and read by the fireplace and smoked a pipe and rinsed their mug like a considerate guest in a house that officially did not exist for a man who officially had been dead for thirty years.

Ruth found the newspaper first. It was folded on the bookshelf, tucked upright between two shrouded volumes like a letter kept in a Bible—and it was this county's paper, three weeks old, and the front page carried the story Juniper had tried to stop from running: *EXHUMATION ORDERED FOR FOUNDER'S GRAVE IN HIGHWAY SURVEY.*

In the margin, in pencil, in a hand that was old but steady, someone had written a single line. Ruth read it aloud, and her voice did not shake, which was somehow the most frightening thing in the whole gray room:

"*They're finally moving the road. Then it's time.*"

Juniper took the paper from her with hands that were not entirely steady, and looked at the date, and looked at the pencil line, and understood with a lurch like a floor giving way that Augustus Verne had read about his own exhumation in this chair, in this light, and had known—had *planned*—that the road would come for him eventually, and had decided that when it did, the long exile was over.

"He's alive," Ruth said. It wasn't a question. It was a door opening onto a drop.

"He's alive," Juniper said, "and he knew we'd come." Because there was one more thing on the table beside the reading glasses, small and deliberate, placed where a hand leaving the room would be sure to leave it: a matchbook from the E'S diner on Route 9, and inside it, in the same old steady pencil, a phone number and two words.

*Alone, Coroner.*
