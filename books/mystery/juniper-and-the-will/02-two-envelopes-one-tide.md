## 2. Two Envelopes, One Tide

The sea gave him back on a Tuesday, which felt somehow typical of the Grand Meridian's luck. Tuesdays were when the laundry truck came, when the boiler made its complaint known to the third floor, when Mrs. Fenwick in 208 rang down to ask — as she did every single week — whether the hotel had considered installing a lift that didn't sound like a dying whale. Tamsin had answered yes, we've considered it, Mrs. Fenwick, so many times that the words had worn smooth as sea glass.

So she was mid-sentence, phone cradled against her shoulder, promising Mrs. Fenwick that maintenance was "looking into it" (maintenance was not looking into it; maintenance was one man named Dev who was currently fighting the boiler with a wrench and a prayer), when the police car pulled up outside and she knew, before anyone said anything, whose morning this was going to be about.

They found Edmund Vane below the sea wall, where the rocks go black with weed and the water moves in that slow, muscular way it does at high tide. A guest out walking in bad weather. A slip. The kind of thing that happens to strangers in coastal towns every year, and the town shrugs, and the tide takes what it's owed.

Except Tamsin Lockridge had checked that man in four nights ago, and she had watched his wet shoes leave prints across her lobby floor, and she had seen the way he flinched at old Ambrose Lockridge's portrait like the painted eyes had reached out and pinched him. People who slipped on rocks did not flinch at portraits. People who slipped on rocks did not sign the register with a name that sat wrong in their own mouths.

"Accidental drowning," said Detective Sorrel, standing at her desk that afternoon, notebook open, pen uncapped. Sorrel was a compact woman of about fifty with the patient expression of someone who had spent thirty years listening to people lie to her and had developed, as a result, an almost theological respect for silence. "No signs of struggle. He'd been drinking, according to the bar tab."

"He had two whiskies," Tamsin said. "I closed his tab myself. He asked me to recommend somewhere to walk."

"And you recommended?"

"The cliff path is popular." She heard her own voice do the thing it always did under pressure — go flat, go precise, go concierge. "But I told him the sea wall was better in rain. Less mud."

Sorrel wrote something down. It might have been *sea wall*. It might have been *she remembers everything, doesn't she*. With Sorrel you never got to see the page.

"Were there any messages for Mr. Vane during his stay?" Sorrel asked. "Phone calls? Letters?"

"No calls I know of. No letters." This was true. There had been no letters for Vane. What there had been, though, was something else entirely, and Tamsin held it behind her teeth the way you hold a hot coal — carefully, and aware that it would burn through eventually.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had been carrying around since yesterday morning, since the postman — old Bertie, who whistled show tunes off-key and had delivered to the Grand Meridian for longer than Tamsin had been alive — had come to the desk with his usual rubber-banded bundle and paused, frowning at it, before handing it over.

"There's one here for you, Miss Lockridge," he'd said. "Personal. Two of them, actually."

Nobody sent Tamsin personal letters. Her mother was eleven years gone; her few friends texted; the hotel's correspondence came addressed to *The Manager* or, more often, misspelled versions of *The Concierge* that suggested the senders thought it was some kind of French cheese. Two personal letters arriving on the same morning was not a coincidence. Two personal letters arriving three days after a guest drowned below the sea wall was something else again.

She hadn't opened them at the desk. She'd put them in her apron pocket, where they lay against her hip all morning like two small stones, warm from her body, heavier than paper had any right to be.

* * *

She opened them that night, in her flat above the boathouse, with the lamp on and the rain doing its usual percussion against the window, and she read them four times each, and by the fourth reading her hands had stopped shaking, which surprised her, because she'd assumed they'd get worse.

The first envelope was cheap, the kind you buy in packs of twenty at the stationer's, and it held a single sheet torn from a hotel notepad — the Grand Meridian's own notepad, cream-colored, embossed with the faded mermaid that had been the hotel's crest since 1924. The handwriting was Vane's. She recognized it from the register: that careful, schoolroom slant, the letters pressed hard enough to dent the page beneath.

It said:

*I have to write this down before I lose my nerve, because if I say it aloud I'll take it back.*

*In 1971 a woman died at this hotel. That is what everyone agreed to say, and saying it for fifty years has made it true, the way saying a thing in ink does. But she did not die. She was helped to disappear, and the helping was done by men who are still respected in this town, and the money that kept everyone quiet has been paid every year since, quietly, in sums small enough to hide inside a hotel's books.*

*I came here to prove it. Someone here knows I'm close. If anything happens to me, it wasn't the rocks and it wasn't the drink.*

*I'm sorry. I should have told you sooner. You of all people.*

Unsigned. Dated the night he died.

Tamsin read it four times, and then she read the second letter, which was typed — properly typed, on a machine with a slightly misaligned *e*, the kind of detail she noticed because noticing was what she was *for* — and which said only:

*Miss Lockridge —*

*You will want to know what the Lockridges owe you. Ask the ledgers. Ask about the housekeeper's line. Come to the centennial prepared.*

*What was taken will be returned.*

No signature either. But the paper was good stock, heavy, the sort used by solicitors and funeral directors, and the envelope bore no return address, only a local postmark.

*The housekeeper's line.*

Her grandmother, Rose Lockridge — no relation, or so the family joke went, the joke being that there was no family, just Rose and then Tamsin's mother and then Tamsin, three generations of women who worked with their hands and kept other people's secrets. Rose had been a housekeeper her whole life. At the Grand Meridian, among other places. Tamsin had grown up on stories of it: the grand lobby, the tides, the guests who were kind and the ones who weren't, the smell of beeswax and salt. Her mother had hated those stories and refused to add to them, and had left for the city at nineteen and come back once, briefly, with a baby and no explanation, and had died at forty-one with her affairs in such perfect order that Tamsin sometimes wondered what she'd been bracing for.

*You of all people,* Vane had written. As if she were part of it. As if she'd been part of it all along without knowing.

She sat with the letters until the lamp burned low and the rain eased and the tide turned, and somewhere in the small hours she became aware that she had stopped being frightened and started being curious, and that this transition, she suspected, was far more dangerous than fear.

* * *

Detective Sorrel came back on Friday.

Tamsin saw her through the lobby glass before she saw the detective — saw the way Sorrel stood a moment on the steps, looking up at the facade the way she imagined surveyors looked at buildings they intended to condemn. Then the door opened, bringing in weather and the smell of the sea wall, and Sorrel crossed the marble floor with the unhurried tread of a woman who had learned that hurrying tells people they've rattled you.

"Miss Lockridge. Do you have a moment?"

"For the police? I have several." She gestured to the desk. "Tea?"

"No, thank you." Sorrel set her notebook down but didn't open it. "A few follow-up questions. Routine."

"Of course."

"Mr. Vane — Julian Pryce." Sorrel let the name drop and watched. Tamsin kept her face pleasant and attentive, the face she wore for guests announcing flight delays. "That was his real name. He was an archivist. Worked out of Exeter, mostly estate records, probate, that sort of thing."

"Goodness," Tamsin said. "He seemed more the holiday type."

"Did he." Sorrel's eyes were gray and steady and missed nothing, which Tamsin respected and resented in equal measure. "We've been through his effects. Some papers. Notes. We're still piecing together why a man researching the Lockridge estate would book into the Lockridge family's hotel under a false name and then drown beneath its sea wall."

"Coincidence can be very tidy," Tamsin said. "My mother always said the sea keeps better records than any clerk."

Something flickered across Sorrel's face — interest, quickly banked. "Did she, now. And your mother's name?"

"Alma Lockridge."

"Lockridge," Sorrel repeated, writing it down anyway, though surely she'd already read it off the staff file. "Any relation to the founding family?"

"The way everyone in this town is related to everyone, Detective, if you go back far enough and squint." She smiled. Sorrel did not smile back. They regarded each other across the desk like two chess players who had both realized, simultaneously, that neither of them was actually playing chess.

"One last thing," Sorrel said, closing the notebook without having written much in it, which Tamsin understood to be the point. "If anything arrives for you connected to Mr. Pryce — a letter, a package, anything — I'd want to know straight away. Posthumous mail can be evidence. Sometimes it's the best evidence there is."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"You do that." Sorrel picked up her notebook, paused. "Your grandmother. Rose, was it? She worked here, I understand. Housekeeping."

The lobby was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a vacuum started and stopped. Out beyond the glass, the tide was coming in over the rocks where Edmund Vane — Julian Pryce — had spent his last evening walking in the rain, asking odd questions, flinching at portraits.

"She did," Tamsin said. "Fifty years ago or more. Why?"

"No reason." Sorrel tucked the notebook away. "Just that names turn up. In old files. Funny how they do that." And she nodded, and left, and Tamsin stood at her desk with her heart going like the faulty lift and her face arranged into perfect professional calm, and only when the detective's car had gone did she allow herself one long breath.

Then she went to the back office, to the locked drawer where she kept her spare keys and her mother's photograph and, now, folded inside a manual for the ancient switchboard, two letters that officially did not exist.

* * *

That night she took them out again, and this time she didn't read them so much as interrogate them.

The confession first. Hotel notepad — so written here, in the building, probably in his room. The ink was blue ballpoint, the pressure heavy; he'd been gripping the pen hard, or cold, or both. *Someone here knows I'm close.* Not *someone knows*. *Someone here.* The hotel itself. And *you of all people* — which meant he'd meant to tell her something, meant to tell *her specifically*, which meant he'd known who she was before he ever arrived, false name and wet shoes and all. He hadn't been checking in. He'd been coming home, in whatever sideways sense that was true.

And the second letter. Typed. Solicitor's stock. Local postmark — posted after his death, which meant whoever sent it was still here, still posting things, still watching to see what she'd do. *Ask the ledgers.* Every hotel kept ledgers; hers kept them in the archive room in the basement, in boxes nobody had opened since the millennium, presided over by a colony of silverfish and the ghost of old Mr. Pardoe, the manager who'd retired in 1989 and still, in a sense, haunted the place, because half the filing system was in his handwriting and defied all logic known to humankind.

*Ask about the housekeeper's line.*

Rose. Her grandmother, who had carried trays along these corridors and known which guests tipped and which ones pinched and which rooms the sea got into during storms. Who had died when Tamsin was nine, leaving a biscuit tin of photographs and a brooch Tamsin still wore on special occasions — a small silver mermaid, tarnished, that had been given to her, Rose said, "by the family," without ever saying which family, in the tone of a woman closing a door.

Tamsin laid the two letters side by side on her kitchen table and looked at them for a long time.

One was from a dead man who had tried to warn her. One was from a living stranger who wanted to arm her. Both pointed the same direction: down, into the basement, into the paper bones of the Grand Meridian, into fifty years of entries in ledgers she had walked past every working day of her adult life without once suspecting that somewhere in all that careful handwriting was her mother's name, her grandmother's name, the whole erased architecture of her own life.

She could take them to Sorrel. That was the sensible thing, the lawful thing, the thing she'd promised the detective she'd do. She pictured herself doing it: sliding the envelopes across the desk, watching Sorrel's gray eyes, surrendering the whole strange inheritance to someone whose job was to make sense of it.

She pictured it, and she found she couldn't.

Not yet. Not until she knew what she was handing over. Because a lifetime at the front desk teaches you one lesson above all others: never give away information you haven't read yourself. Guests will tell you anything if they think you already know it, and detectives, she suspected, were only guests with warrants.

Besides — and this was the thought that settled over her finally, warmly, terribly, like a coat she'd been measured for without knowing — besides, the letters were addressed to *her*. *What the Lockridges owe you.* Not the police. Not the town. Her.

Outside, the tide turned. Down in the basement, behind a door with a sign reading ARCHIVE — KNOCK ANYWAY in Mr. Pardoe's spidery hand, fifty years of the hotel's memory sat waiting in the dark, and tomorrow, Tamsin decided, switching off the lamp, tomorrow she was going to introduce herself to it properly.

She slept badly, and dreamed of ledgers, and woke with the mermaid brooch clutched in her hand, though she had no memory of taking it from its box.
