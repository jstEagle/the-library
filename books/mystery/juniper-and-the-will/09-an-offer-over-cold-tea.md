## 9. An Offer Over Cold Tea

The note came up with the morning post, on cream cardstock, in handwriting so confident it barely touched the page: *Mr. Marcus Lockridge requests the pleasure of Miss Tamsin Lockridge's company for tea. The Palm Court. Four o'clock.*

Tamsin read it twice at the front desk, then a third time while pretending to reconcile the minibar ledger.

"Requests the pleasure," she murmured.

"Of what?" asked Dev, the bell captain, materializing beside her with the particular interest of a man who had worked the Grand Meridian long enough to know that nothing good ever arrived on cream cardstock.

"Nothing," Tamsin said, and filed the note under the desk blotter, where she kept everything that was nothing.

* * *

Marcus Lockridge had been in residence four days, and in that time he had learned every staff member's name, complimented the pastry chef directly, and been photographed by the local paper standing in the lobby looking like a man who had just inherited a painting and couldn't wait to tell you about it. He was forty-ish and handsome in the way of well-kept things — good bones, good tailoring, a smile deployed with the precision of a maître d' seating a difficult party.

Tamsin had watched him work the lobby the way she watched all guests: cataloguing. And what she had catalogued was this — Marcus Lockridge never asked a question he didn't already know the answer to. His questions were performances. Which meant the real questions were happening somewhere else, quietly, without witnesses.

She arrived at the Palm Court at four o'clock precisely, because being early would look eager and being late would look afraid, and she intended to look like neither.

He rose when she came in. Of course he did. He pulled out her chair. Of course he did.

"Miss Lockridge." Her name in his mouth had an odd weight, like a coin he was testing between his teeth. "Thank you for coming. I hope you don't mind — I took the liberty of ordering."

The tea tray sat between them under a silver dome, and beside it, a second object: a manila envelope, unsealed, fat with paper.

"I don't drink tea after three," Tamsin said, sitting anyway. "It keeps me up."

"Then we'll let it go cold together. I find cold tea is excellent for conversations one doesn't want to last." He lifted the dome himself, revealing scones going stiff at the edges, and poured two cups with the ease of a man who had grown up watching other people pour. "How long have you been at the Meridian?"

"Eleven years."

"Eleven years. Remarkable. My grandfather used to say the front desk was the hotel's real brain. Ambrose, I mean. Not the family's fondness for grandfathers generally." He smiled. She did not. "You'd have seen everything, then. Every guest. Every letter."

"Most guests prefer their letters private."

"Oh, absolutely. Privacy is the entire product, isn't it? That's what we're selling. Four hundred rooms of discretion." He pushed a cup toward her. "Which is why I was so distressed to hear about Mr. Vane."

There it was. Tamsin kept her hands still around the warm porcelain. "A tragedy. The whole staff was shaken."

"Were you? I understand you checked him in yourself."

"I check in everyone myself. It's the job."

"And yet." He stirred his tea though there was nothing in it to stir. "The police seem very interested in him. In his papers. I've had two detectives through my office already this week, asking about correspondence. Guests' mail is held at your desk, I take it, until collection?"

"It's held wherever the guest directs. Some have it sent up. Some collect."

"Some collect," Marcus repeated pleasantly. "And some, I imagine, never do. A dead man's mail is such an awkward thing. All those envelopes sitting in a pigeonhole with his name on them, waiting for someone to decide what they are." He looked at her over the rim of his cup. "You must get that sometimes. Mail for people who are never coming back."

The Palm Court hummed around them — the fountain, the distant piano, a tour group being promised the sea view was better after five. Tamsin thought about the two letters currently living inside her mattress, wrapped in a pillowcase, and kept her face arranged in its professional mildness, the one she wore for guests who complained about weather.

"There's a procedure," she said. "Unclaimed mail goes to the manager after thirty days. Then to the police if it's relevant to an open case."

"Efficient." He set down his cup. "I only ask because I've heard — and please correct me if this is gossip, the hotel does breed it like damp — that Mr. Vane received something before he died. Something personal. Hand-delivered, perhaps. Or slipped behind the desk by a guest who didn't want to sign for it."

"I wouldn't know anything about that."

"No?"

"The desk handles hundreds of items a week, Mr. Lockridge. If I remembered every envelope, I'd be no use at all."

Something flickered across his face — not anger; admiration, almost, the way a chess player appreciates a solid defensive structure. He reached for the manila envelope instead.

"Well. Then let me talk about something simpler. Money."

* * *

From the other side of the tea tray, Marcus Lockridge watched the concierge refuse to flinch, and revised his estimate of her upward for the third time since she'd sat down.

His grandfather's ledgers had taught him patience. Fifty years of entries, each one a small brick in the wall his family had built around a lie, and every year Aldous Crane's careful hand recording another payment to a woman who had traded silence for survival. Marcus had spent two nights with the surviving books before realizing the master ledger — the one that mattered, the one Crane had kept closest — was not in the archive at all. Which meant either Crane had destroyed it, or someone else had found it first.

And the archivist, Pryce — Vane, whatever name he'd worn — had spent his final week asking questions at this very desk. Asking *her*.

So here she was: sharp-eyed, calm, eleven years of the hotel's memory sitting behind those polite gray eyes. Either she knew nothing, which made her useless. Or she knew something, which made her dangerous. Or — the possibility Marcus found most interesting — she knew something and didn't yet understand what she knew. Those were the best kind. They could still be managed.

"You may have heard," he said, sliding the envelope across the tablecloth, "that I'm considering the sale of the Meridian."

"I heard you were hosting a party."

"A centennial gala. One hundred years of my family's hospitality." He said it warmly, and meant none of it. "A sale is a natural next step. These old hotels are... heavy. Financially. Emotionally. The past sits in them like water in a foundation." He tapped the envelope. "Severance. Generous — six months' salary, a letter of reference I will personally write, and a quiet word to any buyer that the front desk comes with the building's best asset attached. You could land anywhere you liked. London. Edinburgh. Somewhere the sea doesn't reach."

"That's very kind," Tamsin said. "But I haven't given notice."

"It's not notice. It's foresight. Hotels change hands, Miss Lockridge, and new owners bring new staff. I'm offering you the chance to leave on your own terms, with your pockets full, before terms are decided for you." He leaned back. "And there is a second matter. A warning, freely given, because I admire competence and dislike seeing it wasted."

"Go on."

"There are troublemakers circling this hotel. People digging into old records, stirring up stories from before either of us was born. Grief-eaters, my solicitor calls them — they attach themselves to a family's history and pick until something bleeds. If any of them approach you — ask you to look at ledgers, or remember things, or pass along documents—" He paused, delicately. "I'd want to know. For your protection as much as mine. Trouble has a way of splashing."

The fountain chose that moment to gurgle, absurdly, like punctuation.

Tamsin turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "What kind of trouble, exactly?"

"The kind that ends careers. Sometimes worse." He said it lightly, a man discussing weather. "The police detective — Sorrel — has been asking whether Mr. Vane spoke to anyone on staff. Whether he left anything behind. You can imagine how that sort of attention falls on whoever was closest to him. An innocent person can drown in suspicion as easily as a guilty one. Distance is a kindness I'm able to offer."

* * *

*Trouble has a way of splashing.* Tamsin held the phrase up to the light while her face stayed pleasant, and found it interesting in three ways.

First: he hadn't said *if* anyone approaches you. He'd said *when*, dressed as *if*. Second: he knew about Sorrel's questions — fine, Sorrel had interviewed half the staff. But third, and this was the one that mattered, the one she turned over like a keycard with the numbers worn off: he knew about the letters. Not guessed. Knew. *Hand-delivered, perhaps. Slipped behind the desk.* Nobody had told Sorrel that detail because nobody but Tamsin knew it — except the person who had done the delivering, or the person who had gone through Vane's room afterward, or the person who had gone through Vane's mail.

Mail that should have been sitting in pigeonhole 214, waiting for a dead man who was never coming back.

She had checked, the night after the drowning. Pigeonhole 214 had been empty. She'd assumed housekeeping cleared it during the usual chaos. Now she watched Marcus Lockridge butter a scone he clearly wasn't going to eat, and understood that the empty pigeonhole had not been housekeeping.

"You're very concerned for me," she said.

"I'm concerned for the hotel. Its people come with it." He smiled his lobby smile. "My grandfather built this place on a simple principle: everyone who works here is family."

"I've read the pension plan," Tamsin said. "I know exactly how family is treated."

For a heartbeat the smile thinned, and she saw beneath it — not rage, but calculation recalibrating, a man discovering that the pretty lock on the door had teeth inside. Then it smoothed over, seamless as fresh linen.

"Fair," he said. "Well. No pressure today. Take the envelope, look it over. Think about which side of the splash line you'd rather stand on." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and dropped a tip on the tray for a service neither of them had used. "One more thing, since we're being honest with each other — we are being honest, I feel it strongly — the archive. The old records room off the east stair. Has it been open lately? I've asked maintenance twice about the lock and gotten nowhere. You know how this place is. Doors that were locked yesterday are open today."

"I really couldn't say, Mr. Lockridge. I work the front desk."

"So you do." He said it the way other men said *so you claim*. "Enjoy the tea. Cold tea keeps, I'm told. Like secrets."

He walked out through the Palm Court trailing charm behind him like a coat, pausing to greet the tour group, laughing at something the guide said, and Tamsin sat alone with two cups of cooling tea and an envelope full of money she would never touch.

She didn't open it. Opening it would make it a transaction, and she wanted no part of whatever accounting he was running. Instead she turned her cup over and read the maker's mark on the bottom — Wedgwood, 1971, part of a service bought the year everything began — and thought about pigeonhole 214, and about the word *splashing*, and about a man named M. Ashcombe who had checked out of Room 412 before dawn and never been seen again.

Then she went back to the desk, signed out the master key to the archive under the pretext of a fire-safety inventory, and climbed the east stair with her heart beating in a rhythm she recognized from the night she'd first broken the confession letter's cipher: not fear, exactly. Appetite.

Because Marcus Lockridge had made one mistake over the cold tea, and he'd made it in his first sixty seconds. He had asked about the mail — mail he could only know about if it had passed through hands that weren't hers.

And now she knew exactly whose hands to watch.

Upstairs, in the dark of the shuttered fourth floor, Room 412 waited with its dust and its secrets, and somewhere below, in a penthouse that smelled of camphor and old paper, an old man was feeding pages to a fire — though Tamsin wouldn't learn that until tomorrow, when she found the ash.

Tonight, she had a lock to pick.
