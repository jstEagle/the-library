## 12. Low Tide, High Stakes

The tide table did not lie. Tamsin had checked it three times that morning, once in the newspaper, once in the almanac behind the front desk, and once — because she was a thorough woman and thorough women double-check their double-checks — on the chalkboard by the boathouse, where old Fenwick recorded the tides in handwriting like a spider drowning in ink.

*Lowest tide of the year. 4:47 p.m.*

Which meant that at 4:47 this afternoon, the sea would pull back its grey skirt and show everyone what it had been hiding for fifty years.

She told herself she was going down to the beach for her break. A walk. Fresh air. Perfectly normal behavior for a concierge whose legs ached from standing behind a desk shaped like a coffin lid. She even brought a thermos of tea to sell the story to herself, though the tea was mostly an excuse to have something to grip.

The Grand Meridian sat on its bluff like an aging dowager who refused to admit the neighborhood had gone downhill, and below it the sea wall ran along the shingle in a long, cracked curve of Victorian granite. Tamsin had walked that beach ten thousand times in eleven years of employment. She knew every rock, every rusted mooring ring, every place the wall's foundation showed above the sand at ordinary low water.

But today was not ordinary. Today the sea would retreat further than it had in a decade and a half, and somewhere beneath all that wet grey history was the base of the wall — and, if the 1971 police report was to be believed, something that had been reported missing from it fifty-three years ago and never found.

*Item: one strongbox, iron, approximately eighteen inches by twelve, recovered from the Vane residence during initial inquiries and subsequently unaccounted for.* She had read the sentence so many times she could recite it the way other people recited prayers. The report was careful, bureaucratic, and full of holes you could drive a hearse through. Evidence logged. Evidence vanished. Case closed as misadventure. Everyone moved on, except apparently the sea, which never moved on from anything.

Tamsin crunched across the shingle at twenty past four, thermos swinging, wind pulling her hair into a shape that would have horrified the hotel's dress code if the dress code had ever considered what happened to hair outdoors. Gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their opinions. The light was that strange coastal gold that made everything look important, including driftwood, including seaweed, including her.

The tide was already far out. Further than she'd ever seen it. The sand lay exposed in great glistening flats, ribbed like something breathing, pools left behind in the hollows holding upside-down pieces of sky. And there — she stopped walking — there was the base of the sea wall, revealed for the first time in her tenure, running down into the earth farther than any wall reasonably needed to. The Victorians had built things to last and to hide, usually both at once.

She walked along it slowly, letting her eyes do the work they were made for. Noticing was what Tamsin Lockridge was for. Eleven years of watching guests had taught her that people and walls had a great deal in common: both presented a face, both kept their real business at the foundations.

The lower courses of the wall were furred with barnacles and dark weed, but here and there the stone had been disturbed. Repaired. She crouched. A section perhaps four feet wide had been rebuilt at some point with newer mortar, newer stone — not Victorian granite but cheaper twentieth-century block, mortared over the old work like a patch sewn onto good cloth by someone in a hurry.

Someone had opened the wall and closed it again.

Her heart began doing something undignified. She set the thermos down on a rock with exaggerated calm, as though the thermos might judge her, and knelt in the wet sand.

The patched section was at the wall's base, half-buried still, the tide having only just given it up. Sand had packed against it in a slope. She dug with her hands — cold, immediate, absurdly physical work — clearing a fan of grit until the shape underneath resolved.

Iron. Rusted to the color of dried blood and old chocolate. Rectangular. Roughly eighteen inches by twelve.

Tamsin sat back on her heels and stared at it.

"Well," she said aloud, to the gulls, because someone had to say something and the gulls were unlikely to repeat it. "Hello."

It matched the report. It matched the description in Pryce's notes, which she had copied out twice and hidden in two places and memorized besides, because Tamsin believed in redundancy the way some people believed in saints. *Strongbox, iron, contents unknown, last documented in police custody, September 1971.* Here it was. Fifty-three years in the dark, and it had been under the wall the whole time — walled in, literally, by whoever had taken it, in a repair job that must have happened within days of the so-called drowning. Someone had stood on this exact beach with a box of secrets and a bucket of mortar and had built the past into the foundations of the Grand Meridian.

The hotel kept its books in the ledgers. Apparently it kept its evidence in the masonry.

She touched the hasp. It was fused with rust; the lock itself had surrendered decades ago, probably, but the lid had swollen shut with salt and time. She'd need a pry bar, or Sorrel, or both. Preferably both — Sorrel came with gloves, evidence bags, and an official capacity to be annoyed on her behalf.

*Don't touch it,* said the sensible voice in her head, the one that sounded suspiciously like Detective Sorrel. *Don't disturb the scene. Call it in. Let the professionals—*

The sensible voice was interrupted by the tide table, which Tamsin consulted now with rising alarm. The lowest point was 4:47. It was 4:31. Which meant that in sixteen minutes the sea would begin its patient, indifferent return, and this box — this one object, this single surviving piece of proof that the Lockridge fortune rested on a staged death — would go back under the water, possibly forever, possibly into the hands of whoever else knew about the tide tables.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. She hadn't found this through genius alone. She'd found it because Pryce's notes pointed here, and Pryce was dead, and the person who killed him — if it was murder, when it was murder — might well have read the same notes. Might know exactly what the extreme tide would uncover. Might be planning to come down here tonight, or tomorrow, or next week, and quietly remove the last piece of evidence between themselves and a clean getaway.

Or had already come. Her eyes swept the shingle. No footprints near the wall except her own. No disturbed stones. Just her, the box, and sixteen minutes.

Tamsin looked at the strongbox. The strongbox, being iron, looked back with the smugness of an object that had outlasted everyone who wanted it buried.

"Right," she told it. "I'm not leaving you."

She tried the hasp again. Rust flaked; the lid groaned but held. She looked around the beach for a tool and found, naturally, nothing but stones, weed, and one depressingly intact plastic crab. She considered the thermos. The thermos, sensing danger, said nothing.

Think. She was good at thinking. What did she have? Hands. A coat. Keys — hotel keys, including the ancient brass ones from the archive room, one of which had a stem thick enough to qualify as a small weapon. She selected the oldest key on her ring, worked it into the gap beneath the hasp, and levered.

Nothing. She adjusted the angle. Levered harder, bracing her boot against the wall.

The key bent. The lid laughed at her, insofar as rust can laugh.

"Fine," she panted. "Fine. We'll do this the embarrassing way."

She wrapped her cardigan around her hands — the good cardigan, the one with the buttons, sacrificed now to history — gripped the hasp with both fists, and threw her whole weight backward.

The hasp tore free with a shriek of metal that sent the gulls up off the rocks in a clatter of outrage. The lid lifted an inch, stuck, lifted another, and then came loose entirely with a sound like a coffin agreeing to open.

Inside: a plastic-wrapped bundle, black with age but sealed, and beneath it, wedged tight, a second package — oilcloth, tied with wire, the kind of wrapping done by hands that intended the contents to survive. Water had gotten in somewhere; the bottom of the box held a finger of dark seawater and fine silt. But the oilcloth bundle sat above the waterline, dry-ish, patient.

Tamsin's hands were shaking. She noticed this with detached professional interest, the way she noticed when a guest's hands shook signing a bill they couldn't afford. She unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of someone defusing something, because in every way that mattered, she was.

Paper. Dozens of pages, foxed at the edges, typed and handwritten both. On top, a document with an official-looking header, and even before she read the words she saw the annotation across it in red pencil, in a hand she recognized from fifty years of hotel correspondence:

**FICTITIOUS — DO NOT FILE.**

Ambrose Lockridge's initials beside it. A.L., the same confident loops that signed the centennial ledger, the same hand that had written her family's erasure in sums of money paid out yearly since 1971.

Below the certificate, photographs. She turned them over with fingertips gone numb from cold and adrenaline. A woman on a station platform, face turned half away, unmistakable all the same — Tamsin knew that jawline, that stubborn set of the shoulders, because she shaved above it every morning. Her grandmother. Younger than any photograph Tamsin had ever seen of her, and alive, and walking away from a life that had officially ended at the base of this wall.

More photographs. The woman older. A house in another town. A child on her hip — Tamsin's mother, a baby, blinking at a camera held by someone the picture didn't show.

They lived. That was the thing no ledger line had managed to tell her. The payments, the hush money, the forged death — all of it, and underneath it all, her grandmother had simply *lived*. Had carried a baby. Had grown old. Had died quiet and unfamous in a rented cottage while the sea kept her official grave.

Tamsin sat down fully in the wet sand, because her legs had resigned without notice, and held the photographs, and let herself feel it — all of it, the grief and the fury and the strange, fierce tenderness — for exactly as long as the tide allowed.

Which was less than she'd have liked.

She checked her watch. 4:52. The water was already sliding back up the flats, quickening, the pools joining hands. She repacked the oilcloth bundle with clumsy reverence, tucked it inside her coat against her body where it warmed immediately, and looked at the strongbox. She couldn't carry it and climb the cliff path at speed. She couldn't leave it — the returning tide would cover it, but cover wasn't protect; anyone with a torch and a low tide tomorrow could find it again.

Photograph it first. She took pictures with her phone — the box in situ, the patched masonry, the empty interior, the waterline creeping close — timestamped, geotagged, redundant. Then she dragged the box itself up beyond the new tide line, behind a shelf of rock, and piled loose stones over it in an arrangement that would fool absolutely no one looking for it but would at least hide it from casual eyes and buy her the hours until Sorrel arrived.

Then she climbed.

The cliff path switchbacked up the headland, and Tamsin took it faster than a woman carrying fifty years of stolen paper should, her breath sawing, the bundle pressed to her ribs like a second heartbeat. Halfway up she paused at the bend where the path crossed the old lookout — the flat stone where generations of hotel guests had posed for photographs with the sea behind them — and she stopped.

Not for breath. Though she needed it.

At the top of the path, where it met the coast road, a figure stood beside a parked car. Dark coat. Stillness of the particular kind she'd learned to read across a lobby: not the stillness of someone admiring the view, but the stillness of someone counting. She'd seen Edmund Vane stand exactly that way in the rain, checking the register with his eyes while his hands stayed carefully idle.

The figure was too far away for features. But it was watching the beach. Watching the wall. Watching, quite possibly, her — a small dark smudge on the pale path, caught precisely between the secret and the road, visible to anyone who cared to look, which evidently someone did.

The car door opened. Closed. The figure began, unhurriedly, to descend.

Tamsin did the arithmetic she hated most: the path down took eight minutes. The path to the service gate behind the boathouse took five, if she cut across the lookout and didn't break an ankle, and she had spent eleven years learning every shortcut this cliff owned. She had a head start, a bundle of proof against her ribs, and a phone with one bar of signal and Sorrel's number half-dialed.

She also had, she realized, pressing herself into the shadow of the gorse at the path's bend, a clear view downward — and the descending figure stepped out of the glare for just a moment, onto the open stretch below the lookout, and the light gave her one clean look at the face.

She knew it. Of course she knew it. She was the concierge of the Grand Meridian; knowing faces was the entire job.

The figure paused on the path, directly beneath her, close enough now that she could hear the sea working at the wall below, and looked back up the cliff — straight at the gorse, straight at the shadow, straight, it seemed, at her.

And smiled.
