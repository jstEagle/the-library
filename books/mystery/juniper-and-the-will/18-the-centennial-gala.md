## 18. The Centennial Gala

* * *

Marcus Lockridge had planned the evening down to the last champagne flute, and the last champagne flute was the point.

Three hundred of them, ordered from a supplier in Lyon who had assured him — twice, in writing — that they were identical to the flutes used at the hotel's opening night in 1925. He had held one up to the light in the empty ballroom that afternoon and found it satisfactory. The stem was slender. The bowl caught the chandelier light and scattered it. When three hundred guests raised them at nine o'clock sharp, the room would fill with a single bright glitter, like the sea catching sun, and every camera in Cornwall would capture it.

That was the image he was selling. Not the hotel itself — the Grand Meridian was a beautiful corpse, its plumbing older than his father, its ballroom floor soft in two places he'd had rugs laid over — but the idea of it. A hundred years of salt air and discretion. A century of people who knew how to keep things quiet.

He had spent the morning reviewing the guest list with the focus other men reserved for love letters.

The MP from Truro: coming, bringing his wife, leaving early. The auction house people: confirmed. Two journalists from the broadsheets who wrote about heritage properties the way other writers wrote about doomed romances: seated at table four, plied generously. Detective Sergeant Sorrel had not been invited, which was not the same as saying she would not attend; he had put a word with the doorman to be pleasant about it if she appeared, and another word with himself to be pleasant about it too. Better she stood in his ballroom drinking his champagne than prowled his corridors.

And Tamsin Lockridge — no, not Lockridge, whatever the probate clerks were muttering; Tamsin *Verran*, housekeeper's line, a name that had surfaced like a coin at the bottom of a wishing well — would be there as staff. He had insisted on it personally, through the events manager, with a generosity he wanted witnessed. *All centenary staff to attend the first hour as guests.* It looked gracious. It was also useful. He wanted to see her in the room. He wanted to see what she did with her hands when he gave the toast.

Because here was the arithmetic Marcus performed each night before sleep, and again each morning before coffee: Edmund Vane — Julian Pryce, the archivist, the man with the wet shoes and the careful questions — had come to the Grand Meridian carrying something. People like Pryce always carried something. And then Pryce had gone into the sea, and whatever he carried had not been found on him, which meant it was either in the sea or in the hotel, and Marcus intended to buy the hotel, gut it, and sell it as eleven luxury residences before anyone went looking.

Champagne, he had learned from his grandfather, was excellent for burying things. Everyone remembered the toast. Nobody remembered what was said just before it.

He straightened his cufflinks — his grandfather's, gold, engraved with an *A.L.* he had never had removed because removal would have required explaining — and went down to greet his century.

* * *

In the kitchen, Mrs. Pengelly was losing a war with a vol-au-vent and winning a war with everything else.

"More parsley," she commanded, and a scullery boy obeyed. "No, the *flat* parsley. Curly parsley is for funerals." She caught sight of Devlin, the second barman, hovering by the pass with a tray of canapés and an expression of theological doubt. "What."

"Mrs. P., is it true the new owner's selling us?"

"It's true nobody's told us anything, which in my experience means yes." She jabbed a spoon at him. "You'll hear it tonight with everyone else, same as the newspapers. So stand up straight, keep the trays moving, and if any of those London lot ask you about the history of the place, you smile and say it's been in the family a hundred years and let them draw their own conclusions."

"Whose family, though?" said Devlin, who was twenty-two and had not yet learned which questions got him extra chips and which got him nothing.

"That," said Mrs. Pengelly, "is tonight's entertainment." She surveyed the kitchen — the gleaming trays, the terrified waiters, the soufflé situation holding at Defcon Three — and permitted herself one small satisfied breath. Forty years she had fed this hotel. Whatever happened at the gala, whoever bought the bones of the place, no one could say the food had ever once been less than magnificent. There were worse epitaphs.

At the service door, unremarked, a figure in a waiter's jacket paused to collect a tray of flutes. The figure moved through the kitchen the way water moves through a wreck — knowing where every door stuck, which corridor smelled of damp, exactly how long the dumbwaiter took between floors. Mrs. Pengelly glanced up, frowned faintly at the back of the young woman's head as though trying to place her among the agency staff, and failed, and returned to her vol-au-vents.

Tamsin Verran had worked this hotel for six years. Apparently that qualified as a disguise.

* * *

By eight o'clock the ballroom had filled to a warm, murmuring glow.

Tamsin circulated with her tray and her training, and observed.

She observed the journalists at table four, already comparing notes on the chandeliers. She observed the MP from Truro telling a story about a pheasant to a woman who had clearly heard it before. She observed Marcus Lockridge working the room in a midnight-blue suit, laughing at the right depth, touching shoulders at the right frequency, a man performing ownership of a thing he was quietly selling — and doing it so well that half the room would go home thinking they'd attended a celebration rather than an estate liquidation.

And she observed, at the edge of her vision, near the French doors, a woman in a grey dress holding a glass of champagne she wasn't drinking.

Detective Sorrel, off duty, out of uniform, and pretending very hard to admire the sea.

Their eyes met across the room. Sorrel raised her glass perhaps half an inch — a gesture that could have meant *good evening*, or *I know you're wearing a wire under that tray*, or simply *the canapés are better than I expected*. Tamsin returned it identically. Between them passed the entire unsaid correspondence of the past month: the letters, the ledger, the strongbox, the photograph of Crane's last entry that lived folded in Tamsin's bra strap like a splinter of ice.

*Not yet,* Sorrel's eyebrows said.

*I know,* Tamsin's tray said.

But the brooch — the brooch was hers alone to know about, for now. Her grandmother's silver housekeeper's pin, restored by a jeweller in Penzance who had wept slightly over it ("They don't make hinges like this anymore, miss, they don't make *anything* like this"), polished until the little monogram — *E.V.*, Edith Verran — shone like a small moon at Tamsin's collar. The codicil's token. Ambrose Lockridge's own lawyer had described it to her, in the document she probably shouldn't have photographed: *to the beneficiary of the housekeeper's line, who will know herself by the brooch.*

She knew herself. That had taken rather longer than the brooch had.

"You're new," said a voice beside her, and she turned to find one of the London journalists, a man with kind eyes and a notebook he thought was hidden. "Agency?"

"Temporarily," Tamsin said, which was true in more directions than he could know.

"Remarkable old place. You must know all the stories."

"A few," said Tamsin, and smiled, and moved on, and felt the brooch sit warm against her throat like a hand.

* * *

Marcus felt the evening tipping toward perfect at ten minutes to nine, which was precisely when he should have known better.

The string quartet had just finished. The room had settled into that hush that precedes speeches, the particular silence of three hundred people deciding whether to listen. He stepped to the head of the room, beneath the portrait of Ambrose Lockridge — which the events manager had insisted on hanging for the occasion and which Marcus had privately decided would be the first thing to come down after the sale — and lifted his flute.

"Ladies and gentlemen. One hundred years ago, my great-grandfather opened these doors and made a promise." He let the pause breathe. "That anyone who came to the Grand Meridian would find comfort, discretion, and the best view in England. Tonight we honour that promise. Tonight we—" 

He saw her then.

Not Tamsin — he'd clocked Tamsin twenty minutes ago, tray and all, and filed her away. He saw the *brooch*. Silver, small, antique, sitting at the collar of a waitress who wore it the way other people wore crowns. He had seen that brooch before. In Crane's papers. In the codicil's description, read by lamplight with the door locked, in the week after Pryce drowned, when he had begun to understand what his inheritance actually consisted of.

*Who will know herself by the brooch.*

His glass stayed level. His voice, when it resumed, did not falter by a semitone — he counted on that later, with pride. But the speech changed shape around the discovery, the way water changes around a rock. He heard himself arrive safely at the end: "...and so, to the next hundred years. To the Grand Meridian."

"To the Grand Meridian!" said three hundred voices, and three hundred flutes rose and caught the light, and the glitter was exactly as he had designed it, and Marcus Lockridge stood in the middle of his own triumph feeling the floor tilt beneath the rug he'd laid over the soft spot.

Across the room, the waitress set down her tray, unhurried, and began — calmly, terribly — to remove her waiter's jacket.

Underneath, she was not in uniform at all. Underneath was a plain dark dress, and at the collar, the brooch, and above the brooch, a face that was looking directly at him with the mild, patient attention of someone who has read your mail.

Marcus smiled at his guests. He began, smoothly, to move toward the service corridor behind the quartet's dais — the one that ran past the old linen rooms, the one on the plans marked with his own pencil annotations — calculating exits, calculating time, calculating whether the woman in grey by the French doors had moved.

She had. She was already standing beside the service door. Holding it open.

"After you, Mr. Lockridge," said Detective Sorrel pleasantly. "Or we can stay for dessert. Chef's done something extraordinary with the lemon."

* * *

Tamsin stood in the sudden hush of the ballroom — the toast finished, the arrest not yet begun, the whole room balanced on a knife-edge of not-knowing — and touched her grandmother's brooch once, lightly, the way you'd touch a talisman or a trigger.

Then she stepped up onto the bandstand, took the microphone from its stand, and cleared her throat.

"Ladies and gentlemen," she said, as three hundred faces turned toward her, and somewhere near the doors Marcus Lockridge stopped dead, "before the dancing starts, I've been asked to read a few entries from the hotel's centennial ledger. Dated entries. Room numbers. Sums of money — paid out, every year, since 1971."

She unfolded the pages, and the chandelier light caught them, and the room leaned in.

"The first entry reads..."
