## 2. Paid in Advance

The rain came to Juniper Hollow the way bad news did—sideways, without apology, and determined to get into every seam. By four o'clock on the last Tuesday of May, the Meridian's lobby windows were streaked silver, and the brass umbrella stand by the door had filled to capacity with the town's collective denial that weather existed here at all.

I stood behind the front desk with my ledger open and a cup of tea going cold beside me, which was how I spent most afternoons, and most mornings, and a fair number of evenings I'd rather not itemize. The Meridian was not a grand hotel, whatever the brochure Ruth wrote in 1987 claimed. It was three stories of salt-bleached clapboard with an elevator that moved at the speed of continental drift and a lobby that smelled permanently of lemon polish and old carpet. But it had been standing since 1911, and it had outlasted two fires, one flood, and every attempt by the Brandt family to buy it, which in Juniper Hollow counted as a distinguished service record.

The ledger in front of me was mine. Not the official one—the official one lived in a leather-bound book that guests could see, all neat columns of arrivals and departures, room numbers and rates. The other ledger, the real one, was a plain composition notebook I bought in packs of six from the drugstore, and it recorded what actually mattered: who paid cash and why, who asked for rooms facing away from the harbor, who took phone calls in the lobby alcove at eleven p.m. and hung up when I walked past.

Ruth Calloway taught me the system. Twenty years ago, when I was twenty-three and freshly failed out of a library science program on the mainland, she hired me as a night clerk and put a composition notebook in my hands on my first shift.

"Guests tell you everything," she said, "if you write down what they don't say."

She'd been concierge for thirty years by then. She knew that Councilman Brandt always requested Room 210 because he believed the third floor was unlucky, and that Mrs. Okafor sent her linen shirts out to be pressed even though the hotel offered pressing, because she didn't trust anyone in this town with her collars. She knew which marriages were ending before the couples did. She kept it all in notebooks that filled a shelf in the back office, decades of them, spines labeled in her small precise hand: *1979–81*, *1981–84*. A librarian's instinct applied to human beings instead of books.

I inherited the shelf when she died. That was fourteen months ago now, though some days I still reached for the phone to ask her something—a guest's history, a repairman's reputation—and remembered that the only person who knew more about this town than Ruth was the town itself, and the town didn't return calls.

The anniversary was coming. June ninth. I had marked it in my personal calendar with a small black dot, the way you mark a dental appointment: necessary, dreaded, survivable.

Which is why I noticed the woman the moment she came through the door, because grief makes you observant in self-defense, and also because she was doing such a poor job of being unremarkable.

* * *

She came in like the weather had personally selected her for punishment. Rain plastered her dark hair flat to her skull, and her coat—a good coat once, wool, charcoal, the kind of thing you'd wear to a funeral or a deposition—hung off her shoulders with the defeated sag of fabric that had been wet for hours. Mid-thirties, maybe. Tall in the way of someone who used to stand straighter. She crossed the lobby leaving small dark footprints on the tile, and she stopped in front of the desk and looked at me with the expression of a person who has rehearsed a speech so many times the words have worn smooth.

"Room," she said. Then, correcting herself with visible effort: "I'd like a room, please. For a month."

"A month." I glanced at the rack. Early season, we had space; the summer crowds wouldn't arrive until July, when the mainlanders came to photograph the lighthouse and complain about the fog. "We have several available. Are you looking for anything particular? Harbor view is—"

"No view." The answer came too fast. "Something interior. Facing the courtyard. Or the parking lot. Whatever faces away from the water."

Most people wanted the water. That was the whole point of Juniper Hollow, if you asked the chamber of commerce: the gray-green sweep of the sound, the lighthouse, the gulls wheeling over the breakwater like punctuation marks. People paid extra for the view. In twenty years I could count on one hand the guests who'd asked to face away from it, and I remembered every one of them, because they were the interesting ones—the widow, the debtor, the man who swore the tide talked to him.

I turned the register toward her. "Name?"

A pause. Long enough that rain dripped audibly from her hem onto the tile, a small metronome counting out her hesitation.

"Voss," she said. "Ada Voss."

I wrote it down. My pen hovered over the address line, and she watched it hover, and then she said, "I'll leave that blank, if it's all the same to you."

"It's not, technically."

"I'll pay extra for it to be."

There it was. Most people haggled over the rate. This one was prepared to haggle over her own existence. I looked up from the register and gave her the full benefit of my attention, which is a thing I ration carefully, and she bore it the way people bear cold water—with a stiffening of the jaw and a refusal to flinch first.

Up close she had the kind of face that had been pretty before something happened to it. Not violence—I'd seen violence, or its aftermath, in the faces of guests who checked in at odd hours. This was more like erosion. Sleeplessness, probably, and the particular exhaustion of someone who has been driving for a long time or running for longer.

"Cash," she said, and set a padded envelope on the desk. It made a heavier sound than envelopes usually make.

I opened it with the professional neutrality of long practice. Thirty-one hundred dollars in twenties and fifties, soft old bills, some of them banded, some loose. A month at the Meridian ran twenty-two hundred with tax. I said so.

"Keep the difference," she said. "For the inconvenience."

"What inconvenience?"

"The one I'm about to cause you."

I want to be clear about my state of mind at this moment, because later—much later—I would take a certain grim pride in the fact that I logged everything, even when I didn't yet know what *everything* meant. At that moment, though, my professional assessment was simple: either a woman fleeing a husband, or a woman fleeing creditors, or a woman with a medication problem. All three categories checked in regularly. All three preferred cash. All three asked for rooms without views, because they were watching the road, or watching their backs, or watching nothing at all because looking at open water reminded them how easy it would be.

I gave her back eight hundred dollars. She stared at it.

"The rate is the rate," I told her. "Ruth Calloway's rule. We don't overcharge people who are already having a bad month."

"You knew Ruth?" Something moved across her face—too quick to name, gone before I could catalogue it. "Everyone here seems to have known Ruth."

"She worked this desk for fifty years. Hard to avoid her." I slid a key across the counter, heavy brass fob, the number 214 stamped into the leather. "Second floor, end of the hall. Faces the courtyard. Breakfast is seven to ten. If anyone calls asking for you, I take messages and I don't confirm you're here unless you tell me otherwise in advance."

Her hand closed around the key and for a moment she just held it, like it was evidence in a trial she hadn't expected to win.

"That's very thorough," she said.

"It's the job."

"No," she said quietly. "It isn't. I've stayed in forty hotels in the last six months. None of them offered. You offered before I asked."

I had no answer for that, because it was true, and because the truth was that Ruth had trained me to offer before people asked—it saved them the humiliation of requesting, and humiliation, Ruth said, was the thing that made guests do unpredictable things.

Ada Voss picked up her bag—a canvas duffel, army surplus, light enough that I noted she wasn't planning to stay anywhere long no matter what she'd paid—and walked toward the stairs. She got four steps before she stopped.

Turned around. Came back to the desk.

And here is where I should have understood, should have felt the first hairline crack run through the orderly masonry of my afternoon. Instead I registered only that she'd left wet footprints in a second direction, doubling the mess, and that Mrs. Okafor's Thursday delivery would track mud through it if the driver came early.

"Ms. Voss?"

"I need to ask you something," she said. Her voice had changed. The rehearsal was over; this was the live performance, and her hands on the counter were not quite steady. "And I need you to hear the whole thing before you decide I'm crazy. Can you do that? Hear the whole thing?"

"I'm a concierge. Hearing whole things is most of the job."

"On the solstice," she said. "June twenty-first. Someone is going to be murdered in this town."

The lobby clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice, the way it always did when the hot water ran on the second floor. Outside, the rain kept up its indifferent percussion against the glass.

"That's a serious accusation," I said.

"It's not an accusation. Accusations are about things that happened." She leaned forward, and I caught the smell of wet wool and, beneath it, something sharper—fear has a smell, I've learned, metallic and faintly sweet, like coins held too long in a fist. "This hasn't happened yet. That's the point. That's why I'm here early. There's time. There's still time, but not much, and I can't do it alone, and I've been asking everyone and no one—" She stopped herself. Breathed. Started again, slower, the words laid down like stones across a creek. "I need help stopping a murder that hasn't happened yet. Will you help me?"

I have refused many requests in twenty years at the Meridian. Refused late checkouts, refused refunds, refused the gentleman who wanted me to hold his mistress's letters, refused the Okafor cousin who wanted to know which room his ex-wife was in. Refusal is a muscle, and mine was well developed. It flexed automatically.

"Ms. Voss. I run a hotel. If you believe someone's in danger, the police station is four blocks down, on Harbor Street. Deputy Quintero is—"

"They won't listen." Flat. Final. The voice of someone who had already made that stop, or stops like it, in towns whose names I would never learn. "They listen after. Everyone listens after. I need someone who'll listen *before*, and I walked past your window and I saw you writing in that book, and I thought—that person keeps records. That person might believe paper."

I looked down at my ledger. My innocent, faithful ledger, with its columns of arrivals and its margins full of small observations. *Mrs. Okonkwo, rm 108, requested extra pillows, mentioned daughter's graduation.* *Brandt party, dinner reservation, 8pm, table away from windows.*

Paper. She thought I might believe paper.

"Who," I said, "is going to be murdered?"

And there—there was the crack, though I mistook it at the time for mere curiosity. Because a proper concierge would have ended the conversation at *the police station is four blocks down.* A proper concierge does not ask follow-up questions about hypothetical homicides in her lobby at five o'clock on a rainy Tuesday. A proper concierge maintains the membrane between the hotel and the town's troubles, the way the building maintained its roof against the weather.

But Ruth was dead fourteen months, and the anniversary was nine days out, and I was tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix, and Ada Voss was looking at me with the terrible earnestness of a person who has been called crazy in enough towns to recognize the expression forming on my face and was begging me, silently, not to finish it.

"I can't tell you the name," she said. "Not yet. If I say it out loud and you repeat it—if it gets back to them—they'll change things. Move it up. Do you understand? Naming it makes it real to them, and they're waiting for me to name it."

"*Them*."

"People who have been getting away with something for thirty years." Her eyes went to the shelf behind me—not my working ledgers, but the older ones, Ruth's, the spines with their careful labels. Something in her face cracked open, just slightly, and what showed through was so raw I had to look away from it. "Thirty years," she said again, softer. "You have no idea."

"Ms. Voss." I closed the ledger. A deliberate gesture, a door shutting. "Here is what I can do. You've paid for a month; you'll have your month, quiet and uninterrupted. If you receive threats, if someone bothers you, if you feel unsafe in this building, come to me immediately and I will act on it. If you have evidence of a crime—actual evidence, something a deputy could hold in her hand—take it to Marisol Quintero, and if she gives you trouble, tell her Theo Marchetti vouches for you. Those are the terms. They're generous ones."

"And the murder?"

"I don't involve myself in things that haven't happened," I said. "That's not what the ledger is for."

She studied me for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Upstairs, faintly, the pipes knocked again.

"What *is* it for?" she asked.

"Memory," I said. "Somebody in this town has to remember things accurately. It might as well be the person at the desk."

Something flickered in her expression at that—something almost like recognition, or grief, or both wearing each other's coats. She nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a theory.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Then log this. Since you keep records." She leaned in, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing, meant for me alone under the hum of the lobby lights. "On June twenty-first, the solstice, someone dies. The same way someone died thirty years ago. Same place, same method, same everything. It's a copy. And the people who did the first one are going to let the second one happen, because the second one protects the first one. Log that. Please. Date it. Sign it. So that when it happens, somebody in this town has to admit they were told."

I should have laughed. I should have given her the gentle, patronizing smile reserved for guests who believed the rooms were bugged, and sent her up to 214 with a recommendation for chamomile. Every protocol I owned said to do exactly that.

Instead I opened the ledger. I dated the page. And in the margin, in my smallest handwriting, in the space where I usually noted pillow preferences and dinner reservations, I wrote:

*Voss, A., rm 214. Claims murder scheduled for solstice (6/21), re: event 30 yrs prior. Declined involvement. Advised police. Guest declined.*

"There," I said. "Logged."

She read it upside down, which takes a certain talent, and exhaled—a long, shaking breath, like a diver surfacing.

"Thank you," she whispered. "You have no idea what you just—thank you."

"Don't thank me. I wrote you down as a crank, Ms. Voss. That entry says I think you're disturbed."

"It says you *heard* me." She picked up her duffel. "That's more than anyone else has done. You'll see. In three weeks you'll read that entry back, and you'll understand why I paid in advance."

She climbed the stairs. I watched her go, thin and dripping and straight-backed, turning at the landing once to look at me—not checking whether I was watching her, I realized, but checking whether I was still writing.

Then she was gone up the stairwell, and the lobby settled back into its evening shapes: lamps warming, rain on glass, the clock ticking toward the dinner reservations.

I sat with my tea and told myself the unease in my chest was the anniversary approaching. Grief does that—loosens things, lets strange weather in. Nine days to June ninth. Perfectly natural to feel a crack in the walls this time of year.

At seven, Mr. Adeyemi came down for his usual corner table and complained, as usual, that the chowder was different on Tuesdays. At eight, the Brandt party arrived for their reservation—Councilman Elias Brandt himself, silver-haired and charming, shaking hands with the bellman, laughing at something, a man entirely at ease in the world.

He paused at the desk on his way in. Nodded at me.

"Evening, Theo. Quiet week?"

"Quiet so far, Councilman."

"We could use one of those." He smiled, easy as ever, and followed his party to the dining room.

I wrote: *Brandt party, 6, 8pm, table away from windows,* and underneath it, without deciding to, without understanding why, I added:

*E.B. asked if week was quiet. Seemed to want reassurance.*

Then I sat there a while, listening to the rain, feeling the first hairline crack widen by a degree I couldn't measure, in a wall I'd believed load-bearing for twenty years.

Three weeks, she'd said. I gave it until morning before I started reading that entry back.
