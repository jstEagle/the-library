## 10. Ada's Voice Mail

The trouble with running a hotel front desk is that the phone never stops being a weapon. It rings when you're happy, it rings when you're grieving, and on the morning after I'd spent four hours with Ruth's mutilated files, it rang at 6:47 a.m. with a voice I had already buried once.

"Meridian Hotel, this is Theo."

Static. Then breathing. Then Ada Voss, sounding like she was calling from inside a seashell, or a coffin.

"Theo Zhou." My name in her mouth sounded rehearsed, like she'd practiced it in a mirror. "If this reaches you—when this reaches you—I set it to delay. Forty-eight hours. Don't ask me why. Ask yourself what you'd have done."

I sat down behind the desk. The lobby was empty except for the smell of yesterday's coffee and the tide clock ticking its slow lie about time.

"You went through her files," Ada said. "I know you did, because that's what she trained you to do. The pages that are missing—the ones cut out with a blade, not torn, because tearing is panic and cutting is intention—those were hers. She cut them out herself. I watched her do it when I was nineteen, and I hated her for it, and then I spent twenty years wishing I'd read them before she burned the rest."

The receiver was warm against my ear. Outside, Juniper Hollow was waking up the way it always does: gulls arguing over something no one will ever admit to, the bakery fan pushing cinnamon into the street, the town pretending to be exactly what it is.

"I'm not who you think I am," she said. "Or maybe I'm exactly who you think I am, and that's worse. Check the back of your ledger. The oldest one. Page one. There's a name written in pencil under the hotel's founding date, and it isn't Ruth Marchetti's handwriting, because Ruth never wrote anything down she didn't want found. It's mine. Ada Marchetti. Before I changed it. Before she signed me away to my aunt in Sacramento and told everyone—including you—that she'd never had children."

My hand was shaking. I put it flat on the desk, palm down, the way Ruth taught me to steady myself during a difficult check-in. *The guest is never the problem, Theo. The guest is the weather. You are the building.*

"She didn't sign me away," Ada said, and now her voice cracked along a fault line she'd clearly been walking around for decades. "She hid me. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. Ask Vivienne Cross what the difference is. No—don't. She'll smile at you, and you'll believe her, because you've believed her your whole life. I did too, once. From a distance. Through glass."

I looked up through that same glass, at the portrait of Vivienne Cross's grandmother that has hung above the fireplace since before I could read, and felt the floor of my certainty tilt one degree. One degree is enough. Ask any ship.

"I came here to finish her confession," Ada said. "Ruth started it thirty years ago and couldn't finish it, because finishing it would have cost her everything she loved, including the thing she loved most, which wasn't me and wasn't you and wasn't even the hotel. It was the silence. Silence kept her safe. Silence kept everyone safe. Except Lena Marsh, who is not safe, who has never been safe, whose name nobody in this town says out loud unless they're standing in a cemetery where they think no one can hear."

"Lena Marsh," I said aloud, to an empty lobby, and the word came out ragged.

"I know you found the file," Ada said. "I know you found the gap where 1994 used to be. Here's what I need you to understand: Ruth didn't cut those pages out to hide a crime. She cut them out to hide a person. And that person is still alive, still eating breakfast in this town, still being served coffee by people who call them by a nickname."

A delivery truck hissed past on the street outside. Somewhere upstairs, a vacuum cleaner started its devotional hum. The ordinary world, insisting on itself.

"The solstice is in nine days," Ada said. "Elias Brandt is going to die on the solstice unless someone finishes what Ruth started. Not the confession—the *correction*. There's a difference, and the difference is—" She stopped. I heard her take a breath, and in that breath I heard something I recognized with a jolt so physical I gripped the desk: Ruth's exact pause, the one she used before saying a thing she knew would change the shape of your day.

"The difference is the ledger," Ada said. "You keep her ledger. You inherited it. So finish it. Follow the ink. When she couldn't say a thing outright, she wrote it sideways—in the margins, in the sums that don't add up, in the room numbers that don't exist. Room 214 doesn't exist, Theo. You gave me 214 because I asked for it, and you thought I was being eccentric. I asked for it because it's the only number she ever wrote down twice."

The line clicked, or the sea swallowed it, or forty-eight hours of delay finally ran out of patience. Then dial tone, long and flat as the horizon.

* * *

I sat there for a while. I'll be honest about that, because Ruth taught me that a ledger is worthless if it lies, and I am, whatever else has happened, still her ledger.

Then I went to the back office, unlocked the cabinet, and took down the oldest ledger—the 1987 one, bound in green cloth gone soft as skin, the one I'd catalogued a hundred times and read perhaps three. Page one. Founding date. And there, beneath the faded ink of the Meridian's opening, in pencil so light I'd mistaken it for a shadow every previous time: *Ada M.*

Not a guest. A daughter. Written into the hotel's birth certificate the way you'd write a name into a family Bible—which, I realized, sitting there with my heart going like a gull in a storm, is exactly what a founding ledger is. Ruth hadn't recorded a guest. She'd recorded an heir.

Ada Marchetti. Changed to Voss somewhere between Sacramento and the ferry terminal. Estranged by paper if not by blood—and no, that's backwards, isn't it. Estranged by blood, family by paper. The hotel was the paper. The hotel was the only mother Ruth had left to give her, and instead of handing it over, Ruth had hidden her daughter inside it like a letter folded into a book no one reads anymore.

I thought about Ada checking in, rain-soaked, paying cash, asking me to stop a murder that hadn't happened yet. I'd logged the request in my neat hand and dismissed her as a crank. I had done to her precisely what this town does to everything it can't file: I'd shelved her.

* * *

By ten I had the ledgers spread across the back office table like a card game played by a very lonely god. 1994 through 1997. If Ruth had hidden Ada in the numbers, she might have hidden other things there too.

Here is what I found, and I want you to appreciate how long it took me to see it, because I looked at these pages for twenty years:

Room rates in June 1994 run $84, $86, $88—a normal summer creep. Except for eleven entries across three weeks where the rate is $94.00 exactly. Eleven nights. And on each of those nights, the guest name is a variation of the same anagram: L. Marsh, H. Marsl, S. Marhl, L. Harms. Ruth was recording Lena Marsh's presence in the hotel without recording it—hiding her in plain sight, in the one place no auditor would ever look, because auditors check totals, not spelling.

Eleven nights in June 1994. Lena Marsh drowned on the twelfth.

And on the thirteenth, the ledger shows a payment from the Brandt Family Trust: *Donation — restoration fund, $10,000.* The word *restoration* underlined twice. Ruth never underlined anything. Underlining was emphasis, and emphasis was confession, and Ruth did not confess in ink.

Except she did. Sideways. In sums that don't add up.

I sat back and did the arithmetic I should have done years ago. Ten thousand dollars in 1994 is roughly the price of a life insurance policy, or a boat, or a silence. The Brandts paid it three days after Lena drowned. The Okafor archives—what I'd seen of them—showed a matching "bequest" from the Okafor side. Two founding families, two payments, one dead girl, and a concierge who filed it all under *charitable giving* and never looked again.

Because looking again would have meant seeing Ruth.

* * *

Marisol Quintero called at noon. Not the station line—my cell, which meant unofficial, which meant honest.

"You sound strange," she said, before I'd said anything at all.

"I got a phone call from a dead woman's daughter."

A pause long enough to drive through. "Theo. Who died?"

"Nobody new. That's rather the point." I told her. Not everything—I held back the ledger arithmetic, partly because I wanted to verify it first and mostly because I've learned, these past weeks, that information is the only currency in Juniper Hollow that doesn't devalue—but I told her about Ada. The name. The delay. The forty-eight hours.

"So she's been alive this whole time," Marisol said. "Since the ferry terminal sighting?"

"Alive, mobile, and better informed than either of us. She knew about the razored pages before I mentioned them. She knew about room 214. She knew Ruth." I turned the green ledger toward me again, though the pencil ghost needed no rereading. "Marisol, she didn't come here to warn us about a murder. She came here to finish a confession her mother started thirty years ago, and the murder is just the deadline."

"That's a hell of a way to structure a confession."

"It's a hell of a way to structure a family."

Another pause. When Marisol spoke again, her voice had dropped into the register she uses for things she could lose her job over. "The 1994 case file. I pulled the chain of custody log last night. You know who signed it out in '95, officially, for 'review'?" She let me hang. "Vivienne Cross. Signed as hotel owner, cited as a 'consulting party.' The case was closed six weeks later. Closed from above, I told you that. I didn't tell you the above had a return address."

I looked at the portrait over the fireplace. Vivienne Cross's grandmother gazed back with the serene entitlement of all painted money.

"Marisol," I said. "What do you know about the Meridian's PA system?"

"The ancient one? The one that announces bingo night like it's broadcasting from a submarine?"

"The very one. It runs on copper wire through every wall in this building. Built in '61. Never upgraded, because upgrades require permits, and permits require questions." I closed the green ledger. "No reason. Just taking inventory."

"You're a terrible liar for someone who keeps records."

"It's why I keep records. Paper doesn't have to be good at it."

She laughed—short, unwilling, real—and hung up, and I sat alone in the back office with the weight of a new inheritance settling onto my shoulders. Because that's what this was. Ruth had left me the ledgers, the desk, the duty. Now it turned out she'd also left me a sister, more or less. A woman raised on the outside of the silence, coming back to break it, using me as the door.

Family by paper. I turned the phrase over and found it fit better than I wanted it to. I had spent twenty years being Ruth's heir in every way but blood, and I had told myself that made the inheritance cleaner. It didn't. It just meant I'd inherited the debt without the excuse.

* * *

In the afternoon I did something I'd been avoiding since the files: I went up to Ruth's old apartment on the third floor. Unchanged since her death—Vivienne's instruction, "keep it as a memorial," delivered with a warmth I had mistook for grief. Maybe it was grief. Maybe it was also a sealed room in a house full of sealed rooms.

The key was in my ring. It had always been in my ring. I'd simply never turned it, out of a piety I can now recognize as its own kind of cowardice.

The apartment smelled of lavender and dust doing their long negotiation. Ruth's reading chair. Ruth's teacups. Ruth's wall of tide charts, annotated in her tiny hand. And on the desk, under a paperweight shaped like a lighthouse, a single envelope with my name on it.

I stood there for a full minute before touching it. Some part of me understood that once I picked it up, I would stop being the woman who kept the Meridian's secrets and become the woman who opened them, and there is no un-becoming that.

Inside: a photograph, old and sun-struck. Four young women on the boathouse dock, arms slung around each other, laughing at whoever held the camera. Vivienne Cross, young and radiant. Amara Okafor, mid-laugh. Elias Brandt, holding a bottle of champagne like a trophy. And a fourth girl at the edge of the frame, half-turned away, face blurred by motion or sunlight—

No. Not blurred. Deliberately obscured, a thumb smudge worn into the emulsion by someone handling the photo too often, rubbing at that one face for years.

On the back, in Ruth's hand: *June 1991. Before.* And beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: *Ask Amara what she kept.*

I put the photograph in my jacket, over my heart, where evidence goes when it's also a wound.

Outside, the tide clock in the lobby ticked past four. Nine days to the solstice. Somewhere in this town, Ada Voss—Ada Marchetti, my sister by paper, my mentor's hidden daughter—was moving through the streets with a confession in her pocket and a killer's schedule in her head. And somewhere closer than that, in a wine cellar that smelled of oak and guilt, a woman named Amara was keeping something she'd promised God and three friends she'd keep forever.

Ruth's note said to ask her.

I picked up the phone and dialed the Okafor house.
