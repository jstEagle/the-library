## 12. Thirty Years of Thursdays

I did not sleep at the Meridian that night. I want to be clear about that, because everything I did afterward should be understood as the behavior of a woman who had not slept, and not as the behavior of a fool.

I took the diary home.

That sentence deserves its own paragraph, because taking it was a decision with a shape and a weight, like lifting a stone off a grave to see what's underneath. Amara Okafor sat across her kitchen table from me at two in the morning, a glass of water she wasn't drinking going warm between her hands, and she said, "Take it. I've kept it thirty years and it hasn't done anyone any good." Then she looked at me the way you look at someone you've just handed a live wire to, and she added: "You should know that Ruth knew I had it. She never asked for it back. I always wondered why."

I wondered why too. I would stop wondering around dawn, and I would wish I hadn't.

* * *

My apartment above the dry cleaner on Pell Street has one good chair, a kettle, and four hundred books arranged by a system only Ruth ever fully understood. I sat in the good chair with Lena Marsh's diary on my knees and a pot of coffee going cold beside me, and I read it straight through, the way you'd read a letter addressed to no one—which is what it was, mostly. A diary is a letter to the self you can't lie to. Lena Marsh wrote hers in a small, slanted hand that leaned hard to the right, as if the words were trying to get away from her.

The early entries were ordinary. Weather. A dog named Captain who ate a glove. Complaints about her mother's bridge club, which included, in various combinations, Vivienne Cross, Amara Okafor, and my own Ruth Marchetti—though of course in 1994 she was just Ruth, twenty-nine years old, working the front desk of the Meridian and writing her name in the ledger in that careful librarian's hand I'd know anywhere, because I'd spent twenty years imitating it.

Then, in March of 1994, the entries changed key.

*I met with E. again tonight. He says it's simple. He says everyone does it. He says if I love this town I'll protect it.*

E. I set the diary down and looked at the ceiling for a while. There were three founders' sons of the right age in 1994: Elias Brandt, Daniel Okafor, and—well. The third had died before I ever took the desk, and his widow still sent the hotel a Christmas card every year with a note in a hand I'd copied a thousand times.

I picked the diary back up.

Lena wrote about Thursdays. That was the thing that made my skin crawl—not the money, not yet, but the Thursdays. Every Thursday evening, a meeting. The Brandt boathouse, then the Okafor cellar, then once, memorably, *the back office at the Meridian while R. covered the desk.* The same four people, week after week, for the better part of a year. Thirty years of Thursdays, except it wasn't thirty years. It was fifty-two Thursdays, and then it stopped, because on the last Thursday of May 1994, Lena Marsh went into the tide off the north pilings and did not come out.

The scheme, as Lena pieced it together—and she was smarter than the town ever gave her credit for, which is probably why they gave her nothing else—was an inheritance structure. Not a will. A trust, or rather a lattice of trusts, built in the fifties by the founding generation, designed so that the town's land, its waterfront leases, its fishing rights, its future, would pass down through the families in perpetuity, untouchable, untaxable, unaccountable. Half of Juniper Hollow's fortunes stood on that lattice. And the lattice had a flaw, and the flaw had a name, and the name was Lena Marsh.

She was the last uncontested heir of the Marsh line, and the Marsh share of the trust—the largest share, as it turned out, larger than the Brandts' or the Okafors' or the Crosses', though nobody had ever said so out loud—vested to her absolutely on her thirtieth birthday. Which would have been June 21st, 1994.

The solstice.

She could have dissolved her portion. Sold it, split it, donated it to the county, sunk it into a foundation for wayward dogs. Any of those things would have cracked the lattice, devalued every other share, and dragged thirty years of quiet arrangements into the light where accountants and journalists could pick at them. The other families' fortunes weren't just money. They were the arrangement itself—the understanding that Juniper Hollow belonged to four names and always would.

So they met on Thursdays, and they explained to her how much the town needed her discretion, and when explanation didn't work, they tried something else.

*They've started talking about Mother's debts,* Lena wrote in April. *Debts she didn't have. Papers with her signature that she swears isn't hers. E. says he can make them go away. He says it so kindly. That's the worst part, how kind he is about it.*

Blackmail. They'd manufactured her mother's ruin and then offered to sell it back to her, installment by installment, year by year, in exchange for signing her inheritance into the collective pot—a "stewardship agreement," Elias's term, I'd have bet the hotel on it. And if she signed, she'd be safe, and her mother would be safe, and the town would be safe, and everyone would keep being comfortable forever, which in Juniper Hollow is the highest moral good.

*If I sign, I stop being a person and become a clause,* she wrote. *R. says I should hold out. R. says she'll help me.*

There it was. My mentor's name, in a dead girl's diary, on the side of the angels.

I remember feeling relief. Actual, physical relief, sitting in my chair at four in the morning with cold coffee, thinking: *there she is.* Because I'd spent eleven chapters—I mean, I'd spent eleven days—dreading what the razored pages meant, dreading that Ruth had been in the room when Lena drowned, and here was the counterweight. Ruth telling Lena to hold out. Ruth offering help. Whatever else happened, my Ruth had been the decent one.

I should have known better. I've read enough ledgers to know that the story is never over until the arithmetic closes, and there were still forty pages left.

* * *

May came fast in the diary. The Thursdays got uglier. The kindness drained out of Elias's voice—or out of Lena's record of it—and something harder moved in behind it. *He brought papers tonight. Real ones, from Hollis & Crane. He says Mother's house goes to auction in June unless I sign by the solstice. He smiled when he said solstice. Like it was a gift.*

And then, May 19th, 1994, an entry written in a hand shakier than all the others, pressed so hard into the page that the pen had nearly gone through:

*R. came to me tonight. She cried. She told me things she shouldn't have told me. She said she's been keeping the minutes. All these months, she's been the one writing down what they say on Thursdays, because V. asked her to, because V. wanted insurance against the others. R. has copies. She says if anything happens to me, the copies will come out. She says V. promised.*

*But R.'s hands were shaking when she said V.'s name.*

*I don't think R. is afraid for me. I think R. is afraid of V.*

I put the diary face-down on my knee and sat very still.

Vivienne Cross. Who had been, in 1994, a twenty-eight-year-old with a new business degree and her family's smallest share and, according to every founding-family history I'd catalogued, no particular ambition. Who now owned the Meridian Hotel. Who had hired me, kept me, paid me well, and never once asked me what I kept in the old files.

Who had loved Ruth. I'd known that the way you know weather—never documented, never discussed, just present in the air of certain rooms. Ruth's apartment had held two of everything for twenty years. Two teacups. Two umbrellas by the door. One of them never used.

And Ruth had been keeping minutes. Insurance. Copies.

Which meant that somewhere in the wreckage of Ruth's life—in the files I'd inherited, in the ledger margins I'd spent twenty years learning to read without reading—there might be a second copy of the thing that got Lena Marsh killed. And somewhere in the town, someone had already killed once to keep it buried, and had recently started killing again to keep it buried, and had erased Ada Voss from Room 214 with the ease of long practice.

Ada. Ruth's daughter, per the voice mail. Come to finish her mother's confession. If Ruth had kept copies, Ada might have grown up in a house with them. Ada might know exactly what was in them.

Or Ada might be the next loose end.

* * *

It was near six by the time I reached the last pages, and the light through my window had gone the color of dishwater. The final entries were short. Lena knew how it was going to end—I'm sure of it now, rereading. She wrote like a woman putting her affairs in order.

*Thursday again tomorrow. I told E. I'd give him my answer at the boathouse. He seemed pleased. He said, "You're being sensible at last."*

*One more thing. R. gave me a page tonight. From the minutes. She said, "Keep this where they can't find it, and if anything happens, give it to someone honest." I asked her who's honest. She thought for a long time and said, "Nobody yet. But somebody will come along. Somebody always does, eventually. It's the one thing I believe."*

*Her handwriting is beautiful. Even scared, it doesn't waver.*

I turned the page. The next entry was dated May 26th, 1994, and it was the last one, and it was unfinished:

*If I drown tomorrow, it will look like an accident, because that's what we agreed it would look like, all four of us, and the terrible thing is that I agreed too. I shook hands on it. What was I supposed to do? They have Mother's house. They have—*

The entry stopped mid-sentence. No period. No date of death, no farewell. Just a girl's hand trailing off the edge of a page, the ink thinning where the pen lifted, and then nothing, forever.

*What we agreed it would look like.* I read that phrase perhaps forty times. It refused to mean what it plainly said. An accident that was agreed upon in advance by all parties including the victim is not an accident; it is a script. And a script, once written, can be performed again.

Thirty years later, someone was performing it again. A drifter in the tide wearing Elias Brandt's watch. A warning delivered a month in advance by a woman who called herself a witness. Someone wasn't improvising a murder. Someone was staging a reenactment—down to the props, down to the date, down to the victim. And now I knew why the date mattered, why the solstice mattered: it was the day Lena's inheritance vested. The day the lattice held. The anniversary of the moment half the town's fortunes became permanent.

Someone was commemorating it. Or correcting it. Or collecting on it—one final payment, due on the thirtieth year, from the men who had shaken a drowning girl's hand.

* * *

Here is the part I have been avoiding, and a concierge who avoids things is a concierge who ends up dead, so let me say it plainly.

The fourth name in the diary was smudged.

Not redacted—smudged. Water damage, maybe, or a thumb, or a tear that fell before the ink dried, though Lena's tears are none of my business anymore. On the page where she listed the Thursday attendees for the record—*E.B., D.O., V.C., and*—the fourth set of initials dissolved into a gray bloom, unreadable. Three names I could read. The fourth was a ghost.

But the entry adjacent to it, the one describing the fourth person's role, was intact. And it was the handwriting that undid me.

Because it wasn't Lena's.

Somewhere in the last weeks of her life, someone else had written in Lena Marsh's diary. A single passage, tucked between entries, in a small, upright, meticulous hand—the hand of a person who catalogues things, who keeps minutes, who believes in records the way other people believe in God. The hand I have been practicing in my own ledgers for twenty years, the hand I inherited along with the front desk and the keys and the silence.

*The fourth founder does not attend to speak. She attends to listen. She takes nothing and signs nothing and remembers everything. She is the reason the arrangement holds, and she is the reason it can be broken. If you are reading this, Lena, understand: she is not your enemy. She is the only one of them who is afraid. Fear is a form of honesty. It is the only form they have left.*

I know that handwriting better than I know my own reflection. I have forged it, lovingly, for two decades, in the margins of guest registers and the backs of complaint slips. I learned it from Ruth Marchetti, who taught me that a concierge's true work is memory, and that memory is a moral act.

Ruth had written in the diary. Ruth had known about the Thursdays, kept the minutes, made the copies, given Lena a page, told her to find someone honest. Ruth had been inside it—deep inside it, deeper than a bystander, deeper than a friend. You do not write philosophical passages in a doomed girl's diary unless you are bound to her by something stronger than friendship. Guilt, maybe. Love, maybe. Both, probably, in the way that guilt and love braid together in people who keep records.

And Ruth had razored pages out of her own files—pages about the case, pages about Lena—because whatever she'd done in 1994, she had spent thirty years making sure the paper trail pointed away from herself. The woman who taught me that memory is a moral act had curated her own memory like a crime scene.

I sat in my good chair until the light came up over Pell Street, and I grieved. Not the clean grief of the anniversary, which I know how to carry. This was the other kind—the grief of discovering that your idol has fingerprints all over the murder weapon. Ruth had not killed Lena Marsh. I'd stake the hotel on that. But she had known, and she had stayed, and she had kept the minutes for the people who did, and she had spent the rest of her life paying for it in some currency I couldn't yet see.

Amara's question came back to me. *She never asked for the diary back. I always wondered why.*

Now I knew. Ruth hadn't needed the diary. Ruth had the original. Somewhere in the Meridian—in the files, in the margins, in the coded ledger I'd been reading wrong for twenty years—was the master copy of the Thursdays. Ruth hadn't preserved it out of sentiment. She'd preserved it because it was leverage, or penance, or a bomb with her name on the fuse, and she'd left it to me the way you leave a debt to your heir.

Somebody always comes along eventually. It's the one thing I believe.

She'd written that, or said it, or dreamed it, and then she'd spent thirty years making sure that when somebody came along, that somebody would be holding the evidence—and would have to decide what to do with it. That wasn't faith. That was delegation.

I closed the diary. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Outside, Juniper Hollow was waking up—gulls, delivery trucks, the smell of the bakery on Pell doing its indifferent morning work, all of it standing on a lattice of lies older than I was. Four days until the solstice. Elias Brandt had three days left on Ada Voss's schedule, whether or not he believed in it.

I wrapped the diary in a tea towel, put it in my bag, and headed for the Meridian to open the desk, because the desk opens whether the world makes sense or not.

Elias Brandt was waiting in the lobby.

He was standing under the portrait of the founding families like a man posing for his own arrest, and when he saw me come through the service door, he straightened up and smiled the kind smile from the diary, the kind that had frightened a girl to death, and he said:

"Miss Zhou. Might I buy you breakfast? There are some letters of Ruth's I think you ought to see."
