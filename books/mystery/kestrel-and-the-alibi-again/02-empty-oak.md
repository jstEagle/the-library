## 2. Empty Oak

There is a particular silence that falls over two hundred people when they realize they've been lied to for forty years. I'd like to tell you I identified it by its acoustic properties — the way sound dies in a crowd the way it dies in a fresh grave — but honestly, I identified it because I was standing in the middle of it, holding a shovel, and every one of those two hundred faces had turned toward me like compass needles finding north.

The morning had started better. Mornings in Halloway generally do.

The bicentennial committee had wanted ceremony, and Halloway has never met a ceremony it couldn't stretch to fill a Saturday. They'd strung bunting between the lampposts of Founders' Row. The high school band played something patriotic and slightly off-key. Mayor Hollis — silver-haired, camera-ready, the kind of man who shakes your hand and makes you check afterward that you still have all five fingers — gave a speech about legacy and roots and the deep soil from which our town grew. He said "Edmund Kestrel" eleven times. I counted. Counting is what I do instead of listening, a professional habit I've never seen any reason to break.

I stood at the edge of the gathering with my coat buttoned against the October wind, doing the job I've done for nineteen years: keeper of the town's dead. Coroner, registrar of burials, sexton by inheritance if not by title. My grandmother held the office before me, and her father before her, back through a line of Amberdales long enough that some people in town joke we were burying people before there was a town to bury them in. I don't laugh at the joke. Mostly because it's true.

You learn things about a place from its graves that you can't learn anywhere else. You learn which families intermarry until the surnames braid together like rope. You learn which griefs went unresolved — the graves visited daily for decades, the ones never visited at all. You learn that Mrs. Aldous still brings coffee to her husband's stone every Tuesday, forty years running, and that she pours a little on the ground because he always took it that way. The dead of Halloway are my ledger, my parish, my long conversation. I know them by name, by plot, by the particular slant of their headstones. Belonging to a town, I have come to believe, means belonging to its graves. Everything else is just where you happen to keep your toothbrush.

And this morning, the whole town had come out to honor the grandest grave of them all.

* * *

The exhumation itself was supposed to be dignified. A crane, a strap, an oak coffin lifted from six feet of Kestrel family ground while the band played softly and the historical society ladies dabbed their eyes. Edmund Kestrel, founder, timber baron, the man whose name is on the school and the mill road and half the businesses on Main Street, was to be reinterred in a new marble vault as the centerpiece of the bicentennial. Two hundred years since he supposedly rode into the wilderness and founded Halloway; forty years since he supposedly died of heart failure at fifty-one and was laid to rest before a grieving multitude.

Supposedly. That word would be doing a lot of work by sundown.

I'll admit the first wrong note came early, and I noticed it the way you notice a draft — peripherally, dismissively. When the crane took the weight and the coffin rose out of the earth, water beaded on the oak. Not groundwater seepage, which after forty years would be expected, forgiven, unremarkable. This was condensation. Clean, cold, recent. The wood hadn't rotted. It hadn't even darkened.

Forty years in the ground, and that coffin looked like it had been built last winter.

I told myself: sealed vault, dry soil, good oak. I told myself: stop being morbid, Lior, it's a celebration. I have spent nineteen years telling myself sensible things in order to function, and I am very good at it, and it has never once worked.

They set the coffin on trestles before the assembled town. The mayor said a few more words. Reverend Pike blessed it. And then, because the bicentennial committee had decided — God help us — that the moment should be "authentic," they asked me to open it.

Not the historical society. Not the mayor. Me. The coroner whose signature sat on the 1984 burial permit. I remember thinking, as I reached for the lid, that it was a strange honor to give a man whose job it is to certify death. I remember thinking nothing else at all, because the lid came free on the second pull — no nails, I registered distantly, no nails at all, screws, recently driven — and the crowd behind me made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

It wasn't a gasp. Gasps are short. This was a long, collective inhalation, two hundred people drawing breath at once and none of them letting it go.

The coffin was empty.

Not empty the way a tomb is empty, with dust and dignity. Empty the way a stage is empty between acts. The satin lining lay smooth and unwrinkled, pale as a bride's dress, without so much as a stain of forty years' honest decay. No bones. No suit. No Edmund Kestrel.

What it did hold, placed neatly in the center like an offering on an altar, was a brass key — old, green at the edges, the kind that fits a door nobody has locked in decades — and a single child's shoe. Leather, small, a girl's, the buckle rusted shut.

I stood there with my hands braced on the coffin rim, and I did what any coroner does when confronted with the impossible: I looked for the boring explanation. Flooded grave, remains washed elsewhere, mix-up in plots. But the lining was dry. The interior was clean. Someone had opened this coffin, emptied it, and left those two objects deliberately, recently, arranged with intention. A message. Addressed, apparently, to whoever pulled the lid.

Which was me. In front of everyone.

"Lior." Mayor Hollis's voice came from very far away, pitched carefully for the microphones. "Lior, what — what is this?"

I turned around. Two hundred faces. The band had stopped mid-phrase, a trumpet hanging on a note that died embarrassingly in the open air. Old Mrs. Aldous had both hands over her mouth. The historical society ladies had stopped dabbing. Somewhere near the back, someone laughed once, a single sharp bark, and was shushed into silence that was somehow worse.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truth, and it sounded exactly like a lie.

Because here is the thing about being the town's keeper of the dead: when the dead turn out not to be dead, it is your name on the paperwork. My signature, dated June 14th, 1984, certifying the burial of Edmund Kestrel, cause of death cardiac arrest, remains received and interred. I was nine years old in 1984. Somebody signed my name — or rather, somebody signed the name that would eventually be mine, the office's name, the Amberdale name — and now the office stood in front of two hundred witnesses holding an empty box and a child's shoe, and the office had nowhere to hide.

"Is this some kind of joke?" That was Dale Pruitt, whose family has hated mine since a dispute over cemetery boundaries in 1961. He said it loudly, for the benefit of the crowd.

"It's no kind of joke," I said.

"Then where is he?" Pruitt again, and now other voices picked it up, the question moving through the crowd like fire through dry grass. Where is he? Where's Kestrel? Who certified this? Whose permit? Wasn't it the Amberdale? Didn't the Amberdale sign?

Nineteen years of careful, quiet, competent service, undone in the time it takes to lift a lid. I want to say I kept my composure. What I actually did was stand very straight and very still, because I learned young that stillness is the only dignity available when the ground opens under you. Inside, something older than composure was screaming: *the signature doesn't match.* I'd found that three days ago, in the archive, comparing the 1984 permit against Ruth Calloway's known hand. The registrar's signature on Kestrel's burial permit was almost right and therefore entirely wrong — the loop of the R too shallow, the crossbar of the T missing. I had told myself there'd be a boring explanation. I had planned to raise it quietly, through channels, after the ceremony.

The ceremony had other plans.

"Everybody please," the mayor was saying, hands raised, smile nailed in place like siding in a storm. "Please, let's remain calm. There's clearly an explanation. Lior, perhaps you could—"

And that was when the county car came up Founders' Row.

* * *

I saw her before I saw the badge. She got out of the sedan the way people do when they've been driving angry — door swung wide, one clean motion, no fumbling. Tall, dark-haired going gray at the temples, county detective's shield on her belt, and a face that took in the scene — the open coffin, the crowd, the bunting, me standing over it all like a magician caught without his rabbit — and filed it away in about a second and a half.

"Mara Voss," she said, showing credentials to nobody in particular and everybody in general. "County CID. We got a call." Her eyes found the coffin. Stayed there. Then found me. "Who opened it?"

"I did," I said.

"Who certified the original burial?"

"That would also be me," I said, "in the sense that the coroner's office certifies all burials, and I currently hold the office."

"In the sense that," she repeated, tasting the phrase like milk she suspected had turned. She walked past me and looked down into the coffin, and I watched her do what I had done — catalog the dry lining, the absence of decay, the deliberate placement of key and shoe — only faster, and without the sentimental fog. Then she looked at the shoe for a long moment. Something crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone.

"Gloves," she said, and someone from the historical society produced evidence bags, because Halloway is nothing if not prepared for pageantry and nothing else. She bagged the key herself. She hovered over the shoe, then bagged it too, handling it differently — slower, gentler — though I doubt anyone but me noticed.

"Detective Voss," the mayor said, hurrying over, hand extended, voice performing calm for the crowd's benefit. "Mayor Hollis. Thank goodness. As you can see, there appears to have been some — irregularity—"

"Some irregularity," Voss agreed. "Somebody want to tell me how a forty-year-old coffin comes up looking like it was lined yesterday?"

Silence. The good people of Halloway, so voluble a moment ago, discovered a sudden passion for studying their shoes. All except me, and I was busy studying hers.

"The coroner's office maintains the burial records," I said. "If there's a discrepancy, it'll be in the permits. I can pull the file."

"You can pull the file." She looked at me the way a veterinarian looks at a dog that keeps getting into the trash — not hostile yet, but with a clear-eyed assessment of future probability. "Your name's on the permit?"

"My name is on every permit. It's the office name. I inherited it."

"When?"

"Nineteen years ago."

"So for nineteen years, you've been signing off on burials, including this one's maintenance, transfers, records requests?" She didn't wait for confirmation. "And the original certification? 1984?"

"Before my time. My predecessor's office handled it."

"Name?"

"Ruth Calloway." Saying it out loud hurt more than I expected. "She was the registrar for thirty years. She trained me. She died four years ago."

Voss wrote that down. I watched her write it and understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching his own footprints being cast in plaster, that everything I said from here forward was evidence. "And nobody thought it was strange," she said, not really asking, "that the founder's coffin needed its screws driven recently?"

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell that from anywhere." She straightened, surveyed the crowd — which was pretending very hard not to eavesdrop, which in Halloway means leaning in from twenty feet — and lowered her voice, which somehow made it carry further. "Mr. Amberdale. Here's how this looks from where I sit. A coffin gets exhumed for a photo op. It's empty. The one man whose signature vouches for everything that happened to it for four decades is standing right there, holding the lid. So either your town buried an empty box in 1984 and nobody noticed for forty years—" she let that hang, absurd and enormous, "—or somebody got into that grave recently and staged this. Either way, somebody knew. And either way, the person standing closest to it is you."

The wind moved through Founders' Row and rattled the bunting. Somewhere the band was packing up, instruments clinking, the music already pretending it had never started.

"I didn't stage anything," I said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But you're going to help me find out who did. Starting with that file you offered." She glanced back at the coffin, at the smooth empty satin, and her voice dropped into something almost private. "A child's shoe," she said, mostly to herself. "Why a child's shoe?"

I didn't answer. Not because I had nothing to say, but because I had one thing to say and no right to say it: that I'd seen that shoe before, or its twin, forty years ago in a photograph in Ruth Calloway's office — a little girl on the sanatorium steps, one shoe buckled, one shoe dangling from her hand, laughing at whoever held the camera.

I had told myself there'd be a boring explanation for the mismatched signature, too.

Detective Voss snapped the bag shut around the brass key and looked at me with the flat, patient attention of someone who intends to take apart my entire life bolt by bolt, and I understood that the empty coffin was only the lid lifting on something much larger — and that the next thing to come out of the ground would probably be me.
