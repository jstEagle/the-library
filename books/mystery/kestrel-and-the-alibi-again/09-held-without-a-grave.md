## 9. Held Without a Grave

There is an irony to being held in the Halloway holding cell that I would appreciate more if I weren't the one in it. The cell sits directly beneath my office — beneath the coroner's suite, beneath the room where I have spent twenty-two years signing papers that turn people into paperwork. I know this building the way I know my own hands. I know which pipe groans when the furnace kicks on at 6 p.m. I know that the third floorboard outside the evidence room squeaks in E-flat. And now I know what it sounds like from underneath: the whole building humming with other people's business, indifferent as weather.

They took my belt, my laces, and my fountain pen, which was unnecessary cruelty. A man can hang himself with a shoelace; he cannot hang himself with a fountain pen, but he can certainly feel less like himself without one.

The charge is obstruction of justice, pending murder. Detective Mara Voss read it to me in a voice she kept deliberately flat, the way you'd read a grocery list to someone you were angry at. Her hands didn't shake. I noticed that. I also noticed that they wanted to.

I am not going to pretend I spent the night composing my defense. I spent it doing what I do best, which is lying awake among the dead and taking inventory.

* * *

Let me reconstruct it properly, because reconstruction is a discipline. You start with what cannot be argued.

One: Edmund Kestrel's coffin, exhumed after forty years, contained a brass key and a child's shoe and no Edmund Kestrel. Two: the 1984 burial permit bears a signature that is Ruth Calloway's hand wearing another woman's name like a borrowed coat. Three: Tom Wicker wept at the wrong grave for four decades and dropped a photograph of a living man on his way to fleeing from me. Four: Tom Wicker is now dead at the bottom of his own cellar stairs, and my fingerprints are on his farm gate, and the town that has known me since I was small enough to ride on Ruth Calloway's shoulders is looking at me the way you look at a dog that has bitten someone.

Five — and this is the one I arrived at somewhere around two in the morning, staring at the ceiling while Deputy Hollis pretended to read a magazine he'd been holding upside down — five: none of this could have happened without money, patience, and silence. Lots of all three.

A forged burial permit is not a spur-of-the-moment crime. It requires a registrar willing to burn her career, a doctor willing to sign a death certificate for a breathing man, a funeral director willing to bury an empty box and keep his mouth shut through two hundred funerals' worth of professional gossip. It requires land — because where do you put a disgraced founder who must never be seen? Not out of town; strangers ask questions. Somewhere close. Somewhere with walls and a plausible reason to exist.

And it requires families. Plural. Because one family keeping a secret for forty years is a conspiracy; six families keeping it is a civic institution.

I lay there on the narrow bunk and did what I used to do as a child in Ruth's archive cellar, when she taught me the town's genealogies the way other children learned multiplication tables. *Know who owns whom, Lior,* she said once, tapping the founding-families chart with her reading glasses still hooked over one ear. *Halloway isn't built on the river. It's built on intermarriage.*

The Kestrels, obviously. Then the Harrows, who owned the mill. The Vances, who owned the bank. The Ostranders, land. The Merricks, the newspaper. The Calloways — yes, even Ruth's line, though by 1984 hers had thinned to a single stubborn woman with a registrar's stamp and, apparently, a conscience that arrived forty years too late or exactly on time, depending on how you count.

And the Pryces. Who owned, in 1984, the construction company that built half the town — including, I realized somewhere around three a.m., sitting bolt upright on the bunk so fast that Hollis actually looked up, the old sanatorium on Corben Ridge. Closed in '89, officially. Unofficially shuttered, unforgotten, and large enough to lose a man in the way the ocean loses ships.

The current mayor is a Pryce. Alden Pryce, who stood at the bicentennial ceremony with his hand over his heart while an empty coffin swung above a hole in the ground, and whose face, when the crowd gasped, went through something I had filed at the time as grief.

I refiled it. It wasn't grief. It was arithmetic.

* * *

Morning came gray through the little barred window, and with it came coffee I declined and then accepted, and then, at ten o'clock, came Mara Voss.

She came alone, which was itself a message. She signed the visitor log with her own name — I watched her do it through the door slot, unhurried — rather than slipping in off the record, and I understood that too. She wanted there to be a record of her NOT asking me questions. Mara Voss builds paper walls the way other people build fences.

"Coroner Amberdale," she said, sitting down across from me in the interview room with its scarred table and its mirror that fooled no one, least of all either of us.

"Detective Voss."

"You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping under my own office. The acoustics are unsettling." I slid my hands onto the table, palms up, an old examiner's habit — see, nothing concealed. "You're here off the clock."

"I'm here because the DA wants a statement and I don't give statements before lunch." She set a folder down but didn't open it. "Your prints are on Wicker's gate."

"Yes."

"That's your entire answer?"

"It's the truest short one I have. The longer ones cost things you're not ready to pay."

Something moved behind her eyes — frustration, mostly, but under it that thing I'd seen in the interrogation-by-firelight, the thing I'd started privately calling *the fellow-traveler look* before I caught myself naming anything about her at all. "Lior." First name. Deliberate. "Tom Wicker fell down his own stairs the night after you chased him through a cemetery. You understand how that reads."

"I understand exactly how it reads. I've written it myself, three times." I leaned forward. "Three accidents, Mara. You pulled my history — I know you did, it's in the folder you're pretending not to open. Did you ever wonder why a coroner with a spotless record quietly reclassified three clean accidental deaths as undetermined?"

"Because you're either careless or crusading."

"Because they were murders I couldn't prove. Each one. Each one I dug until the evidence ran out, and each time the evidence ran out in the same direction — toward people who could afford for it to run out." I let that sit. "I'm not telling you this as a confession. I'm telling you because you already suspect it, and because a person can only be lied about for so long before they'd rather be believed."

The furnace pipe groaned overhead. Six p.m. somewhere else; here it was just the building talking in its sleep.

Mara looked at me for a long moment. Then she opened the folder — not the case file, a different one, thinner — and turned it so I could see. A photograph of the brass key, taken on her desk, next to an evidence tag. "It doesn't match any lock in the municipal index," she said. "I've been through every door in every public building in Halloway."

"I know what it opens."

"Of course you do." But she didn't say it with contempt. She said it the way you acknowledge weather.

"Ruth Calloway's safe-deposit box," I said. "First National of Halloway, box 114. The key is brass because Ruth distrusted everything electronic after 1990 and told me so annually, usually while I was trying to get her to use email."

Mara sat back slowly. "The registrar. Your mentor. The forged signature."

"The forged signature." Saying it out loud in this room felt like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt. It did. "Mara, listen to me, because I'm about to hand you the only thing I have left, and I need you to understand what it costs me." I put the truth of it into words carefully, the way you lift something fragile out of rubble. "I cannot explain my fingerprints on that gate without explaining why I was at Tom Wicker's farm. I cannot explain that without explaining the photograph. I cannot explain the photograph without exposing a living witness that somebody just killed an old man to protect. Every honest answer I give you gets someone killed. Possibly you. Possibly me, though I've made peace with the odds on that."

"And the dishonest answers?"

"Are the ones I've been giving. Which is why I'm in here."

She was quiet. Outside the interview room, Hollis's radio murmured traffic reports to no one.

"So give me something I can use," she said finally, "that doesn't get anyone killed."

I reached into my shirt pocket — they'd let me keep the shirt, if not the pen — and produced nothing, because of course they'd searched me. I'd expected that. What I hadn't expected was for them to miss it, but they had, because nobody thinks to search a coroner's collar seam, and because I have been hiding things in corpses' clothing for two decades and have developed certain transferable skills.

I slid the brass key across the table. It caught the light and threw a small coin of it up onto Mara's face.

"That's evidence," she said, not touching it.

"That's trust," I said. "Evidence is what you decide it is afterward. Box 114, First National. Ruth rented it in 1984 and paid the rent every year until she died — I found the bank drafts in her effects. Forty years of payments on a box nobody ever asked her about. Whatever's in there, she thought it would matter someday. She left a note saying *ask the one who weeps at the wrong grave.* That led me to Tom Wicker, and Tom Wicker is dead, and I am otherwise occupied." I pushed the key the last inch toward her fingers. "Open it. Don't tell me what's inside until you've decided what to do with it. I mean that. I don't want deniability; I want it done right."

"Why me?" she asked, and it wasn't rhetorical. There was real weight in it. "You've known these people your whole life. You trust me with this after four days?"

"Because you arrested me," I said, "and I could see how much it cost you. A corrupt cop enjoys the arrest. You hated it. That's the best character reference I can imagine."

She picked up the key. Held it between finger and thumb like something that might be hot.

"If that box contains what I think it might contain," she said, "and if the names in it go as high as I'm starting to suspect they go — Lior, the mayor is a Pryce. The DA golfs with him. If I open that box, I can't take it upstairs. There's no upstairs."

"Then there's only sideways," I said. "Find someone incorruptible. They exist. They're rare, like good teeth, but they exist."

Her mouth twitched — almost a smile, strangled at birth. "You're very calm for a man facing a murder charge."

"I spend my life around the dead, Detective. They're excellent perspective." I paused. "Also I'm terrified, but I find terror keeps better if you don't feed it in public."

This time the smile got halfway out before she disciplined it. She stood, pocketed the key, and gathered her folder. At the door she stopped, back to me.

"For the record," she said, "off the record: I don't think you killed Tom Wicker."

"No?"

"No. Your fingerprints were on the gate latch, not the cellar door. Whoever staged that fall wanted them there and wasn't careful enough about which surfaces." She glanced back, and for one unguarded second the detective and the woman in the firelight were the same person. "Somebody is building a case against you, Lior. Somebody patient, with resources, who knew you'd be at that farm."

"Alden Pryce," I said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. It's in your folder. Page whatever-it-is — the mayor's photo, circled, with three question marks. You circle things when you're arguing with yourself."

She stared at me. "That's genuinely unsettling."

"Occupational hazard. Go open the box, Mara."

She went. I heard her boots on the stairs, heard the outer door, heard the building settle back into its hum. Then I lay down on the bunk and stared at the ceiling and did the only thing left to a man held without a grave of his own making: I waited, and I trusted, and I discovered that trust, at my age, feels almost exactly like falling.

Somewhere below the courthouse square, in a steel drawer forty years rented, Ruth Calloway was about to speak.

I only hoped her voice would reach the right ears before the wrong ones reached her first.
