## 4. The Witness Who Waited

The name came back to me the way names do at four in the morning — uninvited, fully formed, smelling of old coffee.

Ada Voss.

I hadn't said it aloud in three years. I'd barely let myself think it. But standing in my kitchen with the harbor still taped off six blocks away and a dead man wearing my coat floating in the police photographs Kestrel had grudgingly shown me, the name surfaced like something drowned that had finally decided to stop waiting.

She had been the witness in the stairwell case. The first one. The one I'd solved from a dispatcher's chair with a thermos of black tea and a spreadsheet nobody asked me to make.

I pulled the file — my file, the private one, the one I kept in a fireproof box behind my winter boots because I am not a stupid man — and read her statement again for the first time since I'd written it into my notes.

*Ada Voss, 34 at the time. Niece of Henrik Voss, deceased, found at the base of the service stairwell of the Voss Building on Delacroix Street. Witness states she left the building at approximately 9:40 p.m. via the front entrance. Coroner placed death between 9:15 and 10:00.*

Her statement had been clean. Too clean, maybe, but clean isn't evidence. Grief is tidy sometimes. People rehearse their worst nights in their heads until they can recite them like weather reports.

What I'd found, back then, was in the cab logs. A car idling on Delacroix for eleven minutes that night, dispatched off-book by a driver who owed a favor to a man named Rennick — Henrik Voss's business partner, the one whose signature appeared on a transfer of assets dated nine days after the stairwell death. The fall hadn't killed the estate. It had *liquified* it. And Ada Voss, sole surviving heir, had signed it all away to Rennick within two weeks, or so the probate record showed — a woman too shattered to read what she was signing, according to everyone who testified.

Except that wasn't quite what happened either. Because when I cross-referenced the dates, I found that the transfer had been drafted *before* Henrik died. Dated forward. Which meant someone knew he was going to die.

I'd taken all of this to Detective Okafor, back when Okafor was still on the job, and Okafor had looked at my spreadsheet for a long time and then looked at me for longer, and said: *You understand that if this is right, you've just handed me a murder disguised as an accident that we already closed.*

Rennick went to trial. He died before sentencing — heart attack, they said, though I have never entirely believed in coincidence where money is concerned. The Voss estate reverted to Ada. Clean title, finally hers. Case closed, officially and otherwise.

And I had walked away feeling like a man who'd done a good deed in the dark.

Now a stranger was dead in my clothes, and the first thought I couldn't kill was: *who profits from everything I've ever touched?*

* * *

I found her address the way I find most things — through the taxi company's account database, which sounds sinister and is merely boring. Rich people book cars. Ada Voss booked cars twice a week, always the same route: her house on Fenwick Hill to the offices of Halloran & Marsh, attorneys, and back. Tuesdays and Fridays. Today was Friday.

I told dispatch I was doing a quality audit. Nobody questioned it. That's the thing about being reliable — after enough years, reliability becomes invisibility, and invisibility becomes permission.

I took a cab out to Fenwick Hill myself, in the gray hour before sunset, and sat across the street in a coffee shop that charged eight dollars for tea and judged me for asking for tap water.

Her house was the kind of money doesn't advertise: brick, ivy, a gate that needed painting. A garden gone slightly wild in the way of gardens tended by people who tend them to think, not to win prizes. At 5:12 she came out to the gate to collect mail, and I got my first look at Ada Voss in three years.

She was thinner. Her hair had gone almost entirely white at forty-one, which happens, I'm told, after certain kinds of years. She moved carefully, like a woman walking on a floor she no longer trusted to hold.

I gave her ten minutes, then crossed the street and rang the bell.

She opened the door on the chain. Through the gap: one eye, sharp and frightened.

"Ms. Voss. My name is Sebastian Oyelaran."

The eye didn't blink. "I know who you are."

That stopped me. "We've never met."

"No," she agreed. "But you're the dispatcher. The one who caught Rennick. My lawyer mentioned you. Twice." A pause. "He called you a ghost with a spreadsheet."

"I've been called worse." I kept my voice level, hands visible. "I need five minutes. It's about the man they pulled out of the harbor last night."

The chain didn't come off. But the door opened another inch, and the eye moved past me, scanning the street — checking, I realized, for something specific. Not for me. For whoever might be watching *her* watch me.

"You'd better come in," she said. "Not because I trust you. Because I don't trust the porch."

* * *

Her living room smelled of bergamot and dust sheets. Half the furniture was covered, as if she were preparing to leave or had recently decided against it. On the mantel stood a photograph of an older man with Henrik Voss's heavy jaw, and beside it, smaller, a young Ada laughing at something outside the frame.

She sat with her hands folded, and I noticed her knuckles, white around each other.

"Talk," she said.

"The drowned man. They haven't released his name yet—"

"Tomas Lindqvist." She said it flatly, without hesitation. "His name is Tomas Lindqvist. He was an archivist. He worked for the probate court."

I felt the floor tilt a degree. "How do you know that?"

"Because he called me. Three weeks ago. Said he'd found irregularities in the Voss probate file while digitizing records. Said the transfer documents had ink chemistry inconsistent with their stated dates." She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Ink chemistry. Like the whole mess could be settled by science."

"And then?"

"And then he stopped answering his phone, and yesterday he floated up wearing some poor bastard's coat." Her eyes came to mine and held there. "Wearing yours. That's why you're here. That's why you rang my bell instead of calling. You wanted to see my face when you asked."

"Did you kill him?"

"If I were going to kill anyone, Mr. Oyelaran, I'd have started with Rennick, and I'd have done it years ago, slowly, with lawyers." She unfolded her hands, and I saw they were trembling despite the iron in her voice. "No. I didn't kill him. But I'll tell you what I did do, and you can decide how much it damns me."

I waited.

"After your investigation, after Rennick died, I hired someone to go through everything again. Every document, every date, every signature. I wanted to know if the rot went deeper than one man. Whether Henrik's death was the only one bought and paid for." Her jaw tightened. "The auditor found discrepancies in the original transfer — the one I signed. Dates that couldn't be right. A notary stamp belonging to a man who, according to the registry, had been dead for a year when he supposedly witnessed my signature."

"Aldous Hale," I said quietly.

Something flickered across her face — surprise, then a bitter confirmation. "You know the name."

"I know the stamp. It's in my file."

"Of course it is." She stood, walked to the window, stood with her back to me. "Here is what damns me, Mr. Oyelaran. Two weeks ago, someone broke into my house. Nothing stolen. Except my auditor's report was moved — I could tell, the folder was refiled wrong — and a page was photographed, I think, because there was glass dust on the desk where my magnifier sits. Someone knows exactly what I have. Someone has been *inside my life*, learning its shape." She turned. "And now a man is dead in your clothes, and the police are circling you, and I keep thinking — who benefits? Who benefits from the fall, from the fraud, from the drowning, from *you*?"

"That's the question I came to ask you."

"Then we're both late." She sat back down, and the trembling had stopped, replaced by something harder. "My alibi for last night is perfect, by the way. Dinner with my attorney, seven to eleven, four witnesses and a credit card trail. I mention it because you'll check, and I'd rather you checked knowing I expected it."

"It's a good alibi."

"It's a *perfect* alibi. That's what frightens me." She leaned forward. "Don't you see? Whoever did this wants the world looking at you. And the only person alive who understands how these deaths connect — how the stairwell connects to the garage, to the hit-and-run, to the harbor — is sitting in my living room drinking tea he hasn't touched."

I looked down. The tea had gone cold without my noticing.

"Your investigation armed him," she said, not cruelly, just precisely, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "Your methods. Your logic. The way you stitched timelines together from cab logs and coroner's hours. If someone studied what you did — really studied it — they'd know exactly how to build a murder that looks like an accident, and exactly how to build a suspect who looks like a murderer."

The sick recognition she'd promised arrived on schedule, settling into my chest like swallowed ice. I had spent three years proud of my file. Neat. Thorough. Airtight. The kind of document a careful mind could learn from.

The kind of document a careful mind could *teach* from.

"Who else knows the report exists?" I asked.

"My attorney. My auditor. And whoever searched my house." She paused. "And Tomas Lindqvist, God rest him, who called me the week before he died to say he'd found the same notary anomaly independently. He said—" her voice caught, first real crack in the armor— "he said he'd contacted the original investigating officer for comment. Some routine verification. He wouldn't say who."

Okafor was retired in Portugal. That left a short list, and every name on it made my skin crawl.

I stood. "Ms. Voss. I need copies of everything. The report, the probate file, Lindqvist's letters if you kept them."

"I kept everything. I learned that from watching what happened to people who didn't." She walked me to the door, and at the threshold she caught my sleeve — a small, fierce grip. "Mr. Oyelaran. Whoever is doing this learned patience from somewhere. Learned procedure. Learned *you*. Which means they may also have learned the one thing you'd never suspect."

"Which is?"

"How you think when you're scared." She let go. "Because I'd wager you've never once been scared before this week. And a man who's never been scared thinks in straight lines. Straight lines are easy to predict."

I walked down her path through the wild garden, and the evening had gone dark without my noticing, and every shadow on Fenwick Hill had acquired a shape and a patience.

Behind me, her porch light snapped off — deliberate, a curtain falling.

And in my pocket, my phone buzzed once. An unknown number. Four words:

*You should stop digging.*
