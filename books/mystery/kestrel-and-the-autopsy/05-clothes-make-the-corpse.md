## 5. Clothes Make the Corpse

The morgue smelled of bleach and old cold, and Maren Kestrel stood at the observation window with a coffee she'd forgotten she was holding, watching Dr. Imara Sefu work on the drowned man like he was a locked box she intended to open politely.

"Tell me again," Kestrel said.

Sefu didn't look up. "You're going to hate it."

"I already hate it. Say it anyway."

The doctor peeled back the sheet to the shoulders. The man beneath — Viktor Lund, forty-one, dock clerk, no priors, no enemies anyone could find — looked peaceful in the way the dead always did when they hadn't suffered long. His face was gray-blue, his lips faintly darker, as if he'd been caught mid-word.

"Lividity," Sefu said. "Fixed lividity is your fingerprint of death. Blood settles where gravity puts it, and after eight to twelve hours it stops moving. It's stubborn. It doesn't relocate."

"And?"

"And Viktor's lividity pattern is wrong for how he was found." Sefu traced an invisible line along the corpse's back with a gloved finger. "He was pulled out of the harbor face-down, which should mean pooling along his chest and anterior surfaces. Instead the deepest fixation is across his posterior torso and the backs of his thighs. He lay on his back somewhere flat and hard for hours before he ever touched water."

Kestrel set the coffee down. It had gone cold anyway.

"Dressed post-mortem," she said.

"Dressed post-mortem," Sefu confirmed. "And not by an amateur. No ligature marks from dragging, no abrasions consistent with being hauled across concrete. Whoever moved him used a gurney or a board. Somebody practiced. Somebody careful." She pulled the sheet back up with something close to tenderness. "There's more."

"There's always more."

"The clothes themselves. The coat and scarf — Oyelaran's, we're assuming, pending fiber comparison — were put on him after death. Buttoning a coat onto a body with fixed rigor takes patience. You have to work the arms, flex the joints, force them. Our friend here shows postmortem bruising at both shoulders consistent with exactly that." Sefu finally turned, stripping her gloves. "But here's what I keep coming back to, Detective. The scarf was tied in a half-Windsor knot. Neat. Centered. Nobody ties a neat knot on a corpse in the dark unless they've done it before, or unless they wanted it to look right. Wanted it to *read*."

Read. Like text. Like evidence meant to be found and interpreted.

Kestrel thought about Sebastian Oyelaran sitting across from her two nights ago, hands folded, voice level, explaining cab logs and coroner timelines the way other men explained weather. He hadn't flinched when she'd laid out the photos. He'd leaned in. That was the part that bothered her — not that he might be guilty, but that guilt and genius wore the same expression on him, and she couldn't yet tell which one she was looking at.

"Time of dressing?" she asked.

"Between eleven p.m. and two a.m., based on core temperature curve. Body entered the water around three, give or take forty minutes. Tide did the rest."

Eleven to two. She was home by then. Half the city was. But a night-shift dispatcher was awake, working, thirty meters from a wall of screens showing every car in the city.

* * *

The forensics lab on Delancey Street had the fluorescent hum of a place where hope went to be quantified. Tech lead Priya Raman met her at the elevator with a tablet already open.

"You wanted everything on the clothing," Raman said. "I've got good news and bad news."

"Bad first."

"The coat's dry-cleaned recently, but the inner lining holds a cocktail of particulates: diesel soot, brake dust, and a specific industrial degreaser — trichloroethylene, heavy concentration. Same signature appears on the scarf and on Lund's own jacket."

"He worked the docks."

"Right, except the concentration gradient is wrong. The degreaser saturates the coat lining but barely registers on the outer shell. That means the coat hung somewhere near a source — a garage, a maintenance room, a boiler space — long enough to absorb it. Not worn around town. *Stored*."

Kestrel felt the walls of the interview room in her memory, the way Oyelaran's building had felt: narrow stairwell, radiator clanking, a basement level with a garage door that groaned like a dying animal. She'd walked past it without a second look.

"Good news?" she said.

Raman swiped to a new screen. "Fiber transfer. We found synthetic fibers from the coat on Lund's neck and jawline — consistent with someone pulling the coat up over his head to dress him. And we found something better. Latent print, partial, left thumb, lifted from the coat's third button. Smudged, degraded, but the ridge detail is enough for exclusion if not identification."

"Whose?"

"That's the interesting part. It's not yours, not mine, not Lund's, and it's not a match for Sebastian Oyelaran." Raman let that sit. "Whoever dressed the body, it wasn't him. At least not with this hand."

Kestrel stared at the screen. A partial thumbprint that cleared the prime suspect was either mercy or design. She didn't believe in mercy lately.

"Anything else?"

"One thing. The scarf." Raman zoomed until the wool filled the screen, grainy and magnified. "We recovered a single hair, caught in the knot. Brown, four centimeters, natural shed — no follicle, so no DNA without a root. But the color treatment is distinctive. Box dye, ash-brown over darker base, cheap brand, lot number still legible under magnification. We can trace the purchase."

"Do it."

"Already running. Here's the thing, though." Raman lowered the tablet. "Ash-brown over dark. That's a cover-gray job. Someone hiding age, or just hiding. Could be a woman, could be a man who dyes. It narrows nothing and everything."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the arithmetic nobody wanted done.

Post-mortem dressing required three things: access to the body between death and water, access to the clothing, and time alone. Viktor Lund had last been seen alive at nine-forty p.m., leaving a bar on Corvin Street, sober enough to walk straight. Cause of death was drowning, but the water in his lungs was harbor water — he'd gone in breathing, which meant he was alive when submerged, which meant someone held him under or put him somewhere the tide would do the holding. Either way, the killer had been at the waterfront between roughly midnight and three.

The clothing told its own story. Oyelaran's missing coat and scarf had vanished from his building sometime in the previous week — he'd reported nothing, noticed nothing, which meant the thief had access without alarm. Access without alarm meant a resident, a building worker, or someone with a key.

Kestrel pulled the tenant ledger from the property manager, a sweating man named Dombrowski who kept records the way hoarders kept newspapers — technically, and with no system. She spread it across her desk and started crossing names against shift schedules.

Twelve people had routine access to the building: nine residents, the super, a cleaning contractor who came Tuesdays, and — her pen stopped — a dispatcher trainee listed as a frequent overnight visitor to unit 4B. Elias Brandt. Twenty-three years old. Trainee at the same dispatch house where Oyelaran ran the night board.

She pulled Brandt's file. Clean record, community college dropout, hired eight months ago on Oyelaran's recommendation. There was a photograph from his employee badge: a soft-faced young man with careful eyes, the kind of person rooms forgot he was in.

A trainee learning the trade from the best pattern-reader the night shift had ever produced. A trainee with building access, dispatch access, and — she checked the GPS archive request she'd filed that morning — potentially access to historical route data.

Kestrel sat back. The circle was tightening, and the terrible question was around whose throat it would close. Because there were two readings of the evidence, and both fit like tailored coats.

Reading one: Sebastian Oyelaran killed Viktor Lund, staged the drowning, dressed the body in his own clothes through some convoluted misdirection she couldn't yet reconstruct, and his thumbprint's absence was explained by gloves.

Reading two: someone was dressing bodies in Sebastian Oyelaran's life the way you'd forge a signature — carefully, lovingly, with intimate knowledge of how he thought. And the only people who knew how he thought were the ones who worked beside him, learned from him, watched him solve murders the police had closed.

She looked at the partial print again. *Not a match for Oyelaran.* If reading one were true, the killer had been sloppy enough to leave a print but lucky enough that it wasn't the suspect's. If reading two were true, the print belonged to the real hand, and it was sitting in some database or some drawer waiting for her to find it.

Kestrel didn't believe in luck. She believed in patterns. And the pattern said: whoever did this wanted Sebastian Oyelaran inside the frame so badly that they'd studied him like scripture.

Which raised the ugliest question of all. Where does someone learn to think like Sebastian Oyelaran?

From Sebastian Oyelaran.

Or from his files.

She was reaching for her phone to pull his case history — the unofficial one, the notebooks, the cross-references he'd admitted to keeping — when Raman called.

"We traced the dye lot," Raman said. "Box store on Halvard Avenue, purchased six weeks ago. Store camera keeps ninety days."

"Send me the timestamp."

"Already did. But Kestrel—" a pause, the kind techs took when they didn't want to say the next part, "—the purchase was made with cash. No loyalty card. But the register log flags one detail. The same lot number sold twice that day. Two boxes. Two separate transactions, twenty minutes apart. Cash both times."

Two boxes. One buyer, or two people sharing a color.

"Pull the camera footage," Kestrel said. "Every angle. I want faces."

"Working on it. Give me till morning."

She hung up and looked at the board above her desk, where Viktor Lund's face hung beside a photo of a wool coat and a knotted scarf. Twelve names on her pad. Nine residents. A super. A cleaner. A trainee.

One of them had tied a perfect knot in the dark.

Her phone buzzed again — dispatch house internal line, which surprised her. She answered.

"Detective Kestrel? This is Elias Brandt, down at the dispatch house." The voice was young, careful, rehearsed-calm. "I — I found something in the route archives. Something wrong. I think you need to see it."

Kestrel closed her eyes. In her experience, people who volunteered were either innocent, terrified, or performing all three.

"When?" she said.

"Now. Tonight. Please. Before someone else finds out I called."
