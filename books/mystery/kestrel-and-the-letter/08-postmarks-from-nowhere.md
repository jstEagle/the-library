## 8. Postmarks from Nowhere

Delia Sandoval brought the letters in a biscuit tin.

She arrived at Kestrel & Co. on a gray Thursday morning, three days after the door-slamming confrontation, standing in the shop's doorway with the tin clutched to her chest like something she'd been asked to surrender at gunpoint. Rain had gotten into her hair. She hadn't bothered with an umbrella, or hadn't thought to, and there was a look on her face that Bram recognized from fifteen years of watching people decide whether to trust her — the long internal argument between fear and grief, fought out in the muscles around the mouth.

"You said you'd find out who killed my sister," Delia said. "If she's dead."

"I said I'd find out what happened to her."

"Same thing." Delia crossed the threshold as if crossing a border. The bell over the door gave its small tired ring. "I've been thinking about it for three days. About the coffin." She set the tin down on the counter with a click that sounded final. "It was light. I carried one corner of it, at the graveside, because they asked family to. I was thirty-one and grieving and I thought — I thought I was just strong for once in my life. Fifteen years I've been proud of that. That I could carry her." Her voice caught and steadied itself with visible effort. "You're telling me I carried an empty box."

Bram didn't answer. There was nothing true she could say that would be kind.

"The groceries," Delia went on. "You saw them. I told you my sister's ghost doesn't eat, and then I couldn't sleep, and then I couldn't not come here." She pushed the tin an inch toward Bram. "These come every year. Her birthday. March ninth. Fourteen of them now. I never showed anyone, because showing them felt like admitting I believed she was alive, and believing she was alive meant admitting she'd chosen to leave me behind."

"And did you believe it?"

Delia looked at the rain-streaked window, at the shelves of unsold books, at anything but Bram. "Every year on March tenth," she said, "I make two cups of coffee out of habit. So yes. I believed it. I just couldn't afford to."

Bram took the tin. It was heavier than it looked — fourteen envelopes, each one holding whatever Ivy Sandoval had been able to say to the sister she'd abandoned in order to stay breathing.

"I'll take care of these," Bram said.

"You'll take care of *me*," Delia corrected quietly. "That's the deal. You find out where she is, you tell me before you tell anyone else. Not the police. Not your partner. Me first." She held Bram's eyes, and there was steel in her that hadn't been there at the front door of the empty house. Grief had forged it, apparently. Fifteen years of carrying light coffins. "Swear it."

"I swear."

Delia nodded once, turned, and walked back out into the rain without touching anything else in the shop, as though the place were already contaminated by whatever was coming.

* * *

Bram closed early. She hung the BACK IN AN HOUR sign that had hung there, variously, for four hours at a time since 2019, made coffee she wouldn't drink, and laid the letters out across the reading table in the back room in chronological order, oldest to newest, the way she'd once laid out evidence photographs on the long table in the fourth-floor bullpen of the Ninth Precinct.

The room felt different with them spread out. Smaller. More serious. She was aware of herself doing it — the ritual of it, the theater. A detective needs a table. A detective needs the objects arranged just so. But ritual is also method, and method was all she had left, so she let the old machinery engage and stood over the letters with a magnifying glass she'd bought at a hardware store and a legal pad she'd bought for making bookshop inventory lists.

Fourteen letters. Each postmarked, according to Delia, from nowhere — no return address, postmarks smudged or absent, mailed from towns too small to have their own sorting facilities or handed to travelers, dropped into distant boxes. The earliest ones were handwritten. Later ones were typed. One, from 2016, appeared to be cut-and-pasted from printed text, ransom-note style, which had frightened Delia badly until Bram explained that it probably meant the opposite of what it seemed: a person careful enough to vary her methods was a person still alive and still afraid.

The handwriting was the first thing. Bram had spent a career reading statements, confessions, forged alibis, and she knew that handwriting is a body — it carries weight, fatigue, tremor, haste. These letters carried all of those things, deliberately distributed. The 2011 letter was written in a loose, looping hand. The 2013 letter was cramped and upright. The 2018 letter slanted hard to the right like someone writing against a wind. Any examiner would have called them five different writers.

But Bram wasn't looking at the hands. She was looking at the habits underneath them.

*I hope this finds you well* opened six of the fourteen letters. Not "I hope you're well." Not "hope all is well." Always the full, formal, slightly archaic construction: *I hope this finds you well.* Seven letters ended with some variation of *be good to yourself* — again, formal, faintly old-fashioned, the phrasing of someone who had spent years transcribing other people's formal language until it colonized her own. And in nine of the fourteen, in different hands, in different years, the same odd construction appeared: *I am kept well informed*, or *you are kept in my thoughts*, or *the sea keeps me well.*

Kept. Passive constructions. Things done to a person rather than by them.

Bram sat back and looked at the pattern and felt the old cold pleasure move through her chest, the one that had no business feeling like pleasure at all. Whoever wrote these letters had spent years — decades — taking down other people's words verbatim. Court stenographers developed exactly this kind of fossilized formality; their own speech became a sediment of everyone else's speech, depositions and jury instructions and boilerplate rulings layered into the way they framed a sentence. The passive voice especially. Stenographers lived inside passive voice. *The defendant was advised of his rights. The record was sealed.* A life spent writing what was done, never who did it.

And more than that: the deliberate variation of the handwriting was itself a signature. Only two kinds of people varied their own script that systematically — forgers, and people in hiding. And forgers varied it carelessly, because they were performing difference rather than practicing survival. This was practiced. This was a woman who had rebuilt her hand year after year the way a fugitive rebuilds a face, and who had failed, bless her, to rebuild the architecture of her sentences, because you can change how you shape an *a* but you cannot easily change fifteen thousand hours of transcription that taught you how a sentence should stand.

*You are kept in my thoughts,* Bram wrote on the legal pad, and underlined *kept* twice.

Then she did the thing she'd been circling all evening, the thing she'd noticed on the second pass through and hadn't wanted to trust.

In three of the letters — 2012, 2017, and last year's — Ivy had used the same word for the same thing. Not *ocean*. Not *coast*. *The shore road*, in 2012. *Down the shore* in 2017. And last March: *the gulls keep the shore road honest.*

Bram knew that phrase. Not from any case file. From her mother, who had grown up in Harwick, forty miles up the coast, and who had said *down the shore* her whole life the way other people said *at the beach* — a regionalism so specific it mapped to a stretch of coastline barely sixty miles wide. Down the shore. Upstate, down the shore. Nobody outside that ribbon of coast said it. Nobody inside it ever stopped.

Three coastal towns sat inside that ribbon within reasonable mailing distance of nowhere: Harwick, Pelican Narrows, and Graywater Point. Bram wrote them on the pad, drew a line under them, and sat very still in the lamplight while the rain worked at the windows.

Methodical hope, Marcus Hale had once called it, back when he was the kind of man who said things like that. The worst kind of hope, he'd said, because it makes you think you control it.

Bram looked at the three town names and let herself hope anyway.

* * *

Marcus came the next morning.

She saw him through the front glass before he reached the door — the walk was unchanged after all these years, that rolling, deliberate stride of a big man who had learned early to make his size look unhurried. He stood outside for a moment, reading the shop's name off the window the way he'd once read a street number off a warrant, and then came in, and the bell rang, and twenty years of shared history walked into Bram's failing bookshop wearing a rain-dark coat.

"Bram."

"Marcus."

He looked around the shop with an expression she couldn't parse — something between appraisal and apology. "Place looks the same."

"It doesn't. Nothing does. What do you want?"

He winced at that, fair enough, and came to the counter instead of pretending to browse. Up close he was grayer than the last time she'd seen him, two years ago at Okafor's promotion party, the night they'd argued in the parking lot about nothing and everything. His hands were folded on the counter like a man at a hearing.

"I heard you've been asking questions," he said. "About Renner."

"Heard from who?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously, actually, because if you're hearing about my questions, then other people are hearing about my questions, and I'd very much like to know how fast it travels and in what direction."

Marcus exhaled through his nose. Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that made the window glow. "People talk. You know how it works. A retired detective pulls files from storage, visits a cemetery, interviews a gravedigger, knocks on the sister's door twice in one week — that's not quiet behavior, Bram. That's a parade."

"A small parade."

"A parade." He glanced at the reading-room door, closed now, with the biscuit tin's contents locked in the safe behind it. "I'm going to say this once, and then I'm going to leave, and we can both pretend this conversation happened differently. Leave it alone. Whatever you think you found — the case is closed. It was closed correctly. Accidental drowning, verified, signed off. Fifteen years ago. Let it stay closed."

"Closed casket," Bram said mildly. "Verified by whom?"

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, buried, gone. She'd seen that exact flicker a hundred times across interrogation tables, on the faces of men deciding how much of the truth they could afford. She had never once seen it on Marcus's face. Seeing it now was like finding water in a wall.

"By the coroner's office," he said carefully. "By procedure."

"Procedure." Bram leaned on the counter. "Marcus. I'm not asking you to confess anything. I'm asking you to tell me why a retired detective drives forty minutes in the rain to warn off a woman who's reading old newspaper archives. Because that's not concern. Concern sends flowers. This is something else. This is a man checking how close the fire is to his own house."

For a long moment he said nothing. The clock above the register ticked. Somewhere in the stacks the heating pipes knocked, the building settling the way old buildings do, complaining about the weather.

"What do you know?" he asked finally, and his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the warning, almost hungry. "About the letters. About the stenographer. Tell me what you know."

And there it was. Not *tell me what you think you know.* Not *what could you possibly know.* *Tell me what you know* — the question of a man who needed to inventory a threat, or a man who needed, desperately, to learn something he couldn't ask for directly.

Bram smiled at him, and it was the smile she'd worn across twenty years of tables, warm and absolutely opaque.

"I know the same thing you do, Marcus," she said. "That somebody drowned in 2009 who maybe didn't. That somebody's been writing letters every March ninth for fourteen years. And that whoever you're scared of, you're more scared of them than you are of me — which tells me everything I need to know about how big this is." She straightened. "So here's what happens next. You go home. You think about which side of this you're actually on. And when you figure it out, you come back, and you tell me the truth, and I will remember that you did."

Marcus looked at her for a long time. Whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly, once, like a man accepting terms he hated.

"You always were the stubborn one," he said at the door.

"You always were the smart one," Bram answered. "Start acting like it."

The bell rang. He was gone into the mist, and she watched him through the glass until the fog took him, and then she went back to the reading room, unlocked the safe, and spread the letters out again under the lamp.

Fourteen years of postmarks from nowhere. Three towns on a sixty-mile ribbon of coast. A woman who wrote *down the shore* the way Bram's mother had, who built sentences like a courtroom, who had faked her own death and then spent a decade and a half sending love letters to a sister through the mail like messages in bottles thrown backward through time.

Bram got out a coastal atlas, opened it to the page, and set three circles on the paper with a grease pencil — Harwick, Pelican Narrows, Graywater Point.

Graywater Point. She paused over the name. Something snagged — not memory exactly, but the edge of one, the way you sometimes catch the hem of a fact before you can pull it whole into the light. Renner Shipping had a terminal somewhere on that coast. She was almost sure of it. She'd seen the name in the archive clippings, in the background of some photograph or freight manifest, and filed it away in the place where unimportant things wait to become important.

She wrote the name in the margin and underlined it twice, the way she'd underlined *kept.*

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she'd start with Graywater Point.

Outside, the mist thickened toward evening, and somewhere down the block a car engine idled longer than it needed to, and Bram Villalobos, who had spent her whole life learning to notice such things, noticed it — and hoped, methodically, that she was wrong about what it meant.
