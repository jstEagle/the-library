## 15. Two Women, One Truth

The bell over the door of Kestrel & Co. had not rung since seven o'clock, and Bram had begun to believe it would not ring again that night. She was in the back room with a kettle and a stack of unsold stock she'd been meaning to sort since spring — the particular purgatory of a bookseller whose shop was dying slowly enough to feel like a choice — when the bell rang at 9:40.

She did not call out *we're closed*. Fifteen years a detective, five years a woman pretending otherwise: her body knew the difference between a lost tourist and something else before her mind finished the arithmetic. The knock of the bell was too quick, too certain, the door opened by someone who had already decided to come in.

Bram set down the kettle without letting it clatter against the sink.

Through the doorway she saw a woman standing just inside the front of the shop, in the wedge of lamplight that fell between the poetry section and the window display. Small. Mid-forties, maybe, though it was hard to say — the face had the ageless, worn quality of a coin kept too long in circulation. Her hair was an unconvincing chestnut dye grown out at the roots to gray. She wore a canvas jacket two sizes too large and she stood the way people stand when they have spent years making sure no room ever has only one exit.

She held her wrists slightly turned outward, hands loose, fingers curled as though they were resting on keys that weren't there.

Bram's mouth went dry.

"You're holding your hands like a stenographer," Bram said. It came out quieter than she intended. "Ivy Sandoval holds her hands like that."

The woman didn't flinch. That was the first thing Bram noticed about her up close, later, when there was time to notice things: nothing made her flinch. She had been startled out of herself so many times, so thoroughly, that the reflex had been burned away like nerve endings.

"I read about that," the woman said. "In the letters you've been getting." Her voice was low, even, with the flat coastal vowels of somewhere north of Graywater Point. "Whoever writes them knows things. I wanted to know if you did."

"I know a coffin that weighed almost nothing," Bram said. "I know a sister who gets letters every year on a birthday from nowhere. And I know I closed this shop ten minutes ago."

"You left the back door key under the loose brick," the woman said. "You've done it for years. Even after the window was broken." A pause. "I watched for three nights."

Something cold moved through Bram — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. She knew what it cost to watch a building for three nights, and she knew what kind of person paid that price, and it was never a person who meant harm. Harm didn't have the patience.

"Tea?" Bram said.

* * *

They sat in the back room among the unsold stock, at the small table where Bram ate lunch and paid bills, under the one bulb that worked. The woman — she would not yet answer to Ivy, and Bram understood why; a name is a door, and doors are dangerous — wrapped both hands around her cup as if it were something that might be taken from her.

"Tell me how you did it," Bram said. "The death. Start anywhere."

Ivy — Bram had decided to think of her as Ivy even if she couldn't say it aloud yet — looked into her tea for a long moment.

"It starts earlier than the drowning," she said. "It starts with what I heard. You were a detective. You know what a stenographer hears. Everything. Every word, every hesitation, every name dropped like something heavy into a courtroom and lifted out again before anyone notices the dent." She turned the cup a quarter-turn. "Judge Renner's last hearing. The fraud case against Renner Maritime. I transcribed eleven days of it. Eleven days of Edmund Renner's lawyers shredding paper in public, and the judge taking notes in his own hand, and one afternoon — the fourth day — the judge asked a question off the record. Just him and counsel, sidebar, but my machine was running because nobody ever remembers my machine is running."

"What did he ask?"

"He asked whether the accounts the auditors flagged could be traced to a second ledger. And one of the attorneys — Corinne Ashford's then-husband, actually, he's disbarred now, another thread for your wall — laughed. He laughed and said, 'Your Honor, there is no second ledger.' And the judge said, very quietly, 'Then why does your client keep paying for one?'"

Bram felt the old thrill move through her chest, unwelcome and familiar, like a dog waking in a corner of the house.

"The transcript," Bram said. "The original. Do you have it?"

"I have everything." Ivy said it without pride. "That was the problem. I was the record. Eleven days, certified, sealed copies filed with the court — and then the judge died, the case died, and someone started asking which copies existed and who had read them. The clerk who pulled the file for me died in a car accident in March. The auditor who flagged the accounts drowned in his pool in June. Both ruled accidental. I went to the coroner's office with questions and met a deputy named Harlan Voss who was very kind and very interested in exactly which documents I'd read." She looked up. "You found Voss yet? No. Of course not. He's a chapter you haven't reached."

Bram let that pass. "So you ran."

"I ran. And when running wasn't enough — when the third witness died and I understood they weren't going to stop — I stopped running and started disappearing instead. There was a man in the coroner's office who owed me. His brother had been on trial four years before, and I'd transcribed for the defense, and I'd caught an error in the prosecution's timeline that would have convicted an innocent man. I gave it to the defense attorney. His brother walked. He never forgot." Her voice stayed level; only her hands, around the cup, told the truth. "He falsified my certificate. Closed casket — my sister insisted on viewing and he talked her out of it, told her the recovery had been bad, which was cruel and necessary. There was no body. There was never a body. There was a river, a report, and a woman who walked out of her life carrying one box of files."

"Delia thinks you're dead," Bram said. "She slammed a door in my face to protect a ghost."

"She slams that door every year," Ivy said, "when the letter comes. She thinks the letters are from me. They aren't." Something crossed her face then, the first crack in the level voice. "I couldn't risk writing. Handwriting, postmarks, patterns — I taught myself to be illegible. So I type them. Different machines, different fonts, shifted phrasing. But Delia reads them and hears my voice anyway, because sisters do. And every year I stand outside her house on some pretext — meter reader, census taker — and I see her alive, and I leave." She set the cup down with great care, as though setting down something heavier than tea. "Fifteen years. Do you know what it does to a person, to attend their own funeral? To be loved correctly by everyone they know, and to receive all of it through glass?"

Bram did know a version of it. She said so without saying it: "Yes."

Ivy studied her across the table, and Bram watched her decide something — watched the decision happen behind her eyes like weather changing.

"The letters," Ivy said. "Show me the letters."

* * *

Bram spread them on the table under the bulb: the confession, the challenge, the third letter with its offer — *ask me anything*. Ivy read them the way she must have read everything for thirty years, completely, once, her eyes moving in the short efficient lines of a professional.

"He types on a manual," she said immediately. "See the strike depth variation? Electric keyboards don't fatigue. This is a man typing angry, or typing sick — look at how the pressure degrades in the third paragraphs of each letter. He runs out of strength mid-thought and forces himself to finish." Her finger hovered over the confession without touching it. "And here — 'the case number only insiders would know.' He uses the *old* numbering system. Pre-digitization. Anyone under fifty would have looked it up. He remembered it."

"He's old," Bram said. "Or wants to seem old."

"He's dying." Ivy said it flatly. "I've read ten thousand pages of testimony. People lie with facts, Mr.—with Detective. Nobody lies with rhythm. This rhythm is a man putting his affairs in order." She straightened. "Now. What did you ask him, when he said ask anything?"

Bram hesitated. Then she took out the reply she'd gotten two days ago and laid it beside the others. She had tested him with the detail: the sound the boathouse chain hoist made, a specific groan of rusted cable that appeared in no report, no transcript, nothing written anywhere — except in the memory of whoever had been on that dock fifteen years ago.

"And?" Ivy asked.

"He confirmed it. Described the pitch of it. Said it 'sang flat, like the judge's conscience.'" Bram's throat tightened. "Only the killer would know the hoist sang at all."

Ivy was quiet for a moment. Then she began, softly, to laugh — not amusement; something rawer, closer to grief.

"Fifteen years," she said. "Fifteen years I assumed the family killed the judge because he was about to rule against them. Clean motive, clean cover-up. But that's not what the letters say, is it. Read me the sentence again. The one about why."

Bram found it. "'I did not kill him for money. I killed him because he was right, and being right was going to ruin everyone I loved, and I could not bear it.'"

"There it is," Ivy whispered. "A son. It's a son. Only a son says *everyone I loved* about a man he hated enough to drown." She looked up, eyes bright and terrible with understanding. "Julian Renner. The son was estranged — there were articles, he'd left the firm, testified *for* the auditors briefly before recanting. Everyone assumed the family cut him off. But what if he wasn't cut off? What if he found the second ledger himself, confronted his father, and—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "And then the family buried the murder to protect the laundering scheme, and Julian let them, because the alternative was confessing. And now he's dying, and the empire is passing intact to Edmund's line, and he can't—"

"He can't let it," Bram finished. "So he hires a retired detective with a failing bookshop to burn it all down. Using the witness he failed to kill as the match."

The words hung in the lamplight. Outside, rain had started against the back window — the same window that had been broken, now boarded, now raining on again, indifferent.

"He used me," Bram said. "Every lead he fed me. The cemetery, the towns, the ribbon. He's been steering me toward you for weeks."

"Yes," Ivy said. "And?" She almost smiled; it was a battered thing, mostly scar tissue. "Detective, I have spent fifteen years being hunted by men paid to make me nothing. If a dying murderer wants to spend his last months aiming me like a weapon at his own family — then for once in my life, I am the bullet instead of the target. I can live with that arithmetic. Can you?"

Bram thought of Marcus, of the page of notes he'd offered at such a price. Loyalty on one side of the scale, truth on the other, and her whole life spent pretending the scale didn't exist.

"I can live with it," she said. "I just want to know what it costs first."

"Everything," Ivy said simply. "It always costs everything. But listen—" and here she leaned forward, and for the first time her hands came off the invisible keyboard and gripped the edge of the table, knuckles pale, "—listen. I have eleven days of testimony in a box that proves the scheme. You have a killer willing to sign a confession and hand over evidence implicating the whole apparatus. Somewhere there's a deputy coroner with a ledger full of falsified bodies, including mine. And somewhere in that family there's a daughter — Corinne — who married out and came back and has been asking questions of her own; I've seen her name in the files of the investigators they hire. Four separate fires, Detective. All it takes is one dry season and a spark."

Bram looked at her — this woman who had died so thoroughly she'd gotten good at it, sitting in a dying bookshop drinking bad tea, offering to burn down one of the great families of the coast.

"They'll come for you," Bram said. "Once you surface, they'll come, and they won't send lawyers."

"I know." Ivy's voice didn't waver. "I've been dead for fifteen years. I'd like to try being alive in public for one day. Even if it's the last one."

The rain thickened against the boards. Bram got up, fetched the good whiskey from above the register — the bottle she saved for occasions she'd stopped believing would come — and poured two fingers into two chipped mugs.

"To the record," she said.

Ivy lifted her mug. "To the record," she agreed. "May it finally be read."

They drank. And it was somewhere in that small ceremony — two burned women toasting with cheap whiskey under a single working bulb — that the wariness between them quietly put down its arms. Not trust, not yet. Trust was a building that took years. But the foundation stone went into the ground that night, and both of them felt it settle.

It was near two in the morning when Ivy, half-asleep in the shop's reading chair, said suddenly:

"The confessor's next instruction. When it comes — it'll tell us where to go. He's been choreographing this from the start; the letters are a score and we're dancing to it. Which means the last movement is already written." Her eyes opened, fixed on Bram. "Which means somewhere in this city, a dying man is waiting to find out whether we follow the music."

As if summoned, headlights swept slow across the boarded back window — paused — and moved on.

Bram stood very still until the light faded.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we wait for the mail."
