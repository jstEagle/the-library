## 16. The Deputy's Ledger

The page Marcus had given her was creased into eighths, soft as cloth from years of being folded and unfolded in a pocket or a drawer, and it smelled faintly of smoke even now — the ghost of the fire he'd set to his own notes. Bram had smoothed it flat on the counter of Kestrel & Co. three nights running, reading it the way she used to read witness statements: not for what it said, but for what it was trying not to say.

One name. That was all the page held that mattered.

*Deputy Coroner R. Pellman — signed the Sandoval certificate. Also signed Renner transport manifests 2009–2011 (see shipping co. audit).*

The last part was Marcus's addition, made later, in different ink — a breadcrumb he'd kept back even from himself, maybe, a small act of conscience buried inside an act of cowardice. Bram had stared at it a long time. *Renner transport manifests.* The coroner's office had contracted with the family's shipping firm to move bodies upstate. It was elegant, really. The same company that moved cargo containers full of laundered money out of Graywater Harbor also moved its problems. A body was just freight with a story attached.

And Raymond Pellman had been the man who stamped the stories official.

She found him the way she found everything now — slowly, cross-referencing, letting the confessor's letters do half the work. The third letter had come two weeks ago, and one line in it had lodged in her like a splinter: *Ask yourself who profits from a closed casket. Then ask who drinks to forget he profited least.* She'd pulled pension records through a friend at the county clerk's office — a favor owed from another life — and there he was: Raymond Pellman, retired deputy coroner, drawing his check monthly to an address on the wrong side of Carroway Flats, the drowned neighborhood at the edge of the estuary where the houses leaned toward the water like tired old men.

She drove out on a Tuesday morning in rain that couldn't decide whether to be mist or commitment, parking under a dead sycamore whose branches scratched the roof of her car like fingers testing a lid.

* * *

The house was a bungalow gone gray, its porch sagging at one corner, a plastic flamingo faded nearly white standing sentinel by the steps. The smell reached her before she knocked — beer and something sourer underneath, the smell of a body quietly giving up. When Pellman opened the door he filled most of the frame, a big man gone soft and yellowed, wearing a cardigan buttoned wrong and an expression that suggested he'd been expecting this visit for fifteen years and had only stopped hoping for it recently.

"You're the bookshop woman," he said. His voice was gravel steeped in whiskey. "Villalobos. I read about you. Before."

"Before," Bram agreed. There'd been articles, once. *Detective Villalobos Closes Harborview Strangler Case.* A lifetime ago, in a career that had since cost her a marriage, a partner's trust, and most of her sleep.

"They said you retired." He didn't move from the doorway.

"I did." She held up the folded page between two fingers. "But somebody sent me your name anyway."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man seeing the last item on a long list finally arrive. He stepped back from the door without a word, and she took it as invitation.

Inside, the house was dim and meticulous in a way that startled her. Dishes washed. Floor swept. A single glass on the coffee table, rinsed and drying upside down, as if he rationed himself to one at a time and hated every second of the discipline. On the wall hung a framed certificate from the coroner's association, and beneath it, incongruously, a child's crayon drawing of a man in a white coat holding a small dog.

"Granddaughter," Pellman said, following her gaze. He said it the way other men said *alibi.* "She's seven. Thinks I helped people." He lowered himself into an armchair with the care of a man handling his own bruised organs. "You want to know if I faked Ivy Sandoval's death."

"I know you did," Bram said. "I want the ledger."

The silence lasted long enough for the rain to change its mind and commit. Pellman looked at his hands — enormous hands, hands that had lifted bodies out of rivers and off highways and out of bathtubs, hands that had zipped bags and signed forms. "Everyone always assumes we keep trophies," he said finally. "Souvenirs. You watch too many movies, all of you detectives." He laughed, and it turned into a cough that went on too long. "But a man like me — a man who does what they tell him because they own his debts, his mortgage, his daughter's surgery — a man like that needs one thing."

"Leverage," Bram said.

"Insurance." He nodded slowly. "A ledger. Every certificate I falsified. Date, decedent, cause listed versus cause actual, who instructed me, how I was paid. Fifteen entries over nine years." He looked up at her, and his face was terrible in its plainness. "Ivy Sandoval is entry number four. Cause listed: accidental drowning. Cause actual: alive. Instructed by: Edmund Renner's attorney, one G. Marsh. Payment: my daughter's oncology bill, settled in full."

Bram kept her voice level, though her pulse was hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out. "Where is it?"

"A bank box." He smiled, and there was no humor in it, only exhaustion worn down to the bone. "You think I'd keep it here? I'm a drunk, Ms. Villalobos, not an idiot. First National, Carroway branch. Box 114. Key's taped under the drawer of my nightstand, because I figured anyone coming to kill me would search the obvious places first, and anyone honest would look under the drawer, because that's where my mother hid the Christmas money when I was a boy." He coughed again. "I've been waiting for someone honest for a long time."

"Why give it to me? Why not the police, the press, anyone?"

"Because the police are why the ledger exists." Each word came out slow, deliberate, a dying man spending his last clear sentences carefully. "Because the press printed what the Renners wrote. Because everyone I could have gone to was either bought or frightened or both." His eyes were wet now, whether from the coughing or something else she couldn't say. "Because you're the one they're afraid of. I heard it through channels. Corinne Renner herself asked about you. Triple value for your building — that was them trying to buy you, and when you wouldn't sell, they got scared. People like that don't get scared often. When they do, it means someone's finally holding the right end of the rope."

He hauled himself upright, crossed to the nightstand in the bedroom — she followed, watching him peel back the tape with fingers that trembled from more than drink — and pressed a small brass key into her palm. His hand closed around hers afterward, brief and crushing.

"There's a condition," he rasped. "Not for me. For the girl in entry number eleven. Marisol Vega. They told me she overdosed. I wrote it down because I checked — I checked, God help me — and there wasn't a mark on her that alcohol couldn't explain. If the ledger does any good in this world, find out what happened to Marisol Vega. Her mother still lights a candle at St. Brigid's every Sunday. Fifteen years of candles." He released her hand. "Tell her mother something true. That's my price. Absolution isn't for sale, but I'd like to make a down payment."

Bram stood in the small gray bedroom of a dying man and understood that she was looking at the wreckage the Renners left behind them everywhere they went — not just bodies, but the living, corroded, hollowed out and left standing. "You'll have to testify," she said. "When this breaks. Whatever's left of you will have to stand up in a courtroom and read that ledger aloud."

Pellman smiled, and for a moment, behind the yellowed skin and the ruined liver, she saw the man who had once believed the crayon drawing on his wall. "Ms. Villalobos," he said, "I've been rehearsing my testimony for fifteen years. Every night, into the bottom of a glass. I know it better than the Lord's Prayer."

* * *

She didn't go straight home. Fifteen years of detective work had taught her never to carry the prize directly to the door; she drove instead to the storage unit, sat forty minutes with a thermocouple of coffee going cold, watched three sedans and a delivery truck pass, and saw nothing that moved like surveillance. Only then did she retrieve the ledger from box 114 — a green composition book, school-grade, its cover soft with handling, its pages filled in a careful clerk's hand — and drive it home by three different routes.

At the shop, she locked the front door, drew the blinds, and read it cover to cover twice.

Fifteen entries. Four deaths disguised as accidents. One death disguised as a death — Ivy Sandoval, cause actual: *alive*, the two words underlined twice in pencil, as if even while writing them Pellman had needed the reassurance. Three entries named G. Marsh as instructor. Two named no one, with a note in the margin: *instructed by phone, voice known.* And entry after entry after entry, the quiet machinery of a family that treated human lives as line items, each one initialed, dated, paid.

It was everything. It was the spine the whole case had lacked — Julian's confession gave them motive and murder, Ivy's testimony gave them the hearing and the fraud, but the ledger tied the coroner's office, the shipping firm, and Edmund Renner into a single documented chain. With this, the confessor's evidence, and a living witness, there was no version of events in which Edmund Renner walked away.

Bram sat in the dark among her unsold books and felt something she hadn't felt since retirement: not triumph, exactly. Something grimmer and more durable. Vindication with a body count. The truth didn't set anyone free — it just finally put the weight on the right shoulders.

She made copies. Three of them, on the shop's ancient photocopier, feeding pages one at a time with the machine groaning like a dying animal. One copy went into a padded mailer addressed to Delia Sandoval, to hold in trust for Ivy. One went into a deposit box at a bank across town, under a name Bram hadn't used since her divorce. The original stayed with her, wrapped in a plastic bag inside a false-bottomed tin that had once held shortbread and now held the accumulated paper weight of fifteen years of murder.

She finished at nearly midnight. She should have slept in the back room — some instinct told her to — but the instinct arrived dressed as paranoia, and she'd spent enough of her life obeying paranoia. Instead she set the shop alarm, left through the back, and walked three blocks to the apartment above the laundromat where she'd been staying since the window broke, telling herself she was being cautious rather than afraid.

She was awake at 4:17 a.m. when her phone lit with the alarm company's number.

* * *

By the time she arrived, Kestrel & Co. had two patrol cars outside it and yellow tape across the door, and the rain had started again, drumming on the awning like applause from a hostile crowd. The front door stood open. Inside, uniformed officers moved their flashlights across shelves of unsold books.

Sergeant Okafor met her at the tape — young, earnest, Marcus's protégé, though Bram didn't yet know how much that fact would matter. "Dr. Villalobos. Alarm tripped at 3:52. Back window again. Same as before."

"Anything taken?"

Okafor hesitated, and Bram watched the hesitation the way she used to watch witnesses — reading the shape of what wasn't said. "Nothing obvious. Books disturbed in the mystery section. Drawers opened. Whoever it was spent time here."

"Time," Bram repeated. "They weren't burglars. They were searching."

"We'll need a statement." Okafor's flashlight beam caught the tin on the counter — the shortbread tin, pried open, its false bottom lifted and lying empty beside it. Bram's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

"That's mine," she said, keeping her voice flat. "Personal papers."

"Any idea what papers?"

"Old case files. Copies. Nothing that matters." The lie came easily and tasted like ash. The original ledger had been in that tin. She'd wrapped it in plastic, tucked it beneath the false bottom, and walked away — and somewhere between midnight and 3:52 a.m., men who photographed notaries' offices and broke windows and followed women home from libraries had peeled her shop open like fruit and found it.

Except.

Except the copies existed. Except Delia's mailer was already in a mailbox across town. Except the bank box under a dead woman's name held a second set. Except — and here Bram felt the grim arithmetic click into place, cold and satisfying as a lock turning — except they had stolen a prize whose power depended entirely on no one knowing copies existed. Let them deliver it to Edmund Renner. Let him believe the ledger contained, safely, in his own hands. Let him relax. Let him make calls. Let him feel, for the first time in fifteen years, that the walls had stopped closing.

The walls were closing. They just weren't closing on her.

"Dr. Villalobos?" Okafor was watching her closely. "Is there anything else you'd like to report?"

Bram looked past him into the shop — her shop, her refuge, her last clean thing, now violated twice and smelling of rain and flashlight batteries and other people's gloves. Somewhere in the dark stacks, a loose page turned in a draft from the broken window, whispering like a stenographer's keys.

"No, Sergeant," she said. "Nothing else."

But walking back to the car, she pulled out her phone and typed a message to the burner number the confessor had given her, thumbs steady, heart anything but:

*They have the ledger. Or they think they do. Which means your family thinks they've won. How long do we have before they stop being careful?*

The reply came in ninety seconds, and it was shorter than anything he'd ever sent her:

*Until Friday. That's when Edmund burns the boathouse.*

Bram stood in the rain outside her broken shop and read the sentence twice. Friday was three days away. And whatever else was hidden in that boathouse — whatever Julian Renner had told her to expect — it would not survive the weekend.

Neither, she suspected, would Ivy Sandoval.
