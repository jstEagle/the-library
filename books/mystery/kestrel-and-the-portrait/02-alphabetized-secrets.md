## 2. Alphabetized Secrets

The archive lived in the back half of Vesper's studio, a converted feed store on Corliss Street that smelled of dust and old tape and, faintly, of the hay that had been stored there before either of them was born. Five years of the Kestrel case, alphabetized with a rigor that Marisol Vega once called pathological and Vesper called professional. Every clipping, every transcript, every interview logged by date and cross-referenced by name. If the Kestrel file had a spine, it ran through these shelves.

Vesper hadn't slept. The image of those bricks — wet, orange, mortared in a pattern no coffin-maker would ever use — kept surfacing behind their eyes like something drowned refusing to stay down. They had made coffee at four in the morning and drunk it standing at the window, watching the courthouse clock across the square go from dark to gray to the flat pewter of an overcast dawn.

By six, they were pulling files.

The exhumation itself had been documented to death, figuratively speaking: photographs of the open grave, the coroner's preliminary statement, the town council's press release about "historical survey work." But permits were different. Permits were paper trails, and paper trails were where careful lies went to die. Vesper had learned that covering a missing-persons case in Harlan County — a woman named Dovie Ansel who had walked out of a diner in 2011 and into nothing — and it was the permit for the well survey, not the witness statements, that had cracked that story open.

The Kestrel exhumation permit was filed under E, between *Edwards, Hattie — property dispute* and *Election returns, 1938*. A single sheet, typed on a machine that had left its distinctive stagger on every lowercase e. Vesper laid it flat under the desk lamp and read it the way they'd taught themselves to read documents: first for content, then for texture, then for what wasn't there.

*Permit granted for the disinterment and examination of remains interred in the Kestrel family plot, Cedar Hollow Cemetery, pursuant to historical preservation ordinance 12-C.*

Signed: *R. T. Calloway, Town Clerk.*

Vesper sat very still.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere down the street a delivery truck dropped its gate with a bang, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, as if the whole town had been wrapped in cotton since yesterday morning.

R. T. Calloway. Vesper knew that name the way you know a name on a street sign — without ever thinking about it. It was embossed on the brass plate outside the clerk's office on the second floor of the courthouse. It appeared on decades of municipal documents, a tidy signature with a looped R and a decisive cross-stroke through the T.

It had also appeared, three years ago, in episode fourteen of *The Empty Grave*, the podcast's early run, when Vesper had done a background pass on everyone whose name touched the Kestrel file. Raymond Theodore Calloway, town clerk of Cedar Hollow for thirty-one years, keeper of its records, signer of its papers.

Dead eleven years. Buried two rows over from Edmund Kestrel himself, in fact, which Vesper had always found a small, neat irony.

The signature on the permit was dated eight months ago.

Vesper pulled the reference copy of episode fourteen's research notes, and there it was in their own handwriting, dated the year of the recording: *Calloway, R.T. — obituary, Gazette, p.2. Heart. Services at First Methodist.* And beneath it, because five years ago Vesper Joubert had been thorough even when the detail seemed useless: a photocopy of Calloway's signature lifted from a 1994 zoning approval. The same looped R. The same cross-stroke.

The same hand. Or a very good imitation of one.

Someone had signed a legal document with a dead man's name, and someone at the county recorder's office had accepted it, and a grave had been opened under authority that did not exist. This was no longer a historical curiosity. This was forgery. This was a crime wearing the costume of civic housekeeping.

Vesper reached for the phone to call Marisol, then stopped. Not yet. First they wanted to see the original — the copy in the recorder's vault, if there was one, and any accompanying paperwork. Chain of custody mattered. If Vesper was going to stand in front of a microphone and tell ten thousand listeners that the town of Cedar Hollow had dug up its founder on forged papers, they wanted to be holding the paper itself.

They photographed the permit front and back, slid it into a sleeve, and locked it in the firebox under the desk — the same firebox that had survived the studio break-in scare of two years ago, which had turned out to be raccoons. Vesper thought about raccoons for a moment and found, absurdly, that they envied them.

* * *

The county records annex occupied the old Carnegie library building, all limestone and tall windows, and it opened at nine. Vesper was third in line, behind a contractor pulling lien filings and a woman researching her grandmother's homestead. The clerk behind the counter, a young man named Dev with earbuds in and the permanent patience of someone underpaid, fetched the request form without being asked.

"Exhumation permit, Kestrel," Vesper said. "Filed last spring."

Dev tapped at his keyboard. Frowned. Tapped again.

"Huh."

"Huh is not my favorite word, Dev."

"It's showing as received but—" He turned the monitor slightly, an unconscious gesture of honesty. "There's no scan attached. Everything else from that batch is scanned. This one's just... a line item. Physical copy only." He shrugged. "Might be in the vault. Might be misfiled. You want me to put in a retrieval request? Those take ten business days."

"Ten days."

"Vault's backed up. Sorry."

Vesper leaned on the counter and smiled the smile they used on reluctant sources. "Dev. Who processes the vault requests?"

"Ms. Okafor, mostly. But she's out this week." He hesitated, and Vesper watched something flicker across his face — the particular discomfort of a person who has been told something and told not to say it. "Look, I'm not supposed to—"

"I know. You're not supposed to."

He lowered his voice anyway. "Mr. Aldous Kestrel came in last month. Asked about the same file. Ms. Okafor told him the same thing — ten days. He got kind of..." Dev searched for the word. "Firm. Said he'd handle it through channels. Whatever that means." He straightened up, voice returning to public-register. "So. Ten business days?"

"Make it twelve," Vesper said. "I want to give whoever's hiding it time to get comfortable."

* * *

Marisol found them at noon, or rather Vesper found Marisol already sitting in the studio's second chair when they came back with sandwiches, which was her way — she moved through Vesper's space like weather, arriving without announcement and rearranging things by her presence alone. She worked at the town archive three days a week and had, for twenty years, been the custodian of everything Cedar Hollow chose to remember. The other four days she was Vesper's best friend, fact-checker, and the only person alive allowed to touch the alphabetized shelves.

She was holding the permit sleeve when Vesper came through the door.

"You left the firebox key on the hook," she said, without looking up. "You always leave it on the hook. You have the security instincts of a goldfish." Her voice was light. Her face was not.

"Calloway," Vesper said. "Eleven years dead, signing permits eight months ago. Tell me that's not the strangest thing you've seen this week."

Marisol set the sleeve down on the desk with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off. "Where did you get this?"

"Copy from my own files. Original's in the county vault, unscanned, unfindable, and Aldous Kestrel's been asking after it." Vesper dropped into their chair. "Mari. Somebody forged a dead man's signature to dig up Edmund Kestrel, and now somebody's making sure nobody can look at the paperwork. That's not housekeeping. That's a cover-up of a cover-up. There's a story under the story."

"There's a story under every stone in this town," Marisol said. "That doesn't mean you should turn them all over. Some of them have things living underneath."

Vesper laughed, then stopped, because Marisol hadn't.

She was looking at the permit the way she looked at damaged documents — hands hovering, not quite touching, grief already in her posture. Twenty years among fragile paper teaches a person that some things break past repair.

"Mari?"

"You should drop it, Ves." Quiet. Final. The voice she used with researchers who wanted to handle the founding documents with bare hands. "The exhumation happened. It was strange. Bricks in a box — fine, write an episode about Victorian burial customs, people love that stuff. But the permit, the signature, chasing who ordered it—" She shook her head. "Let it be."

"Since when do you tell me to let anything be? You're the one who taught me how to read marginalia. You spent a whole weekend helping me decode the water rights ledgers."

"And I'd spend another weekend telling you to stop." She stood, smoothed her skirt, and Vesper saw that her hands weren't entirely steady. "Some records are closed for reasons, Vesper. Not good reasons. Not legal reasons. Reasons. There are families in this town — old families, founding families — who have been keeping certain accounts balanced for longer than either of us has been alive, and they do not consider it history. They consider it maintenance."

"That sounds like something you say when you know what's being maintained."

Marisol met their eyes, and for one unguarded second Vesper saw something there that frightened them more than any forged signature: not guilt exactly, and not fear for herself. Fear *for* someone. For Vesper, maybe. Or for whatever would happen to Vesper if they kept pulling this thread.

"Eat your sandwich," she said. "And Vesper — if you go to the courthouse, if you go asking about Calloway, don't use your name. Don't use mine. Please." She paused at the door, hand on the frame, and didn't turn around. "I've loved you twenty years. That's the only reason I'm saying any of this instead of nothing at all."

The door clicked shut behind her.

Vesper sat in the humming quiet of the archive, the sandwich untouched, the permit gleaming in its sleeve. Outside, the courthouse clock struck one, and across the square, in the second-floor office with the brass plate on the door, the town clerk's chair sat empty — had sat empty for eleven years, occupied by deputies and acting appointments, a position held open like a coat kept warm for someone expected to return.

Or, Vesper thought, like a signature kept sharp enough to forge.

They unlocked the firebox again, took out the permit, and held it to the light. The ink had browned unevenly, faster than eight months should allow — as if the document had been aged deliberately, tea-stained and baked dry, dressed up as an older paper. Someone had gone to trouble. Real, patient, expensive trouble.

Five years of filing, and the case had finally done what cases do when they're ready: it had stopped being about a man who vanished in 1924 and started being about everyone who stayed.

Vesper picked up the phone and dialed the number they'd sworn off calling until chapter three of the season — the number for the county recorder's retired deputy, a woman named Odile Prue who had processed every vital record in Cedar Hollow for forty years and owed Vester one favor from the Dovie Ansel case.

The line rang twice.

"Prue," said a voice like gravel in honey.

"Odile. It's Vesper Joubert. I need to know who walked the Kestrel exhumation permit into your office last spring — and I need to know before whoever signed R.T. Calloway's name decides I've heard enough."

A long pause. Then, softly, almost kindly:

"Oh, child. You're already asking the wrong question. The permit isn't the secret. The permit is the *receipt*."
