## 6. The Caretaker

The ledger's last living thread led up a road that hadn't been paved since the Eisenhower administration.

Vesper's car bottomed out twice on the washboard gravel before the trees opened onto the Kestrel estate, and even then it wasn't an opening so much as a thinning — oaks giving way to a meadow gone to seed, and beyond it, rising out of the morning fog like something dredged from a riverbed, the house itself. Three stories of gray clapboard. A widow's walk with a railing missing on one side, like a smile with teeth knocked out. The kind of house that had been dying so long that death had become its architecture.

Vesper sat in the idling car for a full minute, recorder in hand, thumb resting on the button they'd pressed a thousand times over five years of this podcast. *Kestrel and the Portrait.* Two hundred and six episodes cataloguing one man's vanishing. They knew his handwriting better than their own mother's. They could recite the coroner's inquest from 1924 backward. And now here was the end of every thread: a shell charity called the Meridian Fund, disbursing eleven hundred dollars a month for a hundred years to a single unnamed recipient, and the only address that had ever appeared in the disbursement files was this one.

*The Kestrel Estate. Groundskeeping and maintenance.*

Eleven hundred dollars a month did not buy what it used to. But it bought groceries. It bought lamp oil, once upon a time. It bought silence, reliably, for four generations.

Vesper killed the engine and got out.

The fog was burning off in rags. Up close the house smelled of wet wood and old paint, and the lawn around it had been mowed recently enough that the clippings still lay in windrows, browning in the sun. Someone kept this place. Someone with a mower and a schedule and no reason at all to expect visitors.

Vesper knocked on the front door anyway, because the front door was where you knocked, and waited long enough to count to sixty before circling toward the back, following the smell of woodsmoke.

That was when they saw him.

He was down on his knees in a vegetable garden behind the kitchen wing, tying up tomato vines with strips of cloth, moving with the slow deliberation of a man who had learned to budget his body the way other people budget money. Old. Not elderly-in-the-way-of-grandfathers old, but *old* old — ninety-something old, a face like paper left out in weather, white hair cut close as a convict's. He wore canvas work trousers and a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled past his forearms.

And on his left hand, catching the light as he worked, a scar ran from wrist to knuckle like a seam someone had tried to close and failed.

Vesper's mouth went dry. They knew that hand. They had spent three nights in the county archive with a magnifying glass over a photograph taken in 1919 — Edmund Kestrel shaking hands with a railroad man at the founding-day ceremony, and in the corner of the frame, half-cropped, the founder's left hand wrapped in a bandage, the injury documented nowhere, explained nowhere, painted over decades later by some restorer with more loyalty than curiosity. A scarred left hand. The detail Vesper had chased through chapter after chapter of the case file like a dog after a fox.

It was standing in a garden tying up tomatoes.

"Help you?" The old man didn't turn around. His voice was rust and gravel, but underneath it there was something else — a cadence, a music in the way he shaped the vowels, the particular rolling rhythm Vesper had listened to so many times in archival recordings that they could pick it out of a crowd blindfolded. Edmund Kestrel had been famous for his voice. Townsfolk said he could talk a flood out of its banks.

"I'm looking for the caretaker," Vesper managed.

"You found him." He rose, joints audibly protesting, and turned. His eyes were pale blue and utterly untroubled, which was somehow worse than fear would have been. "Though I'll tell you free of charge, there's not much left to take care of. House is going faster than I can patch her."

"Mr.—?"

"Hollis." No hesitation. "Ezra Hollis." The name came off him smooth as river stone, worn by use. "You're the podcast one." It wasn't a question. "Joubert. The one on the radio about Mr. Kestrel."

Word travels, Vesper thought. Even up roads that haven't been paved since Eisenhower. Or — a colder thought — word was *sent.*

"That's me," Vesper said. "I've been following some old financial records. The Meridian Fund."

Something moved behind the pale eyes. Not alarm. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man watching a tide come in that he'd been told about all his life.

"Long time since anybody asked about Meridian," he said. "You want coffee? I don't get much call to make two cups, but the pot's big enough."

* * *

The kitchen was a museum of one man's century. A wood stove, black and gleaming with use. A calendar from 1987 hanging beside a calendar from this year, both turned to the same month, as if time here were less a river than a room you rearranged. On the wall above the sink hung a framed photograph: founding day, 1919, men in boaters and women in white, and at the center of it, square-jawed and certain, Edmund Kestrel — with his left hand tucked discreetly into his coat pocket.

Hollis made coffee without asking how Vesper took theirs, and got it right. Black, no sugar. Vesper hadn't told anyone that in years.

"So," Hollis said, settling into a chair across the table with the groan of a ship taking swell. "Meridian Fund. You want to know where the money goes."

"I know where it goes," Vesper said. "Eleven hundred a month. This address. For a hundred years. What I want to know is why. Why would nine founding families pay a groundskeeper's wage to an unnamed man for a century? Wages stop when the worker dies, Mr. Hollis. These payments never stopped. Not once. Not through the Depression, not through two wars. Somebody's been paying them like clockwork since 1924, and somebody's been receiving them, and the paperwork says the recipient doesn't exist."

The old man drank his coffee. Outside, a crow argued with another crow about something neither would concede.

"You've done your homework," he said at last. "Five years of it, they tell me. Two hundred episodes." He set the cup down with a click precise as a clock part. "You know what my trouble is, Miss Joubert? Mister Joubert — forgive me, I don't keep up with how folks introduce themselves these days. My trouble is I'm an old man with a bad heart, and I've had a hundred years of practice at keeping quiet, and quiet gets heavy near the end. Gets heavy as a headstone."

Vesper's hand had drifted, without their quite deciding it, to the recorder in their jacket pocket. "What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you about hands." He raised his right hand, turned it in the light. Unmarked. Then the left, slow, letting the scar catch the window's gray glow. "Got this in 'nineteen. Split it to the bone on a winch cable down at the mill. You can read about it if you look hard enough — mill accident log, October 1919. Most folks never looked. One fellow did, couple years back. Podcast fella." The ghost of something crossed his face — amusement, or its fossil. "Had him repaint it, though. On the portrait. Pretty thing, that portrait. Lies real pretty."

The kitchen seemed to tilt. Vesper gripped the table's edge and heard their own pulse in their ears, loud as surf. Five years. Two hundred and six episodes. The coldest case in the county's history, the disappearance that had named streets and endowed libraries and hung in gilt frame above the courthouse bench —

— and it was sitting across the table drinking coffee, quoting nothing yet, but *about* to, Vesper could feel it, the way you feel weather change.

"Say your piece," Vesper said quietly. "Tell me why I shouldn't think what I'm thinking."

"And what are you thinking?"

"I'm thinking Ezra Hollis doesn't exist. I checked. There's an Ezra Hollis born in 1901, died of influenza in 1918, buried in potter's field. I'm thinking dead boys make good names for living men. I'm thinking the Meridian Fund has paid exactly one salary for a hundred years and never once had to change recipients, which means the recipient hasn't aged out of the job, which means—" Vesper's voice caught, steadied. "Which means either this is the cruelest joke ever played on a podcaster, or I am sitting in a kitchen with Edmund Kestrel."

For a long moment the old man said nothing. Then he stood, unhurried, and went to the dresser in the corner, and took from it a small tin box, and from the tin box a folded newspaper clipping gone brown as autumn. He laid it on the table between them, smoothing it flat with his scarred hand, gently, the way you'd touch a wound.

It was the *Courier*, June 12th, 1924. The headline: **FOUNDER MISSING; RIVER FEARED.** Below it, a photograph Vesper knew intimately — the last image ever taken of Edmund Kestrel, three days before he vanished.

"Recite it," the old man said. "Go on. You've read it enough times. Recite what I said at the labor hall, the week before. The speech everybody remembers."

Vesper stared at him. Their mouth was dry as ash. But the words were there, worn smooth by two hundred and six episodes, and they came out almost against their will:

"*We do not build towns on the backs of the men who break them. We build them on the strength of every hand that holds the hammer — and any man who tells you different is selling you the hammer and the town together.*"

The old man smiled. It transformed his ruined face into something younger, something from a photograph, and it was the most frightening thing Vesper had seen in five years of chasing this story.

"Close enough," Edmund Kestrel said. "I always ended it different, though. There was a line I cut. Every time. Cut it because it was true, and true things cost." He leaned forward, and the pale eyes held Vesper's like hooks hold fish. "The line was: *and God forgive the man who profits from the breaking.*"

He sat back. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs, in the dying house, a floorboard settled like a sigh.

"Now," said the caretaker, in the voice that had talked a town into being, "you're going to want to ask me about Daniel Reyes. About what happened in the mill office on the fourth of May, 1924. And I'm going to tell you — all of it, every word, on that little machine in your pocket. But not today." He raised the scarred hand before Vesper could speak. "Today I'm tired, and the truth deserves better than a tired voice. Come back at dusk on Sunday. Come alone. And Miss Joubert —"

He paused, and for just a moment the certainty cracked, and beneath it Vesper saw something ancient and terrified, a man who had been running for a hundred years and had finally run out of road.

"— come ready to decide what kind of person you are. Because what I give you Sunday, you won't be able to give back."

Vesper walked out through the garden on legs that didn't feel like their own, past the tomato vines and the windrows of cut grass, and only at the car did they let themselves look back.

The caretaker stood on the back step, watching them go, one hand raised — left hand, scarred hand — in something that was either a wave or a warning.

Three days until Sunday. Vesper started the engine with hands that shook, and drove down the mountain with the recorder warm against their ribs like a second heartbeat, knowing that whatever happened next, the case of Edmund Kestrel had just stopped being history.

It had become an appointment.
