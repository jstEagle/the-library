## 3. A Death Certificate with Clean Edges

The Municipal Records Office on Nyropsgade smelled of cold coffee and old paper, a smell Noor Nystrom had always found more honest than perfume. Bureaucracy had a scent, and it never lied the way people did. Paper yellowed at the rate paper yellowed; ink faded on schedule; filing cabinets stuck in the same places year after year. If you wanted to know whether a thing was true, you did not ask the people involved. You asked the paper.

She arrived at 9:04, four minutes after opening, because arriving too early made an impression and impressions were debts. The woman behind the counter — thick glasses, cardigan the color of weak tea — looked up with the particular weariness of someone who processed grief and property disputes in equal measure.

"Good morning. I need to pull a death record," Noor said. "Elias Brandt. Registered in Malmö, but I believe copies were filed here through the cross-border registry."

The woman's fingers moved across her keyboard with the patience of someone who had done this ten thousand times. "Relationship to the deceased?"

"Insurance." Noor slid a business card across the counter. It said *NORDLICKE CLAIMS SERVICES* in small, dignified type, and beneath that, a name that wasn't hers: *Ingrid Søholm*. The card stock was good quality. She'd had it printed three years ago and used it perhaps six times, always for moments exactly like this one, when a lie needed a body to stand in front of it.

"Mm." The woman typed. Clicked. Typed again. "Brandt, Elias. Drowned. Malmö harbor, inner basin. That's — eight months ago now. Case closed, no inquest required. Swedish jurisdiction handled everything; we just hold the mirror copy for the cross-border file." She peered over her glasses. "You'll need to view it here. No copies of death records leave the building without a court order or next-of-kin consent."

"Viewing is fine," Noor said pleasantly. "I have all day."

What she actually had was a month paid in advance by a lawyer whose hands shook when he handed over the cash, and a question that had been sitting in her chest since Anders Vinter walked out of the Hotel Meridian's lobby two days ago: *why does a man pay thirty thousand kroner to prove someone is dead?*

Or alive. That was the strange part. Vinter hadn't asked her to confirm the death. He'd asked her to prove Brandt was still living — as if the certificate everyone accepted were a door he couldn't get through, and he needed someone to find the key.

The terminal beside the counter hummed. The woman turned the monitor toward her. "There you are. Take your time."

Noor took her time.

The scanned document filled the screen: *CERTIFICATE OF DEATH*, Swedish registry format, the header crisp, the fields completed in a tidy electronic hand. Elias Henrik Brandt. Born 14 March 1961. Cause of death: drowning, accidental. Location: Malmö, inner harbor, near the Kronprinsessegade quay. Date: 11 May, eight months and nine days ago. Found by harbor patrol at 05:40. Identified by spouse, Marta Louise Brandt. Signed by the attending physician, by the harbor patrol officer, and — at the bottom, in the registrar's field — by one *Bengt Olof Rask*, district registrar, Malmö Norr.

Noor read it the way she read guests. Not the words first — the shape of them.

Every document has a posture. A genuine record slouches. It arrives late, or early; it contains a misspelling corrected by hand; a doctor writes *drowning* where the form asks for *asphyxia due to submersion*; a patrol officer notes the water temperature because he was cold that morning and it mattered to him. Genuine records are made by tired people at bad hours, and they carry the fingerprints of that tiredness.

This one stood at attention.

Every field complete. Every date aligned. The physician's report attached in full, its language polished, almost literary — *the deceased was recovered wearing his usual attire; no signs of struggle observed; blood alcohol negligible*. Negligible. Not zero — negligible, a word chosen rather than measured. The patrol report described the recovery in a sequence so logical it could have been dictated from a manual: call received, boat launched, body located, body recovered, body identified. No gaps. No corrections. No marginalia.

And the timestamps.

Noor leaned closer, tilting the screen against the overhead glare. The registry's electronic system stamped each step, and those stamps told their own quiet story. The physician's certification: filed 12 May, 10:22. The patrol report: filed 12 May, 10:24. Two minutes apart — plausible, if the physician and officer had sat down together at the same desk, which happened, though rarely. The identification by the spouse: recorded 12 May, 09:15. Fine. But the registrar's final signature — Bengt Olof Rask, the man who closed the case — was stamped 12 May, 08:03.

Before the reports he was signing off on.

Noor felt it then, low in her stomach, warm and unwelcome as a swallowed coal: the click. The sound a puzzle makes when its first piece drops into place. She hadn't heard it in nearly four years. She had built a life specifically designed never to hear it again — a life of luggage carts and restaurant reservations and remembering which guest took lemon in her tea — and here it was anyway, ticking under her ribs like something hatched.

She kept her face neutral. The records clerk had glanced over once; Noor gave her a small apologetic smile, the smile of a bored insurance adjuster, and returned to the screen.

The sequence was wrong. Small wrongness — the kind a coroner would wave past, the kind a grieving family would never check. But wrongness with clean edges. Someone had assembled this death the way a hotel assembles a room for a VIP inspection: everything correct, nothing lived-in. Real deaths are messy even on paper. This one had been made, not suffered.

She scrolled back to the top and began again, slower, hunting for the second mistake, because there was always a second mistake. Perfection was itself the tell; whoever cleaned a document always cleaned it unevenly, spending effort where they imagined scrutiny would fall and neglecting the margins.

The margins. She pulled up the registry metadata — public, blessedly, in the Scandinavian systems, a transparency law she silently thanked every time she used it. Filing history: document created, revised, revised, sealed. Three revisions before sealing. Unusual for a straightforward drowning, but not damning; registrars corrected typos too.

Then she checked the registrar himself.

Bengt Olof Rask. She searched the name out of habit more than suspicion — names were cheap, and most led nowhere. This one didn't. The first result was a notice in a municipal staff bulletin, dated eleven days after the death certificate was signed: *After twenty-six years of dedicated service, Registrar Bengt Olof Rask has retired, effective immediately. We thank him and wish him well.*

Effective immediately. Twenty-six years, and no farewell reception listed, no transition period, no successor named for another month. Registrars did not retire effective immediately. Registrars endured until their pensions ripened, then lingered through the handover like men reluctant to leave a warm kitchen. Effective immediately meant one of two things: illness, or payment.

Noor sat back. Around her, the records office went about its morning — a man arguing softly about a property boundary, a student asleep over a microfilm reader, the radiator ticking. Ordinary life, dense and unexamined. She thought: *eight months ago, a man drowned in Malmö harbor, and within seventy-two hours the paperwork was finished, sealed, and the man who sealed it vanished into retirement.* Either the most efficient bureaucracy in Nordic history, or a story that had been written before it happened.

She thought about Vinter's hands around the envelope of cash. She thought about the way he'd said the name — *Elias Brandt* — carefully, the way you set down something breakable.

*Prove he's alive,* Vinter had said. Not *prove the death was faked.* Alive. As if somewhere, in some specific place he couldn't reach, a dead man was breathing.

*

She spent the afternoon in the office's reading corner with the secondary files, and this was where her method earned its keep. Anyone could spot a forged signature. What Noor trusted was rhythm — the rhythm of institutions, which were creatures of habit far more rigid than any human being.

Malmö harbor deaths, she requested. Five years of them.

Drownings came in clusters, summer-heavy, alcohol-flavored. Fishermen, swimmers who misjudged currents, one shipyard worker crushed and slipped into the water — each file a small chaos of forms in different hands, different speeds, different qualities of attention. She read them not for facts but for texture, letting the ordinary ones teach her what ordinary looked like.

Brandt's file sat among them like a pressed flower in a bed of weeds.

Same day, same hour, same everything. And one detail more, which she almost missed: the harbor patrol log referenced a *recovery at buoy seven*, but the physician's report described the location as *adjacent to the Kronprinsessegade quay*. Buoy seven was four hundred meters out, in the shipping channel, where the water ran nine meters deep. The quay was shallow water, three meters at most, silted. A body doesn't drift four hundred meters against the current and settle somewhere shallower. One of the two documents was describing a different night entirely — or neither of them was describing anything at all, and both had been written at a desk with a map open and a deadline closing in.

Her heart was doing something she resented. Beating faster. Interested.

She closed the file image and sat very still, hands flat on the table, and made herself look at the thing underneath the thrill, because she had learned — expensively, years ago — that the thrill was not free. The last time she had followed this feeling, it had cost her [a name she did not let herself think], and she had promised herself, in the grey light of a hospital corridor in another country, that she was finished. Invisible women don't pull threads. Invisible women dust shelves and forget faces and grow old safely.

But.

But there was a widow somewhere in Malmö who had identified a body — or signed a paper saying she had — and a registrar who had taken money or fear into retirement, and a lawyer paying cash with shaking hands, and a dead man whom somebody very competent had killed on paper so thoroughly that no one had bothered to check the stitching.

Eight months, and no one had checked. Because why would they? Drownings happen. Papers close. The world is full of dead men who stay dead because checking is expensive and grief is tiring.

Except someone had checked. Vinter. And whatever Vinter knew had frightened him enough to hire a hotel concierge instead of going to the police — which meant either that the police were useless, or that they were part of it, or that Vinter himself could not afford to be seen asking.

Noor gathered her coat. At the counter, the tea-colored cardigan woman stamped her visitor slip without looking up.

"Find what you needed?" the woman asked, in the tone of someone making conversation with furniture.

"The record was very thorough," Noor said.

"Mm. Most of them are, these days. System does half the work." The woman paused, stamp hovering. "You know, that one came through odd. Brandt. We got the mirror copy flagged priority — same week it was issued. Usually takes a month, sometimes two, for the cross-border copies. That one arrived in four days with a rush notation." She shrugged. "Someone wanted it everywhere fast. Closed books, closed questions. Can't imagine why, for a drowning."

"No," Noor agreed mildly. "Can't imagine."

She walked out into the Copenhagen afternoon, into the wind coming off the lakes, and let the city swallow her — a woman of average height, average coat, average gait, one of four hundred thousand moving through the center at lunchtime. She was good at this. She had been good at it for years. And yet she caught herself, at the crossing by the bridge, checking the reflection in the pharmacy window behind her — old habit, older than the promise — and finding, for the first time in a long while, that she couldn't tell whether she was checking out of discipline or out of fear.

Because the record was clean. Too clean. And clean documents meant careful people, and careful people who forge a death certificate also notice when someone requests the file five years of harbor drownings from. The registry logged every query. She had used her alias, yes, but aliases were thin paper against real attention.

*Someone wanted it everywhere fast. Closed books, closed questions.*

She had just opened one.

That evening, back at the Meridian, Noor stood in the concierge alcove with the lobby flowing around her — a German family arguing cheerfully about dinner, a businessman rehearsing a presentation under his breath, the doorman shaking rain off his cap — and pulled up the ferry schedules between Copenhagen and Malmö on the terminal screen, telling herself it was idle curiosity, professional habit, nothing more.

Twenty-five minutes across the Sound. She could be at the inner harbor by morning.

Vinter had paid through the month. He had asked for a living man. And the only honest way forward — the way that didn't require her to trust a nervous lawyer's reasons, or her own fading promises — was to go and stand where Elias Brandt had supposedly died, and see what the water remembered.

She closed the schedule. Behind the desk, the night manager called her name — her real one, the only name that had survived — and the lobby lights glowed gold against the early dark, and Noor Nystrom felt the hunt open inside her like a lung taking its first breath in four years.

Terrifying. Warm. Awake.

Tomorrow, Malmö.
