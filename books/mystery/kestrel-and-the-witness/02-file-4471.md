## 2. File 4471

The envelope sat on my kitchen table all day like a guest who wouldn't leave.

I'd tried ignoring it. I made coffee. I washed dishes that were already clean. I reorganized the spice rack alphabetically, then by how much I actually used them, which is a system only a dispatcher would invent and only a lonely one would maintain. The envelope watched me do all of it with the patience of something that had already won.

Around four in the afternoon I gave up and drove to the records annex on Delancey Street, because there are two kinds of people in this world: people who can leave a locked box alone, and dispatchers.

The annex occupies the ground floor of a building that used to be a bank, back when banks were buildings instead of apps. The vault is still there. They keep the cold cases in it, which I've always found either very fitting or very funny, depending on how much sleep I've had. The clerk on duty was a young man named Toby who wore his lanyard like a medal and greeted me with the enthusiasm of someone who doesn't get many visitors.

"Voss. Night shift, right? Kestrel Cab?" He said it like he was confirming a rumor.

"Guilty."

"You're kind of famous down here. You've pulled more files than anybody." He squinted at my request slip. "4471? That's an old one. Give me a minute."

I heard him before I saw him — the wheeze of a drawer that hadn't been opened since the last administration, the shuffle of cardboard, a small sneeze from somewhere deep in the stacks as if the archive itself were allergic to being remembered. Then Toby came back carrying a box the color of weak tea, tied with string that had given up any pretense of holding anything together.

"Sign here," he said. "And hey — you find out what happened to that guy?"

"That guy" being whoever 4471 was about. I hadn't looked yet. That was the thing about cold case numbers: they're just numbers until they aren't.

"No idea," I said, which was true in the way that matters least.

I took the box to the reading table by the window, where the light comes in at an angle that makes everything look like evidence. I untied the string. I lifted the lid.

And I froze.

Not dramatically. No gasp, no dropped pen. Just the particular stillness of a body deciding not to move while the mind runs screaming through every room of the house looking for an exit.

The victim's name was Daniel Osei. Age thirty-four. Last seen June 19th, seven years ago — two days after the solstice — near the intersection of Halloway and Ninth. Employed as a night auditor at the Meridian Hotel. Reported missing by his sister. The file contained a photograph: a thin man with kind eyes and the careful smile of someone who has learned to be photographed against his will.

I knew that face.

Not from the news. Not from a wanted poster. From a phone call. Seven years ago, 11:52 p.m., a woman's voice, breathless, half a sentence about a man being put into the trunk of a car outside the Meridian, and then the line went soft the way lines do when someone decides mid-sentence that they've said too much. I'd taken the call. I'd logged it. And then —

And then the log had changed. Not by me. I want to be clear about that, to whoever's listening, to whatever jury of my own conscience is always in session: not by me. But I'd let it change. I'd seen the correction come through the next morning, the call reclassified as a prank, the address scrubbed, and I'd told myself the story we all tell ourselves in that building: *the system knows what it's doing.* Ruthie Halloway had put her hand on my shoulder and said, "Some calls don't want to be found, sweetheart. Let sleeping logs lie," and I'd laughed, because Ruthie could make anything sound like wisdom, and I'd gone home and slept fine.

Daniel Osei had not slept fine. Daniel Osei had, as far as anyone knew, stopped sleeping entirely.

His sister's statement was in the file, three pages, the handwriting getting worse as it went. She wrote that Danny never missed a shift. She wrote that he called her every Sunday at six. She wrote that the police told her adults were allowed to disappear, which is technically true and morally obscene, and she underlined it twice, hard enough to tear the paper.

There was no body. There was no arrest. There was a single follow-up interview, conducted eleven days after the disappearance, noting that a taxi had been flagged near the scene but that the company's records for that hour were, quote, "unavailable due to a logging error."

A logging error.

I sat in that chair for a long time. Outside, the light moved off the table and left everything in shadow, which felt appropriate. Somewhere behind me Toby sneezed again, faithfully, like a metronome.

Here's what I couldn't stop doing: arithmetic. Seven years minus the statute of limitations for the relevant charges equals almost nothing. The client's envelope hadn't mentioned a deadline, but deadlines don't need mentioning when they're standing behind you breathing on your neck. Whoever prepaid triple my rate knew exactly how much time was left on this clock. Which meant they knew about the misfiled call. Which meant they knew about me.

You can't prove a murder won't happen. That was the instruction, remember. Prove a negative. It's the kind of job a fool takes and a coward refuses, and I've spent my whole career trying to figure out which one I am.

I photocopied the entire file. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Then I signed the return slip, thanked Toby, who asked if I'd found out what happened to that guy, and said, "Working on it," which was the first completely honest thing I'd said all day.

* * *

Detective Amara Okafor had a rule about cold cases: you don't announce them. Announcing a reopening is like announcing a surprise party to the guest of honor. So when she pulled File 4471 from the vault on the fourth floor — a nicer vault than the annex, with better lighting and worse coffee — she did it quietly, on a Tuesday, under the cover of a request for something boring and unrelated.

Her partner, Reyes, watched her carry the box past his desk and raised an eyebrow.

"Please tell me that's the Henderson fraud thing."

"It's the Henderson fraud thing," she said.

"It's not the Henderson fraud thing."

"It is absolutely the Henderson fraud thing, and if you keep talking I'm going to assign you the Henderson fraud thing."

Reyes, wisely, returned to his sandwich.

Okafor had come to 4471 sideways, the way most truth arrives. A routine audit of unsolved cases ahead of statute expirations had flagged it — not for the disappearance itself, which was thin as tissue paper, but for an anomaly in the ancillary records. A taxi company's dispatch log for the relevant hour existed in summary but not in detail. Every other hour of that night was preserved in granular, obsessive, minute-by-minute fidelity. One hour, missing. It was like finding a diary where every page was accounted for except the Tuesday something happened.

She'd spent three weeks pulling the thread gently, afraid it would snap or, worse, unravel into nothing. But threads kept appearing. The responding officer had retired to Florida and remembered nothing, cheerfully. The hotel night manager had died. The sister had moved away and didn't return calls, and Okafor couldn't blame her; hope was expensive and the city charged interest.

But the dispatch log. The dispatch log bothered her the way a loose tooth bothers a tongue. Someone had cleaned it. And cleaning a log required access, and access required a person, and persons leave fingerprints even when they wear gloves — not the kind on your fingers, the kind in your choices. People tidy according to habit. They delete too thoroughly or not thoroughly enough. They miss things a machine wouldn't miss because machines don't know what matters.

She'd requested the full archive from Kestrel Cab Company. The request was sitting in someone's inbox, gathering the particular dust of institutional reluctance.

In the meantime, she read the file again. Daniel Osei's face looked up at her from the photograph, patient, uncomplaining. She'd worked enough of these to know the dead don't haunt anyone. It's the unfinished paperwork that haunts you. The case that was closed without being finished. The question that got filed instead of answered.

"Okafor," Reyes said around his sandwich. "You've read that page four times."

"I'm looking for what isn't on it."

"That's either great detective work or a cry for help."

"Both," she said, and turned the page.

What wasn't on it: any mention of the dispatcher of record for the original call. The name field was blank. Not redacted — blank, as if the system itself had shrugged. In twenty years of policing, Okafor had learned that blanks are louder than names. Names can lie. Blanks have to be created.

She wrote *WHO TOOK THE CALL?* on a sticky note and pressed it to the inside of the box lid, where it would wait for her tomorrow, faithful as a dog.

* * *

I got home after midnight, which for me is technically morning, and spread the photocopies across the kitchen table next to the envelope, so that the whole mystery could sit together like a family reunion nobody wanted to host.

The client's letter had referenced "the events of the solstice" and named Marcus Ellery. The file in front of me named Daniel Osei and a date two days after the solstice, seven years ago. Two different men, two different years, one shared window of longest days. Coincidence is a word professionals use when they haven't done the math yet, and I had done the math, and the math said: somebody thinks these things rhyme.

I made tea I didn't drink. I turned on the police scanner low, out of habit — the night sounds of the city, other people's emergencies, the static lullaby I've fallen asleep to for fifteen years. A unit responded to a noise complaint on Delancey. A ambulance rolled toward the river. The world continued its long argument with itself, and I sat in the middle of it with a dead man's photograph and a living man's name and the growing, sickening certainty that the two were connected by a road I had helped pave.

Because here's the part I hadn't written down anywhere, the part that lived only in the soft tissue of my memory: the voice on that misfiled call. The woman. Half a sentence, then silence. I'd listened to it maybe forty times in the week after, before the recording was purged in a routine cleanup that was anything but routine. And in those forty listenings, beneath the panic, I'd caught something small. A sound in the background. A radio squelch. The specific double-click of a cab company handset.

Whoever made that call was calling from inside a Kestrel car.

I'd told myself it meant nothing. Witnesses call from everywhere. But seven years later, sitting in my kitchen with the statute of limitations ticking down like a bomb with good manners, it meant everything. The killer — if there was a killer, if there had ever been a killer — had been wearing our uniform. Driving our streets. Logging our fares. Close enough to touch the archive.

The scanner crackled. A dispatcher's voice, calm and bored, assigning a unit to a fender-bender. I knew that cadence. I'd trained half the night shift in it myself.

And then, underneath the routine traffic, something else. A number, spoken once, quiet, almost lost in the static:

"Four-four-seven-one."

I sat up so fast the tea finally spilled.

The scanner hissed. The dispatcher droned on about the fender-bender, unbothered, unaware, or performing unawareness so well that I almost applauded. I replayed it in my head — I have a dispatcher's memory, it's the one organ I've maintained — and it was there, plain as a signature: four-four-seven-one, tucked into the dead air between calls like a note passed under a door.

Someone knew I'd pulled the file today. Someone was watching the archive. Or watching me.

I looked at the envelope, then at the photograph of Daniel Osei, whose kind eyes had waited seven years for someone to stop filing him and start finding him.

"All right," I told the empty kitchen, because talking to yourself is free therapy with no waiting list. "You have my attention."

Outside, the city went on pretending to sleep. Inside, I opened a fresh notebook, wrote **FILE 4471** at the top of the first page, and underneath it, because I am a professional and professionals start with the obvious:

*Question one: who else has keys to the archive?*

I had a bad feeling I already knew some of the names. And the worst feeling of all — the one that would keep me up until dawn, staring at the ceiling while the scanner murmured — was that one of them might be mine.
