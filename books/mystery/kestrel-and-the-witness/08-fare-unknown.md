## 8. Fare Unknown

There is a particular kind of silence that lives on a residential street at two in the morning, and Silas Voss knew it the way other people know their mother's voice. It was the silence of porch lights left on out of habit, of sprinklers ticking through their cycles, of a city that had finally stopped pretending it needed anything from anyone until sunrise.

Silas sat in their own car — not a company car, never a company car tonight — parked three houses down from 1141 Alder Court, across from a fire hydrant that gave them a plausible reason to be there if anyone asked. Nobody would ask. That was the thing about stakeouts: ninety percent of the job was being ignored by the world on purpose.

The thermos of coffee had gone lukewarm an hour ago. Silas drank it anyway, because lukewarm coffee at 2 a.m. was less a beverage than a discipline.

Marcus Ellery's house was dark except for one window on the second floor, where a lamp burned behind curtains the color of old tea. A retired coroner who kept a light on all night. Either he was afraid, or he was reading, or — and this was the possibility Silas kept turning over like a stone in a pocket — he was waiting for something he already understood better than anyone hired to protect him.

*Whoever hired you already knows how this ends.*

Silas said it aloud to the empty car, quietly, the way you'd test a floorboard before putting your weight on it.

"Then why hire me at all?"

The street did not answer. Streets rarely did. But somewhere down the block a dog sighed dramatically, and Silas chose to take that as agreement that the question was a good one.

They'd been on Alder Court for four nights now. The routine of Ellery's life had revealed itself the way routines always do — grudgingly, in small confessions. Trash cans out Tuesday nights, dragged back in Wednesday mornings by a man who moved like his knees were negotiating separate peace treaties. A grocery delivery every Thursday. A visitor on Saturday afternoons: a woman in her fifties with a canvas tote and the brisk walk of someone who cleaned houses and judged her clients silently but fairly. And every night, without fail, between 11:40 and midnight, Ellery turned off the downstairs lights and climbed the stairs, and the lamp upstairs came on, and stayed on until dawn.

Tonight was night five. Tonight, something finally happened.

At 12:17, headlights swung onto Alder Court from the north end — moving slow, slower than the street deserved. Silas's hand found the radio they'd brought, then stopped. Company channel. If the car belonged to the company, listening in might tip whoever was driving. Instead Silas picked up the binoculars they'd bought from a pawnshop whose owner had winked and said, "Birdwatching, huh?" with a tone that suggested he'd heard that lie from every private eye in the county since 1987.

The car was a sedan. Dark blue. Fleet number stenciled small on the rear quarter panel, because the company was too cheap for door magnets and too proud to leave the cars unmarked entirely.

Car 31.

Silas knew Car 31. Everyone on nights knew Car 31. It was the vehicle that spent more time in the shop than on the road, the one with the passenger seat that reclined whether you wanted it to or not, the one drivers fought over the way sailors fight over a lucky ship. It should have been logged out to the day shift, which ended at eleven. It should have been sleeping in the lot on Kessler Street behind a chain-link fence.

It was instead crawling past the home of a man who might or might not be murdered on the solstice, at a speed that said *I am not lost, I am looking.*

Silas started the engine and pulled out with the particular artlessness that good surveillance requires — no squeal, no hurry, just another tired person going nowhere at 12:19 in the morning.

* * *

Tailing a car through a sleeping city is a conversation conducted entirely in restraint. You want to close the gap; you don't. You want to run the light behind them; you catch it instead and watch their taillights shrink and tell yourself the city is a grid and grids forgive. Silas had learned tailing from Wren Halloway, back when Silas was nineteen and thought dispatchers just answered phones.

"You're not chasing them," Wren had said, tapping ash into an ashtray shaped like a cactus. "You're accompanying them. Politely. From a distance. Like a chaperone nobody invited."

Wren had also taught Silas how to read a driver's hands through a windshield at forty yards. "People lie with their mouths," she'd said. "Their hands are terrible at it."

The hands on Car 31's wheel were steady. Ten and two. Unhurried turns, signaled properly — which was almost funny, a ghost run through the dark obeying traffic law — and after twenty minutes of gentle wandering, the car did something interesting.

It went to the depot.

Not the main lot. The old depot on Cannery Row, the one the company had abandoned six years ago when the new facility opened, the building that still had FALCON & SONS TAXI painted on its brickwork in letters gone the color of bone. The gates were chained. The windows were boarded. The city had been threatening to condemn it so long that the threat itself had become part of the architecture.

Car 31 slowed in front of it. Stopped. Sat there for a full ninety seconds, engine running, wipers off despite the fine drizzle that had started somewhere around the bridge.

Then it pulled away, unhurried, and drove back toward the main lot like a dog returning to its kennel after sniffing a fence post.

Silas let it go. Following it into the lot would mean being seen, and being seen meant the whole exercise collapsed into nothing. Instead they wrote down the time — 12:41 — and the plate, though they already knew the plate, and sat in the drizzle feeling the first cold thread of something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite anger but lived in the neighborhood of both.

Because here was the thing about Car 31's little pilgrimage: it wasn't authorized. Silas had spent three evenings cross-referencing the dispatch logs against the garage manifests, and Car 31 had been signed in at 10:52 p.m. Signed in. Parked. Locked. And yet here it was, at 12:17, doing a slow drive-by of Marcus Ellery's house and a lingering pause outside a dead depot, like a man visiting a grave.

Somebody had taken it out without logging it. Somebody who could open the lot gate without tripping the alarm, which meant somebody with supervisor access.

Silas sat with that thought for a long moment while the drizzle ticked on the windshield.

There were exactly four people with supervisor access to the night operation. Four keys, four codes, four names. And Silas trusted all four of them, which was suddenly the most uncomfortable fact in the entire city.

* * *

Back at the dispatch office at 4 a.m., the night shift humming its low electric hum, Silas pulled the GPS history for Car 31.

Or tried to.

The fleet tracking system greeted them with its usual ugly green interface, all monospace text and the aesthetic confidence of software designed in 2003 and defended ever since. Silas typed in the vehicle ID, set the date range, hit enter, and waited for the little map to bloom with the car's overnight wanderings.

The screen returned a single line:

**NO DATA AVAILABLE FOR REQUESTED PERIOD.**

"That's not right," Silas told the monitor.

They ran it again. Same answer. They widened the range — a week, a month. Car 31's history ended cleanly at 10:47 p.m. the previous evening, a tidy full stop, and resumed at 5:58 the next morning as if the car had spent the intervening hours meditating in a void.

GPS units didn't do that. GPS units were dumb, faithful things; they recorded everything, including the times drivers got lost, argued with their girlfriends over the phone, and stopped for gas station taquitos at 3 a.m. The archive of Car 31's sins was supposed to be complete and eternal. Deleting a stretch of it required going into the admin console, selecting the records, and purging them — a function Silas had never once used in nine years, because the logs were sacred. Wren had drilled that in early. *The log is the truth, Voss. Everything else is gossip.*

Someone had made the log lie.

And the admin console required supervisor credentials.

Silas leaned back in the chair until it complained, and looked up at the ceiling tiles, where a water stain from some ancient leak had dried into a shape everyone agreed resembled Ohio. Four people. Four names. Silas laid them out mentally like cards on a table.

Dean Kessler, day-shift supervisor. Gruff, rule-bound, the kind of man who alphabetized his complaints. He'd once written Silas up for drinking coffee at the console — *open container near equipment* — and Silas had loved him a little for the purity of it. Could Kessler wipe a log? Technically. Would he? Only if the alternative involved disorder, and wiping logs was disorder wearing a trench coat.

Priya Nandakumar, operations manager. Sharp, funny, perpetually mid-sip of tea, the only person in the building who remembered everyone's birthday and used the knowledge strategically. Priya had access to everything, but Priya also had a family and a visible horror of anything resembling trouble.

Tomás Reyes, night supervisor, Silas's direct boss, a man whose entire personality was a shrug rendered in human form. Tomás forgot things constantly — meetings, birthdays, his own lunch — but he had never once forgotten a log entry, because the logs were the one thing he treated like scripture.

And Wren Halloway. Night manager. Trainer of dispatchers. Keeper of the sacred log. The woman who had taught Silas that the record was the truth, who had held the office together through three ownership changes and one genuinely biblical flood, and who had, seven years ago, stood beside Silas while a call came in that they had both — 

Silas stopped that thought and put it back in its box. Ruthie's warning was still ringing in their ears: *let the statute run out.* File 4471 sat in a drawer at home like a coal that refused to cool.

Four people. Silas trusted all of them. One of them had driven a taxi past a marked man's house at midnight and then reached into the system and erased the road behind them.

That was the shape of betrayal, Silas realized. It didn't arrive wearing a stranger's face. It arrived wearing the faces you'd memorized.

* * *

At 6:40, Priya came in early, which she did maybe twice a year and always announced loudly, and Silas watched her from the corner of their eye while pretending to reconcile fare sheets. She hung up her coat, greeted the morning dispatcher with genuine warmth, poured her tea, and settled in. Nothing about her movements said *woman who erased evidence at midnight.* But then, Silas reflected, nothing about anyone's movements ever did. Guilt was famously bad at choreography.

Tomás arrived at seven, yawning, carrying a paper bag of pan dulce he distributed with the generosity of a man buying absolution for oversleeping. He offered Silas a concha. Silas took it. Eating the pastry of a possible murderer felt disloyal to the pastry, so Silas decided not to decide anything yet.

Kessler wouldn't be in until noon. And Wren —

Wren worked nights, same as Silas, and had gone home at five, pausing at Silas's desk on the way out the way she always did.

"You look terrible," she'd said, with affection.

"I've been birdwatching."

"Mm." She'd studied Silas for a moment with those pale, patient eyes that seemed to file everything away in a cabinet only she could access. "Whatever it is you're chewing on, Voss — make sure you've got the whole bone before you bite down. Half-truths choke people."

She'd said it lightly. She'd said everything lightly. That was her gift. And Silas had smiled and said something noncommittal, and watched her walk out through the glass doors into the gray morning, and felt the first real crack run through the load-bearing wall of their professional life.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't stop running: whoever wiped the GPS data knew the system the way a surgeon knows anatomy. Not the user's knowledge — the architect's. They hadn't just deleted records; they'd done it cleanly, leaving no gap-flag, no audit trail, the digital equivalent of removing a brick and repointing the mortar so perfectly you'd never know the wall had been opened. Silas had only caught it because they'd been looking for it, and because they'd spent nine years learning this system's tics the way you learn a lover's breathing.

Only one person had that depth of intimacy with the fleet software. Only one person had helped install it, back when the company modernized, back when Silas was still answering phones under her supervision.

Silas pushed the thought down. Hard. It bobbed back up like a cork.

* * *

By eight, unable to sit still, Silas did what Silas always did with an unsolvable feeling: they made a list. On paper. In pen. The physical act of writing imposed order on the mind the way a funnel imposes order on spilled rice.

**Who took Car 31 out?**
- Not logged. Gate opened without alarm. Supervisor code.
- Driver unknown. Hands steady. Obeyed traffic laws. (Polite ghost.)
- Route: Ellery's house → old Cannery Row depot → home.
- Why the depot?

Silas stared at that last line. The old depot. Chained, boarded, condemned-in-waiting. Why would a stolen taxi pause there, in the rain, for ninety seconds, unless —

Unless it was checking on something. Or someone.

The pen stopped moving.

Silas thought about Marcus Ellery's staged calm, his rehearsed answers, his lamp burning behind tea-colored curtains. Thought about the anonymous envelope, triple rate, one instruction: *prove the murder will not happen.* Thought about Ruthie going pale at a file number, begging Silas to let the clock run out.

Everything kept pointing backward. Every road in this case, no matter where it started, curved around eventually toward the same dark intersection seven years ago — and toward the old depot, where a phone call had once come in from a payphone that hadn't worked in years.

Silas folded the list, pocketed it, and pulled up the maintenance schedule for Car 31. If someone had wiped the GPS remotely, there might still be a local copy on the unit itself — the onboard memory that synced to the server nightly. The unit was in the car, and the car was in the lot, and the lot, at this hour, was quiet.

Getting into the lot without a supervisor badge was impossible.

Getting in *with* one required asking one of four people for help, any of whom might be the person erasing the roads.

Silas closed the laptop, drained the last of the terrible coffee, and sat in the hum of the morning shift — phones starting to ring, the day crew shuffling in with their newspapers and their complaints — surrounded by colleagues, by friends, by the only family they'd ever had, and felt, for the first time in nine years, completely alone at the busiest desk in the building.

Then the internal line lit up. Extension 12. Wren's office.

Silas picked up.

"Voss," Wren said, her voice easy as Sunday. "Come see me when you get a minute. I want to talk to you about Car 31."

The line clicked dead before Silas could answer.

Silas sat very still, concha crumbs on the desk, heart performing a slow, deliberate drumroll, and understood that whatever happened next, the comfortable version of their life had just been signed out of the lot — no destination logged, no return time recorded.
