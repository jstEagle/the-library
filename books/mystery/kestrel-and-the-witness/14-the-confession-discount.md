## 14. The Confession Discount

Detective Amara Okafor's office smelled like burnt coffee and old paper, which Silas found immediately trustworthy. Nobody faked an office like that. People who were performing authority kept plants and motivational posters. Okafor had a space heater with a broken dial that made the room either arctic or equatorial, a corkboard in active rebellion against its pins, and a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST DETECTIVE — clearly a gift, clearly tolerated rather than cherished.

"Sit," she said.

Silas sat. The chair across from her desk was the kind designed to make visitors uncomfortable, all hard plastic and wrong angles. Okafor noticed them noticing it and, without comment, dragged over a second chair from the empty desk beside hers. A better chair. That small courtesy told Silas more than any opening statement could have: she wasn't treating this as an interrogation. Not yet.

"You requested an interview," Silas said. "Polite and lethal, I believe, was the phrase going through my head on the drive over."

"I've been called worse." She opened a folder — File 4471, Silas knew it by shape alone now, the way you know your own handwriting — and laid it flat between them like a card player showing a hand she hadn't decided how to play. "Seven years ago, a call came into Meridian Taxi dispatch at 11:42 p.m. on October ninth. Caller reported seeing someone carried out of a house on Delmore Street, unconscious or worse. The dispatcher of record logged it as a prank."

"I know," Silas said. "I was the dispatcher of record."

The words came out steadier than Silas felt. Seven years of carrying that call around — the way you carry a stone in your shoe so long you forget it's there until you take the shoe off — and here it was, finally out in the open air, ugly and undeniable.

Okafor didn't gloat. That was the first surprise. She just nodded, as if Silas had confirmed something she'd already written down twice.

"Why?" she asked. Not *why did you do it* — no accusation in it. Just *why*. The question underneath the question.

"Because I trusted the person who told me to log it that way." Silas held her gaze. "Because I was twenty-four and she'd trained me, and when Ruthie Kowalczyk says a call is a prank, you log it as a prank. That's not an excuse. It's just the truth, and I've had seven years to get comfortable telling it to myself at three in the morning."

"And now?"

"Now somebody's killing their way through everyone who touched that night. Or planning to. And the last name on the list is Marcus Ellery, and the statute runs out in nine days." Silas leaned forward. "So let's skip the part where we pretend we're not both already working this case, Detective, because I've read your pull requests on the dispatch logs. You're two steps behind me and one step ahead of yourself."

Something flickered at the corner of Okafor's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"Two steps behind," she repeated. "Bold claim from a civilian sitting in a murder squad."

"Night-shift dispatcher," Silas corrected. "Which means I know things about this city at 3 a.m. that you only ever see in reports. And I'm not a civilian in this case. I'm a suspect, a witness, a victim-in-waiting, and possibly bait. Depends which hour you ask me."

Okafor studied them for a long moment. Silas let her. There was a particular skill to being looked at by a detective — you didn't fill the silence, you furnished it. Let her find what she needed to find.

Finally she closed the folder.

"Here's my problem, Voss. Your misfiling is obstruction at minimum, accessory after the fact if I squint, and it poisons every statement you give me from here to the grand jury. Any defense attorney worth their retainer would take one look at your history with this file and turn your testimony into confetti." She tapped the folder once. "But I also can't argue with results. You found the GPS wipe before my techs did. You identified the rehearsal pattern in the cold cases before I did. And you got Dean Kessler to confess to an affair, which, while useless to me, was apparently very useful to his wife, so there's that."

"He cried," Silas said. "It was uncomfortable for everyone."

"So here's what I'm offering, and I'm offering it once." Okafor pulled a legal pad toward her and wrote as she talked, the pen moving in quick, decisive strokes. "Your misfiling stays sealed. Not forgiven — sealed. It goes in a drawer, and it stays in the drawer, on one condition: you deliver me the person behind these murders before the statute expires. Nine days. If the clock runs out, the drawer opens, and everything you buried comes up with it."

"That's not a bargain," Silas said. "That's a hostage situation with paperwork."

"Yes," Okafor agreed pleasantly. "Welcome to criminal justice. Do we have a deal?"

Silas thought about Ruthie, who had begged them to let the statute run out. Thought about Wren Halloway teaching them, years ago, that a log entry was a promise — *the record is the only witness that never sleeps, kid.* Thought about Marcus Ellery, rehearsed and unsurprised, saying *whoever hired you already knows how this ends.*

They thought about the version of themselves that had logged a scream as a prank because loyalty was easier than courage.

"Yeah," Silas said. "We have a deal. But I want one thing in writing."

"Lawyers love that."

"Not lawyers. Me." Silas took the legal pad, turned it over, and wrote in careful block letters: *S. VOSS DELIVERS THE KILLER IN FILE 4471 BY SOLSTICE. IN EXCHANGE, THE MISFILED CALL OF OCT 9 REMAINS SEALED.* They signed it, dated it, and slid it back. "You keep that. If anything happens to me, it surfaces, and the whole city learns what your department traded away. That's not blackmail, Detective. That's a dispatch log. It's the only kind of promise I know how to make."

For the first time, Okafor smiled fully. It changed her whole face — took ten years off and added ten pounds of trouble.

"You know what I did before homicide?" she said.

"Traffic?"

"Dispatch. County sheriff's office, four years." She folded Silas's note and put it in her wallet, behind her badge, which Silas chose to read as respect. "I lasted four years before the radio drove me insane. So when I say I know exactly how good you are at your job, Voss, understand that it is both a compliment and a threat assessment."

*

They spread the files across two desks pushed together, and for the next six hours the homicide squad's conference room became, briefly, the best-run dispatch center in the city.

It started awkwardly. Two loners don't share easily; they trade, like cats exchanging territory one square foot at a time. Okafor had the forensic picture — the staged scenes, the signature details, the lab work still trickling in. Silas had the operational picture — shift rotations, radio protocols, the unwritten grammar of who talks to whom at 3 a.m. Neither picture made sense alone. Together they started to look like a map.

"Walk me through it again," Okafor said, around hour two. "The killer stages a murder scene. Complete scene. Blood, struggle, the works. But nobody dies."

"Nobody dies *yet*," Silas said. "Look at the sequence. Fourteen months ago, a staged scene at the Delmore house — the original location. Eleven months ago, a staged scene at the county records annex where the original report would've been filed. Eight months ago, the retired beat cop who first responded. Each one escalates. Each one gets more accurate." Silas laid the photos side by side. "This isn't someone practicing murder. This is someone practicing *documentation*. Every scene is built the way a dispatcher would build a log entry. Sequence, timestamp, verification. They're making the record tell a story."

"What story?"

"The true one. The one that got misfiled." Silas sat back. "Whoever this is isn't trying to get away with murder, Okafor. They're trying to make the paper trail confess."

Okafor was quiet for a moment, looking at the photos with new eyes. "That's why the scenes are so clean. No forensics to find because the forensics aren't the point. The *narrative* is the point."

"And here's the part that kept me up last night — well, more than usual." Silas pulled out the dispatch logs, annotated in three colors of ink. "Every staged scene incorporates real dispatch procedure. Not movie procedure. Real procedure. The fake distress call fourteen months ago used our exact priority codes — including Priority Three, which half our own drivers get wrong. The timestamps on the staged scenes follow our logging conventions down to the rounding rules. Whoever built these has either worked a dispatch console or studied one obsessively."

"Expert-level," Okafor said.

"Beyond expert. Institutional memory level. This is someone who knows not just how we do things but *why* we do them — the little redundancies, the backup procedures, the stuff that never makes the training manual." Silas met her eyes. "How many people does that describe?"

Okafor pulled a whiteboard marker and wrote on the glass wall, which Silas privately admired as a power move: *Current and former dispatch staff. Supervisors. Archive personnel. IT with system access. Police dispatch — cross-reference.*

"That's still forty people," she said.

"It was sixty yesterday. Kessler's out — he's a coward, but he's a coward with an alibi and an affair and no archive access. Ruthie's out; she's terrified, not calculating, and she hasn't touched a console in six years." Silas counted on their fingers. "That leaves the day supervisor, two archive clerks, the IT contractor, and—"

Silas stopped.

"And?" Okafor prompted.

"And Wren Halloway." The name came out carefully, each syllable set down like glassware. "Night manager. She trained me. She trained half the people on that list. She has master access to everything — logs, archives, GPS, the works. She's been running nights longer than anyone."

"Alibi?"

"For the night of the Delmore staging? She was on shift. Which means—" Silas heard the sentence finish itself in their head and hated it. "Which means she could stage a scene and log herself elsewhere in the same breath. Her alibi is her access."

Okafor wrote Wren Halloway's name on the glass, circled it once, and — Silas noted — did not underline it. Small mercy. Or professional patience.

"You don't want it to be her," Okafor observed. Not unkindly.

"She taught me that the record is sacred." Silas looked at the board. "If she's the one corrupting it, then I learned everything I know about honesty from a liar, and I'd like another week before I sit with that."

"You might not have a week." Okafor capped the marker. "Statute's nine days. Ellery's already been rehearsed once. So let's assume it's her until proven otherwise — quietly, professionally, without you burning anything down. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Silas said, and meant about sixty percent of it, which they suspected was as high as Okafor expected.

*

They worked through the evening, and somewhere around the time the squad room emptied and the vending machine became the only other living thing on the floor, something shifted. Silas couldn't name the exact moment. Maybe it was when Okafor, stuck on a gap in the timeline, asked for help instead of pretending she didn't need it. Maybe it was when Silas caught themselves explaining radio protocol with their hands, the way Wren used to, and Okafor followed every gesture because she'd done the job too.

Two loners, learning to share the load. It went against both their training. It worked anyway.

"County dispatch," Silas said, during a lull. "Four years. What broke you?"

Okafor laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she'd startled herself. "A domestic call where the caller whispered for eleven minutes because he was hiding in a closet, and I stayed on the line with him the whole time, and we both lived, and I still quit three weeks later. Couldn't do it anymore. Every quiet night felt like the universe holding its breath." She shrugged. "Homicide is quieter, ironically. The screaming's already over."

"That's the most honest thing a detective's ever said to me."

"Don't spread it around. I have a reputation." She gathered her notes, then paused. "Voss. The misfiled call. When this is over — after — I'm going to want the full story. Not for the file. For me."

Silas nodded slowly. "When this is over, you'll get it. Every word. Even the parts that make me look bad."

"Especially those," Okafor said. "Those are usually the true ones."

*

Silas walked out into the parking lot at 1 a.m., tired in the specific way that comes from thinking hard alongside another person — a good tired, almost unfamiliar. The city hummed its night hum. Somewhere out there, Wren Halloway was either innocent or the most patient killer Silas had ever imagined, and either answer cost something.

Their phone buzzed. A text from the dispatch office's automated system — routine, the nightly archive backup confirmation.

Except the confirmation was wrong. The nightly backup ran at 2:15, not 1:04. Someone had triggered it manually, ninety minutes early, from a terminal Silas recognized by its station ID:

Console One. Wren's console.

Silas stood very still in the empty parking lot, watching the message glow, and understood that somewhere across town, the record was being edited again — and that whatever came next, they had nine days, one reluctant ally, and a promise signed in block letters to keep.

They got in the car and drove toward the depot.
