## 22. Open Channel

The city had learned to be quieter at night, or maybe Silas Voss had finally learned to hear it that way.

Six months after the solstice, the office above the old laundromat on Delmore Street — one room, one desk, one radio rig that hummed like a contented cat — belonged to Voss Dispatch Services. The sign in the window was hand-painted by a teenager from the building next door who charged forty dollars and threw in a free spelling correction: *DISPATCH*, not *DISPTACH*. Silas had framed the first draft anyway.

The business model was simple, which was good, because Silas's tolerance for complicated things had been permanently spent. Independent dispatch for independent drivers — the little cab companies, the medical couriers, the woman who ran a single tow truck and swore like a dockworker over channel four every Friday night. No corporate archive. No supervisor access tiers. No logs that could be quietly rewritten by someone with a keycard and a grudge. Every call went into a ledger Silas kept in their own handwriting, because some lessons you learn with your whole body.

The radio was quiet tonight. Not dead — never dead; Silas checked it the way other people checked their pulse — just quiet. A courier named Baptiste had signed off at eleven with his usual benediction ("Night, Voss. Try to have a boring one"), and since then the only traffic had been a drunk man on an open channel singing what he insisted was Patsy Cline. Silas let him finish. It seemed important to him.

That was the other part of the new business model, the part that didn't go on the invoice: Silas answered everything now. Every voice on the frequency got a voice back. It had taken a murder investigation, a mentor in handcuffs, and a career burned down to its studs to teach them that the worst thing a dispatcher can do is let a call go into the dark unanswered.

Silas was halfway through a cup of tea that had gone cold out of pure neglect when the envelope came.

It didn't come through the mail slot — the mail had come hours ago, all bills and one postcard from Amara Okafor showing a beach so aggressively sunny it had to be a vacation she'd been ordered to take. No, the envelope arrived the old way: a soft knock at the street door downstairs, three beats, and by the time Silas leaned over the stairwell rail, the hallway was empty except for the envelope lying square in the center of the mat, aligned with the door's edge.

Neat. Precise. Cornered like paperwork.

Silas stood there a moment, hand on the rail, and felt the old familiar thing wake up in their chest — the thing that used to feel like hunger and now felt more like curiosity with better manners. Six months ago, an envelope like this had cost them everything they thought they were. They'd learned what lived inside such envelopes. They'd learned what it cost to open them.

They went downstairs anyway.

"Of course I do," Silas said aloud, to nobody, unlocking the door. "I always do. That's my whole problem."

* * *

Upstairs, under the good lamp, Silas turned the envelope over twice before opening it. Prepaid postage, no return address, no name. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind of thing you bought at a stationer and not a drugstore. Whoever sent it had money or taste or both.

Inside: a single card, typewritten. Actual typewriter — Silas could tell by the slightly uneven impression of the letters, the way the *o* sat a hair lower than its neighbors. Someone who wanted the message to look like it came from another era. Or someone who actually owned a typewriter, which in Silas's experience was usually the same kind of person.

One line:

**I know something you don't.**

Silas read it three times. Then they laughed — a short, surprised sound, the kind of laugh that escapes before permission is granted.

"You're kidding me," they told the card.

Because here was the thing. Seven months ago, an anonymous client had paid triple to buy Silas's attention, and the job had nearly eaten them alive. They had lost the company that raised them. They had watched Wren Halloway — the woman who taught Silas how to hold a frequency steady, how to find a driver in a blackout, how to be calm when calm was the only useful thing left — confess to a string of staged murders and one intended one, with terrifying serenity, over the barrel of a storm-wrecked depot. They had testified against her. They had watched Ruthie walk into the precinct two days later and turn herself in for a seven-year-old misfiled call, and Silas had stood in the gallery and let her do it alone, because standing up and saying *it was partly mine too* would have been true, and truth was the whole point now, even when it cost.

And in the wreckage of all of it, Silas had found something unexpected: a life that fit. Small. Honest. Theirs.

So no, the line on the card did not frighten them the way it once would have. It delighted them. It was practically a love letter.

*I know something you don't.*

"Well," Silas said, pulling a pen from the mug Wren had given them years ago — they'd kept it; some things you keep because throwing them away would be a lie about what they meant — "let's fix that."

They opened a fresh file.

The folders were different now. Cream cardstock, same as the envelope, bought in bulk from the same stationer as a small act of aesthetic spite. Across the top, in block capitals: **FILE 0001**. Because whatever this was, it wasn't 4471. Nothing ever would be again. This was the first case of the second life.

Silas wrote the date. Wrote the time. Wrote, under *SUBJECT*, the only information available:

*Unknown correspondent. Claims superior knowledge. Method of delivery: hand-delivered, 12:40 a.m., Delmore St. Typewritten. Confident. Possibly smug.*

Then, after a pause, they added: *Possibly friendly.*

Because that was the other change, the one Okafor had noticed before Silas did. "You used to treat every unknown as a threat," she'd said last month, over diner coffee she pretended not to enjoy. "Now you treat them as a puzzle. That's either growth or brain damage."

"Growth," Silas had said.

"We'll see," she'd said, in the tone of a woman keeping a file open.

* * *

The phone rang at 1:15. Landline, the office number, listed nowhere but the window sign and the directory of the building. Silas picked up on the second ring.

"Voss Dispatch."

A pause on the line. Then a voice Silas knew, warm and dry as autumn: "You answer fast. You always did."

Silas closed their eyes briefly. "Ruthie."

"Am I calling too late?"

"It's a dispatch service. There's no too late. There's only late." Silas leaned back in the chair until it creaked. "How are you? How's — everything?"

Ruthie had gotten eighteen months, suspended, plus community service, for the misfiling — the judge had weighed the seven years of silence against the seven years of a career spent otherwise clean, and against the fact that she'd walked in on her own. She did her community hours at a crisis line across town, answering phones at night, which everyone involved agreed was either poetic or cruel, depending on the day.

"I'm fine. I'm — learning to be fine. That's a real skill, turns out. Nobody teaches it." A breath. "I heard a rumor today. From one of my crisis-line people, who heard it from a cop, who probably heard it from Okafor, because that woman cannot keep a secret she thinks you should have."

"What rumor?"

"That somebody's been asking around about you. About your old cases. Not the police. A private researcher, maybe. Or a journalist." Ruthie's voice did the thing it always did when she was worried — it got quieter and more precise, each word placed like a stone. "Someone pulled records from the courthouse. Old ones. And Silas — one of the case numbers they asked about was 4471."

The tea sat forgotten. The lamp hummed. Outside, a car passed on wet pavement, tires whispering.

"That file's public record now," Silas said carefully. "Wren's trial made sure of it. Half the city knows that number."

"Sure. But this person didn't ask about the trial. They asked about the *original* call. The one from seven years before. The one that was never supposed to exist." A pause. "The one only four people ever knew the number of. And one of us is in prison, and one of us turned herself in, and one of us is a detective who already knows everything."

Silas looked at the card on the desk. *I know something you don't.*

"And the fourth," Silas said slowly, "just left an envelope on my doorstep."

"You think it's connected?"

"I think nothing is ever not connected. That's the job." Silas picked up the pen. "Ruthie. Why are you really calling?"

A long exhale traveled the wire. When Ruthie spoke again, the precision had cracked, and underneath it was the woman who had once taught a nervous twenty-three-year-old how to talk a lost driver home through fog.

"Because I buried that call once to protect people, and it grew teeth. And because if someone is digging around the edges of it again, I want to be useful this time instead of careful." Her voice steadied. "So. What do you need?"

Silas smiled at the empty office, at the quiet radio, at the fresh file with almost nothing in it.

"Everything you remember," they said. "Start with the weather that night. Start at the beginning."

* * *

They talked for an hour. Ruthie remembered more than Silas expected — the rain, the garbled address, the driver whose name had been scrubbed from the company roster within a week. She remembered the sound of the caller's voice, young and terrified, and the fact that the call had come in on a line that shouldn't have existed, a test channel, a ghost frequency. "I told myself it was a prank," Ruthie said. "For seven years I told myself that. It's amazing what you can tell yourself when telling yourself something else means losing everything."

"Yeah," Silas said. "I know the genre."

After they hung up, Silas sat with the file open and wrote it all down in the tidy, unshowy hand Wren had once praised across a dispatch desk — *good logs, kid; a log should read like a confession someday* — and the irony of that landed soft and heavy, the way old ironies do when they've stopped cutting.

At 2:50, the radio crackled.

"Dispatch, this is Baptiste." His voice was tight in a way Silas had catalogued long ago: not danger-tight, embarrassed-tight. "You up?"

"Always. Go ahead, Baptiste."

"So. Hypothetically. If a courier were running seventeen minutes behind on the Kessler contract because he stopped to help a lady chase a parrot off a fire escape—"

"Hypothetically," Silas said, "dispatch would note that the delay was due to a rescue operation involving exotic wildlife, and that the courier performed admirably."

A pause. "...There's a note field for that?"

"There is now." Silas wrote it down, grinning. "Get home, Baptiste. Log entry: heroism, avian. Signed and sealed."

The radio clicked off, and the quiet came back — the good kind, the earned kind. Silas looked at the file. One page of notes, one card, one ghost frequency, one question mark where a person should be.

*I know something you don't.*

"Okay," Silas said to the card, capping the pen. "Your move."

But the card, being a card, said nothing, and Silas Voss — case-closer, night-keeper, belonging at last to themselves — leaned forward into the lamplight, opened a clean page, and started the work of finding out.
