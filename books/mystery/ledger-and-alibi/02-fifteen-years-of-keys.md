## 2. Fifteen Years of Keys

The morning after the exhumation, Marguerite Dravenmoor did what she always did when the world betrayed its own order: she polished the brass.

She worked the front desk of the Vane Grand Hotel with a cloth and a tin of polish, small circles, patient, until the counter gleamed like a frozen river. It was a ritual she had performed some five thousand four hundred mornings, and it had never once failed to make the day feel survivable. This morning it failed. The brass shone, and behind the shine she could still see the hole in the earth, the four corners of the grave, the foreman's face as he looked down into a coffin that held nothing but a folded suit and a pillow of dark air.

*Empty.*

She put the cloth away. She squared the guest ledger so its spine ran true with the desk's edge. She straightened the pen cup, the bell, the little brass sign that read **CONCIERGE** as though the word were a title of nobility rather than a job description. And then she unlocked the drawer beneath the register — the deep drawer, the one no guest had ever seen opened — and took out her own ledger.

It was bound in oxblood leather, older than the hotel's current carpeting, and its pages were written in her hand: small, upright, unhurried. Fifteen years of Bellhaven Hollow, catalogued. Every guest. Every grievance. Every whispered apology in the lobby at two in the morning, every cheque that arrived unsigned, every name that appeared in the register twice under two different spellings. She had built it the way a mason builds a wall — one stone at a time, without ceremony, until one day you looked up and there it was, holding something up.

She turned to the first page and read the first entry, though she did not need to. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat.

*October 3rd. Arrived by the afternoon coach. Room 12. Paid in advance. No luggage beyond a carpetbag and a violin case he would not let out of his sight.*

Tomas.

Her brother had been in Bellhaven Hollow for eleven days before he vanished, and she had been in it for none. She had come looking for him the way people come looking for anything in a small town: with hope in one pocket and a photograph in the other, and she had found the town instead. Or the town had found her. She had never quite decided which.

* * *

The coach had let her off at the bottom of Vane Street on a grey afternoon in late October, fifteen years ago, and the first thing she had seen was the statue.

Aldous Vane stood in bronze at the centre of the square, one hand extended as though in perpetual welcome, the other holding a book — the founding charter, the plaques said, though the book was blank, which Marguerite had thought at the time was either an oversight or a joke. She learned quickly that it was neither. It was doctrine. Aldous Vane had founded the town on timber money and a promise, had built the mill and the school and the church and, last of all, the hotel that bore his name, and when he died — in the autumn of 2009, full of years and honours — the town had buried him with the book of his life closed, as it were, and opened a legend in its place.

*He gave us everything,* the woman at the coach office said, when Marguerite showed her the photograph. *Aldous Vane was a saint to this valley.*

*Have you seen this man?* Marguerite asked. *My brother. He wrote me he'd taken a post here. A porter's post, or something of the kind.*

The woman looked at the photograph a long moment — Tomas, twenty-six, dark-haired, with the crooked smile that had gotten him out of every trouble he'd ever fallen into and into several he hadn't. Then she looked at Marguerite, and something in her face closed like a shop shutter at the end of a bad day.

*Can't say I have, dear.*

Marguerite would hear that sentence, in one variation or another, forty-one times over the following three weeks. She counted. Counting was how she kept the grief in its box; as long as she was counting, she was working, and as long as she was working, she was not lying awake in the hotel's cheapest room imagining her brother at the bottom of the reservoir, or on a train to anywhere, or dead in a ditch with his violin case missing and his pockets turned out.

It was on the forty-second asking that the answer changed.

She had asked the concierge.

His name was Ambrose Fell, and he was seventy if he was a day, a tall dry stick of a man with white hair combed like a court witness and hands that smelled of ink and lemon polish. He stood behind the great desk of the Vane Grand the way a lighthouse stands behind its lamp, and when she came to him — damp, exhausted, out of towns to try and nearly out of money — he listened to her whole story without interrupting, which no one in Bellhaven Hollow had yet managed to do.

Then he said: *You count things. I noticed. The coaches. The times you've been refused. You'll want to sit down.*

She did not sit down.

*Your brother worked here,* Ambrose said. *Nights. Six weeks. He was good at it — better than good; he had a way with the late arrivals, the ones who come in weeping or drunk or both. Guests remembered him. But he left in September. No notice. No forwarding address. And Miss Dravenmoor — I am going to tell you something I have never told a living soul, and I am going to tell it to you because you count things, and a person who counts things can be trusted with them.*

*What?* Her voice came out very small.

*People leave this town,* Ambrose said, *more often than this town admits. And when they leave badly — without goodbyes, without luggage — the town prefers to have never noticed they were here at all. I keep the register. I keep everything. And in fifteen years behind this desk I have learned that the truth in Bellhaven Hollow is like the water table: everyone stands on it, and no one speaks of it.*

He hired her that same day. Not as a kindness — he made that clear, in his dry way. He was old, he said, and the hotel was a ship, and a ship needed two people who knew where everything was sunk.

*You'll learn the keys,* he said. *All of them. And you'll learn what each key is really for. The brass ones open doors. The others open people.*

* * *

She had learned the keys. God help her, she had learned them all.

In the present, at her gleaming desk, Marguerite turned the pages of her private ledger and let the years go by under her thumb like towns seen from a train window.

The first year had been nothing but grief wearing an apron. She had catalogued guests by day and searched for Tomas by night, walking the reservoir road, the mill ruins, the old timber camps, showing her photograph to anyone who would hold it. She had written to every police force within two hundred miles. She had hired, briefly and ruinously, a private inquiry agent from the city who had taken her savings and produced a report of eleven pages, nine of which were padding and two of which said, in essence, that men who vanish sometimes wish to be vanished.

In the margins of that report, in her own small upright hand, she had written: *Then why did he leave the violin?*

Because that was the thing. That was the splinter under everything. Tomas had loved that violin the way the devout love scripture — he had slept with the case by his bed since he was nine years old, when their mother had bought it secondhand off a man who claimed it had survived a fire. He would not have left it. He would not have left *her*. Whatever had happened in Bellhaven Hollow in September of that year, it had happened too fast for goodbyes.

Ambrose had found her, one night in her first winter, sitting in the dark of the archive room with the report open on her knees. He had not turned on the main light. He had lit the small green lamp on his desk instead, and set a cup of tea beside her without comment, and said:

*You will not find him by walking the roads. The town has already decided he was never here. You cannot argue with a decision like that. But you can document around it. You can become, Miss Dravenmoor, the memory this town refuses to be.*

*What do you mean?*

*I mean that a concierge sees everything and is seen as furniture. Furniture hears everything. I have spent thirty years writing down what this town says when it thinks no one is listening, and I have never once needed it — and I have never once thrown it away. When I die, it goes in the fire. But you — you have a reason to keep a record. You have a hole in the world shaped like a man, and you intend to stand at the edge of it and take notes until something falls in.*

It was the cruelest and kindest thing anyone had ever said to her, and both at once, and she had loved him for it from that night forward.

So she had built the archive. Not the hotel's official archive — the ledgers of record, the invoices, the fire-safety logs — but the other one, the true one, the oxblood book and the boxes behind it. She recorded the mill owner who checked in every year on the anniversary of a fire no one would explain, always alone, always in room 4, always leaving before breakfast. She recorded the county clerk who paid his bar tab in cash and once, very drunk, said the words *Aldous would have wanted it that way* about a matter that had nothing to do with Aldous, as far as she could tell, and everything to do with a parcel of riverside land. She recorded the year three separate guests asked, within a single week, whether the hotel had ever employed a night porter named Tomas, and she recorded that she had said no, and she recorded that she did not know why she had said no, and she recorded, in the smallest handwriting she owned, that she was afraid of her own answer.

Fifteen years. She learned the seasons of the town's silences the way farmers learn the seasons of rain. She learned that Bellhaven Hollow did not lie, exactly — lying required effort and imagination, and the town had neither to spare for its own history. It simply *agreed*. It agreed so thoroughly, so unanimously, so rehearsedly, that disagreement came to feel like bad manners. When the school taught local history, it taught the statue. When the church rang its bell on Founders' Day, it rang for the statue. And when Marguerite, in her early years, made the mistake of mentioning her missing brother to more than a handful of people, she watched the agreement close over the subject like water closing over a stone, and she stopped mentioning him, and she kept the photograph in the drawer with her ledger, and she polished the brass.

Grief, she had learned, could be worn as competence. It was the only way she had found to wear it at all.

* * *

There was one entry she had never shown to Ambrose, and one she had never shown to anyone.

It was dated the previous spring — a year ago now, nearly to the month — and it read:

*New night porter engaged. Name of Ivor. Quiet. Competent. Knows the building strangely well for a stranger — found the breaker panel in the dark, first week, no torch. Says he's worked hotels in the north. Hums when he thinks no one hears. Old tune. Don't know it. It gets into my head anyway.*

She had written that last line and then sat looking at it for a long time, because the truth was she *did* know the tune, and had known it since she was six years old, and had spent a week persuading herself that half the folk songs in the world shared four notes, and that a melody was not a face, and that hope was a thing she had sworn off the way other people swore off drink.

She had not looked at that entry since. She did not look at it now.

Instead she closed the oxblood ledger, locked the drawer, and made herself do the thing she always did when the ground shifted: she took inventory. The hotel was full — the land dispute had brought surveyors and solicitors and a journalist or two, all of them wanting the same three things from her: a key, a recommendation, and the town's official story, which she supplied in its standard edition. Aldous Vane, founder, benefactor, buried with honours in the churchyard on the hill. The trial of the gravedigger was set for the spring sessions; the whole town had already agreed on his guilt the way it agreed on everything, quickly and without appetite, like swallowing medicine.

Wren Okafor. She had trained him herself, two summers ago — a boy with careful hands and a habit of looking at the ground as if it might say something worth hearing. He had weeded the churchyard paths without being asked. He had once spent an entire afternoon re-setting a fallen headstone that wasn't even his responsibility, and when she'd asked him why, he'd said, *Because someone put it up, and someone should keep it up,* and she had thought: *this one counts things too.*

And now the town wanted to feed him to its agreement, and the grave he was accused of disturbing had been empty before his spade ever touched it, and Marguerite Dravenmoor — keeper of fifteen years of keys, heir to Ambrose Fell's green lamp, the memory this town refused to be — had sat behind her brass-polished desk and let them.

She looked at the drawer where the ledger slept. She looked at the register, open to today's page, where a journalist had signed in that morning with a room number and a byline and an appetite. And she thought of Wren in the cell up at the county building, and of what he had reportedly told the constable — that he'd opened the coffin and found it light, found it wrong, found it *empty* — and of how no one in Bellhaven Hollow had asked the obvious next question, because the town did not ask questions to which the agreement had already supplied answers.

But she was not the town.

Marguerite took out a fresh sheet of paper, and in her small upright hand she wrote a single line at the top of the page, the way she began every investigation she had ever run from behind this desk:

*What the register remembers.*

Then she went to the locked cabinet where the old guest registers were kept, bound by year in grey cloth, and she ran her finger back along the spines — 2015, 2012, 2011, 2010 — until it stopped on the one she had been circling for fifteen years without quite daring to open.

The register for 2009. The year Aldous Vane died. The year her brother disappeared.

She lifted it down. It was heavier than she remembered, or her hands were lighter. She carried it to the green lamp at the back of the desk — Ambrose's lamp, which she had never let them replace — and she opened the cover, and she began to turn the pages toward September, toward the eleven days when a man with a violin case had slept in room 12 and signed his name in this very book in handwriting she would know anywhere.

She found the month. She found the week. She found the page.

And the page was gone — razored out at the spine, so cleanly and so long ago that the cut had yellowed to the same ivory as the paper around it, like a wound that had learned to pass for skin.

Marguerite sat very still under the green lamp. Somewhere in the hotel, the grandfather clock struck the hour, and she did not hear which hour it struck.

Because she remembered that page. She had handled it herself, years ago, when she was still learning the keys — she could see it now, the names in their faded ink, the last of them a crooked, laughing signature that had made her hands shake so badly she'd had to set the book down.

Someone had cut her brother out of the record. Someone had done it carefully, and early, and from inside this hotel.

And there was only one person who could have taught her everything she knew, and only one person who had sworn the register would pass over his dead body.

She reached for the telephone to call the county building, and stopped, because the first thing she would have to say to them — the first thing anyone would ask — was: *And how, Miss Dravenmoor, do you know what was on a page that no longer exists?*

Her own archive, she understood at last, had been compromised from the very beginning. By her own mentor. Perhaps by her own hand.

Outside, the churchyard bell began to ring — not the Founders' Day peal, but the slow single toll they rang when the court sat, and it would ring, she knew, for Wren Okafor, while the true occupant of Aldous Vane's coffin slept on in whatever grave the town had agreed not to see.

Marguerite closed the mutilated register, and went to find the boy who had opened it.
