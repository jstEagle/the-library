## 15. The Fifth Letter

The call came at 6:47 in the morning, while Silas was still asleep in the rented room above the bait shop, and it was the coroner's voice on her voicemail that woke her — slurred, hurried, wrong.

"Miss Dravenmoor. It's Walt. Walt Ferrier. I need to — there are things I told myself I'd take to the grave, and I find I'm not willing to do that anymore. Not after your letters. Not after what you found. Can you meet me? The old boathouse, out past the marina, tonight at eight. Come alone. And Silas—" a pause, a wet breath, the sound of a man deciding, "—bring the ledger page. The one with my initials. I want to tell you what they bought."

Then he was gone, and she lay in the gray light listening to the message three more times, memorizing the tremor in it, and felt something cold settle into her chest that was not quite fear and not quite hope. A man about to confess. She had heard that voice before, in a hundred interviews — the voice of someone setting down a weight they'd carried so long their spine had grown around it. Walt Ferrier had carried his for twenty-six years.

She spent the day working. That was the discipline of it, the thing Marcus had taught her without meaning to: when the case moves, you move with it, but you don't run. She transcribed Hale's hospice interviews. She catalogued the ledger photographs, cross-referenced the trust disbursements against the county death records, built a timeline on the back of a paper grocery bag because the motel notepad had run out of pages. At four o'clock she drove past Ferrier's house on Cordage Street — a low brick bungalow with a wheelchair ramp and a garden gone to seed — and saw his car in the drive and a shape moving behind the kitchen curtain that might have been him. Alive. Preparing.

At seven she went back to the room to charge her recorder and change into something that didn't smell like archive dust.

The knock came at 7:12.

Not a knock, really — a soft, deliberate scrape, like something being drawn along the door. She crossed the room in four steps, put her eye to the peephole, and saw nothing but the corridor's dead bulb and its long shadow. When she opened the door, the hallway was empty. On the mat lay an envelope. Heavy cream stock. Her name in that same hand she now knew better than her own — the hand from the twin letters, the hand from the second letter naming Ferrier, each letter formed with a draftsman's patience, upright and unhurried, as if written by someone who had never once needed to hurry in their life.

Inside was a single sheet.

*You were asked to come alone. He will tell you what he was paid. He will not tell you who paid him, because he doesn't know. He only ever knew the voice on the telephone. Ask him about the portrait. Then go home, Miss Dravenmoor. Thirty days is a promise, but promises can be kept early.*

Silas stood in the empty corridor holding the page and understood two things at once, the way you understand a blow before you feel it: first, that whoever wrote this had been close enough to see her come and go; second, that the letter wanted her at the boathouse. It steered her. *Ask him about the portrait* — an instruction dressed as a gift. Every instinct she owned said trap, and every instinct she owned also said: he's real, the danger is real, and Ferrier is real, and if she stayed away and something happened to the old man, she would carry that the way she carried everything else.

She photographed the letter, sealed it in a baggie, and called the Hollowmere sheriff's office from a pay phone outside the laundromat — a pay phone, because her cell had developed a habit of dying near windows and she no longer trusted coincidence. She did not report a crime. There was no crime yet. She reported a welfare concern: an elderly man, a known drinker, alone, recently threatened. She gave them Ferrier's address and hung up before they asked for a name.

Then she drove toward the marina anyway, because she was her father's daughter and her brother's sister and she had never once in her life done the safe thing when the true thing was available.

* * *

She never made it to the boathouse.

The detour came as blue lights on Cordage Street — two cruisers angled across the road outside Ferrier's bungalow, an ambulance parked with its doors open and nobody hurrying, which is how you know, before anyone says anything, that hurrying has stopped mattering. Silas pulled over half a block down and got out and walked, because running would have looked like guilt and walking was all she could manage anyway.

A deputy she didn't know met her at the tape. "Ma'am, this is an active—"

"Is it Walt Ferrier?"

The deputy's face did the thing faces do. "Are you family?"

"I'm the one who called it in." She showed him nothing, offered nothing, and after a moment he stepped aside, because small towns process grief by letting people through.

They found him in the bathtub. That detail reached her in fragments over the next hour, standing on Ferrier's lawn while the techs worked and the neighbors gathered in the way neighbors gather, holding phones and porch light and the particular hunger of a town that had learned, twenty-six years ago, what happened to people who talked. He was in the tub, fully clothed, and there was a bottle of bourbon on the floor beside it, and a glass on the sink edge, and the medical examiner — a young woman, new, imported from the county seat, which meant she owed nothing to anyone in Hollowmere — kept saying the word *accident* in a tone that suggested she was trying it on for size and finding it loose in the shoulders.

"Slipped getting in," she said, when Silas edged close enough. "BAC was high. It happens. Older men, alcohol, hard surfaces." But her eyes went, once, involuntarily, to the man's wrists, and then away.

"Was there water in his lungs?" Silas asked.

The ME looked at her then, properly. "Who are you again?"

"A friend of the case."

"There was some," the woman said carefully. "There's usually some. It doesn't mean—" She stopped herself, and Silas watched her decide, right there on the lawn, in front of God and the gathering crowd, to be careful instead of honest. Twenty-six years of practice, Silas thought. This town teaches it fast.

But the young ME's hands were less disciplined than her mouth. Later, when they wheeled the gurney out, Silas saw the bruising on Ferrier's forearm — yellow-green, days old, finger-shaped — and the absence of the *cadaveric spasm* grip you'd expect if a drowning man had clawed at a tub edge. His fingernails were clean. His knuckles weren't marked. A man slipping into a tub full of water fights. Everyone fights. The body fights even when the mind has given up.

Walt Ferrier's body hadn't fought at all.

* * *

She sat in her car outside the bungalow until the streetlights buzzed on, and she let herself feel it — all of it, in order, because skipping steps was how mistakes got made.

Guilt first. It arrived whole and enormous, wearing her own handwriting: *she called him, she pushed him, she showed up in town asking questions with a microphone's worth of consequence attached, and an old man who had been surviving quietly for twenty-six years decided, because of her, to set the weight down.* He had been alive at 4 p.m. She had seen the shape behind the curtain. He had been alive at 6:47, drunk enough to slur but sober enough to choose, and somewhere between that voicemail and 8 o'clock, someone had helped him into a bathtub and held him under, or pressed a rag, or simply waited — waited being the elegant method, the method of a patient man — until the bourbon and the water did the work together.

Fury next, and the fury was worse, because fury wants a face and hers kept sliding off. Crane's face fit for a moment — Aldous Crane, seventy-one years old, silver-haired pillar, still on the board of the trust that had bought a town's silence one forgiven debt at a time. But the letters' hand wasn't an old man's hand. The letters were precise, unhurried, *draftsman's* hand. And the letter tonight had steered her — *ask him about the portrait, then go home* — which meant the writer had wanted Ferrier dead and wanted her to arrive just late enough to witness the aftermath. Choreography. She was not a target. She was an audience.

And then, last, the coldest thought, arriving the way the truth always arrives for her: quietly, and already certain.

The letter had promised five deaths. Four were named in the confession — the drowned girl, Lorna Pike; the two witnesses who recanted; the boy who crashed his truck on the way to testify. Four. And the fifth letter had named Ferrier. So when Ferrier died tonight, the count closed. Five for five. Except—

Except Edmund Hale had counted differently. Hale, dying in his hospice bed, giving her half a torn canvas and a story about Ada Voss, had said: *she counts too. Whoever keeps this ledger, they count her.* Five victims, plus Ada, plus Ferrier — the arithmetic refused to sit still, and Silas understood finally that the discrepancy wasn't confusion. It was the point. The sender was keeping score in a system only he recognized, and tonight's death was a payment on an account she couldn't yet read.

Four-and-a-half, she thought. He's counting Ada as half, or counting Ferrier as half, or counting himself — and the not-knowing was the cruelest instrument in the whole kit.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number. She let it ring twice, then answered without speaking.

"You went to the pay phone," the voice said. Male, middle-aged, sanded smooth, unplaceable. "Clever. It won't matter."

"Who is this?"

"You know who I am. You've known since the conservator called you about the face behind Ada Voss's shoulder." A pause, gentle as a hand on the shoulder itself. "You should know the old man suffered very little. Less than he deserved, honestly, given what he signed."

"If you think threatening me—"

"I don't threaten, Miss Dravenmoor. I schedule." The voice warmed, almost kindly. "Thirty days. We're at eleven. There's one name left on the list, and you're going to help me reach it, because you're the only person left in this county that name still trusts."

The line clicked dead.

Silas sat in the dark with the engine ticking and the porch lights of Cordage Street going out one by one around her, and she did the only thing left that felt like motion: she dialed Marcus.

It rang four times. Five. Six. Then his voicemail, his recorded voice warm and professional and utterly absent — *you've reached Detective Ravensworth, leave a message* — and she hung up without speaking, because there was nothing she could say into a machine that wasn't either a lie or a confession.

Eleven days. One name left. And the only person that name trusted was her brother, who had sold the truth once already, and who wasn't answering his phone.

She started the car and pointed it toward the county line, toward Marcus, toward whatever came next, and in the rearview mirror the coroner's house shrank to a single lit window — the kitchen, where the shape had moved behind the curtain that afternoon, now blazing empty, someone having forgotten to turn it off, and it seemed to Silas like the whole town's history in miniature: one bright room, long vacated, burning on for nobody.
