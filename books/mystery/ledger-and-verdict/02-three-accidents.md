## 2. Three Accidents

The studio was a closet that had learned to pretend.

Jude had built it himself, three winters ago, in the spare room of his rented house on Corliss Street: acoustic foam glued over the water stains, a secondhand broadcast arm clamped to a desk made from a hollow-core door laid across two filing cabinets. The microphone was good. Everything else was apology. But at two in the morning, with the town asleep and the river muttering somewhere below the bluff, the little room did what it was supposed to do — it held his voice the way a cupped hand holds water, and let him pour it out later into other people's ears.

He hadn't poured anything yet tonight. Tonight he was doing the other thing, the thing he never talked about on air, the thing that lived in the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet like something drowned.

He pulled the drawer open.

Three folders. He'd labeled them with a label maker he'd bought for exactly this purpose and used for nothing else since — clean white strips, block capitals, no flourish, as if plainness could keep them honest. *HARLAN VOSS. DELIA MARCHETTI. WENDELL PRYCE.* Three names in alphabetical order of when they'd died, which was also, not coincidentally, the order in which Jude had stopped believing in accidents.

He spread them across the desk under the lamp and sat down in the chair that creaked, and began, as he had begun a dozen times before, to read.

* * *

Harlan Voss first. Harlan was always first; Harlan had been first in everything, including out of this world.

Surveyor, fifty-one years in the county, a man who could walk a property line blindfolded by the feel of the ground under his boots. In June of 1968 — no, Jude corrected himself, tapping the photocopy, 1969, the newspaper had gotten it wrong and everyone had repeated the error for half a century — Harlan Voss went into the Ash River at the bend below the old tannery and did not come out. *Drowning, accidental.* The coroner's report ran four pages and said almost nothing: blood alcohol twice the legal limit, though Harlan's widow swore until her own death that he'd quit drinking years before, that he'd taken the pledge at her insistence after their daughter's wedding and kept it like a vow. No witnesses. His transit case was found on the bank, dry, closed, latched. Inside were his instruments and a notebook whose last twenty pages had been torn out.

Torn out. Not waterlogged, not lost. Torn.

Jude had found the rest of the notebook eleven years ago, or rather Harlan's granddaughter had found it, wedged inside a wall of the tannery office when she was helping gut the building for renovation, and she'd heard enough of Jude's podcast to know who to call. The surviving pages were survey notes — metes and bounds, chain measurements, the careful arithmetic of a man who trusted numbers more than people. And in the margin of the third page, in pencil gone soft with age, a single line:

*Verne parcel doesn't match grant. Going to ask E.V. about it Monday.*

E.V. Elias Verne. Founding father. The name on the monument, the school, the scholarship fund, the bronze plaque outside the historical society that tourists photographed every summer with their squinting faces turned up toward history.

Monday never came for Harlan Voss. He went into the river on a Saturday night, sober by his wife's testimony, drunk by the coroner's paper, and the notebook lost its ending the way all these stories lose their endings — neatly, deliberately, by hand.

Jude had spent two years on Harlan. Two years of records requests and cemetery walks and phone calls to people who remembered people. He'd never aired a word of it. That was the arrangement he'd made with himself early on: some cases he solved for the living, quietly, handing what he found to whoever still grieved, and kept his microphone out of it. He told himself it was mercy. Some nights he suspected it was cowardice wearing mercy's coat.

Either way, he'd gotten far enough to be sure. Harlan Voss had seen something in the numbers that threatened someone, and the river had closed over it, and Dr. Aldous Prewitt had signed the death certificate.

Jude turned to the last page of the folder. There it was, in faded violet ink, the signature he now knew better than his own: *Aldous Prewitt, M.D.*, with the little confident flourish on the double-t that looked, in the right light, less like handwriting than like a man signing his name to a finished thing.

* * *

Delia Marchetti next. Delia was the one that kept him up at night, even now, even after everything.

Tenant farmer, forty-four, working land she didn't own off the Kettle Road — and Jude paused on that, as he always paused on it, the way your tongue finds a chipped tooth. Kettle Road. He'd never thought anything of it before. A road was a road. But names were sediment; they settled in layers, and something in him had begun, lately, to sift.

March of 1974. A stove fire in the tenant house on the Hollis parcel, one of the old Verne holdings leased out for generations. Delia had been home alone; her husband and sons were at a church supper two miles away. By the time anyone raised an alarm there was nothing left of the house but the chimney standing in the dark like a headstone, and nothing left of Delia but what the fire left, which was identified by a wedding ring and a dental chart so incomplete it might have belonged to anyone.

*Death by misadventure, cause: accidental ignition.*

Jude had driven out to the site once, years ago, on a gray afternoon with sleet ticking against the windshield. The chimney was still there. Forty years of weather and it was still there, brick by stubborn brick, because chimneys are the last things to surrender. He'd stood in the field with his collar up and felt the wrongness of the place the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm — not evidence, exactly. Atmosphere.

What he'd found later was worse than atmosphere. It was arithmetic.

The insurance on the tenant house had lapsed six weeks before the fire. Lapsed — not canceled. The premium notice had been sent, per the insurance company's surviving records, to an address that hadn't existed since 1971. Someone had let that policy die on purpose, quietly, the way you'd close a window before breaking glass. And the lease itself — Jude had pulled the county copy — contained a clause, initialed by Delia's husband, giving the landlord the right to terminate tenancy on thirty days' notice if the tenant "disputed boundary determinations."

Boundary determinations. On a farm where Delia's husband had been fighting the landlord's surveyor for two seasons over a fence line that ate forty acres of their best bottomland. A surveyor. Jude had sat very still in the county records room when that word surfaced, feeling the cold move up through the chair.

And the fire marshal's original worksheet — not the polished report, the worksheet, the one with the investigator's handwritten notes — contained a line that had been struck through in red ink by a superior's pen: *accelerant pattern consistent w/ pour trail near rear door. Recommend further inquiry.*

Further inquiry was never made. The file closed. The family moved to Ohio. And the death certificate, when Jude finally tracked it through the state archive, carried the same violet ink, the same confident double-t.

*Aldous Prewitt, M.D.*

Prewitt had signed a drowning in 1969 and a burning in 1974. Five years apart. Two men dead at the edges of Verne land, both inconvenient, both filed away under words like *misadventure* with a doctor's tidy hand sealing the envelope.

Jude got up and stood at the window. The street was empty, blue under the streetlight, frost beginning its slow silver work on the lawns. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at something only dogs could see. He pressed his forehead briefly against the cold glass and thought about how a town is a story a place tells about itself, and how much of any story is load-bearing, and what happens to the whole structure when you pull out a beam nobody knew was holding anything up.

Then he sat back down, because there was one more folder, and folders like these don't wait well.

* * *

Wendell Pryce. The clerk. The youngest. The one that hurt.

Nineteen seventy-nine. Wendell was twenty-six, a junior clerk in the county recorder's office, engaged to be married in the spring, known around town as a boy who asked questions the way other boys chewed gum — constantly, cheerfully, without much awareness of how it annoyed people. He'd been digitizing — no, that word came later; he'd been *transcribing*, copying the old grant books into newer ledgers, a preservation project, his supervisor called it, though Jude could never find any record of who had ordered it or why.

On a Tuesday evening in October, Wendell stayed late at the courthouse alone. This was established, undisputed. At some point he went up to the attic storage level, where the oldest volumes were kept, and from there — somehow — he fell through the gap between the third-floor landing and the stairwell, a drop of perhaps eighteen feet onto stone.

*Accidental fall.* Prewitt signed it. Of course Prewitt signed it. By then the doctor must have been in his sixties, his hands maybe less steady, but the signature on Wendell's certificate was as confident as ever, the double-t flourishing like a flag planted on claimed ground.

Here was what bothered Jude, what had bothered him from the first week, what still bothered him at two in the morning five years later: the drop was eighteen feet, and Wendell Pryce had broken both wrists.

Both wrists. Jude was no pathologist, but he'd interviewed enough of them by then, off the record, trading favors and whiskey, to know what they'd told him. People falling backward throw their arms out. People falling forward try to catch themselves. You break a wrist falling, sometimes. Both wrists, together, cleanly, in a way the attending physician at the time had privately described to Jude as "consistent with bracing against something coming down" — that wasn't a fall. That was a man putting his hands up.

Against what? Against whom? The attic had been locked since. The old grant books Wendell had been transcribing had been "relocated" in the eighties during a renovation, and no one, including the current county archivist, could say relocated to where. His fiancée had married someone else and moved away and, when Jude finally reached her by phone, had said only, before hanging up: *"He told me he'd found something in the old books. He wouldn't say what. He said it was going to make some families very upset."*

Some families. Singular, probably. One family, wearing the plural like a disguise.

Jude closed the folder and laid his palm flat on it, the way you'd quiet a dog. Three folders. Three deaths, spaced across a decade, each one at the edge of Verne property or Verne paper, each one ruled accident, each one signed by the same doctor. He had solved them — solved was maybe too proud a word; he had *assembled* them, the way you assemble a skeleton from scattered bone, knowing the shape but never quite able to make it breathe. And he had kept them off the air, all three, because the shape they made when assembled was a shape no town wants to look at, least of all this one, whose entire economy of pride rested on the story of Elias Verne and the hundred and fifty years of benevolence that supposedly grew from his grave.

His grave. Jude's eyes drifted, without his permission, to the fourth item in the drawer.

It wasn't a folder. It was a single sheet, in a plastic sleeve, and he'd pulled it three days ago from the sesquicentennial preparation files — public documents, blandly organized, the kind of paperwork towns generate when they're getting ready to celebrate themselves. The exhumation permit for Elias Verne's coffin, authorized for Friday's ceremony, signed and countersigned by a small forest of officials.

And among the signatures, in the registrar's box where the original interment records had been verified against the historical society's copy, a certification of the death record itself — the 1874 certificate, re-examined, confirmed, stamped authentic.

Signed, in a copyist's careful reproduction of the original hand: *Aldous Prewitt, M.D., attending physician.*

1874. Prewitt would have been — Jude did the math again, hoping it would come out different, knowing it wouldn't — twenty-six years old. Fresh out of whatever passed for medical school in those days. Young enough to owe someone everything. Old enough to sign.

Four certificates. Four deaths. A drowning, a fire, a fall — and then, a hundred and fifty years back, the death that started all of it, the founding death, the one the whole town was preparing to dig up and honor. The same hand on all four. The same violet confidence. A doctor's signature recurring through a century and a half of Ashford's misfortunes like a watermark, visible only when you held the pages up to the light.

Jude sat in the creaking chair and felt the vindication arrive, quiet and warm, the way it always did — the click of the last piece, the pattern locking, the private electric joy of being right that had powered him through a thousand lonely nights of this work. He'd been right about Harlan. Right about Delia. Right about Wendell. And now the pattern didn't just repeat — it *reached*. It reached all the way down to the roots, to the founder himself, to the coffin that in two days' time was going to be lifted out of the consecrated ground in front of the whole town, cameras rolling, choir singing, the mayor in her ceremonial sash.

He should have felt triumphant. Instead, sitting there in the foam-padded dark with the lamp buzzing and the frost climbing the window, he felt the warmth curdle, slowly, into something colder and older than excitement.

Because here was the thing he couldn't stop turning over, the thing that had finally driven him out of bed and down to this closet at two in the morning: the exhumation order hadn't come from the mayor's office. He'd checked. It had come, officially, from the historical society — a routine request, the paperwork said, initiated by the society's board to coincide with the sesquicentennial. Somebody inside that building full of lovingly preserved paper had decided, deliberately, that Elias Verne should be dug up in front of everyone.

Why would anyone do that? Why would the keepers of the legend volunteer to open the box?

Unless they already knew what was in it. Or rather — unless they already knew what *wasn't*.

Jude looked at the four signatures lined up across his desk, a century and a half apart, identical in their assurance. A dead doctor who had spent his whole career, it seemed, certifying that Ashford's dead had died honestly. And somewhere upstream of all of it, someone had to have given the orders — someone whose name never appeared on any certificate, whose hands stayed clean of ink while other hands did the writing.

The microphone waited on its boom arm, patient as a confessional. Outside, the dog had stopped barking, and the silence that followed felt less like peace than like listening.

Jude reached over and switched off the lamp, and sat in the dark with his three solved murders and his one unbearable suspicion, and understood that tomorrow he would need to start asking questions in a building where all the answers were kept by someone whose job, whose family, whose entire life depended on the story staying exactly as it was.

The historical society opened at nine.
