## 7. The Third Letter Arrives Early

The pattern broke on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day the sea takes things back.

I had been in Marrow Bay eleven days by then, long enough to develop a routine I distrusted myself for enjoying: coffee at the chipped table by Marion Wren's window, the tide chart pinned above it like a horoscope, my notebook open to a page of timelines that refused to cooperate. Eleven days of asking questions and watching doors close with the particular courtesy of people who had practiced closing them for a decade.

The letter came at nine-forty in the morning, and it did not come to me.

I was still at the table when the postman's bicycle rattled up the lane, and I heard Marion at the door—her voice pleasant, her footsteps unhurried—and then a silence that had a shape to it. A silence like a held breath.

When she came back into the room, she was holding an envelope the way you hold something that might be warm-blooded.

"It's addressed to me," she said. "Not you."

I stood. My body knew before my mind did; ten years as an investigator teaches you that the body is the better archivist. "Let me see it."

She didn't hand it over. She turned it over once, twice, reading the back as if the address might have changed in transit. "It's my name," she said. "In full. Marion Ellery Wren." Her thumb moved across the ink. "Nobody uses my middle name. Tobias didn't even—" She stopped. Set the letter down on the table between us, face up, and stepped back from it as though it were a dish that had gone off.

The handwriting was the same. Of course it was the same. The same narrow, patient letters, the same upward tick at the end of every word, like each one was trying to leave the page. The same hand that had written *I killed him* and the same hand that had written *more will follow unless the verdict stands*. Two letters, one confession and one threat, arriving in my mailbox in London like bookends around a decade of silence.

And now a third, ahead of schedule, addressed to the widow.

"You said the next one would come to you," Marion said. "You said that was the pattern."

"I said the pattern was the point." I picked up the envelope without opening it. Paper stock identical to the first two. Postmark: Marrow Bay, dated yesterday. Which meant whoever wrote it had walked it to the post office themselves, or dropped it in the coastal box at Harrow Point, because the collection there runs late and the sorting stamp would show it. Small thing. Useful thing. The kind of detail I used to file away and never use, back when filing away was safer than knowing.

"Open it," Marion said. "Or don't. But open it."

I opened it. I am not proud of how long my hands took to do it.

The note inside was shorter than the others. Four lines. I read them twice, and the second time I read them aloud, because some things need air to be believed:

*The tide gives back what the town keeps. Tell her to stop asking about the ledger, or Thursday she walks the flats alone and doesn't walk back. You know how punctual I am.*

Thursday. Today was Tuesday. And the second death—the foreman, Mr. Calloway, drowned in three inches of bathtub water on dry land—had arrived exactly on the schedule the promise-letter had set, to the hour, as if the writer kept a diary and the diary kept them.

"He knows I've been asking," Marion said quietly. "About Tobias's ledger."

"Everyone knows you've been asking. You ask loudly."

"I ask *honestly*. There's a difference." She sat down across from me, and for the first time since I'd known her, Marion Wren looked her age—not old, exactly, but weathered, like wood that has been out in all seasons and stopped pretending otherwise. "Edmund. Why does someone who confesses to murder also threaten to commit more?"

That was the question, wasn't it. It had been the question for eleven days, turning over in me like a stone in surf. A confession says *I did it.* A threat says *stop digging.* They don't live in the same house. Unless—

Unless the confession wasn't a confession at all. Unless it was bait, and the threats were the trap, and the whole architecture—the letters, the deaths, the timing—was built not to hide a crime but to steer an investigation. To steer *me*.

Someone who knows how I think. Someone who knows I can't resist a pattern, and knows I'll trust a pattern right up until it breaks.

Someone teaching me a lesson.

"Marion," I said. "From now until Thursday, you're not walking anywhere alone. Not to the shop, not to the churchyard, not to the end of the lane. If I'm not with you, you lock the door and you don't answer it."

"And where will you be?"

"Being followed," I said, "if I'm any good at my job. Which I'm starting to suspect I'm not."

* * *

Wednesday I did something I hadn't done since I arrived: I went looking for the person watching me, instead of the person writing to me.

It's a different discipline. Finding a killer is paper work—dates, ledgers, inconsistencies, the arithmetic of lies. Finding a watcher is a matter of standing very still in a landscape and letting your eyes do what they were trained to do, which is notice what's wrong. Vane taught me that too. *The eye is a servant, Edmund. It only reports what you ask it to.* He said it on a stakeout in my second year, both of us drinking terrible coffee in a car outside a warehouse in Grimsby, and I had thought it was wisdom. Now it sounded like instruction. Like curriculum.

Marrow Bay sits in a shallow bowl of cliffs, and below it the tidal flats spread out at low water like a grey tongue, ribbed with channels, studded with the black fingers of old pilings from a pier that burned before I was born. From the cliff path you can see the whole of it. From the flats, the flats can see you back—there is no cover out there except the fog, and the fog comes when it pleases.

I walked the flats at half-tide, when the light goes flat and silver and shadows lie about their owners. I walked without appearing to look, and I let my eyes report.

There. On the headland north of the bay, where the gull colony nests in the scree—a figure. Too far to make out anything but posture. Standing. Not walking, not birdwatching, just standing, in the way a person stands when they are looking at something specific and waiting for it to move.

I did not turn my head. I altered course by degrees, the way you do, drifting toward the old pilings as if drawn by idle curiosity, counting my heartbeats, giving the figure time to decide whether I'd seen them. When I judged the moment right, I turned fully.

The headland was empty. Between one heartbeat and the next, gone—which meant they'd known the exact instant I'd check, which meant they'd been reading me, not just watching me. Which meant they knew my habits of attention. My tells.

Very few people alive know my tells. I could count them on one hand and have fingers left over.

I told myself it was coincidence. I told myself Marrow Bay is full of headlands and empty of entertainment, and anyone might stand on one. I told myself a great many reasonable things while the unreasonable thing settled into my chest like cold seawater into a boot.

Then the fog came in.

It came fast, the way it does here—no warning, just the world dissolving at its edges until the pilings were ghosts and the cliff path was a rumor and I was alone on a grey stage with the tide coming in behind me. I know the tide tables. I had checked them that morning, twice, because checking twice is what I do. I had ninety minutes of safe margin.

I started back anyway, and within fifty yards understood that I had made an error, because the channels on the flats don't run where the map says they run—they run where the storms of the last ten years have cut them, and the map in my memory was ten years old. The water found my left boot at the shin. Then the right. The fog ate my landmarks. Somewhere behind me the sea was filling the channels I couldn't see, patient as arithmetic.

I will record here, plainly, what happened, because I have spent my life recording things plainly and this seems like the place to practice.

I went down. The mud under the surface of a channel gave way—or was pulled away, I still don't know—and I went down into water the temperature of a verdict, and the mud held my leg the way mud holds everything it takes, gently and completely, and the tide came on.

I got the other leg free. I got my coat off. I did the sensible things, in order, because panic is just bad filing. But the channel was widening around me and the fog gave me nothing to navigate by, and for perhaps four minutes—I counted, it's what I do—I stood chest-deep in a rising sea on a drowned plain with no direction in any direction, and I understood, with the clarity that only cold water provides, that I was going to die on the flats of Marrow Bay ten years after helping bury what happened there, and that no one would ever find enough of me to investigate.

And then, through the fog, a sound. A voice. Calling my name.

Marion. Standing at the edge of the last sandbar with a lantern and a coil of rope, having ignored every instruction I gave her, having noticed I'd been gone too long, having walked out onto the flats alone—the exact thing the letter threatened—with nothing but stubbornness and a lamp.

"Take the rope," she shouted. "Don't think. Take it."

I took it. She hauled, swearing with a fluency that would have surprised the congregation of St. Bride's, and somewhere in the hauling the fog thinned, and we came up the shingle together, soaked and gasping, and collapsed against a boulder like two shipwrecks that had decided to keep each other company.

We sat there a long time. The tide filled the channel behind us, smooth and innocent, a crime scene erasing itself on schedule.

"That wasn't chance," Marion said finally. Her teeth were chattering. "The fog, the ground going from under you. That wasn't chance."

"No," I said. "It wasn't."

"Then who? Who knows these flats well enough to time it? Who knew you'd be out there?"

I looked at the headland. Empty. Looking at the empty headland, I felt something worse than fear, and it took me a moment to name it, because I had never felt it before: recognition. The intimacy of being watched by someone whose habits you know. The specific horror of realizing that the watcher on the cliff had known I'd check at that instant because they had *taught* me when to check. That the voice calling through fog, if it had been anyone's, would have known my name without being told.

"The person who wrote those letters," I said, "isn't threatening me, Marion. They're examining me. This was a test. Everything is a test." I wrapped my hands around my knees and watched the water take the flats. "And I think I know who's grading."

She turned to look at me. "Who?"

And I found that I couldn't say it. Not yet. Saying it would make it real, and real things demand action, and action would burn down the only bridge I have left to the life I had before—to the man who taught me everything, who fed me coffee in a cold car in Grimsby and told me invisibility was safety, who signed my certification and shook my hand and said, *you'll do, Edmund, you'll do nicely.*

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tomorrow I'll tell you. Tonight I need to be sure."

But I was already sure. That was the worst of it. Somewhere between the cold water and the empty headland, certainty had arrived ahead of schedule, like a third letter, addressed to a version of me that no longer existed.

Behind us, the sea closed over the place where I had almost died, and began, patiently, to erase the evidence.
