## 3. Anniversary Eve

The violets came first. They always had.

Twenty years ago, Ada Voss had worn a sprig of pressed violets pinned inside her coat lapel the day she gave her statement—Mirabel remembered it now with the terrible clarity of a thing long suppressed, the small purple smudge of them against grey wool, how Ada's fingers kept drifting to them as if to a talisman while Mirabel, young and ambitious and already half in love with her own rising career, fed her the words she was to say.

And now: a pressed violet in the dead genealogist's pocket. A violet tucked into the lining of Mirabel's own missing overcoat. A third one, this morning, wedged into the letter slot of Pemberton's Books, no note, no explanation, just the flower lying on the mat like a small purple accusation.

The past hadn't just found her address. It was knocking.

* * *

Mirabel closed the shop at noon, turned the sign, and stood in the dim aisle between biography and local history with the file spread across a stepladder. She'd spent the night with it—the old case, *R v. Halloran*, the anniversary of whose "resolution" the town would celebrate tomorrow evening with canapés and self-congratulation—and by daylight the pattern was undeniable.

The drowned man, Elias Crane, genealogist, had been hired by the anniversary committee itself to verify that Ada Voss was dead. A formality, Chairman Aldous Wren had called it in the correspondence Mirabel had photographed from the archive. A matter of closing the record cleanly before twenty years.

But Crane had been thorough. Thorough enough to find what Mirabel had never let herself find: that the death certificate for Ada Voss was a forgery, filed eleven days after the trial, signed by a doctor who had died himself two years later and could not be asked why his signature sat on paper dated after his last known day at the surgery.

Someone had made Ada Voss disappear. And someone had wanted very much for her to stay disappeared.

Crane's notes—copied onto the back of a canal-side parking receipt the police had overlooked or dismissed—listed three addresses in Ashcombe, a village forty minutes east. The last one was circled twice.

Mirabel looked at the circled address for a long time. Then she got her coat—the other coat, the brown one, the one nobody had taken—and drove east through rain that came down in grey ropes.

* * *

Ashcombe smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke. The house was a narrow cottage at the end of a lane, ivy strangled across its face, a blue bicycle leaning against the wall with a basket full of leeks. A hand-painted sign by the gate read *MRS. HOLLIS — ALTERATIONS & MENDING*.

Alterations. Mirabel almost laughed. Almost.

She knocked. Footsteps, slow, deliberate. The door opened on a chain, then fully, and there she was—older, silver-haired, face lined like a map of everywhere she'd been—but unmistakable. Ada Voss. Alive. Standing in a doorway in Ashcombe under another woman's name, looking at Mirabel Pemberton with eyes that held neither surprise nor warmth.

"You took your time," Ada said.

"Twenty years," Mirabel said. Her voice came out cracked. "Give or take."

Ada unhooked the chain and stepped back. "Come in before the lane sees you."

* * *

The cottage kitchen was warm and close, smelling of bread and lavender. Ada poured tea without asking, hands steady, and Mirabel sat with her own hands wrapped around the cup because otherwise they shook.

"The clothes," Mirabel said. "The coat. The scarf. My things, gone from my own wardrobe over months—I thought I was losing my mind. Then a drowned man wearing my coat."

"I sent them." Ada sat opposite, spine straight as a plumb line. "One piece at a time. I wanted to see how long it would take you to look. Truly look. Not at the clothes—at what they meant."

"They were messages."

"Every stitch." Ada's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "You taught me that, did you know? All those hours coaching me. 'Details make testimony credible, Ada. Specifics. What was she wearing? What did you press into your pocket?' You built me into a witness out of details. So I spoke back to you in details. Your grey coat, left where Crane would find it. Violets, because you never once asked me about them, though you stared at them for three hours while you rewrote my memory."

Mirabel set down the cup. It rattled against the saucer. "I told myself I believed you."

"No." Ada said it gently, which was worse. "You told yourself you believed me because believing was easier than asking why a frightened girl who couldn't swim knew the tide tables by heart. Because Wren was standing behind you with his hand on your shoulder, telling you what a fine career this would be. Because Tom Halloran had a wife and a temper and no friends in high places, and a conviction needed a witness, and you—" her voice caught, steadied— "you needed to be the detective who solved it. And I needed to be anywhere but in that room. So we made each other, Mirabel. You made a witness of me. I made a liar of us both."

"Tom Halloran is dying," Mirabel said quietly. "Did you know? Cancer. Eighteen years inside for something he didn't do, and now he won't live to see the outside."

Ada looked away, toward the window where the rain traced the glass. "I've sent him letters. Unsigned. He'll never know they were mine."

"He could know. Tomorrow. If the truth comes out."

"And if it comes out wrong—if it comes out as *the witness faked her death and framed an innocent man*—then I hang alone, and Wren walks to the head table tomorrow night and raises his glass to justice served." Ada turned back, and her eyes were fierce and wet. "Wren wrote my statement, Mirabel. You carried it; he wrote it. You must have wondered, all these years, how a dockworker's daughter produced language like *I observed the accused at approximately the hour stated, in proximity to the water's edge.*"

Mirabel had wondered. She had buried the wondering so deep it had taken a drowned stranger in her own coat to dig it up.

"He knows you're alive," Mirabel said. "That's why Crane died. He found you—or nearly—and Wren learned his hire had stopped being a formality."

Ada went very still. "Crane came here. Three weeks ago. Bought thread at the shop in the village, asked about the woman who did alterations. Kind eyes. He said he wasn't going to publish anything—he only wanted to know if she was safe." Her voice dropped. "Two days later he was in the canal."

"Then he's not the only one Wren will tidy away." Mirabel leaned forward. "Tomorrow night, the whole town gathers to toast the case. Wren will stand up and seal the record forever, and every loose thread—including you—gets cut. Unless the truth comes out first, at that table, in front of everyone, where he can't bury it."

"That's suicide."

"It's testimony." Mirabel heard her own voice harden into something she hadn't used in twenty years. "Properly given, this time. No coaching."

Ada studied her for a long moment. Outside, the rain eased; somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and went quiet. Then Ada rose, went to the dresser, and drew out a tin. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: a bundle of letters, a photograph, and a page in a handwriting Mirabel knew better than her own—her own younger hand, drafting Ada's statement, crossed out and rewritten in Aldous Wren's ink.

"I kept everything," Ada said. "In case anyone ever finally looked."

Mirabel reached for it—and froze. Through the window, at the mouth of the lane, a dark saloon car idled, wipers sweeping, engine running. It had been there when she arrived. She'd taken it for a farmer waiting out the weather.

It had been waiting for something else.

"Ada," she said softly. "Don't turn around. Is there a back way?"

Ada followed her gaze to the reflection in the window, and Mirabel watched twenty years of hiding collapse into a single moment of recognition. "That's Wren's driver," Ada whispered. "He's been past twice this month. I told myself it was coincidence."

The car door opened. A man stepped out into the rain and began walking up the lane—not hurrying, unhurried as a man with all the time in the world and a job to finish before morning.

Mirabel took Ada's hand. Cold fingers, gripping back.

"Not by morning," Mirabel said. "By tonight. Get the tin. We're leaving now—and tomorrow, we're going to raise a glass."
