## 3. Three Accidents

There is a particular hour in an archive when the building forgets it's a church. The organ goes quiet, the tourists thin out, the radiators tick like something counting, and what's left is just me and the dead paperwork of the living. I used to find that hour restful. Lately it has started to feel like the pause before someone says your name.

I had Maren Voss's diary locked in my desk drawer, wrapped in a cotton cloth like a relic, which in a way it was. I hadn't opened it since Tuesday. Not because I'd finished with it—because I hadn't finished *thinking* about it, and there is a difference. An archive teaches you patience. It also teaches you that nothing is ever really finished; things are only shelved.

So instead of the diary, I did what I always do when a thought won't leave me alone: I gave it company. I pulled the files on my other cases—the three that never made it into any official record, because officially they were never cases at all—and I laid them out across the long oak table under the green-shaded lamp like a dealer laying down cards she already knows are bad.

Three folders. Three winters ago, all three. And before you imagine me as some trench-coated avenger of the stacks, let me be clear about what "solved" means here. It doesn't mean anyone was arrested. It doesn't mean anyone was even accused. It means I know the truth, filed it in my head where no one could burn it, and told exactly no one. Three truths, kept warm in the dark for eleven years, like eggs under a hen who has begun to suspect the eggs are rotten.

I opened the first folder.

* * *

Edmund Pike. Verger, forty-one years old, fond of sherry and of complaining about the heating. He fell from the scaffolding in the south transept on the fourth of December, eleven years back—a restoration project, new stone for the clerestory, the whole cathedral swaddled in poles and planking like a patient mid-surgery. The coroner called it a misstep. Wet timber, worn boot soles, a man who'd had two glasses at lunch. Open-and-shut, said everyone, including, loudly and often, Dean Corvus Hale, who spoke at Edmund's funeral with such genuine-seeming grief that half the congregation wept and the other half resolved to give more to the restoration fund.

I didn't weep. I catalogued Edmund's effects, as was my job, and I noticed two things that didn't fit the story the way a key doesn't fit a lock it was cut for.

The first was his boots. They came to me in a box with the rest of his belongings, and they were old boots, yes, cracked at the welt—but the soles were nearly new. Someone had resoled them that autumn. I know because the cobbler's ticket was still folded in Edmund's own coat pocket, dated the third of November. A man does not pay thirty pounds to resole his boots and then wear them to fall off scaffolding four weeks later without a single scuff on the fresh leather. There were no scuffs. There was one long smear along the inner sole of the right boot, dark, waxy, and I did not need a laboratory to tell me it was candle wax. The kind we use in the sanctuary. The kind Edmund handled by the pound.

The second thing was the ledger. Edmund kept the verger's petty cash book, and the last page had been torn out—not ripped in haste but sliced, cleanly, with a blade. Whoever took it wanted one entry gone. I spent a week reconstructing what I could from carbon impressions on the following page, the way an archivist reads a palimpsest, and what surfaced was a payment authorization. Dated the second of December. Signed with initials I have never been able to match to any legitimate account: **C.H.**, countersigned by a charity name I'd never heard of—the Aldhelm Restoration Trust, which sounded respectable and appeared nowhere in the diocesan registry.

A man falls. A ledger loses a page. A trust exists that shouldn't. Any one of those is nothing. Together they were the first crack in the ice, and I stood on it for a long time before I understood I was standing on a lake.

I told no one. That was the arrangement I had with myself, then: I see, I record, I keep. Invisibility as a discipline. Nobody suspects the archivist; nobody invites the archivist to conspire; nobody wonders what the archivist knows. For years this felt like wisdom. I want you to hold onto that word, *felt*, because feelings in archives are like damp—they get into everything and rot the structure slowly, from inside.

* * *

The second folder was thinner, because there was less left of its subject to leave papers behind.

Dorothy Anselm—no relation, though the shared name gave me an unpleasant frisson every time I typed her file header. She was the cathedral accountant's assistant, sixty-three, meticulous, unmarried, the sort of woman who alphabetized her spice rack and knew to the penny what the flower fund held. She went into the River Wyre on the seventeenth of January, eleven years ago, walking home along the towpath after evening service. Slipped on the frost, said the police. Drowned. Tragic, said the parish. Careless of her, murmured a few, unkindly, to have been out so late alone.

But Dorothy had crossed that towpath six evenings a week for nineteen years, through ice storms and gales, and had never once gone in. And when I catalogued her effects—I was given them because her next of kin was a nephew in Perth who couldn't be bothered to come—her handbag contained her house keys, her bus pass, and her reading glasses. No glasses case. Dorothy never carried loose glasses; I'd seen the case a hundred times, tortoiseshell, clipped inside her bag like a holstered weapon. The case was gone. And a woman slipping on frost does not stop, mid-fall, to remove her glasses and pocket them carefully before she drowns.

Someone had gone through her bag. Someone looking for something specific, and they'd found it—or part of it—and taken the case because the glasses lived in it and perhaps something else did too. A ledger page, maybe. A receipt. A key.

And here is the detail that kept me up that whole grey February: Dorothy's last timesheet, the one for the week she died, showed her working late on the fifteenth and sixteenth of January. On what? The year-end accounts were closed. I checked. But the restoration fund books were open—open and being *adjusted*—and the adjustment entries were initialled **D.A.** in a hand that grew shakier with each line, as though written by someone who was being watched while she wrote, or being told what to write.

Two initials. Two ledgers. Two deaths ruled accidental, both within six weeks of each other, both in the same winter, both touching the same stream of money flowing toward a charity that did not exist.

I was twenty-nine years old and I learned that winter that a pattern is not a comfort. A pattern is a debt. Once you see it, you owe it something, and I paid in the only currency I had: silence, and careful filing.

* * *

The third folder I almost didn't open. Not because it hurt—it did—but because opening it meant admitting the shape of the thing, and shapes, once admitted, cannot be unadmitted.

Father Ambrose Kettleby. Sixty-eight. My predecessor's predecessor's colleague, a canon emeritus who still came in twice a week to pester me about misfiled incunabula and to drink tea he never finished. He died on the ninth of March, eleven years ago, of what the GP confidently recorded as a cardiac event, alone in the crypt vestry among the old records. Found the next morning by the sexton. Natural causes, said everyone, and why would they not? Old man, big heart, stone floor.

Except Ambrose had a heart condition he managed with tablets, and his tablet bottle—catalogued with his effects, because everything in this building eventually comes to me—was full. Full to the brim, freshly dispensed, dated the first of March. A man who takes his medication does not die of his heart with a full bottle, unless he stopped taking it, and Ambrose complained to me not ten days before he died that the new pills disagreed with him and he'd never miss a dose because missing doses was how his brother had died. He was fastidious about it. He counted them out into a little silver box each morning, the way he counted everything.

And Ambrose, in his last weeks, had developed an interest. He'd asked me—casually, over unfinished tea—whether I kept duplicates of the chapter's financial minutes. Whether older records might be consulted. Whether, and he phrased it with that donnish delicacy of his, "one ever encounters documents one wishes one hadn't." I told him the truth: yes, constantly. He laughed, and looked at me a long moment, and said, "Then you understand, Miss Sterling, why some doors stay locked."

He was talking about the crypt door. The one whose key hasn't turned in eleven years. The one that sealed shut the same month Edmund fell, the same winter Dorothy drowned, the same season Ambrose sat down among the records he'd finally worked up the courage to read, and did not get up.

Three deaths. One winter. One money trail. One locked door. And every witness to whatever moved beneath all of it—dead, or scattered, or silent. The bookkeeper's assistant gone. The verger gone. The old canon gone. The shell charity dissolved quietly the following autumn, its paper trail ending in a final disbursement that I once traced, at two in the morning, to an account I could not follow further without resources I did not have.

For eleven years I kept those three folders the way you keep a splinter: too deep to remove, too shallow to ignore. And I told myself the story that invisible people tell themselves—that seeing is enough, that keeping the truth safe is a form of justice, that someday someone would come asking and I would produce my folders like a magician and be believed.

Nobody came asking. Until now, somebody had come *writing*.

* * *

I sat back under the lamp and let the three folders sit there in a row, and I made myself say it plainly, in the privacy of my own skull, where even the cathedral can't quite eavesdrop.

Maren Voss was the bookkeeper.

Not the assistant. Not the deputy. The bookkeeper herself—young, competent, hired eight years ago, long after the winter of the accidents, but promoted into the very seat Dorothy Anselm had warmed, working the same funds, the same accounts, the same quiet channels that the Aldhelm Restoration Trust had carved and that I now suspected had never fully closed. She had inherited the position the way you inherit a haunted house: unaware, at first, of which rooms creak.

And her diary—the diary in my drawer, with entries dated after her disappearance, entries in her own verified hand—had named someone inside the cathedral. I hadn't let myself connect it. I'd kept the diary in one mental compartment and the folders in another, the way archives separate collections to prevent cross-contamination. But contamination is just another word for truth spreading.

Three witnesses to one scheme, all removed in a single winter, all ruled accidental, all filed away by a church that needed them gone and a police force that saw no reason to look harder. And now a fourth person, holding the same knowledge in a younger head, had vanished—and her diary kept writing.

If Maren had found what Dorothy found, what Ambrose died reading, what Edmund's torn page had hinted at—if she had followed the money far enough to see the face behind the initials—then she wasn't a runaway. She wasn't a tragedy. She was the fourth thread, and someone had already pulled three.

I looked at the clock. Nearly nine. The radiators ticked their patient count. Somewhere above me the great roof held the dark the way it had held it through all of it—through Edmund's fall, Dorothy's drowning, Ambrose's quiet heart, through eleven years of my silence—all of it sheltered, all of it witnessed, none of it innocent. Buildings aren't accomplices, I'd always said. Buildings don't choose.

But buildings keep secrets. And whoever controls the keys controls what the building keeps.

I gathered the three folders, and then I stopped, because a thought arrived with the cold clarity of a dropped pane of glass: the diary's last entry hadn't named Dean Hale outright. It had named him the way the ledgers named him—initials, indirection, a figure glimpsed through paper. Which meant Maren had been afraid to write the name. Afraid even in a diary she expected someone to find. Afraid enough to hide the meaning, and clever enough to hope the right reader would decode it.

She had expected *me*. Or someone like me. Someone who read everything and told no one.

Eleven years I had treated invisibility as armor. Tonight, for the first time, it felt like what it might actually be: a cage with very good lighting. Because if Maren Voss was alive and hiding, then somewhere out there she was waiting for the one person in this cathedral who notices things—and she was running out of time, because the man who emptied three lives out of one winter had just lost his bookkeeper, and men like that do not lose things twice.

I put the folders back in their drawer. I did not lock it. Some habits deserve to die.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would go back through the diary—not reading for words this time, but reading for hands. Because if those later entries were truly hers, truly written after the world decided she was dead, then Maren Voss hadn't just been hiding.

She'd been writing to me.
