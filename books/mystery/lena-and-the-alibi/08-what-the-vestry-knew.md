## 8. What the Vestry Knew

There is a particular smell to old paper that people romanticize and archivists distrust. The romantics call it history. I call it chemistry—lignin breaking down, acidity creeping, a slow, patient act of self-destruction happening on every shelf in my care. Paper wants to destroy itself. My job is to stand in the way.

I had been standing in the way of St. Aldhelm's paper for nineteen years, and until this week I had believed the cathedral's sins were, like everyone else's, mostly small ones. Gossip. Grudges. A warden who padded his expenses. A verger who drank. The ordinary sediment of any institution large enough to have a budget and small enough to have secrets.

Then Maren Voss's diary told me a man inside these walls had staged a death, and I stopped trusting the sediment.

So on Thursday morning, before the cleaners came through, before Dean Hale's morning smile could find me in a corridor, I pulled the vestry minutes.

* * *

Vestry minutes are the most honest dishonest documents ever devised. They record everything and confess nothing. *The Finance Committee met at seven o'clock. Present: the Dean, Mr. Ashcombe, Mrs. Prewitt, Miss Voss (recording secretary). The minutes of the previous meeting were approved.* Approved, approved, approved—a drumbeat of institutional assent that means precisely nothing except that nobody objected loudly enough to be minuted.

I started eleven years back, because that was when the crypt door had last turned its key, and because grief and paperwork share a calendar whether we admit it or not. Dean Corvus Hale's predecessor, Dean Ambrose Fenn, died in November of that year—a fall on the north stair, ruled accidental, mourned beautifully. Hale was installed by February. And in March, the Restoration Fund appeared.

That was the first thing I noticed, once I knew to look: the fund hadn't existed before Fenn died. It wasn't a scandal; it was a birth. Hale arrived with it fully formed, like Athena from Zeus's head, and the chapter embraced it with the relief of men being handed something to do with their hands. *Restore the fabric of St. Aldhelm's,* the founding resolution read. *To preserve our heritage for generations unborn.*

Generations unborn are wonderfully easy to defraud. They never audit.

* * *

I worked through the ledgers the way I work through everything—slowly, with cotton gloves and a growing coldness in my chest. The Restoration Fund received roughly forty thousand pounds a year in donations, grants, and legacies. That part was real; the money existed. What happened next was where the ink began to lie.

Every quarter, the fund made payments to an organization called the Aldhelm Heritage Trust. The name was lovely. It sounded like stained glass and tea rooms. The Trust, in turn, paid contractors: stonemasons, glaziers, a firm of architects whose invoices were models of professional vagueness. *Consultation re: tower stabilization. Site assessment, west cloister. Materials, various.*

Various. In twenty years of cataloguing other people's lies, I have learned that *various* is the most expensive word in the English language.

Here is what kept me at the table past midnight, gloves off now, fingers bare against the pages like a woman checking a pulse: the cathedral's own works department kept a separate set of records. Actual repairs. Actual invoices from actual masons, paid directly, modest sums. And those actual masons were not the same firms the Heritage Trust was paying. The Trust's contractors existed—I looked them up in the trade directories—but they operated out of mail drops and registered agents, and one of them shared an address with a solicitor's office in Clifton that also served as registered office for two shipping companies and a firm that sold, according to its letterhead, "agricultural futures."

Shell upon shell upon shell. Money went into the fund as devotion and came out of the Trust as dividends, and somewhere along the chain it changed into a private income wearing a hair shirt of good intentions.

And every single quarterly payment—the Trust disbursements, the contractor invoices, the architect's consultations—had been authorized by the same signature block. Two names. The Dean's, confident and looping. And beneath it, smaller, neater, tireless:

*M. Voss, bookkeeper.*

Maren Voss signed her name the way she apparently did everything: carefully, completely, with no flourish and no escape hatch. Hundreds of signatures across eleven years. I laid them side by side under the lamp and watched them change—not much, but enough. Early ones tentative. Middle ones automatic. The last ones, from eighteen months ago, pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn it. A hand writing itself into a corner.

People think embezzlement schemes are discovered. Mostly they're survived. Someone inside keeps the books because keeping the books feels safer than refusing, and then one day the books contain something they can't un-know, and the bookkeeper becomes either a co-conspirator or a corpse. The three accidents I'd already solved—the bell-ringer, the churchwarden, the scaffolder—all of them had touched the fund. All of them had, in their final months, asked questions in minuted meetings or unsigned memoranda. All of them had died within a season of asking.

And all three deaths had been followed, within weeks, by a quiet entry in the Trust ledger. Not payment—that would be crude. Adjustments. Write-offs. *Reconciliation of outstanding queries.* As though the dead men's questions had been debts, settled at last.

Corruption wearing vestments. That was the phrase that came to me, sitting alone in the archive with the radiators ticking, and it has the advantage of being exact. This wasn't sin bursting through the doors with a crowbar. This was sin in a cassock, saying grace, passing the plate. Every fraudulent pound had passed through hands folded in prayer. Every cover-up had been minuted, seconded, and approved.

Except by Maren Voss. She'd signed it all—and then, some months ago, she'd begun writing a diary addressed to whoever might one day read it, which meant that somewhere in that careful, cornered mind, she had decided to survive as a witness rather than die as an accomplice. She had faked her own drowning rather than sign one more page.

I closed the ledgers and sat in the dark a while, listening to the building breathe above me. Stone doesn't conspire. Stone just stands there, holding up the roof over whatever happens underneath it. The conspiracy was entirely human, and it had a face I'd smiled at across the refectory table perhaps two hundred times.

* * *

One more thing, and it is the thing that turned my dread into something with edges.

In the box of Fenn-era papers—the papers Hale had sealed in the crypt eleven years ago, or believed he had, because deans forget that archivists keep duplicate accession registers—I found a memorandum in Ambrose Fenn's own hand, dated nine days before his death. It was brief. Fenn wrote like a man who trusted brevity the way some men trust God.

*I have asked C.H. for a full accounting of the restoration monies disbursed to date. His answers have been evasive. If I do not receive satisfaction by month's end, I shall refer the matter to the Charity Commission personally.*

Nine days later, he fell down a staircase he had climbed every day for thirty years.

I held that single sheet for a long time. It was nothing, legally. A suspicion, a deadline, a dead man. But threaded together with the diary, the ledgers, the three accidents, and Maren Voss's forged drowning, it was a spine—and every document I owned hung from it.

Hale hadn't merely killed to protect a scheme. He had inherited the scheme's necessity on the day he decided to want the deanery. Fenn was the first accident. Everything since had been maintenance.

I put Fenn's memorandum in my coat pocket, not the archive. Some things you stop trusting to shelves.

Then I heard footsteps on the archive stairs—unhurried, familiar, arriving at the worst possible hour—and a voice I knew too well calling down pleasantly, "Miss Sterling? You're here late. Might I have a word?"

Corvus Hale stood at the bottom of the steps with his hands clasped behind his back, smiling the smile that had raised millions for the fabric of this place, and I felt Fenn's memorandum press against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

He wanted a word. I wondered which word, and how many people had heard his last one before they fell.
