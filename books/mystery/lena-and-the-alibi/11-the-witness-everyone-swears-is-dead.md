## 11. The Witness Everyone Swears Is Dead

The diary had been lying to me for weeks, and I had been a willing audience.

That's the thing about codes—they don't hide from you so much as they wait for you to stop looking at them like a stranger. I'd spent three nights with Maren Voss's little green book propped against a book stand in my workroom, cross-referencing her post-disappearance entries against everything I knew about her handwriting, her habits, her particular genius for making the mundane sound like scripture. And somewhere around two in the morning on the third night, drinking tea that had gone the color of regret, I finally saw it.

She wasn't writing to herself.

Every entry after the eleventh of February—the day she walked into the sea, according to the coroner, though no body was ever recovered—contained a small anomaly. A word out of place. A date that didn't match the day. A name spelled just slightly wrong, as if the writer's hand had slipped. *St. Brendan's* instead of *St. Brandon's*. A reference to "the larch walk" when everyone knew the convent garden path was lined with yew. Little errors, scattered like breadcrumbs by someone who understood that breadcrumbs get eaten by birds, but patterns don't.

Maren Voss had been a bookkeeper for eleven years before she vanished. Bookkeepers don't make mistakes. Bookkeepers leave reconciling entries.

I sat back in my chair and laughed—a short, ugly sound, the kind you make alone in a stone room at two in the morning—and then I got out a fresh sheet of paper and started pulling the threads.

* * *

By dawn I had a list.

The wrong dates clustered. Not randomly—she hadn't been careless enough for random—but grouped in threes and fours, and when I converted each cluster to its position in the calendar year, treating the false dates as numbers rather than days, they resolved into something almost embarrassingly simple. Maren had used the church's own liturgical calendar as her key. Feast days. The dates she wrote were always a fixed number of days off from a feast day, and the number of days off was the message.

I won't pretend it took me only one morning. It took me two, and most of a pot of coffee, and one long detour through a 1970s guidebook to religious houses because I needed to confirm what the decoded fragments kept circling: *the house above the grey water. Where the sisters keep the bees. Forty miles west, where the land gives up.*

There is exactly one convent on this coast that keeps bees. St. Hilda's-by-the-Sea sits on a headland forty-one miles west of the city, a scatter of grey stone buildings that have been staring down the Atlantic since before anyone thought to write down when. I knew it the way archivists know things—peripherally, through donation records and a correspondence file from the 1990s regarding the disposition of some chalices. Sisters of St. Hilda. They kept bees. They sold honey at a stall outside the cathedral every October, which meant that twice a year, the convent came to us.

And once a year, apparently, so did a letter.

Because here is where the decoding stopped being an intellectual exercise and started being a wound. The last cluster of false dates—the final entry, the one that named someone inside the cathedral, the one I'd already read a dozen times—decoded to a single phrase, and the phrase was not a place or a warning or a plea.

It was a question. *Did it arrive?*

She had sent confirmation. She had written from St. Hilda's—or arranged for something to be written—to someone she believed would come looking, and the last entry wasn't a message at all. It was Maren checking the mail.

Which meant there was a letter. Which meant, if it never arrived—if the answer to her question was *no, it did not arrive*—then somewhere between a headland forty miles west and a locked archive room in the undercroft of St. Aldhelm's, someone had opened the cathedral's mail and made a decision.

I put down my pen and looked at the diary for a long time.

"You clever, frightened woman," I said to the empty room. "You've been asking me a question for eight months."

* * *

The postal records were, thank God, boring.

Cathedral mail is logged—not out of any love of procedure, but because Dean Hale inherited from his predecessor a mania for documentation that he has never quite managed to kill. Every parcel, every registered letter, every item of official correspondence gets entered into a ledger with a date, a sender, and a disposition. The ledgers live in my archive, because everything lives in my archive; that is both my superpower and, increasingly, my death sentence.

I pulled the volume covering the spring after Maren's disappearance and worked backward from the decoded dates. There it was, four days after her final entry. Logged in the neat hand of the vestry secretary:

*Registered letter, St. Hilda's Convent, Westcliff Head. Addressee: The Archivist. Disposition: delivered.*

Delivered.

I read it three times. Then I checked the ledger against itself, because that's what you do when the ground moves—you check whether the ground or your feet are lying. The entry was in the secretary's hand, but the disposition column had been amended. The ink was different. Slightly darker, slightly bluer, written over what looked like a correction fluid patch. Someone had changed the record. Someone had taken a letter addressed to *me*, marked it delivered, and it had never reached my desk.

I know every piece of paper that crosses my threshold. I have known for eleven years. Nothing arrives in this archive without my seeing it, because seeing is the entire job. If a registered letter from St. Hilda's had been placed in my hands, I would remember it the way I remember everything—which is to say, completely and against my will.

So either the vestry secretary lied, or someone above the secretary had access to the ledger, access to incoming mail, and a reason to intercept a letter from a convent to a reclusive archivist.

I thought about Dean Corvus Hale shaking my hand at the Advent service, his warmth like a well-tailored coat. I thought about his gentle suggestion that the archive might benefit from being *thinned*. I thought about the cigarette butt at the boathouse, and the hurried departure, and the growing, sickening understanding that whatever game we were playing, he had been reading my board.

But doubt is a discipline, not a conclusion. I wanted it to be Hale. Wanting is not evidence. So I did what I always do when the living confuse me: I went to the dead, or the next best thing—paper older than anyone currently scheming.

* * *

The convent's correspondence file gave me Mother Cecilia.

Not by name, at first. By hand. The 1990s letters regarding the chalices were signed by a "Mother Prioress" whose script was upright, economical, faintly stubborn—the handwriting of a woman who has spent decades writing letters she expects to be obeyed. The current prioress, per a more recent exchange about the honey stall's insurance, signed herself *Cecilia*. Same hand. Twenty-six years apart, same unyielding verticals. Whatever else St. Hilda's was, it was stable. Stability mattered. You cannot hide a fugitive in an institution that churns.

I composed and deleted six letters before I settled on the seventh.

Archivists have one great advantage over detectives: we are expected to be tedious. Nobody suspects an inquiry about provenance. I wrote to Mother Cecilia in my driest register, explaining that the cathedral was conducting a routine review of historical donations, that certain records from the 1990s referenced a lay oblate associated with the convent in the early years, and that I would be grateful for any light the community could shed on names from that period. I enclosed a list. On the list, buried among nine genuine names of women who had passed through St. Aldhelm's orbit in the relevant years, I included *Sister Miriam*—a name I invented from whole cloth—and beneath it, in smaller type, *formerly known to this office?*

Then I did the thing I had been avoiding. I didn't post it.

Posting it meant trusting the mail. The mail, I now knew, had a censor. Instead I waited two days for my scheduled day off, told no one, and took the coastal bus west with the letter in my handbag and the diary in an inner pocket sewn shut by a tailor who owed me a favor and asked no questions, which is the best kind of tailor.

Forty-one miles of grey sea and hedgerow. I watched the water the whole way and tried not to think about drowning.

* * *

St. Hilda's-by-the-Sea looks like it was designed by God in one of His more austere moods. Grey stone, slate roofs, a bell cote with no bell, and a wind that comes off the Atlantic with the specific intention of stripping you of anything decorative, including pretensions. The bee hives sat in a walled garden behind the chapel, and even in the cold the air near them hummed.

A sister met me at the gate—a young one, rosy from the weather, with the frank stare of someone who has taken vows precisely to avoid small talk.

"I'm here about the archives," I said, and produced my letter of introduction, which was real, because I am the archive, and introduced myself accordingly. "From St. Aldhelm's. Regarding historical donations."

"Mother Cecilia will see you," she said, in the tone of someone reporting a fact about the tide.

Mother Cecilia received me in a low room with a fire, a table, two chairs, and a teapot that had clearly seen service in several wars. She was smaller than her handwriting suggested, which is often the case with formidable people—the pen gets the stature the body economizes on. Her eyes were pale grey and missed nothing, including, I suspected, the shape of the object pressing against my inner pocket.

"Archivist Sterling." She poured tea without asking. "You've come a long way for provenance."

"The cathedral takes its history seriously."

"Mm." She set down the pot. "We take our privacy seriously. It makes for an interesting negotiation."

I laid out my cover story—the donation review, the list of names, the routine tedium of it all—and watched her listen with the patience of a woman who has heard confessions for thirty years and can smell a rehearsed speech the way her bees smell rain. When I finished, she was quiet long enough that the fire became loud.

"You listed ten names," she said finally. "Nine of them mean nothing to me. One of them doesn't exist."

"Sister Miriam."

"There has never been a Sister Miriam at this house." She said it flatly, without heat, and then she did something I will think about for the rest of my life. She turned her teacup slowly on its saucer, aligning the handle to face the door, and said, "There was, however, a lay guest who arrived in March of last year. Quiet. Capable with figures. Terrible gardener. She asked to be called Miriam because she said it was plain enough not to attract attention, and I allowed it because"—her eyes came up and fixed on mine—"she was plainly running from something, and this house does not return people to the sea."

The room tilted slightly. I gripped my teacup like a railing.

"She's alive," I said. My voice came out lower than I intended.

"Guests' whereabouts are not a matter I discuss." But she said it gently, the way you decline a child's request, and the gentleness was the answer. Mother Cecilia had neither confirmed nor denied, and in doing so had confirmed everything. Maren Voss—dead woman, drowned woman, the witness everyone swore was gone—was alive, forty miles west, keeping bees badly and books perfectly, waiting for a letter that had never come.

"Mother," I said carefully, "did she send correspondence? In her first weeks here?"

Something moved behind Cecilia's eyes—the flicker of a woman recalculating. "Why do you ask?"

"Because a registered letter left this house addressed to me, and it never arrived. It was logged as delivered. It wasn't. I'd like to know what it said, because I suspect the person who intercepted it is the reason your guest ran in the first place."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the wall, the bees hummed their indifferent hymn.

"We posted one letter," Cecilia said at last, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Early April. She wrote it herself, sealed it herself, and asked that it go registered, to the archivist at St. Aldhelm's, because—" and here the old nun paused, and I watched her decide how much truth a stranger could carry, "—because she said the archivist was the only person in that building who read everything and belonged to nothing. She said you were invisible, and that invisible people see."

My throat tightened in a way I found professionally embarrassing.

"It was acknowledged," Cecilia continued. "We have the receipt. Signed for at the cathedral. And three weeks later, a second letter went out—an inquiry, unsigned, to our bishop's office, asking whether St. Hilda's was in the practice of harboring persons evading legal process. Asking whether we could produce records of our guests." Her mouth thinned. "The bishop forwarded it to me with a note suggesting cooperation. I replied that our guest list is between God and the bursar. We heard nothing further. But Sister Agnes saw a car on the coast road twice that summer. A dark car, parked past the lane, engine off. Watching."

"A man who smokes," I said. "Cigarettes with a gold band."

Cecilia went very still. "How do you know that?"

"Because I found one at a boathouse in the city, and I've been trying to convince myself I was wrong ever since."

The old woman rose, crossed to a bureau, and stood with her back to me for a moment—a small figure silhouetted against grey light, carrying a decision. Then she returned and pressed something into my hand: a small card, cream-colored, with nothing on it but a phone number written in that upright, stubborn hand.

"When she first came here, she made me promise something," Cecilia said. "She said: if anyone ever comes asking, and that person seems true, give them this. She said the archivist would know what it means." Her pale eyes held mine. "Do you know what it means, Miss Sterling?"

I turned the card over. On the reverse, in Maren Voss's careful bookkeeper's hand, was a single line—the same line that closed her diary, the line I had read fifty times and decoded twice:

*The fourth thread holds the cloth.*

"Yes," I said. "I think I'm beginning to."

"Then understand this also." Cecilia sat again, and leaned forward, and the warmth went out of her voice entirely, replaced by something older and harder than kindness. "Two days ago, a solicitor telephoned this house. He claimed to represent the estate of a deceased parishioner and asked whether we had any record of a woman named Maren Voss. He used the past tense throughout. He was very smooth." She let that sit. "Someone knows, or nearly knows. And whoever he was, he called from a number my switchboard logged. I had it traced by a friend who owes me favors. It belongs to a firm that does work for the Diocese of St. Aldhelm's."

The fire crackled. Outside, the wind pushed at the stones, patient as water.

"He's closing the net," I said.

"He is moving," Cecilia corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference is time. He believes she is a loose end. Loose ends get tied." She stood, and the audience was over. "I will tell her you came. What she does with that knowledge is hers—I promised her that too. But I'll say this much, Archivist, since you crossed forty miles of bad road on a hunch and a forged provenance inquiry, which I respect enormously: her trial starts in nineteen days. Not yours. His. The innocent man's. And she has spent eight months believing that coming forward means the girl suffers for it."

"The girl?"

"His daughter." Cecilia's voice dropped. "Bracken's daughter. That is the leash, Miss Sterling. That is how you hold a man who would otherwise talk."

I walked out through the walled garden, past the hives, past the sister at the gate who nodded as if I'd passed some test I hadn't studied for. The bus back east wouldn't come for two hours, so I stood on the headland with the sea hammering the rocks below and the card in my glove, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the boathouse.

Nineteen days until Tom Bracken was convicted of a murder that never happened, in a trial built to bury the truth forever. Eight months of intercepted mail. A watcher on the coast road. A solicitor asking questions in the past tense. And somewhere behind all of it, a man in vestments who smiled at me across the Advent crowd and suggested, so gently, that my archive might need thinning.

He didn't know yet that I'd found her. But he knew the convent existed—that much the second letter proved—and he had a car, and a telephone, and nineteen days.

Everyone swore Maren Voss was dead. Everyone except a nun, a bookkeeper, and now me.

I got on the bus going east, toward the cathedral, toward the man who thought he owned the ending of this story, and I began, in my head, drafting the sworn statement that would take it away from him.
