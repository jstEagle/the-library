## 2. A Will That Shouldn't Exist

The county records office smelled of dust and cold coffee and the particular melancholy of paper that no one had loved in decades. Sable Okafor had spent enough of their life in rooms like this to find the smell almost comforting — the smell of facts, filed and numbered, indifferent to anyone's wishes. Facts didn't grieve. Facts didn't lie. They only waited.

Marguerite Voss's retainer had come with a list of requests, typed on cream stock in a hand that trembled at the descenders. The third item was simple: *Confirm the legal status of Lena Voss-Ashcombe.* Sable had assumed it would take an hour. A birth certificate, perhaps a marriage record, something tidy to tuck into a file that would otherwise hold nothing but an old woman's fears.

Sable pulled the first drawer at nine-forty in the morning and did not look up again until the light through the high windows had gone amber.

* * *

The death certificate was beautiful, in the way that official documents are beautiful — all certainty and no doubt. *Lena Marguerite Voss-Ashcombe. Cause of death: drowning. Date: June 14th, six years past. Location: the lake below Voss Hall.* Witnessed by three named parties. Investigated by the county coroner. Case closed with a stamp so crisp it might have been applied yesterday.

Sable read it four times.

It wasn't the document itself that held them — documents died all the time; Sable made a comfortable living off the paperwork of endings. It was the *completeness* of it. Every field filled. Every signature present. The fisherman who'd seen her go under, his statement notarized. The attending physician, one Dr. Ilsa Renner, certifying the body. Even the housekeeper's daughter, a girl of nineteen at the time, swearing she'd watched from the boathouse dock and heard the last cry.

Three witnesses. Three signatures. A closed case.

And yet.

Sable laid the sealed will beside the certificate on the scarred oak table, breaking the wax with a letter knife older than both of them. Marguerite Voss had insisted the will be dated next month — a formality, she'd said, a matter of timing — but she'd had Sable read it aloud twice in her sickroom, and Sable had a lawyer's memory for language. There it was, in the second recital, in the same trembling hand:

*I leave the whole of my estate, real and personal, to my ward, Lena Voss-Ashcombe, living as of this signing.*

Living as of this signing.

Six years dead. Living as of this signing. Both statements certified, both sworn, both sitting on the same table like two strangers who claimed to be the same woman.

Sable felt the old thrill rise — the one that had pulled them into probate law in the first place, the pleasure of a document that shouldn't exist, a contradiction with a seal on it. Somewhere in this stack of paper was either a fraud worth prosecuting or a truth worth telling, and either way it was the most interesting thing to cross their desk in eleven years of practice.

Then they read the coroner's supplementary notes, and the thrill went cold in their chest.

*Body recovered within the hour. Identified by witness statement. Interred in the Voss family plot, Ashcombe section, plot 12.*

No autopsy. No photographs in the file. The identification rested entirely on the witnesses' word — the same three witnesses whose statements, when Sable spread them side by side, used identical phrasing in places where independent accounts should have diverged. *She went under without a struggle,* said the fisherman. *She went under without a struggle,* said the doctor. *She went under without a struggle,* said the housekeeper's daughter, though by her own account she was two hundred yards away on the dock.

People don't drown without a struggle. That was the first thing anyone who'd ever worked a drowning case learned. The body fights. The lungs fight. Something always shows.

Sable sat back. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the row of filing cabinets, a clerk dropped a box and swore softly, and the sound seemed very far away.

There were two possibilities, and Sable turned them over like stones. Either a dead woman had signed nothing, witnessed nothing, and existed nowhere — in which case Marguerite Voss was leaving her fortune to a ghost, and someone wanted that documented badly enough to pay triple rates. Or Lena Voss-Ashcombe had never drowned at all, and every signature on this certificate was a kindness or a crime, and Sable could not yet tell which.

Either way, the will was a bomb with a six-year fuse already lit.

* * *

They found the Ashcombe lawyers at four o'clock, because of course they did. Sable had been in county records long enough to know that nothing drew lawyers like the smell of money changing hands, and the Voss estate was the largest unprobated holding in three counties.

The younger one reached Sable first — a narrow man in a gray suit, all elbows and cologne, who materialized at the end of the aisle with the practiced casualness of someone who had been waiting there for an hour.

"Sable Okafor." He pronounced it carefully, having clearly rehearsed. "Pierce Halloran, Ashcombe family counsel. We understand you've taken a retainer from Mrs. Voss."

"We understand correctly," Sable said, and kept walking toward the cabinets.

"Mrs. Ashcombe — Edmund's mother, that is — she likes to stay informed. Family matters." He fell into step, hands clasped behind his back like a man touring a garden. "Sentimental woman. She'll want to know what you're pulling."

"You're welcome to file a records request," Sable said. "Same as anyone."

"Oh, we have." He smiled. "We pulled this drawer six weeks ago, Mr. Okafor. Before your client retained you. Which is interesting, isn't it? That we were interested before there was anything to be interested *in*."

Sable stopped. The words settled slowly, the way silt settles in still water. Six weeks ago. Six weeks ago, Marguerite Voss had not yet hired anyone. Six weeks ago, according to any public accounting, there was nothing at all unusual about the Voss estate — a dying woman, a standard succession, cousins waiting with folded hands.

Unless the cousins had known what was coming. Unless somewhere in the Voss household, someone had talked.

"What exactly is the Ashcombe family's interest?" Sable asked. "Mrs. Voss is still living. Her will is her own affair."

"Her will is everyone's affair when it names a dead person as sole heir." Halloran's voice stayed pleasant, but something moved underneath it, quick and cold, like a fish turning under ice. "A will bequeathing property to a deceased individual is void as to that individual. Intestacy follows. The bloodline takes. That's not opinion, Mr. Okafor, that's statute. And the bloodline is *us*."

"I'm aware of how intestacy works."

"Then you're aware your client is paying you triple rates to chase a ghost." He produced a card, held it out between two fingers. "Edmund Ashcombe is returning to the county next month. He's fond of resolving things personally. I'd advise you to have your findings in order before he arrives. He does dislike surprises."

Sable took the card. It was heavy stock, cream-colored, the name embossed in silver: *Edmund Ashcombe*. Underneath, in smaller type, a London address. The card felt warm, as if Halloran had been carrying it against his skin, waiting.

"Was there anything else?"

"Just this." Halloran paused at the end of the aisle, silhouetted against the tall windows, and for a moment his pleasantness slipped entirely, and what showed beneath was neither kind nor cruel but simply *certain*, the certainty of a man reading from a script he believed had already ended. "Whatever Mrs. Voss has told you, whatever she's paid you to believe — the girl is dead. She has been dead for six years. Everyone swears to it. Everyone who matters."

He left. The door swung shut behind him with a sound like a book closing.

* * *

Sable stood a long while among the cabinets after he was gone, the card in one hand and the photocopied certificate in the other, watching the dust turn in the amber light.

Everyone swears to it. Everyone who matters.

It should have been reassuring. A closed case, three witnesses, a stamped and notarized ending — the machinery of the law agreeing unanimously that this woman was gone. Most clients would have accepted it. Most lawyers would have billed their hours, filed the caveat, and let intestacy do its slow, inevitable work.

But Sable Okafor had built a career on being the person nobody remembered to lie to, and something about the unanimity itched like a wool collar. Witnesses disagreed. That was what witnesses *did*. Memory blurred, angles differed, details scattered like spilled seed. These three accounts fit together too well, dovetailed like carpentry, each gap covered by another's certainty. It was not testimony. It was choreography.

And Halloran — Halloran had said *before there was anything to be interested in*, and had not seemed to realize what he'd confessed. The Ashcombes had known. Six weeks before Marguerite Voss summoned a lawyer to prove a murder hadn't happened yet, the people who would benefit from that murder's erasure had already sent men to the records office.

Which meant the murder was not a fear. It was a plan. It had perhaps always been a plan, and the drowning six years ago —

The drowning six years ago might have been the first attempt.

Sable locked the certificates into their briefcase, snapped the clasps, and stood in the emptying office with the particular dread that comes not from finding a contradiction but from realizing which side of it you're standing on. Marguerite Voss had paid triple rates and spoken of a crime *scheduled but not committed*, and Sable had taken it for the rambling of a dying woman, a superstition dressed in legalese.

Now the dates arranged themselves in Sable's mind like evidence on a timeline: a girl declared dead six years ago without a struggle; a will naming her alive as of a signing next month; cousins whose lawyers arrived six weeks early; and a man named Edmund, fond of resolving things personally, returning to the county on a schedule that matched, almost to the day, the date written into the sealed will.

Almost to the day.

Sable clicked off the desk lamp. In the darkened records office, forty years of the county's deaths slept in their drawers, orderly and final, and somewhere among them — or not among them, and that was the whole terrible question — was Lena Voss-Ashcombe.

Tomorrow, Sable decided, shrugging into their coat, they would go and ask the witnesses themselves. All three of them. Starting with whoever had signed first.

Because if everyone swears she's dead, Sable thought, pushing out into the cold, then the most important question in this county is not whether she is.

It's why they all rehearsed.
