## 7. The Bookshop Trap

There's an old rule in detection that nobody writes down because everybody learns it the hard way: if you can't find your enemy, make yourself interesting enough that he finds you.

I'd spent five days being uninteresting. I'd shelved returns, argued with the electric company about a bill they swore I owed and I swore was written in a dead man's handwriting, and sold exactly one book — a paperback western to a man who paid in quarters counted out of a coin purse like he was performing surgery on it. Meanwhile Corliss had missed our Tuesday meeting, my flat had been turned over with the delicacy of a burglar who respected good furniture, and someone had taken precisely one thing: my copy of the 2003 inquest file.

They left the money. Three hundred dollars in cash, sitting on my kitchen table under an ashtray, untouched, arranged almost neatly. That detail had kept me up two nights running. Thieves take money. Investigators leave it. Whoever went through my flat wasn't robbing me — they were *editing* me, removing one page from the story of Juniper Yarrow and hoping I wouldn't notice the revision.

I noticed. Noticing is the whole job.

So on Thursday morning, with frost still on the windows of Yarrow's Books and the radiator making its usual sound of a dying animal, I began to commit fraud.

* * *

The plan was simple enough to fit on a napkin, which is where I wrote it, over coffee at Tessa's bar before she opened — because Tessa poured coffee at seven a.m. for anyone sad enough to ask, and because I wanted to see her face when I mentioned the levee again. She'd gone carefully blank both times, the way people do when they're holding something behind a door and pretending the door isn't there. I didn't push. Pushing a witness too early is how you get a witness who lies just to make you stop.

The napkin read:

1. FAKE THE FILE.
2. MAKE IT FINDABLE.
3. WAIT IN THE DARK LIKE AN IDIOT.
4. SEE WHO COMES.

Under item four I'd drawn a small stick figure falling down stairs, which felt less like humor than prophecy.

The forgery itself was the fun part, and I'll admit I enjoyed it more than a retired detective strictly should. I took a genuine-looking manila folder from a box of office supplies I'd bought from the drugstore — the same drugstore where the clerk now watched me with the expression of a man tracking a slow-motion disaster — and filled it with paper. Some of it was real: photocopies of the coroner's log page I'd photographed before things got complicated, notes in my own cramped hand, a timeline of the three conflicting death dates I'd found for Lena Marsh. Some of it was theater. I aged a few sheets with cold tea and a radiator. I wrote out a fake letter, supposedly from "a source inside the courthouse," describing the original verdict document in loving detail — its location, its condition, the fact that I had *photographs of the signature comparison*, which was even true, though the photographs were of nothing useful yet.

Then I added the bait. A single sheet, folded once, tucked into the folder's back pocket, labeled in pencil: **VANE — ORIGINAL — DO NOT COPY AGAIN**.

You have to understand the psychology. These people had already stolen one file. They weren't going to steal this one — stealing confirms value and creates risk. No, what they'd do is *read* it. They'd stand in my shop after hours with a flashlight and turn every page, looking for how much I knew, and the folded sheet labeled DO NOT COPY would burn a hole through the folder until they unfolded it, because human beings are incapable of leaving a folded thing alone. I know this about people. It's most of what twenty-two years as a detective taught me, and it cost me two marriages to learn it, so somebody was going to use it.

I placed the folder where a professional would find it but an amateur wouldn't: not in the safe behind the register, not in the back office, but in the bottom drawer of the front desk, under a stack of order forms, weighted so the drawer sat slightly open — ajar by exactly the width that says *someone was interrupted while searching here*. Staging, they call it. Lying with furniture.

Then I made sure the shop had witnesses of its own. Two of them, actually, both cheap. The first was Dev.

Dev Okafor was twenty-four, a paralegal at Hollis & Grange who did research for me on cases where the client couldn't afford real help, which was all of them lately. He had the energy of a man who hadn't yet learned that institutions always win, and I liked him for it the way you like a candle in a drafty room. He arrived at noon with sandwiches and his laptop and set himself up in the reading nook like he owned it.

"Explain to me again why I'm sitting in a bookshop doing homework," he said.

"Because you're a young man studying for the bar exam who happens to enjoy the ambiance."

"I'm playing solitaire."

"You're a young man enjoying the ambiance." I handed him the important part: a cheap motion-sensor camera, the kind hunters use, camouflaged in a hollowed-out copy of *Lorna Doone*. Nobody has ever stolen *Lorna Doone*. It faced the front desk from the shelf of regional interest, which nobody browsed, because regional interest is where books go to be forgiven. "If anyone opens that drawer between six p.m. and midnight, I get a photo and a timestamp."

"And where will you be?"

"Here."

"Here *where*?"

"The dark part of here."

Dev looked at me for a long moment, the way young people look at old people when they're recalibrating their estimate downward. "You're going to hide in your own shop."

"It's my shop. I know where everything is. That's an advantage."

"It's a disadvantage. You'll fall asleep. You're fifty-eight."

"Fifty-six."

"You'll fall asleep twice, then."

He wasn't wrong, which is why the second witness was the door itself. I'd rigged it years ago during a rash of break-ins on the street — a strip of clear tape across the jamb at knee height, invisible unless you knew to look, broken by any entry. Low-tech, unfalsifiable, and immune to power cuts. The camera might fail. The tape never does.

By six I sent Dev home with half a sandwich and instructions to call me at ten, midnight, and two a.m., and to keep calling until I answered, and if I ever answered sounding strange, to call Tessa instead of the police.

"Why Tessa?" he asked.

"Because the police in this town work for whoever's paying them, and right now I don't know who that is. Tessa works for herself."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard about a barmaid."

"It's the truest one I've heard about anybody."

* * *

I turned off the lights at seven and sat down in the gap between the biography section and the window, on the floor, with my back against the shelving and my coat around my knees like a man waiting for a bus that stopped coming years ago.

Here is what nobody tells you about surveillance work: it is mostly a conversation with yourself. The first hour is fine. You catalog sounds. The radiator. The ice cream freezer two doors down clicking off. A car passing with its radio going, some song about a woman named Delilah or maybe the singer just liked saying the name. You tell yourself you are a professional, patient, disciplined, a stone in the river of the night.

By nine you've reorganized your entire opinion of your life. By ten you've drafted the conversation you'd have with your ex-wife if she called, and won it. By eleven you're negotiating with God, or whatever's on duty, offering to be a better man if only your left foot comes back to life.

At 11:40 the tape broke.

I didn't hear it — tape doesn't announce itself — but I saw it, or rather I saw the consequence: the front door, which I'd left locked, drifted open two inches, and a rectangle of streetlight fell across the floorboards and lay there like something spilled.

I stopped breathing in the amateur way and started doing it properly, slow and shallow, the way you learn to when hiding is the job. The door eased shut. Footsteps — careful ones, weight rolled heel-to-toe, someone who'd done this before or thought about it a lot. A flashlight came on low, hooded by a hand, and the beam moved along the shelves with the unhurried confidence of a person following directions.

They went straight to the desk.

Not browsing, not searching — straight to the drawer, as if they'd been told *bottom left, under the order forms*. Which meant either my staging was very good or my staging was irrelevant, because they'd already searched this place once, or watched me plant it, or both. The thought arrived cold: *they may have watched me build the trap.* And then a second thought, colder: *then let them spring it anyway, because a sprung trap still tells you who's hungry.*

The flashlight clicked off. In the dark I heard paper. Pages turning, quick and competent. Then a pause — long, deliberate — at the back pocket. I could practically hear the argument happening inside their skull, the little angel and devil of burglary arguing over a folded sheet labeled DO NOT COPY. The fold crackled open.

Silence. Ten seconds. Twenty. Long enough to read it twice.

Then the strangest thing happened. They laughed. Quietly — a single breath of amusement, muffled, almost polite — and put the folder back in the drawer, closed it, and stood there in my dark shop for a moment like a customer deciding whether to buy.

And then they spoke. To the room. To me, whether they knew it or not.

"She was dead then," a voice said — male, middle-aged, unhurried, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "She's dead now. The date doesn't change anything. It's just ink."

I committed the voice to memory the way you'd press a flower: flat, slightly nasal, a faint rural drag on the vowels. Not Vane — I'd heard Vane speak at two public functions, all courtroom polish. This was hired furniture. But furniture belongs to a house.

Footsteps toward the door. Pause. And then, deliberately, something placed on the floor near the threshold — a small click of card against wood. The door opened, closed, and the lock, I swear, was turned gently from outside with a key or a pick, so gently it barely caught.

I gave them ninety seconds. Then I gave myself thirty more, because my legs had gone to sleep in a way that suggested permanent renegotiation with my body, and a detective staggering out of the stacks like a newborn deer undermines the whole mystique.

The envelope on the floor was heavy cream stock, the kind judges use. Inside, a single card. No handwriting except one line, printed in careful block letters:

**SOME VERDICTS ARE FINAL. ATTEND TO YOUR BOOKS.**

Below it, stamped in faded violet ink, was the seal of the Calder's Bend County Coroner's Inquest, 2003 — the gull-and-scales device, the date, the case number. The same seal that appeared on the verdict Aldous Vane had signed as a junior clerk twenty years ago. Someone had gone to the trouble of finding an original impression of that seal — or keeping one — and pressing it onto a threat, like a signature on a piece of art.

They weren't warning me off. Warnings say *stop*. This said *we are the record now*. They'd stamped their authority on it the way you stamp a passport. We own the border between alive and dead, the card said. We decide what crosses.

I sat down on the floor of my own shop, in the dark, holding the thing, and I want to report that I felt afraid, and I did — a cold, crawling, honest fear, the kind that starts in the stomach and files paperwork in the hands. But underneath it, unwelcome and unmistakable, something else was waking up. Something I hadn't felt since the last year I worked homicide, before the pension, before the shop, before the long gray afternoon of selling used novels to people who mostly wanted the smell of them.

It was the feeling of being *relevant*. Of mattering enough to threaten. For five years I had been a man the town looked through, and tonight somebody had looked straight at me — and blinked first, because they'd come in the dark and left before dawn, because they needed me ignorant more than they needed me gone.

That's not strength on their side. That's fear wearing a seal.

I turned on one lamp, the small one by the register, and laid the card on the counter next to the broken strip of tape, evidence and exhibit, and I made myself a promise with the specific gravity of a man who has just been told, in writing, to mind his own business: I was going to find Lena Marsh. Alive, if she was alive. Dead, if she was dead. But *found* — found by me, on the record, in daylight, with her name spoken correctly in a room full of people who'd spent twenty years pronouncing it wrong.

The phone rang. Dev, right on schedule, 12:01.

"Well?" he said.

"They came. They read it. They laughed at it. They left me a note signed with the inquest seal telling me to attend to my books."

A pause on the line. "Juniper... that's not a warning. That's a receipt. That means the seal still exists somewhere. Physical stamps don't survive twenty years unless somebody's still using them."

I looked at the violet impression, at the gull holding its little scales, weighing whatever it is gulls weigh.

"I know," I said. "Which means somewhere in this town there's a drawer with the 2003 inquest still in it — not closed. Open. Waiting for a date."

"What date?"

I told him, and heard him go quiet the way people do when arithmetic turns mean.

"Get me everything on Aldous Vane's clerk staff from that year," I said. "Everyone who touched the file. Everyone who could still be holding a stamp."

"On it." A pause. "Juniper? You're smiling. I can hear it."

"Go home, Dev."

I hung up and stood a while in the lamplight, among my unsold books, holding a dead woman's seal in my hand — and for the first time since Corliss walked through my door, the case didn't feel unclosable at all.

It felt like it was closing toward me.
