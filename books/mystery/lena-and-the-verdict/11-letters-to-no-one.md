## 11. Letters to No One

The dead have excellent posture. Nobody tells you that. When you stop existing, you stop slouching — there's no one to slump for.

I learned this the hard way, twenty years ago, on a night in December when I ran along the levee with my lungs full of broken glass and my name already being written into the past tense behind me. By morning I was Lena Marsh, deceased, cause of death: drowning, verdict: accidental, signed, sealed, and filed by a clerk named Aldous Vane who had never once seen me wet. I have thought about that signature a great deal over two decades. It is the most intimate thing anyone has ever done to me. He held a pen and ended my life in four strokes, and he didn't even smudge the ink.

Now I live in a cabin upriver where the road gives up about a mile before you arrive, which suits me fine. The roof leaks in exactly one spot, over the bed, so I sleep in the chair by the window like a sensible corpse. There's a woodstove, a well, forty feet of dock rotting into the water, and a view down through eleven miles of bare winter trees to the lights of Calder's Bend, which at night look almost tender from here, the way a trap looks tender when it's baited with something soft.

Every evening I watch the town through binoculars. This is not madness. I want to be very clear about that, because Tessa Okafor once told me — back when we were both young enough to believe being believed was possible — that if she ever said what she saw, they'd call her mad. They did say it. She stopped saying it. I never stopped watching, because the alternative to vigilance isn't peace. The alternative is a shallow grave with your name spelled wrong on the marker, or no marker at all, which is what they gave me: no grave, just paper. A woman erased entirely on documents is safer than one buried under stone. Stone can be dug up. Paper just gets refiled.

So: binoculars. Every evening. The courthouse clock, the church steeple, the string of streetlamps along Front Street like a rosary someone dropped. And lately — this is the new thing, the thing that has me sleeping even less than usual — lately, a light in a bookshop window that stays on too late, and a man moving behind it with the particular restless gait of someone reading old files.

Someone is asking about me.

* * *

Let me tell you about the letters, since everyone assumes the dead don't write.

There are two hundred and six of them. I know the number because I count them every year on the anniversary, which is either a ritual or a wound; after twenty years the distinction stops mattering. They live in a tin biscuit box under the floorboard by the stove, and each one is addressed to someone who must never receive it.

There's one to my mother, who died believing her daughter drowned, and whose grief was real even if the reason wasn't, and who went to her grave praying for a body that was never in the river. That letter is nine pages. I wrote it the first winter, when the cold made honest. I've rewritten it maybe forty times since. It always ends the same way: *I couldn't come to the funeral because there wasn't one, and there wasn't one because there wasn't a body, and there wasn't a body because I was standing in the trees watching them lower an empty coffin of paperwork onto our family name.* You can't mail that. You can't mail any version of that. So it goes in the box.

There's one to Tessa, the barmaid who saw me run. Shorter. *You weren't mad. I'm sorry they made you think you were.* Fourteen words I have been unable to send for twenty years because sending them would require explaining how I know she saw me, and explaining requires existing, and existing requires the whole rotten apparatus of testimony and protection and lawyers, and I had none of those things. I had a canoe and a dead woman's cash — my own cash, technically, withdrawn from my own account three days before I "died," which is the kind of trick that only works once and only works on people who trust their ledgers more than their eyes.

And there's one to Aldous Vane. That one is one sentence long, and I will not repeat it here, except to say that it is not forgiving and it is not threatening and it lives somewhere between the two, in the country where the truth goes when nobody will buy a ticket to visit.

I write letters the way other people pray. Not because anyone's listening. Because the alternative is talking to the stove, and the stove has heard quite enough.

* * *

Here is what I know about the man in the bookshop, because knowing things is the only hobby I've been allowed to keep.

His name is Juniper Yarrow. Yes, really. A detective named after a shrub that grows in poor soil and burns well — I looked him up years ago, back when looking things up meant a bus ride to the county library and a wig I still miss. He was the one honest cop in a building where honesty was treated as a filing error. He worked the Hollis case — the whistleblower, the man Danny Vane put in the river two weeks before I supposedly joined him there. Yarrow got within a breath of the truth and then the file grew a seal and his career grew a slow leak, and by 2005 he'd quit rather than become furniture. I watched him leave town from the levee, actually. I was in the trees. I do a lot of my living in trees.

He came back five years ago and opened a bookshop, which is what honest ex-detectives do instead of drinking themselves into the river, and for a while I checked on him the way you check on a scar — occasionally, wincing, out of a responsibility I couldn't name. He was the last person who'd tried to find me before it became fatal to try. Somewhere in a sealed file there's a note in his handwriting that says *witness unaccounted for*, and I have thought about that note more times than I can count, because it was the closest thing to a headstone I ever got, and it said, in its way, *she might be alive.*

And now he's asking around. I know because the town's nervous system runs straight past my cabin whether it wants to or not. The ferryman mentioned a stranger paying triple rate for an unclosable case. The feed store man said a fellow was in asking after records from '03, polite as Sunday. And three days ago, a car I didn't recognize crawled the logging road below my property at walking speed, twice, and I lay on my floor in the dark with a shotgun across my knees doing arithmetic: if they've found me, the bookshop man led them here without knowing it, and if the bookshop man led them here, then someone in Calder's Bend has decided that twenty years of quiet is finally worth disturbing.

Or — and this is the thought I keep trying to starve out, the thought that keeps coming back fatter every time — or he's working for someone who wants me found alive. Which would be worse. Dead, I'm safe. Alive, I'm evidence, and evidence gets handled.

* * *

This morning I did something reckless, and I want it noted that I recognized it as reckless at the time and did it anyway, which is either courage or the particular insanity of the long-isolated, and honestly at my age I've stopped caring which.

I walked down to the mailbox.

Not my mailbox — I don't have one, dead women don't get mail, though I confess I once mailed myself a birthday card just to see my own name in an official hand, and then burned it unread, which tells you everything about the psychology of erasure. I mean the general store at the crossroads, eleven miles down, where the road remembers it's a road. I go twice a month for flour and lamp oil and the newspaper, wearing my hair gray now — I'm fifty-one and I earned every strand but I help the color along, because nobody looks hard at a gray-haired woman buying flour — and I buy my goods and I read the notice board and I leave.

But today, pinned to the board between a lost dog notice and a flyer for the solstice memorial — *TWENTY YEARS OF REMEMBRANCE, LENA MARSH, GONE BUT NOT FORGOTTEN*, which is the funniest thing I have read in two decades, since the entire point of their machinery was to make sure I was forgotten precisely and permanently — there was a classified page torn from the Calder's Bend Courier, and circled in pencil was a small ad.

*Seeking information regarding estate matters, Marsh family. Discretion assured. Inquire J.Y., above the bookshop, Front St.*

J.Y.

I stood in front of that notice board for perhaps four seconds, which is three seconds longer than a careful woman stands anywhere, and I felt something I hadn't felt since 2003, and it took me a moment to identify it because it's such a rare vintage: hope. Hope is a terrible thing to feel when you're dead. Hope says *maybe*, and maybe is a door, and doors open from both sides.

Then I bought my flour, and I walked out past the pump, and I saw the car.

Black, county plates, parked with the kind of studied casualness that means anything but. A man sat behind the wheel reading nothing. He looked at me the way men look at gray-haired women buying flour, which is to say he didn't, and I kept walking, heart going like a bird in a jar, and I did not turn down the logging road, and I took the long way home along the creek bed where no car follows, and I arrived at my cabin with my legs shaking and my mind perfectly, terribly clear.

They're sweeping. Whoever they are — Vane's people, and let's not pretend otherwise, the Vanes have been sweeping this valley for twenty years like it's their hearth — they're checking the roads, the crossings, the store. Something has spooked them. Something has made the dead girl suddenly worth finding, whether to bury her again or to finish the job the river declined to do.

Which means Juniper Yarrow's little advertisement isn't just a question anymore. It's a flare. And flares show everyone where you are, including the people you're signaling and the people you're hiding from.

* * *

Evening. Woodsmoke, lamplight, the binoculars warm from my hands. Downriver, Calder's Bend is dressing itself in memorial bunting, little white lanterns going up along the levee rail for the solstice, one for every year of my death. Twenty lanterns. Someone spent real money on the death of a woman who is currently eating beans out of a pot and complaining, to the stove, about the salt.

I should feel nothing. That's the discipline. Twenty years of discipline: feel nothing, want nothing, be weather. But tonight the discipline is slipping, and I know exactly why, and it's humiliating, so I'll say it plainly and get it over with.

I want to be found. Not caught — found. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole rest of my life. Caught is what happens when they get there first. Found is what happens when someone comes looking with clean hands and stays anyway. And I am so tired. God, I am tired in a way that sleep doesn't touch, tired of being a rumor in my own biography, tired of writing letters to no one, tired of loving people exclusively through binoculars and biscuit tins.

Tessa Okafor still works at the Anchor, nights. I can see the bar's windows from the ridge if the leaves are right. Some evenings I watch her carry trays and laugh at things I can't hear, and I think: *she's the only person alive who knows I exist, and she doesn't know she knows.* We are the loneliest pair in this valley and we've never managed to be lonely together.

If I answer the ad — and I haven't decided, I want that on the record, the record being me and the stove — if I answer, I do it the only way a dead woman safely can: sideways. A phrase. A test. Something only the real Lena Marsh would know, so that if Yarrow is honest, he'll understand, and if Yarrow is bait, he'll have nothing but a sentence he can't use.

I take out paper. My hand shakes, which annoys me. I write:

*Tell him: the witness remembers the whistle.*

Danny Hollis whistled while he worked the mill office, the night he died. Nobody living knows that but me and the man who killed him. If Yarrow is what I remember — the one who read files all the way to the bottom — he'll find it in the old statements, or he won't, and either way he'll know the sender knew things the river never told.

I fold the paper. Then I do what I always do.

I put it in the box.

Two hundred and seven.

I sit with the tin on my knees for a long time. Outside, the wind comes down the valley carrying, faintly, the sound of the town tuning itself for remembrance — a fiddle somewhere, a hammer on the bandstand. They're building a stage for my ghost. Twenty years of lanterns, and speeches, and the Vane family no doubt sending flowers with solemn cards, the way arsonists admire a good fire.

"Last chance to stay dead," I tell the stove.

The stove, being a stove, says nothing. But the wind shifts, and down through the black lace of the trees I see the bookshop light burning late again, stubborn as a pilot flame, and beside it — new tonight — a second silhouette at the window, still, patient, watching the street the way I watch the town.

Someone else is watching Juniper Yarrow.

And whatever happens next, it's happening fast, and I have until the solstice — six days, the same date they gave my death, because powerful men love symmetry the way I love silence — to decide whether Lena Marsh stays a letter addressed to no one, or becomes, at fifty-one, with gray hair and shaking hands and two hundred and seven unsent reasons, a woman who walks into a room and says:

*I'm here. Check your records.*

I blow out the lamp and sit in the dark with the tin box against my chest like a heartbeat I borrowed.

Six days.
