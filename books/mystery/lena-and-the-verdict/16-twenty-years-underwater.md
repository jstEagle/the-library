## 16. Twenty Years Underwater

The first thing you learn about being dead is that it's boring.

Nobody tells you that. The movies have it all wrong — no dramatic montages, no new identity in a sunlit city where you learn to love again. What there is, instead, is a cabin upriver with a woodstove that eats cordwood like a starving man, and a radio that only comes in clear when the frost sets in, and twenty years of mornings in which the first thought, before coffee, before anything, is: *still dead, then. Good.*

I've gotten so good at the thought that I sometimes say it out loud, to hear how it sounds. Still dead, then. Good. It has the cadence of a prayer. It has the warmth of a tax form.

My name is Lena Marsh, and I have been dead since the fourteenth of December, 2003, which is a lie I have maintained with more discipline than I ever maintained anything alive.

* * *

Here is what actually happened, since apparently someone is finally asking, and since I have spent two decades rehearsing the telling to an empty room and a kettle that never answers.

I was twenty-six. I worked the counter at Halloran's Pharmacy, which meant I knew everyone's business in Calder's Bend before they knew it themselves — who was buying antacids, who was buying the other thing, whose wife had switched shampoos, which is the town's most reliable tell of an affair. I liked the work. I liked knowing things. I did not yet know that knowing things was the most dangerous profession in this river town, more dangerous than logging, more dangerous than the barges, and I found out the hard way, in the ordinary way, by being in the wrong place at the right time.

It was a Tuesday in November. Cold enough that the river had that flat, hammered-metal look. I'd stayed late at Halloran's doing inventory because Mr. Halloran paid in cash and patience, and I was walking home along the levee when I cut through the yard behind the old Vane shipping office — the one they'd shuttered after the elder Vane died, the one everyone said was empty.

It wasn't empty.

There was a man I recognized — everyone recognized him; he had the family jaw and the family way of standing like the ground owed him rent — and there was another man I also recognized, a county man named Ellery Pike who had been making noise, the kind of noise people make when they've found something in a ledger that doesn't add up. Pike had been saying for months that the Vane shipping contracts were built on a verdict, that the family fortune had a body under it. People smiled at him the way you smile at a man who talks to pigeons.

Ellery Pike was not smiling. Ellery Pike was on his knees in the frozen mud, and the man with the family jaw was holding something, and I want to tell you I did something brave, that I shouted, that I ran for help. What I did was stand very still behind a stack of rotting pallets and watch a man die, and then I did the second bravest thing I have ever done, which was to keep standing there, memorizing everything — the coat, the car, the way the man wiped his hands on a rag like he was cleaning up after a chore — and then walk home without running, because running attracts eyes.

I got home. I wrote it all down. I called the sheriff's office, and I got a deputy who took my name, and I remember thinking, even then, *that was fast. That was too fast.* He didn't ask me to come in. He didn't ask questions. He wrote down my name and address and said someone would follow up, and I hung up the phone and looked at the piece of paper on my table with everything written down, and I thought: *I am a witness. That's what I am now. That's my whole new job.*

I was right about the job. I was wrong about the title.

* * *

They didn't come for me with guns. That's the part that took me years to understand, and it's the part that matters most, so listen.

They came for me with paper.

Ten days later, a woman I'd never seen came into Halloran's and asked me about aspirin, and I answered, and she left, and that was the last conversation I ever had as Lena Marsh. That night there was a car idling near my building — I saw it, I was already watching for cars — and a shape on the levee, and I did what I'd been planning since the night in the yard: I went out the back, I took the bus to the next county, and I started becoming no one.

I thought I was running ahead of a bullet. What I was actually running ahead of was a coroner's report.

I learned about my own death the way you learn about weather in another state — secondhand, from a radio in a diner outside Marlow Junction, three counties east, eating eggs I paid for with the cash under my floorboards. *Calder's Bend woman missing, feared drowned,* the announcer said, and gave my name, and gave the date, and gave the detail that made my eggs come back up: *authorities say the inquest has already concluded the death accidental.*

Already concluded. I'd been missing for four days. You can conclude a drowning that fast, I suppose, if the river is cooperative. But I hadn't drowned. I was sitting in a diner eating eggs. And the inquest — I knew enough about inquests to know they took months, that they had coroners, that coroners had to examine something, a body, at minimum a shoe.

There was no body. There was no shoe. There was a verdict, signed and sealed, and a town that closed around it like water over a stone.

That was when I understood the shape of it. They didn't need to kill me. Killing me would have created another witness, another body, another loose thread. They needed something better. They needed my testimony to be legally impossible. A living witness can recant, can come forward, can swear an oath. A dead one is furniture. So they made me furniture — filed me, stamped me, sealed me — and Ellery Pike's murder went into the same drawer, and the Vane fortune sat on top of the drawer like a paperweight, and the town went on buying its aspirin.

I have thought, over the years, about the moment they decided. Somewhere in a back room, some man — and it was a man, it's always a man with good shoes — said, *the girl saw it. What do we do about the girl?* And someone else, and I'd bet the cabin I know who, said the clever thing. The paper thing. Aldous Vane was the county clerk then. Twenty-six years old and already the kind of clerk who could make a person stop existing between lunch and supper. I imagine he said it almost gently. He has that voice. I've heard it since, at a distance, at the memorial they hold every year for me. *A tragedy,* he says, every solstice, into the microphone, about me. He has never once looked at the river while saying it. Guilty men look at crowds. Only the innocent look at water.

* * *

You want to know how you survive twenty years dead. I'll tell you, because the answer is shameful and I'm tired of shame being the only thing I own outright.

You survive by being small. You take work that's paid under tables — orchards in the fall, canneries in the summer, a winter once cleaning rooms at a lodge where nobody looked at the maid because looking at maids is beneath gentlemen. You learn which counties don't ask. You learn that a woman alone who keeps her eyes down is the most invisible creature in America. You learn to flinch from your own name — and that's the one that costs, that's the tax they collect annually. For years, if a stranger called out *Lena* in a market, my whole body would go to ice, because the name was a hook, and hooks are how dead things get pulled up.

I moved eleven times. I kept a rule: never the same town two winters. I kept a box — one box, always small enough to carry — with the things I couldn't burn: my mother's brooch, a photograph of my father I'd cut down to the size of a stamp so it would fit in a boot heel, and the notebook. The notebook with everything from the night in the yard, written in my twenty-six-year-old hand, in ink that has gone brown. The coat. The car, a dark sedan with a cracked windshield on the passenger side. The rag. The time, which I'd checked against the pharmacy clock on my way past: 9:40.

For twenty years that notebook was the only place in the world where I existed. Everything else — the verdict, the memorial, the grave they put up in the churchyard with my name on it, which I visited exactly once, at night, in my third year, and stood there like an idiot not knowing whether to laugh or weep — everything else said I was dead. The notebook said: *I was there. I saw. I remember.* One notebook against a sealed verdict and a judge's signature. I did the math on that fight a long time ago and it never once came out in my favor.

So I stayed under. That was the whole strategy. Stay under, stay small, outlive them if you can. I told myself it was patience. Some nights I told myself it was cowardice, and the truth, which I only admit to the woodstove, is that it was both, in about equal measure, and that the measure shifted year by year until patience was winning again, lately, for reasons I'll get to.

Because here is what nobody tells you about drowning, and I mean the metaphorical kind, the kind I did for two decades: you don't drown all at once. You drown by inches, annually, a little at a time. The first year you lose your address. The third year you lose your mother — she died in my sixth year dead, and I read about it in a paper two counties over, and I could not go to the funeral, because the dead don't attend funerals, not even their own mothers', and I sat in a bus station and did the crying there, quietly, between departures, and a woman offered me a tissue and asked if I'd lost someone, and I said yes, and it was even true. The tenth year you lose your laugh — you notice it's gone because you hear it coming out of you, thin and careful, a stranger's laugh. The fifteenth year you lose your face. I'd catch my reflection in a shop window and think, *who's that tired woman,* and it would take me a full second to remember she was me, and the me I remembered was twenty-six and had a whole life ahead of her that she never got to live because I was too busy keeping her dead.

That's the grief they never put in the verdict. They killed a witness. But the sentence I served wasn't death — it was the slow murder of everyone I would have been. The wife I might have been. The fool I might have made of myself at someone's wedding. The ordinary, visible, touchable life. I mourn her every December, on the anniversary, the same night the town lights candles for me on the levee. They mourn the girl who drowned. I mourn the woman who never got to exist. We light the same candles. It's the closest thing we have to a shared grave.

* * *

The letters started about nine years ago. Don't ask me why — grief does strange bookkeeping — but I began writing letters to people I had been, or might have been, and I never sent a single one. There's a shoebox of them now, four hundred and some, addressed to no one. *Dear the woman who works at Halloran's.* *Dear the girl who was going to take the nursing course in the spring.* *Dear whoever you'd be now.* I write one every solstice, like a report. Still dead, then. Still safe. Still the only one who remembers Ellery Pike's face, and the coat, and the rag, and the time — 9:40, and the cracked windshield, and I will carry that to whatever actual grave they eventually give me, because a witness who forgets is just a rumor, and I refuse to be a rumor.

I watch the town, some years. I tell myself it's reconnaissance. It's not. It's homesickness with binoculars. I've watched the memorial from the tree line above the levee — the candles, the choir, Aldous Vane in his good coat saying *a tragedy* into the microphone — and I have stood there hating him with a hatred so old it's gone smooth, like a stone in a pocket, and I have watched Tessa behind the bar of the Levee House through the window, older now, and I have wondered if she remembers the woman running along the levee that night, because I saw her see me. She looked right at me. And then the town spent twenty years telling her she was mad, and I know they did, because I've heard the way they say it — *Tessa's story, Tessa and her ghost* — and I let them do it. Every year I let them do it, because a ghost with a defender stops being a ghost, and I needed to be a ghost. I'm not proud. I'm alive, which is different, and smaller.

But lately the math changed. You can feel a statute like weather coming. Dev whatever-his-name-is — the young paralegal who's been pulling records, I've watched him too, the boy doesn't know how to be followed, he walks like a puppy — lately the records started moving. Someone was asking about the inquest. Someone had the coroner's log. Someone was pulling the thread, and the thread, I could see through the binoculars, was frayed in three places, because they'd made three different versions of my death and never reconciled them. Paper is honest that way. Paper always confesses eventually. It's people who lie well.

And then the man in the bookshop. I've watched him too. Yarrow. The detective — retired, they say, but you don't retire from that, you just stop being paid. He stands in his failing shop among books nobody buys and looks out the window like a man doing surveillance on his own life. And he started asking. And then his client vanished, and he didn't. That's the part that got me. Everyone in this town, when the water rises, gets in the water and goes under quietly. This man stood on the bank and started taking names.

I have spent twenty years believing that no one would ever come for me. Not rescue — I'd given up on rescue around year four — I mean come *for* me, come to find me, come to say *you're real, I read the paper, the paper lied.* I built a whole life on the certainty that I would die in some anonymous county under some other name and that the notebook would go in the box and the box would go in the ground and that would be the whole of it: a witness, unwitnessed.

And then three days ago, pinned to the levee rail, at the exact spot where I ran — the spot only I and one other living soul would look at — there was a note.

I've read it maybe two hundred times. It's nothing. It's a barmaid's handwriting on a napkin, four lines, half of it apology. But it says *I saw you,* and it says *I'm sorry it took so long,* and it says *he's asking after you, the bookseller, and I think he's true.*

He's asking after me. The dead girl from the verdict. The furniture. Somebody is finally doing the arithmetic I've been doing alone for twenty years, and the sum is coming out different this time, and I don't know if that's hope or if it's the sound of the water rising over my head at last.

The solstice is in nine days. The appeal — I know about the appeal now, I've read the filings through the binoculars of a borrowed library card — the window closes at midnight that night, and when it closes, everything Ellery Pike was killed for gets buried forever, and I get to stay dead on the record, permanently, with a judge's signature as my headstone.

Nine days. One notebook. One barmaid who finally looked. One bookseller who doesn't know I'm watching him back.

I took out the shoebox tonight and I wrote this year's letter, and for the first time in twenty years I didn't write it to a woman who might have been.

I wrote it to the bookseller. And at the bottom, where the signature goes, I wrote my name — my real one, the one that's been a hook and a grave and a stranger's laugh — and I sat there with the pen hovering, asking myself the only question that's ever mattered:

If I surface, will there be anyone on the bank to pull me out — or have I stayed under so long that I've forgotten how to breathe air?
