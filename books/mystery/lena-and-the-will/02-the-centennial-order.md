## 2. The Centennial Order

The notice arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were when Halloway's Hollow did its civic housekeeping — trash pickup, council meetings, the quiet reshuffling of other people's affairs.

I was elbow-deep in a liver when Mara Quist stuck her head into my autopsy suite without knocking, which she never did, because Mara had once watched me remove a brain and hadn't eaten lunch for two days afterward.

"You're going to want to sit down," she said.

"I'm standing over a seventy-year-old man whose heart gave out on a fishing boat. I'm already sitting down in every way that matters."

She held out an envelope. Heavy cream stock, embossed with the town seal — the oak tree split by lightning, which the chamber of commerce insisted was a symbol of resilience rather than an omen nobody had thought to take seriously. "Council meeting's tonight. You're on the agenda."

"Coroners don't get agendas. Coroners get blamed."

"It's about Edmund Halloway."

I set down my scalpel, which was as close to ceremony as I ever came. "What about him?"

"They're exhuming him." She said it lightly, the way you'd announce a bake sale. "Centennial reinterment. Marble tomb up on Founders' Hill, big ceremony before the anniversary dinner. The whole town's going to watch them lift the founder out of the ground and put him somewhere fancier."

"That's not how exhumation works," I said. "That's not even adjacent to how exhumation works."

"It's how it works when Vivian Halloway wants it to work."

* * *

The council chambers smelled of lemon polish and self-regard, same as they had when I was twelve years old and testified — badly, tearfully — about the night my mother went into the river. The same wood paneling. The same portrait of Edmund Halloway above the dais, painted from a photograph taken sometime around 1970: a stern man with a jaw like a fence rail and eyes that looked at you the way weather looks at a crop.

Vivian Halloway sat at the center of the council table like a queen holding court in exile. Ninety-one years old, spine straight as a surveyor's line, silver hair pinned with the precision of someone who had never once been surprised by her own reflection. She wore pearls. She always wore pearls. In Halloway's Hollow, pearls weren't jewelry; they were credentials.

"Coroner Delacroix," she said, when I took my seat at the folding table they'd provided for me, slightly below the level of the dais, which I noted and filed away. "How kind of you to come."

"You summoned me, Councilwoman. Kindness didn't enter into it."

A ripple of discomfort moved through the room — Councilman Pruitt adjusted his tie, the school board representative suddenly found her notes fascinating. Vivian smiled as though I'd said something charming.

"We'll keep this brief," she said. "As you know, next month marks one hundred years since our founder established this town. The centennial committee has planned a series of observances, culminating in the anniversary dinner. As part of these festivities, the council has voted to move Mr. Halloway's remains from the old cemetery to a new memorial tomb on Founders' Hill. A place of honor. A permanent monument to what he built."

She slid a folder across the table toward me. Permits. Site plans. An artist's rendering of the tomb — white marble, columns, an inscription already chosen: *EDMUND HALLOWAY, 1924–1985. HE GAVE US EVERYTHING.*

"He gave us everything," I repeated.

"The committee liked it."

"The committee has a generous relationship with the truth." I opened the folder anyway, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a document unread. That is both my profession and my affliction. "And the coroner's office is involved because...?"

"Because the disinterment requires your sign-off. Standard procedure."

It was standard procedure. That was the trap of Vivian Halloway — everything she did was standard procedure, right up until it wasn't. "Then I'll need the original burial records," I said. "Interment permit, transit documentation, and the death certificate. My office doesn't authorize moving remains without a complete chain of custody."

Something happened then. It was small — so small that anyone who wasn't watching for it would have missed it entirely. But I have spent twenty years watching people's faces for the moment the story slips its moorings, and I saw it: a flicker behind Vivian's eyes, gone before it finished arriving. Not surprise. Recognition.

"Of course," she said smoothly. "The records will be made available."

"Wonderful." I clicked my pen. "Let's do it now, actually. Saves us all a second meeting, and I know how everyone values their Tuesday evenings."

Pruitt laughed nervously. Nobody joined him.

"The archive would need notice—"

"Councilwoman." I kept my voice pleasant, almost apologetic, the voice I use when I'm about to ruin somebody's evening. "The man has been dead forty years. Whatever notice the archive needs, it's had time."

* * *

They produced the file within the hour, which told me it had been close at hand. That was the first thing I wrote down in my notebook, in the small cramped handwriting I reserve for things that matter: *File too accessible.*

The second thing I wrote down came twenty minutes later, after I'd laid the documents side by side on the folding table while the council pretended to discuss parking ordinances in the adjoining room.

The death certificate was signed March 14th, 1985. Cause of death: cardiac arrest, natural causes. Attending physician: Dr. Aldous Wren, since deceased. Signed, sealed, filed. Clean as a hymnal.

The burial permit was dated March 21st. Interment at Hollow Cemetery, Plot 114, recorded in the cemetery ledger in a careful clerk's hand. Also clean. Also filed.

But between them — between the certificate that said he died and the permit that said we buried him — there should have been a transit record. A removal permit. Something, anything, documenting the movement of a body from the place of death to the place of burial. For a private citizen, that paperwork is often thin. For the founder of the town, dead at sixty-one, with the state's eyes presumably attentive? Nothing. No transit record. No removal permit. No entry in any log between the doctor's signature and the gravedigger's shovel.

Seven days. A week-long gap in which Edmund Halloway existed only as a signature.

I've spent enough years among the dead to know that bodies are heavy, literal things. They require transport. They require witnesses. They leave paper trails the way rivers leave silt. A missing week isn't sloppiness. A missing week is a door held open.

"Councilwoman," I called through the doorway. "Who handled the funeral arrangements in 1985?"

Vivian appeared in the frame with the unhurried grace of a woman who had never once hurried anywhere in her life. "The family, of course. We kept it private. Father despised spectacle."

"No funeral home?"

"A family friend prepared him. It was a different era, Coroner. People cared for their own dead."

"People did," I agreed. "And then they buried them within three days, because that's what you do with a body. Yours took seven."

The lemon-polish silence stretched. Somewhere down the hall, a radiator knocked twice, like a fist on a door.

"There was a delay," Vivian said. "Grief, Coroner Delacroix. You may not be familiar with it, given your line of work, but it slows ordinary tasks."

I am familiar with grief. I have lived inside it for twenty years the way some people live inside a house they can't afford — familiar with every creaking stair. I thought of my mother's file, still sealed in the county archive where I'd requested it twice and been denied twice, and I felt the old cold thing turn over in my chest.

"Grief does slow things," I said. "It doesn't erase paperwork. There's no transit record. No removal permit. Nothing between Dr. Wren's signature and the interment. Either the founder of this town was buried illegally, or—" I stopped myself. I'd learned that much, at least. You don't finish sentences like that in front of Vivian Halloway. You let her do it for you.

"Or?" she prompted, mild as morning.

"Or nothing," I said. "Or I'll need a week to review the full file before I sign the disinterment order. Standard procedure."

Her smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a recalculation, quick and cold. "Take the time you need," she said. "Father waited forty years. He can wait a little longer."

* * *

Mara found me at my office afterward, where I was doing what I always do with documents that bother me: copying them. Twice. One set for the file, one set for the fireproof box under my floorboards that contains everything I've ever found suspicious in this town, which is to say, most of what I've found.

"You upset her," Mara said, leaning in the doorway. "Nobody upsets her. People apologize to her for things she did to them."

"She invited it. She handed me a folder full of holes and dared me to look."

"So what did you find?"

"A dead man with a missing week." I tapped the copies. "No transit record, no removal permit, no chain of custody. And a death certificate signed by Aldous Wren."

Mara frowned. "Why does that name—"

"Aldous Wren signed my mother's drowning report," I said. "Twenty years ago. Same signature. Same careful little loops. He certified Edmund Halloway dead of natural causes, and four months later he certified my mother drowned by accident, and then he retired to Scottsdale and died before anyone could ask him a single question in person."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Outside, the streetlights came on along Main Street, one by one, lighting the way to a centennial nobody knew was built on a gap.

"Orsen," she said carefully, "what are you saying?"

"I'm saying that when they open that coffin next month, I intend to be standing there with a camera, a scale, and absolutely no illusions." I closed the folder. "Because either Edmund Halloway is in that grave, or he isn't. And if he isn't, then somebody in this town has known for forty years — and they just gave me a signed invitation to find out."

Mara shifted her weight. "You know the county will claim jurisdiction the second you file an objection."

"Then I won't file one yet." I looked out at the dark street, at the banners already going up for the centennial — *100 YEARS OF HALLOWAY'S HOLLOW*, red and gold, cheerful as a lie. "Let them dig. Let the whole town watch. I want three hundred witnesses when that lid comes off."

"And if it's him in there?"

"Then I've lost a week of sleep and gained a marble tomb to admire." I picked up my coat. "But if it isn't him — if that coffin holds anything other than Edmund Halloway — then every document in this town gets read again. Every signature. Every date. Including the ones from twenty years ago."

Mara understood. Mara always understood; that was her gift and her burden. "Your mother's case," she said quietly.

"My mother's case," I said. "The river's been quiet a long time, Deputy. But rivers keep what they're given."

I locked the office behind us and walked home past the old cemetery, where Plot 114 sat in the shadow of the split oak, marked by a granite stone worn soft at the edges. Somebody had left fresh flowers. White lilies, arranged with care, still beaded with water.

Somebody, I noted in the mental ledger I keep beside the paper one, had visited the founder's grave very recently.

And tomorrow, I decided, I would find out exactly who held keys to the archive — because a file that accessible is a file somebody's been reading.

The coffin, whatever it held, had waited forty years.

It could wait one more month.

But I wasn't sure I could.
