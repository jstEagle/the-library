## 7. Ledger of Small Lies

The county records annex smelled like every government building I'd ever been in: old paper, older coffee, and the faint chemical sweetness of a carpet that had been cleaned once, in 1998, and never again. I signed the visitor log with my left hand — a habit from my academy days, when a deputy's signature was evidence and you learned to write like someone else on principle — and told the clerk at the desk I was pulling property transfers for a boundary dispute out past Miller's Creek.

It wasn't even a good lie. There was no boundary dispute out past Miller's Creek. But Dale at the front desk was sixty-two years old and had been filing things alphabetically by feel since before I was born, and he looked at my badge, then at my face, and decided whatever I was actually doing was above his pay grade. He waved me back toward the stacks with the weary benevolence of a man who had learned that curiosity was just overtime you didn't get paid for.

Here is what nobody tells you about owing someone your career: it doesn't fade. It doesn't amortize. Four years ago, Orsen Delacroix sat across a table from me and a review board and explained, in that flat, patient voice of theirs, exactly how a chain-of-custody error I'd made could have happened to anyone, and how the evidence itself was sound regardless, and how the case would survive without my testimony if it had to — and then they testified anyway, carefully, precisely, in a way that made my mistake look like a rounding error instead of what it was. The board gave me a written reprimand instead of termination. Orsen never mentioned it again. Not once. Not even when we were arguing about something else entirely, which we did regularly, because Orsen Delacroix argued the way water argues with stone: persistently, and without any visible need to win.

You don't forget a debt like that. You just wait for the invoice.

I'd known the invoice was coming since Tuesday, when I stood in an open grave with three hundred other people and watched a coffin that should have held the founder of Halloway's Hollow hold nothing but river stones instead. I'd watched Orsen's face while the crowd gasped around us. Most people gasp and then settle, like water finding its level. Orsen hadn't settled. Orsen had gone very still, the way a person goes still when a long-expected noise finally happens in an empty house — not surprised so much as *confirmed*.

And I'd seen something else, too, because watching people is most of my job and all of my habit. I'd seen Councilwoman Vivian Halloway watching Orsen. Not watching the coffin. Not watching the crowd. Watching Orsen, with an expression I can only describe as an accountant's expression — the look of a woman checking a figure against a ledger she keeps in her head, and finding, to her satisfaction or her alarm, that the number still balanced.

So here I was, in the annex, lying to Dale about Miller's Creek.

* * *

Payroll records live in the basement, because everything true lives in the basement. The ground floor is for the public-facing stuff — deeds, plats, the pretty copies with the ribbons and seals. The basement is where the working documents go: the carbon copies, the triplicates, the tax filings, the wage ledgers. Nobody visits the basement unless they want to know something somebody else would prefer they didn't.

Halloway Timber's employment records had been donated to the county in 2009, when the company finally folded — "donated," in the corporate sense of *we are legally required to keep these somewhere and our new owners would rather burn them*. Eleven boxes. I pulled the ones covering 1983 through 1989 and carried them to the reading table under the flickering fluorescent tube that buzzed like it was trying to tell me something.

My plan, such as it was, was simple. Three people had died in this town in ways that got filed under *accident*, and Orsen had spent years quietly proving each one was nothing of the kind. I knew their names because everybody in Halloway's Hollow knew their names; we just weren't supposed to say them together in one sentence. Raymond Tuller, dead in 1984, fell from a catwalk at the mill. Dolores Finch, dead in 1986, carbon monoxide in a sealed garage. Walter Brice, dead in 1988, drowned in the shallows of the Kettle River where a grown man would have to work hard to drown.

Orsen had proven — quietly, forensically, in paperwork nobody read — that each death bore fingerprints. A bolt that had been filed half through. A vent deliberately taped. Bruising on Brice's collarbone consistent with being held down. Each finding buried in an addendum, each addendum buried in a file, each file buried in a cabinet in a building where nobody goes looking for anything they don't already believe.

What I wanted to know was simpler than method. I wanted to know *why*. Motive is the skeleton key. Method you can argue about forever — accidents happen, people fall, garages seal themselves. But motive is arithmetic. Motive either adds up or it doesn't.

So I started with payroll.

Raymond Tuller first. I found him in the 1984 wage ledger, third box, and there he was, week after week, forty hours, time-and-a-half during the autumn push. Ordinary. A man earning a living. I ran my finger down the entries until I reached October, and then I stopped, because the ordinary ended.

In the last six weeks of Raymond Tuller's life, he'd worked almost no regular hours. Instead, line after line of special notation: *doc prep*, *doc prep*, *records transport*, *doc prep*. Overtime rates. And on the final entry, dated nine days before he fell from that catwalk, a payment coded to an account I had to look up twice to believe: *estate services*.

Estate services. For a man who wasn't dead yet.

Dolores Finch was next, and I'll admit my hands weren't steady when I opened the 1986 ledger. Because if Tuller was a fluke, fine. Towns are full of flukes. But Dolores Finch's last five weeks read like a photocopy of Tuller's last six. *Doc prep.* *Notary liaison.* *Records transport.* She'd been the office manager, so maybe that was normal for her — except I checked her prior year, and the year before that, and in four years of employment Dolores Finch had never once been paid for doc prep. Then, in the last month of her life, she was paid for it eleven times.

Walter Brice. 1988. Same pattern, same codes, same eerie acceleration as the end approached. Brice had been a millwright his whole life — hands like baseball mitts, according to everyone who remembered him — and in his final weeks Halloway Timber was paying him, at clerical rates, to prepare documents.

Three deaths. Three men and women with no connection to each other except one thing: in the last weeks of their lives, they were all handling paper for Halloway Timber. Not timber. Paper.

* * *

I sat in the basement for a long time after that, listening to the fluorescent tube buzz and letting the shape of it form.

Because here's the thing about patterns: they're only meaningful if they're impossible. Coincidence is one person. Coincidence is two people, if you squint. Three people, all employed to handle documents in the final weeks of their lives, all dead within weeks of finishing the job — that's not coincidence. That's a schedule.

But a schedule needs a subject. What documents? Doc prep for what?

That's when I went upstairs and lied to Dale about land records instead.

Land transfers in Halloway County run through the recorder's office, and the recorder's office indexes by grantor, which means if you know whose name to look under, the history of the county unspools for you like thread off a spool. I looked under Halloway. Edmund Halloway, founder, patriarch, the man in the empty coffin. His holdings were vast and, per the legend every schoolchild learned, generously distributed — parcels deeded to the town, to the church, to families who'd served the mill faithfully for generations.

Generously distributed. That's what the plaque outside the library says. *EDMUND HALLOWAY, WHO GAVE THE LAND BENEATH OUR FEET.*

I pulled the transfer index for 1983 through 1989 and started matching dates against my payroll notes.

Raymond Tuller died October 14th, 1984. On October 2nd, twelve days earlier, a parcel deed bearing Raymond Tuller's signature as witness was recorded — a transfer of nearly two hundred acres of riverfront timberland from Edmund Halloway's personal holding into Halloway Timber's corporate name. Witnessed by R. Tuller. Notarized by D. Finch.

I stared at that line for a long time.

Then I kept going, because once you see the machine you have to watch it run. Dolores Finch died March 9th, 1986. On February 21st, sixteen days before, another transfer recorded — this one moving the mineral rights under the north ridge, witnessed by D. Finch, notarized by W. Brice. And Walter Brice died August 30th, 1988, and on August 19th, eleven days before, the largest transfer of the lot: the entire eastern tract, four hundred acres, witnessed by W. Brice himself, notarized by an out-of-county notary I wrote down the name of because it was the first thing in the whole chain that didn't fit the pattern, and the things that don't fit are always the important ones.

Every major asset consolidation in Halloway Timber's history — the moves that turned a family company into a family fortress — had been witnessed and notarized by people who were dead within the month.

They weren't employees handling paperwork. They were witnesses. Living, breathing, sworn witnesses to transactions that somebody needed certified and then needed silent. And when the transactions were done, when the ink was dry and the documents were locked away, the witnesses stopped being assets and started being liabilities.

Cleanup. That's the word that came up out of my gut like bile. This wasn't greed running alongside murder. Greed doesn't need to kill its own notaries. This was *cleanup* — the cold, systematic erasure of everyone who could testify to what the papers actually said.

And if they'd clean up witnesses to land transfers, what would they do to the executor of a will?

I didn't know about any executor then. That word didn't exist for me yet. But I knew about Orsen's mother — everybody of a certain age in this town knows about Lena Delacroix, the coroner's daughter who went into the Kettle River in 1985 and stayed there, and how the ruling came down *accidental drowning* so fast and so smooth that some people swore the ink was wet before her body was cold. I knew Orsen had spent twenty years picking at that ruling the way you pick at a scab you can't leave alone and can't make heal.

And I knew — sitting in that basement, with the wage ledgers open in front of me and the transfer index photocopies stacked beside them — that 1985 sat right in the middle of my pattern. Between Tuller and Finch. Dead center.

* * *

I photocopied everything. It took two hours, and Dale came down once to ask if I'd found what I needed for Miller's Creek, and I said yes, thank you, almost done, and he nodded and left, and I felt the lie sit in my chest like a swallowed stone. I'm a bad liar. It's why I'm a good cop, mostly — liars make lousy investigators because they spend all their attention managing the story instead of reading it. But I lied to Dale, and I lied to the logbook, and I carried my folder of photocopies out to my truck like it was contraband, which, in a town like this one, it probably was.

I sat in the cab for ten minutes before I started the engine.

Here was my problem, laid out plain on the seat beside me. Everything I had was circumstantial. Payroll codes and notarization dates don't convict anybody; they don't even accuse anybody, really. A defense attorney would stand up in court and explain doc prep and estate services until the jury's eyes glazed over, and the jury would walk out believing three people had simply died at their desks, as people do. What I had was a pattern, and patterns are whispers, and whispers get people like me reassigned to traffic duty in counties far from home.

What I needed was a document. One piece of paper that tied the deaths to the transfers to whatever it was all protecting. And I had a pretty good idea where such a paper might live, because there was exactly one transaction in this whole town that everybody treated as scripture and nobody had ever examined: the last will and testament of Edmund Halloway, the founding document, the one that built the legend, the one that had been read aloud at every anniversary dinner for forty years.

The will that belonged to a man whose coffin was full of rocks.

I thought about taking it to Orsen right then. Drive straight to the coroner's office, slap the folder on the desk, say *look at this, look at the pattern, tell me I'm crazy.* And part of me wanted to — wanted it the way you want to hand a hot coal to someone else so your own hands stop burning.

But I didn't. And I want to be honest about why, because honesty is the whole point of this story eventually, mine included. I didn't take it to Orsen because I wasn't sure yet what I was handing over. If I brought Orsen a pattern and the pattern turned out to be grief wearing a detective's coat — if I was seeing cleanup because I owed Orsen and debts make you credulous — then I'd be feeding the loneliest person I know a reason to keep digging, and digging in this town has a body count. Three bodies that I now knew about. Possibly more.

So I did the cowardly, careful thing. I drove home, put the folder in the firebox under my floorboards where I keep the things that matter, and told myself I'd sleep on it.

I slept maybe three hours. Every time I closed my eyes I saw that ledger line: *estate services*, nine days before Raymond Tuller learned to fly off a catwalk. Estate services. Somebody in that office had been preparing for a death that hadn't happened yet, and paying for it out of petty cash.

In the morning I called the state archive and requested the original 1985 will for inspection — proper channels, official request, my badge number on the form. If the will was genuine, my pattern meant nothing and I'd apologize to the legend. If the will was forged, then every death in my folder had a single author, and the author was still alive, still revered, still pouring tea for the town every June.

The archive clerk called me back at noon. Friendly voice. Bad news.

"Deputy Quist? That item's unavailable. Checked out last week."

"Checked out," I repeated. "By whom?"

A pause. Papers shuffling somewhere in Harrisburg or wherever the voice lived. "No checkout name logged. Just an authorization code." Another pause, longer. "From the Halloway estate."

I hung up and stood in my kitchen holding a phone that suddenly weighed forty pounds.

Somebody had gotten to the will before me. Somebody with the authority to sign the estate's name — which narrowed the field to exactly the people my folder said were cleaning house. And whoever it was had taken the founding document of Halloway's Hollow out of the public record one week after the founder's coffin turned out to be empty, which meant they weren't trying to hide the past anymore.

They were trying to hide it faster.

I got my keys. Orsen was going to hear about this today, pattern or no pattern, coal or no coal — because the window between *knowing too much* and *being known to know* was closing, and I had watched enough men die in ledgers to recognize the sound of it shutting.
