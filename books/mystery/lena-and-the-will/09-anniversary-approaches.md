## 9. Anniversary Approaches

The invitations arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day towns do their damage — council meetings, court dockets, the small bureaucratic machinery that grinds people into line items. Halloway's Hollow had chosen Tuesday to invite three hundred of its citizens to celebrate one hundred years of itself, and me to attend.

The invitation was heavy cream stock, embossed with the Halloway Timber crest: an oak tree split by a lightning bolt, which in retrospect was either honest or deeply ironic. *You are cordially invited to the Centennial Anniversary Dinner honoring the legacy of Edmund Halloway, founder.* Beneath it, in smaller type, as if the printers had been embarrassed: *Black tie. Founders' Hall. Saturday next.*

I set it on my kitchen table next to my mother's drowning file, which had taken up permanent residence there like a cat that refuses to be put out. The two documents looked at each other across the wood grain. One celebrated a dead man. The other described how a living woman had been found face-down in the Miller's Bend shallows on a June morning twenty years ago, ruled accidental by a coroner named Aldous Prentiss, who had certified Edmund Halloway's death eleven years before that and then died himself with a convenience I've come to regard as professionally suspicious.

"Legacy," I said to the empty kitchen. My kitchen hears a lot from me these days. It's the only relationship I maintain with any consistency.

The phone rang. Mara Quist.

"You got one too," she said. Not a question.

"Founders' Hall. Black tie. I don't own black tie."

"Borrow something. You're going."

"Am I?"

"You're going," she repeated, "because if you don't, they'll read your absence like a confession, and because Vivian Halloway personally asked the council to make sure you were invited. Personally, Orsen. The matriarch doesn't do personal unless she wants an audience."

That was true, and I hated how much comfort I took from Mara knowing it. After tea at the Halloway house — after *let the founder rest, dear* delivered over porcelain thin enough to see light through — I'd spent three nights telling myself Vivian's mention of my mother had been coincidence, the idle cruelty of an old woman who'd forgotten what she knew. Three nights is about my limit for self-deception. On the fourth I'd gotten up at 2 a.m. and written her name on an index card and pinned it above my desk where I keep the names of things I refuse to explain away.

"I'll go," I said. "But I'm not wearing a tie I can't get out of fast."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's said to me all week."

Mara hangs up without goodbyes. It's a deputy thing. Or possibly a Mara thing. With her the categories have merged.

* * *

The town, meanwhile, had gone mad in the way small towns do when given permission to decorate.

Bunting went up along Main Street overnight — green and gold, the Halloway colors, though everyone called them "the town colors" with the same straight face they used for "the town founder" and "the town legend." The historical society mounted photographs in the library window: Edmund Halloway in his forties, beard like a storm front, standing in front of the first mill. Edmund older, silvered, at some ribbon-cutting. Edmund's portrait, painted posthumously from those photographs, hanging now above the fireplace in Founders' Hall where it had hung for forty years, watching every dance and funeral luncheon with painted eyes that knew something the painter hadn't.

I know this because I stood outside that window Wednesday evening for a long time, looking at the photographs, looking for what Silas's tin had shown me: Edmund alive in 1991, four years after his certified death, thinner, clean-shaven, squinting into coastal light. Once you've seen a man's real face, the official ones look like costume.

"You're blocking the display," said Mrs. Tandy Osgood, who has run the historical society since before I was born and considers me a fixture of approximately equal status to the drinking fountain.

"Just admiring the founder."

"He was quite a man." She said it the way people say *he was quite a man* when they mean *don't ask questions*. "Your mother would be proud, you know. Of the ceremony. She loved the centennial planning — we were going to have her on the committee."

My mother died twenty years ago. The centennial committee formed eight months ago. I let the lie sit there between us, unchallenged, because challenging it would tell Tandy Osgood exactly how much I remembered, and I wasn't ready to spend that coin.

"She did love this town," I said, which was true, and which cost me something to say, and which Tandy received with a nod of satisfaction, filing it under *the Delacroix boy is settled*.

Gossip moved through the town that week like weather. At the diner, at the hardware store, in the church parking lot: the dinner menu (trout, naturally, and a cake shaped like the mill), the seating chart (Vivian at the head, the county commissioners flanking), the rumor that a journalist from the *Capital Gazette* had requested press credentials, and the counter-rumor that the council had declined him. The town wanted its hundredth birthday to be perfect. Perfection, in Halloway's Hollow, has always been less a condition than a project — something maintained, defended, paid for.

I wondered, watching them string lights, how many of the three hundred invited guests had any idea what the celebration was actually papering over. I decided: none. That was the genius of it. You don't need three hundred conspirators. You need three, and a story so old and so load-bearing that tearing it down feels like sacrilege rather than correction.

* * *

Mara's week went worse than mine, which she later described to me in the flat voice she uses for things that frighten her.

It started Monday, when she pulled the payroll ledgers again — third time through them, because repetition is how you catch your own errors, and because the numbers kept getting worse the more honestly she read them. Dale Pruitt, dead in the logging accident of '94. Raymond Ochs, dead in the truck rollover of '99. Both notarized documents for Halloway Timber in their final weeks. Both signed away mineral rights, easements, water access — the unglamorous plumbing of an empire — weeks before dying in ways the county called bad luck.

And now, cross-referencing land transfers against the assessor's rolls, a third name she hadn't expected: her own uncle, Walt Quist, who'd sold his river parcel to Halloway Timber in 2001 at a price so low it could only be called a donation with paperwork, and who'd stopped speaking to her family six months later and never said why. He lived in a trailer outside Corvane now. He drank. When she'd been a kid he'd been the fun one.

She sat with that ledger open for forty minutes before she closed it.

Tuesday brought the county detectives back — Detective Rourke and his silent partner, whose name might have been Voss or might have been nothing at all. They commandeered the conference room and spread the empty-grave file across it like a poker hand they didn't want seen.

"Quist," Rourke said, catching her in the hallway. "The Delacroix situation. We're aware there's a — friendship."

"Colleague."

"We're aware there's a colleague-ship. The empty grave is county jurisdiction. If the coroner's office has developed theories, we'd appreciate hearing them through channels."

"Orsen hasn't shared theories with me."

Rourke smiled. He had the smile of a man who'd learned it from a manual. "Good. Keep it that way until Saturday. After the dinner, everything loosens up. Politics." He said *politics* the way other men say *weather* — as an explanation that ends conversation.

She should have left it there. She knows she should have left it there. But Wednesday afternoon, alone in records, she pulled the index card for the original will — Estate of Edmund Halloway, 1985 instrument, state archive deposit — and made a photocopy of the card itself, front and back, and folded it into her boot, because boots are where Mara Quist keeps the things she isn't ready to be caught holding. She'd been doing it since the academy. It had saved her career once already, the night Orsen's testimony kept her from being the fall person for an evidence-handling scandal that went three ranks above her. She owed a debt she'd never figured out how to price. The index card weighed maybe a gram. It felt like a piano.

* * *

Thursday I drove to the state archive in the capital, two hours east through country that flattened out and stopped pretending to be picturesque. I'd filed the request a week earlier — certified copy request, Estate of Edmund Halloway, original 1985 will, for forensic examination — and the confirmation letter had come back with a date and a time and the bland efficiency of institutions that believe paper is neutral.

Paper is never neutral. Paper is where people hide.

The archive reading room smelled of dust and climate control. A woman named Ms. Ferro, glasses on a chain, took my request slip and disappeared into the stacks for longer than retrieval takes. When she came back she was carrying not a document box but a ledger, and her expression had shifted from institutional boredom to something more careful.

"Dr. Delacroix. The item you requested — the 1985 will, original instrument, deposited 1998 upon probate closure." She turned the ledger toward me. "It's not available."

"Not available."

"It was checked out last week." Her finger rested on a line in the register. Date: nine days ago. Authorized signature required for release of estate materials held in trust. There was a signature in the authorization column, a confident, looping hand I didn't recognize, and beside it, in the printed field reserved for the requester's affiliation, a single word:

*Halloway.*

"Checked out by whom?" I asked. "There has to be a name. An individual. Estate materials don't circulate to families — they're held in trust precisely so they can't—"

"The authorization came through counsel," Ms. Ferro said. "Estate counsel. Which entitles the holder to review. It's unusual," she added, and I gave her credit for the admission, "for materials to leave the building at all. But the signature is valid. The bond posted is valid. I checked twice myself, after your letter came in, because I thought perhaps we'd made an error."

"And did you?"

"No." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "We made no error. Someone with excellent credentials and a great deal of money asked for that document, and our rules say yes."

"When is it due back?"

"Thirty days from checkout." She did the arithmetic aloud, because she was kind, or because she wanted me to hear it. "Which falls the Sunday after your town's anniversary dinner. I saw the newspaper piece. Everyone's very excited."

Everyone's very excited. I thanked her. I walked out past the granite columns and sat in my car in the archive parking lot with the engine off and my hands on the wheel, doing my own arithmetic.

Nine days ago, someone authorized by the Halloway estate — by *counsel*, by *bond*, by machinery too expensive for a town of four thousand to own — walked into this building and removed the single most incriminating document in the county's history. Not destroyed it, not yet. Checked it out. Legally. Which meant they were either confident enough to keep it, or careful enough to want it somewhere it could be burned without a fire report.

Either way, they'd done it nine days ago. Before I'd even finished my comparison of the two hands on the probate copy. Before Silas pressed the tin into mine. Which meant someone had known I was coming before I knew it myself.

I thought about Vivian's tea. *Let the founder rest, dear.* I thought about the hairpin I'd found in the records hall annex, silver and jet, the kind of thing a woman wears to be underestimated. I thought about Rourke telling Mara that everything loosens up after Saturday.

Everything loosens up after Saturday. Yes. I'd begun to suspect that was the plan — that the dinner wasn't a celebration but a deadline, the last night the story held, after which whatever remained of the truth would be ash in a legal fireplace and the centennial would seal it under marble.

I started the car. Two hours home, headlights eating the dark, and somewhere behind me in that climate-controlled room a ledger lay closed with a signature in it that I couldn't identify and couldn't stop seeing — a confident, looping hand, belonging to somebody who had signed for a dead man's will the way you sign for a package.

Saturday, three hundred people would raise their glasses to Edmund Halloway.

I intended to be holding something better than a glass.

* * *

Friday night, Mara called again. No greeting.

"The will," she said. "You requested it from the state archive. Don't ask how I know."

"Mara—"

"It's not there, Orsen. It was checked out nine days ago under estate authority. And here's the part I shouldn't tell you, so listen fast." A breath. "I pulled the checkout authorization through inter-agency records access. The counsel of record — the firm that posted the bond — is Calloway, Prentiss & Rowe."

Prentiss. Aldous Prentiss, the coroner who certified both deaths, had a nephew in estate law. I'd noted it weeks ago and filed it under *coincidence*, that drawer where I keep things I'm not ready for.

"The same firm," Mara said, "that handled the Halloway estate since 1985. The same firm whose senior partner signs off on Halloway Timber's annual filings. Orsen — whoever checked out that will didn't need to steal it. They own the lock on the box." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into the register she uses for the things that scare her. "And the return date is the Sunday after the dinner. Whatever happens Saturday night — that's the last chance anybody gets."

Outside my window, Main Street glowed with centennial bunting, green and gold, strung tight as a garrote.

"Then I guess," I said, "we'd better make Saturday count."
