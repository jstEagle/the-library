## 14. Silas Talks

I have been rehearsing this confession for forty years, which is either admirable discipline or a symptom, depending on who you ask. My wife, before she passed, would have said symptom. She caught me talking to myself in the garden shed more than once, going over that night at the train station like a man polishing a stone he couldn't put down.

The trouble with keeping a secret that long is that it stops being a secret and starts being furniture. You walk around it every day. You dust it. You forget it's there until somebody new moves into the house and asks why the living room has such an odd shape.

Orsen Delacroix was the somebody new. Except they weren't new at all — they were the coroner's daughter grown tall and careful, with their mother's same way of looking at you like you were a document with a forgery somewhere on page three. When Silas Hargrove pressed that rusted tin into their hands after the memorial service, I watched their face do something I hadn't seen since 1985: hope with its teeth bared.

They came to my house the next morning. Of course they did. I'd have been insulted if they hadn't.

* * *

I made coffee because my hands needed a job that wasn't shaking. Orsen sat at my kitchen table — the same table where Edmund Halloway had once eaten eggs and complained about his children like any other rich man — and set the tin between us with the reverence people usually reserve for communion wafers.

"Tell me about the photograph," Orsen said.

"No pleasantries? No 'how's your hip, Silas'?"

"How's your hip?"

"Lying to me," I said, "is what keeps it young." I poured two cups. "All right. You want the night. I'll give you the night. But I want you to understand something first."

Orsen waited. Patient. Their mother's patience, God help us both.

"I didn't keep quiet because I was paid. People will assume that, when this comes out. Vivian will say it, if she's smart enough to say anything at all. I kept quiet because I loved this town." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "That sounds like the opening line of every coward's memoir ever written, doesn't it? But it's true, and the truth gets to be ugly even when it's mine."

"Mr. Hargrove—"

"Silas. You're about to hear forty years of my sins; we can skip the mister."

"Silas." They wrapped both hands around the coffee cup like it might try to escape. "I've spent two years proving that three men died as murders dressed as accidents. I found river stones in a founder's coffin. Somebody razored pages out of a county archive while wearing my grandmother's hairpin style. My mother drowned the week she was named executor of a will nobody can find. I don't need you to be brave. I need you to be accurate."

Well. There it was. Accuracy. The one currency I had left.

* * *

It was October of 1985, the third week, cold enough that your breath showed but not cold enough for anyone to feel sorry about it. Edmund Halloway sent for me at dusk. Not called — sent. His housekeeper, a nervous girl named Patty whose name I still remember because she quit the next morning and never told anyone why, brought me a note in his own hand: *Come around back. Bring the truck. Tell no one.*

Now, when the richest man in three counties says *tell no one*, a sensible man tells everyone. That's the joke of it. That's the whole joke of small towns — we survive by gossip, and the one time gossip could have saved lives, everybody involved had learned to be discreet.

I drove around back of the Halloway house with my flatbed, expecting furniture, maybe a body — I'll admit it, I expected a body, because rich families only go around back when there's something they don't want the front door to see. Instead there was Edmund himself, seventy-one years old, standing on the loading dock in a brown coat I'd never seen him wear, holding a leather satchel like a schoolboy running off to see the ocean.

"You look terrible, Silas," he said.

"You look like a man committing a crime."

"I look like a man escaping one." And then he laughed — that big rolling laugh from the photographs in the library, the one they'd chiseled onto the base of his statue. Hearing it out there in the dark, with no audience, no occasion, I understood suddenly that everything public about Edmund Halloway had been a performance, and here, finally, was the man offstage.

He climbed into the cab beside me instead of the bed, which told me this wasn't cargo. This was a passenger. He gave me directions to the Larchmont station — forty minutes east, past the county line, a depot so small the trains stopped twice a day out of politeness rather than schedule.

We didn't talk for the first ten miles. Then he said, "Do you know what my children have done this year?"

I knew some. Everybody knew some. Halloway Timber had been eating land like a locust season — the Delacroix bottomland, the Pruitt acreage, old man Corbett's orchard. Men signed papers and then died in ways the coroner called accidents. I want to be honest with you, since honesty is apparently the theme of my evening: even then, even before, we all felt it. The town felt it the way a dog feels weather. We just didn't have the words, or the spine.

"They've been getting signatures," Edmund said. "Mine among them. Do you know how easy it is to sign a dead man's name? You practice the loop of the *d*. That's all. One letter, practiced enough, and lawyers nod along, because lawyers want to nod. Nodding is how they bill."

"The will," I said. Because by then the whole town knew the founder's will was the holiest document in the county, read aloud, framed, quoted at graduations.

"The will is real," he said. "That's the tragedy of it. The will they read at the funeral is real paper, real ink, my real name — and none of it true. A man can tell the truth with a forged signature just as easily as a lie. What matters is which version got signed, and when, and who was standing in the room." He looked out the window at the dark fields going by. "My daughter stood in the room, Silas."

I nearly ran us into a fence post.

"Vivian," he said, and said it gently, which frightened me worse than shouting would have. "Vivian held the pen. Her brother held the door. And I sat in my study like a fool, drinking the brandy they poured me, signing whatever was put in front of me because I was tired and vain and wanted to believe my children loved me more than they loved the timber rights." He turned to me. "So I wrote another one."

"A second will."

"A true one. Dated properly, witnessed properly, sealed properly. Naming an executor who owed my family nothing and feared nothing, as far as I could tell." He reached into the satchel and took out an envelope, heavy stock, wax-sealed, and held it in both hands like a bird he wasn't sure would fly. "I addressed it myself."

"To whom?" I asked, though the road was dark and I should have watched it.

He told me. And I have carried that name for forty years, and I am going to carry it right up until the moment I hand it to the person sitting across my kitchen table, because that is the entire point of tonight — but let me finish the night first, the way it happened, because the order of things matters. Confession isn't just content. It's sequence.

* * *

At the Larchmont station we sat on the bench under the one lamp, waiting for the nine-forty northbound, and Edmund Halloway talked like a man paying off debts in a hurry. He told me he'd been planning it for a year. He told me the death certificate had been arranged through a doctor in the next county who owed the family more than money — grief makes strange collateral. He told me the coffin would hold stones because stones were honest, at least; stones never pretended to be anything else.

"Why the station?" I asked him. "Why not just die quietly in your own house, the way rich men are supposed to?"

"Because dying in my house means my children bury me," he said. "And everything I am becomes theirs to edit. If I leave—" he patted the satchel "—then somewhere out there is a version of me they can't burn. An original. Every archive needs an original, Silas. You taught me that, actually."

"Me?"

"That flood of '78. Your records shed washed away and you spent that whole winter rebuilding every deed and bill of sale from memory and duplicates. I watched you do it. I thought: now there's a man who understands that paper is the only immortality worth trusting." He smiled. "You think nobody notices the groundskeeper. Everybody notices the groundskeeper. That's the whole trick of being one."

The train came in hissing like a kettle. He stood, and for a moment — I'll swear this on anything you like — for a moment he looked afraid. Not of his children. Not of dying. Afraid of the smallness of it. Seventy-one years old, stepping onto a two-car local with a satchel, erasing himself.

"Edmund," I said, "what do I tell people?"

"Nothing," he said. "Tell them nothing for as long as you can bear it. And when you can't bear it anymore—" he pressed the envelope into my hands, closed my fingers over the wax seal with his own, which were cold as creek rocks "—give this to her. Only her. Not the sheriff, not the lawyers, not the church. Her. She'll know what to do with it. Her father raised her right, and I intend to test that from beyond my own grave, which is more than most men get to attempt."

The whistle blew. He stepped up. At the door he turned and said the last thing he ever said to me, and I have repeated it to myself so many times over the decades that it's worn smooth as sea glass:

"Loyalty built this town, Silas. But loyalty without truth is just a longer word for silence."

Then the doors shut, and the lamps swung, and the nine-forty took Edmund Halloway north to a coastal parish he'd named to me once and I swore I'd never forget, and I drove home through the dark holding an envelope addressed to the coroner's daughter, feeling like the richest and poorest man in Halloway's Hollow simultaneously.

* * *

Here is the part I am not proud of. Here is the part where the wry old groundskeeper routine falls apart and you see what's actually underneath, which is a man who failed.

I meant to deliver it. I want that on the record, however much record ends up existing. I drove past the Delacroix house three times that week. Three times. First time, there were sheriff's cars out front — the drowning had just happened, though I didn't know yet what the cars meant. Second time, the blinds were drawn and a neighbor's casserole dish sat on the porch like a headstone with a lid. Third time, I got as far as the gate.

And then I saw Vivian Halloway's car parked across the street.

Black sedan, engine off, a woman in the driver's seat who was not Vivian but wore Vivian's coat. Watching the house. Watching whoever came and went. And I understood — slowly, the way understanding comes to men like me, arriving late and unwelcome — that the envelope in my glove box was not a delivery. It was a detonator. Whoever carried it became the explosion.

So I went home. I put it in a tin — a Prince Albert tin, tobacco, the kind every man of my generation owns six of — and I put the tin in the dry well behind my shed, under a board, under dirt, under forty years of telling myself *next month. Next spring. When the girl's older. When it's safer. When Vivian dies.*

Vivian did not die. Vivian chaired the centennial committee. Vivian shook my hand at every Founders' Day picnic and thanked me for my service to the family, and every handshake was a key turning in a lock I'd built myself.

Forty years. I dusted that secret like furniture. I walked around it every day.

* * *

Orsen hadn't interrupted once. That's the thing about them — they listen the way their mother must have listened, completely, like your words were evidence and they intended to preserve the chain of custody. When I finished, the coffee had gone cold in both cups and neither of us had touched it.

"The envelope," Orsen said. "The one Edmund gave you. Is it in the tin?"

"The tin has the envelope and the photograph. The photograph you've seen — I put that in later, maybe fifteen years ago, when I finally worked up the nerve to visit him. He was alive, Orsen. Living under another name, tending a garden smaller than my shed, happier than I have ever seen a human being. He asked me one question: *Has the truth come home yet?* And I lied to him and said soon." My voice went sideways on that word and I let it. "I lied to a dying man about his own will. Add it to the list."

"What was in the envelope? Did you ever open it?"

"Once. Christmas Eve, 1989, alone, with a lamp and a bottle and no decent excuse. I read it and sealed it again." I looked at my hands, which had finally stopped shaking, probably out of exhaustion. "It's the will. The true one. Witnessed, dated, signed in a hand no lawyer in this county will fail to recognize once it's compared against the funeral version. And it names its executor by name, and it gives her instructions, and those instructions include what to do if she herself should die before carrying them out."

Orsen went very still. "If she should die."

"He knew," I said. "God forgive me, he knew what his children were capable of, and he wrote the contingency anyway, and I sat on it while his contingency drowned." I pushed the tin across the table. It made a small sound, metal on wood, the loudest sound of my life. "Take it. Take all of it. Read it tonight, in a locked room, and don't tell me what it says, because what I don't know, Vivian can't make me confess."

Orsen's hands closed around the tin the way Edmund's had closed around mine at the station, and I thought: there it is. Forty years, passed hand to hand to hand. Paper really is the only immortality worth trusting.

They stood to leave, and at the door they paused. "Silas. Does anyone else know you kept it? Anyone at all?"

And I opened my mouth to say no — the easy no, the furniture-polishing no — and stopped. Because there was one. Just one. A kindness, I'd thought it, back when kindness seemed safe.

"Patty," I said. "The housekeeper. The night after Edmund left, she came to my shed, white as milk, and said she'd seen the flatbed go out the back drive and heard the nine-forty mentioned, and she begged me to tell her it wasn't what she thought. I told her to forget it and she did — or I believed she did. She moved away that winter. Changed her name when she married." I frowned, reaching back through the decades. "She writes me a card every Christmas. Hasn't missed one in thirty-eight years. Odd little notes. Weather, gardens, her grandchildren."

"Why does that matter?" Orsen asked.

"Because last year's card was different," I said slowly, hearing myself say it as if someone else were confessing. "Last year's card asked whether I still lived at the same address. And this year's hasn't come."

Orsen and I looked at each other across my kitchen table, and I watched them arrive at the same place I'd just arrived, and neither of us said it aloud, because saying it aloud makes it load-bearing.

Outside, at the road, a car sat with its lights off. Black sedan. Engine ticking as it cooled, which meant it had been there awhile — long enough to hear, through thin October windows, an old man finally setting down his stone.

Orsen slipped the tin inside their coat, pulled their collar up, and walked into the dark like a person who had decided, sometime in the last hour, exactly how far they were willing to go.

I stood in my doorway and prayed — first time in years — that they'd move faster than I had.
