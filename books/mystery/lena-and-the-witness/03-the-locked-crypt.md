## 3. The Locked Crypt

The cipher had a shape.

That was the first thing I understood on the third morning, sitting in the archive with the donation ledgers fanned around me like the wings of some paper bird. Ciphers always have shapes — patterns in the noise that the untrained eye slides past because it expects numbers to mean money and nothing else. But I had spent eleven years among St. Aldhelm's books, and I knew what our numbers were supposed to look like. They were supposed to be boring. Boring was their whole vocation.

These weren't boring.

I'd pulled every ledger from 1740 forward, working backward from the reference number the gray-eyed man had given me — V-1911-07, which had turned out to be less an accession number than a taunt. The entries clustered around certain dates: baptisms, funerals, consecrations, the quiet administrative weather of a cathedral's life. And at each cluster, the amounts went strange. A candle fund donation of £4.11. A masonry repair of £9.3. A bell-rope replacement for £15.20 — which was impossible, because pre-decimal currency didn't have a twenty-pence unit, and no verger alive or dead had ever been paid in one.

Letters. Of course they were letters. D=4, K=11, I=9, C=3, O=15, T=20. I wrote them out on a fresh sheet in my narrow hand and stared at what they gave me: *D K I C O T*. An anagram, or a fragment, or a key waiting for its lock.

I did what any archivist does with an impossible string. I looked for its siblings.

They were there, scattered across two centuries like breadcrumbs dropped by someone who never expected to be followed. Clusters of impossible amounts, three to seven entries each, spaced years apart, always within a fortnight of a Vane family event. A marriage in 1832. A probate in 1867. A funeral in 1901. Each cluster decoded to a short string of letters, and each string refused, politely and consistently, to be English.

Which meant substitution wasn't enough. Which meant there was a key — a second text, or a table, or a word — and keys in this cathedral did not float free. Keys lived in places.

I spread my strings out chronologically and read them not as words but as objects. That was the trick nobody teaches you: sometimes a cipher isn't trying to say something. Sometimes it's trying to *point*. And these pointed, entry after entry, decade after decade, at the same small set of names. Vane. Vane. Aldhelm itself, once, in a 1788 cluster that resolved to A-L-D-H-E-L-M so cleanly I laughed out loud alone in the archive at nine in the morning, which is the kind of thing that gets you talked about.

But mostly: Vane.

And once — only once, in the last cluster, the one dated three weeks from now, the one whose amounts spelled out six letters I hadn't yet cracked — the adjacent margin carried a symbol I'd seen exactly one other place in eleven years of cataloguing.

A small stamped seal in the ledger's gutter, half worn away: a mitre above a crossed key and crozier, with the number **1911** beneath it.

I knew that stamp. Every archivist at St. Aldhelm's knows it, the way sailors know the mark on a chart that means *here be rocks*. It was the consistorial seal — the bishop's court — and it appeared on exactly one class of document in our holdings: instruments of sealing. Orders closing things.

There were four sealed spaces under St. Aldhelm's. Two were plumbing chases and nobody had romantic feelings about them. One was the muniment room's inner closet, sealed in 1954 over a dispute about fire insurance that I had read the file on twice out of boredom. And the fourth was the Vane vault.

*Here be rocks.*

* * *

The Vane vault sat beneath the north transept, down a stair most parishioners didn't know existed, behind a door most parishioners assumed was a cupboard. It had been sealed by ecclesiastical order in March 1911 — I'd found the order itself during my first year, cataloguing the consistory court records, and I had thought about it perhaps once a year since, the way you think about a locked room in your own house. The order ran to a single paragraph: whereas irregularities had arisen touching the interment of the late Ambrose Vane, and pending resolution thereof, the vault was to be closed, the key deposited with the registrar, and no opening permitted save by further order of the court.

Pending resolution. For a hundred and fourteen years.

The irregularities had never been resolved. I had checked. There was no further order, no resolution, no file marked closed. The court had simply — stopped. Somewhere between March and December of 1911, the Church of England had looked into the Vane vault, seen whatever it saw, and decided the cheapest course of action was to forget the room existed. Institutions are very good at that. Forgetting is the one sacrament they never lapse in performing.

Ambrose Vane. Edmund Vane's grandfather. Dead in 1911, buried in haste, and then — not buried, actually, because if the vault was sealed *pending resolution of irregularities touching his interment*, then either he'd been moved, or he'd never arrived, or what arrived in his coffin wasn't what the paperwork said it was.

I sat with that for a long time while the radiators ticked and the February light moved its slow inch across the reading table.

Then I did the thing I should have done on day one instead of day three. I went looking for the 1911 burial fee in the ledgers.

It took me forty minutes. The entry was ordinary, which is to say it was extraordinary: £6.6 for interment, standard rate, paid by the family solicitor, witnessed by the sexton. No cipher cluster near it. No impossible amounts. The cleanest, dullest line in fifty pages.

Every other Vane event in two centuries of bookkeeping had been wrapped in code. The burial was naked.

Unless the code wasn't missing. Unless the code was the absence — unless whoever built the cipher wanted the 1911 entry findable, wanted a reader to stand at that clean little line of £6.6 and ask the question I was asking now. *What's under the floor?*

The cipher didn't hide the vault. The cipher advertised it. And the final cluster — the murder date, the six uncracked letters — sat closest of all to the seal-stamped page, as if the whole two-hundred-year apparatus had been a funnel narrowing toward one locked door.

You don't build a funnel to a door unless something behind the door matters.

I put on my coat.

* * *

Dean Halloway received me in his study at half past two, which surprised me; I'd expected to be made to wait through evensong at minimum. He was a compact man, sixty-ish, with the careful niceness of clergy who have learned that warmth is cheaper than honesty, and he gestured me into the chair across from his desk with genuine-looking pleasure.

"Ivo Crane. The archive's ghost." He smiled. "We only see you when something's wrong."

"Or when something's interesting," I said. "Today it's both."

I laid it out plainly, because plainness was my best tool with men like Halloway. The Vane vault. The 1911 order. The fact that no resolving order existed, which meant the seal was legally stale, administratively orphaned — a lock whose authority had died with everyone who imposed it. I had prepared a petition, I told him, formal, respectful, requesting the vault's opening for archival assessment. I cited the care of records act, the cathedral's own statutes, and the awkward precedent of the 1954 muniment closet, which had been opened without the sky falling.

He listened with his hands folded, nodding at intervals like a man following a sermon in a language he'd studied but never loved.

When I finished, he was quiet for perhaps five seconds. Five seconds is a long time in a conversation. I counted them.

"You've been busy," he said finally.

"The ledgers are my job."

"Mm." He unfolded his hands and refolded them differently, which I noted the way I note everything: automatically, uselessly, completely. "The petition is well-made. I'll give you that. You always were precise." He said it as though he knew me, which he didn't, which meant he'd read my file, which meant my request had been anticipated before I climbed the stairs. "But the answer is no."

"On what grounds?"

"Grounds of order." He said it gently, the way you decline a child's request to keep a wild animal. "The seal stands until the court lifts it. That the court has been slow is not a defect in the seal. It's rather the opposite — it shows the seal has held."

"A hundred and fourteen years isn't slowness, Dean. It's abandonment. There's no living officer with authority over that order. By any reasonable construction—"

"Ivo." My first name, deployed softly, a small knife. "May I ask you something? As one keeper of records to another." He leaned back. "Why now? You've been here eleven years. The vault has been sealed since before your grandmother was born. What makes an archivist suddenly petition to open a grave?"

*Because a stranger with gray eyes paid me triple to prove a murder hasn't happened yet,* I did not say. *Because the money in those ledgers spells words, and the words point down.*

"Scholarship," I said. "The Vane papers are surfacing in litigation. Chancery, next month. If the estate's history comes into evidence, the cathedral will want its own account of the 1911 irregularities in order. Better we open the vault ourselves than have a judge's order do it for us."

It was a good argument. It was the kind of argument that works on institutions, because it speaks their native tongue: self-protection dressed as prudence. I watched him receive it, watch him understand it, watch him — this was the part I would turn over for nights afterward — watch him *not be moved by it*. Because it was true, and useful, and correct, and none of that mattered, which meant the thing he was protecting wasn't procedure.

Halloway looked at me for a moment with something almost like regret.

"There are no irregularities," he said. "There is a sealed vault, and there is peace, and the two are related. Some doors are locked not because of what's behind them but because of what opening them does to the people who remember why they were shut." He stood, ending the audience the way deans end everything, by becoming furniture. "I'll keep your petition. Formally declined, formally filed. You do beautiful work, Miss Crane. Take up knitting."

At the door I paused. Not for effect — I want that recorded, even if only here, in my own head, where all my effects go to die. I paused because there was one more question and I couldn't leave without asking it.

"Dean. Who was dean in 1911?"

He didn't turn around. "Prentiss. Why?"

"His papers. Are they in the archive?"

"They were burned," Halloway said. "In 1911. All of them. By his own instruction." And then, over his shoulder, with the first honest thing I'd seen on his face all afternoon — something tired and old and frightened wearing a polite expression like a borrowed coat:

"You're not the first person to come up those stairs asking about that vault, Miss Crane. You're the first in thirty years. The last one stopped asking too. I'd take that as instruction."

* * *

I walked back down the cloister with my petition unsigned and my hands cold, and I made myself inventory what I actually had, because fear lies and lists don't.

One: the cipher was real, generational, and pointed at the Vane vault. Two: the vault held something worth burning a dean's entire correspondence to protect, in 1911, by the dean's own hand — which meant Prentiss hadn't been ordered to destroy his papers. He'd been cleaning up. Three: Halloway knew what the cleanup was. His fear had a specific flavor. It wasn't the fear of dust or liability or even scandal in the abstract. It was the fear of a man guarding a grave he didn't dig, on behalf of people he may never have met, because the alternative to guarding it was finding out what he'd been standing on.

Four — and this was the item I wrote down last, in smaller handwriting, as though size could shrink it —

Four: the last person to ask about the vault had stopped asking. And I did not know whether *stopped* meant satisfied, discouraged, or silenced, and Halloway's face had offered no clue except that he knew, and that the knowing sat on him like wet wool.

The light was going amber over the close. Somewhere above me the bells were practicing for nothing in particular, throwing their bronze voices against the stone, and beneath my feet — beneath the transept, beneath the stair, behind a door shaped like a cupboard — the answer to everything sat in the dark doing what answers do when nobody opens the door.

Waiting. Patiently. The way the dead wait, and the way, I was beginning to suspect, the guilty wait too.

I went back to the archive, took out the final cipher cluster — the six letters dated three weeks hence — and pinned it above my desk where I'd have to look at it every time I looked up.

Whatever was in that vault, someone had killed to keep it there once already.

And someone, soon, intended to kill again.
