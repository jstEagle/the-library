## 4. Sister of the Accused

The Vane estate file weighed four pounds and eleven ounces. Mara Crane knew this because she had weighed it — actually weighed it, on the postage scale in her chambers — at two in the morning, three nights before trial, when her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Four pounds, eleven ounces. Eleven years of a dead man's intentions, folded into manila and red rope. Somewhere in those pages was the original will of Edmund Vane, executed in his own hand, witnessed by his housekeeper and his gardener, leaving everything — the house at Cold Harbour, the timber interests, the small stubborn fortune in gilt-edged securities — to a niece nobody in the family had spoken to in a decade.

And somewhere outside those pages was a man who wanted that will dead.

Mara set the file down on the long table in her chambers and looked at it the way she'd learned to look at opposing counsel: without blinking.

"You're talking to paper," she said aloud, and heard how much she sounded like her sister.

She hadn't thought about Ivo in — no. That was a lie, and Mara had built her whole career on never lying to herself in front of witnesses, even when the witness was only the rain against the window. She thought about Ivo every day. She thought about her the way you think about a tooth that's been pulled: the tongue keeps going back to the gap.

Six years. Six years since the courtroom where their father sat with his hands flat on the table like a man waiting for a verdict he'd already decided to deserve, and Ivo stood up in the witness box with her gray, careful voice and said, *I saw him take the money, and I can show you where he put it.* Not cruelly. That was the thing Mara could never forgive — not the testimony itself, which was true, but the precision of it. Ivo hadn't raged or wept or stumbled. She'd archived their father. Filed him under *fraud* with a reference number, and closed the drawer.

Their mother never came back from it. Their father served eighteen months and moved to Spain and died there, alone, in a town whose name Mara refused to learn. And Ivo had gone back to her cathedral and her dust and her ledgers, and Mara had gone to law school with something cold packed behind her sternum, and neither of them had called.

Mara checked her watch. Four-forty in the morning. The trial opened Monday, nine-thirty, before Judge Ellery, who was deaf in his left ear and sentimental about testators. She had seventy-six hours.

She made coffee in the chamber's little sink-side machine, the kind that tasted like hot cardboard, and drank it standing up, and told herself the shaking in her hands was caffeine.

* * *

The Ashworth & Pell offices occupied the third floor of a converted wool warehouse near the river, all exposed brick and glass partitions, and by seven-fifteen Mara was at her desk with the Vane exhibits spread out in a fan. Her junior, Dev Anand, arrived at seven-thirty carrying two proper coffees and an expression of controlled dread.

"You slept here," he said.

"I slept enough."

"Nobody sleeps enough before an Ashe trial." He set one coffee down in her reach, which was why she kept him. "Any movement on their side?"

"That's what I'm going to find out."

Julian Ashe was acting for the cousins — the dispossessed Vane relations, three of them, led by a smooth man named Richard Vane-Ashe, which should have been her first clue years ago that the family tree had some grafting in it. Ashe himself was new to the matter; he'd taken over from the cousins' old firm six weeks ago, which was unusual this close to trial, and his entry appearance had come with a letter so courteous it read like a threat. *My clients are confident the court will find the instrument of 14 March to be inconsistent with the testator's settled intentions.* Settled intentions. As if Edmund Vane had been a sofa.

Mara had met Ashe once, at a procedural hearing — ten minutes in a corridor, gray eyes, gray suit, a handshake calibrated to the gram. He'd said, "Ms. Crane. The estate's executor *and* its counsel. Ambitious." And smiled as if ambition were a disease he was diagnosing.

She'd gone back to her office and re-read the will twice.

Now she pulled the exhibit list toward her and began the morning's accounting: originals versus copies, seals intact, chain of custody documented for each page. It was work she loved, though she'd have bitten off her tongue before admitting it — the same love her sister had for ledgers, probably, the same hunger for a world that could be made to add up. Their father had taught them both that much, at least: that numbers were honest even when people weren't. He'd just never expected either daughter to turn the lesson on him.

"Ms. Crane." Dev's voice had changed. "There's someone at reception asking for you. Won't give a name. Says it's about the Vane matter."

"Send them up."

"He won't come up. Says you should come down. Says—" Dev hesitated, and Mara looked up, and whatever he saw in her face made him finish. "Says it's about your family."

* * *

The man at reception wasn't the gray-eyed stranger. He was younger, soft-bodied, wearing a courier's jacket over a shirt too good for a courier's wages, and he stood with the studied blankness of someone being paid to remember nothing.

"Package for Ms. Crane," he said, and held out a plain brown envelope.

"I don't accept unverified deliveries."

"It's verified." He said it without inflection, the way you'd say *it's raining*. Then he set the envelope on the reception desk, turned, and walked out into the wet street, unhurried, not looking back.

Mara stood over the envelope for a long moment. Reception was empty except for the night security guard dozing by the monitors, and the river light through the glass doors made everything look underwater. She put on a glove from her coat pocket — a habit from handling old documents, and she noted, distantly, that she and Ivo shared that habit too, another inheritance their father never intended — and picked it up.

Inside was a single sheet. Cheap paper, block capitals, no fingerprint she'd ever find:

**WITHDRAW FROM THE VANE CASE. YOU HAVE UNTIL FRIDAY. ASK YOUR SISTER WHAT HAPPENS TO PEOPLE WHO DON'T KNOW WHEN TO STOP.**

Mara read it three times. The lobby seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship does.

Then the last line caught fire in her chest, and the fear burned off it like fog.

*Ask your sister.*

Six years of silence, and whoever wrote this knew about Ivo. Knew enough to use her name as a knife. Which meant they knew the Cranes were estranged, which meant they'd done their homework, which meant — Mara's mind was already moving, cold and fast, the advocate's mind, the mind that had carried her through every dark hour since the trial of her father — which meant this was professional. This was strategy. Someone wanted her frightened, and frightened people withdraw, and withdrawn executors get replaced by ones more amenable.

Or someone wanted her looking at Ivo. Watching the wrong door while the real thief came through another.

She walked back upstairs slowly, envelope in her gloved hand, and found Dev hovering by her office door.

"Well?"

"We're not withdrawing," Mara said. "Photograph this, log it as Exhibit M-1, provisional, and call the clerk's office — I want a copy of every filing Ashe's firm has made in any matter touching the Vane estate, going back five years. And Dev?" She stopped at her desk. "Find out if my sister is still listed at St. Aldhelm's Cathedral. Don't call her. Just find out."

Dev blinked. "Your sister?"

"The archivist," Mara said, as if it were nothing, as if the word didn't cost her anything at all. "She has a talent for finding things out. Apparently somebody thinks I might ask her to use it."

* * *

Friday came the way Fridays come before trials: all at once, sideways, with three motions filed and one witness gone soft. Mara spent the day in chambers with the blinds half-drawn, rehearsing her opening until the sentences wore grooves in her voice. At six she dismissed Dev, sat alone with the Vane file, and allowed herself, for exactly four minutes by her watch, to think about her sister.

What would Ivo do with a threatening note? Mara tried to picture it and found the picture had faded, like a photograph left in sun. She remembered a girl of nineteen sitting cross-legged on the floor of their childhood house with a shoebox of receipts, sorting their father's lies into piles by date. She remembered the way Ivo used to hum when she worked — tuneless, constant, a sound like a lock turning. She did not remember her sister's face anymore, not precisely. She remembered the idea of it.

*People who don't know when to stop.*

Was that what Ivo was? A person who didn't know when to stop? Or was that what Mara was — the sister who stayed angry for six years because staying angry was easier than making the first call?

Her phone sat on the desk, black and reflective. She picked it up. Put it down. Picked it up again, and got as far as searching *St. Aldhelm's Cathedral archive contact*, and stared at the number on the screen with her thumb hovering, and then the phone rang in her hand and she nearly threw it across the room.

It was Dev.

"Mara — sorry, I know it's late. Two things. First, the clerk's office came through. Ashe's firm filed an application yesterday, ex parte, sealed. I couldn't see the contents, but the hearing's set for Monday morning, eight o'clock, before the trial opens."

"Sealed from opposing counsel. Charming. Second thing?"

A pause. She could hear him deciding whether to say it.

"There's no current listing for an archivist at St. Aldhelm's. But there's a record — it's old, from a diocesan directory, maybe eight years back — of an I. Crane, Assistant Keeper of the Archive." Another pause. "Mara, are you okay? You've gone quiet."

"I'm here." She was looking at the threatening note, logged and photographed, lying square on her desk like a card played face-up. *Ask your sister what happens to people who don't know when to stop.*

Someone out there believed Ivo Crane was already asking questions. Someone out there thought the sisters were a single machine, and had decided to throw sand in the gears before it started turning.

Which meant, Mara thought, rising slowly from her chair, that somewhere in this case her invisible, estranged, six-years-silent sister was already moving — and whoever wrote this note was afraid of her.

"Dev," she said. "Cancel my Saturday. All of it."

"You're going to the cathedral, aren't you."

Mara looked out the window at the city going dark, at the distant smudge of the cathedral's spire against the last gray light, and felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to name — pride, maybe, or its twin.

"No," she said. "I'm going to find out what my sister knows. There's a difference."
