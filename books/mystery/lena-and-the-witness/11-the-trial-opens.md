## 11. The Trial Opens

The courtroom smelled of lemon polish and old fear.

Mara Crane had argued in this room — Court 4 of the Chancery Division, wood-paneled, badly lit, the radiators clanking like a ghost with a grievance — a dozen times before. She knew which bench wobbled, which clerk took bribes in the form of decent coffee, which window caught the afternoon sun at an angle that made opposing counsel squint. None of that knowledge helped her now. What she felt this morning was not the familiar cold engine of preparation but something closer to vertigo, as if she were standing on the edge of a building she'd once jumped from and hadn't landed yet.

Six years since she'd spoken to her sister. Six years, and here she was, thinking about Ivo anyway, because thinking about Ivo was what she did whenever the stakes turned mortal. It was a habit she despised and could not break, like biting her nails or checking the lock twice.

"Ms. Crane." Her junior, Priya Nair, slid into the seat beside her, folders squared to the edge of the table with the precision Mara demanded and secretly found endearing. "Ashe's team arrived twenty minutes ago. They brought more boxes than they filed."

"How many more?"

"Enough for a surprise."

Mara looked down the aisle. Julian Ashe stood at the respondent's table arranging his papers with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been interrupted mid-sentence. Gray suit, gray eyes, gray composure. He was handsome the way a knife is handsome — clean lines, honest about its purpose only when it was too late. He caught her looking and inclined his head, courteous as a duelist.

She did not return the nod. She had learned, over three weeks of pre-trial skirmishing, that Ashe collected courtesies the way other men collected debts: to be called in later, with interest.

"All rise."

Justice Okafor took the bench — a woman in her late fifties with reading glasses on a chain and a reputation for cutting through rhetoric the way a hot wire cuts through ice. She scanned the room once, unhurried, then said, "This is the matter of *Vane v. Vane*, the estate of Edmund Vane, deceased. Ms. Crane, you appear for the estate under the will of 2014?"

"I do, my Lady."

"Mr. Ashe, for the claimant, Mrs. Rosalind Vane-Holt?"

"As ever, my Lady." Ashe's voice was warm oak. Juries loved him; judges tolerated him; Mara distrusted him on principle and, lately, on evidence.

The evidence was thin and strange. Three weeks ago, someone had been inside her office. Papers shifted by millimeters, the safe copy of the will disturbed — not taken, not copied as far as she could tell, just *handled*, the way a burglar handles a thing to confirm its weight. She had reported it to no one, because reporting it meant explaining why she kept the original will's location secret even from her own chambers, and that explanation led back through six years of silence to a sister who read dead people's handwriting for a living.

Mara pushed the thought away. Today was about the living.

Okafor dispensed with preliminaries in forty minutes flat. The case, stripped of its legal dressing, was simple: Edmund Vane, industrialist, widower, dead eleven years, had left his entire estate — Vane House, the manufacturing patents, and some forty million pounds — to a trust benefiting his estranged daughter Rosalind's children, bypassing Rosalind herself on grounds the will described, in Edmund's own acid prose, as "self-evident to anyone who has met her." Rosalind challenged. Mara defended the will. Standard stuff. Family money curdling into family war.

Then Ashe stood to open, and the temperature of the room changed.

"My Lady," he began, "when these proceedings were commenced, the claimant believed the document before the court — the will of the ninth of March, 2014 — represented the final testamentary intentions of Edmund Vane. We have since discovered otherwise." He let the pause stretch until even the radiator seemed to hold its breath. "Three weeks ago, while cataloguing papers held in ecclesiastical storage at St. Aldhelm's Cathedral, my instructing solicitors located a codicil executed by Mr. Vane on the second of February, 2014 — five weeks *before* the will — expressly revoking any prior testamentary instrument and directing that his estate pass to his daughter absolutely."

Mara kept her face still. Inside, every alarm she owned went off at once.

*Three weeks ago.* The same window in which someone had gone through her office. The same window in which, though she couldn't have said why she connected them, a gray-eyed stranger had walked into a cathedral archive and paid triple rates for a job nobody sane would take.

"May I see the document?" Okafor asked.

"Certainly." Ashe passed it forward with two hands, reverent as an acolyte.

Mara watched the judge read. She watched the way Okafor's thumb rested on the paper, the slight tilt of her head, the pause at the signature page. And she watched the codicil itself as best she could from thirty feet — the browned edges, the foxing across the corner, the ink gone that particular tea-colored shade that age gives to iron gall.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing wrong with it.

Genuine documents are ugly. They're folded wrong, stained with coffee rings, annotated by solicitors who died decades ago. This codicil was pristine in the way of things made to look old rather than things that had simply gotten old. The foxing sat on top of the paper instead of in it. And the ink — she'd need the original under magnification to be sure, but from here, at this angle, with fifteen years of archival work absorbed through proximity to a sister who never stopped lecturing — the ink was too uniform. Iron gall fades unevenly. Light gets one edge of a page, damp gets another. This ink had faded democratically, everywhere at once, like something baked in an oven rather than lived in a drawer.

Provenance immaculate. Execution flawless. Aging artificial.

A forgery. A very good one.

"My Lady," Ashe continued, "the codicil was found among the Vane family papers deposited with the cathedral chapter in accordance with Mr. Vane's standing arrangement with the Diocesan Registry. Its discovery is documented, witnessed, and — we submit — conclusive. If the court finds the codicil valid, the claim succeeds and there is nothing left to try."

"There is a great deal left to try," Mara said, rising, "because the codicil is a fake."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water. She felt heads turn along the press bench. Good. Let them write it down.

"Ms. Crane," Okafor said mildly, "that is a serious allegation."

"It is, my Lady, and I make it advisedly. I'd ask the court to note several matters. First: the claimed provenance. The Vane papers at St. Aldhelm's have been catalogued continuously since deposit. My instructing solicitors requested the full catalogue index ten days ago. The index shows no codicil. No accession number, no conservation record, no entry whatsoever — yet Mr. Ashe's team claims it was found 'while cataloguing.' Cataloguing produces records. Where is theirs?"

"They will be provided in disclosure," Ashe said smoothly.

"Second." Mara held up two fingers. "The document itself. From the copy served on us yesterday evening —" she let the word *yesterday* land, saw Okafor's eyebrows move a fraction — "I observe that the alleged codicil revokes the 2014 will in terms almost identical to a revocation clause in another Vane family instrument. Draftsmen repeat themselves, my Lady, but they do not usually repeat themselves *verbatim across generations*. Third: the aging. I am instructed by a forensic document examiner —" a small lie; the examiner was booked for Thursday; the instruction was real enough in spirit — "whose preliminary view is that the oxidation pattern on the ink is inconsistent with natural aging over eleven years and consistent with accelerated chemical treatment."

"Do you have that report?" Okafor asked.

"It will be before the court by Thursday."

"So you are asking me to proceed on your assertion alone."

"I'm asking the court to order production of the *original* codicil, my Lady, so that it can be examined properly. Not Mr. Ashe's copy. Not photographs. The physical document, released from whatever custody it currently enjoys, into the control of the court, with an unbroken chain of custody documented at every step."

Something flickered across Ashe's face. It lasted less than a second — a tightening around the eyes, a fractional stillness — and then it was gone, replaced by reasonable concern. But Mara had spent her career watching witnesses lie, and she knew what she'd seen. He hadn't expected the chain-of-custody demand. Or rather: he had prepared for everything except the possibility that she'd understand exactly why it mattered.

"The claimant has no objection to examination of the original," Ashe said. "Indeed, we welcome it. The document speaks for itself."

"Where is it?" Okafor asked.

"In secure storage pending trial, my Lady. With the cathedral chapter, under seal."

"Under seal," Mara repeated. "My Lady, may I ask under whose authority? The crypt archive at St. Aldhelm's has been sealed by ecclesiastical order since 1911. Documents don't simply walk out of a sealed crypt. Either the seal was lawfully lifted — in which case I want the paperwork — or it wasn't, in which case Mr. Ashe's 'discovery' is not merely forged but *stolen*, and the court should know whose hand opened the door."

"I'm sure the chapter's permissions are entirely regular," Ashe said.

"I'm sure they aren't," Mara said, "and I intend to prove it."

Okafor looked between them for a long moment, then removed her glasses. "Here is what will happen. The original codicil is to be produced to the court by Friday morning. It will be examined by a jointly instructed expert — Ms. Crane, name yours by Wednesday; Mr. Ashe, you'll have your objection or your agreement within twenty-four hours. Until then, trial proceeds on the assumption that both instruments exist and neither is yet proved. Ms. Crane, you may cross-examine on the discovery circumstances. Mr. Ashe, if your document is genuine, you have nothing to fear from science. If it isn't—" she put her glasses back on, and her voice cooled by ten degrees— "you'll wish you'd never seen a cathedral. We adjourn until tomorrow at ten."

* * *

In the corridor afterward, Priya fell into step beside her, breathless. "Thursday's impossible. Dr. Okonkwo can't get the original before Friday at the earliest, and if Ashe contests joint instruction—"

"He won't contest it. He wants us examining that document. He wants it *legitimized* by our own expert's handling." Mara stopped by the tall window at the corridor's end, where rain was starting to stitch the pavement below. "Think it through, Priya. He serves the copy the night before opening. He produces a document with perfect provenance from a sealed archive he shouldn't be able to touch. He's not afraid of forensic examination. Which means either the forgery is good enough to survive it—"

"Or he controls the examination somehow."

"Or both." Mara watched the rain. Her reflection hung ghost-gray against the glass, and behind it, faintly, the reflection of the man himself, crossing the lobby below with his boxes and his calm, pausing at the doors to check the weather like any innocent man.

There was one more thing she didn't say aloud, because saying it would make it real and because Priya was brilliant but loyal, and loyalty was a liability in a case like this. The thing was this: three weeks ago, someone had been in her office and handled the safe copy of the will. Not stolen it. *Measured* it. And a codicil had surfaced dated five weeks before that will, in a hand that would need to imitate Edmund Vane's — a hand that could only be perfected against a known exemplar.

Whoever forged the codicil had studied the will. Closely. In her office.

And the only person she knew who could read a dead man's hand well enough to teach a forger how to fake one was a sister who hadn't returned her calls in six years.

No. That was grief talking, dressed up as deduction. Ivo testified against their father because the evidence compelled her — Mara had read the transcript a hundred times, hating every line of it, hating most that it was true. Ivo didn't forge things. Ivo *caught* forgers. It was the whole architecture of her strange, closed life.

But someone had been in that office. And someone had opened a crypt sealed for a hundred and fourteen years. And those someones were working together, on a clock, toward a Friday deadline that Mara had just handed them on a plate.

"Get me everything on St. Aldhelm's chapter," she told Priya. "Governance, staff, the verger, the dean, the secretary — everyone with keys. And find out who petitioned to access the crypt archive in the last six months. There'll be a record. Sealed doesn't mean invisible; it means somebody signed something."

"And if the record's been cleaned?"

"Then the cleaning is the record." She turned from the window. "People think evidence is what's written down. Evidence is what's *missing* from what's written down. Go."

Priya went. Mara stood a moment longer, watching the rain, feeling the case shift beneath her feet like deck plating in bad weather. This morning she had come to defend a will. By lunchtime she understood she was defending something larger — that the codicil was not an argument but a weapon, that the trial was not a dispute but a cover story, and that somewhere inside it, threaded through the paperwork like wire through wax, ran a design she could feel but not yet see.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number. A photograph: the interior of a stone vault, coffins ranked in the dark, and propped against the nearest one a sheet of old paper, legible even in the dim light. Beneath the image, four words:

*It's already down there.*

Mara stared at the screen until it dimmed, then darkened, then showed her own stricken face reflected back at her.

The handwriting, she realized — the handwriting of the message, slanted, economical, the terminal letters clipped short — belonged to the last person on earth who should be sending it.

Her sister.
