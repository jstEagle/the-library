## 13. Buried With the Evidence

The door closed with a sound like a full stop.

Not a slam — the crypt was too old, too thick with stone, for anything so dramatic. It was a grinding, patient sound, iron meeting stone on its own terms, and then a click that Ivo felt in her teeth rather than heard. The last thread of candlelight from the stairwell shrank to a line, thinned to a wire, and went out.

Darkness. Total. The kind of dark that has weight.

Ivo stood very still.

Panic, she knew, was a luxury with an interest rate. She had watched it bankrupt better minds than hers — witnesses who talked too much, clients who burned documents they should have kept. Her own rule, the one she'd built her whole practice on: *never take a case you can't close.* Well. She appeared to be inside the case now, sealed like correspondence.

*Breathe.* Four counts in. Six counts out. The air tasted of cold stone, old iron, and something sweeter underneath — dust that had once been flowers, perhaps, laid on coffins by mourners who were themselves now dust.

She counted coffins by touch as she turned slowly in place. Three along the north wall, marble, their lids beaded with condensation. Two more against the east, lead over wood, the wood gone soft as old bread. And in the center of the floor, raised on two stone trestles, the Vane vault's centerpiece: Edmund Vane himself, eleven years dead, sleeping above the document Ivo had broken holy law to find.

*Someone knew she'd come.*

The thought arrived without drama, the way the worst thoughts do. The door hadn't fallen shut. Doors like this didn't fall; they were held open by counterweights and closed by hands. She had checked the mechanism before descending — she always checked mechanisms — and it had been oiled. Recently oiled. She had noted it at the time and filed it under *verger's diligence.*

Now it filed itself under *trap.*

Her matches were in her left coat pocket. Her torch — the small brass one she carried for reading spines in the archive's dimmest stacks — was in her right. She took out the torch first and pressed the switch.

Nothing.

She pressed it again, harder, the way pressing harder has ever fixed anything. Nothing. She unscrewed the cap and shook it: the faint dry rattle of a battery loose in its housing, or no battery at all. She had checked the torch yesterday. She was almost certain she had checked it yesterday.

*Almost certain* was doing a great deal of work, and she knew it. That was the thing about being hunted by someone meticulous — you began to doubt your own meticulousness, and that doubt was the real weapon. Ashe hadn't locked her in. He had made her unsure whether she'd locked herself in.

"Right," she said aloud, because the dark deserved a voice, and hers was steadier than she'd expected. "One thing at a time."

Match one. The phosphorus flare lit the crypt in a stuttering amber wash — coffins, trestles, the far wall where a shelf of ledgers sat behind a grille, exactly where Halloway's half-confession had promised. The match died in nine seconds. She struck another and held it to the shelf while she worked the grille's latch with her free hand. It opened on hinges that shrieked like something being murdered, which she supposed, in a way, they were.

On the top shelf: a tin deed box, green with verdigris, stamped with the Vane arms. On the second shelf, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone brittle as hair: a folder bearing the seal of the Consistory Court, 1911. The deposition. Halloway had told her enough to know what it was — another forged will, another family ruined, another institution choosing silence over scandal — but knowing what a document was and holding it were different sacraments.

She set the box on the trestle beside Edmund's coffin, because there was nowhere else to put it, and because there was a bleak comedy in using the dead man's bier as a worktable. He would have understood. From everything the ledger cipher had taught her about Edmund Vane, he had spent his last decade understanding exactly how institutions treated documents they disliked.

* * *

The deed box opened with a key taped beneath it — someone's courtesy, decades stale. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, lay a document that did not look like a forgery, which was the first sign it wasn't one.

Edmund Vane's true will.

Ivo struck a third match and read.

It began conventionally enough — the recitals, the revocation of prior instruments, the formal language of testamentary intent. Then it stopped being conventional.

*"I, Edmund Ambrose Vane, being of sound mind (a condition my solicitors dispute, which tells you what my solicitors are worth), do declare..."*

She read the dispositions twice to be sure. The estate — house, land, endowments, the lot — to **Mara Crane**, executor and sole heir, in trust for the causes named in Schedule B. Not a codicil. Not a bequest. The whole of it, to a woman who wasn't a Vane at all, whom Edmund had met — according to the recital — through his correspondence on archival provenance, and whom he had come to trust "more than any blood relation I possess, having reviewed the evidence."

Mara. Six years of silence between them, six years of Christmas cards returned unopened, and all that time Edmund Vane had been writing her name into history.

Ivo's eyes stung, and she told herself it was the match smoke. There was very little match smoke. She struck another anyway.

Then came the part that made her forget her sister entirely for four full minutes.

*"And whereas I am aware that certain parties will seek to undo this instrument after my death, as instruments have been undone before in this family and in this parish, I set down here what I know."*

He named them.

**Julian Ashe, senior,** solicitor of this city, who in 1911 — no. Wait. Ivo's historian's mind snagged on the date even as her fingers shook. Not Julian Ashe senior. The name in the will was *Ashe*, and the dates were wrong for the man she'd traced through the shell firm, but the hand — the *method* — was the same. Edmund wrote:

*"...the same hand that forged the codicil of 1911, a crime confessed in the sealed deposition held in this vault, a crime the Church chose to bury rather than face. The Ashes have practiced this trade across three generations: forge the paper, wait out the memory, inherit by patience. My own 'codicil' is already in preparation; I have seen the draft in the offices of Ashe & Prentice, where I was shown it as a courtesy, as one shows a man his own funeral card."*

He accused them by name, by firm, by method. He described the cipher — *the ledger letters, amounts standing for alphabet, a bookkeeping older than any living memory of it* — and instructed that whoever found this will should compare it against the cathedral donation ledgers, "where my grandfather's generation recorded their sins in figures, believing figures pious."

And then, near the end, a paragraph that stopped Ivo's breath:

*"To the archivist who finds this: I do not know your name, but I know your type. You will have come down here against orders, at night, alone, because that is the only way anything true gets done in this building. You are in danger, and I am sorry for it. If the door has closed behind you, know that it was meant to close for someone — and that the vent in the northeast corner still draws air, because I paid a mason in 2009 to see that it always would. Consider it my last charitable bequest."*

Ivo lowered the will and looked up into the darkness, toward the northeast corner, where nothing moved and nothing breathed and yet she felt — distinctly, absurdly — acknowledged.

"He knew," she whispered. "The mad old devil knew."

* * *

But someone else knew too. That was the thought she could not shake, and it grew teeth as she worked.

The door had been oiled. The torch had been empty — or had she merely failed to check it? Either answer convicted someone. If Ashe's people had prepared the crypt for her arrival, they had known not only *that* she would come but *when*. Which meant the verger's keys, which she'd copied from the secretary's hook, had been bait on a hook, and she had swallowed it whole with the line and leader.

Or — and this possibility arrived colder than the crypt — the trap hadn't been set for her at all. Perhaps it had been set for whoever came, and Ashe simply hadn't expected anyone to be fool enough to come. In which case her imprisonment was an accident, and accidents killed people in places like this just as thoroughly as intentions did.

Neither version changed her immediate arithmetic. Air, warmth, time. In that order.

First: memorize the will. Every word. Because whatever happened next — whether she walked out of here or was found here — the document itself might be seized, destroyed, disputed, tainted. Paper could be argued with. A mind that had it word-perfect could not. This was the discipline she'd been born to: her father had made the sisters recite inventory lists at dinner, catalog numbers with the soup, and Mara had rebelled while Ivo had *excelled*, and here was the fruit of that poisoned orchard, finally useful.

She struck matches and read aloud in a whisper, passage by passage, then recited each back with her eyes shut until it held. The dispositions. The accusations. The dates. The reference to Schedule B — which was missing from the box, and whose absence was itself information: someone had taken it, or Edmund had hidden it elsewhere, and either way it was a thread leading out of this tomb.

The matches were going faster than she liked. She counted them. Eleven left. She allowed herself three per hour until she found the vent, then one per hour after that. Cold was the slower killer and the quieter one; she'd read enough coroners' reports to respect it.

The vent. Northeast corner. She found it by walking the walls with her palm, counting paces — twelve along the north, seven along the east — until her fingers crossed a seam in the stone where the air moved. Faintly. Barely. A thread of wind cool enough to feel and thin enough to despair over.

It was a vent, not a door. No human being was passing through it. But it drew, and where it drew from, somewhere above, there was sky, and where there was sky there was eventually a person, and where there was a person there was eventually a chance.

She shouted into it. The vent ate her voice and gave back nothing.

Fine. Then she wouldn't shout. She would think.

* * *

She sat on the floor with her back against Edmund's trestle, wrapped in the oilcloth from the deposition folder, the will's contents reciting themselves in her head unbidden, the way memorized things do when the body is frightened and the mind insists on working.

Fear was still there. She didn't pretend otherwise. It sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed. But somewhere in the last hour it had changed state — the way dread changes when it finally has a target. She was no longer afraid of the dark. She was furious at the man who had arranged it.

Fury was fuel. Fury had a plan.

Because here was what the dark had clarified, the way only genuine danger clarifies: Ashe had not come down here himself. He had let the crypt do his work, the way the Church had let the crypt do its work in 1911, the way the whole rotten apparatus let stone and silence do what hands were too squeamish for. He thought in systems. Systems had schedules. Tomorrow morning, the trial resumed, and Ashe would produce his immaculate codicil, and no one would miss the archivist until she failed to appear at some appointment she'd never told anyone about.

Except one person might notice. One person had spent six years pretending not to watch her sister's life from across the gap, and had received — Ivo realized with a start — an anonymous note in her own handwriting, because she'd sent it to Mara's chambers days ago, a calculated nudge she'd half regretted ever since.

*Mara would come looking.* Not tonight. Maybe not tomorrow. But Mara Crane did not leave threads unpulled, and the note had given her enough to pull.

Ivo looked up at the vent, at the invisible thread of moving air, and made her decisions in order, the way she did everything.

She would keep the will on her person, wrapped against her skin. She would ration her matches and her voice. She would mark the hours by recitation — the will, the deposition's opening lines, the cipher key — so that if her mind started to fail she'd know it by the errors. And when she heard footsteps above — when, not if — she would make noise enough to raise the dead she was keeping company with.

Until then, there was one more thing the dark offered, and she took it: time to admit what she'd known since the gray-eyed man first laid triple payment on her desk. She had taken this case against instinct because some part of her had already known whose name she'd find at the end of it. She had wanted a reason to walk back into her sister's life that wasn't apology. Apology was too naked. A mystery was armor.

Well. The armor had brought her here, to a floor of cold stone, wrapped in oilcloth beside a coffin, betting her life that the sister she'd betrayed would come for her.

"It was never duty," she said to the vent, quietly. "You knew that, didn't you, Edmund. Love was the case. It was always the case."

The vent drew its thin breath and gave nothing back.

Above her, faint as a memory, stone shifted somewhere in the cathedral's upper dark — a door, a footfall, impossible to say. Ivo went still, one match held unlit between frozen fingers, and listened with her whole life.
