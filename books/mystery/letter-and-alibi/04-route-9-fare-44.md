## 4. Route 9, Fare 44

The ledger sat on my kitchen table all day like a cat that had decided to live with me whether I liked it or not.

I'd carried it home from my mother's storage unit in a canvas grocery bag, tucked between a fondue set nobody had ever used and a shoebox of Christmas cards from people whose names I only half remembered. The ledger itself was nothing to look at—green cloth cover gone gray at the corners, spine cracked in three places, the gold lettering worn down to *MERIDIAN TAXI CO. ROUTE LEDGER* and then just *MERID*. My mother had worked dispatch for Meridian for eleven years. She'd kept this copy the way some people keep a hymnal: not because they expect to need it, but because throwing it away would feel like a small betrayal.

I made coffee I didn't drink and opened it to the first page.

Here is what I knew about ciphers before that morning: absolutely nothing, unless you count the summer I was nine and tried to invent a secret language with a boy named Petey Ostrander, which collapsed after four days when we couldn't agree on how to spell "helicopter." But I knew ledgers. Ten years of night-shift dispatching will teach you a ledger's grammar the way living near train tracks teaches you their schedule—you stop hearing it and start feeling it. Fares go here. Times go there. Driver numbers in the margin. Everything in its column, everything in its row.

And everything in this ledger was almost right, which is another way of saying it was wrong.

* * *

The trouble started—or rather, announced itself—on page forty-four.

Route 9 was the eastside loop. I knew it the way you know a scar: it ran from the old depot up through the mill district, past St. Brendan's, out to the edge of town where the streets gave up on ambition and became gravel. A normal entry for Route 9 looked like this:

**9 — 22:15 — fare 3.40 — drv. 12 — Kessler Ave / return**

Time, amount, driver, streets. Clean as a receipt.

But scattered through the book—maybe one entry in thirty, always late at night, always Route 9 or Route 2—were entries like this:

**9 — 23:47 — fare 44.00 — drv. 7 — [blank]**

Fare of forty-four dollars. In 2003. For a cab ride. You could cross the entire county for eleven dollars back then; I know because I did it once, broke and nineteen, and the driver complained about it for forty minutes. Forty-four dollars wasn't a fare. Forty-four dollars was a message.

And the times were wrong too. Not illegible—my mother's handwriting was small and ferocious, a schoolteacher's hand applied to a dispatcher's job—but *arranged*. Look at enough numbers and you start seeing posture. These numbers stood too straight. The fours had flat tops where her fours curved. The sevens were crossed where she never crossed them.

Someone else had written these entries. Someone who wanted them to look like my mother's work but couldn't quite manage the tilt of her grief, because by 2003 my mother's sevens leaned left, the way she did, into everything.

I sat with that for a long while, watching rain come off the eaves in ropes, feeling the particular vertigo of realizing your dead mother had a handwriting imitator. Then I did what I do, which is the thing I'm actually good at: I stopped looking at the pages and started looking at the pattern underneath them.

* * *

Here's the trick of being invisible for twenty years: you get very good at finding things that don't want to be found. I find the customer who calls every taxi company in town under different names. I find the driver skimming fares by rounding up quarters. I find the pattern, because patterns are just lies that got tired.

So I laid the odd entries out in order—the forty-four-dollar fares, the blank destinations, the straight-backed numerals—and I asked the ledger a simple question: *if you're not recording money, what are you recording?*

Times. They had to be times. Real ones, logged in plain sight, because a fake time can be checked against anything and a real time checks against nothing—it's just true. So the printed time was the decoy, and the true time had to be hiding somewhere nearby.

The fare column. Forty-four dollars. Four-forty. 4:44? No—too cute, and the amounts varied slightly across entries: 44.00, 43.75, 44.25. Unless. Unless the cents were the point. 43.75. 4375. No. Too long.

I got up, paced the length of my kitchen—six steps, turn, six steps—and came back, and that's when I saw it, the way you see the face in the wallpaper only after someone points at it.

Driver numbers. Every odd entry had one, same as the clean ones. Driver 7. Driver 7 again. Driver 7 a third time. On a night with dozens of drivers working, the strange entries all belonged to one man.

And the fare column, read not as money but as a clock: 44.00 could be 4:40 if you let the zero be a placeholder. 43.75—no. But wait. What if the cents weren't minutes but *minutes past*, encoded? 44.00 = 4:40. 44.25 = 4:41 (twenty-five hundredths of an hour is fifteen minutes—no, that gives 4:55)—

I want to be honest about this part: it took me four hours, two more pots of coffee, and one moment of talking out loud to an empty apartment like a man negotiating with furniture. The breakthrough was embarrassingly small. I dropped my pen. It rolled off the table, and I bent to pick it up, and from below, upside down, I saw the ledger page hanging over the table's edge—and the 44.00 looked like 00.44.

Zero-zero, forty-four. Twelve forty-four a.m. And 43.75 upside down was still 43.75, but beside it the printed time was 01:15, and 1:15 minus... no. Simpler. Much simpler, the way real codes always are, built by tired people in a hurry: the fare column recorded *duration*. Fare 44.00 meant a run of forty-four minutes. The printed time was the start. Add the duration, and you got the true end time—which mattered, because the end time was when the car went somewhere the ledger refused to name.

I checked it against the clean entries. Route 9, 22:15, fare 3.40—well, no, the clean entries were honest money. But the odd ones: 23:47 plus 44 minutes. 00:31. 01:15 plus 43 minutes. 01:58. Each strange entry ended at a half-hour mark or just past it. Shift-change gaps. The holes in the night when nobody official was watching the board.

Somebody had been running ghost runs at the seams of the shift, and somebody else—my mother, or the person imitating her—had written down exactly how long each ghost took, disguised as money, because money is the one thing nobody audits twice.

* * *

That left the addresses. Or rather, the absence of them.

Every strange entry had a blank destination. But blanks aren't empty—not in a ledger kept by a careful woman. I held page forty-four under the desk lamp and tilted it, and there it was: an indentation. Whatever had been written in the destination space had been erased—erased hard, with pressure, the way you erase when you're terrified someone will hold the page to the light. Which, I note for the record, works. Pencil forgives. Paper remembers.

I got a pencil of my own, soft graphite, the kind artists use, and shaded the indentations the way I'd seen done in exactly one documentary about document forensics, which I had watched at 3 a.m. seven years ago for reasons I could not now reconstruct. The graphite caught the valleys. Letters rose out of the gray like something surfacing in a lake.

Not words. Numbers. House numbers, and a street abbreviated the way dispatchers abbreviate: *1417 Q—* and then a fragment I couldn't lift, and beneath it, smaller, pressed so faintly it was nearly prayer: *9 / 44 — T.R.*

T.R. Two letters. Initials. Somebody's initials, written by whoever wrote the strange entries, hidden inside a fake fare, on the night of—

I stopped. Set the pencil down. Made myself say it plainly, because plainly was the only safe way to hold it now.

Tomas Reyes. Union organizer. Disappeared October 2003, last seen leaving the union hall on Fourth, case cold before the coffee at his wake went stale. Twenty-two years ago this autumn. The statute of limitations on the relevant charges was a door swinging shut, slowly, and everyone in town with money had spent two decades leaning on it.

And the address. 1417 Quarry Road.

I knew that house.

* * *

You have to understand what knowing does to a body. It isn't a thought. It's weather. It moved into me all at once, barometric and total.

1417 Quarry Road was the yellow house with the porch swing that didn't swing anymore because somebody's kid had used the chains for a rope swing into the ravine. It was where Mrs. Adeyemi grew tomatoes in coffee cans along her front steps. It was four houses down from the place we lived when I was eight—the year my father still came home, smelling of cigarettes and engine cold, the year before the year he didn't.

I had walked past 1417 a thousand times. I had sledded in the vacant lot behind it. A girl named Corinne lived there briefly and had a pool table in her basement and let me lose to her at it for an entire winter. It was a house the way houses are: ordinary, lit at dinnertime, holding its small human weather.

And on a night in October 2003, according to a coded line in my dead mother's taxi ledger, a Meridian cab—driver number seven—picked up or delivered something at that house during a forty-four-minute window at the seam of the shift, and someone initialed it *T.R.* in graphite pressed so lightly it was practically a whisper.

My hands were not steady. I noticed this with the detached interest of a dispatcher noting a driver going erratic on the radio. Okay, I told my hands. Okay. We'll sit down.

I sat down.

* * *

Here is the strange part, the part I keep circling back to even now: underneath the dread, there was a sound in me like a lock turning. Because whoever sent those letters wanted me to find this. This was the first door, and it opened onto my own childhood street. That's not coincidence; that's authorship. Someone had chosen the address the way you choose a word in a crossword, knowing I'd feel it land.

Which meant the sender knew where I grew up. Knew the house. Possibly knew the girl with the pool table, the rope-swing chains, the whole geography of a life I had buried under ten aliases' worth of careful nothing.

Being unseen had rules. Rule one: nobody knows where you're from. Rule two: nobody knows your name—the real one, the one on the birth certificate in a county I hadn't visited in twenty years. The letters had already broken rule two. Now the ledger was chipping at rule one, and I felt the architecture of my quiet life shifting the way you feel ice shift underfoot: not yet falling, but no longer pretending to be solid.

And still—God help me—I turned the page.

Because there was a thrill in it. I'll admit that here, in the privacy of my own head, where honesty costs least. Twenty-two years of being overlooked, of being *Sand* on the radio and nothing on paper, and someone out there had looked. Had looked *closely*. Had studied my mother's handwriting well enough to fail at imitating it. Being seen, it turns out, feels like being flayed and like being warmed, and the two sensations are apparently neighbors, sharing a wall.

* * *

I worked until the light changed. That's the other thing about the invisible life: no one notices when you don't sleep.

By dawn I had lifted five destination fragments from the strange entries. Three were Quarry Road addresses. One was the union hall on Fourth. The fifth was a place I had to look up, and when I found it, I laughed—one short, involuntary syllable with no humor in it, the laugh of a man reading his own obituary and noticing a typo.

The fifth address was the ravine access road off Miller's Flats. The ravine. Everyone in town knew the ravine the way towns know their wounds: kids fell in it, cars went off it, a body had supposedly never been found in it, except that everyone's uncle had a cousin who swore otherwise.

The last strange entry in the book—the very last, on the final page my mother's copy contained—was different from the others. Same straight-backed numerals, same fake fare. But the duration was longer: 61.00. Sixty-one minutes. And in the destination space, instead of an address, the eraser had missed one small pressed word, so faint my lamp nearly lied about it:

*return?*

With a question mark. Written by someone who knew the car had gone out heavy and come back—what? Empty? Lighter? Who was asking the question a year or a decade later, pressing it into a dead woman's ledger?

And below that, in my mother's own hand—her real hand, the left-leaning grief-struck sevens, unmistakable—two words that closed the page like a door:

*Ask Augustin.*

Augustin. I said the name aloud to my kitchen, to the rain, to the fondue set. There was only one Augustin attached to Meridian Taxi, then or now. Augustin Vey. Founder. Chairman. The man whose face was on the "Meridian Cares" posters in the break room, silver-haired and benevolent, holding a giant novelty check over some children's hospital. The man whose name was on the building, on the pension fund, on the little brass plaque by the dispatch desk that Renata dusted around and never read aloud.

My mother had worked for him for eleven years. My mother had kept this ledger hidden in a box under a fondue set for twenty years. And the last thing she ever wrote in it was an instruction, addressed—to whom? To herself? To whoever found it? To me, though she couldn't have known I'd be the one, could she? Mothers leave instructions the way trees leave seeds, without needing to know which of them takes root.

*Ask Augustin.*

I looked at the clock. 6:40 a.m. My shift started at nine. Outside, the rain had quit, and the sky had that rinsed, guilty cleanliness it gets after crying all night.

I should have gone to bed. I should have shredded the page, melted the pencil, returned to the warm dark of my unlisted life, where the worst thing that happens on any given morning is a driver calling in sick. Every instinct I had spent two decades training voted for bed.

Instead I took out my phone, looked up Meridian Taxi Company, and read the corporate page top to bottom for the first time in ten years of working there. Board of directors. Charitable foundation. And under *Leadership*, a photograph I had walked past a thousand times without seeing, the way you walk past wallpaper:

Augustin Vey, younger, dark-haired, arm slung around a row of drivers in front of a fleet of checkered cabs. Company picnic, judging by the bunting. And third from the left, laughing at something outside the frame, cigarette in hand, cap pushed back—

A man I recognized the way you recognize your own hands.

Driver number seven. My father, Emilio, aged thirty-one, standing close enough to Augustin Vey to touch him.

The phone screen dimmed, then went dark, and I sat in the gray morning light with my mother's ledger open to a page that said *ask Augustin*, and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a fare meter clicking over, that the route I'd just decoded didn't lead to a house on Quarry Road at all.

It led home.
