## 9. The Night Manager

Renata Okafor had been the night manager of Meridian Taxi for eleven years, and in that time she had developed a theory: every building keeps a smell of what it's afraid of. The Meridian dispatch office smelled of burnt coffee, toner, and the particular dust that collects on things nobody is allowed to throw away. Which was to say, it smelled exactly like an archive.

She stood at 2:14 a.m. in the back room—the one officially called "Records" and unofficially called "the Tomb"—holding a flashlight between her teeth while she counted boxes. Not because anyone had asked her to count them. Because three days ago a lawyer had asked her to *not* count them, and Renata had been contrary since the age of four.

"Forty-one," she said around the flashlight. "Forty-two."

The Tomb occupied the windowless rear third of the office, past the break room where the microwave had a dent shaped like somebody's forehead and a sign reading DO NOT MICROWAVE FISH (THIS MEANS YOU, GERALD). Metal shelving ran floor to ceiling. Boxes of dispatch logs, fare ledgers, driver files, radio maintenance records—decades of the small bureaucratic sediment that a taxi company produces without ever meaning to. Nobody had opened most of it since the nineties. There was a spider in box nineteen that Renata had named Chairman and fed occasionally out of professional respect.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. The dispatch channel. She spat out the flashlight and answered.

"Go for Renata."

"Unit twelve wants to know if he can refuse a fare at Delmore and Fifth," said Marisol, the dispatcher on shift, who had the unflappable tone of a woman who had heard every excuse a drunk man could produce at two in the morning.

"Is he waving money?"

"He's waving a sandwich."

"Then yes, he can refuse. Company policy: we don't compete with deli meat."

Marisol laughed and clicked off. Renata smiled at the dark, then stopped smiling, because the dark reminded her why she was in it.

* * *

The lawyer's name was Corbett Ashe, and he wore suits the color of expensive regret. He had come into the office Tuesday morning—not night, never night, men like him didn't trust the hours when working people were awake—with a leather folder and a smile calibrated to suggest they were old friends about to share a joke.

"Routine housekeeping," he'd said. "Mr. Vey is doing some estate planning. We'll need the pre-2005 archives collected and transferred to our offices. Standard retention review."

"Sure," Renata had said. "Let me check our retention policy."

"There isn't one on file," Ashe had said, with the mild confidence of a man who had already checked.

"No," Renata agreed. "There isn't." She'd let that sit for a moment, watching him decide whether she was being slow or being difficult. She enjoyed watching lawyers make that calculation. It was one of the few free entertainments this job offered. "So before I hand over twenty years of company records to an outside firm, I'll need something in writing from ownership. A directive. Signed."

Ashe's smile had thinned by perhaps a millimeter. "Mr. Vey owns the company."

"Mr. Vey owns the company through a holding structure I've seen exactly once, on a laminated chart, during a fire-safety meeting. What I need is a signature on letterhead. You understand. If a box goes missing and someone comes asking, I want to be able to point at a piece of paper instead of pointing at myself."

It was, on its face, an act of pure bureaucratic cowardice. Cover yourself. Get it in writing. Every middle manager in the world would have nodded along. And that was precisely why it worked: there was nothing to argue with, nothing to suspect, no resistance to crush. Just a tired woman asking for paperwork, as tired women had done since the invention of the filing cabinet.

Ashe had promised the letter by Friday. It was now Sunday, or technically Monday, and the letter had not arrived, which told Renata something important: whatever was in those boxes, it was worth more to them unsigned than signed. A signature created a record. Records could be subpoenaed. The absence of a signature meant they intended to take the archive quietly, the way you'd remove a splinter—quickly, locally, with minimal bleeding.

Renata had worked nights at Meridian long enough to know how quiet things got done here.

* * *

Here is what the day people never understood about the night shift: the night shift remembers everything. Days are for forgetting. At night, the building talks. The boiler knocks twice when the pressure drops. The front door sticks unless you lift the handle. And the people—night people are a different species, gentler and stranger, drivers coming in off their routes with their collars damp, dispatchers drinking coffee that has achieved a chemical state unknown to science, everyone a little more honest at 3 a.m. because nobody's performing for anybody.

Renata knew all her drivers' children's names. She knew which ones were sober and which ones were managing it. She knew that Gerald microwaved fish anyway, and that he always apologized first, which somehow made it worse.

And she knew the archive mattered, because eleven years ago, on her second week, the manager before her—a gray, kind, defeated man named Hollis—had walked her back to the Tomb, put his hand flat on box seven, and said, "Whatever anyone ever asks you about these, ask for paper. Paper protects everybody. Paper is the only honest thing left in this building."

She'd laughed then. She wasn't laughing now.

Because last Thursday, a request had come through the system. An email, routed through the general contact address, asking whether Meridian retained dispatch records from 2003, and if so, under what conditions they might be reviewed. The name on the email was S. Underhill.

Renata had stared at that name for a long time. Not because she recognized it—she didn't—but because of the way it was written. No title, no explanation, no flattery. Just a question, clean as a scalpel. In her experience, only two kinds of people wrote emails like that: journalists, and people who already knew the answer and wanted to see if you'd lie.

She hadn't replied. She'd forwarded it to her personal address, deleted it from the work server, and then sat with her hands in her lap feeling like a woman who had just picked up a live wire without checking whether it was live.

Then Corbett Ashe had appeared on Tuesday, smelling of cologne and intention, and the wire had started humming.

* * *

At 2:40 a.m., Renata did something she had never done in eleven years. She took box seven off the shelf.

Hollis had never said what was in it. He'd only touched it, that one time, like a man touching a headstone. It sat at chest height, third shelf, labeled in faded marker: DISPATCH LOGS 2002–2004. Ordinary. Everything dangerous in this world was labeled ordinary; that was practically a law of nature.

She set it on the table, cut the tape with her keys, and lifted the lid.

Ledgers. Dozens of them, cloth-bound, the kind with columns ruled so fine they looked like sheet music. She opened the first at random. Fares, times, route numbers, driver codes. A whole city's worth of anonymous movement, recorded nightly by people whose job was to notice everything and remember none of it.

She turned pages. Rain-smudged entries. Coffee rings. Someone in 2003 had drawn a tiny dog in the margin next to a fare on Elm Street, and Renata felt an absurd pang of affection for that long-gone dispatcher, whoever they were, bored at 4 a.m., doodling dogs among the numbers.

Then she reached a page where the handwriting changed.

Not sloppier—tighter. The numbers were still neat, but certain entries had been annotated in a cramped, slanted script, small notations squeezed into margins where no notation belonged. Fare amounts circled. Route numbers underlined twice. Next to one entry, a single word she had to hold the flashlight close to read:

*held.*

Just that. *Held.* A fare logged, a route noted, and beside it, in ink darker than the rest: *held.*

Renata closed the ledger. Then she opened it again, because closing it felt like the kind of thing a person did in a movie right before the music got scary, and she refused to score her own life.

She photographed the page with her phone. Flash off, hands steadier than she expected them to be. Then she put the ledger back exactly as she'd found it—same angle, same dust shadow—and re-taped the box, smoothing the tape down with her thumb until it looked untouched.

Chairman the spider watched her do it from the shadow of box nineteen. Renata pointed a finger at him.

"You saw nothing."

* * *

She thought she'd feel worse. That was the strange part. All week she'd been carrying the sense of a door opening somewhere behind her, of Ashe's visit and the missing letter and the email from S. Underhill converging on some point she couldn't see yet. She'd expected dread to arrive like weather. Instead, standing in the Tomb with the box re-taped, what she felt was—clean. Like she'd finally written down a number she'd been keeping in her head for eleven years.

Hollis would have understood. Hollis, who had retired to somewhere with a porch and died within a year, because some men can only rest once they've handed the weight to somebody else. She resented him for that, a little, and missed him completely.

Back at the front desk, Marisol had the headset half-off, eating vending-machine crackers with the concentration of a scholar.

"Everything okay back there?" Marisol asked. "You were in the Tomb a while."

"Inventory."

"Nobody's inventoried that room since the Clinton administration."

"I'm a trailblazer." Renata settled into her chair and pulled up the log screen. On the monitor, the night's fares scrolled past in green text, each one a small ordinary life moving through the rain. Unit nine taking a nurse home. Unit three idling outside a bar where somebody was crying, probably. The city, dispatched.

"Hey, Mari. Hypothetical question."

"Mm?"

"If somebody emailed the company asking about old records, and then a fancy lawyer showed up wanting the same records, and neither of them would tell you why—" Renata tapped a pen against the desk. "Which one would you worry about?"

Marisol considered this with her mouth full. "The lawyer. Lawyers get paid to show up. Emails are free." She swallowed. "Why? Somebody email us?"

"Somebody did."

"About what?"

"Old stuff. Two-thousand-three-ish."

Marisol shrugged and went back to her crackers. "Before my time. Before your time too, right?"

"Right," Renata said. "Before my time."

But that was the thing about the night shift, she thought, watching the green fares scroll. *Before my time* was a lie the day people got to tell. Nights kept the ledger. Nights remembered which routes ran late, which drivers came in shaking, which mornings the coffee tasted like fear. Somebody had written *held* in the margin of a page in 2003, and whatever it meant, it had waited twenty years in a cardboard box for a woman with a flashlight and a bad attitude about lawyers to find it.

* * *

At 5:50 a.m., the sky over the parking lot went the color of dishwater, and the day crew started trickling in. Renata ran the handoff—open complaints, vehicle statuses, Gerald's fish warning, reissued—and was shrugging into her coat when the phone rang.

"Meridian Taxi, good morning."

A pause. Then a voice she didn't know, low and careful, the voice of someone who had rehearsed the call and was now discovering rehearsal doesn't help.

"Yes—I sent an email last week. About the old dispatch records. I was wondering if—"

"We received it," Renata said.

Another pause. She could hear the person deciding something. Rain ticked against the office windows. Behind her, the day shift clattered into motion, oblivious.

"The answer to your question," Renata said, "is yes. We retain records going back further than anyone sensible would want. They're in poor condition and nobody's catalogued them." She lowered her voice, just slightly, just enough. "And as of this week, other people have been asking about them too. People with lawyers. So if I were you, I wouldn't wait for a convenient time. Convenient times have a way of getting cancelled around here."

Silence on the line. Long enough that she checked the display to make sure the call hadn't dropped.

"Who is this?" the voice finally asked. "Who am I talking to?"

"Night manager," Renata said. "We're the ones who remember."

She hung up before she could think better of it, wrote *S.U. — called, 5:52 a.m.* on a sticky note, folded it once, and slid it into her coat pocket, where it lay against her hip like a coal.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The lot glistened. Somewhere across town, a man named Corbett Ashe was asleep, dreaming, presumably, of unsigned letters and unlabeled boxes, unaware that the night shift had already begun keeping a record of him.

Renata buttoned her coat, stepped into the wet morning, and went home to sleep the sleep of a woman who had just become, entirely without meaning to, somebody's ally.
