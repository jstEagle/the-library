## 14. Renata Opens the Archive

The basement of the Meridian Taxi Company smelled like every basement Renata Okafor had ever worked in: damp cardboard, old toner, and the particular sweetness of paper that had given up on being important. She stood at the top of the stairs with her coffee cooling in one hand and her conscience doing something complicated in the other, and she told herself—for the fourth time that morning—that she was only going down there to reorganize.

That was technically true. The archive *did* need reorganizing. It had needed reorganizing since approximately 2011, when a pipe burst and turned half the 1990s fare cards into papier-mâché, and nobody had done anything about it because nobody cared about the archive except the fire inspector, who cared about it exactly once a year for eleven minutes.

But Renata knew why she was really going down there, and it had nothing to do with organization, and everything to do with a phone call three nights ago from a dispatcher named Sand who had never once, in six years of sharing a shift, asked her for anything.

*"Renata. I need to know if the old dispatch tapes still exist."*

She'd said yes before she'd thought about it. That was the trouble with Sand—Isandro, she corrected herself, because apparently she'd been calling them the wrong name for six years—they made requests sound like weather reports. Calm. Inevitable. The kind of thing you agreed to before you understood you were agreeing to something.

*The pre-2005 tapes,* they'd said. *Before the digital system. Dolores Kane's shifts especially.*

And now here Renata was, standing over a locked cabinet in a basement that officially did not exist, holding a cup of coffee and a decision she hadn't finished making.

* * *

Let it be known that Renata Okafor was not a reckless woman. She had raised two daughters on a dispatcher's salary, which required the fiscal discipline of a Swiss bank and the emotional regulation of a hostage negotiator. She paid her bills early. She flossed. When the company memo came down last month—the one from Vey's lawyers, all letterhead and menace, requesting "the orderly transfer of all archival materials predating 2006"—she had read it twice, signed the acknowledgment, and filed it in the drawer where memos went to be forgotten.

She had not handed over anything. She had also not refused. She had performed the great administrative art of *processing*, which could take anywhere from a week to a geological era depending on how much she liked the person asking.

Mr. Calloway, Vey's lawyer, had called twice since. Both times he'd been pleasant in the way that men are pleasant when they believe the outcome is already purchased. *"These things take time,"* Renata had agreed. *"Paperwork, you understand. Insurance requirements. We wouldn't want anything lost or damaged in transit."*

What she hadn't said—and what had been sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone ever since—was that she'd already gone down to the basement once, alone, after hours, and found the cabinet.

It was a gray steel monstrosity, older than she was, stenciled DISPATCH AUDIO 1979–2005 in paint so faded it looked like a ghost's handwriting. And taped inside the door, yellowed and curling, was a handwritten note:

*If you're reading this, the reels are catalogued by month. Don't let Augustin know we kept these. — D.K.*

D.K. Dolores Kane. Who had retired in 2004 and vanished into some condo in the hills like a woman escaping a burning building at a walking pace.

Renata had stood there reading that note for a long time. Then she'd locked the cabinet, gone back upstairs, and spent the rest of her shift being professionally, impeccably normal, while somewhere behind her sternum a small stubborn animal began digging.

* * *

Now it was Saturday morning, the quietest stretch Meridian had—weekend day shift ran on skeleton crew, mostly drivers doing oil changes and complaining about their knees—and Renata punched in the code Isandro had given her and let them in through the side door.

They arrived looking, as always, like a person assembled from the concept of discretion. Gray jacket. Neutral expression. A canvas bag held against their body like a shield.

"You look terrible," Renata said, by way of good morning.

"Thank you. I've been working on it."

"Come on. Coffee's fresh. Well—coffee exists."

They went down the stairs single file, Isandro's flashlight beam sweeping across shelves of dead technology: punch-card machines, a wall-mounted radio console with its microphone long since stolen, boxes labeled in a dozen extinct hands. Renata watched them take it in and saw something flicker across their face—not fear exactly. Recognition. Like a man returning to a house he'd been born in but couldn't remember.

"This is where Dolores sat?" Isandro asked, nodding at the old console.

"Nobody knows where Dolores sat. Dolores is a rumor with a pension." Renata set her coffee on a shelf and dug the key out of her bra strap, because she was fifty-eight years old and had learned that no pocket on earth was as secure as the place between. "But yeah. This was dispatch, before they moved us upstairs. Twenty years of nights, logged on tape."

"And the company kept them."

"The company kept *everything*. That's the thing about hoarders—you never have to dig, you just have to survive the digging." She unlocked the cabinet. The note with D.K.'s handwriting fluttered slightly in the basement's exhaled air, and Isandro leaned in and read it, and Renata watched them go very still.

"She knew," Isandro said quietly.

"Looks like it."

"She wrote this for whoever came after her." Their fingers hovered near the yellowed tape without touching it, the way you hover near something holy or radioactive. "She was leaving a trail. All this time, she was leaving a trail."

"Or she just didn't trust Augustin Vey, which in my experience is less prophecy and more basic competence." Renata pulled open the first drawer. Inside, rows of reel-to-reel tapes stood in their cardboard sleeves like books in a very boring library, each spine marked in fading marker: *03/98*, *04/98*, *05/98*... She ran her finger along them until she found what she wanted. *10/03.*

October 2003. The month Tomas Reyes died in a ravine off Route 9, though officially—per the police report Renata had read twice at two in the morning, hating herself for reading it—it was a hit-and-run that was never solved, involving a union organizer nobody in City Hall had ever heard of, in a town where everybody had heard of everything.

She lifted the reel out and felt its weight. Half a pound of magnetic tape. Somewhere in those oxide particles, if they were lucky, was a young woman's voice logging fares on the worst night of somebody's life.

"Okay," Renata said. "Here's the situation, and I want you to hear all of it before you say anything."

Isandro looked up.

"I am about to commit several forms of career suicide, ranked here in ascending order of severity." She counted on her fingers. "One: unauthorized access to company archives. Two: unauthorized copying of same. Three—" she paused, because this was the one that actually scared her, "—three, whatever it is you're going to do with what's on these tapes, which I don't know, and which I've decided I'm better off not knowing, which my daughters tell me is called 'willful blindness' and is apparently a whole legal concept, so add that to the list."

"Why are you doing it, then?"

And there it was. The question she'd been avoiding since the phone call. Renata picked up her coffee, found it cold, drank it anyway.

"My mother cleaned houses," she said. "Forty years. For rich people in the hills, the kind of people who owned things. And she used to tell me—she had this saying, she'd say it while she scrubbed other women's toilets—she'd say, *'Renata, the powerful can survive almost anything except being remembered accurately.'*" She shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, the way she got whenever she revealed she had an interior life. "I used to think that was just bitterness. Now I think it might be the only true sentence anybody's ever said to me."

Isandro said nothing. They were good at silence, Isandro Underhill—or whoever they actually were; Renata had decided not to ask, another item for the willful-blindness file. They just stood there in the basement light, waiting, the way you wait for someone to finish a hard sentence on their own terms.

"Also," Renata added, because sincerity had a short shelf life with her, "Calloway called me again Thursday and called me 'dear,' and I've decided I would rather lose my pension than let that man win."

"There it is," Isandro said. "The real reason."

"The real reason is layered, like an onion, or a felony."

* * *

The copying took longer than either of them expected, because the reel-to-reel deck—a museum piece that had survived every purge and upgrade out of pure institutional laziness—had opinions.

First it refused to spin. Then it spun too fast. Then it produced, through the little monitor speaker, a sound like a whale being interviewed, which Renata chose to interpret as encouragement.

"It needs cleaning," Isandro said, already elbow-deep in the machine's guts with a cotton swab and a bottle of isopropyl alcohol they had apparently brought for exactly this occasion, because of course they had. Of course this unremarkable, gray-jacketed person carried swabs and alcohol to a covert archive raid. "When's the last time anyone serviced this?"

"When's the last time anyone serviced *you*? Look at your hands."

Isandro looked at their hands, which were shaking slightly, and tucked them under their arms like a bird folding its wings.

"Sorry," Renata said, gentler. "Nerves talking. Mine too." She nodded at the shaking hands. "You sleep at all this week?"

"Some."

"Liar."

"A little."

"That's also a lie, but I admire the commitment." She handed them her coffee. "Drink. You're no good to history dehydrated."

While Isandro cleaned heads, Renata kept watch at the top of the stairs, which meant she stood there drinking bad coffee and thinking about Mr. Calloway's second phone call, the one where his pleasantness had developed a hairline crack. *"Ms. Okafor. The transfer of materials is time-sensitive. Mr. Vey is an elderly man with a strong sense of legacy. He would hate for the historical record to be... incomplete."*

Incomplete. As if history were a scrapbook and Vey just wanted his good angles included.

She'd said, *"I'll get to it when I get to it,"* and hung up, and then she'd sat in the break room for ten full minutes feeling the shape of the trap she was already inside. Because that was the thing about machines like Vey—they didn't need you to do anything. They just needed you to be slow, and tired, and reasonable, and eventually the statute ran out or the evidence rotted or the witness died quietly in her condo, and the machine rolled on, having never once raised its voice.

Well. Renata Okafor had spent twenty-two years raising her voice over radio static to move two hundred cabs through a city that never thanked her. She could raise it once more.

"Got it," Isandro said from below. "Clean. Try it."

The deck spun. The tape hissed. And through the static came a voice—young, female, clipped and professional with something underneath it, some tightness around the vowels—

*"Meridian dispatch, go ahead."*

Renata's skin prickled. She knew that voice. Older now, thinner, frightened—but she knew it.

"Dolores," she said.

"Yeah," Isandro said softly. "That's her."

They worked through the morning like surgeons, or burglars, or both. Isandro had brought a laptop and a converter cable from God knew where, and they fed the October 2003 reels through the deck one at a time, digitizing as they went, labeling files with a methodical care that Renata found oddly moving. *1003_night1. 1003_night2.* Each reel was maybe four hours of a city breathing: cabs dispatched, fares logged, drunks negotiated with, weather complained about. Ordinary life, recorded faithfully by a woman who had no idea she was building a time capsule.

On the third reel, Isandro stopped.

"What?"

"Night of the eleventh." They didn't look up. Their voice had gone flat and careful, the way people talk when they're carrying something heavy across ice. "Reyes disappeared on the night of the eleventh."

Renata came down the stairs and stood beside them. On the laptop screen, the waveform scrolled past like a seismograph of something long buried. Isandro's hand hovered over the playhead.

"Play it," Renata said.

And Isandro pressed the key, and through the little speakers, twenty-two years collapsed into a hiss and a young woman's voice saying words that made the basement go cold around them.

* * *

They copied everything. Not just October—all of 2003, and 2004 besides, seven hours of work compressed into a drive smaller than a matchbox. Isandro made two copies. Renata insisted on taking one.

"So if they come for you, they come for both of us," she said. "Solidarity. Also I want to hear how this ends, and I refuse to find out from a newspaper."

"They might come for you anyway. Once they figure out the archive was accessed."

"Oh, they'll figure it out. Calloway will figure it out by Tuesday, because Calloway is many things but he isn't stupid, merely well-paid." Renata locked the cabinet and stood with her hand flat on the cool steel, on the other side of which Dolores Kane's secret note waited in the dark. "You know what I keep thinking about? That note. *Don't let Augustin know we kept these.* She wrote that twenty years ago and walked out and never came back, and the whole time she was counting on some stranger finding it and doing the right thing."

"Do you think we're doing the right thing?"

Renata considered it honestly, because Isandro deserved honesty even if honesty was inconvenient. "I think right and safe stopped speaking to each other a long time ago, and I've finally chosen which cousin I like better." She shouldered her bag. "Go home. Sleep. Actually sleep—I'll know if you're lying, I've been listening to liars on the radio for two decades."

At the top of the stairs, Isandro paused. For a moment they looked like they wanted to say something large—thanks, maybe, or warning, or the name they'd buried—and then they just said, "Renata. If this goes badly for you—"

"It won't."

"But if—"

"If it does, I'll be furious, and I'll be fine, and I'll expect you at Sunday dinner." She waved them toward the door. "My daughters have questions about the famous ledger case. Apparently it's on the internet now. Apparently I'm famous by association. Go."

Isandro went, out into the gray Saturday, and Renata watched them cross the lot and disappear around the corner, small and careful and carrying half a company's sins in a canvas bag.

Then her phone buzzed. Unknown number. Local prefix.

She answered the way she answered everything, brisk and neutral. "Meridian, Okafor."

A pause. Then a woman's voice, old and dry and shaking at the edges, a voice Renata had heard an hour ago on degraded tape and would have known anywhere:

"This is Dolores Kane. I know you opened the cabinet." Another pause, longer, the sound of a woman deciding something she'd been refusing to decide for twenty-two years. "We need to talk. Tonight. Before they realize I'm the one who left the note—and before I lose my nerve again, which, fair warning, has happened twice already this decade."
