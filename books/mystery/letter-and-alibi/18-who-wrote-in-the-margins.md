## 18. Who Wrote in the Margins

The graphologist's name was Priya Nandakumar, and Isandro had met her the way they met most people who mattered—by accident, at 2 a.m., in a diner that smelled like burnt coffee and forgiveness.

She'd been a handwriting expert for the public defender's office for eleven years before she'd burned out on testifying and taken a job teaching calligraphy to retirees at the community center on Delmore Street. Isandro had wandered in one insomniac night looking for the vending machine and found instead a room full of people over seventy practicing copperplate script with the intensity of bomb technicians.

"You hold your pen like you're apologizing to it," Priya had said, materializing beside them with a cup of tea she hadn't been offered either. "Loosen up. The paper can take it."

They'd been friends ever since—the kind of friendship conducted mostly in the margins of other obligations, a text here, a shared plate of fries there. She was sixty-three, sharp as a box cutter, and had once told Isandro, apropos of nothing, "Most people think forgery is about drawing letters. It's not. It's about drawing *hesitation*. Every hand has a rhythm, and liars can't keep time."

Isandro thought about that now, sitting across from her at her kitchen table with three envelopes fanned out between them like tarot cards.

"I need you to look at something," Isandro said. "And I need you to tell me what you see without asking me why I'm asking."

Priya raised an eyebrow. "That's never how this works."

"Tonight it is."

She looked at them for a long moment. Then she pulled her reading glasses from the chain around her neck, put them on, and picked up the first letter.

* * *

Watching Priya read handwriting was like watching someone listen to music no one else could hear. Her fingers hovered above the page without touching it. Her lips moved slightly. Twice she tilted the letter toward the window light, angling it so the faint impressions of pressure showed themselves—the ghost-grooves where the pen had pressed harder than the ink admitted.

"Ballpoint," she murmured. "Cheap one. Bic or its cousin. But look—" she turned the letter sideways, "—the writer slows down on certain words. See here? 'Forgive.' And here, 'your father.' The strokes get heavier. Not because they're pressing harder. Because they're going slower. When you slow down mid-word, your hand compensates by bearing down."

"What does that mean?"

"It means those words cost them something." She set the letter down and picked up the second. "Whoever wrote this wasn't performing. A performer would have kept the pressure even. This person meant every word and hated some of them."

Isandro's throat tightened. "Can you tell me anything about them? Age, handedness—"

"Hush. I'm working." She moved to the third letter, the one with the photograph of young Emilio Underhill standing beside Augustin Vey in front of a taxi that looked like it had rolled off a movie set. One line beneath it: *He wanted to tell you. I promised him you'd read it instead.*

Priya went very still.

"Priya?"

"Give me a minute." She fetched a loupe from a drawer—a jeweler's glass, absurdly formal—and bent over the line like a surgeon over an incision. "Interesting. Very interesting. Look at the descenders. The tails on the g, the y. They hook left."

"Okay," Isandro said carefully. "Is that bad?"

"It's *left-handed*. Or at least trained by a left-handed writer, which is rarer and stranger. Left-handers drag their hands through wet ink moving right-to-left across the page, so they develop workarounds—overwriting, underwriting, a backward slant. This writer hooks their descenders back to avoid smudging. It's baked in. Deep habit. You don't learn this at fifty; you learn it at eight, when Sister Agnes raps your knuckles for mirror-writing your b's."

"So our correspondent is left-handed."

"Our correspondent is left-handed, older—I'd say sixty-plus, maybe seventy. Look at the letter forms. That capital M has a double-V construction nobody under fifty uses anymore; it's a schoolbook model from the fifties and sixties. And there's tremor control here—" she tapped a long word, "—see how the baseline wobbles but the writer corrects within two letters? That's not disease tremor. That's an aging hand with discipline. Someone who wrote for a living, or near enough."

"A clerk?"

"Better than a clerk. Look at the numbers." She flipped to the photocopied ledger page from the second envelope, the one with the deadline scrawled across the top. "Numbers are the skeleton key of handwriting analysis. People fake letters all the time, but almost nobody thinks to disguise their numerals. These fours are open-topped. These sevens are crossed with a flat European stroke. And these zeros—" she drew a circle in the air with one finger, "—are slashed. Consistently slashed, same angle every time, roughly forty-five degrees, always top-left to bottom-right."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because slashing zeros isn't a personal quirk. It's a *professional* convention. Dispatchers, telegraph operators, air traffic controllers, old-school loggers—anyone trained to write entries that another human being has to read fast and read right learns to slash their zeros so they can't be mistaken for O's or sixes. It's drilled. It's standardized. Your writer didn't invent that slash; they were taught it."

Isandro felt the floor tilt gently, the way it did when a route on the map suddenly connected to a street they knew.

"Dispatchers," they repeated.

"Mm-hm." Priya took off her glasses and polished them on her cardigan, a habit that meant she was about to say something she considered obvious. "Someone who sat at a board, took calls, logged fares or flights or freight, probably for decades. Left-handed, older, careful, emotionally invested, and trained in a very specific professional shorthand." She looked up. "You know who I'm describing, don't you?"

Isandro did.

Dolores Kane. Retired Meridian dispatcher. Thirty-one years at the board. The woman who had told them, with shaking hands and a voice like a door closing, *Your father drove that car.* The woman who had denied everything twice before letting that one sentence slip out like blood through a bandage.

Left-handed? Isandro tried to remember. They'd met her twice—in her apartment with its plastic-covered couch, and once outside the Meridian depot when she'd still worked there, years ago, signing out a shift log. Isandro could picture the pen in her hand. Could picture the angle of it.

Coming in from the left.

"Oh no," Isandro said quietly.

"Oh yes," said Priya. "Your mysterious correspondent, the person who's been terrorizing you with confessions and deadlines, is very likely someone who knows exactly what happened twenty-two years ago, was close enough to it to feel guilt about specific words, and spent their career writing dispatch logs." She folded her hands. "So. Are you going to tell me what this is about, or do I have to guess dramatically?"

* * *

Isandro told her most of it. Not everything—not the ravine, not Mateo Reyes, not the name they'd buried—but enough: the letters, the ledger, the murder, the father who'd helped cover it up and then evaporated from the world.

Priya listened the way she did everything, completely and without interruption, stirring a tea she never drank. When Isandro finished, she was quiet for a while.

"You think it's this Dolores," she said finally.

"The evidence fits."

"The evidence fits a *category*, Sand. You've got one sample population of one." She held up a finger. "How many dispatchers worked at that company over the years? Dozens? Hundreds? Any of them left-handed and over sixty fits your profile. You're doing what juries do—falling in love with the first theory that makes the story tidy."

"You just told me—"

"I told you what the *handwriting* says. The handwriting says left-handed, older, dispatcher-trained. It does not say Dolores Kane. It doesn't even say woman. Handwriting analysis can't reliably determine gender, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something." She leaned forward. "What it *can* tell you is whether two samples come from the same hand. So the question isn't 'who fits the profile?' The question is: do you have anything else written by the person you suspect?"

Isandro did.

They'd taken it without quite admitting to themselves they were taking it—a shift-change memo Dolores had handwritten and slipped under Renata's door at Meridian, complaining about the new scheduling software. Renata had shown it to Isandro weeks ago with an eye-roll and a *can you believe she still writes these by hand*, and Isandro had asked to borrow it, and somehow it had migrated from their jacket pocket to the shoebox where they kept everything that scared them.

It was in the shoebox now. Isandro retrieved it and laid it on Priya's table between the letters.

Priya put her glasses back on.

For four full minutes she said nothing. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs a neighbor's dog expressed opinions about the mail carrier. Isandro watched Priya's face for a verdict and got nothing—her expression was the professionally blank one Isandro wore themselves when a driver called in panicked and needed calm more than answers.

Finally Priya set both documents side by side and sat back.

"Well?" Isandro said.

"It's not her."

The words landed like a dropped wrench. "What?"

"It's not Dolores Kane. Or if it is, she's the best amateur forger on the Eastern Seaboard, which contradicts everything else we know about her." Priya tapped the memo. "Look. Same era, same schoolbook models, sure—that's generational, not individual. But the *hand* is different. Kane writes fast and loose; her i-dots wander, her t-bars are lazy, they float above the stem like they're embarrassed to commit. Your letter-writer is precise. Disciplined. Every t-bar lands exactly on the stem. Every margin is respected. This is someone who took pride in legibility—who understood that a written record might someday be the only witness."

"But the left-handedness—"

"Kane is left-handed too, I'd wager, given the slant. But look at her zeros." Priya pointed with a pencil. "Unslashed. Round little Os, every one. No dispatcher training in her numerals at all—which is odd, if she really ran a board for thirty years. Either she ignored the convention her whole career, or..." She trailed off, frowning at the memo.

"Or what?"

"Or she wasn't trained the way you think she was. Or—" Priya's pencil stopped. "Huh."

"What?"

"Her sevens." Priya bent closer. "Kane crosses her sevens with a little upward flick. Your letter-writer crosses them flat. Flat cross-stroke, European style. That's not a family variant, that's a different *teacher*. Different region, possibly different country." She looked up, and Isandro saw genuine puzzlement in her face, the pleasure of a puzzle refusing to be solved. "Sand, whoever wrote your letters learned their numbers somewhere else entirely. Not at Meridian. Not anywhere Kane learned hers."

Isandro stared at the letters spread across the table—the confession, the threat, the tender terrible line about a father who wanted to be heard.

Somewhere else entirely.

Not a dispatcher from the company. Not Dolores. Someone trained elsewhere, who had nonetheless ended up holding Meridian's secrets, Emilio's photograph, and Isandro's buried name.

"Could the writer have disguised their hand?" Isandro asked. "Deliberately? To throw off exactly this kind of analysis?"

Priya gave them a look of grudging respect. "Now you're thinking like a criminal. Yes—it's possible. Disguise usually shows itself as over-control: unnaturally slow speed, tremor where the writer fights their own habits, inconsistent slant. There's a little of that in the letters, but I read it as emotion, not deception." She paused. "Although. If your writer spent their career around forged documents, or around people who studied them..."

"If they spent their career around dispatch logs," Isandro said slowly, "they'd know exactly what a graphologist looks for. They'd know to change their zeros. Their sevens. Their t-bars."

"They'd know," Priya agreed, "if they wanted to be unrecognizable. Which raises a question I can't answer for you." She gathered the letters into a neat stack and pushed them back across the table, handle-with-care. "Why would someone who knows how to erase themselves from a document spend months writing you letters at all? Forgers hide. This person is *announcing*. Those are opposite instincts, Sand. Unless—"

"Unless the disguise isn't for hiding," Isandro said. "It's for framing."

Priya's eyebrows went up. "Go on."

"If the letters ever became evidence—if this ever went to court or a newsroom—someone could produce a second sample. A real one. From the actual sender. And the analysis would say *different hand, different person, letters are fraudulent.*" Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "The letters would discredit themselves. Whoever sent them built in their own alibi."

The kitchen was quiet except for the refrigerator and the dog upstairs, who had moved on to grievances with the vacuum cleaner.

"That," Priya said at last, "is a genuinely unpleasant thought. And cleverer than whoever wrote these, I'd guess—no offense to your correspondent, but this level of forethought smells institutional. Corporate. Legal-adjacent." She stood and began clearing the table, needing her hands busy. "Letters designed to fail authentication. Who benefits when the letters fall apart?"

Isandro thought of Augustin Vey in his mansion, burning papers but keeping one folder. Burning papers but keeping one folder—because a man that careful never destroyed anything he might need later, including a future reason to call his accusers liars.

"Vey," Isandro said. "If Vey's people wrote the letters—or dictated them, or had them copied—he gets a terrified messenger leading me around by the nose, and when the whole thing surfaces, he produces handwriting experts who prove the confessions are forgeries. The ledger becomes tainted by association. My father's page becomes a hoax. Everyone who believed it feels foolish." They laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow. "He wouldn't even have to deny anything. He'd just let the documents impeach themselves."

"Then again," Priya said mildly, rinsing her teacup, "you're building quite a cathedral on a foundation of maybe. Let me offer the boring alternative: your writer is exactly who they seem—an old left-handed person trained somewhere else, who disguises nothing, and whose numbers simply come from a different school. A foreign-trained dispatcher. A military clerk. A railroad man. Someone who worked for a *rival* company once, or a partner firm, and learned Meridian's secrets secondhand." She dried her hands. "Occam's razor, Sand. Sometimes the simplest answer is a sad old person with good penmanship and a guilty conscience."

"And sometimes," Isandro said, "the simple answer is what someone wants you to find."

Priya snorted. "There's the dispatcher talking. You people trust nothing." She walked Isandro to the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame. "One more thing. Free of charge, since you fed my paranoia so nicely." Her voice dropped. "The pressure analysis. On the line about your father—'I promised him you'd read it instead.' The writer slowed down there. Bore down. Like I said, those words cost them something." She met Isandro's eyes. "But here's what I didn't say at the table. The hesitation pattern on that line matches the hesitation pattern on the word 'forgive' in the first letter. Identical micro-rhythm. Same emotional fingerprint, months apart, on the two sentences that mention him."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning whoever wrote those letters wasn't threatening you and wasn't manipulating you. Not in those moments. In those moments, they were grieving." Priya opened the door onto the cold evening. "Find your left-handed dispatcher, Sand. But be gentle with them. Whoever they are, they loved your father—or owed him—and it's eating them alive."

* * *

Isandro walked home the long way, past the shuttered depot and the corner where the Meridian cabs used to queue at midnight, headlights idling like patient animals. The city was doing its nighttime impression of innocence—wet streets, lit windows, everyone inside pretending the day had gone normally.

Dolores. Not Dolores. Both, somehow. The profile fit and the samples didn't, and Priya was right that Isandro was building cathedrals, but there was one fact neither of them could argue with: somebody had taught that hand to slash its zeros, and it hadn't been Meridian.

Isandro let themselves into the apartment, turned on the single lamp, and opened the shoebox to file the letters back among the things that scared them.

Their phone buzzed against the table.

Unknown number. Local prefix. For a moment Isandro almost didn't answer—years of training, years of being unfindable, screaming the old rules. But the rules had been dying since the first envelope, and they answered.

A voice they knew, thin and papery and wrong, rushed out before Isandro could speak.

"You have to come. Tonight. Someone was in my apartment—they took the—" A breath, ragged. "Sand. *Sand.* I never told you the truth about that night. About any of it. And I don't think I'm going to get another chance to—"

The line went dead.

Isandro stood holding the phone in the lamplight, Priya's words still warm in their ears—*be gentle with them, whoever they are*—and reached for their coat.

Outside, three blocks away and idling with its lights off, a Meridian cab waited with no passenger inside.
