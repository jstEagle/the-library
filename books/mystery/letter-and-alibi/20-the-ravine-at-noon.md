## 20. The Ravine at Noon

The bus let me off at the end of Kestrel Road at eleven forty in the morning, which felt wrong in a way I couldn't immediately name. Ravines are night places. In my head, the place where Tomas Reyes died existed only after dark, lit by whatever light a dispatcher's voice could throw at it across twenty-two years of static. Seeing it at noon—birds going about their business, a jogger's footsteps slapping past on the gravel path, sun lying flat and generous on everything—felt like catching a funeral home hosting a birthday party.

I'd told myself this was research. That word does a lot of quiet work when you're frightened. *Research* sounds clinical, safe, like something done with gloves on. What it actually was: I had spent three days staring at Dolores Kane's slip of a sentence—*your father drove that car*—and then her death notice in the paper, two lines long, *peacefully in her sleep*, and I had understood that if I stayed in my apartment one more night I would start talking to the walls. So I came to the place where it all began, because the letters kept pointing here, because entry after decoded entry bent toward this stretch of green like water finding a drain.

The ravine ran behind the last row of houses on Kestrel Road, a seam of old forest the city had never quite figured out what to do with. There was a maintenance path, cracked concrete studded with dandelions, and a wooden sign so weathered the lettering had gone ghost-gray: *COLDWATER RAVINE — PLEASE STAY ON MARKED TRAILS.* Somebody had carved a heart into the post years ago; somebody else had tried to sand it off and given up halfway.

I stood at the top of the steps for a moment and did the thing I do—the thing I've trained myself to do for fifteen years, the thing that used to be survival and has lately started to feel like a symptom. I looked at the jogger until he passed. I noted the woman watering geraniums in the nearest yard, the way she hummed without melody, the fact that she never once glanced at me. I catalogued exits: the path down, the path along the ridge, the gap in the fence behind the blue house where teenagers clearly cut through.

Then I made myself stop. Because here's the joke of my life, the punchline nobody asked for: the person who wrote me those letters already knew everything about being overlooked. They'd found the one man in the city who had made an art form out of it, and they'd written his buried name on an envelope anyway. Hiding wasn't going to save me from whoever this was. It hadn't saved Dolores Kane, either, and she'd hidden for twenty-two years inside her own small apartment with the curtains drawn.

So I went down the steps.

* * *

The ravine floor was cooler than the day above it, the air smelling of wet stone and leaf rot and, faintly, someone's laundry detergent drifting from an open window up the slope. The creek was down to a trickle this time of year, threading between smooth gray stones. On either side, the banks rose steep and green, laced with roots that gripped the earth like arthritic hands.

I didn't know exactly where Reyes had died. The ledger entries gave me a route and a time—a fare logged from the corner of Alder and Ninth at 11:52 p.m., June 14th, 2003, driver number four, destination entered only as *R.* But routes bend, and ravines are long, and I'd walked maybe six minutes downstream, stepping over a fallen birch, before I stopped pretending I knew what I was looking for.

That's when I saw the flowers.

They were wrong for the setting, is the first thing I noticed. Wrong the way a piano is wrong in a machine shop. A small cluster of white carnations and one stem of something purple—I want to say statice, the kind that dries and keeps its color—laid at the base of a big-toothed aspen, maybe ten feet off the path. And beneath them, half-swallowed by moss but unmistakably deliberate, a marker.

Not a grave marker. Not anything official. A flat piece of slate, maybe the size of a dinner plate, its edges worn round, with letters scratched into it by hand. The scratching was old—the grooves had softened, filled with dark lichen—but someone had recently re-cut them, or traced them, because the letters themselves stood out pale and clean against the stone, like teeth in an old mouth.

*T.R.*

Two letters. Nothing more. No dates, no epitaph, no name spelled out. Just T.R., and above them, smaller, almost shy, a single word:

*hermano.*

Brother.

I crouched down, careful not to touch anything, and the detective in me—the one these letters had been patiently teaching for weeks now—started taking inventory.

The carnations were fresh. Not day-old fresh. Fresh fresh. The stems were still turgid, the cut ends pale and moist, and when I leaned close I could smell them over the green rot of the ravine. Someone had bought these within the last few hours. Maybe this morning. Maybe while I was still asleep in my apartment, dreaming badly about envelopes.

And the ground around the marker told the rest of the story, because ground always does if you make it talk. The moss at the base of the aspen was worn in a rough semicircle, a patch about knee-wide where it had been compressed and regrown, compressed and regrown, season after season. Someone knelt here. Regularly. For years. Long enough that the moss had learned the shape of their knees.

There was more. At the edge of the clearing, a coffee can—old tin, label long gone—had been wedged into the crook of a root, and inside it, dry and protected, were the leavings of previous visits: dried purple statice, brittle and color-faded, several seasons' worth at least. Whoever came here didn't just come once a year on an anniversary and call it grief. They came often enough to need storage.

I sat back on my heels on the path, not wanting to disturb even the dirt near the marker, and felt something turn over in my chest that I couldn't file under any of my usual headings.

Here's what I knew, or thought I knew: the letters were written by someone who loved Tomas Reyes—or at least mourned him—with a devotion that had outlasted two decades, a murder investigation that went nowhere, and every reasonable hope of justice. Someone who re-carved two letters into slate when the lichen got ambitious. Someone who brought flowers in coffee cans and kept the dead ones like ticket stubs.

And here's what I also knew, with the cold settling into me the way cold settles into ravine air at noon: that same person had spent weeks terrorizing me. Letters timed like detonations. A photocopied ledger page. A deadline measured against a statute of limitations. *The next confession names you.*

Mourner and menace, in the same handwriting. I'd compared them enough times now—the blocky capitals, the leftward slant, the odd pressure spikes on the letter T—to know it was true. Whoever knelt here with carnations was the same person who had decided that fear was the only key that would fit my lock.

"You could have just knocked," I said out loud, to no one. My voice sounded strange in the ravine, too loud, instantly apologetic. A crow somewhere upstream gave a single derisive note, as crows do.

But standing there, I started to understand it. Really understand it, for the first time, in a way that made my ears hot.

I had spent fifteen years making myself unfindable. No lease in my own name, no photos, no social footprint, a work name sanded down to three letters. If you wanted to reach Isandro Underhill—actually reach him, past all of it—you couldn't send a polite request. You couldn't knock. You'd have to do what these letters did: find the one door I couldn't ignore, the one addressed in a name I'd buried, and set it on fire.

Whoever wrote those letters had studied me the way I was now studying this marker. Patiently. Thoroughly. With love, maybe, or something adjacent to it.

*He wanted to tell you,* the third letter had said. About my father. About Emilio, driver number seven, whose name sat in the ledger on the cleanup run like a splinter working toward the surface.

I looked at the slate again. *Hermano.* Brother. Mateo Reyes, I thought—not for the first time—the brother who'd spent twenty years asking questions in the wrong rooms until the questions stopped getting answered and started getting punished. Odile Ferran had mentioned him once, back when she still talked to me in full sentences instead of encrypted fragments: *There was a brother. Mechanic. He wouldn't let it go. Then he stopped talking about it entirely, and that scared me more than the talking ever did.*

A retired Meridian mechanic. A man who would have known the fleet, the drivers, the ledgers. A man with reason to hate Augustin Vey with the kind of patient, load-bearing hatred that holds up a house for twenty years.

And a man, I realized, looking at the worn moss, the coffee can, the fresh-cut stems, who had been coming here recently. Very recently. Which meant—

Which meant he might come again.

I checked my phone. 12:34. The jogger had long since passed; the geranium woman's window had closed; the ravine had settled back into its noon hush, the trickle of the creek and the tick of cooling wood the only voices. Nobody was watching me. Nobody was watching the marker.

Except that somebody had been here this morning. And people who keep rituals like this—rituals worn into the moss itself—don't break them easily. Whatever schedule governed these visits, it was a living thing, and living things repeat.

I should have left. Every instinct I'd honed over fifteen years said leave, go home, draw the curtains, become Sand again, wait for the world to lose interest in me the way it always had. That instinct had kept me alive through things I don't write down even in my own head.

But Dolores Kane had waited, and she was dead. My father had run, and he was—whatever he was, wherever he was, a blank space in a ledger and a hole in my childhood shaped exactly like a man coming home at dawn smelling of cigarettes and fear. Waiting and running had both been tried, by better people than me, and the score was Vey: everything, everyone else: nothing.

So I did something new. I took out my phone and photographed the marker from four angles, the flowers, the coffee can, the worn moss, the path, the aspen, the fence gap behind the blue house. I photographed the light itself, and the timestamp on my screen, because timestamps are testimony. Then I found a spot thirty yards downstream where a fallen trunk made a natural bench, mostly screened by a wall of hazel, with a clean view of the marker through the leaves.

And I sat down to wait.

* * *

Waiting, it turns out, is a skill, and mine had atrophied. Dispatching teaches you a different muscle—the quick hands, the fast read, the constant low hum of *something is about to happen, be ready*. Sitting still in a ravine for hours, doing nothing but watching a piece of slate, was like trying to play piano after years of drums. My legs fell asleep twice. A spider built an entire architectural career across the brim of my hood and I let it, partly out of respect, partly because moving felt expensive.

Around two o'clock I ate the sandwich I'd wrapped in a napkin that morning without any plan beyond feeding myself, and the absurdity of it hit me sideways: eating a ham sandwich twenty feet from a murder memorial, surveilling grief. I thought about what Renata would say—she'd started texting me little dispatch-floor reports, coded as complaints about the espresso machine, each one a small flag planted on my side of the line—and I thought she'd laugh, and then go very quiet, and then ask if I was being careful, and I'd lie, and we'd both know it.

At four, the light began to go gold and long, and the ravine changed character. Shadows pooled in the hollows. The creek's voice seemed louder, or maybe I was just quieter. Twice I heard footsteps on the upper path and both times my heart performed a drum solo, and both times it was nothing—a dog walker, a pair of teenagers cutting through with the elaborate boredom of the young.

By six I had constructed and demolished four theories. The sender was Mateo Reyes (likely). The sender was someone hired by Mateo Reyes (possible). The sender was Vey himself, staging sentiment as misdirection (the paranoid special, served nightly). The sender was my father, alive, kneeling in the moss (this one I handled carefully, the way you handle a wire that might be live).

At seven, the light went from gold to copper, and I understood I had a decision to make: stay through the dark, or come back tomorrow. My body voted for home. Everything else in me—the part that had read *he wanted to tell you* forty times, the part that had listened to Dolores's shaking young voice on that degraded tape logging a fare to the ravine—voted to stay.

I was negotiating the treaty between those factions when I heard the gate.

It was a small sound, high on the ridge behind the blue house—the particular squeak of an old wooden gate that needs oil, followed by slow footsteps on the gravel path above. Not jogging feet. Not teenage feet. Feet that took each step as its own complete project, with pauses for breath between them.

Old feet.

I sank lower behind the hazel without deciding to, my body making the choice my mind was still litigating. Through the leaves I watched the path above the marker, and after a long minute—long enough for my eyes to start inventing shapes in the shadows—a figure came down the maintenance steps.

An old man, broad-shouldered even stooped, wearing a canvas jacket the color of wet bark and a cap pulled low. He moved like a man carrying something heavy that no one else could see. In one hand, a paper bag, the flowers inside it visible at the top—white, the same white as the morning's carnations.

He stopped at the top of the slope and stood there for a moment, just looking down at the marker, the way you look at a house you used to live in. Then he descended the bank with surprising sureness, root to root, a route worn into him the same way the moss was worn into the earth, and knelt.

From thirty yards, through hazel leaves, I watched him take the old flowers from the morning visit and move them gently to the coffee can with the others. I watched him lay the new ones at the base of the slate. I watched him press his palm flat against the two letters—T.R.—and hold it there, the way you'd hold a hand to a fevered forehead, or a wound.

His lips moved. I couldn't hear the words over the creek. I didn't need to. Some sentences you can read at any distance.

I had my answer. The mourner was real, the ritual was real, and the man kneeling in the copper light was almost certainly Mateo Reyes—retired mechanic, brother, and, if the shape of everything held, the author of every letter that had turned my life inside out.

I should have stood up then. Walked out of the hazel, hands visible, voice gentle: *I'm sorry. I'm Isandro Underhill. I think we've been writing to each other.*

That was the plan. That was absolutely the plan.

But as I shifted my weight to rise, the old man's head came up—sharp, sudden, a deer's motion, nothing old in it at all—and he stared directly at the hazel wall where I was hiding. Directly, it felt, at my face, though there was no way he could see me through the leaves in the failing light.

Twenty seconds passed. Thirty. The creek talked. My heart forgot its job.

Then Mateo Reyes reached into his canvas jacket, drew out an envelope, and placed it on the slate beneath the flowers. Weighted it with a stone. Stood, slowly, joint by joint, and climbed back up the bank without once looking at the hazel again.

He paused at the top of the steps. He said something—two words, maybe three, pitched not to carry, and yet somehow carrying perfectly, the way things do when they're meant for you specifically:

"Take it home."

Then the gate squeaked, and he was gone, and I was alone in the ravine with the dark coming down and an envelope sitting on a dead man's name, addressed—I could see it even from thirty yards, even in the last light—in blocky capitals, leftward-slanting, to a person I had spent fifteen years pretending did not exist.

To Isandro Underhill.
