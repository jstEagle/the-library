## 23. Emilio's Last Run

Mateo Reyes made coffee the way he did everything else—slowly, deliberately, as if the world had never once hurried him successfully.

"Percolator," he said, catching Isandro's eye as it hissed and sputtered on the hot plate of the garage apartment. "People laugh at them. Let them. A percolator tells you the truth. It takes as long as it takes."

The apartment above the garage was small and smelled of motor oil and cedar, and Isandro had slept in it for three nights now—the first nights in twenty years they had slept in a place belonging to someone else. It should have felt like exposure. Instead it felt like a coat borrowed from a generous friend, still warm at the collar.

Mateo poured two chipped mugs and set one down in front of Isandro on the worktable he'd cleared of carburetor parts for the occasion. He sat across from them on a stool that groaned under him, and for a long moment he just looked at his coffee, steam rising between them like something being released from custody.

"You want to know about your father," he said.

Isandro's throat tightened. "I want to know what he told you. Before he disappeared."

"Before he disappeared." Mateo repeated it the way a man repeats a phrase in a language he's still learning. "You know, for twenty-two years I told myself he *left*. Ran off, the way men do. It was easier than the other thing." He looked up. "It's not easier anymore. So. Sit. Drink. This will take a while, and the coffee's going to get cold, and that's fine. That's the percolator's whole philosophy."

* * *

"He came to me in October of 2003," Mateo began. "Three weeks after Tomas died. Three weeks after we buried my brother with the whole city pretending he'd gone to visit cousins in Guadalajara." His jaw worked. "You remember what they said back then. The papers. 'Union organizer leaves town amid contract disputes.' Amid contract disputes. My brother didn't own a suitcase. He owned two pairs of boots and a guitar with a broken string he swore he'd fix someday."

Isandro held the mug with both hands. "I was eleven. I remember the name. My mother went quiet for a month, and I thought—I don't know what I thought. That she was sick."

"She wasn't sick. She was *afraid*. Your mother, Elena—she was the smartest dispatcher Meridian ever had, and she knew exactly what a quiet month meant." Mateo's voice gentled. "She knew what her husband knew."

"Tell me."

Mateo set his mug down and laced his fingers together, and Isandro realized he was steadying himself the way a man steadies himself before lifting something very heavy.

"Emilio came to my garage at two in the morning. Knocked the way he always knocked—three times, then a fourth, like an apology for the first three. He looked like he hadn't slept since the ravine. Because he hadn't. And he sat where you're sitting, and he told me everything, and I have spent twenty-two years wishing to God he'd stayed home."

* * *

"It was the eleventh of September, 2003," Mateo said. "A Thursday. Tomas had a meeting at the union hall that ran late, and Vey had him flagged—every driver on the night board knew it. Fare 44, Route 9, the ravine road. On paper it was a routine pickup: a fare from the industrial park to the ravine overlook. Nobody takes a cab to a ravine overlook at eleven at night. Everybody knew what it was anyway, and everybody looked at their shoes."

"The ledger entry," Isandro said. "Route 9, Fare 44."

"Route 9, Fare 44." Mateo nodded. "Your mother coded it herself, later. But that night it was just a job on the board, and the man who took it—" He stopped. Started again. "Vey didn't drive. You understand that. Vey never drove anything in his life except the company and the men in it. He had a driver he trusted the way a surgeon trusts a scalpel. Quiet man. Did what he was told. And when the job came back wrong—when the fare didn't get out of the car on his own two feet—Vey needed the problem to go away, and he needed it to go away *quietly*, and he needed someone he could own."

"Emilio."

"Emilio." Mateo's voice cracked on the name and he let it. "Your father drove that car up the ravine road at one in the morning with Tomas Reyes in the trunk, and he drove it back empty, and he came home at dawn smelling of cigarettes and river water, and he never told your mother, and he never told anyone—except me. Three weeks later, at two in the morning, in this exact garage, on that exact stool."

Isandro looked at the stool. It was nothing. It was a stool. It was suddenly the most terrible object Isandro had ever seen.

"Why you?" they managed. "Why tell you?"

"Because I was the mechanic who signed off on that car the next morning." Mateo's laugh had no humor in it at all. "Vey sent the car in for 'routine maintenance.' Interior detail. Steam-clean the upholstery, replace the floor mats, the whole works. And Emilio stood there while I worked and watched me find what the steam-cleaning didn't get, and he watched me say nothing, and that's how we knew each other from then on. Two men who knew. It's a terrible kind of friendship. I don't recommend it."

"What did he say? Exactly. As close as you remember."

Mateo closed his eyes. "I remember all of it. You remember the things you wish you could flush out of yourself, and they just—settle. Like silt." He opened his eyes. "He said, 'Mateo, I need to tell a living person, because if I only tell the dead, I'll start hearing answers.' That's how he opened. Like a man walking into a confessional that happened to have a workbench in it."

* * *

"He told me the whole night," Mateo continued. "The pickup, the argument he heard through the partition—he stayed in the car, he swears it, and I believe him, because Emilio couldn't have hurt a fly, he was the kind of man who apologized to furniture he bumped into. Vey's man did it. Emilio heard it, and then he was in it, because Vey's man looked at him through the partition and said, 'Congratulations, you're a driver with a clean record and a family, and the company takes care of its people.'"

Isandro's hands had gone cold around the mug. "And then the body."

"And then the body. Vey's man told him where. The ravine, the same spot—no, that's not right, I'm getting ahead of myself, and your father would correct me, he was precise, he was a dispatcher's husband down to the bone." Mateo held up a finger. "First: the car. He drove it to the reservoir road, and there was a second man waiting with a boat, and Emilio was told to help load and forget. He helped load. He said the weight of it was—he used a word I didn't expect. He said it was *administrative*. Like the whole thing had been reduced to paperwork, and he was just moving a file from one drawer to another."

Isandro thought of their own nights in the dispatch chair, rerouting the city's loneliness one fare at a time, everything reduced to numbers on a board. *Administrative.* The word fit the whole rotten company like a key.

"Then the insurance," Isandro said quietly. "The ledger page."

Mateo's eyebrows went up. "Your mother's blood in you, all right. Yes. The insurance." He leaned forward. "Here's the part I've never told anyone, and I'm telling it now because you've earned it, decoding that thing half-blind the way you did. Emilio went back to the office before his shift ended. He knew your mother kept the master ledger, and he knew the night's entries would be coded by morning, and he knew—because he'd just learned it the hard way—that coded things can be rewritten. So he made a copy. By hand. The whole night's page, every fare, every route, every driver number, in his own handwriting, including the entry Vey's people were going to scrub. And he hid it."

"Where?"

"He wouldn't tell me. He said, 'If I tell you, you're not a friend anymore, you're a target.' And I argued with him, God help me, I argued. I said, Emilio, you can't fight a man like Vey with a piece of paper, and he said—" Mateo's voice caught, steadied. "He said, 'You can't fight him with anything else. Bullets he's got. Money he's got. But a page in a dead man's handwriting, with the fare that never happened written on it—that scares him, because paper is patient. Paper waits.'"

Isandro sat very still. Somewhere below them, in the garage, a pipe knocked twice and went quiet.

"He kept it for insurance," Isandro said. "Against Vey."

"Against Vey, and against the other man, and against the world in general. He said if anything happened to him, the page would come out. He said Vey knew it, and that was the only reason he was still breathing. A dead Emilio was a loose page. A live Emilio was a quiet one." Mateo's mouth twisted. "It worked for eleven months."

* * *

The coffee had gone cold. Neither of them had touched it in some time.

"Eleven months," Isandro repeated. "Then what?"

"Then August of 2004. He came here one last time. He'd lost weight. He had a bruise on his jaw he said was a door." Mateo's hands flattened on the table. "He told me Vey had started asking about the copy. Not accusing—Vey never accuses, he *inquires*, warmly, the way a spider inquires. And Emilio said the math had changed. That a live Emilio with a page was becoming more expensive than a dead one, and he could feel the ledger of his own life being balanced against him."

"So he ran."

"So he ran. That's what I believed for twenty years, and I want you to hear the whole of it, because you deserve the whole of it." Mateo took a breath. "He told me he was going to disappear. He said it was the only move left on the board—take the page, take himself, and let Vey spend the rest of his life wondering where both of them were. He said your mother would understand, someday, and that his kid—" Mateo's eyes came up and met Isandro's, and held. "He said his kid would hate him, and that he'd rather be hated alive than mourned on schedule. His words. *Mourned on schedule.* He knew exactly what Vey would do with a funeral."

Isandro's eyes burned. They had spent a childhood constructing a father out of absence—a coward, a wanderer, a man who chose the road—and here was the truth sitting in a garage that smelled of motor oil: a man who chose disappearance the way a soldier chooses to fall on a wire. Not because it was noble. Because it was the only shape the sacrifice could take.

"He said something else," Mateo said softly. "At the door. Last thing he ever said to me. He said, 'If I don't make it out, the page will find its way to my kid. Paper's patient. And my kid will be patient too. They get that from Elena.'"

Isandro laughed—a wet, broken sound that surprised them both. "I'm not patient. I'm a night dispatcher. I'm the least patient person in this city."

"You decoded a twenty-two-year-old cipher in a few weeks," Mateo said. "Don't sell yourself short."

* * *

"But you don't believe he made it out," Isandro said, when they could speak again. "You said it's not easier anymore. The other thing."

Mateo was quiet for a long moment. Outside, morning was arriving in the small, gray increments it used in this part of town, and the light through the garage window caught the dust in the air like evidence suspended in amber.

"No. I don't believe he made it out. And I'll tell you why, and it's the worst part, so hold onto your coffee." He turned his mug slowly on the table. "Six months after Emilio vanished, Vey sent a man to my garage. Friendly visit. Company man, all warmth and generalities—you've met the breed. And he said, 'Mr. Reyes, the founder worries about loose pages. Old paper, it yellows. It crumbles. It's a shame when that happens.' And I said, 'I wouldn't know anything about paper.' And he smiled and said, 'No. I don't suppose you would. The founder just wanted you to know that everything gets filed, eventually. Everything.'"

Isandro felt the cold move up their arms. "He was telling you Emilio was dead."

"He was telling me the file was closed." Mateo's voice was flat and steady, the voice of a man who had carried this for twenty years and knew every seam of its weight. "And then, two years later, Emilio's effects showed up. A box, no return address, postmarked from nowhere in particular. Inside: his wedding ring. His watch, stopped. And a note in his handwriting, one line, that I have read ten thousand times."

"What did it say?"

Mateo recited it from memory, the way you recite something that has been carved into you:

"'If this reaches Mateo, tell my kid: I kept my promise, and the paper is where I said.'"

Isandro's heart stuttered. "Where he *said*? Where did he say?"

"He never said. Not to me. Not to anyone I ever found." Mateo spread his hands. "I've spent twenty years turning that line over. *The paper is where I said.* Said to whom? Not me. Not your mother—I asked her, near the end, and she went white and said she'd never heard him say any such thing. Which means he said it to someone else. Or—" Mateo's eyes narrowed, and Isandro watched the thought arrive in him like weather. "Or he said it somewhere it could be *read*. Emilio thought in dispatch codes. Routes, fares, entries. He didn't hide things in places. He hid them in *systems*."

Isandro sat forward, the exhaustion of three sleepless nights burning off all at once, replaced by something sharper. A dispatcher's husband. A man who coded his whole life. *The paper is where I said.*

"He'd have said it somewhere official," Isandro murmured. "Somewhere permanent. Somewhere Vey's people would walk past every day and never look twice, because it was already supposed to be there—"

Mateo was already on his feet, stool scraping. "The office," they said together.

"The archive," Mateo said. "The wall. The founder's memorabilia—Vey's got that whole corridor of framed garbage, twenty years of ribbon-cuttings and newspaper clippings, and every driver in the company walks past it every shift and sees nothing, because who looks at a founder's wall?" He was grinning now, a fierce, grieving grin. "Where would a dispatcher hide a page? In the one place the whole company agrees not to read."

Isandro stood, heart hammering, and for the first time since the letters began, the fear and the hope in their chest were pointing the same direction.

"Mateo," they said. "I need to get into Meridian's office."

* * *

Mateo was already pulling a canvas jacket off a hook, moving with the sudden urgency of a man twenty years younger. "Renata's on days this week," he said. "And she owes me a favor from 1998 I've never once called in."

"What kind of favor?"

Mateo paused at the door, and the gentle humor came back into his face like sun through parting cloud.

"The kind involving a company truck, a broken transmission, and a story about a raccoon that I will tell you in the car," he said. "Come on, kid. Your father waited twenty-two years. He can wait forty more minutes—but let's not make him wait a minute past that."

The percolator hissed once on the cold burner, telling the truth to an empty room, and they went out together into the gray morning, two people carrying one page between them, toward the wall where it had been hiding in plain sight all along.
