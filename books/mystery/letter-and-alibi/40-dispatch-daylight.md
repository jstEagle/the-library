## 40. Dispatch, Daylight

The first thing I learned about day shift was that the city has a different voice in the morning.

Night shift had taught me its whispers—the 2 a.m. fare from the hospital who didn't want to talk, the drunk singing to himself on Hold Three, the long silences between calls where you could hear the rain working. Daylight Meridian was all clatter and coffee and someone's radio playing a station that only existed between eight and ten. People called in with their whole lives attached: school pickups, airport runs, a woman who needed a cab that could accommodate "one parrot, medium-sized, opinionated."

"Opinionated how?" I asked her.

"He disagrees with traffic."

I liked her immediately. That was the strange part of this new life—how much I liked things now. How much liking cost me, and how willing I'd become to pay it.

My desk sat by the window, which Renata had insisted on. "You spent twenty years facing a wall," she said, dragging the chair around herself like it was a personal mission. "You've earned a view." The view was the parking lot, mostly, and beyond it the gray lip of the harbor, and beyond that the ravine—which I could see if I leaned, just the green seam of it, the place where everything started and ended. I'd expected it to hurt, looking that direction. Mostly it just looked like trees doing what trees do: standing over something buried and saying nothing, the way everyone did for twenty-two years.

Except me. I'd stopped saying nothing.

The name badge was laminated, because Renata believed in lamination the way some people believe in prayer. ISANDRO UNDERHILL, DISPATCHER II — TRAINING LEAD. Under new ownership, Meridian had reorganized its titles, and Dispatcher II apparently meant I got a slightly better chair and the solemn duty of teaching nervous newcomers not to panic when three drivers went silent at once.

I wore the badge clipped high, where it couldn't be missed. First week, I kept touching it like a bruise—checking it was real, checking I was real. By the second week I'd stopped checking. That's how belonging works, I think. You stop auditing it.

* * *

The new dispatcher's name was Priya Chandra, and she arrived on a Tuesday with a thermos the size of a fire extinguisher and a notebook already full of questions she was too polite to ask out loud. She was twenty-four, quick, and had the particular intensity of someone who had read every article ever written about the Ledger of Meridian and was now sitting six feet from one of its footnotes.

I let her stew for three days. Not out of cruelty—I'd been invisible too long to enjoy making anyone squirm—but because there's a rhythm to training. You don't start with the famous parts. You start with the phones.

"Priority queue is medical first, always," I told her Thursday morning, walking her through the screen. "Then weather holds, then everything else. The computer will try to tell you the order. The computer is wrong about half of everything, and your job is to know which half."

"The ledger," she said, on Friday, finally cracking, in the middle of a lesson on shift-change protocol. "Sorry. I've been trying not to ask. But—you were there, right? You're the one who decoded it? Odile Ferran's source? The—" she lowered her voice like the building might be bugged, which, given our history, was not an unreasonable instinct, "—the byline?"

I looked at her. Young face, open, hungry for the story the way I'd once been hungry for silence. Somewhere behind her, a driver named Kowalski was complaining into Channel 4 about a seagull that had gotten into his cab, and honestly, after everything, a seagull felt like exactly the right amount of drama for my life now.

"Pull up a chair," I said.

* * *

Here's what I told her, or the shape of it, while the morning calls came in and we took them together, her hands learning the board under mine:

I told her about two envelopes arriving on a rain-gray morning, both bearing a name I hadn't answered to in two decades. I told her about being a person who had made an art form of being overlooked—cash rent, no photographs, a name sanded down to "Sand" until even I sometimes forgot the grain underneath. I told her about a route ledger in my dead mother's boxes, marginal notes in a stranger's handwriting, and a cipher where fares were times and routes were addresses and driver numbers were people.

Priya wrote none of this down. She just watched me with her thermos cradled in both hands like a small warm animal.

"There was a man," I said. "Tomas Reyes. A union organizer. He got into a Meridian cab on a night in 2003 and never got out again—not alive, anyway. The founder, Augustin Vey, ordered it done because Reyes was close to something. And my father drove the car afterward." My voice did what it always did there—a small hitch, a pothole in the sentence. Twenty-two years later and still the road wasn't smooth. "He helped hide the body. Then he tried to leave proof behind, and then he left everything behind, including me. For a long time I thought those were the same act. They weren't. He hid the page that would have convicted Vey inside a picture frame on the office wall behind us. In plain sight. Where nobody looks, because looking is dangerous and everybody knows it."

"And the letters?" Priya said softly. "Who sent them?"

"Mateo Reyes. Tomas's brother. A mechanic here for thirty years. He was dying—he is dying, slowly, stubbornly, on his own schedule—and he couldn't crack the cipher alone, and he couldn't trust anyone inside Vey's reach, so he found the one person whose father was on that ledger page and terrified her into helping." I smiled despite myself. "He apologized. Eventually. After I staked out the ravine through an entire night shift waiting for him to show up and put flowers on his brother's marker. He brought carnations. I brought a thermos of terrible coffee. It was, objectively, the worst first date in the history of grief."

Priya laughed, then caught herself, then decided laughing was allowed. It was. That was another thing daylight taught you: you were allowed.

"What happened to Vey?" she asked.

"He's ninety-one years old and he lives in a facility now, the kind with locks you can't pick from the inside. The obstruction charges stuck before anyone had to settle the statute question—turns out when you burn papers, bribe witnesses, and order a 'final cleanup' on a journalist's sources, the law finds a door. He never confessed. At the end, when I stood in his room and read my father's letter aloud to him, he just listened with his hands folded like a man at a recital. Then he offered me a job." I shook my head at the memory, at the sheer durable audacity of the man. "Power doesn't understand refusal. It hears it as a negotiation."

"And your dad?" she asked, gentler now. "They found him?"

"In the reservoir. Two years ago this spring." Outside the window, the light moved on the harbor the way it does in autumn, low and forgiving. "We buried him properly. Mateo came. Renata came. Odile Ferran came, and cried, which I mention only because she'd deny it. There were maybe twelve people there, and every single one of them knew my full name, and I stood there in the open air letting them know it too." I tapped the badge. "Turned out that was the whole prize. Not the indictment. This."

* * *

At noon, Mateo called the dispatch line, which he did most days now, officially to report that Cab 12's meter was running sticky and unofficially because he liked hearing my voice come through a Meridian radio with my real name attached to it.

"Cab Twelve to dispatch," he rasped. "Meter's fine, actually. Just checking the new kid hasn't scared you off yet."

"The new kid is excellent," I said, and watched Priya sit up straighter. "She asks better questions than I did at her age."

"Low bar," Mateo said. "You spent twenty years asking no questions at all." A pause, and then, quieter, the way he said the true things: "Lunch Thursday? Dolores's grave could use the visit. I'll bring the carnations."

"I'll bring the coffee," I said. "Terrible, as tradition demands."

When I hung up, Priya was studying me with an expression I recognized from mirrors—someone recalibrating what a life could hold.

"You knew all of them," she said. "The mechanic, the manager, the reporter. The people in the story."

"That's the part nobody prints," I said. "The ledger gave back the dead man's name. But the living ones—that was the actual work. You can't decode your way into a family. Believe me. I tried. It takes showing up. It takes lunch on Thursdays. It takes wearing your own name on a laminated rectangle and flinching the first forty times somebody reads it out loud."

"Fifty-two," Renata said, passing behind us with a stack of schedules. "I counted."

"You counted?"

"Somebody had to. You certainly weren't going to admit it was happening." She set the schedules down, patted my shoulder once—Renata's affection came rationed, like wartime sugar—and pointed at Priya. "Break her in well. And Underhill—Harbor Group's rep is coming at two about the training manual. Try to look like the famous one they hired."

"No promises," I said.

* * *

Two o'clock came and went in a blur of manuals and liability language, and by four the light had gone gold and long across the parking lot, and the day shift was winding down into the soft handoff hour where the city changes voices again. Priya logged her last call—a fare to the airport, a parrot owner returning home, the bird reportedly victorious—and stretched, and looked at the board, and then at me.

"One more question," she said. "And then I'll never ask anything again, I swear."

"You'll ask plenty tomorrow. That's the job."

"The letters. The ones that started it. Do you still have them?"

I opened the bottom drawer of my desk. Inside, in a folder that had once held overtime forms, lay three envelopes, softened at the corners from handling, their handwriting slanted and careful—left-handed, older, trained as a dispatcher, though in the end it hadn't been Dolores's hand at all, just a style she'd learned from the same school of night work. Next to them, in a plastic sleeve Odile had insisted on, a photocopied ledger page, and beneath that, folded twice, a letter in a different hand entirely, addressed to *my child*, in a father's careful print.

"Yeah," I said. "I keep them. Not as threats anymore. As receipts. Proof that somebody thought I was worth finding, even when I'd done everything possible to be lost."

Priya nodded slowly, the way young people do when they've been handed something heavier than they expected and lighter too.

"So," she said. "The famous ledger. The murder, the cover-up, the cipher, the ravine, the frame behind the wall—" she gestured at the wall behind us, at the empty hook where the memorabilia frame used to hang, replaced now by a framed copy of Odile's front page, THE LEDGER OF MERIDIAN, which Renata refused to take down no matter how many times corporate suggested something more "brand-forward." "How does the story actually start?"

Outside, the harbor light was fading toward evening, and somewhere out there a night-shift dispatcher I'd helped train was settling in with their coffee, ready to listen to the city whisper. The phone blinked. A fare wanted picking up on Route 9.

I smiled, and touched the badge on my chest without needing to check it was there, and began.

"It starts with rain," I said, "and two envelopes in a mailbox, and a name I thought I'd buried—"

The line lit up. I let it ring once, just once, the way you do when the story matters more than the call.

"—and a man who drove the last run of a night in 2003, who happened to be my father."
