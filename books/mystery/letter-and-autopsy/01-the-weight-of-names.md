## 1. The Weight of Names

There is a particular hour in Hollow Creek when the town pretends it doesn't exist. It comes around four in the morning, after the last truck has rattled over the bridge on Route 9 and before the bakery ovens wake up on Delancey Street. In that hour the fog comes off the creek and lies in the streets like something spilled, and the only light burning is the one above the back door of the county morgue.

I have kept that light burning for ten years. Not for sentiment—though I am not entirely without it—but because the dead deserve to see where they're going, and the living deserve to know someone's awake when they knock.

My name is Cyrus Vasquez, and I am the coroner of a town that would prefer I were anything else.

* * *

The ritual started before I was old enough to call it a ritual. My mother did it, actually—spoke names over things. She'd talk to the bread dough. She'd talk to the garden. She said everything you handle long enough deserves to hear its own name, because a thing that goes unnamed gets treated carelessly. I was nine years old and thought she was being silly. Then she and my father burned in a house fire that the county called accidental, and I learned what happens to people who go unnamed: they get filed away.

So now, every body that crosses my table hears me say their name out loud. First thing, before the Y-incision, before the photographs, before any of the machinery of my trade. Just the name, spoken into the cold air of the examination room, where the only other listener is the hum of the refrigeration unit.

"Marjorie Teale," I said this morning, at 6:40 a.m., to an eighty-one-year-old woman who had died in her sleep three days after her cat stopped visiting her bedroom. "Marjorie Alice Teale."

The middle name matters. The full name matters. A name spoken whole is a small act of witness, and witness is the entire job, whatever the county paperwork says. The paperwork says I determine cause and manner of death. What the paperwork doesn't say—and what nobody in Hollow Creek likes to remember—is that determining how someone died means paying attention to them more closely than anyone ever did while they lived. I know where Marjorie Teale carried her worry: left shoulder, knotted so tight the muscle had remodeled itself over decades. I know she'd broken her right wrist at nineteen and set it herself, badly, because there was no money for a doctor and apparently no one to insist on one. I know her heart gave out quietly, without drama, the way she seems to have done everything.

I knew her better than her nephew did, and her nephew lives eleven miles away.

That's not a boast. It's the loneliest fact of my profession, and I've had twenty years to make peace with it, which is to say I have made no peace with it whatsoever. You get good at the dead. You get fluent in them—their habits written in their bones, their secrets stitched into their organs, their whole biography legible to anyone willing to read slowly. And then you go to the diner and order coffee and realize you cannot hold up your end of a conversation about the weather, because weather is nothing compared to what you spent all night reading.

Competence is a fine disguise for loneliness. I recommend it. It fits well and never needs mending.

* * *

I finished with Marjorie by eight, wrote *natural causes — cardiac arrhythmia* in a hand steadier than my mood, and was rinsing my instruments when the phone rang. Sheriff Dane Okonkwo. He does most of his talking by phone these days, ever since the incident at the Hollis funeral—I'll come back to that; the incident is sort of the point of me.

"Morning, Cyrus." Dane has a voice like gravel poured slow. He also has a habit of pausing a half-second too long before saying my name, as if checking it against some internal list. "Got a hunter found dead up past the ridge road. Fell from a tree stand, looks like."

"Looks like," I repeated.

"That's what the deputy says."

"And what do you say?"

Another pause. Dane is a careful man in a careless town, which makes him either the most honest person in Hollow Creek or the most exhausted. Possibly both. "I say the tree stand's got a strap that looks cut, but the deer season's been open two weeks and hunters cut straps all the time when they snag 'em. I say bring him in and tell me what you find."

"I always do."

"Yeah." And he hung up, which is Dane's version of affection.

* * *

The hunter was Gerald Pruitt, fifty-four, and by noon I had said his name aloud—"Gerald Thomas Pruitt"—and begun the slow conversation the dead have with those who listen.

Here is what Gerald told me. He told me he was right-handed, from the wear on his glove. He told me he drank more than his wife knew, from his liver, though I'd keep that between us. He told me he'd been losing weight fast in the last six months, the kind of loss that isn't dieting. And he told me, through a fracture pattern in his left forearm and the absence of certain defensive injuries, that he had not grabbed for anything on the way down.

People who fall accidentally reach. It's reflexive, hardwired deeper than thought. Hands come up, arms extend, palms take the brunt. Gerald's hands were clean of it. His arms hung in the posture of a man who was already unconscious—or already dead—before gravity got involved.

And the strap. Dane was right that hunters cut straps. But they cut them with knives, quickly, annoyed, leaving ragged pulls in the webbing. This cut was clean. Single pass. A blade that knew what it was doing.

I sat with Gerald for a while after I finished, which is another part of the ritual nobody taught me and which I'd be embarrassed to admit if embarrassment still had jurisdiction over me. I sat and I said, "Gerald Thomas Pruitt, somebody didn't want you coming down off that ridge under your own power. Let's find out who."

Then I picked up the phone and called Dane, and we had the conversation we have had three times now in ten years. The conversation where I say the word *homicide* and the town of Hollow Creek inhales and does not exhale until the word is put away again.

* * *

Three times. Let me be precise about that, because precision is the only inheritance I trust.

The first was Dale Wicker, seven years ago. Ruled a fall from his own roof while clearing gutters. Except Dale had a shattered ankle consistent with landing feet-first from a height of perhaps four feet, not eighteen, and a contusion on the crown of his head from something that wasn't shingle. Somebody had hit Dale Wicker with something heavy and rolled him off a low ledge onto the driveway. I amended the manner of death. The sheriff's office investigated for eleven days. The file closed with a phrase I have come to loathe: *insufficient evidence to proceed.*

The second was Lena Marsh, four years ago. Found in her car at the bottom of the boat ramp at Miller's Bend, drowned, ruled accidental—a car slipping its brake. But Lena's seatbelt was buckled, and Lena, whom I'd known slightly in life, was a woman who once drove forty minutes to return a wallet containing three dollars. She did not forget her brake. More to the point, her blood alcohol was clean, and the gearshift was in park, and park does not slip. I amended. Eleven days. *Insufficient evidence.*

The third was the one that ended dinner invitations I hadn't been receiving anyway. Old Sam Corliss, two years ago, ruled a heart attack in his workshop. Sam's heart was indeed bad—I don't dispute the county on hearts; I dispute everything else. There was a punctate injury at the base of his skull, small, easily missed unless you turn the head over, which the responding deputy had not seen fit to do. Something thin and pointed. An awl, maybe. Sam's own awl was missing from its pegboard, and the pegboard outline where it hung was dusted clean around it, recently wiped. I amended. This time it wasn't eleven days. This time the county sent a man from the state office to review my findings, and the state man used the word *overzealous* twice, and the Founders' Society passed a resolution commending the coroner's office for its service while cutting my budget by a third in the same fiscal year.

That's Hollow Creek arithmetic: praise publicly, punish privately, and never, ever connect the two in writing.

* * *

You might ask why I stay. People have asked, in the way people ask questions they hope will end conversations. A reclusive coroner in a dying mill town, correcting verdicts nobody wants corrected—you could do that anywhere, they say. Why here?

Because here is where the unnamed are.

Because my parents' death certificate sits in a drawer in my office, yellowed and sealed, with the word ACCIDENTAL typed across it in a font that hasn't been used since before I was born, and every correction I make is a letter mailed to a case I can't reopen. Because the fire that killed Elias and Rosa Vasquez twenty years ago this autumn was investigated for six days, ruled accidental—electrical, they said, though our house had been rewired the year before—and sealed by court order at the request of parties I have never been able to identify. Sealed. A house fire with two bodies and a surviving child, sealed like evidence in a trial that never happened.

I was fifteen. They shipped me to an aunt in Ohio who collected obligations the way other people collect stamps, and I came back twelve years later with a medical degree and a manner of death certification and a name that made half the town look at their shoes. Vasquez. Say it in the diner and watch the forks pause. We weren't founders. We weren't even old. My father bought the property on the aquifer road in 1987 from a family that had already gone west, and twenty years later the house burned and the county shrugged, and the Founders' Society held its first anniversary dinner that same winter—for the town, they said, to heal.

Heal. As if the wound were communal. As if my grief had been a potluck item everyone could share.

* * *

But that's later. That's the drawer I keep locked. This morning there was only Gerald Pruitt on the table and the slow work of making his death mean something official, which is the closest thing to meaning the county permits.

By late afternoon I had my report drafted. *Blunt force trauma antemortem; fall postmortem or perimortem without protective reflex; ligature cut with sharp instrument.* Manner: pending, pending, pending—because Dane asked me to hold the paperwork until he could walk the ridge again with daylight and a warrant for Gerald's hunting partners' phones. Dane is learning. Ten years ago he'd have sighed and said *Cyrus, sometimes a fall is just a fall,* and gone back to his coffee. Now he walks ridges twice. I'd like to think my stubbornness is contagious. He'd probably say it's indigestion.

I locked up at six. The fog was coming back in off the creek, patient as paperwork, and the streetlights came on one by one down Delancey like witnesses raising their hands. From the diner I heard the ordinary music of a town closing its day—flatware, laughter, the register bell. Nobody in there was thinking about Gerald Pruitt. Nobody in there was thinking about Dale Wicker or Lena Marsh or Sam Corliss, whose names I carry the way other men carry wallets.

Nobody in there was thinking about the fourth name. The one I haven't said aloud in twenty years because I don't yet know whose name it is—the name that belongs to whatever or whoever the sealed file is hiding. I keep a space for it in the ritual. Every time I speak a name over a body, I leave a silence afterward, one beat long, where the fourth name will go when I finally earn it.

It was in that silence, standing on the morgue steps with my collar up against the fog, that I saw the lights at Miller's Bend. Flashlights, moving quick and wrong—not the lazy sweep of kids looking for a place to drink, but the urgent, crisscrossing pattern of people searching for something they hoped not to find.

Dane's voice came through my phone before I could dial. For the first time in ten years, he sounded shaken.

"Cyrus. Get down to Miller's Bend." A pause, longer than his usual half-second. Longer than any pause he'd ever given me. "There's a body in the water. And Cyrus—it's wearing your coat."
