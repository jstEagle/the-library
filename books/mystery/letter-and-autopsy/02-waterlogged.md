## 2. Waterlogged

The river gives back what it takes on its own schedule, and it never apologizes for the delay.

I was elbow-deep in the county's paperwork when Deputy Ferro called—Tomas Ferro, twenty-six years old, still says "sir" to me even though I've told him twice that I answer to "Cyrus" and once, memorably, to "hey, you." His voice had a particular texture I've learned to catalog the way other people learn wine notes. There's the flat tone he uses for drunk drivers. The careful tone for domestic calls. And this one—the one where the words come out slightly too fast, like they've been rehearsed in the truck and are still warm from it.

"Dr. Vasquez? We've got a body at Miller's Bend. Fisherman found it snagged on the willow roots." A pause. "It's been in the water a while."

"Define 'a while,'" I said, already reaching for my bag.

"That's—I think you should just come see."

Which is the sentence every coroner learns to dread, because it means the scene has something in it that a deputy doesn't have vocabulary for yet. I locked up the office, told the filing cabinet I'd be back, and drove out Route 9 with the windows down because October in Hollow Creek is the one month the air forgives you for living here.

* * *

Miller's Bend is where the creek slows down and gets ideas above its station, widening into something the founding families liked to call a river when they were selling land to flatlanders. The willows there are old enough to remember things, which is more than I can say for most of the town's residents. When I arrived, two deputies had strung tape between the trees with the ceremonial seriousness of men who mostly use that tape for parade routes, and old Walt Jensen—the fisherman—sat on an overturned bucket being consoled with bad coffee and worse company.

Walt had caught a lot of things at Miller's Bend over the years: bass, catfish, a child's bicycle, once a complete set of dentures that he wore as a joke until his wife made him stop. He looked up at me with the expression of a man whose luck had finally curdled.

"Cyrus," he said. "I didn't touch him. Just hooked his coat trying to get loose of the roots, and then—" He rubbed his face. "Forty years I've fished this bend. Forty years."

"I know, Walt."

"He was just sitting there. Like he'd been waiting for somebody specific to come along."

Walt Jensen, retired schoolteacher, occasional prophet. I noted it and moved on.

The body lay at the waterline where the deputies had drawn it clear, on its back, arms at its sides with a neatness the river rarely manages on its own. Male, middle-aged, six feet if he was an inch. The water had done its patient work—skin gone gray and slippered, features softened into a general apology for a face—but death by drowning leaves its signature for those who read it, and I read it: the froth residue at the nostrils and mouth, the wrinkling of the fingertips into what we call washerwoman's skin, the particular bloat distribution of a body that has taken water and held it.

I knelt on the tarp Tomas spread for me and began the work. This part never bothers me. People assume it must, that proximity to the dead corrodes something in you, but in ten years of holding this office I've found the opposite true. The dead are honest. They don't interrupt. They don't ask you how you're holding up in a tone that means they want you to ask them back. Every body I examine, I do what my mother taught me before I knew why it mattered: I say their name aloud. Even when I don't know it yet. Especially then.

"All right, friend," I said quietly. "Let's find out who you were."

That's when I saw the clothes.

* * *

Here is a thing about me that the town finds either charming or pitiful depending on which church they attend: I dress like a man who owns exactly one outfit and has it copied. Charcoal wool trousers. White shirt, collarless, buttoned to the throat. A dark cardigan in winter, sleeves pushed up for work. I buy the fabric from the same mill supplier my father used, because sentiment is the only extravagance I permit myself, and I have everything cut by the same tailor in Ashford, a sixty-mile drive I make twice a year like a pilgrimage. My wardrobe is uniform in both senses. It saves me from decisions I don't care about, and it means that anyone describing me from a distance describes a shape, not a person. I have always considered this a small professional advantage.

The drowned man was wearing charcoal wool trousers. A white collarless shirt, buttoned to the throat. Over it, a dark cardigan with the sleeves worn slightly long, as mine are, because I have long arms and refuse to pay extra for adjustment.

For perhaps four seconds I allowed myself the comfortable explanation. Coincidence. The mill sells that fabric to half the county; charcoal wool is the color of respectability and grief, and Hollow Creek has always had plenty of both. Men dress alike. Widowers dress alike. It means nothing.

Then I turned back the right cuff of the cardigan, because procedure requires checking pockets, and procedure saved me from comfort.

There is a mended tear on the inner left cuff of my good cardigan. Three inches, diagonal, from the winter I caught it on a morgue drawer latch—a stupid, private little wound in the fabric that I stitched myself at my kitchen table at one in the morning, badly, because I don't sew well and I was too stubborn to drive to Ashford for a repair I could do myself. The stitching is uneven. The thread is gray where it should be black. It is, without exaggeration, the single most identifiable detail of my entire existence. Fingerprints are shared by twins. My lopsided cuff repair belongs to me alone.

The drowned man's cardigan bore the same tear, mended the same way, with the same wrong thread, in the same crooked hand.

My hand. Or a copy of it so precise that the distinction stopped mattering.

I sat back on my heels. The creek went on talking to itself behind me, indifferent, and somewhere upstream a heron lifted off with a sound like a folded newspaper opening. Tomas said something. I heard it the way you hear rain through a roof—present, meaningless.

"Dr. Vasquez?" Tomas again, closer now. "You okay?"

"These clothes," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. Ten years of practice at sounding level. "They're tailored. Look at the seams. Not off-the-rack—cut for this man specifically, or cut to look like it." I turned the cuff back so he could see, and watched his face do the arithmetic. "Does anything about this look coincidental to you?"

Tomas looked at the stitching for a long moment. He was quiet in the way young people are quiet when they realize a thing is bigger than the report form has boxes for.

"Somebody dressed him," he said finally. "Like a—"

"Don't say it."

"—like a letter," he said anyway, because he was twenty-six and hadn't yet learned which thoughts to keep in the drawer.

A letter. Yes. That was precisely the word, and I hated him mildly for finding it first. Because here is what I know about letters: they are addressed. They have an intended recipient. And whoever had dressed this dead stranger in the fabric of my life, down to the private scar in the wool, had not been sending a message to the county, or to the sheriff's department, or to the good people of Hollow Creek.

They had been writing to me.

* * *

I worked the body properly after that, because grief and dread are no excuse for sloppy procedure—my mother's voice again, though she'd have phrased it differently, something warmer about doing things right so the dead aren't done wrong. I documented the washerwoman's skin, the lividity fixed anteriorly with blanching consistent with the position of recovery, the absence of ligature marks, the faint petechiae in the eyes that suggest struggle against water rather than against hands. No obvious trauma. No defensive wounds worth noting. The water would tell me the rest in the lab, in its own slow dialect: diatoms, temperature history, time since submersion. The creek keeps meticulous books. It just doesn't share them willingly.

In the trouser pockets: nothing. In the shirt pocket, a single item wrapped in wax paper with a deliberateness that made my teeth ache.

A key. Small, brass, older style, the kind that fits interior doors in houses built before the war. No tag. No fob. Cleaned carefully—someone had wiped it down before placing it there, or the water had, but the wax paper argued for intention. You don't wrap a random object in wax paper. You wrap evidence. You wrap a gift.

And pinned inside the cardigan, over the heart, where nobody would look unless they were looking: a tailor's label. Hand-sewn, not machine-stitched, which is wrong for any garment that ever came out of the Ashford shop. The fabric of the label itself was fine white linen, and on it, in ink that had survived the water better than it had any right to, a single line of small, precise script:

*For C.V.*

Not a name. An initial. Mine.

I stood there with the label photographed and bagged and my hands very steady, and I thought about the strange mathematics of my position in this town. I am the coroner of Hollow Creek, which means I am the person the dead are delivered to, and I am also, by the town's ancient and unspoken accounting, something slightly less than a full citizen. The Vasquez name has weight here the way a stone at the bottom of a well has weight—you feel it when you lean over, but nobody talks about it. My parents died in a fire twenty years ago this autumn. The file says accident. The file has always said accident. I was fourteen years old, and I was shipped away to an aunt in another state before the ash had finished settling, and when I came back a decade later to take this office, the town welcomed me with the particular warmth people reserve for someone they've agreed not to discuss.

I have spent ten years quietly correcting the record. Three deaths ruled accidents that were not accidents—old Mr. Pruitt's fall down stairs that had a fresh oil sheen on the third step; the Delgado girl's single-car crash on a road she had no reason to be driving at midnight; the Hollis boy's hunting mishap with a rifle angle no competent marksman produces by accident. Each correction cost me something. Friends, such as they were. Comfort, certainly. The sheriff's patience, in increments. But the dead had names, and I say the names, and the record is the only grave marker some of them will ever get.

Nobody in Hollow Creek had ever sent me a message about it. Not thanks, not threats. Silence is the town's native language, and I'd become fluent.

Until now. Until a stranger surfaced at Miller's Bend wearing my life like a costume, labeled *for C.V.*, carrying a key wrapped like a relic.

Because that's what this was, once I let myself see it whole. Not a murder scene, or not only that. A composition. Someone had gone to extraordinary trouble—tailoring, staging, placement at the exact bend where Walt Jensen fishes every Saturday morning like clockwork—to make sure that when this body came out of the water, it would come out addressed to me. The clothes weren't a disguise. They were a signature. Whoever did this wanted me to stand over this body and understand, with total certainty, that they knew me. Knew my wardrobe, my tailor, my habits, my mended cuff. Knew me the way I know the dead: intimately, completely, and without permission.

Someone had been inside my life. Close enough to copy its stitching.

* * *

By late afternoon the body was in my cooler under a temporary number, and I was standing in my office with the evidence bag on my desk, turning the brass key over in my palm through the plastic. It was a house key. I was almost sure of it. And I was almost sure of something else, something that had been circling the edges of my mind all day like a moth around a bulb, refusing to land.

Twenty years ago, in the weeks before the fire, there had been a man around town asking questions. I remember him only in fragments—I was fourteen, and grief makes a poor archivist—but I remember my parents speaking low in the kitchen, and the word *investigator*, and my father saying, "He'll find it, Vee. That's what we paid him for." Find what, I never learned. The fire came, and the questions burned with everything else, and the man himself vanished from my memory the way hired people vanish: completely, and without anyone thinking to wonder where.

I looked at the drowned man's face through the cooler glass. The water had smoothed him toward anonymity, but underneath it there was a jaw, a brow, a structure—and something in it plucked at a string I hadn't known was still strung.

Tomorrow I'd run dental, fingerprints, everything. Tomorrow I'd start pulling threads. But tonight I sat at my desk and wrote in my logbook, beneath the case number, the sentence I would build the next months on:

*This body is correspondence. Find the sender.*

Outside, the creek kept moving in the dark, carrying whatever else it knew downstream toward Miller's Bend, patient as a postman with one more delivery to make.
