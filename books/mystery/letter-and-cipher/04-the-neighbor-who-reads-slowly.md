## 4. The Neighbor Who Reads Slowly

The bookshop had been Wren's idea, back when we were both still employed by the city and still speaking to each other without weighing every word. *You'll need somewhere to put your hands when you retire,* she'd said, and she'd been right, the way she was usually right about things I didn't want to hear. So there were my hands now: shelving a stack of secondhand Le Carrés, ringing up a paperback for a teenager who paid in exact change, watering the fern that refused to die out of what I suspected was spite.

For eleven months the shop had been the one place where nothing contradicted itself. Books stayed where you put them. The till balanced or it didn't. Nobody died between chapters unless the author said so.

Then came Alden Reeve, and the envelope of cash, and the death certificate signed by a dead man's pen.

I'd been at the Reeve file three weeks by then. Three weeks of sealed dockets and a whistleblower named Tomas Vey who had walked out of a courthouse in 1995 and into a silence no one had ever thought to question. Three weeks of the same sentence circling my skull like a moth: *no body, no report, no case — so why am I being paid to prove a murder didn't happen?*

The shop was supposed to be my break from that. It failed me on a Tuesday.

* * *

Mr. Finch came in most afternoons around four, when the light through the front window went amber and the street outside got loud with school traffic. He was maybe sixty-five, thin as a bookmark, with a cardigan the color of weak tea and reading glasses he wore pushed up on his forehead like a headband. He bought one book a week. Never two. And he read slowly — genuinely slowly, I'd come to realize, not performatively — because he'd return a week later having finished perhaps eighty pages, and he could tell you exactly what happened in those eighty pages, down to which character had lied in paragraph six.

I liked him. That's the part that stings to write down. I liked him the way you like a fixture, a clock that ticks, something reliable in a business where reliability is mostly dust jackets.

He'd been coming in since spring. Longer than the envelope. Longer than the case. Which is why it took me until that Tuesday to notice what his questions were actually doing.

"Blackwood," he said, setting down his cup of the coffee I kept behind the counter for regulars. "That fraud trial from the nineties. The shipping company. You'd know it — you were police."

"Which one?" I asked, and it wasn't even a lie; the nineties had produced fraud trials the way rivers produce silt.

"The one with the man who died before sentencing." His eyes stayed on the page in front of him. He turned a page as he said it, unhurried. "Reeve. Alden Reeve."

"Heart attack," I said. "Before sentencing. Why?"

"No reason. It was in the paper this morning — some anniversary piece, thirty years." He adjusted his glasses. "Odd, though. The article said he died at home. But I thought I remembered an inquest. A delay. Something about the body."

"There was no inquest," I said. "There wouldn't be. Attending physician certifies, done."

"Mm." He read another line. "And the doctor who signed it — would he still be practicing? You'd think someone would have checked, all these years."

Something cold slid down between my shoulder blades, slow as syrup. Because that question — *would he still be practicing* — was not a question a man asks who learned about Reeve from a newspaper anniversary piece. That was a question you ask when you already know the answer is *no*, and you want to know whether *I* know it too.

Dr. Halloran. Died last February. I'd found it in an obituary archive at one in the morning, sitting in the shop's back room with the lamp on and the rain doing its usual percussion against the glass.

"He retired," I said. "Years ago."

"Ah." Finch smiled at his book. "That explains it, then."

He bought his weekly novel — a Ruth Rendell, dog-eared stock — and left at ten past five, the bell over the door giving its small tin cough. I stood at the counter with my hands flat on the wood and made myself count to sixty before I moved.

* * *

Here is what paranoia does that ordinary fear doesn't: it makes you revisit everything. Fear looks forward. Paradox makes you audit the past.

That night I sat in the back room and reconstructed every conversation Mr. Finch and I had ever had, going back to spring. Most of it was books. He favored locked-room mysteries and complained, mildly and correctly, that modern writers cheated. He'd once spent twenty minutes explaining why a particular edition of *The Moonstone* was worth twice its price, and he'd been right about that too.

But threaded through it, thin as wire, were the other questions. In June: *did you ever work homicide, or just fraud?* In July, apropos of a thriller's plot: *could a man really disappear in this country now, with cameras everywhere?* In August, buying a biography of a con artist: *do you think people are ever sorry, or only sorry they were caught?*

At the time each one had sounded like a lonely man making conversation. Laid end to end, they sounded like a deposition.

I got up, went to the shelf where I kept my own working files — disguised, badly, inside a box marked KITCHENWARE — and pulled the folder on the 1995 trial. There were photographs. Court artists' sketches, mostly, since the press had been kept out of the early sessions, plus one grainy news photo of the courtroom steps: Reeve's legal team descending toward the cameras, and behind them, half-cropped, the man who'd run Reeve's document archive. A clerk. A fixer of papers. The caption didn't name him. The trial transcript did: *identified in proceedings as a records assistant for the defense.*

In the photo, the man's hands were visible because he was carrying a box of files against his chest, fingers spread across the cardboard.

On his left hand, across the base of the thumb and the heel of the palm, a scar. Pale, old even then, running diagonal like a fault line.

I set the photograph down. My hands weren't quite steady, which annoyed me.

Tuesday afternoon. Four o'clock. Amber light. Mr. Finch at his table by the window with his coffee, turning pages at his patient pace, and when he reached for the cup — left hand, fingers wrapping the ceramic —

The scar ran diagonal across the base of the thumb, pale, thirty years settled into the skin.

He felt me looking. Of course he did; men like him always feel it. He glanced up over the rim of the cup, mild as milk, and said, "Good book this week. She cheats in the last chapter, though. The killer couldn't have known about the alarm system."

"Maybe he watched the house," I said.

"Maybe," Finch agreed. "Or maybe he was the one who installed it." He drank his coffee. "People always forget what the quiet man in the background knows."

* * *

I didn't sleep much that night, and what sleep I got had teeth in it. By morning I'd made a list, because lists are how I pray:

One. Finch's questions predated the envelope. Either coincidence — and I no longer believed in those — or Finch had been interested in Reeve long before anyone hired me to un-murder him.

Two. The scar. Scars can be coincidental. Hands can resemble hands. But the scar plus the questions plus the timing was a pattern, and patterns were the only scripture I'd ever trusted.

Three. If Finch was who the scar suggested he was — a defense insider from '95, someone whose face never made the papers but whose hands had — then he hadn't stumbled into my bookshop. He'd chosen it. A retired detective with a history in financial crime, three streets from the old courthouse, three streets from Sorrel the court clerk, three streets from everything.

Unless. Unless he'd chosen it because of *me*. Because a bored ex-detective was the perfect instrument: point him at the Reeve mystery and let him dig. Or because a bored ex-detective was the perfect threat, and someone wanted to keep an eye on the digging before it started.

Either way, the shop was compromised. That was the thought that actually hurt. Eleven months of ordered shelves and exact-change teenagers and the spiteful immortal fern, and now every creak of the floorboards sounded like surveillance. I caught myself cataloguing the regulars. Mrs. Okafor, who bought poetry and smelled of church incense — fine. The twins who came in for graphic novels — fine. The tall man who browsed but never bought, Thursdays, always the true-crime section—

I stopped. Made myself stop. This was how it worked, this was exactly how it worked: you fed the paranoia one confirmed stranger and it came back hungry for all of them. The shop was a refuge because I'd built it as one, and I'd be damned if I let a scarred hand take that from me.

But I also took the photograph of the courtroom steps home that night, and I also wrote *FINCH?* on the back of it in pencil, and I also moved the Reeve file out of the KITCHENWARE box and into a place no browser would find it.

* * *

Thursday, he came in early. Rain again — October had committed to the bit — and Finch shook out his umbrella and stood dripping politely by the door, and instead of going to his table he drifted along the new arrivals, and then, casually as a man asking about the weather, he said:

"You've been quiet lately. Working on something?"

"Stocktaking," I said.

"Mm. I heard otherwise." He tilted his head, rain-light moving on his glasses. "I heard you'd been asking about Sorrel."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. I kept my face still through long practice, but inside, everything rang.

Sorrel. The retired court clerk from the '95 trial. The third witness on my list, the one I'd telephoned only yesterday, the one whose voice had trembled on the word *Reeve* like a crack through china. I had told no one. Not Wren, not Ansel, not the empty chair across from me at dinner. The call had lasted four minutes and I'd used no names beyond hers.

"I don't know a Sorrel," I said.

"No," Finch said pleasantly. "I imagine you don't." He selected a novel from the new arrivals — a fresh hardcover, the first full-price book he'd ever touched in my shop — and carried it to the register himself. "Ring this up for me, would you? And Blackwood —"

He waited until I looked at him. Up close, his eyes were a washed-out blue, the kind that reads as friendly from across a room and as nothing at all from four feet away.

"— whoever hired you, they'll have hired others. That's how it's done. Layers. Everyone knows a little, no one knows enough." He counted out exact change, coin by coin, unhurried. "The trick isn't finding out who knows what. It's noticing who's afraid. Fear leaves fingerprints too."

He tucked the book under his arm, opened his umbrella, and stepped out into the rain. Through the window I watched him go, thin and slow and careful on the wet pavement, a man reading the street the way he read his novels — patiently, completely, missing nothing.

Then I picked up the phone and dialed Sorrel's number, because if fear left fingerprints, hers had been all over our conversation, and because someone had clearly gotten to her before I did — or after.

She answered on the first ring, which meant she'd been holding the phone.

"Don't come here," she whispered, before I said a word. "He was just—" A breath, ragged. "Someone was just here asking me the same questions you asked. Word for word. Ivo—"

The line clicked, and went dead.
