## 10. Closing the Case

The envelope sat on Ivo's desk for three days before he could touch it.

Not the first one — that had been cash, thick and insulting. This one was a manila folder, soft at the corners from handling, containing everything: Sorrel's letter with its cipher of small lies, the decoded marginalia mapped against the first edition of *The Cartographer's Daughter*, photographs of the burned cottage and the patch of earth that had turned out to be exactly what it looked like, and the ledger — Reeve's own ledger, recovered from the shop floor after Finch's arrest, its fake entries now evidence instead of bait.

Ivo had built cases for thirty years. He knew the weight of a folder like this one. It wasn't paper. It was people.

He picked up the phone and called Detective Ansel.

* * *

Ansel came to the bookshop rather than the station, which Ivo took as a courtesy or a warning — possibly both. She sat in the back room among the unsold stock, her coat still buttoned, and didn't reach for the folder until Ivo pushed it across the table.

"You understand what this does," she said.

"I know what it doesn't do." Ivo kept his voice level. "It doesn't protect anyone."

She opened the folder and read for a long time. The shop was quiet around them — closed, actually, a sign Ivo hadn't hung since he'd started this case. *Back in an hour.* The lie of it had begun to feel permanent.

"The letter is admissible," Ansel said finally. "The cipher, if your decoding holds up under review. The ledger puts Reeve in the cottage. The remains put Vess there in '95." She looked up. "And Wren Halloway's statement puts her at the staging of the death. You're giving me all of it."

"I'm giving you what's true."

"That's not the same as saying you're giving me *her*."

Ivo had prepared for this question. He'd prepared for it in the shower, in the stacks, at three in the morning staring at the ceiling of his flat above the shop. He'd prepared answers and discarded them all, because every answer that spared Wren was a lie, and every lie was just another brick in the wall Reeve had spent thirty years building.

"Wren helped a man disappear," Ivo said. "She believed him innocent of murder. She was wrong about the murder, but she wasn't wrong about what she did. She falsified a death certificate trail. She misled investigators. If someone else had done it, I'd have arrested them myself." He paused. "You'll be gentle with her because I'm asking you to be. But she goes in the file."

Ansel studied him for a moment. Thirty years of reading faces, and he watched her do it now — looking for the crack, the angle, the thing underneath.

"You could have sat on this," she said. "Reeve's money would have kept coming. Your friend would have stayed free. Nobody even knew there was a case until you started pulling threads."

"Vess's daughter knew," Ivo said. "She knew for thirty years that her father walked out one morning and never came home, and the world told her he'd simply chosen to. That's the case. Everything else was furniture."

Ansel closed the folder and stood. At the door she stopped, half-turned.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the ones who hand it over are rarer than the ones who bury it. Doesn't make it hurt less. But it's rarer."

Then she was gone, and the folder went with her, and Ivo stood alone in the back room listening to the particular silence of a thing finished.

* * *

Wren came at dusk.

Ivo heard the bell over the door and knew her step before he saw her — the same step that had come through that door for forty years, through two marriages, one retirement, and every case they'd ever worked side by side. She stopped in the doorway of the back room. She'd heard about Ansel's visit. Of course she had.

"You gave them everything," she said.

"I did."

"I asked you—"

"I know what you asked me." Ivo set down the book he'd been pretending to shelve. "You asked me to let a murdered man stay buried twice. I can't do that. I've never been able to do that. It's the only thing about me that's ever been reliable."

Wren's face did something complicated — anger and grief fighting for the same ground. "Thirty years, Ivo. I protected his family. His wife was dying. His kids were kids. And I thought—" Her voice caught. "I thought he was innocent of the worst part. I staked my career on it."

"And when you found out you were wrong?"

She didn't answer. That was the answer.

"They'll offer you cooperation," Ivo said quietly. "Take it. Testify about the staging, tell them everything about how the death was done. Ansel will listen. Juries listen. You'll lose the pension, maybe worse, but you'll walk out of it eventually, and you'll walk out clean in the way that matters."

"And you? What do you walk out with?"

Ivo looked around the back room — the dust, the dead stock, the desk where the retainer had sat like a tumor. "A shop," he said. "And the truth that nobody paid for."

Wren laughed, and it broke in the middle. "You always were insufferable."

"It's why we worked."

She crossed the room then, and for a long moment they held each other the way old partners do — briefly, hard, with everything important left unsaid because saying it would have been a kind of lying too. Then she stepped back, straightened her coat, and walked out through the shop without another word.

The bell rang behind her. Ivo didn't watch her go. Some doors you have to let close on their own weight.

* * *

The trial of Alden Reeve began on a Tuesday in April, and Ivo did not attend.

He read about it instead, in the papers he'd sworn off during the case and returned to now like a man returning to a bad habit. *REeve Fraud Trial Opens; 1995 Cold Case Linked.* They'd found Vess's name again, given it back to him after thirty years of being a footnote. There was a photograph of his daughter outside the courthouse — gray-haired now, composed, holding a copy of the missing-persons flyer that had gone yellow decades ago. Ivo cut it out and kept it in the drawer with the retainer envelope, which he had never spent.

The shop reopened on a Wednesday.

He swept the floors himself, restocked the front table, and wrote a new sign in his own hand: *Open. Ask us anything except who done it.* A customer laughed at it within the hour, and the sound of laughter in the shop did more for him than any verdict.

Mr. Finch's chair by the window sat empty. Ivo couldn't bring himself to move it. Let it stand as a monument to the strange truth that monsters read slowly, and sit quietly, and ask precise questions, and are only ever caught because someone finally counted their scars.

* * *

In May, Ivo took the train to Marlowe, two towns over, where Mara Vess — born Elena Vess, daughter of the whistleblower Daniel Vess — ran a small accounting office above a bakery that smelled of cinnamon all the way to the street.

She met him with wary eyes. She'd gotten a bank draft the week before, no note, no return address. Fifty thousand pounds. She'd assumed it was a scam.

"It isn't," Ivo said. "It was payment for work I did. Badly, at first, and then well, and then at a cost I'm still counting. It isn't mine. It should have been your father's, in a sense — it came from the man who took everything from him. Consider it restitution with extra steps."

Mrs. Vess looked at him for a long time. "You're the detective. From the articles."

"I'm the detective."

"My father used to read mysteries," she said suddenly, unexpectedly. "Cheap ones. He'd fold the corners of pages where he figured out the ending early, so he could prove it later." Her eyes glistened but didn't fall. "Did you figure it out early?"

"No," Ivo said. "I figured it out late, and wrong, several times. But I got there."

"That's better than most people manage." She accepted the second copy of the bank draft — the formal one, with a letter explaining provenance, the kind lawyers liked. "Will there be a trial?"

"There will be justice," Ivo said. "Slow, ugly, partial. But yes."

She nodded once, and shook his hand, and he walked back down past the bakery into the ordinary afternoon, feeling lighter than he had any right to feel and heavier than he could explain.

* * *

The shop was dark when he got home, except for the lamp over the reading chair, and someone was sitting in it.

Ivo stopped in the doorway, and his hand — old habit, old life — went to where a weapon would have been, twenty years ago.

Sorrel looked up from the book in her lap. *The Cartographer's Daughter.* First edition. The one from the shop, bought back at auction after the trial evidence was photographed and released.

"Relax," she said. "I knocked. You weren't answering. I let myself in with the key you've never changed since 2011."

"You could have been anyone."

"I could have been Mr. Finch," she agreed. "But Finch is in custody, and I'm just an old clerk who misremembered dates for thirty years and finally remembers everything." She held up the book. "Page 214. Did you know he folded the corner here? Reeve. Before he sent me the letter. Like he wanted me to find the ending early."

Ivo lowered himself into the other chair. Outside, the street did its evening business — footsteps, a bicycle bell, someone laughing near the corner.

"What's on page 214?" he asked.

Sorrel turned the book toward him. In the margin, in pencil, in a hand Ivo now knew better than his own, were four words he had never decoded, because they had never been part of the cipher:

*There is one more.*

Ivo stared at it for a long time. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the shop settled into its night sounds.

"One more what?" he said finally.

Sorrel smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "That, Detective, is why I've come to hire you."

* * *

Ivo Blackwood had closed his last case by letting go of it. He'd handed over the cipher, the letter, the ledger; he'd let Wren face her part; he'd given away the blood money and reopened the shop and told himself the story was over, the way you tell yourself any comforting story late at night.

Now he picked up the pencil from the cup on his desk, and turned to page 214, and began — slowly, carefully, the way a man begins anything that matters — to read.
