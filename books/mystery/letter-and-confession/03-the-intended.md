## 3. The Intended

The bus from the city took four hours and cost Ilsa Brannock the last of her patience somewhere around the county line, where the road narrowed and the pines closed in like a jury deliberating. She watched the trees and thought about turning around. She had turned around twice before in eleven years — once at a gas station outside Millbrook, once at the actual town limits, her hand on the door handle of a cab she couldn't afford — and each time the town had let her go without complaint, the way it let everything go. That was Hollowmere's whole genius, she'd come to understand. It never held you. It just waited, patient as limestone, for you to come back on your own.

She came back because it was her father's seventieth birthday, and because her father was dying — not quickly, not dramatically, but the way old quarries die, settling by inches, and because she had decided, somewhere over the summer, that she would rather regret the visit than the absence.

The bus let her off at the corner of Quarry Road and Main, where the hardware store still stood with the same sun-bleached sign, and where three separate people said her name before she'd walked a block. *Ilsa Brannock. Is that Ilsa Brannock?* A woman from her father's church pressed a casserole schedule into her hands without being asked. A man she vaguely remembered as the son of a foreman — one of the foremen, one of *those* foremen — tipped his cap and said, "Your daddy's a good man," in the tone of a man reciting something he'd been taught.

That was the thing about Aldous Brannock in Hollowmere. He wasn't just beloved. He was load-bearing. The town's whole architecture of itself rested on him: the scholarship fund, the restored bandstand, the way he still showed up to every funeral uninvited and stood at the back with his hat in his hands. Seventy years old and straight-backed, white-haired, voice like gravel poured slow. People said his name the way they said the town's — with a small involuntary pride, as if he were a natural feature. *Aldous Brannock* and *Hollowmere* in the same breath, forever.

Nobody said the word *quarry* the same way anymore. That had gone quiet sometime in the nineties, the way a room goes quiet when the argument is over and nobody won.

The house on Brannock Hill hadn't changed either, which was its own kind of unsettling. Same fieldstone, same wraparound porch her grandfather had built, same brass knocker shaped like a horseshoe. Her mother's roses were still alive along the front walk, tended now by a gardener Ilsa had never met, blooming for a dead woman out of what Ilsa could only assume was habit or guilt. The front door opened before she knocked.

"There she is." Her father filled the doorway the way he always had, big and unhurried, and for one suspended second he looked exactly like the man who used to lift her onto the fence rail at the quarry overlook and point out the herons. Then he hugged her, and he was smaller. Not much. Enough. His arms still closed around her completely, but there was a tremor in them, or maybe that was her.

"Happy birthday, Dad."

"Best present's already here." He said it easily, warmly, and she hated herself a little for listening for the seam in it. Eleven years of estrangement teaches you to listen for seams. "Come in. Kitchen's got coffee that'll strip paint, just how you like it."

*Just how you like it.* He remembered. Of course he remembered. Fathers remember; it's the daughters who are allowed to forget.

* * *

The party was Saturday. She had two days with him first, and she spent them the way she'd spent her childhood — trailing him through his routines, except now she was cataloguing instead of admiring. He rose at five. He walked the property line with a stick he'd cut himself. He ate eggs and toast, read the obituaries first — "gotta know who's left," he joked, and the housekeeper laughed the way staff laugh — and then he drove into town in the old truck to shake hands at the feed store and the bank and the historical society, where they were apparently naming a reading room after him. Aldous Brannock, patron of books. The quarry had paid for the library too. Everything in this town had been paid for out of that hole in the ground, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, and the not-saying had a texture, Ilsa realized, like felt lining a drawer. Soft on everything. Concealing the sharp edges.

She watched him work the town and she thought: *he's saying goodbye.* She couldn't have said why, exactly. It was something in the thoroughness of it. He wasn't campaigning; he was settling accounts. He touched the shoulder of the feed store owner's son — the boy had a new baby — and he lingered at the war memorial and he stood a long moment at the cemetery gate without going in. When Ilsa asked about it, he said, "Paying respects," and left it there, and she let it lie there too, because that was the family art: leaving things lying.

Friday night she couldn't sleep. The house made sounds she'd forgotten — the boiler's knock, the wind working the chimney — and the guest room was still, impossibly, decorated for a teenager. Her old ribbons were in a jar on the desk. Her mother's reading glasses sat folded on the nightstand as if set down mid-chapter eleven years ago. She got up at two in the morning for the kitchen, for water, for anything, and that was when she saw the light under the study door.

Her father was a five-o'clock man. He was not a two-o'clock man.

The study door was ajar. She stood in the hall with her glass of water going warm in her hand and looked through the gap, and what she saw was her father — Aldous Brannock, the man the whole town leaned on — kneeling at the cold fireplace, feeding papers into a steel basin and burning them one at a time. Not crumpled, not tossed. One at a time, held by the corner, watched until the flame took, then laid down with something close to ceremony. He'd done this before. You could tell by how little ash escaped.

She should have knocked. She knew that even as she pushed the door instead, and it swung wide, and the hinge — the one hinge in that whole house that had never been oiled — announced her.

Her father didn't startle. That was the first wrong thing. A man caught burning papers at 2 a.m. should startle. Aldous Brannock simply looked up, and his face did something complicated and quick and then arrived at mild, the way a river arrives at flat water after the rapids, and he said, "Couldn't sleep either, honey?"

"What are you burning?"

"Old papers." He stood, knees cracking, and put himself — she noticed this, she made herself notice it — squarely between her and the basin. "Tax stuff. Your mother kept everything, God rest her, and the shredder's busted."

"Since when do you burn tax stuff instead of shredding it?"

"Since the shredder busted." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had opened a hundred doors in this county and closed a hundred conversations, and she had grown up believing it was the truest thing about him. Standing in the firelight at two in the morning, she saw it for what it was: a door. "You want coffee? There's that decaf the church ladies send."

"Dad." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "It's your birthday in two days. The whole town's coming. And you're down here at two in the morning burning papers like a man getting rid of evidence."

The firelight moved on his face. For a moment — a real moment, she'd swear to it later — the mildness dropped and something else looked out at her, something tired and old and frightened, and she thought: *say it. Whatever it is, say it now and I'll stay, I'll stay this time.* She was thirty-four years old and she was begging her father with her whole chest to be guilty of something, because guilty she could help. Innocent and frightened she couldn't.

Then the mildness came back down like a visor.

"You've been in the city too long," he said, gentle, almost teasing. "Everything's a crime show now." He crossed to her and kissed the top of her head, and he smelled of woodsmoke and the camphor he'd used all her life, and his hand on her shoulder was steady. "Go to bed, Ilsa. Big day Saturday. I want my girl beside me when they give the toast."

She went. Of course she went. She lay in the guest room under her mother's quilt and listened to the house knock and settle, and at some point she heard the study door close, soft as an apology, and heard nothing after that.

* * *

In the morning the basin was gone and the fireplace held a clean, careful ash, and her father was out on the porch in the early light reading the paper like a man with nothing on his conscience and a full day of being beloved ahead of him. She sat across from him and drank the paint-stripping coffee and said, "I'll help you set up for the party," and he said, "That's my girl," and neither of them mentioned the night.

But she caught things now. That was the change the firelight had made — she'd gone to bed a daughter and woken a witness. She caught the way he checked the mailbox twice before the mail even came. She caught the phone call he took in the barn with the door shut, his voice low and even, saying only, "That's handled. I said it's handled." She caught the way he flinched — actually flinched, a small full-body thing, a horse at a snake — when the caterer's kid, setting up the tent, joked too loud: "Careful on the overlook, folks, don't nobody fall in, ha!"

"Mind the equipment," Aldous said, mild as milk, and the kid laughed and apologized, and her father's hands, folded on the table, did not unclench for a full minute.

The quarry overlook. He'd chosen it for the party — the flat shelf of rock above the flooded pit where the company had built the pavilion years ago, all white columns and string lights, the prettiest ruin in the county. Everyone said it was romantic, the old pit turned lake, the water so deep and still it held the sky. Ilsa had learned to swim in it. Half the town had learned to swim in it. And half the town, she now remembered with a chill that had nothing to do with the morning, had learned to drive around the long way to avoid looking at it.

"Why the overlook, Dad?" she asked. "The hall's cheaper."

"The overlook's ours," he said simply. "Everything good in my life happened up there." He said it with such plain feeling that she believed him, and believing him frightened her more than the burning papers had, because it meant the fear and the love came from the same place, and she didn't know what lived in a place like that.

* * *

Saturday came up golden. The town turned out the way it turns out for a funeral, only smiling — three hundred people, it seemed, all the load-bearing citizens of Hollowmere, the mayor first among them with his tanned competent face and his hand out. The string lights came on as the sun went down. The band played old songs. Someone had commissioned a cake shaped like the pavilion itself, which Ilsa found either sweet or cannibalistic, she couldn't decide.

Her father stood at the rail above the black water in his good suit and gave a speech. He talked about his father and the quarry, about the men who'd built the town, about the "hard years" — one sentence, one wave of the hand, the felt lining smoothing over the edge — and about family, and about how a man ought to leave a place better than he found it. And then, near the end, his voice dropped into something quieter, and he looked out over the water and said, "This town has been kinder to me than I deserve. That's all I'll say tonight. Kinder than I deserve."

Everybody laughed and toasted. Ilsa didn't. She was watching her sister-in-law's face — no, not a sister-in-law, a cousin's wife — no. She was watching the mayor, who had stopped smiling for exactly the length of that sentence, the way a man stops smiling when a debt is named aloud in mixed company.

Later, when the band took a break and the crowd thinned toward the cake, her father found her at the rail. The water below held the string lights like a net of drowned stars.

"You'll stay a while?" he asked. "After the weekend. There's room. There's always room."

"Maybe," she said. "Dad. Before everyone gets back — what did you burn?"

The lights moved on the water between them. He was quiet so long she thought he wouldn't answer, and when he did, he didn't answer at all. He reached over, and with a movement so smooth it was nearly invisible — she'd have missed it if she hadn't spent three days learning to watch him — he slipped something into the pocket of her coat. His hand didn't even pause on its way to refilling her glass from the champagne tray.

"Happy birthday to me," he said, "having you home," and kissed her cheek, and turned back to his guests, his face already arranged into the beloved patriarch, seventy years old, beloved by everyone, kinder than he deserved.

Ilsa stood at the rail above the drowned quarry with her heart going hard, and waited until no one was looking, and put her hand in her pocket.

Her fingers closed on a small cold key. No tag, no fob — just a brass key, old-fashioned, worn smooth, with a number stamped on the bow so faint she had to hold it to the string lights to read it.

The band started up again. Down in the black water, the lights kept burning, patient as everything else in Hollowmere, waiting for her to come back on her own.
