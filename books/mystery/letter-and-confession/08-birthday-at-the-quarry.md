## 8. Birthday at the Quarry

The invitations had been letterpressed on paper thick as skin, and Ilsa had thrown hers in the trash twice before fishing it out again.

*Seventy Years of Aldous Brannock — Quarry Overlook Pavilion — Black Tie.*

Black tie, at a quarry. Only her father would dress up a hole in the ground and call it a legacy. She'd flown in from Denver with one suitcase and a return ticket she kept touching like a talisman, and now she stood at the edge of the pavilion her family's money had built on the rim of the flooded pit, watching two hundred people toast the man who had taught her to skip stones across water that had since swallowed three of them.

She hadn't been home in six years. The town looked smaller and meaner in the way towns do when you've escaped them, but Hollowmere hadn't shrunk so much as calcified — same brick facades, same names on the shopfronts, same polite silence hanging over everything like weather. She'd forgotten how loud the quiet was here.

"Ilsa Brannock." A woman in gold lamé seized her elbow before she could reach the bar. "I didn't know you were coming. Your father must be *thrilled*."

"He doesn't know yet," Ilsa said, which was true. She'd told his assistant, not him. Some experiments you run blind.

"Well, he'll be over the moon." The woman's smile held a fraction too long. "Though between us — things have been strange lately. He canceled the board meeting. He never cancels the board meeting."

"Maybe seventy makes a man reflective."

"Maybe." Gold Lamé drifted off toward the canapés, satisfied or dissatisfied, impossible to tell.

Ilsa got a glass of something sparkling and expensive and found a spot near the railing where the overlook dropped away into the quarry itself. The pit was full now, had been full for decades — a lake of unnervingly green water, deep enough that the old crane foundations vanished a few feet down and became rumor. In summer, kids swam there despite the fences and the signs. In winter it froze to a color like bottle glass.

Three people had drowned in that water in the nineties. She'd been a teenager then, living in the big house on Wren Street, and she remembered the funerals more than the deaths: closed caskets, her father's arm around weeping widows, his voice steady at every graveside. *Accidents,* everyone said. *Terrible accidents.* And then the drownings stopped, and Hollowmere moved on the way Hollowmere always did, sealing its dead under fresh grass and fresh money.

She was thinking about this because of the envelope currently folded in half inside her clutch. It had arrived at her apartment ten days ago, no return address, postmarked locally. Inside was a single sheet of yellowed paper, typed, dated thirty years ago:

*I record for the archive what will not be believed until it is too late: Aldous Brannock will die by water, in the quarry, as the others did. The ledger keeps its count. One name per decade.*

No signature. No explanation. Just that, and beneath it, in different ink, someone had written a phone number she had not called.

She'd almost thrown it away. Then she'd booked the flight.

"There she is."

His voice still worked on her like a hand on the back of the neck. Aldous Brannock crossed the pavilion floor toward her in a charcoal suit that cost more than her rent, silver-haired, broad through the shoulders still, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who had never once been told no by anything softer than geology. Seventy years old and he looked carved.

"Dad."

He kissed her cheek. His hand lingered on her shoulder, warm and heavy, and for one unguarded second his eyes went wet. "You came."

"You invited me."

"I invited you every year." He said it without accusation, which was worse. "Come. Everyone wants to meet the daughter who got out."

"That's not how I'd phrase it."

"It's how they phrase it." He steered her into the crowd, and she let him, because that was the oldest dance they knew — he led, she followed, both pretending it was a choice.

* * *

The party was, objectively, magnificent. String quartet. Raw bar on ice. A cake shaped like the quarry headframe that people photographed with their phones while laughing at the audacity of it. Her father's world, assembled and gleaming: councilmen, bankers, the mayor himself red-faced and booming, two generations of families whose names appeared on buildings and trusts and, if you knew where to look, on the silent ledgers of who owed whom.

Ilsa worked the room the way she'd learned to work hostile depositions — smiling, listening, saying little. She heard the town's small music: a mill closing, a school levy failing, someone's son arrested two counties over. And underneath it, threaded through every conversation like wire through cable, the Brannocks. Everything here touched them. Everything here bent around them.

At nine o'clock the speeches began. The mayor went first, long and lubricated, calling Aldous "the bedrock of this community," and the crowd laughed warmly at the geology joke because they laughed at everything Aldous's orbit produced. Then her father took the microphone himself.

He was good. He'd always been good. He talked about his own father digging the first cut in 1958, about the men who'd built the town with their hands, about legacy and stone and water — and Ilsa watched his face while he spoke, looking for what she'd come looking for.

"We owe this place everything," he said. "And I intend to spend whatever years I have left repaying that debt."

Applause. Then a voice from near the stage, one of the younger cousins, wine-forward and grinning: "Careful, Uncle Aldous! Around here when a Brannock talks about going into the water—" he made a diving gesture, waggled his eyebrows— "we start checking your life insurance!"

It was nothing. It was a wedding-style joke, the kind told at every family gathering in every town in America, and the crowd chuckled obligingly.

But Ilsa was watching her father's face, and she saw it.

A flicker. Not a laugh-line movement, not surprise — something deeper, involuntary, the face doing something the man hadn't authorized. His jaw tightened. His glass stopped halfway to his mouth. For perhaps a second and a half, Aldous Brannock looked at the green water beyond the railing as if it were looking back, and all the carved confidence drained out of him like water through limestone.

Then it was gone. He laughed, raised his glass, said something gracious, and the moment sealed over so smoothly that anyone not staring directly at it would have sworn it never happened.

Ilsa stared directly at it. She had spent six years reading faces across conference tables; she knew the difference between a man joking about death and a man remembering it.

Her father was afraid of the quarry.

* * *

She waited an hour. She ate nothing, drank one glass slowly, and watched him work the room — and noticed, now that she was looking, the other things. How he positioned himself all evening with his back never fully to the water. How he declined, with a wave and a smile, when someone suggested a group photo at the railing. How twice, when his assistant approached with a phone held out, he turned away to take the call behind the pavilion's support pillar, out of sightline.

At eleven the party began to shed guests. Ilsa found him on the far side of the pavilion, alone for once, looking out at the quarry with an expression he would have hated anyone to see.

"Nice party," she said.

"You hate parties."

"I hate *these* parties. There's a difference." She came to stand beside him at the railing. Below, the water lay black-green and utterly still, holding the reflected string lights like drowned stars. "Who's Edmund?"

The question landed the way she'd intended — sideways, from depth. Her father went very still.

"Why do you ask that?"

"The guest list. I looked. There's a table reserved — card says *E. Brannock, party of one.* Nobody sat there all night." She'd checked the seating chart on her way in, an old habit from galas she'd worked professionally. "You set a place for a dead man, Dad."

"Edmund is dead." Flat. Immediate. Too immediate. "Twenty years ago. Drowned."

"That's what the grave says."

"Then the grave is right." He turned to face her, and the warmth was back, deployed like equipment. "Ilsa. Why are you really here? You don't call for six years, you don't come home for Christmas, and then you show up to a birthday party asking after a corpse. What is this?"

She should have lied. She'd planned to lie — she had a whole cover story about wanting to reconnect, about Denver being lonely, about turning forty and rethinking things. It was a good story. She opened her mouth to tell it.

"What's in the study?" she said instead.

Something crossed his face like a shadow of a bird.

"Because I saw you burning papers," she said quietly. "Last night. Through the study window. I got in late, I was walking past the house — my house, Dad, I grew up there — and the lights were on, and you were standing at the fireplace feeding documents into it. Pages and pages. At midnight. Two days before your seventieth birthday."

The pavilion had emptied around them. The quartet was packing down; the caterers moved in loose formation; the last stragglers called their goodbyes somewhere behind her. Her father said nothing for a long time. The water below them said nothing either, which felt deliberate.

"Burning old files," he said finally. "Shredding backlog. A man turns seventy, he cleans house."

"At midnight? By hand? When you employ three lawyers and a shredding service?" She shook her head. "You taught me better lies than that. You used to drill me on them — remember? *If you're going to lie, Ilsa, lie boring.* Boring lies only. That one isn't boring. That one's interesting, and interesting means true."

"Ilsa—"

"Someone sent me a letter." She hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, thirty days of pressure finding the fault line. "Dated thirty years ago. It says you're going to die by water. It says the others died by water. It says there's a ledger, and a name per decade, and—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated that even now, even knowing what she knew, love could ambush her mid-sentence— "and I flew four hundred miles because I needed to look at your face and know it wasn't true."

For a moment — one raw, suspended moment — she thought he might tell her. She saw the door open behind his eyes, saw sixty years of weight shift toward confession the way a load shifts toward collapse. His mouth parted. His hand rose halfway toward her, trembling slightly, an old man's hand, a father's hand.

Then it closed. The door shut. She watched him decide, watched the decision move through him like a shudder through a structure, and when he spoke his voice was level and kind and completely closed.

"There is no ledger. There is no plot. Someone is playing a cruel joke on you, sweetheart, and I'm sorry for it, and when I find out who—"

"Don't." She stepped back. "Don't do the voice. Don't be the bedrock of the community at me. I watched you flinch tonight, Dad. When that kid joked about drowning, you looked at that water like it had your name on it. Like it was *waiting*."

"You're seeing ghosts."

"I'm seeing my father, which is harder." Her throat was tight. "Whatever you burned in that fireplace — whatever you're paying people to keep quiet, whoever E. Brannock is — it's coming due. I can feel it. This whole town feels like a held breath."

He looked at her for a long moment. Seventy years old, and in the pavilion light she could suddenly see all of it — the age in his neck, the fear banked behind the authority, the terrible tiredness of a man who has been carrying something for three decades and has just realized his arms are giving out.

"You sound like your mother," he said softly.

"My mother left."

"My mother stayed," he said, "and it killed her. Stay angry with me, Ilsa. Anger keeps you clear of me, and right now—" he stopped himself, jaw working. "Right now being clear of me is the safest place you can stand."

"Dad—"

"Happy birthday to me," he said, with a smile that broke her heart precisely because it was fake, and kissed her forehead, and walked away across the emptying pavilion with his shoulders squared like a man walking into weather.

* * *

She stayed at the railing another twenty minutes, long enough for the caterers to strike the bar and the quartet to load their cases, long enough for the string lights to click off section by section until only the moon lit the quarry. The water didn't move. It never seemed to move. Three hundred feet of stillness sitting in a scar in the earth, keeping whatever it kept.

She went back to the house — her childhood house, keys still on her ring, because Aldous had never changed the locks, which she'd always read as sentiment and now read as something else entirely. The study light was off. The fireplace was cold and swept, the ash already gone, the hearthstone scrubbed. Efficient. Guilty-efficient.

In the coat closet, she pulled on her jacket to leave for the hotel, and felt the weight.

Left inside pocket. Something small and hard, tucked against the lining — placed there during the kiss on the cheek, or the handshake, or some other pass close enough to be innocent. She fished it out and held it under her phone's light.

A key. Small, brass, older style, with a paper tag wired to the bow. On the tag, in her father's handwriting — the handwriting from her childhood lunchboxes, from the notes he'd left on the kitchen counter — a single line:

*First National. Box 441. If anything happens to me, don't trust the water. Trust Ada Pell.*

Ada Pell. The coroner. Dead, what, eight years now? The woman whose signature sat at the bottom of every death certificate in Hollowmere for forty years, including, if memory served, the three drownings nobody talked about.

Ilsa stood in the dark coat closet of her childhood home, the key biting cold into her palm, and understood that her father had just done the one thing he'd sworn he'd never do.

He'd picked a side. He just couldn't say so out loud.

Outside, far off across the sleeping town, she thought she heard a car pull away from the curb — slow, unhurried, gone before she reached the window. She stood a long time in the dark, listening to the silence, and the silence sounded less like peace than like patience.

Box 441. First National. Opens at nine.

She didn't sleep at all.
