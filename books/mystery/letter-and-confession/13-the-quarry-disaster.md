## 13. The Quarry Disaster

*The following accounts were assembled from depositions, letters, and recorded statements gathered by Kestrel Fairweather in the weeks following the discovery of Ada Pell's ledger. Some were given freely. Some were given because the alternative was worse. They are presented here in the order they were spoken, not the order they occurred.*

* * *

**Testimony of Ruth Calloway, widow, recorded at her kitchen table, Hollowmere, October of this year**

You want to know about the day of the collapse. Everyone wants the day. Nobody wants the year before it, or the year after.

But fine. The day.

It was October ninth, 1994. Cold enough for your breath to show by seven in the morning. My Danny kissed me at the door and said, "Ruthie, if the east wall goes, I'm going home." He said it like a joke. He'd been saying it for two years, because he'd been telling them for two years — writing letters to Aldous Brannock, to the site manager, to the county inspector. The east wall was undercut. Everybody who worked that pit knew it. You could hear it, some mornings. A sound like a big animal shifting its weight in its sleep.

Danny was the safety steward. That's what they called it — safety steward — like he was a man who kept the flowers watered. He filed eleven written complaints in nineteen months. I know because I kept the carbon copies. I kept them in a biscuit tin under the bed, and when the lawyers came after the funeral, the tin was empty. Every copy gone. I never found out who took them. I have my suspicions, but my suspicions have kept me alive this long, so I'll keep them to myself.

The wall went at 11:40 in the morning. Not all at once. First there was the sound — the animal waking up — and then a crack that ran the length of the pit like lightning frozen in stone, and then four hundred tons of limestone came down on five men who had asked, in writing, eleven times, to be moved.

Danny was forty-one. We'd been married nineteen years. He had a laugh you could hear from the road.

They told me it was his fault. Did you know that? At the inquiry — the one that lasted four days — they said the safety steward had failed to properly assess the risk. They said my Danny, who wrote the complaints, who kept the carbons, who told every man on that crew to keep his hard hat on and his eyes on the east wall — they said it was his negligence that killed them. Him and four others, dead, so a dead man could carry the blame.

The foreman, they meant. Silas Crane. Silas was under that wall too. He couldn't argue with any of it.

I took the money. I'll say it plainly since nobody else in this town will say anything plainly. They offered me a settlement and a funeral and the promise that Danny's name would be cleared "in time," and I signed, because I had two children and no wage coming and the alternative was years of lawyers against men who owned the lawyers. I signed. And then for thirty years I've watched this town be polite to me at the market and look through me at the same time, and I have kept the biscuit tin, empty as it is, under the bed.

You asked what I remember most. Not the sound. Not the phone call.

I remember that Danny came home the Sunday before and sat in that chair — that one, don't move it — and said, "If anything happens, Ruthie, it wasn't me. Whatever they say. It wasn't me."

I've had thirty years to decide whether that was a premonition or just a man who knew exactly what kind of town he lived in.

I've decided it was both.

**Testimony of Aldous Brannock, given under caution, present but unrecorded, this year — transcribed from memory by Kestrel Fairweather**

You have to understand what the quarry was in '94. It wasn't a business. It was the town's heartbeat. Two hundred and thirty men on the payroll, and every one of them feeding a family, and every family buying from the shops, and the shops renting from the landlords, and the landlords — well. You can draw the rest of the picture yourself. When the quarry breathed in, Hollowmere ate.

The east wall. Yes. I knew about the east wall.

I knew because Danny Calloway made sure I knew. Eleven letters. I read every one. I want that on the record, if this ever is a record — I read them, and I did nothing, and I will carry that until I die, which I am told may be sooner than I'd planned.

Here is what I did instead. I asked the engineers what remediation would cost. They said — and I've remembered the number for thirty years, it's carved into me — they said two hundred and ten thousand pounds and eleven weeks of stopped production. Eleven weeks. Do you know what eleven weeks of stopped production would have done to us in '94? The bank was already circling. My brother Edmund — you'll want to ask about Edmund, everyone does, now — Edmund sat across from me at that table and said, "The wall has stood for forty years. It'll stand for forty more. Danny Calloway wants a bigger budget and a title. Give him a title."

So I gave Danny Calloway a title. Safety Steward of the Year. There's a photograph. I handed him a plaque at the Christmas do and shook his hand and looked him in the eye, and he looked back at me like a man looking at a wall.

Then it fell on him.

After — after, when the dust was still in the air over the pit and the widows were still being notified — my brother took over. I want to be clear about that too. Edmund ran the aftermath. Edmund hired the lawyers, Edmund spoke to the county men, Edmund decided that Silas Crane — dead, poor Silas, who'd flagged the wall himself in March and been overruled by Edmund — would carry it. Edmund decided what the settlements would be. Edmund handled the payments, and I signed what he put in front of me, and I told myself that grief made me stupid, and then I told myself that money made the widows whole, and then I stopped telling myself anything at all, because a man can only lie to himself for so long before he hires someone else to do it.

The coroner. You're her protégé, so you'll want this part exact. Ada Pell certified all five deaths as accidental, consistent with industrial collapse. That was true. What was not in her reports — what she fought to include and was made to remove — was the eleven complaints. The engineering assessments. The letters between my brother and the site office. She had copies. I know she had copies because Edmund spent one entire night, I believe in the spring of '95, trying to find out what she'd kept and where.

I don't know if he found out. I think, now, that he never did. I think she was cleverer than all of us.

You're looking at me like you want me to say the rest, so I'll say the rest. Five men are dead because my brother valued eleven weeks of production over their lives, and I let him, and then I let him buy the town's silence with its own money, and I have spent thirty years being beloved for it. They gave me a plaque too. Citizen of the Year, 2009. It's in a drawer. I've never hung it. I've never been able to explain to anyone why, and I'm not going to try with you.

Ask me about the ledger another time. Ask me about the names. But not today. Today I've said what the wall took from me, and my hands won't stop shaking, and I'd like to stop.

**Testimony of Tom Marrow, recorded in his trailer, this year, second session, sober**

First session I told you about the files. What I was ordered to do with them. This time you want the day itself, and I'll give it to you, but I want it understood that I was twenty-six years old and I thought a uniform was a personality.

I was second on scene. First was Deputy Hargrave, who's dead now, so he gets to be whatever version of himself the living want. We came over the ridge at 12:15 and the pit looked like God had taken a bite out of it. Dust so thick it was snowing grey. Men running up the access road carrying other men. The noise — people think a disaster is loud, but the loud part is short. What stays is the quiet after. Forty men standing at the lip of that pit, not talking, just looking down at where their friends were.

We got three out alive. Two died in the ambulance. Five under the wall.

I knew the wall was bad. That's the thing I can't get out from under. Not because I was an engineer — I wasn't anything, I was a kid with a radio and a citation for drunk driving the year before. But Danny Calloway was my neighbor. He'd fixed my truck's alternator in his driveway two weeks before, wouldn't take money, and while he worked he talked, the way he always talked, about the east wall, about the letters, about how "somebody's going to listen eventually or somebody's going to bury us." I laughed. I remember laughing. He had grease on his hands and he was telling me he was going to die at work and I laughed, because Danny was a talker, and in Hollowmere a talker is just weather.

After, when the inquiry started, Sheriff DeWitt called me in. Not Hargrave — me, the junior man, the one with the drunk citation hanging over him. He said the county was going to want statements from everyone on scene, and that statements were like knives, son, you had to be careful where you pointed them. He said, "You saw a collapse. You didn't see complaints. You didn't see letters. A collapse is what you saw."

I said I'd seen Danny's letters on his kitchen table.

DeWitt looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "Marrow, you've got a record. You've got a future that fits in a thimble. Think about which one you want to carry."

I carried the thimble. I wrote my statement the way he wanted it. And when the files came through the office two years later — the real ones, the engineering reports, the letters with Danny's handwriting on them — I was the one DeWitt told to box them and take them to county storage, and I was the one who signed the transfer log, and I was the one who knew, when the log came back stamped *not received*, that they were never going to arrive anywhere at all.

You asked me last time if I knew where they went. I said no. I'm correcting that now, because I've been reading what your Ada Pell left behind, and because I'm done carrying the thimble.

They went into the mayor's archive. I saw the driver take the boxes. I didn't know whose driver he was then. I know now.

That's my testimony. Write it down before I sober up enough to take it back.

**Testimony of Ada Pell, Coroner of Hollowmere, dictated to cassette, undated — found in the ledger box, transcribed this year**

Third attempt. The first two tapes are in the quarry. I dropped them down the vent shaft myself, and I'd do it again, because a voice is evidence and evidence is a thing that gets people killed in this town.

But a letter — a letter can be denied. A letter is just paper. So I'm going to say this once, onto this tape, and then I'm going to write it down in a form no one will find until they've earned it, and then I'm going to bury the tape with the others.

October ninth, 1994. Five men dead: Daniel Calloway, Silas Crane, Peter Oss, Michael Dunn, and the Ostrander boy — Tomas, nineteen, three weeks into the job. I examined all five. I certified all five. Accidental death, industrial collapse. Every word of it true, and every report a lie, because of what I left out.

I had the complaints. All eleven, with carbon copies, given to me by Danny Calloway himself, eight days before he died. He came to my office — the first living person to come to my office for anything but a death certificate, which should have told me something about Hollowmere right there — and he put the carbons on my desk and said, "Hold these. If anything happens, they'll come for the originals. They'll come for everything. But they won't think to come to you, because to them you're just the woman who signs."

He was right on every count. They came for the originals — I don't know how, I don't know who, but the inquiry made no mention of them and neither did the county men, and papers like that don't walk. And they never thought to come to me. Edmund Brannock sent a lawyer to *remind* me of my duties of discretion, and I nodded like a woman being reminded, and I kept the carbons in the one place in Hollowmere no one would ever search.

The funeral urns. Behind them. Where the dead keep watch over the living, which is the only arrangement this town has ever honored.

I want the record to show that I was not brave. I certified the lies because Edmund Brannock's lawyer explained to me, with great courtesy, what would happen to the clinic funding, to my position, to the pension of my predecessor's widow, if the town's only coroner developed a conscience. I signed because I was afraid, and I have been afraid for thirty years, and I am tired of it in a way that has stopped being fear and become something with edges.

But I did this. I kept the carbons. And every ten years, when the anniversary comes around and I take them out and count them, I add a line to a letter I will mail to myself, because a letter can be denied, and a letter can wait, and I have learned to wait too.

The third one is coming. I can feel it the way Danny Calloway felt the wall.

If you're reading this, you found the urns. So you're either the coroner I trained — in which case, child, I'm sorry, and you were the only one I could trust with the shape of a lie because you know the shape of a true thing better than anyone I've ever met — or you're one of them, in which case the carbons you want are in a quarry vent shaft at the bottom of four hundred feet of limestone, and Danny Calloway is keeping them company.

Either way: count the names. Count the decades. And when the third one comes — and it is coming — don't certify it.

Don't be me.

**Testimony of Ilsa Brannock, given at the kitchen table of the house she grew up in, this year, her father present and silent**

I was nine in 1994. So my testimony is a child's, and I've spent thirty years not knowing it was testimony.

Here is what a nine-year-old knew. She knew that October had a hole in it. She knew that her father stopped going to the quarry overlook, where he used to take her to point out the trucks. She knew that her uncle Edmund — who was the fun one, the one who taught her to skip stones — started coming to the house every Sunday, and that the Sundays had a sound: low voices in the study, the good whiskey, and her father's laugh not happening.

She knew that the school got new gym equipment that spring, and the church got a new roof, and the library got computers, and that when the teachers thanked the Brannock family at assembly, her father stood up too fast and sat down too fast, like a man avoiding a chair he thought might be pulled away.

And she knew — this is the part I've only just understood, putting it next to Ruth Calloway's words and Marrow's and Ada Pell's tape — she knew that Danny Calloway's widow moved to the far end of Fenwick Street, and that her father drove past that street every single week for years, slow, and never turned in. I thought he was checking on her. A child would think that.

Now I think he was doing penance at ten miles an hour, and that penance you don't act on is just driving.

Here is what a nine-year-old didn't know: that the gym equipment and the church roof and the library computers were bought with hush money. That the town's gratitude was purchased in the same transaction as its silence. That her uncle kept a ledger. That the ledger had names in it, and that thirty years later, in a safe-deposit box two towns over, she would find that ledger and read her own name in it, uncrossed, waiting its turn.

My father says he's going to tell me everything. He's said it three times now, and each time he gets as far as *Edmund and I* and then looks at his hands.

I've stopped waiting for him to finish the sentence. I've got the tape now. I've got Ruth Calloway's biscuit tin — empty, but she kept it, and keeping it is its own kind of testimony. I've got Marrow's transfer log. I've got a coroner who exhumes graves for a living and doesn't flinch.

The sentence my father can't finish? I can finish it. Edmund and I let the wall fall, and then we bought a town, and the town let us, because the town was hungry and the money smelled like charity instead of blood.

That's the disaster. Not the wall. The thirty years after it.

**From the working notes of Kestrel Fairweather, this year, undated entry**

I have listened to the tape four times. I have read the carbons — the real ones, pulled from the vent shaft by a man I paid too much and thanked too little — and the eleven complaints in Danny Calloway's hand are exactly what Ada said: precise, dated, escalating, and addressed each time one level higher. Calloway to site manager. Site manager to Aldous. Aldous to Edmund. The last one, dated September 30th, nine days before the collapse, is addressed to the county inspector, and it says, in full:

*Mr. Harrow — I have written eleven times. I am writing you now because the others have decided the wall can wait. I am telling you it cannot. If it goes, it will not be an accident. It will be a decision. Please come before it becomes one.*

The county inspector never came. The county inspector's office, I have learned from Marrow's files, was notified of the collapse on October 9th at 1:30 p.m. — fifty minutes before the bodies were recovered — by a call placed from the Brannock estate.

They knew the wall would fall the way you know a storm is coming, and they stood under it anyway, and when it fell they called it weather.

Five names. Five decades of silence. And Ada Pell, who signed the lies and kept the truth in a biscuit-tin world of her own making, waiting all this time for someone to count.

I've counted. The ledger's last name is Ilsa Brannock, and the third drowning is coming, and somewhere in this town a man who has been dead for twenty years is deciding when.

I think I know where he's been standing all along.

I think he's been standing at the overlook — the same place Aldous used to take his daughter to point out the trucks — watching the quarry breathe in, and waiting for Hollowmere to eat.
