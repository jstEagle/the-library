## 15. Dead Letter Drop

The clue was a postmark.

Ilsa had been staring at it for three days, pinned to the corkboard above her father's old drafting table — the one she'd claimed as her own the week she moved back into the house, because claiming things in that house had always been an act of aggression. The postmark was on the back of the note Ada Pell had left in the safe-deposit box, tucked behind the half-confession and the list of names. A small thing. A return address printed in faded violet ink: *Pell, P.O. Box 11, Hollowmere*.

Everyone in Hollowmere knew Ada Pell didn't have a post office box. She'd had one, decades ago, before Kestrel Fairweather took over the coroner's office and Ada retreated into her house full of urns and silence. But the box number was still legible, and the ink was thirty years old at least, and Ilsa had learned from her father's ledgers — the ones she wasn't supposed to have read — that in this town, dead paper trails were the only honest ones.

So on a gray Thursday with rain needling the windows, she walked down to the post office on Corben Street and asked the woman at the counter about Box 11.

"Closed," the woman said. "Been closed since—" She frowned, consulted nothing, remembered anyway. "Since Miss Pell died. They hold mail for closed boxes, though. Ninety days, then return to sender." A pause. "But nobody ever came for hers. There's a whole crate of it in back. Undeliverable. You family?"

"I'm her executor," Ilsa said, which was a lie so smooth it frightened her. Her father's daughter, after all. "Probate's finally cleared."

The woman looked at her for a long moment — long enough that Ilsa felt the lie settle into her bones like silt — then shrugged and disappeared into the back room. When she returned, she set a plastic mail tote on the counter, heavy as a paving stone, stuffed with envelopes bound in crumbling rubber bands.

"Sign here."

Ilsa signed. Her hand did not shake until she was outside.

* * *

She didn't open them in the car. She didn't open them at the house, where her father might wander in with his tea and his terrible cheerfulness and his birthday-party smile still hanging around somewhere like a coat he hadn't put away. She drove out past the quarry, past the overlook where three hundred guests had toasted Aldous Brannock's seventy years, and parked at the trailhead to the reservoir, where the trees closed overhead and the rain became a sound instead of a feeling.

There were forty-one letters.

She counted them twice on the passenger seat, rubber bands biting grooves into the paper. The oldest envelope was yellowed to the color of weak tea, postmarked 1989. The newest was dated eleven days before Ada Pell's death. All addressed the same way, in the same careful hand:

*To Ada Pell, from Ada Pell.*

Dead letters. Letters mailed to oneself — not to be read, but to be *dated*. To exist in the postal system's memory, stamped and sorted and officially witnessed, so that no one could later say they'd been forged. Ilsa understood it immediately, the way you understand a trap only after your foot is in it. Ada Pell had been building evidence for thirty years and hiding it in plain sight, in a box no one visited, waiting for someone to come asking.

Waiting, maybe, for exactly this.

Ilsa opened the earliest letter first. It seemed right, chronological, orderly — the way Kestrel would have done it, and the thought of Kestrel steadied her hands.

*March 14, 1989.*

*Aldous came to my office again tonight. He wants the Hollis boy's certificate amended. Says the blood alcohol will "complicate things." I told him the blood alcohol was .19 and that complications are what the truth is made of. He smiled at me the way his brother smiles — Edmund was there, standing by the window, saying nothing, which is how Edmund says everything — and he said, "Ada, you have a son in college. Tuition is expensive. Accidents are cheap."*

*I signed the amendment. God forgive me. I am going to keep every copy of everything, and one day I am going to bury them all so deep in daylight that no one can dig them up without the whole world watching.*

Ilsa sat very still. Rain drummed the roof. Somewhere downslope, water moved through the reservoir spillway with its low constant throat-sound, the sound this town never stopped making if you listened.

Her father. Not a monster in the abstract, not a name crossed off a list, but a man who had stood in a coroner's office and priced a dead boy against tuition. She thought of him teaching her to skip stones on the quarry pond when she was seven. *Wrist loose, Ilse-bird. Let the stone do the work.* She thought of the papers burning in the study grate, and the key pressed into her coat pocket like a hot coal, and the way he'd flinched at the word *drowning* among his friends.

She opened the next letter. Then the next. She stopped crying after the fourth one, mostly because there wasn't time.

* * *

The letters built a cathedral of cowardice and conscience, brick by brick.

1991: *Edmund handles the money now. Aldous signs what Edmund puts in front of him. I used to think Aldous was the strong one. I no longer think anything about the Brannock brothers except that they are two halves of one animal, and the animal is hungry.*

1993: *Five men went into the east cut yesterday and four came out. No — that is not true. Five came out, and one of them was wearing a suit. The collapse report is being written as we speak, and I have been asked to certify the foreman's death as negligence, his alone, a dead man blamed for living men's greed. The widow Voss came to my office today. She has three children. I could not look at her. I gave her tea I could not drink myself.*

Ilsa's breath caught. *Voss.* She read the paragraph again. The widow Voss. Three children. She filed it away the way her father had taught her to file things — carefully, in the dark, where it would keep.

1994, after the official disaster: *It is done. The town believes what the town needs to believe. Four families took the money; two did not, and those two are being made to suffer in small legal ways that leave no marks. I certified five deaths today. Four were true. The fifth — Marat Okafor — died three days before the collapse, and everyone in that quarry office knows it, and the certificate says otherwise, and my signature is on it like a brand on a steer.*

*I am keeping the negatives. Photographs of the east cut, taken the morning before, showing the shoring Edmund ordered removed. If anything happens to me, they are with the rest of it. If anyone ever reads these letters: I was not brave. I was a bookkeeper of the dead who finally started keeping books for myself.*

Ilsa's phone buzzed. Kestrel. She silenced it. Not because she didn't trust them — she did, more than she trusted almost anyone, which was its own kind of vertigo — but because she needed to do this alone, the way you need to be alone to hear your own bones creak.

* * *

By late afternoon the light through the windshield had gone the color of old brass, and she had reached 2004, and the letters changed.

They got shorter. The handwriting, always so controlled, began to lean. And the subject shifted — from cover-ups to something stranger, something that read at first like grief and then, paragraph by paragraph, like fear.

*June 2004. Edmund is dead. Drowned in the quarry, they say, fell from the north ledge in the dark. I certified it myself. I looked at the body in my own cooler and I signed the certificate and I buried the man under my own seal, and I want these words to survive me: I did not believe it, even then. The body was right. The height was right. Everything was right except the truth, which was nowhere in the room.*

*August 2004. I drove past the Brannock place and saw a figure at an upstairs window. Tall. Edmund's build. It was dusk and I had been drinking more than I should, and I told myself it was a cousin, a caretaker, a shadow. I have decided to stop drinking. I have not managed it.*

*October 2004. Someone went through my files while I was at the Hollis funeral. Nothing taken. Things moved. Small things — a folder turned, a drawer not quite shut. Edmund always checked drawers. It was a habit of his, checking whether people had looked. I used to think it was control. Now I think it was reconnaissance.*

*December 2004. I exhumed nothing. I want that written plainly: I suspected, and I did nothing, because I am a coward with a filing system. But I wrote to the grave. I wrote to the ground itself: EDMUND IS NOT IN THE QUARRY.*

That sentence stopped her heart for one full beat.

She read it three times. *Edmund is not in the quarry.* Not *I don't believe he drowned.* Not *something is wrong.* A flat declarative sentence, written the way Ada wrote death certificates — with the authority of someone who had seen enough bodies to know when a fact refused to be one.

Ilsa thought of the list in the safe-deposit box. Names, one crossed out per decade. Her father's generation bled on a ten-year clock, and the last name uncrossed was her own. She had assumed the hand holding the pen was history — a pattern, a curse, a debt compounding across the years. She had not let herself imagine a *person*, walking, breathing, patient as groundwater.

And now the letters were telling her the person had been presumed dead for twenty years, and that the only witness who'd doubted it had kept her doubt in a post office box and drunk herself quiet rather than say it aloud.

* * *

The last letter was dated February 2nd — nine days before Ada Pell died in her sleep, officially, of heart failure, certified by her own protégé.

The envelope was different. Thicker. Ilsa's fingers went cold before she even opened it, some animal intelligence in her skin knowing before her mind did.

Inside were two pages, and a photograph.

The photograph first, because it slid out and landed face-up on her lap and there was no unseeing it. It showed the north ledge of the quarry at night, grainy, taken from below with a long lens or a steady hand and a lot of patience. Two figures stood at the edge. One was unmistakably her father — the slope of his shoulders, older now but the same, the shoulders she'd hugged at the party while guests sang. The other figure was taller, thinner, standing with a particular stillness, head tilted slightly toward Aldous as if listening, as if instructing.

The face was turned three-quarters away. But the jaw, the height, the way the man held his hands folded at his waist like a man at a graveside —

Ilsa had grown up around Edmund Brannock. He'd taught her to gut a fish when she was nine, folding her small hands over the knife the same way his were folded in the photograph. She was thirty-eight years old and her whole body recognized him before her mind consented to.

The letter, in Ada's failing hand:

*If you're reading this, then either I am dead or I have finally found my courage, and we both know which is more likely.*

*The third drowning is coming. I can feel it the way I felt the other two — the town goes quiet in a certain way, polite in a certain way, and the Brannock checkbook comes out. This time the name at the end of it will be Ilsa. I have seen the ledger. I know how they count.*

*Understand what I am about to write, because I have spent twenty years not writing it and I will not soften it now: Edmund Brannock is alive. He did not drown in 2004. That body in my cooler belonged to no one — a John Doe, unclaimed, delivered to me by a driver whose face I cannot recall, which frightens me more than anything else in this file. I certified it because I was told to, and I was told to by a voice on a telephone, and the voice was Edmund's, and I would know it anywhere, because that voice once offered me tuition money over a dead boy's blood alcohol.*

*He has been here the whole time. In the town. Watching. The quarry gave him the perfect gift — a death with his name on it, certified by me, mourned by everyone. He walks free among us as no one. And Aldous knows. Look at my photograph. Whatever passes between those two brothers on that ledge, it is not grief.*

*I am too old and too compromised to fight him in the open. He owns too many signatures, mine chief among them. So I am doing the only thing left to a bookkeeper of the dead: I am mailing the truth to myself, one stamp at a time, and I am leaving it where a certain stubborn coroner — or whoever that coroner sends, because I know Kestrel, and Kestrel does not work alone anymore, whatever they pretend — will eventually come looking.*

*Box 11. Tell them: Box 11.*

*And tell them the thing I could never sign my name to:*

*EDMUND IS NOT IN THE QUARRY. HE NEVER WAS. HE IS HERE.*

*— A.P.*

The rain had stopped. Ilsa didn't know for how long. The reservoir below the trailhead lay flat and pewter-colored, holding the sky the way the quarry held its water — patiently, without opinion, ready to take whatever was given to it.

She sat with the photograph in her lap until her legs went numb. Then she picked up her phone and called Kestrel, and when Kestrel answered on the second ring, she heard her own voice come out level and cold, her father's gift to her at last, and say:

"It's not Aldous. It was never Aldous. Get to the cemetery, Kestrel. Bring a shovel. We need to open Edmund Brannock's grave."

On the line, silence — and then, quietly, in a voice Ilsa had never heard them use, Kestrel Fairweather said, "I already filed the permit this morning. I've been dreaming about that coffin for a week."

"Ilsa," they added, before hanging up. "Don't go home tonight. Don't be near water. And don't — *whatever you do* — don't let your father know what you've read."

She was already looking at the road home, dark and wet and empty, the lights of the Brannock house just visible beyond the ridge, one window burning on the upper floor.

Where a tall figure stood, perfectly still, watching the reservoir.

Watching her.
