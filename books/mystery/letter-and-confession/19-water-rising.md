## 19. Water Rising

The trap was Kestrel's idea. The bait was mine.

That's what I kept telling myself as I sat alone in the Brannock Quarry office at seven in the evening, a folder of nothing on the desk in front of me and my heart going like a drill bit through limestone. *You volunteered,* I told myself. *Nobody made you.* But volunteering and meaning it are two different animals, and only one of them was in the room with me.

The notice had gone up that morning — a legal filing, public record, exactly the kind of thing Edmund Brannock could not afford to ignore. *Estate of Ada Pell: sealed materials scheduled for unsealing, Friday, nine a.m., county courthouse.* Photographs. A full confession. Names.

Kestrel had written it in their careful coroner's hand and then looked at me across the morgue table — under the fluorescent light, with Marrow's arm still bandaged from the reservoir — and said, "He'll come for it himself. He can't risk a copy existing past Friday. And when he comes, he'll talk. Men like him always talk when they think they're winning."

"And if he doesn't talk?" Marrow had asked.

"Then we'll have bigger problems," Kestrel said, "because he'll have brought tools instead of words."

So here I was, the tool-bait's sister, sitting in my family's quarry office where the windows were fogged with cold and the radiator ticked like something counting down. The folder on the desk contained forty pages of blank paper and one real document: a photocopy of the unsealing notice, because Edmund would want proof the threat was real before he did anything stupid.

I'd insisted on being the one inside. Kestrel had argued — quietly, in that flat way they had, like reading out findings — and lost. My argument was simple: Edmund knew all three of our faces by now. He'd been watching this town for twenty years from behind other people's eyes. But he didn't know I'd found the safe-deposit box. He didn't know about the list with the names crossed out, decade by decade, ending one name short of mine. As far as Edmund knew, Ilsa Brannock was just the estranged daughter who'd come home for a birthday and stayed for the wrong reasons.

I was the only one of us he might still underestimate.

The recorder was in my coat pocket. Kestrel had shown me how to work it with two fingers, no fumbling. "Don't lead him," they'd said. "Let him fill the silence. Silence is where confessions live." They'd said it like an autopsy finding. Kestrel talked about everything like an autopsy finding. It was strangely calming.

Outside, the light went from gray to blue to black. The quarry below the office terrace filled with shadow, its flooded lower levels invisible now, just a smell of wet stone rising through the floor vents. Somewhere down there were the pumps Kestrel had checked twice — running, functional, wired to the emergency drain. Insurance, they'd called it. I tried to believe in insurance.

At 7:40, headlights swept the gravel lot.

Not Aldous's truck. Not the mayor's town car. A plain sedan, unremarkable, the kind of car you'd never describe to a sketch artist because you'd never look at it twice. It parked at the far end of the lot, and the man who got out moved like water finding its level — unhurried, inevitable, taking the low ground.

Edmund Brannock. Alive for twenty years while his own headstone grew moss. He looked older than the photographs in Ada's letters, thinner, his hair gone white at the temples, but the face was still recognizably the face from the 1994 company picnic photo — the brother who stood slightly apart from Aldous in every picture, smiling a half-second after everyone else.

He didn't knock. The door opened and he was inside, closing it softly behind him, and the temperature of the room changed the way a room changes when a door opens onto winter.

"Ilsa." He said my name like he'd practiced it. Maybe he had. Twenty years is a long time to practice being someone's uncle. "You've grown into your mother."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the family thing. You don't get the family thing."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I suppose I don't." He crossed to the desk without being invited and looked down at the folder. His hands were steady. That was the worst part — watching those hands, knowing what they'd done at the reservoir, what they intended to do with the last name on the list, and seeing them perfectly, insultingly steady. "May I?"

"It's your funeral," I said, and immediately hated myself for the phrasing.

He opened the folder. Flipped through the blank pages with the patience of a man who already knew what he'd find, and stopped at the photocopy of the unsealing notice. Read it once. Twice. Then he smiled, and it was the smile from the photographs, the half-second-late smile, the one that never reached anything above the cheekbones.

"Blank paper," he said. "Clever girl. Where are the real documents?"

"Somewhere you'll never find them. If anything happens to any of us, they go to the courthouse automatically. Kestrel arranged it. You know how thorough Kestrel is — you hired them, after all."

Something flickered behind his eyes at that. The first crack. "Voss was careless."

"Voss was a decoy. Like me." I stood up, because sitting felt too much like waiting. "Here's what I don't understand, Edmund. You've had twenty years. Twenty years of being dead, of being free, of being nobody. Why start killing again? Why now? What does Aldous's death buy you that twenty years of quiet couldn't?"

He set the folder down and looked at me with something almost like affection, which was the most frightening thing yet.

"You think I ever stopped?" he said. "1994 wasn't the beginning, child. It was the middle. This quarry has been eating people since before your father learned to walk, and every generation, the Brannock holding it has had to decide what the quarry is worth. Your grandfather decided it was worth five men. Your father decided it was worth five more, plus a foreman to blame, plus a coroner's conscience, plus a deputy's career." He ticked them off on those steady fingers. "Me? I decided it was worth everything. The house. The name. The town. All of it, held underwater, where it's safe."

"The workers' families—"

"Were paid. Generously. Ask around; nobody starved." He said it like a balance sheet closing clean. "Grief keeps, Ilsa. Guilt doesn't. Guilt is the thing that rots. Your father has been rotting for thirty years, and rot spreads. Ada Pell started keeping records. Then she started keeping *evidence*. Then she started mailing letters to herself like some madwoman tucking receipts under her mattress. Every ten years I closed another account, and every ten years the books balanced, and every ten years your father wept and paid and said *never again* and meant it right up until the next time meaning it cost him something."

"So you killed them. The drownings. Three people who had nothing to do with any of it."

"They had everything to do with it. They were the leaks." He straightened the folder's corners, aligning them with the desk edge, and I understood suddenly that this was a man who had spent two decades arranging a room no one could see into. "And now there's one leak left, and it isn't even a leak. It's a daughter who came home." He looked at me then, directly, and for one moment the pleasantness dropped away and I saw the thing underneath, the thing that had watched my family from inside a fake grave for twenty years. "I'm sorry it's you. Truly. If you'd stayed away — if you'd stayed in the city with your mother's people — none of this would touch you."

"You'd have let me live."

"I'd have preferred to."

*Let him fill the silence,* Kestrel had said. But the silence was filling with him, and I needed him moving, needed him off-balance, needed him anywhere but standing between me and the door.

"The documents aren't here," I said, "but there's something else. Something you should hear before you decide what kind of night this is."

"And what's that?"

"Aldous is coming."

For the first time, Edmund went still in the wrong way — not the stillness of control but the stillness of a man hearing a floorboard creak overhead. "No," he said. "Aldous is at home. I made sure of his evening."

"He got your message. The one you sent through the lawyer last week — the anniversary notice, the *final accounting*. He knows tonight is the night. He's known for three days." I took a step toward the window, angling my body so the recorder in my pocket had clear air, and lied with my whole chest: "He's coming to give you what you want. Himself. On the record. Isn't that what you've actually wanted all along? Not his death. His surrender."

Edmund's jaw worked. "You're lying."

"Am I?" I nodded past him, through the fogged glass, at the twin threads of headlights just turning off the county road onto the quarry access drive. "Then explain the truck."

* * *

He believed me, or he believed the truck, which amounted to the same thing, and that was the mistake I'd built the whole evening on. Because Edmund Brannock could not leave. Not with the unsealing notice real, not with the clock running to Friday, not with his brother possibly arriving to say aloud the things that had lived underwater for thirty years. He had to control the room. Control was the only currency he'd ever held.

So he took it.

His hand closed on my upper arm — not violent, worse than violent, *administrative* — and he walked me out of the office, across the terrace, and down the steel stairs into the quarry's throat. The lower level. The flooded galleries where the pumps throbbed in the dark and the water stood black and flat as poured tar, deep enough to drown a town's whole history in.

"There's a room down here," he said conversationally, as if giving me a tour. "Old foreman's station. Dry, mostly. Door locks from the outside. When your father arrives, he and I are going to have our accounting, and you're going to be somewhere you can't interrupt it. Afterward—" he considered, "—afterward depends on how the accounting goes."

"Edmund." I planted my feet on the stairs. The water smell rose around us, mineral and old. "Listen to me. Whatever you think happens tonight — whatever story you've rehearsed — it ends with police. Kestrel filed the notice through the courthouse. There are copies. There are people who know where I am right now. Killing me buys you hours, not freedom."

"Hours," he said, "have historically been sufficient."

He pressed me down the last flights. The stairwell lights had been dead for years; he carried a flashlight, and its beam made the water below gleam like something alive. At the bottom, the corridor ran along the flooded gallery, and through a rusted grate I could see it — the black water, maybe eight feet below the walkway level, lapping at stone that hadn't seen sun since 1994. Five men had died down here when the collapse came. Their names were on a plaque upstairs in the office, brass gone green. I'd walked past it my whole childhood.

The foreman's station was a concrete room with a heavy door and a hasp lock, and he put me inside it with the gentleness of a man shelving a book.

"Aldous will want to see you," I said through the gap before it shut. "When he gets here. He'll ask where I am. What will you tell him, Edmund? What lie have you prepared for your brother?"

Edmund paused. In the flashlight's spill his face was all planes and shadow, and for a moment — one strange, vertiginous moment — he looked tired. Old. Like a man at the end of a very long shift.

"The truth," he said. "For once. I'll tell him the truth. It's the one luxury of finishing something."

The door closed. The hasp clacked. And I heard his footsteps recede up the stairs, unhurried, inevitable, going to meet the headlights.

* * *

I didn't wait to be rescued. Waiting was for people without coat pockets.

The recorder was still running — I'd checked it on the stairs, thumb over the switch, praying he hadn't noticed the small red weight of it against my ribs. Now I turned to the room itself. Foreman's stations had radios. Ours had a radio, dead, and a wall phone, older than me, a landline strung on a cord that vanished up through the ceiling conduit toward the office above. I lifted the receiver. Dial tone. Of course there was dial tone; Aldous had never let anyone decommission the hard lines, not after '94, not when the cell towers died every time the wind came off the ridge. My father's paranoia about communication had finally done one useful thing.

I dialed the number Kestrel had made me memorize and only me, drilled into me at the morgue table until I could recite it asleep. It rang twice.

"Morgue," said Kestrel Fairweather, at nine at night, in a voice with no surprise in it at all.

"He took the bait. Lower level, foreman's station, locked in. Recorder's running. Aldous is pulling in now — Edmund thinks Aldous is coming to surrender. Kestrel, he's going to make him confess and then he's going to kill him anyway, I can hear it, it's the *accounting*, he said afterward depends on—"

"Breathe," Kestrel said. "Marrow's twelve minutes out with the sheriff's department, delayed on purpose — we need the confession complete before uniforms arrive, or Edmund lawyers up and Aldous recants by morning. Can you stay on the line?"

"I'm locked in a concrete box next to eight feet of black water."

"That's a yes, then." A pause, and beneath the flatness, something that in anyone else would have been fear. "Ilsa. If it goes wrong down there — the emergency drain is on the east gallery wall, red wheel, behind the grate. Do you see it?"

I pressed my eye to the rusted mesh. Forty feet down the corridor, past the black water, a dull red circle caught the faint light. "I see it."

"Don't touch it unless the water rises. Kestrel out."

The line went dead, and I stood in the dark with a dead radio and a live phone and my father's footsteps approaching somewhere above me, and I did the only thing left to do.

I listened.

* * *

Sound carries strangely in a quarry. The voices came down the stairwell and along the gallery and arrived at my door already worn smooth, syllables rounded off, but I knew both voices the way you know weather.

Aldous first, breathless from the stairs: "Where is she? Edmund. *Where is my daughter?*"

"In the foreman's station. She's fine. She's listening, probably — she strikes me as the listening type." A dry laugh. "Come to the water, Aldous. Let's do this where it's honest."

Footsteps. The flashlight beam swinging wild through the grate, throwing bars of light across the black water. Then my father's voice again, closer, and broken in a way I had never heard it, not at Mother's funeral, not at the bankruptcy, not at any of the dinners where he sat silent while the town toasted him.

"Thirty years," Aldous said. "Thirty years I've waited for you to come out of that grave and finish it. You want a confession? Here it is. Here's all of it, brother, take it, it's yours."

"Careful," Edmund said softly.

"No. No more careful. Nineteen ninety-four. I signed the inspection reports. I knew the west gallery supports were shot — my engineer told me in March, told me in writing, and I buried the memo because shutting down for repairs would cost the season, and the season was *everything*, Edmund, you taught me that, you were the one who taught me that the quarry eats or gets eaten." His voice cracked and kept going, a truck rolling through its own wreck. "Five men. Dario Voss — yes, Edmund, *that* Voss, the stranger's uncle, did you know that? Did you know whose nephew you sent to my door? — Marek Ostrowski, the Halloran brothers, and Tomás Reyes. Five names. I paid their wives out of the operating account and called it severance. We blamed Silas Crane, God rest him, blamed a dead man because a dead man can't defend his ledger, and Ada Pell certified the bodies accidental because I went to her house at two in the morning with your letter in my pocket and your instructions in my ear, and I told her she'd sign or the next certificate issued in Hollowmere would be hers."

Silence. Even the pumps seemed to hold their breath.

"She was coerced," Aldous said. "Every ten years, when someone remembered too much, I brought them your arithmetic and she signed. And I told myself it was you, all you, that I was only the messenger — but that's the lie I've lived in, Edmund. The signature was mine. The quarry was mine. The sin was mine, and I let you be dead so I wouldn't have to be guilty."

"Stop," Edmund said.

But Aldous Brannock had finally, after thirty years, gotten rolling, and there was no stopping him. "And the money. The hush payments, the four families, the mayor's little ledger — mine, authorized by me, disbursed by me, renewed every year like insurance on a fire I set myself. And the drownings — God forgive me, I *knew*, I knew it was you the first time, 2004, when they pulled poor Ruth Calloway out of the reservoir and the coroner's report said accidental and I read your handwriting in the margins of the file like a signature. I knew. And I said nothing, because saying something meant losing everything, and I had already decided back in '94 that everything was worth five men, so why not five more, why not a dozen, why not my own daughter's name on your list—"

"*Stop.*"

The word came out of Edmund with a force that made the flashlight beam jump, and in its sudden sweep I saw them both, framed in the grate: my father standing at the water's edge with his arms open like a man inviting arrest, and my uncle three feet away with the flashlight shaking in his steady, steady hand.

"You don't get to confess," Edmund said, and his voice had come apart at last, the administrative calm gone, and underneath it was not rage. Underneath it was something smaller and much older. "You don't get to stand there and hand it over like a receipt. Thirty years I carried it *for* you. I took the grave so you could keep the name. I drowned your leaks so you could sleep. I did the dying so you wouldn't have to, and now you want to *say it out loud*, in front of her, in front of whoever's listening, and be forgiven — you want the confession to be the end of it—"

"It was never supposed to be you," Aldous said. "Edmund. Brother. It was never supposed to be you in the water. When the gallery collapsed, you went down to warn them — I know that now, I've always known that — you went down to warn them because you were the better of us, and the town needed a body to close the books, and I gave it yours." His voice dropped to almost nothing, and the microphone in my pocket strained after it. "I have regretted it every day of my life. Come home. Put it down. Whatever the courts do, let them. I'll stand beside you. I'm asking you, brother. I'm begging you. Stop."

The flashlight beam steadied.

For three full seconds — I counted them against my own pulse — I believed it had worked. I believed in the word *brother*, in thirty years of shared blood finding its way through all that black water.

Then Edmund said, gently, sadly, in the voice of a man closing a final account:

"You went down to the gallery yourself, Aldous. You pulled the supports. I only took the blame for that, too."

And the beam swung — not toward my father, but toward the east gallery wall, toward the pipes, toward the pump controls — and somewhere below us, deep in the flooded dark, machinery that had slept for thirty years began, with a sound like a swallowed scream, to turn over.

The water shivered.

Behind the grate, forty feet down the corridor, the red emergency wheel gleamed, and I was already running.
