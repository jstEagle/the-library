## 9. Aubrey's Debt

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which Cormac Aubrey would later remember as the only strange thing about it. Marrowgate's misfortunes had a taste for Fridays—audits arrived on Fridays, deaths were announced on Fridays, and twelve years ago, on a Friday in October, a boy named Danny Ferro had gone into Blackwater Quarry and not come out. But this envelope came on a Tuesday, slipped between the second mortgage statement of a trout farmer and a birthday card from a woman in Ashford, and it bore no return address, no stamp, and no handwriting at all except his own name.

*Cormac Aubrey*, in block capitals, pressed so hard into the paper that he could feel the letters from the back, like braille, like a scar.

He was standing at the teller window nearest the door, doing what he did every morning at 8:47, which was to confirm that the night deposit bag had been logged and that Mrs. Hollis's coin rolls had been counted twice, because Mrs. Hollis counted them once herself and always came up short and always wept about it, and Cormac had learned long ago that it was cheaper—in every currency—to let her be short by four dollars than to watch her cry in front of the young tellers. He was thinking about none of that when Ruthie brought him the mail tray, and he was thinking about even less when he slit the envelope open with the bone-handled letter opener Edith Vane had given him eleven Christmases ago, a gift he had never decided whether to keep or burn.

Inside was a single sheet of paper, photocopied, slightly skewed, the kind of copy made in a hurry on a machine with a dirty glass.

It was a ledger page.

His name was on it.

---

Cormac locked his office door. He did this perhaps four times a year, and each time he did it, he stood afterward with his hand still on the brass knob, listening to the lock's small click the way other men listened for the sound of a gun being cocked. The office was modest and deliberately dull: framed certificate, dying fern, a photograph of a wife who had left him nine years ago for a man who sold boats in Kingston and laughed easily. On the wall behind the desk hung a print of the Marrowgate bridge in autumn, all amber and rust, the town looking from above like something gentle.

He sat down. He put the page flat on the blotter. He read it three times, because the first two times his eyes kept sliding off the figures the way a tongue keeps returning to a chipped tooth.

The page was dated. Fifteen years of dates, actually, arranged in a column down the left margin like a spine, and beside each date an amount, and beside each amount a routing notation in a shorthand he recognized instantly because he had invented half of it himself: *V.E. — disbursement — per instruction — A.P. review.* And there, in the third row, in the fourth column, was the entry that had ended whatever life Cormac Aubrey might otherwise have lived:

*14 March — C.A., First Marrowgate Savings — signature obtained — $40,000 initial — quarterly thereafter.*

*Signature obtained.*

Not *given*. Not *earned*. *Obtained*, the way one obtains a confession, or a specimen, or a tooth.

The quarterly figure matched, to the dollar, the consulting fee that had appeared on his books every ninety days since the spring Danny Ferro died—the fee he had told himself was retainer money for "estate advisory services," the fee he had laundered through a shell entity called Blackwater Holdings LLC that existed on paper only, in a filing cabinet only he could access, in a drawer only he could lock. He had built that structure himself. Ambrose Pell had sketched it on a napkin at the Gull & Anchor, laughing softly, saying, *You want it clean, Cormac? Then make it boring. Nothing survives scrutiny like boredom.* And Cormac had gone home and drafted the paperwork that same night, hands steady, whiskey untouched, because steadiness was the whole trick of it—you could do anything in Marrowgate if your hands stayed steady.

They were not steady now.

The photocopy trembled against the blotter like a trapped moth. He pressed it flat with both palms, the way you'd press down something alive, and became aware—suddenly, horribly—of the sounds of the bank going on without him: Ruthie's laugh through the wall, the pneumatic hiss of the tube system, old Mr. Tennyson complaining about a hold placed on his pension check. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world that believed Cormac Aubrey was a dull, decent man with a dull, decent ledger of his own.

Someone knew. Someone had the page. And someone had chosen Tuesday to say so.

---

He did not go home after close. He drove instead out the Harrow Road, past the shuttered cannery, past the church whose bell had been cracked since before anyone living was born, and parked where the gravel shoulder widened near the turnoff to Blackwater. He didn't get out. He never got out. In twelve years he had driven this road perhaps forty times and left the car perhaps none, sitting in the driver's seat with the engine ticking and the quarry invisible beyond the treeline, a great grey wound in the earth that the pines had spent a decade trying to bandage over.

You could hear water down there, if the wind was right. That was the thing nobody warned you about Marrowgate. The drowned places sounded patient.

He thought about the boy. Everyone in town thought about the boy in their own private way, the way you think about a stain under wallpaper—rarely, briefly, and always with the relief that it's covered. Cormac's version was more specific. He thought about the morning after, fourteen years ago now—no, twelve, God, twelve—and Ambrose Pell sitting across from him in this very car, in this very spot, while the mist came off the water like breath on cold glass.

"He was alone," Ambrose had said. "That's the finding. A boy wandered onto posted land at night and fell. The caretaker was negligent about the fencing. It's tragic, and it's simple, and simple things stay buried."

"And the Vane money?"

Ambrose had looked at him then, and Cormac had understood, even before a word was spoken, that the question itself was the transaction—that by asking it, cleanly, without trembling, he had already signed.

"The Vane money," Ambrose said, "is how grief becomes quiet. Some people need a lawyer. Some people need a banker. You're going to be a very good banker, Cormac."

He had been. That was the hell of it. Twelve years of quarterly disbursements routed to three families—R.V., C.A., M.P.—and never a missed date, never a flagged transaction, never so much as a raised eyebrow from a regulator. He had been excellent at burying money, which is to say at burying things generally, which is to say at burying the boy a second time, deeper, in figures instead of water.

And now someone had dug up page one and mailed it to him like a bill.

---

At home—a narrow brick house on Larkspur Lane that he'd bought with the first year's payments and never once thought of as bought with anything else—he poured two fingers of the good rye he saved for occasions and drank it standing at the kitchen window, watching the streetlight make its small yellow island on the asphalt. Nobody walked past. Nobody ever walked past Larkspur Lane after dark; it was that kind of street, the kind that repelled witnesses.

He set the glass down and examined the envelope again under the kitchen light. No fingerprints worth lifting, surely, but he checked anyway, tilting it, seeing nothing but his own thumbprints blooming gray in the lamplight. Block capitals. Heavy pressure. The pen had been a felt tip—no, a soft graphite pencil, dragged hard, the letters slightly shivering at their edges, as though the hand that wrote them had been shaking.

That detail lodged in him and would not come loose. Whoever sent it had been afraid. Afraid, and doing it anyway. He knew the sensation intimately; he simply hadn't known it could travel through stationery.

There was a phone number on the back of the ledger page. Just seven digits, no name, written smaller, in the same shivering capitals. He recognized the exchange—local, town proper, probably a payphone given the way the world was going. Below it, a single line:

*Page two names the amounts. Page three names the dates they were paid to keep quiet. Decide by Friday.*

Friday. Of course. Even anonymous blackmailers in Marrowgate kept banking hours.

Cormac laughed—one dry note, no humor in it, the sound a branch makes before it breaks. Then he stopped laughing, because the arithmetic arrived, uninvited, the way arithmetic does.

Page two. Page three. Which meant pages four, five, six. Which meant the whole ledger existed somewhere outside the drawer only he could lock, outside the napkin-sketch structure only he understood, copied entire, waiting. Fifteen years of disbursements. Three families. And threaded through all of it, in the routing notations, initials that any competent investigator would chew on for an afternoon: R.V. C.A. M.P.

M.P. Ambrose Pell, dead two years now, safe in the ground and in the town's fond memory—*the finest estate lawyer the county ever produced*, the plaque at the library said. If the ledger surfaced, the plaque would come down. If the ledger surfaced, Rosalind Vane would have lawyers of her own, better ones, hungry ones, and the first thing they would do was establish who handled the money, and the answer to that was a man on Larkspur Lane with a dying fern and a quarterly fee.

If the ledger surfaced, Tom Bracken's trial—which began Monday, the whole town murmuring about it, the courthouse steps already being sanded against the press—would become something else entirely. Something with more defendants.

Cormac looked at the phone number for a long time. Then he did the thing he was best at, the thing Ambrose had trained him for without either of them naming it: he made the problem boring. He burned the envelope over the sink, ran the tap until the ash went down, wiped the basin dry. He folded the ledger page into eighths and slid it inside the hollowed-out spine of a tax code volume on his study shelf, third row, between two identical volumes, because nothing survives scrutiny like boredom. He washed the rye glass and turned it upside down on the rack, exactly aligned with the others.

Then he sat in the dark study with the phone in his lap, and this was the part he would be ashamed of later, in the small hours, in the years after, if there were years after: he did not call the number, and he did not call a lawyer, and he did not call Rosalind Vane, whose voice on the charity-line last spring had been silk over wire—*Cormac, darling, everything's fine, isn't it? Everything's always fine with us.* He sat in the dark and calculated.

What would it cost to run? He had, conservatively, two hundred and six thousand dollars in liquid accounts, plus the house, plus a fishing camp on Loon Lake titled to Blackwater Holdings. Enough to disappear to, if disappearing were a thing men like him could do. But men like him couldn't. Men like him were signatures; a signature without its document was just ink. They would find him the way he'd found defaulters for thirty years—through the boring, beautiful machinery of records—and the finding would look worse than the staying.

What would it cost to pay? He didn't know yet. That was the design of it, he realized—the sender hadn't named a figure, which meant the figure would be named later, after the fear had compounded. Interest. His whole life had been interest, paid quarterly on a principal he'd never been allowed to see in full.

And what would it cost to talk? To walk into the courthouse Monday, or the state police barracks on Route 9, and put the whole rotten architecture on a table?

He imagined it with a clarity that frightened him more than the envelope had: the courtroom, the gallery full of familiar faces, Ruthie from the bank, Mrs. Hollis, the trout farmer. Xanthe Halloran somewhere near the front, that pale, precise, unreadable man who filed the town's sins so neatly—Halloran, who had witnessed the trust instruments, who had seen the disbursement authorizations with his own careful eyes and signed his name beneath them and never once asked. Halloran, who must know. Who had always known, maybe, the way Cormac had always known, both of them keeping the same terrible books in different formats.

Would Halloran testify? Would Halloran burn too?

Cormac found that he was gripping the armrests of his chair, and that outside, far off toward the river, a dog was barking in the flat, rhythmic way dogs bark at something that will not go away.

Friday. He had until Friday. Two days to decide whether to pay the interest again, or default on twelve years of silence and let the whole note come due at once—his name, his signature, his small clean boring life, called in full, in public, in front of everyone who had ever believed he was decent.

He took the ledger page out of the tax code and read it once more by the desk lamp, and this time he noticed what terror had made him miss: in the bottom margin, faint, almost erased, someone had written a fourth set of initials—not R.V., not C.A., not M.P., but *X.H.*, followed by a question mark, as if the sender wasn't sure.

As if the sender wanted to be sure.

Cormac Aubrey sat very still in his tidy study on his tidy street, holding the page that held his name, and understood at last that whoever was sending these pages wasn't finished—and that the next envelope might not be addressed to him.
