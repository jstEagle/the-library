## 20. The Choice

The night before testimony, Xanthe Halloran did not sleep, and he did not expect to.

He sat in his kitchen at one in the morning with the lights off except for the lamp over the stove, the small yellow circle he had read under for twelve years, and he laid Rosalind Vane's offer out on the table the way he would lay out any document: flat, face-up, examined under good light until it gave up its terms.

She had made it three days ago, standing in his office doorway with her coat still buttoned against the rain, and she had spoken softly, the way people speak in hospitals and churches, as though volume itself might do damage. Partnership at a firm in Hartford. A house already found—she had found it, she said, which meant she had been looking for some time, which meant the offer was not an impulse but an architecture. Protection of a kind she did not name and did not need to name; they both understood that protection, in Marrowgate, was a service rendered by silence to those who could afford it. And the ledger gone. Not destroyed, necessarily. She had even allowed him that dignity. *Filed away,* she'd said. *Somewhere no one will ever look.*

*Which is where I live,* Xanthe had thought. *That's the address you're offering me. Somewhere no one will ever look.*

He had told her he would consider it. He had not lied when he said it—that was the terrible part, the part he kept turning over now like a stone in the palm. He had genuinely considered it, in the way a drowning man considers the water's offer to keep holding him. It was not nothing, what she offered. It was everything he had spent twelve years building, polished and guaranteed, with a view of a different river.

Now, in the dark kitchen, he took stock of that life honestly, because if he was going to refuse it—and some quiet, load-bearing part of him had already refused it, had refused it the moment Bracken said *I wasn't alone* in a courtroom full of people who had spent twelve years pretending not to hear—he wanted to know exactly what he was refusing. He wanted the refusal to be priced correctly.

* * *

What he had built was this: a practice on Fenwick Street above the old pharmacy, two rooms and a waiting area that smelled of paper and lemon oil. Four hundred and eleven closed estate files, alphabetized, cross-referenced, each one a small sealed room in the house of Marrowgate's respectability. A reputation for discretion so complete that people brought him their worst selves the way they brought broken watches to a repairman—not because they expected judgment, but because they trusted the case would close again over the works.

He had thought of it, all these years, as competence. Ambrose had taught him to think of it that way. *A lawyer's first duty is to the document,* Ambrose used to say, tapping a clause with one blunt finger, *and a document's first duty is to hold its shape under pressure.* Xanthe had been the document. He had held his shape under pressure for twelve years, and the pressure had been the whole point, and he had never once asked who was pressing, or why, or on behalf of whom.

There were things in that life worth keeping, he admitted. The morning light through the pharmacy's faded awning, green-gold on his desk. Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery bringing him a roll every Friday without being asked, because his mother had once done the same for her. The particular satisfaction of a well-drafted clause, clean as joinery. These were real. He did not want to pretend they weren't, because pretense was the disease and he was done administering it to himself.

But underneath the light and the rolls and the joinery ran the other thing, the thing he had filed so deep he had nearly lost the reference number: Danny Ferro, fifteen years old, going into Blackwater Quarry on a June night with his shoes on and coming out of it in a body bag with his shoes missing, and a town arranging itself around that absence like water around a dropped stone. Twelve years of files, and Xanthe had curated parts of the record himself. He knew it now. He had known it since the night he pulled the coroner's file and found the gaps shaped exactly like his own hands.

Rosalind's offer was a request to keep curating. That was all. A generous salary for the same work, in a town where the gaps didn't have his fingerprints on them yet.

* * *

At two o'clock he got up and went to the hallway closet where he kept his coat, and he stood there a long moment with his hand on the door, not opening it, because he knew what he was really reaching for. Then he opened it anyway. Honesty, he was learning, was mostly a series of small doors you opened knowing what was behind them.

The bottle was where it always was—a decent rye, bought four years ago for a celebration that never came, kept since then the way one keeps a fire extinguisher. He carried it back to the kitchen, set it on the table beside Rosalind's imagined contract, and looked at the two of them together. The drink was the older offer. The drink had been the original terms: numbness, invisibility, the long anesthesia of a man who has decided that feeling things is a luxury his position doesn't permit.

He poured two fingers, watched the amber catch the lamplight, and then—deliberately, ceremonially, hating himself a little for the theatricality of it and doing it anyway—poured it down the sink. He put the bottle back in the closet. He wasn't making a vow. He was simply declining, one more time, on one more night, to be handled.

Then he went upstairs to the spare room.

* * *

The filing cabinets lived in the spare room, four gray steel monoliths he had bought secondhand from the county courthouse when they digitized, and to any visitor they contained exactly what they appeared to contain: decades of mundane estates, the wills of schoolteachers and farmers, probate notices, tax filings, the sedimentary record of ordinary deaths. This was the joke, or the genius, or the sickness—depending on which hour of the night you asked him—which was that he had hidden the decoded ledger pages among them precisely because no one would ever look. Because looking through another person's probate files was a boredom beyond criminal endurance.

He unlocked the third drawer with the small brass key he wore on the same ring as his office keys, and there they were, thirty-one pages in Edith Vane's ledger hand, interleaved between the estate of Harold Tull (deceased 2009, bequeathed his stamp collection to a nephew who sold it within the month) and the estate of Margaret Osei (deceased 2011, whose cat trust Xanthe had administered faithfully for six years). Fifteen years of payments routed through the Vane estate to three families, dated, initialed, cross-referenced to clause numbers in a will that legally could not exist. The town's respectability, purchased line by line, in Edith's beautiful, merciless cursive.

He had hidden them here after the break-in, after someone came through his office window on a rainy Tuesday and took the original will and left everything else—the computers untouched, the cash box unopened, the theft so surgical it might have been performed by a colleague. They had taken the will because they believed the will was the treasure. They had searched his office because they believed he kept his secrets where he kept his work.

They had never considered the home cabinets. They had underestimated, as everyone always had, how thoroughly a man can disappear into his own diligence.

Xanthe knelt in front of the open drawer, the cold of the floorboards coming up through his knees, and he understood that this was the actual choice—not Rosalind's offer, not the rye, but this: these pages, right here, in this drawer, tonight. What happened to them in the next twelve hours was the whole of his character, distilled. Everything else was preamble.

* * *

He did not burn anything. He wants that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of his own accounting: he stood in his spare room at three in the morning with a metal wastebasket and a book of matches he had taken from the diner years ago, and he considered burning them, truly considered it, held the first page over the basket and felt the match rasp against the striker and stop there, unlit, because burning was just Rosalind's offer conducted by other means. Fire was the town's native language. *They burn things here,* he had written once in his notes, and it was true, and he would not give Marrowgate the satisfaction of fluency.

He did not delete anything either, though deletion was less available to him anyway; most of what mattered existed on paper, in ink, in the stubborn physical world. But there were scans, copies on a drive in his desk, and he sat with the drive in his hand for a while, weighing the small mercy of an accident, a corrupted file, a version of events that could be quietly lost. He put the drive in his breast pocket instead, where it sat over his heart like a small cold coin.

And then he began to make certified copies.

This was, he reflected as he worked, the most lawyerly act of rebellion imaginable, and he almost smiled doing it. Not destruction—certification. Not erasure—attestation. At half past three he drove to the twenty-four-hour copy shop on Route 9, the one that served the community college students and the insurance adjusters, and the kid at the counter barely looked up from his phone as Xanthe fed page after page through the machine: the ledger, Edith's confession from the strongbox, the coroner's file with its curated gaps, Ambrose's letter with its devastating tenderness, the photographs of the quarry taken at midnight with a flashlight and a tripod and hands that would not quite stop shaking. Thirty-one pages became sixty-two became ninety-three. And then, because certification was the point, he drove to the all-night notary at the truck plaza outside town—a bored woman named Dolores with reading glasses on a chain, who stamped and signed and embossed each copy without asking what it was, because in her professional life she had learned that questions were not part of the service.

By five-fifteen, Xanthe Halloran possessed four complete, certified, individually attested sets of the documents that would end Marrowgate's longest silence. One went into his briefcase. One went into a padded mailer addressed to the state attorney general's office, to be posted at the first light from the post office in Corning, two towns over, where no one knew his face. One went to Judge Alvarado's chambers via the court's secure intake, sealed and marked. And the fourth—

The fourth he wrapped in plastic and buried, at dawn, in a coffee can beneath the lilac at the corner of his property, in soil still soft from the week's rain, marking the spot with nothing at all, trusting the location to the same memory that had kept four hundred and eleven estates in perfect alphabetical order. If anything happened to him on the courthouse steps—if the town decided, as towns do, that some silences are structural and must be defended like load-bearing walls—then the fourth set would outlast the attempt. He had learned from Ambrose how to bury things in documents. He had learned from Edith how to bury documents in the ground. Between the two of them, dead and dust, they had taught him everything except how to live with it, and that lesson, he supposed, was the one reserved for the living, and tomorrow he would begin paying tuition in public.

* * *

He came back inside as the sky went the color of pewter, and he made coffee he did not drink, and he sat at the kitchen table while the light came up slow over the rooftops of Marrowgate, the town where he had been invisible for twelve years, the town that had mistaken his silence for virtue and his filing for loyalty. Somewhere down the hill, the courthouse clock struck six. In nine hours he would raise his right hand. In ten, he would say Danny Ferro's name aloud in a room full of people who had spent twelve years not saying it.

His hands were steady. He noticed this with something like surprise, and then with something like peace. For twelve years his hands had shaken only in private—in the dark of his office the night the confession letter came, alone with Edith's posthumous handwriting, filing it and taking it back out, filing it and taking it back out. All that trembling, banked and hidden, and now, on the eve of the costliest day of his life, his hands were quiet. As if some part of him had been waiting twelve years for exactly this appointment and had finally stopped dreading it.

He showered, dressed in the dark suit he kept for funerals and closings, and knotted his tie in the bathroom mirror without avoiding his own eyes, which was new. The man in the mirror looked tired and ordinary and slightly gray at the temples, a middle-aged estate lawyer in a town too small for him, and Xanthe looked back at him steadily and thought: *there you are.*

At seven-forty he picked up his briefcase. It weighed what it had always weighed, more or less—paper is paper—but walking out the door he felt the true mass of it, the weight of fifteen years of payments and one drowned boy and one old woman's late-arriving conscience and one mentor's posthumous love disguised as a trap, all of it certified, all of it witnessed, all of it addressed.

On the front step, the morning air smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. And pinned to his door, square and white against the wood, was an envelope with no stamp and no return address, containing a single sheet of paper and a single line of type:

**THE COURTHOUSE HAS TWO EXITS. USE THE ONE FACING THE RIVER.**

Xanthe read it twice. Then he folded it, put it in his breast pocket next to the drive, over his heart, and walked toward the square—by the long way, past the bakery, past the pharmacy, past the whole sleeping architecture of the town he was about to wake up—wondering, with the calm of a man who has already chosen, whether the note was a threat, or a warning from a friend, or both.
