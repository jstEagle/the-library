## 6. The Archivist's Perfect Memory

Marguerite Vane had a theory about dust, and it was this: dust was honest. It settled where attention failed. It thickened on the boxes nobody opened, thinned on the ones that were handled too often, and it never, ever lied about what a person truly cared about.

Which was why, at nine-forty on a Tuesday morning, she was standing in the reading room of the Marrow Bay Historical Archive with a feather duster in one hand and a perfectly composed expression of mild surprise on her face, watching Petra Eastwick come through the door like a man entering a church he didn't believe in.

He paused just inside, letting his eyes adjust to the amber light — the archive occupied the old Carnegie building, all oak and leaded glass and the particular hush of a place where paper went to be remembered — and Marguerite took the opportunity to study him properly. She'd done her homework, of course. Retired detective. Twenty-two years with the provincial force before the incident she never discussed, then the bookshop on Harrow Lane, which lost money so consistently it had to be a lifestyle choice rather than a business. His aunt had raised him; his aunt had died in March; his aunt had left him a house full of books and no one to argue with about them.

And now here he was, three days after an empty coffin had been opened in front of two hundred of her neighbors, asking to see her by name.

"Dust," he said, nodding at the duster.

"It's load-bearing," Marguerite said. "The whole institution would collapse without it. Can I help you, Mr. Eastwick?"

"Petra."

"Petra." She set the duster down with the care of a woman laying down a sword rather than a tool. "Marguerite Vane. Though I suspect you knew that."

"I read the plaque."

"The plaque is generous. It says *curator*. What it actually means is I'm the only person willing to climb into the crawlspace when the roof leaks." She smiled — warm, self-deprecating, exactly calibrated — and gestured toward the reading room table. "Coffee? I have a machine that produces something technically legal in this province."

"That depends. Is it archival coffee or drinking coffee?"

"There's no difference here. Everything is preserved. Including the coffee, possibly since 2011."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if laughter were a language he'd studied but rarely spoken — and sat where she indicated. Good. People who laughed early were people who could be steered gently. People who refused to laugh required other methods.

She brought two mugs and sat across from him, folding her hands. Up close, Petra Eastwick had the look of a man built out of patience: gray at the temples, a face that did its work quietly, eyes that catalogued the room the way hers catalogued visitors. She recognized the habit because she shared it. He was noting the fire exit, the camera over the stacks, the fact that the reading room had one door.

Let him note it. The room was supposed to have one door.

"So," she said. "The centennial. You've had an eventful week."

"You could say that. A hundred years of local history, and the ground gives back a coat full of rocks."

"A *rotted* coat full of rocks," she corrected gently. "If we're going to be accurate. Accuracy is rather my religion." She sipped her coffee and made a small face of betrayal at it. "You'll forgive me if I say the whole business has been exhausting. Two hundred witnesses, four television crews, and Mayor Pemberton calling me six times a day asking whether we have 'anything in the collection' that might explain it. As if the answer might be in a scrapbook between the 1974 regatta photos and someone's recipe for chowder."

"And does it?"

"Does what?"

"Explain it."

Marguerite let the pause sit for exactly as long as charm allowed — a beat and a half, no more — and then laughed softly. "Mr.— Petra. Coffins from 1924 rot. Wood swells, floods come through that cemetery twice a decade — there was the great storm of '61, you may have read about it — and things shift. Groundwater moves stones. Sometimes it moves everything else too. I'd have expected a detective to be less romantic about it than the television people."

"And yet they gave you the microphone at the ceremony," he said. "Not the mayor. Not the priest. They asked the archivist to say a few words about Aldous Vane."

"Because I'm the one who knows the words." She said it lightly, but she watched it land. "That's not vanity, it's job description. I can tell you what he paid for the first wharf, what he wrote to his brother in 1919, what brand of cigar he preferred and how much he complained about the price. When a town is a hundred years old, somebody has to remember it on purpose, or it gets remembered wrong."

"Somebody," Petra said, "or some family."

There it was. Early, and a little blunt — he was better than his file suggested, or worse, depending on how you looked at it. Marguerite kept her smile at its current setting and tilted her head. "The Vanes have been generous to this town's memory, if that's what you're asking. My great-great-grandfather's papers are the backbone of the collection. Would you like to see them? Most people ask about the ghost stories instead. We have a very good photograph of a light in the sea caves that is almost certainly a fishing boat."

"Maybe later." He reached into his coat — slowly, deliberately, the way men do when they want you to watch them reach into their coat — and produced a small notebook. "I'm helping Constable Reyes with some background. Unofficially. The police are reviewing the 1924 file, and I thought the archive might fill in some texture."

"What kind of texture?"

"Context around the death certificate. Dr. Ellery Marsh signed it on November third, 1924. Died himself on the tenth. That's an unusual week."

"It's an unusual century," Marguerite said pleasantly. "Influenza was still working through the coast towns that year. Half the doctors on this coastline died within months of each other. It's in the parish registers if you'd like to check — I'll pull them for you myself." She rose, already moving, because motion was a wonderful way to end a sentence. "Anything else? The funeral arrangements? The estate inventory? I have the probate abstract somewhere—"

"The letter," Petra said.

Marguerite stopped with her hand on the stack ladder.

She turned back with an expression of polite inquiry, and inside herself, behind the expression, in the room where she kept the actual Marguerite — the one who had been eight years old learning which drawer stayed locked, the one who had recited the family story like a rosary until the story became her spine — she felt the old cold attention settle over her like a coat being buttoned.

"Letter?" she said.

"You mentioned he was a letter-writer. Vane. Correspondence with his brother. I wondered if any of it survived from the autumn of 1924. The last weeks."

"Ah." She resumed climbing the ladder, unhurried, selecting a box from the shelf marked VANE, A. — CORRESPONDENCE, 1919–1923, because the dates mattered and she wanted him to see that they mattered. "Here's the difficulty. The correspondence runs to the spring of 1923 and then stops. Either the brothers quarreled, or the letters were lost, or — most likely, knowing this family — Aldous simply got busy dying." She descended and set the box on the table between them like a small, harmless offering. "Everything after August 1924 is secondhand. Funeral notices, memorial tributes, the odd reminiscence printed years later. If there was a final letter, it isn't here."

"Isn't here," Petra repeated. "Or doesn't survive?"

"In an archive those are often the same thing." She opened the box, lifted out a folder, and laid three letters before him with the tenderness of a nurse arranging pillows. "His hand, if you're interested. Very upright. Accountant's penmanship. He dotted his i's like he was pinning them down."

Petra bent over the letters. Marguerite watched him read — watched the stillness of him, the way his finger hovered above the ink without touching it, a man respecting evidence even in someone else's family papers — and she made her assessment the way she made all her assessments: calmly, completely, and with the door already closing behind it.

He had come about 1924. Not the coffin, not the festival, not the charming mystery of it. He had come about the autumn itself, about the doctor and the letter and whatever else was stirring. Which meant someone had talked, or something had surfaced, or — and this was the possibility she held up to the light and examined from all sides, the way her father had taught her, the way her grandmother had taught her father — he had found something in that house on Harrow Lane.

Aunt Vivienne's house. Aunt Vivienne, who had been the wrong sort of Vane, the sentimental sort, the sort who kept things.

"We should digitize these someday," Marguerite said aloud, conversationally, while he read. "Paper doesn't last forever, whatever we tell school groups. Ink fades. Moisture gets in. Things get smudged."

"Smudged," Petra said, not looking up.

"Handling. Humidity. Grief, sometimes — people clutch letters at funerals and ruin them." She smiled at the back of his head. "Why do you ask about smudging, Petra?"

Now he looked up, and for a moment neither of them performed anything at all, and the reading room was very quiet, quiet enough to hear the tick of the humidity gauge and the sea, far off, doing what the sea always did.

"Just thinking about how much a single word can lose in a hundred years," he said.

"More than a word," Marguerite agreed. "Whole sentences. Whole people." She gathered the letters back into their folder with both hands, gently, reverently, and slid the box closed. "But that's why we keep them. Even damaged, they tell the truth eventually. That's the bargain you make with paper. It outlasts your version of events."

She walked him to the door. At the threshold he paused, and she saw him decide whether to say the thing he'd come to say, and then decide against it — file it away, pocket it, the way he'd pocketed whatever else he was carrying.

"Thank you for the coffee," he said.

"Come back anytime. The archive belongs to everyone." She said it warmly, sincerely, and meant every word except the ones underneath it. "Though fair warning — the more time you spend in here, the more you'll start believing the town remembers everything."

"Doesn't it?"

"No," Marguerite said, smiling. "It just hires me to."

He laughed again, that surprised short laugh, and went out into the gray morning, and she watched him cross the square through the leaded glass until he turned the corner by the bakery and was gone.

* * *

She locked the front door behind him. Turned the sign. Drew the blind.

The archive after hours was a different country — the light went amber to brown, the hush deepened, and Marguerite moved through it along routes her feet had known since childhood, past the county records, past the maritime maps, past the display case where Aldous Vane's portrait gazed out with the painted confidence of a man who had, as far as this town knew, died honorably in his bed with a fever and a fortune.

At the back of the curatorial office stood a cabinet of map drawers, oak, older than the building's plumbing. The bottom drawer held the 1890s survey plats, which nobody had requested since the Bicentennial Committee's brief and regrettable flirtation with historical property lines.

Marguerite unlocked it with the small brass key that lived on the same ring as her house keys, disguised among them the way a certain kind of truth lives among other truths — unremarkable, unremarked upon, carried daily.

Inside, beneath the plats, in a shallow tray lined with cloth gone soft as skin, lay the things that were not in the catalogue.

Her grandmother's locket, holding a curl of hair that predated the town. A ledger, half-burned, its surviving pages recording sums paid in 1924 to three names, two legible. And a letter.

She took the letter out and carried it to the window, though the light was poor, though she knew every line of it the way she knew her own signature. Some things you read again not for information but for communion.

The paper had gone the color of weak tea. The handwriting was upright, accountant-neat, each i pinned down like it might otherwise escape.

*I am not dead,* it began. *I am sorry for the trouble this will cause whoever finds it, but I find I cannot bear to leave the record entirely in the hands of liars.*

She had first read it at eleven years old, kneeling on this same floor while her grandfather unlocked the drawer and said, *This is the family, Marguerite. This is what we are.* She had thought then that it was a treasure. She had been right, in the way children are right about monsters under beds — correct in substance, wrong in shape.

*I am not dead, and three people helped me vanish. One name is smudged beyond saving, and perhaps that is God's mercy. The doctor wrote what I paid him to write and was paid again, afterward, in the coin that ends all further transactions. The partner's widow believes what the town believes, and grief makes a fine accomplice. And the third — well. The third keeps this letter, and will keep the others, and their children will keep them, because a secret kept by one man dies with him, but a secret kept by a family becomes a legacy.*

*Do not look for me. Do not mourn me wrongly. And if you are reading this because the ground has given me back — then someone has broken faith, and you will know what must be done, because you are reading this, which means you are one of us, and we have always known what must be done.*

Marguerite folded the letter along its hundred-year-old creases and held it a moment against her sternum, the way her grandmother had taught her, the way you hold a thing that is both inheritance and instruction.

Outside, the fog was coming in off the water, softening the square, erasing the bakery, the bookshop's distant sign, the whole honest visible town. Somewhere out there, Petra Eastwick was carrying questions home to a shop full of books, and somewhere in that shop — she was nearly sure, but only nearly, and *nearly* was a condition her family had never once survived complacently — nothing dangerous remained.

Nearly sure wasn't sure.

She returned the letter to the tray, locked the drawer, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand flat on the oak, listening to the building breathe.

Then she took out her phone and dialed a number she had never had occasion to use, and when it answered, she said only:

"He came asking about the autumn. Yes. About the doctor." A pause. "No. Not yet. But I think it's time we checked on Harrow Lane."
