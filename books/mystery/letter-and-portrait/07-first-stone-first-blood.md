## 7. First Stone, First Blood

The call came at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, which was the kind of hour that made Imogen Reyes feel like the world had broken a promise to her personally.

"Quarry," said Dispatch, in the flat voice of someone reading off a screen. "Flooding's gone down enough for divers. Fisherman spotted something."

She was out of bed before her brain finished assembling the word *quarry*. Marrow Bay had exactly one quarry, flooded since 1938 and beloved of teenagers with more courage than sense, and nothing good ever floated up from it. She dressed in the dark, put coffee in herself rather than waiting for it to be ready — a mistake she'd regret by eight — and drove through streets still blue with dawn, past bunting already strung for the centennial festival, cheerful red-and-white pennants that suddenly looked like something else entirely.

The quarry sat a mile inland, a great drowned bowl ringed with rusted fencing and warning signs so old they'd become part of the scenery. Two patrol cars were parked nose-in at the gate. A fisherman in oilskins stood talking to Officer Brandt, gesturing with his thermos as though the story required props.

Imogen showed her badge to nobody in particular and walked to the edge.

The water below was the color of old bottle glass, calm now that the spring rains had stopped feeding it. A dive team was already in, their orange floats bobbing. And on the near shelf of rock, where the waterline had dropped, lay a body.

He'd been arranged.

That was the first thing, the thing that lodged under her skin and stayed there all day: not sprawled, not tangled in weed the way a drowning victim should be after days in the water, but *arranged* — laid on his back on the flat stone shelf, arms at his sides, coat buttoned, boots together. Someone had placed him there like a man settling into bed. The water had covered him when it rose; the fisherman had only seen him because the level had fallen again last week.

"That's Hollis," said Brandt beside her, and his voice did something complicated. "Ernie Hollis. Retired gravedigger. He helped us with the Vane exhumation prep — surveyed the plot, checked the coffin shell before the lift."

Imogen looked down at Ernie Hollis's face, pale and peaceful and wrong, and felt the morning tilt.

*He worked the centennial preparations.*

*He would have handled the coffin.*

*He would have known it was empty.*

* * *

By nine the quarry rim had sprouted the usual constellation: coroner's van, techs in white suits, a drone buzzing overhead like an impatient fly, and a growing crowd of townsfolk held back by tape they kept leaning over as if the tape were merely decorative. Marrow Bay loved its dead the way some towns love their football clubs — devotedly, loudly, and with strong opinions about management.

Petra Eastwick arrived at 9:20, walking from town because of course he walked, his coat collar up against a wind that hadn't committed to being cold yet. Chief Danvers had signed the consultation paperwork Friday — quietly, without ceremony, the way you sign for a package you're slightly ashamed of ordering — and Petra had spent the weekend in the 1924 files with the air of a man relearning a language he'd sworn he'd forgotten.

He stopped at the tape. Looked down at the shelf of rock. And Imogen, watching him instead of the body — she was learning to do that, watching Petra watch things — saw his face go very still.

Not shocked. Not horrified. *Recognizing.*

"Oh no," he said softly. It wasn't grief yet. It was arithmetic.

"You see it too?" she asked, lifting the tape for him.

"The position." He crouched at the edge, hands in his pockets, studying the body from above with the particular respect he always paid the dead — never looming, never casual. "On his back. Arms at his sides. Face turned just slightly toward the water." He paused. "Boots together?"

"Boots together," confirmed the coroner's assistant, who had heard this exchange three times already and was starting to look rattled by it.

"Coat buttoned?"

"All buttons."

Petra stood. For a moment he looked older than his years, and then, paradoxically, much younger — the way he did when the machinery behind his eyes started turning fast. "Get me the 1924 file," he said. "Page forty-one. The Corven drowning."

"There was a drowning in '24?" Imogen asked, falling into step beside him as he headed for the cars.

"One. Thomas Corven, quarry laborer, found in this exact quarry in June of 1924, two weeks after Aldous Vane's funeral." He said it evenly, but Imogen caught the thread of tension underneath, a wire pulled taut. "Ruled accidental. Drunk, slipped, fell in — the file has a witness statement from a man who claims he saw Corven drinking at the Anchor and stumbling toward the quarry path. Case closed in four days." His jaw tightened. "Four days, Imogen. For a man found posed like a sleeping child on a rock shelf."

"You think it wasn't an accident even then."

"I think," Petra said, "that I've read that file until I could recite it, and what's lying down there is page forty-one wearing a modern coat." He stopped walking. Turned to face her fully, and his eyes had the flat bright quality she was coming to dread, the look of a man seeing a pattern complete itself. "Hollis worked the exhumation. He opened the coffin shell. He *knew*, or he saw something, or he mentioned something to someone. And now he's been killed and staged to match a ninety-nine-year-old drowning that everyone in this town has forgotten except—" he gestured vaguely at his own head, "—me, apparently, and whoever did this."

The wind came up the quarry bowl and stirred the tape. Somewhere above them the drone wheeled, recording everything and explaining nothing.

"So it's not random," Imogen said. "It's not even really about hiding anything. It's—"

"It's a performance," Petra said. "Someone is performing the file."

* * *

They set up in the station's back room, the one with the bad fluorescent light and the long table nobody used since the poker game got banned. Petra laid the 1924 file open beside the fresh incident report, and Imogen watched him work, and understood, finally, why Constable Okafor in Records spoke about Petra Eastwick the way other people spoke about retired athletes.

He read with his whole body. He leaned into the pages. When he found something his hand would rise halfway and hover, as if touching the paper might disturb the evidence of his own attention.

"Here." He turned the old file toward her. A photograph, grainy, silvered at the edges: a rock shelf, a pale shape, men in hats standing at the rim looking down. "Corven. Same shelf — you can tell by the split in the rock, it's still there, third ledge from the north rim. Same posture. Coat buttoned to the throat, which the coroner noted as 'unusual but consistent with the deceased's habits.'" Petra tapped the note. "Convenient, isn't it? Every odd detail pre-explained. Whoever staged the original knew the coroner would want reasons, so the reasons were planted in advance."

"You're saying the same person staged both."

"I'm saying the same *script* staged both, ninety-nine years apart." He pulled the fresh report over. "Hollis was last seen Saturday evening, alive, buying pipe tobacco at Merrow's. The medical examiner puts death between Saturday night and Sunday morning. Cause of death—" he glanced at the ME's preliminary, and his mouth went thin, "—drowning, post-mortem immersion complicating time of death, et cetera. But here's the thing. Hollis was seventy-three years old with a hip replacement. He didn't walk a mile inland to the quarry at night. Somebody brought him. In a vehicle, most likely, down the old maintenance track on the east side that doesn't show from the road."

"You've been out there."

"I've been everywhere," Petra said, without apology. "There are tire marks. Fresh ones, Sunday-morning rain took the edges off but the tread pattern's readable. Someone will match them eventually." He rubbed his eyes. "Do you know what bothers me most?"

"The staging," Imogen said. "That it's not concealment. He wanted Hollis found."

"No — well, yes, that too, and it's ugly, because it means the point isn't getting away with murder. The point is the *telling*." Petra leaned back, chair creaking. "What bothers me is the neatness. Corven's drowning closed in four days because it was tidy. No loose threads, no awkward questions, one cooperative witness. And now Hollis — tidy again. Found by a fisherman, in a spot with a hundred-year precedent, in a town so busy rehearsing its own anniversary that nobody looks twice at a dead man who matches history." He shook his head slowly. "This killer isn't improvising. He's working from a text. And I've read the text, which means I should be able to read *him*."

"And can you?"

Petra was quiet for a moment. Outside, someone laughed — a normal sound, festival-season laughter, and it seemed to land strangely in the room, like music playing during a fire.

"I can tell you what comes next," he said finally. "If the pattern holds. If he keeps going in order."

"What comes next in the file?"

He didn't answer right away. He looked at the 1924 file the way you might look at a snake you'd picked up believing it was a stick.

"A fever," he said. "In August of 1924, Vane's business partner, Silas Merrick, died of what the certificate calls 'gastric fever.' Very sudden. Very convenient — Merrick held half the Vane shipping fortune and his death consolidated the estate." Petra's voice had gone quiet and careful. "There's a letter in the file from Merrick's sister asking why no doctor attended him in his final week. There's no answer in the file. There's no follow-up at all."

"You think it was poison."

"I think arsenic was called 'inheritance powder' for a reason, and I think if our performer follows the script, somebody connected to the Vane story dies of a sudden gastric illness before the festival banquet." He met her eyes. "And I need you to hear how insane that sentence sounds, Imogen, because I'm going to have to say it to Danvers tomorrow, and I'd like one person in this building to believe me first."

Imogen looked at the two files side by side — the yellowed page and the white one, the hat-men and the white suits, the same rock shelf holding the same terrible pose across a century.

"I believe you," she said. And discovered, saying it, that she meant it all the way down, past professionalism, past admiration, into whatever stubborn place had decided somewhere along the way that this difficult, deflecting, brilliant old man was hers to stand beside. "Which frightens me more than the quarry does, honestly."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face and vanished. "Good," he said. "Hold onto that. You'll need it."

* * *

She went to see the widow that afternoon, because somebody had to, and because Petra had gone quiet in the way he did when he was thinking too hard to be useful to anyone.

Edith Hollis received her in a small front room smelling of lavender and toast, a woman of seventy with her husband's photograph on the mantel and her hands folded very still in her lap, the stillness of someone holding something shut.

"He was proud of that job," Mrs. Hollis said. "The centennial. Said it was the biggest thing he'd done since he buried half the town in '61. Surveying the founder's plot — he talked about it for weeks." Her voice wavered and steadied. "Then about ten days ago he went quiet about it. I asked him what was wrong and he said, 'Nothing, Edie,' which is what he says when something's wrong."

"Did he say anything else? Anything at all, even small?"

Mrs. Hollis was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped, as if the walls of Marrow Bay had ears with long memories.

"He said the coffin was light," she said. "He said — Ernie lifted things his whole life, he knew weight like you know your own name. And he said the founder's coffin was too light, and he told someone, and he shouldn't have told someone, and now—" She stopped. Her folded hands tightened until the knuckles whitened. "Now he's in the quarry. And I keep thinking, whoever put him there *buttoned his coat*. Who does that? What kind of person takes the trouble?"

Imogen had no answer that wouldn't be monstrous, so she gave none. At the door, Mrs. Hollis caught her sleeve.

"Constable. Whoever you're looking for — they'll be someone who loves this town's stories too much. That's all I know. People who love stories too much stop being able to tell them from the truth."

* * *

Evening found Imogen back at the shop, because it was becoming a habit and she'd stopped pretending otherwise. The lamp was lit in the window; the CLOSED sign hung at its habitual forty-five degrees, meaning *closed to strangers, open to the persistent.* Inside, Petra sat amid a fortress of open books and photocopies, tea gone cold at his elbow, looking like a general whose map had begun to move on its own.

"Edith Hollis says he told someone the coffin was light," Imogen reported, dropping into the chair across from him. "Ten days ago. Then went quiet."

Petra absorbed this without surprise, which frightened her more than surprise would have. "So we know when the clock started. He noticed, he told the wrong person, and the file gained a new entry." He pushed a photocopy across the table — the 1924 list of deaths, five names in his cramped handwriting, each annotated. "Look at it as a program, Imogen. Like a playbill. Corven, the drowning — performed Tuesday. Next on the bill, Merrick, the poisoning. And after that—" his finger stopped at the final name, and Imogen watched his hand go very slightly unsteady, "—the return of the founder himself. The closing ceremony. They're planning to rebury that empty coffin in front of the whole town, and if he follows the script to the end, that ceremony is the finale."

"You think he'd do something at the reburial. In front of two hundred people."

"I think he's been performing for an audience of none so far," Petra said. "Men like this don't stay in rehearsal forever."

The lamp hummed. Rain had started against the window, soft and indifferent. Imogen thought of Edith Hollis's buttoned coat, of the pennants strung across Fore Street, of Marguerite Vane's serene face at the archive, and felt the dread in her chest harden into something with edges — not fear anymore, but certainty, which is fear's less useful but more determined cousin.

"Then we'd better learn the rest of the script before he does," she said.

Petra looked up at her, and for once didn't deflect, didn't joke, didn't retreat behind the counter of his irony. "There's one thing I haven't told Danvers," he said quietly. "One thing I've been sitting on since the quarry."

Imogen waited.

"The Corven drowning in 1924 — the file lists a witness. The man who claimed he saw Corven drunk that night, the statement that closed the case in four days." Petra turned the old page toward her, and his finger rested beneath a signature faded to brown. "I pulled the name this afternoon. Cross-referenced it." He looked at her, and the lamplight made his eyes very dark. "The witness was a gravedigger too, Imogen. And his surname was Vane."
