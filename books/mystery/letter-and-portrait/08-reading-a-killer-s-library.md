## 8. Reading a Killer's Library

The bookshop at three in the morning had a different voice than the bookshop at noon. At noon it creaked and settled and muttered to itself like an old man dozing in a chair. At three it was silent in the way that made silence feel less like absence and more like attention — as though the building were leaning in, waiting to see what Petra Eastwick would do next.

What he did was spread the 1924 case file across the back table, which was really four tables pushed together and still not enough, and begin — with the patience of a man who had once spent eleven months reading nothing but harbor manifests — to read a killer's library.

He had borrowed the file legitimately. Mostly. Chief Danvers had signed it out to him with the weary air of a man handing a lit match to someone who insisted they knew what they were doing, and Petra had signed the ledger, and if he had then carried it home instead of leaving it in the evidence room, well. The ledger said what it said. He'd worry about that later, or more accurately, Imogen would worry about it for him, loudly, at some future date he could already hear.

He made tea anyway. It was that kind of night. The kettle's whistle sounded obscene in the quiet, and he shushed it out of habit, which was ridiculous, and he knew it was ridiculous, and he did it again when he poured the second cup.

The second cup was for his aunt. Not her ghost — he didn't hold with ghosts, professionally speaking; ghosts were just unfinished paperwork — but for the version of her that had sat at this same table doing the Times crossword in pen, because she believed erasing your mistakes was how people learned to lie to themselves. He missed her the way you miss a load-bearing wall. You don't notice it every day. You notice it when something shakes.

"All right," he told the empty chair. "Let's see what we're reading."

* * *

Here is what Petra knew by dawn, laid out on the table like a hand of cards he'd been dealt face-up:

Item one. Elias Grange, retired gravedigger, sixty-eight years old, found drowned in the flooded quarry on the ninth of October. The file contained a nearly identical incident: Thomas Wren, quarry laborer, drowned in the same quarry on the ninth of October, 1924. Ruled accidental. Both men had been found face-down. Both had water in lungs consistent with drowning. Both had been last seen alive within a quarter mile of the Vane property line.

But here was the detail that had kept Petra up past the tea: Thomas Wren, according to the coroner's note in the margin — a cramped, impatient scrawl — *had been seen earlier that evening arguing with persons unknown near the east gate.* And Elias Grange, according to the statement Imogen had taken from his neighbor, had spent his last evening asking questions. Specifically, questions about the centennial exhumation. About who had ordered the coffin opened. About whether anyone had ever actually looked inside it before.

A gravedigger, asking whether anyone had ever looked inside a coffin. That wasn't curiosity. That was a man who had noticed something wrong with his work and couldn't leave it alone.

Item two. The staging was too good. This was the thing that nagged at Petra the way a misprinted page nags at a proofreader. A drowning is easy to fake, granted — the sea does most of the work in this town. But the *details*. The date matching to the day. The location matching exactly. The argument-before-death matching. Whoever had killed Elias Grange hadn't just read the 1924 file. They had *studied* it. They had underlined it. They had, in the manner of a certain kind of reader Petra both loved and feared, taken it personally.

There were readers who read books, and there were readers who read books as instructions. Petra had met both kinds. The second kind worried him.

Item three. The file had a rhythm. This was the part he'd only seen at two in the morning, sitting back with his cold tea, looking at the whole spread of paper rather than any single page. The 1924 file wasn't one story. It was several stories braided together, and the braid had a pattern:

First, the disappearance of Aldous Vane himself — the coffin, the funeral, the famous grief of the widow. Then, nine days later, the drowning of Thomas Wren. Then, twenty-two days after that, the death of Cornelius Ashford, Vane's business partner, of what the certificate called "bilious fever, complicated by exhaustion." Then a long silence. Then, in December, the quiet closing of the file, signed off by a police chief whose signature deteriorated over the final pages from confident loops to a tired scratch, like a man writing his way downhill.

Three events. A founder, a laborer, a partner. If the pattern held —

Petra set down his pen and looked at the wall calendar hanging crooked above the till, the one with photographs of lighthouses that nobody had turned since August.

Elias Grange had been the laborer. Which meant the next page in somebody's script was the business partner.

And the current mayor's chief rival — the man who'd spent six months publicly questioning the cost of the centennial festival, who'd called the Vane exhumation "a vanity project for dead men" in the *Marrow Bay Gazette*, who was scheduled to speak at the festival banquet in five days' time — was named Ambrose Fennick. Owner of the cannery. Businessman. Partner, in a loose civic sense, to every founding family in town.

It fit. God help him, it fit like a key.

* * *

By seven he had written it all out — three pages, cross-referenced, dated, with the relevant file passages copied out in his neat bookseller's hand. He'd learned long ago that a prediction is only as persuasive as its presentation. Nobody believes a hunch. Everybody believes a document.

He was on his second pot of tea and first regret — the regret being that he'd promised himself he'd never do this again, the standing-in-a-police-station-telling-people-the-future thing, because the last time had ended with a commendation and a cardiologist — when Constable Imogen Reyes came through the front door of the shop at eight sharp, two coffees in hand, wearing the expression of a woman who had already lost an argument before arriving.

"You look terrible," she said, setting a coffee on the table beside the corpse of the file.

"Thank you. I've been cultivating this look since 1987."

"No, I mean it. When did you sleep?"

"I slept." He had not slept. There had been a period between four and five where he'd rested his eyes against his own hand, but he felt that fell under a different category.

Imogen pulled up a chair, read the three pages upside down — she'd gotten fast at that, working with him — and went very still in the way she did when her face needed a moment to catch up to her brain.

"This is a prediction," she said. "You're predicting a murder."

"I'm predicting a *staging*. There's a difference, and the difference matters. A murderer kills whoever's inconvenient. A performer kills whoever fits the page. Fennick isn't inconvenient to anyone — he's loud and annoying and half the town agrees with him. But he's *scheduled* to be at that banquet, and the banquet is twenty-two days after the Grange drowning, and twenty-two days is what the file says."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm a bookseller who wasted a morning, and I'll apologize to Mr. Fennick personally and buy him dinner at the Lobster Pot, which will cost me more than the apology."

"And if you're right?"

Petra turned his coffee cup a slow quarter-turn on the table. "Then we'll have known in advance and done nothing, and that's a sentence I'd like very much never to hear spoken aloud about us."

Imogen stood. "Chief Danvers is in at nine. Come with me."

"Constable—"

"Don't 'Constable' me. You wrote it down. Documents get meetings. Hunches get ignored. Your rule."

His rule. She'd been keeping his rules better than he did. He gathered his pages and followed her out into the bright, indifferent morning, and behind them the shop door swung shut on the smell of old paper and cold tea, and if the building had an opinion about where they were going, it kept it to itself.

* * *

Chief Danvers listened.

That was the remarkable thing, and Petra noted it even in his frustration: the chief actually listened, elbows on the desk, reading all three pages without interruption, which put him ahead of every superior officer Petra had ever briefed in thirty years of this work. It was the response that undid him.

Danvers set the pages down. Aligned their edges. Looked at Petra over his glasses with the expression of a kind man about to say an unkind thing.

"Mr. Eastwick. Do you know what I can arrest Ambrose Fennick for?"

"Nothing yet. That's the point—"

"Do you know what I can do to protect him?"

"Put a man on him. Quietly. Tell him he may be a target—"

"Tell *Ambrose Fennick* he might be murdered?" Danvers almost smiled. "Fennick would be on the six o'clock news by lunch declaring the festival a conspiracy, and by dinner he'd have announced his candidacy for my job. Assuming he believed me, which he wouldn't, because the premise is that a hundred-year-old police file is being used as a *murder script*."

"The Grange drowning matched the file on six points. Six. Date, location, cause, posture, prior argument, proximity to the Vane property. The odds of coincidence are—"

"The odds are that a man drowned in a quarry in a town full of quarries." Danvers took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I read the same file you did. I see a sad old case and a sadder new one. You see a pattern. Maybe you're right. But I can't staff a protection detail on a maybe, and I can't go to the council and tell them the centennial is cursed based on numerology."

"It's not numerology. It's reading. Somebody in this town has read this file the way you read a warrant, and they're working through it page by page, and the next page is a poisoning at a public banquet, and I am telling you the date, the place, and the method in advance, which is more than any killer in my career ever volunteered."

Silence. Outside the window, the flag over the fire station snapped in the wind, cheerful and oblivious.

"Here's what I can do," Danvers said finally. "I can make sure there's a medic on standby at the banquet. I can ask the kitchen to keep the food service logs. And I can promise you that if anything happens to Fennick, the first person I call is you." He slid the pages back across the desk. "I'm sorry. I know that's not what you came for."

It wasn't. It was worse than nothing, actually, because it was reasonable, and reasonable was so much harder to fight than stupid.

In the corridor outside, Imogen waited, and read his face in one glance. "No."

"He'll have a medic on standby."

"A *medic*."

"After the fact, constable, a medic and a detective are nearly the same profession. We differ mainly in scheduling."

She didn't laugh. She looked at the pages in his hand, at the underlined date, at the word *arsenic* he'd written in the margin with a certainty he could feel radiating off the ink like heat. "So we do it ourselves," she said quietly. "We watch the banquet. Off duty. As guests. You still have your tickets."

Petra looked at her — this young woman who kept choosing, over and over, to stand in the exact spot where trouble would find her, simply because he happened to be standing there too — and felt the familiar war inside him lose another battle.

"Tickets are forty dollars," he said.

"I'll buy yours. Call it mentorship."

* * *

That night he locked the file in the shop's safe, beneath the rare first editions, and stood a moment in the dark with his hand flat on the cold metal, thinking about readers.

Somewhere in Marrow Bay was a person who had spent months — years, maybe — living inside a hundred-year-old document, learning its sentences, rehearsing its scenes. And somewhere in that same town, in five days, a loud, unpleasant, entirely innocent man would stand up at a banquet and raise a glass, unless two people with forty dollars' worth of tickets got there first.

Petra turned the sign on the door to CLOSED, and thought: *the fever killed Ashford in the file. But fevers don't arrive on schedule.*

Whatever was coming to that banquet would come in a glass, or on a plate, or in a handshake — and it would come dressed as hospitality.

He just hoped he'd recognize it in time.
