## 15. Confession at Gunpoint

The shotgun did not waver.

Marguerite Vane had held many things steady in her life — teacups during awkward bequests, her chin during the mayor's interminable centennial speech, the family name through three generations of gossip — and she held the gun the way she held all of them: as though it were a duty she had rehearsed until rehearsal became grace.

"Mr. Eastwick," she said pleasantly, "you are standing on Aldous Vane's sternum."

Petra looked down. In the lamplight of the sealed room beneath the museum floor, the second coffin lay open, its occupant a century past caring what happened to his bones. Petra stepped aside with the careful courtesy of a man who had spent forty years apologizing to crime scenes.

"Forgive me," he said. "I wasn't raised to walk on founders."

"No," Marguerite agreed. "You weren't raised here at all. That has always been your tragedy, I think." She gestured with the barrel toward the far wall, where Imogen Reyes stood very still beside a shelf of forged death certificates, her hand nowhere near her holster because they both understood arithmetic: one shotgun, two intruders, no witnesses expected before morning. "Constable. You may sit on that crate. It's been in my family since 1890 and it has borne worse than you."

Imogen sat. She looked, Marguerite thought, like a young woman doing rapid and unhappy mathematics behind her eyes, which was to her credit. Most people who found this room never got as far as mathematics.

"Now," Marguerite said, and set the stock of the shotgun against her hip the way another woman might set down an umbrella indoors. "I imagine you have questions."

"You could put the gun down," Imogen offered.

"I could," Marguerite said warmly, "and then we would all pretend this is a conversation between equals, when it is in fact a conversation between a keeper and two trespassers. The gun simply makes the taxonomy honest. Ask your questions anyway. I find confession clarifies the mind. My grandmother swore by it, though she confined hers to the church, where nobody listened."

* * *

Petra lowered himself onto an upturned bucket with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and Marguerite felt something almost like affection stir in her. He was not what she had expected. She had imagined the retired detective as a bulldog — all jowl and stubbornness — and instead he was a long, dry, tired man who looked at her coffin-room the way a scholar looks at a rare edition: appalled, perhaps, but taking notes.

"The letter," he said. "My aunt's letter. Vane wrote more than one."

"He wrote eleven." Marguerite said it the way she might report the museum's attendance figures. "One for each person who helped him vanish, one for my great-grandmother, who did not help but guessed, and one extra — the one your aunt had — addressed to *whoever digs me up*. He had a sense of humor about it, near the end. Grim, but present."

"And the others?"

"Burned, mostly. Kept, a few. I keep everything." She let that sit. "That is rather the point of me."

"Your ancestor's name was in my aunt's letter," Petra said. "Not smudged. Clear as print. Edmund Vane, the brother. So the family knew from the first hour."

"From the first minute," Marguerite corrected. "Edmund drove the cart. There were three helpers — the doctor who signed the certificate, the gravedigger who filled the coffin with stones, and Edmund. My great-great-grandfather. He was nineteen years old and terrified, and by the time he died he was the most respected man in Marrow Bay, because respectability is simply fear that has learned to dress itself."

She heard her own voice and was briefly surprised by it — how easily the story came, after all these years of carrying it unspoken. Like a trunk carried up too many stairs; once you set it down, you wondered why you'd gripped so hard.

"Tell me about 1924," Petra said quietly. "The murder charge."

And there it was. Not *tell me why the coffin was empty* — any fool would ask that — but the question underneath, the one he had clearly been circling for weeks. Marguerite regarded him across the lamplight and decided, with the strange calm of a woman stepping off a ledge, to answer.

"Aldous Vane killed a man named Tobias Finch," she said. "His business partner. Over money, over a woman, over pride — the usual trinity. It was a bad killing, clumsy, done with a boat hook in the harbor warehouse, and there was a witness. Aldous had two choices: the gallows, or disappearance. He chose the more theatrical option, being Aldous. A 'fever' took him — the doctor's pen made it official — and the town buried a box of harbor stones and wept beautifully, and Tobias Finch's murder went unsolved forever, because the only suspect was dead."

"But the witness," Imogen said from her crate. "The one who saw the killing."

Marguerite smiled at her. Clever girl. She had known Constable Reyes was clever; it was one of the things she had liked about her, back when liking people was still a thing she permitted herself.

"The witness drowned," Marguerite said. "In the quarry. June of 1924. Ruled accidental. The file calls it that — *accidental* — in a clerk's tidy hand. And in September, a fisherman who'd mentioned seeing Aldous alive on the north shore also drowned, also accidentally. And in 1931, a schoolteacher who'd found one of the letters. Accidentally. There is a great deal of accident in my family's history, Mr. Eastwick. We are, you might say, accident-prone by profession."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, through the floorboards, the museum clock struck eleven, and Marguerite counted the strokes out of habit, the way her mother had taught her — *count everything, Marguerite; what is counted cannot lie.*

"My grandmother kept the room," she went on. "And her father before her, and his father before him. Each generation learns the same lesson: the secret is not a burden. It is an inheritance. The Vane fortune — the shipping money, the land, half the waterfront your charming festival is built on — rests on the fact that Aldous Vane is a founder and a hero and not a fugitive murderer rotting under our floorboards. Take away the myth and the wills get contested, the land grants get questioned, the whole beautiful edifice comes down. So we keep it. We curate it. I am merely the current curator."

"With a body count," Imogen said. Her voice shook slightly, and Marguerite gave her credit for continuing anyway. "Hollis, the gravedigger. Councilman Ashe. Two people, Marguerite. Staged like the file. Like a — a performance."

"Yes," Marguerite said, and did not look away. "I staged them. Hollis was going to talk — he'd found the stones when he prepared the grave, and he'd found the seam in the ledger, and he'd begun asking questions with that awful cheerful way of his, over pints, to anyone who'd listen. Ashe was blackmailing me. Badly. He quoted the file wrong, which I found unforgivable on several levels." She paused. "I want to be honest with you both, since honesty is apparently the evening's theme: I did not enjoy it. I am told one ought to say that, and in my case it happens to be true. But I would do it again. That is the part I need you to understand. Not the grief of it — the certainty. Four generations of women in my family have stood exactly where I am standing, holding exactly this weight, and every one of them chose the family. I am not a monster. I am a daughter. There is a difference, and the difference is that monsters act alone."

Petra had gone very still on his bucket. "And now you're deciding what to do with us," he said. "That's the next item on the agenda, isn't it? You've read enough files to know how evenings like this end."

"I've *written* how evenings like this end," Marguerite said gently. "But yes."

* * *

She crossed the room — slowly, deliberately, letting the shotgun speak for her — and knelt at the old writing desk against the wall, the one with the false drawer that had fooled three county assessors and one very persistent insurance investigator in 1968. From it she took a document, yellowed and soft as cloth, and laid it on the desk between them.

"This is Aldous's own account," she said. "Written in the cave, the winter after he vanished, when the loneliness had gotten to him. He confesses to Finch's murder in it. Full detail. It is, frankly, the only honest document in this entire building, and I have kept it locked for thirty years because it is also the only thing that could destroy us." She tapped it once with one finger. "Do you know what my mother said when she handed it to me? She was dying, and she pressed it into my hands, and she said: *Now it's yours, darling. Isn't it heavy? I always thought so.*"

Imogen made a small sound — not quite a laugh, not quite anything else.

"And here is what I have learned," Marguerite continued, rising, settling the gun comfortably against her shoulder, "in my turn under this weight. There are only ever two doors. The first door: you join. You become a keeper. You learn the ledgers, the forgeries, the little ceremonies — yes, we have ceremonies, Mr. Eastwick, we light a candle every March for a murderer, and I defy you to find a religion stranger than ours. In exchange you get the truth, the whole of it, and a place in the only real history this town has. The second door—" she inclined her head toward the flooded quarry direction, north and east, with the courtesy of a hostess indicating the garden, "—is the one Hollis and Ashe took. It is not a door I enjoy opening. But it opens."

"And if we refuse?" Imogen asked.

"Then I shall be very sorry," Marguerite said, and meant it, which was the worst part. "I liked you, Constable. You asked good questions at the exhumation. You noticed the coat was buttoned wrong — I saw you notice. People who notice things are so rare, and so inconvenient."

Petra cleared his throat. It was a small sound, dry as the man himself, but it stopped the room.

"One question," he said. "Before I choose a door."

"Ask."

"The body." He nodded at the open coffin, at the bones that had waited a hundred years beneath the floorboards while the town sang hymns over stones. "Why keep it? Burn it, scatter it, sink it in the quarry with the others, and there's nothing left to find. Keeping it is the risk. Every year. Why?"

Marguerite looked at the bones for a long moment. The lamplight moved on them, patient as tide.

"Because he was my grandfather's grandfather," she said finally, "and because a lie without its evidence isn't a legend, it's just a rumor, and rumors die. Because someone in this family, in every generation, has to know exactly what we are, and bones don't let you forget." Her voice did something she hadn't authorized — softened, frayed at one edge. "Because my mother made me sit with that coffin when I was nine years old and understand what our name cost. You don't throw away the receipt, Mr. Eastwick. Not when the bill might come due."

Silence filled the room like water filling a hull. Above them the clock ticked. Below them, technically, they were already below everything.

"So," Marguerite said, straightening, and the warmth came back into her voice like a curtain drawn across a window — practiced, seamless, hiding the room behind it. "It is nearly midnight. The centennial closes Saturday, and there is a great deal still to arrange, and I regret to inform you both that you will not be leaving this room tonight. There are blankets in the cedar chest — my grandmother quilted them, and I'll thank you to treat them with respect. There is water. There is even, God help me, a chamber pot, because the previous keepers were thorough people."

"You're keeping us prisoner," Imogen said, "with quilts."

"I'm keeping you *alive*," Marguerite corrected, "with quilts, until I decide which door you've chosen. It is not the same thing, though I grant the distinction may feel academic from inside the cedar chest's reach." She backed toward the ladder, gun level, and paused with one foot on the bottom rung. "Oh — and Constable? Your radio. Hand it up, please. And Mr. Eastwick, whatever you're reaching for in that coat pocket — and do stop looking innocent, you've been reaching for it since the doctor's certificate — I'll thank you for that as well."

Petra's hand stilled. Slowly, he drew out the folded paper — his aunt's letter, worn soft as cloth now too, twin to the one on the desk — and held it out.

Marguerite took it, and for a moment their fingers were a foot apart and the gun hung between them like a chandelier, and she looked into the old detective's gray face and saw something there that frightened her more than exposure ever had. Not defiance. Not fear.

Patience.

"Good night," she said, and climbed, and drew the trapdoor shut, and shot the bolt home from above — the bolt her great-grandfather had installed, the bolt her mother had oiled, the bolt that was, in its way, the truest heirloom of all.

Through the floorboards, faintly, she heard the constable whisper something to the old man, and the old man chuckle — actually *chuckle*, down there in the dark with her family's bones — and Marguerite Vane stood alone in her museum among the portraits of her liars, and told herself, as four generations had told themselves before her, that everything was still under control.

Then she remembered the transom window above the reading room, cracked open since summer for the breeze, sitting directly over the sealed room's far corner.

And she began, very calmly, to count.
