## 18. Chase Through the Founders' Streets

* * *

Marguerite heard the roar of the crowd before she understood it, the way a swimmer hears thunder before she knows the storm is hers.

She had been standing at the edge of the ceremony dais, hands folded, spine straight, wearing the face she had practiced for forty years — the face of the town's faithful servant, the keeper of its memory. On the stage, Aldous Vane's empty coffin gleamed under fresh varnish, waiting to be lowered into the ground a second time. The mayor was speaking. The brass band had finished its overture. Two hundred folding chairs sat in tidy rows, and every one of them was occupied by someone who believed her.

Then Petra Eastwick had stepped out from behind the police barrier — released, she'd thought, under escort, cowed, finished — and said, in that dry bookseller's voice that carried farther than any microphone: *Ladies and gentlemen, I know where Aldous Vane's real body is. I've touched it. It's been under the museum floor for a hundred years.*

The murmur had started somewhere near the chowder tent and rolled toward her like a tide.

Marguerite did not run. That was the first discipline her grandmother had taught her, before she'd taught her anything else: *A Vane never runs. A Vane is seen walking, unhurried, and is remembered as calm.* So she walked. Down the dais steps. Past Constable Doyle, whose mouth was opening to say something official. Through the crowd, which parted for her the way it always had — *Miss Marguerite, is it true? Miss Marguerite, what's he on about?* — and she gave them the smile, the archive smile, the one that said *the records will clarify everything in due time.*

The records would clarify nothing. The records were the problem. The records were in her satchel.

She had taken them from the hidden room only that morning — the letters, the forged certificates, the ledger of payments her family had made across four generations to doctors and sextons and one very expensive silence — because she had known, the way she always knew, that the end was coming. She had meant to burn them. She had meant to burn them at home, in the fireplace, properly, with kindling, the way documents deserved. Instead she had carried them to the ceremony like a fool carrying her own confession, and now they rode against her hip, heavy as a second heart.

The sea caves. That was the thought that came to her, unbidden, as she turned off Founders' Street and onto the cliff path. *He hid there. Great-great-grandfather. The first of us.* She had walked that path a thousand times in her imagination, tracing the route in the margins of the old letters, and now her feet knew it without her permission, as though a hundred years of family will had been poured into her legs.

Behind her, the murmur became a roar. Someone shouted her name — not *Miss Marguerite* now, just *Marguerite*, the way you shout at a criminal — and she heard the folding chairs going over like dominoes.

She ran.

* * *

Imogen Reyes had spent three years learning to read crowds — festival crowds especially, the shuffling, sun-dazed, fudge-sticky crowds of Marrow Bay — and she had never seen one turn so fast. One moment they were sitting in their folding chairs being bored by the mayor; the next they were a single animal, and the animal was afraid.

"Eastwick, you absolute lunatic," she breathed, shouldering through toward the dais, where Petra stood with the microphone still in his hand, looking like a man who had just jumped off a cliff and was pleasantly surprised to find he could swim.

"Imogen." He handed her the microphone as though returning a borrowed pen. "The museum. She'll go for the museum first — she'll want the documents under the floor — but she won't get ten steps inside. She's too smart to walk into a building the whole town is watching. She'll go for the boat."

"The boat?"

"The Vane family kept a skiff at the old harbor stairs. It's in the 1924 file — Vane's escape plan was a boat from the caves. Marguerite has read that file more times than anyone alive. She'll do what he planned." He was already moving, cutting through the crowd with an economy that made Imogen realize, not for the first time, that under the cardigans and the mildness there had always been a working detective, folded up small and waiting.

"Then we take the cliff path," Imogen said. "It's the only way to the harbor stairs on foot."

"It's the only way *we* know," Petra said. "She knows a second one. Through the Kestrel garden. But the garden gate's been locked since the estate sale, and locks are for people in a hurry." He almost smiled. "We're in a hurry. She's in a *panic*. There's a difference, and it's about four minutes."

They came off Founders' Street onto the cliff path, and the town fell away below them — the bunting, the band, the overturned chairs, the coffin gleaming on its bier like a lie finally caught in daylight — and the sea opened up ahead, gray-green and patient.

Imogen ran. She was twenty-eight and she ran five mornings a week, and she still had to work to keep pace with Petra Eastwick, who was sixty-one and had the cardiovascular enthusiasm of a man being chased by his own conscience.

"The cave," she said, breathing hard. "If she reaches the caves—"

"Then she's in the one place in this town where she's the expert and I'm the amateur." Petra's voice was even, but his eyes had gone sharp and cold, and Imogen understood that the warmth she knew — the tea, the marginalia, the patient way he explained things — was a coat he wore, and underneath it was the man who had once cleared every unsolved case in three counties. "Which is why we don't let her reach it."

Below them, far down the cliff path, a figure in a dove-gray coat moved fast and low, satchel bouncing at her hip.

"There," Imogen said, and put her whistle to her lips.

"Don't." Petra caught her wrist. "A whistle tells her exactly where we are. Right now she thinks the crowd is behind her and the cliffs are empty. Let her keep thinking it." He paused, and something gentler moved through his face. "Imogen. When we catch her — and we will catch her — remember what she is."

"A murderer."

"Yes. Also a great-granddaughter. Also the last person alive who loved Aldous Vane." He released her wrist. "Both things are true. Hold them both, or you'll do something in the next hour you'll regret."

They ran on, down through the gorse and the salt wind, and below them the dove-gray figure ran too, and between the two of them the sea kept its own counsel.

* * *

The Kestrel garden gate was exactly as locked as Marguerite had promised herself it would be, and she went over it anyway, tearing her coat on the latch, landing hard on a bed of dead lavender that smelled like every summer of her childhood. She had picked flowers in this garden at seven years old. Her grandmother had taught her the names of them. *A Vane never runs.* She was running. The thought arrived and she put it away, the way she put away everything inconvenient, into the drawer in her mind that locked.

The second path — the family path, the one that appeared on no map, that her grandmother had walked her down at nine years old saying *memorize this, Marguerite, memorize it the way you memorize everything* — dropped through a fold in the cliff like a secret kept in a book's spine. She took it at a speed that was nearly a fall. Stones skittered ahead of her into the dark.

At the bottom, the old harbor stairs. The skiff.

It was still there. Of course it was still there. She had paid the harbor trust's maintenance fee herself, every year, in the archive budget, coded as *coastal preservation* — because a keeper of secrets keeps everything, keeps the boat and the path and the cave and the lie, keeps them oiled and ready, because you never know which generation will need them. Her grandmother had needed them never. Her mother had needed them never. Marguerite had told herself, for years, that she would be the one who needed them never too.

The skiff sat on its line, bobbing, and beyond it the open water lay flat and silver toward the horizon, toward anywhere, toward a life in which she was not Marguerite Vane but simply a woman in a torn coat with a satchel.

She got the boat into the water. She got one foot over the gunwale. The sea rocked her, gentle as a nurse.

And then she smelled it.

Smoke.

She looked down. The satchel. In her scramble down the cliff path, the satchel had torn too — the same latch, the same stone — and the old papers had shifted, and one corner of the oldest letter, the 1924 letter, the one in Aldous's own hand that she had read so many times she knew the ink blots like freckles on a lover's face, had been resting against the flint striker in the outer pocket. The friction of the fall. The dry century-old paper. A thread of smoke curling up like a question.

Marguerite stood with one foot in the boat that meant freedom and watched her family burn.

She did not decide. That was the thing she would never be able to explain afterward, to the lawyers, to the psychiatrists, to the woman with the kind eyes who took her statement. She did not *decide*. Her body simply turned, the way a compass turns, the way it had always turned, toward the thing she had been keeping. She hauled the satchel against her chest, smothering the flame with her coat, with her hands, feeling the heat bite through the wool, and the skiff drifted out without her, knocking softly against the stairs, and then drifted farther, and then was gone — a small dark shape growing smaller on a large silver sea, carrying nothing, going nowhere, free.

Marguerite Vane stood on the harbor stairs of Marrow Bay, holding a burning satchel of letters to her heart like a child, and wept for the first time in thirty years.

She heard boots on the path above her. Two sets. One light and quick, one slower, deliberate, the gait of a man who had never in his life hurried for anyone and was hurrying now.

She did not run. There was nowhere left that was not the cave, and the cave was only a hundred yards along the shore, and some part of her — the archivist, the keeper, the last loyal daughter — understood with terrible clarity that the cave had always been the destination. The boat had been the fantasy. The letters were the truth. And the truth, her whole life long, had only ever pointed one direction.

She went into the dark with her family in her arms.

* * *

They came down the last stretch of the cliff path at a dead run and found the harbor stairs empty, the sea flat, one small boat far out and unoccupied, and a scatter of scorched paper on the stones like black butterflies.

Imogen's stomach dropped. "The boat—"

"Nobody's in the boat." Petra crouched, touched the scorched paper, held it to the light. A fragment of handwriting. A fragment he recognized. "She was here. She lit something — no. No, it caught by accident, see the tear in the satchel strap, the striker—" He stopped. Imogen watched him assemble it, the whole story, in about four seconds, the way he assembled everything, and watched something like grief move across his face when he finished.

"She had the letters," Petra said quietly. "The real ones. And she was leaving. And they caught fire, and she—" He stood, and looked down the shore, toward the black seam in the cliff where the sea went in and didn't come out. "She went back for them. She had the whole ocean in front of her, Imogen. And she went back for the paper."

"Because they're evidence," Imogen said. "Because she couldn't leave them behind to—"

"Because they're *hers*." Petra was already moving along the shore, fast, the wind pulling at his coat. "A hundred years of her family, in her arms. You and I would call it evidence. She calls it the only thing that ever loved her back." He glanced back at her, and his voice gentled. "Stay behind me. Radio Doyle — tell him the harbor, tell him the cave mouth, tell him nobody comes in after us without light and a rope. And Imogen — she has the shotgun from the museum. We saw it. She left it, or she didn't. Assume she didn't."

Imogen radioed. Doyle's voice crackled back, thin and official and far too late, promising boats and men and procedure. Petra was already at the cave mouth.

It was smaller than Imogen had imagined — a black seam at the base of the cliff, half-drowned at high tide, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and salt and something older, something like a sealed room, like the space under floorboards, like a hundred years of held breath.

"She's inside," Petra said. Not a question.

"You can't know that."

"I can know that." He turned on his torch. "She spent her whole life learning this story. The cave is the last chapter. Marguerite Vane has never once in her life skipped the last chapter." He paused at the threshold, and for a moment, in the torchlight, Imogen saw him not as her mentor, not as the bookseller, not even as the detective, but as something older — a man standing at the mouth of a story that had been waiting a century for him.

"Eastwick," she said. "Whatever's in there. We do it together."

"Together," he agreed. And then, almost smiling, in the voice she'd first heard across a bookshop counter, the voice that had made her want, more than anything in her career, to be the kind of person who got to stand next to him: "Mind the low ceiling. The founder of this town was apparently a short man with excellent taste in hiding places."

They went in.

The dark took them gently, and somewhere ahead of them, deeper in, a torch flickered — and with it, faintly, the sound of paper being smoothed flat, page by page, by hands that would not let go.
