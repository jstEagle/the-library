## 21. Reburial

The morning of the reburial, Petra Eastwick stood in front of his closet for eleven full minutes, which was ten minutes longer than he had spent deciding anything since his retirement, including the time he'd been asked to identify a body.

The problem was the tie. His aunt Edith had left him exactly one good tie, burgundy with a pattern of tiny anchors that she'd bought for his father's funeral and never mentioned again. It hung there now like a small accusation. He put it on, looked at himself in the mirror — a lean, gray man with a face that had spent sixty years learning not to give anything away — and decided he looked like a detective pretending to be a townsman.

Which, he supposed, was what he was. Except that lately the pretending had gotten thinner.

Outside, Marrow Bay was dressed for the occasion. Bunting along the harbor road, blue and white, the colors from the 1924 festival photographs that Marguerite Vane had once curated so lovingly and so falsely. The same bunting, Petra noted, though someone had taken down the banner reading ONE HUNDRED YEARS OF OUR FOUNDER'S LEGACY and replaced it with one that read MARROW BAY: A CENTURY, TOLD HONESTLY. The new banner had cost the town council three emergency meetings, two resignations, and one fistfight outside the Anchor & Ale that Imogen Reyes had broken up personally, and it still wasn't everyone's favorite.

Petra locked the bookshop behind him — *Eastwick's Books, Open Most Days*, the sign he'd never bothered to fix because fixing it would have meant committing to something — and walked down toward the cemetery through streets that were already filling.

People nodded at him now. That was the strange part. For thirty years he had lived in this town the way a hermit crab lives in a borrowed shell: present, sheltered, fundamentally not of the place. He'd kept his head down after leaving the force, kept his books alphabetized and his opinions shelved, and if the town had noticed him at all, it had been as *that Eastwick fellow, odd family, keeps to himself*.

Now Mrs. Calloway from the bakery pressed a paper bag of cinnamon rolls into his hands without being asked. "For after," she said. "Burials make people hungry, even when they won't admit it."

"Thank you," Petra said, and meant it, which startled him more than the rolls did.

Old Tom Ferrier, who had spent forty years telling anyone who'd listen that the Vane legend was "a load of polished nonsense," shook his hand at the corner of Larkspur Street and said, gruffly, "You did right, Eastwick." Then, because Tom Ferrier had never in his life let a compliment stand unburdened: "Took you long enough."

"I only lived here thirty years," Petra said.

"Excuses."

* * *

The cemetery had never held two hundred people before. It held them now, spilling past the iron gates and down the hill toward the sea, where the water glittered with the indifferent beauty of a thing that had watched everything and testified to nothing.

They had buried Aldous Vane twice in one year, which Petra suspected was some kind of municipal record. The first coffin — stones and a rotted coat — had been removed with considerably less ceremony than it had been opened. What went into the ground today was a plain oak box containing actual remains: the bones they'd found beneath the museum floorboards, catalogued by a forensic team from the county, verified by dental records that had survived ninety-nine years in a lead-lined box because Aldous Vane, even in death, had insisted on being documented properly.

The new headstone waited beside the old one. The old one still read ALDOUS VANE, 1849–1924, BELOVED FOUNDER. The new one said the same dates but added a line that had taken the stonecutter four drafts and the town council six weeks:

*He lied about how he died. We are done lying for him.*

There had been a faction — a loud one, led by Councilman Harrow, whose great-grandfather had been the doctor who signed the false certificate — that wanted simply *Aldous Vane, 1849–1924*. Clean. Traditional. A lie by omission, which was the town's native dialect. But Mayor Okafor, who had frozen so memorably on exhumation day and then found her spine somewhere around chapter ten of the scandal, had pushed the honest inscription through, and here it was, cut in granite, permanent as weather.

Petra found Imogen near the front, in her dress uniform, standing the way she always stood at official functions: like a woman guarding a door only she could see. She glanced over as he approached.

"You wore the anchor tie," she said.

"It seemed nautical. He was a coastal founder."

"He was a fugitive who faked his death to dodge a murder charge."

"Nautical," Petra said firmly, "in spirit."

Her mouth twitched. She didn't quite smile — she'd been rationing her smiles around him since the night in the museum, as if she'd used up a year's supply holding a flashlight on a sealed room while an archivist pointed a shotgun at them both — but the twitch was there, and Petra had learned to count it as a whole sentence.

Below them, the crowd murmured. Petra did what he always did at gatherings now, out of habit or instinct or whatever you called the thing that had woken up in his chest the day he'd found a sealed letter in his aunt's papers: he watched the crowd instead of the ceremony.

He saw gratitude and fury sitting side by side in folding chairs. He saw the Harrow family, all nine of them, arranged like a jury that had already voted. He saw young Danny Pell, sixteen, holding up his phone to film, wearing a t-shirt that said ASK ME ABOUT THE REAL ALDOUS VANE — the boy had started a walking tour, God help them all, and it was apparently selling well. He saw Mrs. Calloway dabbing her eyes, though whether for the founder or the cinnamon rolls' intended recipients was unclear.

And he saw the empty chair.

Second row, aisle side, where the archive had been given seats. Marguerite Vane's name was on a card taped to it in careful block letters. She was in the county facility now, awaiting trial, and had declined — through her lawyer, in language so precise it could have been catalogued — to attend.

Petra thought about her every day. Not with fear anymore; the fear had faded sometime around the third week, replaced by something more complicated. She had held a shotgun on him and offered him a place in a hundred-year conspiracy, and when he'd refused, she hadn't pulled the trigger, and part of him — the part that had spent a career studying why people do what they do — understood her completely. She had been the last loyal daughter of a lie. Someone had to be. Her whole life had been a long apprenticeship in keeping a secret she hadn't chosen.

He'd testified at the preliminary hearing. She'd listened to his entire account without blinking, and at the end she'd said, clearly enough for the stenographer to record: *"The detective's timeline is accurate."*

It was, he suspected, the closest thing to affection she was capable of offering anyone.

* * *

Mayor Okafor spoke first. She was better at this than she'd been in April; grief and scandal had burned off her polish and left something plainer and stronger underneath.

"A hundred years ago," she said, "this town agreed to a story. Some of us inherited that story honestly, believing it. Some of us inherited it knowing better. And one of us—" a breath, steady— "one of us killed for it. Today we don't bury a founder. We bury a lie. And we start telling the truth, which is harder, and slower, and worth it."

She stepped back. The reverend said words. The choir sang something about anchors and harbors that Petra was fairly sure had been written for a different, less complicated funeral.

Then they lowered the box, and two hundred people watched real dirt go onto real remains, and Petra felt the crowd around him do something he'd witnessed only a handful of times in his career: change its mind about itself. You could almost hear it, a low collective creak, like a ship coming about. Some of them would swing back — the Harrows, probably, and half the families whose names were carved on the founders' plaque — but the ship had moved, and it wouldn't come all the way back around.

Afterward, the crowd dispersed in slow eddies, and Petra stayed, as he'd planned to, until the groundskeeper gave him a look and he tipped an imaginary hat and wandered off through the gates.

Imogen caught up with him on the hill road. "Cinnamon roll?" she said, eyeing the bag.

"Mrs. Calloway's. I'm told burials create appetite."

"She gave me two." They walked. The gulls argued overhead about something eternal. After a while Imogen said, quietly, "You know what today is, really?"

"Tuesday."

"It's the day the town stops owing you an apology and starts owing you a thank-you. Those are different things. I've been working on the wording."

"Don't strain yourself."

"Petra." She stopped walking. Below them the bay lay flat and bright, and the new banner snapped in the wind off the water. "You could have stayed in your shop. You could have handed me that letter and gone back to alphabetizing. Instead you got searched, slandered, held hostage overnight by a woman with a shotgun and a filing system, and you still chose us. Chose the truth, I mean. Out loud. In front of everyone."

"I had help," Petra said. "A constable broke into a museum with me. Very reckless. I hear she's studying for her detective's exam."

"She has a mentor. Terrible influence. Makes her watch crowds instead of coffins."

"The nerve."

She smiled then — a full one, unrationed — and Petra felt something in his chest settle that had been unsettled for thirty years, and understood, with the mild surprise of a man finding a room in his own house he'd never noticed, that this was what belonging felt like. Not granted. Earned. Which meant it couldn't be taken away, only neglected.

* * *

The archive had changed.

Marguerite's curated exhibits were gone — the heroic portraits, the commemorative china, the lovingly lit case of documents attesting to Vane's saintly character. In their place: the forged death certificate under glass, annotated in red; the second coffin's photographs; a wall panel titled *How the Lie Was Built*, laid out with a thoroughness that would have made Marguerite herself proud, because the interim archivist, a nervous young woman named Beth Okonkwo, had insisted on doing it properly.

"Mr. Eastwick!" Beth met him at the door, flustered and pleased. "We — I mean, the board — we wanted you to see it before the public opening Thursday."

He walked the rooms slowly. It was good work. Honest work. And in the back room, where the genuine collection was now stored under proper archival conditions, he took out the envelope he'd carried for months.

His aunt Edith's letter. *To whoever digs me up.* Vane's hand, sharp and slanted, confessing the vanishing, naming the helpers, one name smudged and later recovered under ultraviolet light: not a person, but the house itself. The document that had started everything, because a dying old woman had kept a secret for fifty years and then trusted her nephew with it, posthumously, on a technicality.

"I'd like to donate this," Petra said. "With conditions."

Beth's pen hovered. "Conditions?"

"One: it's displayed next to Marguerite's statement, not instead of it. The confession and the letter belong together — the beginning and the end of the same story. Two:" he hesitated, then committed, "the accession card lists my aunt Edith Hartwell as the donor. She carried this longer than any of us. She should be in the record."

"That's — yes. Of course." Beth wrote it down immediately, which Petra approved of. Write it down. That was the whole religion, really.

"There's a third condition," he said, and now he was the one hesitating, because the board had made the offer through Imogen two weeks ago and he'd said he'd think about it, and thinking about it had turned out to mean rehearsing the refusal and failing to say it. "I'm told you need someone to co-curate the 1924 materials. Someone who knows the file."

"We were rather hoping," Beth said carefully, "you might know the file better than anyone alive."

It was true. He did. He knew it page by page, the way Marguerite had known it, the way the killer had used it — except that he'd spent his life reading such documents to find the truth inside them, and he could think of no better use for that particular curse than teaching it to a town that was finally ready to read.

"I keep Tuesdays for the shop," Petra said.

"Tuesdays are fine."

"And I'll want final review on anything touching my aunt."

"Also fine."

He set the letter on the table between them, and Beth received it with both hands, the way you receive something living, and Petra Eastwick — outsider, hermit crab, the odd Eastwick fellow — stood in the archive of the town he'd kept at arm's length for thirty years and felt the arm finally lower.

* * *

That evening he closed the shop early, which the sign had technically permitted all along, and sat in the back room with tea and the day's small aftershocks. Through the window, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere down the hill the festival's last stragglers were singing badly and happily.

There was a knock. Imogen, off duty, holding a folder.

"Board paperwork," she said. "Co-curation contract. They want your signature." She set it down, then didn't leave. She stood there in the doorway with the particular expression she wore when she'd rehearsed something.

"Out with it, Constable."

"Danny Pell's walking tour ends at the cave now. The real cave. He's got twelve bookings for Saturday." A pause. "And this morning, a man came into the station asking about the 1924 disappearances. Not the ones in the file. The ones that were never written down. He said his grandmother used to talk about 'the ones the founder left behind,' and nobody at the station knew what he was talking about."

Petra set down his tea.

"But I did," Imogen said. "Because I've read everything now. And there's no record of them anywhere. No file. No names. Just a hole in the story shaped like people." She met his eyes. "I thought you'd want to know. Since Tuesdays are yours now, and the rest of the week appears to be, too."

Petra looked at the contract, unsigned, waiting. Then at the folder. Then at the harbor lights, and the dark water beyond them, patient as ever, keeping whatever it kept.

"Well," he said, reaching for a pen. "It seems the archive has an opening."
