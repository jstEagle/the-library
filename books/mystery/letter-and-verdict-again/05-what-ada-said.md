## 5. What Ada Said

The schoolteacher lived eleven miles out, past where the county stopped paving and the pines closed in like a jury deliberating. Quentin drove with the windows down because the garage smell of formaldehyde and creek water still clung to her hair, and because she wanted a reason not to think. Wind was a good reason. Wind asked nothing of her.

Marion Ellery's house sat at the end of a gravel spur, white paint gone gray, a wind chime made of spoons turning slow in the heat. The woman who answered the door was small and straight-backed, seventy if she was a day, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and eyes that went from Quentin's face to the county seal on her ID and back again.

"Coroner," Marion said, tasting the word. "You're the one who signed off on Ada Voss."

"I'm the one who notarized her confession."

"That too." Marion didn't move from the doorway. A cat wound around her ankles, orange and opinionated. "Ten years I've waited for somebody from that town to drive out here. Ten years, and it's the notary."

"You knew Ada?"

"I taught her American history when she was sixteen. She wrote an essay on the Dust Bowl that made me cry at my desk." Marion's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Then she turned eighteen and confessed to killing nobody, and everybody in Hollow Creek nodded along like it was weather. Come in before the mosquitoes eat us both."

* * *

The kitchen smelled of coffee gone bitter on a hot plate. Marion poured two cups without asking, set one in front of Quentin, and sat down across from her with the posture of a woman who had spent thirty years making teenagers sit up straight.

"Ask me what you came to ask."

Quentin wrapped her hands around the cup. "Did Ada ever talk to you after the confession?"

Marion looked at her for a long moment. Then she got up, went to the pantry, and came back with a tin — the kind flour used to come in, rust freckling the lid. She set it on the table between them but kept her hand flat on top of it.

"Three days before she died, Ada Voss sat in that chair you're sitting in and told me she'd lied. Told me she never touched that boy who drowned in '09. Told me a man had sat across from her in an interview room for six hours and explained, very patiently, what would happen to her little brother Milo if she didn't sign. Foster care first. Then worse. He had a way of saying *worse* that made you believe him."

Quentin's coffee went cold in her hands. "Who?"

"You know who." Marion's voice didn't rise. "I've known Harlan Bell since he judged the county pie contest. That man has never once raised his voice. That's how you know."

"He was retired by then. He had no business in an interrogation room."

"He had every business. He built half the people in that town. They don't question him any more than they question gravity." Marion lifted her hand off the tin. "She left this. Said if anything happened to her, I should keep it until somebody came asking with clean hands. I've been deciding for ten years whether yours were."

"And now?"

"Now there's a dead man in your morgue wearing your clothes, and I figure clean hands are a luxury nobody in Hollow Creek can afford anymore." She popped the lid.

Inside, on top of a nest of yellowed recipe cards, lay an envelope. Unsealed. Ada Voss's name written across the front in a schoolgirl's careful cursive — no address, no stamp. A letter that had been written to be read someday, by someone, though Ada couldn't have said who.

Quentin took it out with both hands, the way she handled evidence, the way she handled bones.

"She recanted," Marion said. "Formally. Went to the sheriff's office — old Sheriff Pruitt, God rest his cowardice — and told them she wanted to withdraw her confession. And three days later her car went off Miller Bend into the water, same water her brother drowned in, and everyone said *tragic, so tragic, that road's a devil,* and they buried her next to the boy she supposedly killed and called it closure." Marion's jaw tightened. "Closure. You people love that word. It means stop looking."

Quentin unfolded the letter.

*If you're reading this, then I didn't get to send it, which means something happened to me, which means I was right to be scared. My name is Ada Voss and I confessed to a drowning I didn't cause because Judge Harlan Bell told me my brother Milo would be taken away and hurt if I didn't. He wasn't angry. That's the worst part. He was kind about it. He brought me a soda. He said the town needed peace more than it needed truth, and that peace was a thing girls like me could give.*

*I want it known that I take it back. All of it. Every word I signed. Quentin Eastwick watched me sign it and I could see she didn't believe it either, and I've always been sorry I put her in that room with me. It wasn't her fault. She was young and they told her it was procedure.*

*Tell Milo I'm sorry I couldn't protect him twice.*

Quentin read it three times. Her own name sat in the middle of it like a splinter working toward the bone. *She could see she didn't believe it either.* Ten years ago, in that beige interview room, Quentin had stood with her notary stamp and watched a nineteen-year-old girl sign her life away, and she had felt the wrongness of it in her teeth, and she had stamped the page anyway, because the judge was present, because the sheriff was present, because everyone nodded and nodding was easier.

"Take it," Marion said quietly. "That's why I kept it. Not for sentiment. For the day someone would need it more than me."

"Marion, if this names Bell, and it does—"

"I'm eighty-one years old, child. What's he going to do, wait me out?" But her hand, Quentin noticed, trembled slightly as she folded the tin closed. "Get it somewhere safe. Not your office. Not anywhere with a Hollow Creek address."

* * *

Quentin drove back with the letter inside her coat, against her ribs, and told herself the chill she felt was just the evening coming on.

She almost went straight to Dane. Almost. But it was nearly nine, and the letter named a man half the county still bowed to, and she wanted one night to decide how to play it — whether to go loud or quiet, whether to bring Ruth in. So she parked in her driveway, clicked the garage remote, and pulled in under the single bare bulb.

She knew before she got out of the car.

The door between the garage and the kitchen stood open six inches. She always closed it. Always — a habit from twenty years of handling other people's worst days; you sealed the scene behind you. Now it hung ajar, and beyond it the kitchen light spilled onto the concrete floor, and on the floor, in the spill, were her files. Her case boxes. The ten years of her office, dragged off the metal shelving and fanned across the garage like a dealer laying out a losing hand.

She stayed in the car. Engine ticking. Hand on her phone. She counted to sixty, watching the dark corners, the space behind the water heater, the gap under the stairs. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moved. That was the trick of it — whoever did this was long gone, and the emptiness itself was the message: *we were here, we could have stayed, we left this much for you.*

She got out. Walked the wreckage. Files rifled, folders upside down, her father's old tackle box opened and its contents sorted through and left in neat rows — searched, not robbed. Nothing missing that she could see except—

Her stomach dropped.

She checked the coat. Checked it again, patting herself down like a drunk looking for keys. The envelope had been against her ribs the whole drive. It was still there. Still hers. Which meant they'd torn her life apart looking for something they hadn't found — and didn't know yet how close they'd come.

Or did they? Had she been followed from Marion's? Had the spoons-on-a-string wind chime masked the sound of another engine on that gravel spur?

She stood in the middle of her ruined garage holding a dead girl's unsent letter, and the bulb overhead buzzed, and somewhere down the street a dog started barking and wouldn't stop.

They hadn't found it. But they'd learn soon enough that she'd driven out to Marion Ellery's — a gas station camera, a neighbor, Bell's web of small-town eyes. And when they learned it, they would come back, and next time they wouldn't leave the letter.

Quentin climbed the steps into her kitchen, locked the door, slid the bolt, and sat down at her own table with the envelope flat in front of her under the light.

Ada Voss had named Harlan Bell in ink ten years ago, and the ink had waited. Quentin picked up her phone, thumb hovering over Dane's number, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever she did in the next sixty seconds would decide who else ended up in the water.
