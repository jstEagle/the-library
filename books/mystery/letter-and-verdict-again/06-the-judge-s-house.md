## 6. The Judge's House

The Bell house sat on the last rise before the creek bent north, a white clapboard two-story with a wraparound porch that had been freshly painted sometime in the last year. Quentin had driven past it a thousand times without ever really seeing it — the way you stop seeing anything that has always been part of the town's furniture. Tonight she saw it the way a stranger would: the flag bracket with no flag, the hedges cut square as testimony, the porch light burning yellow and patient, like something waiting to be asked inside.

She parked at the curb instead of the drive. She didn't want her car visible from the road if anyone came looking for her later, and she was already thinking in terms of *later*.

Judge Harlan Bell opened the door before she knocked twice. He was seventy-three years old and moved like a man of fifty-five, silver-haired, cardigan buttoned once over a collared shirt, reading glasses pushed up onto his forehead where they'd been sitting all evening over some forgotten book. His face did what it always did in public: arranged itself into welcome.

"Quentin Eastwick." He said her name the way he'd said it ten years ago across his bench, with a warmth that made you feel promoted just to be addressed. "I wondered when you'd come."

"You wondered."

"I've been wondering since they pulled that boy out of my creek wearing your suit." He stepped back and held the door. "Come in out of the damp. I have coffee still hot, and I'd rather not discuss this on the porch where Mrs. Aldous can count our words through her binoculars."

*My creek,* she noted. Not Hollow Creek. His.

Inside smelled of lemon polish and old paper. The front hall ran back toward a study lined floor to ceiling with law books whose spines had gone the color of weak tea. On the hall table sat a framed photograph of Bell shaking hands with three governors and, in the center of the arrangement, a smaller frame she didn't look at yet. She looked at everything else first. That was the discipline. Let the eye pass over the room like water before it settles on the stone.

He poured coffee in the kitchen without asking how she took it. Black, one sugar. He remembered. Of course he remembered; remembering was half of what power was.

"Sit," he said, and gestured to the table where a single place setting waited under a cloth napkin, as though he'd expected company. "You've had a hard week. A drowned man in your clothes. Dane treating you like a suspect. The whole town whispering." He set the cup in front of her and folded himself into the chair opposite, hands laced on the table, an old judge settling into the most comfortable posture of his life. "You should know I've spoken to the county review board on your behalf. Informally. They listen to me."

"I know they do."

"And I'll keep listening to them for you, Quentin, for as long as you hold that office." He smiled gently. "Which could be a very long time. Indefinitely, even. There's no reason a coroner of your competence should ever face an election she might lose. Appointed seats are so much more stable. So much quieter."

There it was. Offered in the second minute, wrapped in sympathy, priced before she'd even named what she'd come for. She wrapped both hands around the cup and didn't drink.

"In exchange for what, Judge?"

"For nothing dramatic." He spread his hands, a man declining applause. "Milo Voss came home after ten years away, drank too much — the boy always drank, you remember, everyone remembers — and fell into the creek in a coat that happened to be yours because he'd broken into your garage looking for shelter. Tragic. Sad. Closed by Friday." He tilted his head. "You write those words. I make some calls. And you never stand for election again in your life. You grow old in that office doing honest work, respected, protected. Isn't that what you wanted, all those years ago when you took the job? To be left alone with the dead?"

"The dead don't leave me alone," Quentin said. "That's the whole job."

Bell laughed — a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and somehow that was worse than menace. "You were always quick. Ada was quick too."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"She was quick," he went on, softer now, "and it bought her nothing but trouble. She came to me at nineteen, frightened, half-coached by that fool public defender into a story about being pressured. And do you know what I saw? A girl who would spend her whole life being pulled apart by this case if we let her. By lawyers, by reporters, by her own guilt. I gave her peace, Quentin. A signed confession, a closed file, a town that could grieve instead of tear itself open. You notarized it yourself. You thought it was mercy then."

"I thought it was paper," Quentin said. "I was twenty-six and you told me it was routine."

"It *was* routine." Something moved behind his eyes, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "And Milo coming back after ten years to stir it up — that was not routine. That was cruelty. He was going to recant, wasn't he? Stand up at some church meeting or courthouse step and say his sister lied, say the confession was coerced, drag Ada's name through the mud a decade after we buried her decently." He shook his head slowly, mourning the living. "Some things are kinder left closed. You of all people understand closure. It's literally your trade."

Quentin set down the cup. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. "I'd like to use your bathroom before I go."

He blinked at the non sequitur, then waved vaguely down the hall, amused, indulgent, already filing her away as a woman who would take the deal — they always took a day or two to say yes, and the saying-yes was sweeter when it looked like reluctance.

She walked the hall slowly. Passed the framed photograph. Passed the small frame she hadn't let herself look at.

It sat at eye level between two law books on the study's threshold shelf, angled to catch light from the window. A mantel piece, really — the little marble mantel in the study beyond, and she drifted toward it the way mourners drift toward caskets, unable not to.

On the mantel, among the awards and the faded photograph of a young Harlan Bell in robes, lay a wristwatch.

Silver case. Cracked crystal. Leather band darkened along its underside in a way that no amount of cleaning fully reversed once creek water had gotten into it. The second hand stood still at the eleven, and the crown was bent slightly inward, the way metal bends when a body is dragged over stones.

She knew the watch. She had photographed it — or meant to. It was item number four on the intake list she'd started the night Milo Voss came out of the water, the list she'd never finished, because when she'd reached for the watch to log it, it hadn't been there. She'd assumed the sheriff's deputies had taken it. She'd assumed a lot of things.

It had been here the whole time. On a mantel. In a warm room, above a cold hearth, displayed like a trophy or a warning — she couldn't decide which, and understood, with a slow cold turning in her stomach, that the distinction probably didn't matter to him. Maybe both. Maybe the watch was exactly where he wanted it to be found, if it came to that. Or maybe he simply liked keeping time the way he liked keeping everything else: stopped, owned, on his shelf.

"Ada gave him that watch," Bell said behind her.

She hadn't heard him cross the hall. He stood in the study doorway with his coffee, unhurried, and his voice had changed — the warmth stripped off it now, laid aside like the reading glasses, revealing the instrument underneath.

"When she turned eighteen. Before any of it." He came closer, set his cup on the mantel beside the watch, aligned the handle with a small precise motion. "So you see, it isn't a trophy. It's a memorial. I loved that girl in my own fashion. I tried to save her from herself, and when I couldn't, I kept something of hers. People do that. You keep her confession in your files. I keep her brother's watch on my shelf. We're not so different, you and I."

"We're nothing alike."

"No?" He studied her with genuine curiosity, head tilted. "You notarized a coerced confession and slept fine for ten years. You closed every question Ada's death raised because closing them was easier, and safer, and the town thanked you for it. The only difference between us, Quentin, is that when I tidy things away, I know what I'm doing. You needed me to teach you."

The words went in clean, the way a blade goes in when it's been sharpened against you specifically. Because some part of her — the part that had signed that notary stamp ten years ago, the part that had felt relief when the file closed — knew there was truth in it. He watched her feel it. He'd built his whole life on watching people feel things and knowing exactly when to press.

"So," he said, and picked up the watch, weighed it in his palm, held it out to her. "Take it. Log it. Item number four. Write your accident report, close the boy gently, and put this back where it belongs — in evidence, in a box, in the past. And in exchange, you will die in that office an honored woman, and no one will ever again ask you what you saw in this house tonight."

The watch lay in his open hand between them, silver and dead, still keeping the hour of its drowning.

Quentin looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked up at him, and found that the fear she'd carried all week — of Dane, of the town, of the whispering — had quietly drained out of her and left something simpler behind.

"No," she said.

Bell sighed, and closed his fingers over the watch, and the kindness left his face entirely, and what remained was patient and old and very sure of itself.

"I'm sorry to hear that," he said. "Truly. Because now I'll have to wonder, out loud, publicly, why the coroner who lost her own suit — who let evidence walk out of her own garage — is fit to hold office at all. And Quentin? Do check your locks on the way home. This town has had so many accidents lately."

* * *

She drove home with the windows cracked despite the cold, needing the air. The porch light of the Bell house shrank in her mirror until it was one yellow point, then nothing.

Her garage door stood slightly open.

She had locked it. She always locked it. She sat in the car for a full minute watching the gap, the darkness inside it, thinking of Ruth's careful silence these past days, of Colm Reyes's too-ready helpfulness at the scene, of a suit that had walked out of her closet and onto a drowned man.

Then she got out, and crossed the yard, and reached for the door — and heard, from inside the garage, in the dark, the soft deliberate click of someone else's flashlight switching off.
