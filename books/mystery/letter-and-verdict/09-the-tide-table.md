## 9. The Tide Table

The tide table for Greywater hung in the bait shop on Harbor Street, laminated and sun-bleached, updated by a man named Denny who had never once been wrong. I stood in front of it at six in the morning with a coffee I wasn't drinking and read the numbers like scripture.

Spring tide. Thursday. Low water at 4:17 a.m., minus 1.3 feet—the marsh would pull its skirt back farther than it had in months, exposing the flats where Mara Voss's satchel had slept for twenty-seven years, where Ruth and I had dug it out two nights ago under a moon that didn't judge us.

I had put it back.

That was the part that still made my hands shake when I thought about it too long. We'd lifted it free of the mud—Ruth crying without sound, me holding the plastic-wrapped bundle Brandt had sealed in 1998—and then I had wrapped it again, weighted it, and pressed it back into the cold silt exactly where it had lain, because a thing hidden for twenty-seven years is worth more as bait than as evidence. Evidence could be burned. Evidence could be stolen from my house by men who knew how to walk through a closet without disturbing the hangers. But bait—

Bait made them come to me.

* * *

I spent Wednesday doing arithmetic.

Not money arithmetic. Power arithmetic. The kind you do when you're planning to stand in front of three men who have killed at least twice to keep a secret, and you need to know what they'll risk and what they won't.

Silas Marsh wanted the satchel because of what Brandt had hidden inside it: photographs, lab correspondence, a second autopsy sketch in Elias Brandt's own hand showing the ligature marks the first report called "post-mortem artifact." Physical proof that the verdict was manufactured. Marsh couldn't let it surface. He'd burned my studio over less.

Judge Aldous Crane wanted it because Crane thought in legacies. He was seventy-four. He had spent his whole life being the man who kept Greywater orderly, and he genuinely believed—he'd said so in my living room, with tea going cold between us—that some truths were load-bearing walls, and pulling them brought the whole house down on everyone. He would come personally. He wouldn't trust the retrieval to anyone else.

Tom Beake would come because Beake did what the other two told him, and because Beake was the one whose hands had actually done things in 1998, which meant he was the one with the most to lose and the least imagination about losing it.

Three men. One satchel. One window: low water, 4:17 a.m., plus or minus forty minutes before the tide turned and swallowed the flats again. They couldn't wait. Every hour the satchel sat exposed was an hour someone else might find it—I'd made sure of that, telling no one but making sure the *Greywater Ledger* received an anonymous tip about "disturbances in the marsh near the old Voss property." A town that had ignored its dead for twenty-seven years suddenly couldn't stop looking at the mud.

They had to come Thursday night. And they had to come while I was there, broadcasting, because if they came any other time they'd simply take the satchel and I'd be another body the tide gave up eventually, wearing whatever clothes they chose for me.

So I would be there. On air. Wired. Alone—or looking alone.

* * *

"You're insane," Ruth said.

We sat in her kitchen, the one with the wallpaper Mara had picked out at seventeen, peonies faded to ghosts. Ruth's hands were wrapped around a mug she hadn't touched. She'd aged ten years since we opened the satchel. Grief does that—it doesn't crease you gradually, it drops on you all at once, like a wave you'd been standing braced against for decades finally letting go.

"Probably," I said.

"They'll kill you."

"They might." I laid the tide table printout on her table, next to a diagram I'd drawn of the marsh—the channel, the flats, the boardwalk, the old pilings where the crab boats used to tie off. "But here's the thing, Ruth. They can't kill me quietly anymore. That was always their method. Quiet. A closed casket, a quick verdict, a town that agrees not to ask. So we take quiet away from them."

I showed her the plan the way I'd built it, piece by piece, because saying it out loud was the only way to find the holes.

At 3:30 a.m. Thursday, I would go live from the marsh. Not from a studio—from my phone, streaming through every platform I had, pushed to every listener on my list. Fifteen years of archiving had bought me something I'd never fully appreciated: reach. Forty thousand people subscribed to *Letter and Verdict*. Most of them listened on their commutes, half-asleep. But true crime audiences are a strange congregation—they show up. When I'd gone live after the studio fire, eleven thousand people had tuned in within twenty minutes, and by morning the clip had been heard in nine countries.

Eleven thousand witnesses. Maybe more. You cannot quietly drown a woman in front of eleven thousand witnesses.

"I'll tell them where I am," I said. "Exactly. Coordinates, landmarks, everything. And I'll tell them why I'm there—that I've found evidence in this marsh that proves Mara Voss was murdered, and that I believe the men responsible are coming to destroy it tonight. Then I'll wait."

"And if they just stay home?"

"They won't. Because if they stay home, the satchel surfaces, and the story runs without them ever getting a chance to control it. Their only play is to come get it. Crane will convince himself he can talk his way through anything—maybe even on camera. Beake will want to end it physically. Marsh will be the one holding the shovel." I tapped the diagram. "And Rennick?"

Ruth looked up. "You're trusting Rennick?"

"No," I said. "I'm giving him one last door out of the room he's been standing in for twenty-seven years. I sent him the tide table yesterday. No note. Just the numbers and the coordinates. If he comes, maybe somebody with a badge is standing between me and them. If he doesn't—" I shrugged, and tried to make the shrug look like it cost me nothing. "Then the audience is my badge."

* * *

Wednesday night I prepared my body the way you prepare a letter you may not survive the mailing of.

The recorder was small—a backup unit from my old field kit, the kind court stenographers used before everything went digital, analog tape, no cloud to wipe, no drive to burn. I taped it flat against my sternum, under three layers, wired into a lapel mic disguised as the zipper pull of my jacket. If they took the phone, the tape survived. If they took the jacket, well. I'd practiced unclipping it in under four seconds.

I wrote two letters. One to Ruth, short, mostly instructions about the archive—where the master drives were hidden, which attorney to call, how to release everything if I stopped posting. One to my listeners, scheduled to auto-publish at dawn Friday if I hadn't posted anything myself. It began: *If you're reading this, the tide came in.*

I read it four times and couldn't make it better. There's a particular terror in writing your own ending, and a particular calm on the other side of it. I kept thinking of Daniel Pryce, who had mailed me anonymous tips for three years, careful little envelopes with clipped newspaper corners, and who had ended up face-down in the marsh wearing my cardigan—wearing my invisibility, my armor of ordinariness, stolen right out of my closet while I slept. He had died trying to finish what I started. The least I owed him was to not die stupidly.

I checked the locks anyway. Old habits. Useless habits. If Silas Marsh wanted into my house, my locks were a suggestion.

* * *

At 11 p.m. I sat in the dark of my ruined studio—the fire had taken the walls but left the frame, black beams against the stars like the ribs of something beached—and I recorded a practice run. Just my voice, testing levels, testing the sentence I planned to open with.

*"My name is Ines Thackeray, and I'm standing in Greywater Marsh, at low tide, on the ground where Mara Voss died."*

It sounded different out loud than it had in my head. Smaller. More afraid. I almost deleted it.

Then I thought about Mara. Twenty-six years old, pregnant, walking home along the channel road on a October night in 1998, and somewhere in the dark the three pillars of her town decided she was a problem to be solved. She hadn't chosen her ending. Nobody asked her. Her sister had spent twenty-seven years being told she was crazy for grieving loudly.

I deleted nothing.

* * *

Thursday, 2:50 a.m. The sky was clear and starless behind high cloud, the kind of flat black that makes the horizon disappear, land and water one darkness. I parked half a mile out on the fire road, offloaded my pack, and walked in with a headlamp off, navigating by memory and the pale ribbon of the channel.

The marsh at low tide breathes. You can hear it—the water draining off the flats in thin silver threads, the mud sighing, the smell of sulfur and salt and old, old time. Somewhere out there, under eighteen inches of silt I'd re-laid with my own hands, Mara Voss's satchel waited like a mine.

I reached the old pilings at 3:12. Set up the phone on a small tripod wedged between two barnacled posts, angled to catch my face and, behind me, the open flats. Tested the stream. Eleven seconds to connect. Good.

I clipped the lapel mic. Touched the tape recorder under my coat like a talisman.

3:20. I went live.

"Good morning," I said, and my voice held, which surprised me. "If you're hearing this, you're one of thousands standing in the dark with me. My name is Ines Thackeray. Behind me is Greywater Marsh. Under that mud is the evidence that will prove Mara Voss was murdered in 1998—not drowned accidentally, not lost, murdered—and I have reason to believe the men who killed her are coming here tonight to destroy it. I'm going to wait for them. Out loud. With all of you."

The viewer count climbed as I talked. Three hundred. Nine hundred. Two thousand. Names scrolled past in the chat—people from Greywater, people from three states away, strangers typing *we're with you* and *don't move* and, once, chillingly, nothing at all, just a user named **T.BEAKE** joining the stream at 3:31 and leaving thirty seconds later.

They knew. Of course they knew. I'd wanted them to know.

3:40. The flats lay open and gleaming under my lamp. The tide table said the turn would come at 4:17, and after that the water would start reclaiming everything, inch by patient inch.

At 3:52, headlights appeared on the far levee road. Three vehicles, moving slow, lights cutting off one by one as they descended toward the marsh.

I looked straight into the lens, and I said, "They're here."

Out of the dark, three silhouettes started across the flats toward me—one heavy and deliberate, one upright and precise, one lagging behind like a man being pulled on a rope.

And behind them, farther back on the levee, a fourth set of headlights flicked on, and stayed on, and did not move.
