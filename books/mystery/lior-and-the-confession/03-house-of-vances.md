## 3. House of Vances

The taxi turned off the coast road at a quarter past four, and Blackthorn Grange rose out of its own shadow like something surfacing from deep water.

Tamsin Sterling had prepared for many things in his career — hostile executors, forged signatures, the particular cruelty of families reading a will aloud in a room too small for their grief. He had not prepared for the house itself. It was larger than the photographs in the probate file suggested, a grey stone mass of gables and mullioned windows that seemed to have been built not to shelter anyone but to endure them. Ivy climbed the eastern wall with the patience of something that intended to be there long after everyone inside was gone. The gravel drive crunched under the tires like the house clearing its throat.

He paid the driver and stood a moment with his case at his feet, breathing the salt-and-rot smell of the estuary. Somewhere behind the house, gulls were arguing over something dead. It was, he thought, exactly the sort of soundtrack a dying woman would choose.

The door opened before he knocked.

"You're late," said the woman holding it open. She was perhaps sixty, small and square-shouldered, with iron-grey hair pinned back so severely it looked structural. Her apron was clean, her hands were not. "Mrs. Vance said half three."

"The roads—"

"Are always slow. That's why she said half three." She stepped back to admit him. "Nell Harkness. Caretaker. Housekeeper. Whatever needs doing. You'll want the good room; it's got the only radiator that works properly."

She took his case without asking and walked ahead of him down a hall lined with portraits — Vance faces, generation after generation, all of them painted with the same tight mouth, as if the artist had understood something about the family that the family hadn't authorized. Tamsin counted eight of them before Nell stopped at a door and pushed it open onto a room with a view of nothing but the sea.

"Mrs. Vance will see you at six. Dinner's at seven. Don't be late to either." She set his case down. "And Mr. Sterling — she's having a good day today. They're not all good days. Use this one."

Then she was gone, her footsteps receding down the hall with the brisk economy of someone who had spent decades learning which rooms needed her and which needed her absence.

Tamsin unpacked slowly, by habit: documents on the left of the desk, toiletries right of the basin, the copy of the impossible will in the inner pocket of his jacket, where he'd carried it since London like a man carrying a diagnosis he hadn't yet read aloud. Through the window the tide was going out, exposing mudflats the color of old bruises. A single figure stood far out on them, motionless, watching the water leave.

He watched the figure for a long time before he realized it was watching the house.

* * *

At six o'clock precisely, Tamsin was shown into the study.

Lior Vance sat behind a desk that could have served as a lifeboat, in a wheelchair angled toward the window, wrapped in a shawl the color of garnets. Up close, the illness was visible in the architecture of her face — the cheekbones standing too proud above hollowed ground, the skin stretched thin over a skull that was winning. But the eyes were untouched. They were dark, quick, and appalled by nothing.

"Mr. Sterling." Her voice was dry and precise, a voice that had spent eighty years refusing to be interrupted. "You found us. Most people get lost on the coast road and decide the universe is telling them something."

"I don't listen to the universe. It's a poor witness."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where one had once lived. "Sit. You've met Nell. The others you'll meet at dinner, and I'd rather give you the inventory first, so you can watch them being introduced and see what they do with their hands."

"That sounds like advice from experience."

"It sounds like advice from eighty years." She folded her hands on the blanket. "Four people in this house besides you and me. My son Marcus, thirty-four, adopted at seven. His wife Priya, who married into this family the way people marry into weather — hoping it'll be kinder than forecast. My brother Aldous, who hasn't spoken to me in eleven years and accepted my invitation in nine days, which should tell you how badly he needs whatever he thinks I'm about to give him. And Nell, who has kept this house for thirty years and knows where every body would be buried if this family ever produced any."

"And which of them do you suspect?"

"All of them," Lior said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of hiring you. If I knew which one, I'd have saved myself a great deal of money and simply locked my door."

She turned the wheelchair toward him fully, and the light through the window caught her face and made it briefly translucent, a lantern with a candle guttering inside.

"You'll want to know why I'm certain enough to pay what I paid. Here is why. I have spent my whole life reading documents — deeds, contracts, letters, the small treacheries people commit in ink because ink feels final. When I read my own death warrant, Mr. Sterling, I recognized the handwriting of the room. Someone in this house wrote that will. Not copied it. *Wrote* it. And whoever they are, they are still here, eating my food, sleeping under my roof, waiting for me to die on schedule." She smiled then, and it was the worst thing he'd seen all day. "So tonight you will watch them very carefully, and tomorrow we begin. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Tamsin said, and heard in his own voice the sound of a man signing something.

* * *

Dinner was served at seven in a dining room built for twenty and set for six, which made the table look like a runway and the meal look like a hearing.

Marcus Vance came in first, and Tamsin understood immediately why clients trusted him and colleagues envied him. He was tall and fair in a way none of the portraits were, dressed with expensive restraint, and he crossed the room to kiss his mother's temple with the fluency of long practice. "You must be Mr. Sterling," he said, offering a hand that was warm and dry and calibrated. "Mother doesn't explain anything, so forgive me — are we being sued, or saved?"

"Neither. Yet."

"Then it's estate work." Marcus laughed easily. "At last, some honesty in this family. We're all just waiting to find out what we get."

"MARCUS." Aldous Vance said it from the doorway, quietly, and the word landed like a dropped glass.

Aldous was seventy-one and looked assembled from spare parts of the portraits in the hall — the same narrow jaw, the same disapproving mouth, worn now like an inheritance he resented. He wore a tweed jacket two seasons past its usefulness and sat down without greeting anyone, unfolding a napkin as though it had personally wronged him.

Priya came last. She was slight, dark-eyed, and moved through the room the way people move through a museum after closing — present, careful, touching nothing. She greeted Lior with genuine tenderness, Tamsin noted. She did not greet her husband at all, and the omission had the smoothness of long practice.

Soup was served. Conversation performed its opening movements.

"Aldous," Lior said, "you remember Mr. Sterling is handling my affairs."

"I remember you summoned me here to watch you handle your affairs," Aldous said. "Eleven years, Lior. Eleven years of silence, and now a letter saying come for the summer. People don't summon their estranged siblings for sentiment. Sentiment doesn't hire lawyers."

"No," Lior agreed. "It hires nurses. I couldn't afford both."

Marcus laughed — a beat too quickly, Tamsin thought, and a note too loud. "Family reunions," he said, "are just probate with better catering."

And there it was, Tamsin noted, writing nothing down because he didn't need to: the joke about inheritance offered before anyone else had raised the subject. Alibi-shaped behavior. People reveal themselves not in what they hide but in what they pre-empt. Marcus had mentioned the estate before dinner began, framed it as comedy, made it safe to say aloud — so that when the real conversation came, he would already have been the man who joked about money, not the man who wanted it.

Priya, meanwhile, had barely touched her soup. She kept her eyes on her plate and her hands in her lap, and when Marcus's knee pressed hers under the table — Tamsin saw the small shift of fabric, the deliberate contact — she went very still, the way a person goes still when a hand rests on a wound.

Aldous ate methodically and asked no questions, which was itself an answer. A man invited to a dying sister's house for a summer does not ask *why am I here* only if he believes he already knows, or believes the answer will arrive with paperwork attached.

And Nell — Nell moved around the table refilling glasses no one had emptied, and every time the conversation touched the future, the will, the summer, the end, her ladle paused for half a second. Half a second. Thirty years in a house teaches a person to hear everything, and Tamsin would have bet his retainer that Nell Harkness had heard more in this dining room than any of the people seated at it.

"So," Lior said, setting down her spoon with a click that silenced the table. "Mr. Sterling has agreed to oversee the disposition of my estate. All of it. Beginning immediately."

The silence lasted four seconds. Tamsin counted.

Aldous's fork stopped halfway to his mouth — then resumed, unhurried. Controlled. A man who had rehearsed this moment or one like it.

Priya's breath caught audibly, and she covered it by reaching for the wine, pouring for Marcus without asking, filling his glass nearly to the brim. Nervous generosity. Or a warning.

And Marcus — Marcus smiled, warm and rueful and utterly composed, and said, "Well. Then I suppose we should all be on our best behavior," and raised his glass to his mother, and drank.

But his other hand, below the table's edge, had closed around the napkin in his lap, and the knuckles had gone white, and he held it that way for the rest of the course.

* * *

After dinner, the house revealed its second nature.

Tamsin walked the corridors alone, mapping. This was his method and had been for twenty years: learn the building before you judge the people, because buildings keep honest records. Blackthorn Grange kept dishonest ones — doors that looked like panels, panels that looked like doors, a main staircase wide enough for a coffin and a servants' stair narrow enough for secrets. The library smelled of pipe smoke though no one admitted to smoking. The study windows overlooked the drive, the terrace, and the mudflats, which meant whoever sat at Lior's desk could see anyone coming and going, including, Tamsin noticed, the figure who was out there again now, standing on the flats in the failing light.

He found the side door and stepped out into the cold.

The mudflats at low tide were a grey country of channels and pools, and Lior Vance's wheelchair had left twin tracks across them, ending where she sat facing the water, shawled against the wind, hands folded as though awaiting a delivery.

"You shouldn't be out here," Tamsin said. "The cold, the damp—"

"I'm dying, Mr. Sterling. The damp is the least of my conditions." She didn't turn. "I come out at ebb tide. My husband used to walk these flats. He said the sea leaving was the most honest thing he ever saw — everything exposed, nothing pretending to be deeper than it is." A pause. "Do you know what an ebb tide reveals, in a family?"

"Everything buried."

"Everything buried," she agreed. "Which is why I've asked everyone here. Let the water go out. Watch what the flats show us." She finally turned her head, and the last light caught her eyes. "You cataloged them at dinner. Don't deny it; you have a lawyer's face, it never stops taking notes. Tell me. First impressions."

Tamsin considered lying, decided she'd hear it anyway, and gave her the truth. "Your son performs affection better than he feels it. Your daughter-in-law is afraid of something specific, not something general — fear with an address. Your brother came for money and hates himself for needing it. And your caretaker knows more than she says and has decided, for reasons of her own, that you're worth protecting."

Lior was quiet for a moment. The tide whispered its retreat across the mud.

"Nell, yes," she said softly. "Nell is the only one of them I'd trust with my actual death. Which is convenient, since apparently I need to trust someone with my scheduled one." She gripped the wheels and began turning the chair back toward the house. "Come along, Mr. Sterling. The water's going out fast tonight, and I find I want to be indoors before everything's exposed."

They reached the terrace as the first window of the house lit up — the library, second floor, where a tall fair-haired silhouette stood looking down at the flats, at the tracks in the mud, at the two of them.

Watching. Counting, perhaps. Checking, perhaps, whether his mother was keeping her appointments.

Tamsin felt the chill settle into his professional bones, the one he'd been carrying since London, sharpening now into something with a shape and a date attached.

Because he had re-read the will on the train, and he remembered — with the particular horror of a man remembering too late — that its fourth clause specified, in language of terrible precision, the condition of the household at the time of death: *the tide withdrawn, the family assembled, the deceased observed from the upper gallery.*

He looked up at the lit window. The silhouette did not move.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he would pull the police files. Tomorrow he would start matching the will's words against the world's.

Tonight, the house settled around him like a closed fist, and somewhere above, a floorboard creaked under a weight that was walking, patiently, back toward bed.
