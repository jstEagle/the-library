## 10. Storm Warning

The barometer at Blackthorn Grange had been falling all day, and Tamsin Sterling had learned to read it the way he read clients: not for what it said, but for what it was about to do.

By four o'clock the light had gone the color of old pewter. The sea, visible in bruised slabs between the pines beyond the garden wall, had turned the gray of unpolished silver, and the wind had begun its long rehearsal — gusts that came up the lawn, tested the shutters, and withdrew, like a man trying a door before deciding whether to knock or break it down.

Tamsin stood at the study window with Lior's will open on the desk behind him, though he no longer needed to look at it. He had the relevant passage memorized down to its punctuation, the way a man memorizes the diagnosis he refuses to believe.

*In the event that the testator should die on or before the twenty-ninth day of this instrument's term, and in the event that such death should be adjudged violent...*

The twenty-ninth day was tomorrow.

And the storm, according to every forecast Nell had relayed from the village radio, would make landfall tonight.

"You're doing it again," Lior said from her chair by the fire.

He turned. She was wrapped in a shawl the color of garnets, her hands folded over the head of her cane, her eyes bright and dry and utterly without mercy. Terminal illness had burned away everything soft in her face and left only architecture — cheekbone, jaw, the sharp ridge of will beneath the skin. People mistook her frailty for fragility. Tamsin had stopped making that mistake in their first hour together.

"Doing what?"

"Calculating. You get a crease between your eyebrows, right here." She touched the spot with one knuckle. "It's the same crease you had when you told me the will's phrasing matched police evidence descriptions that don't exist yet. You're calculating whether you can still get me out."

"I'm calculating," Tamsin said, "whether I can still get you out *legally*. If I carry you to the car bodily, that's kidnapping. If I persuade you, that's just Tuesday."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Lior Vance did not smile; she conceded points.

"The roads close at the causeway first," she said. "You know that. The tide comes in and the lane to the village goes under, and after that there is no car, no doctor, no police constable, nothing but us and the rain until morning." She looked past him at the window, where the first real sheets of weather were beginning to fling themselves against the glass. "You want me gone before dark. It's already half past four."

"Then we leave now. Tonight. There's a hotel in Keswick—"

"A hotel in Keswick where I will die in eleven days anyway, in a bed that isn't mine, surrounded by strangers, while Marcus inherits everything because my lawyer ran away from a piece of paper." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she said everything, and somehow that made it worse. "No."

"Lior—"

"*Mrs. Vance*," she corrected, as she always did when he grew familiar out of fear. Fear made people presumptuous; she'd spent ninety years refusing to permit it. "Mr. Sterling. Let us be precise with one another, since precision is the only religion either of us practices. You believe someone in this house intends to kill me. You believe they intend to do it in a manner described, in advance, in a document that person may have written. And you believe that if I leave, that person walks away clean, richer, and vindicated — because the prophecy fails, and failed prophecies have a way of being forgotten."

"That's exactly what I believe. Which is why—"

"Which is why I stay." She unfolded herself from the chair with the slow, grinding effort of the very ill, crossed to the window, and stood beside him looking out at the coming ruin of the evening. Up close she smelled of lavender and something medicinal underneath. Her reflection in the darkening glass was thin as a candle flame. "Listen to me, Mr. Sterling, because I will say this once. I am dying. Not might die — *am* dying. The doctors gave me a season and the season is nearly spent. Every day I have left is a day I chose, and I choose to spend them here, catching the person who decided my death was theirs to schedule."

"And if the cost is your life?"

"Then the cost is a few days I was going to lose regardless." She turned her head, and her eyes caught the last of the light. "But understand what you're actually asking. You're asking me to trade a certainty for an uncertainty. If I run, I die soon, somewhere else, and the killer inherits. If I stay, I might die tomorrow — or I might live out my term watching them fail, and then watch you take everything they built away from them, clause by clause, in front of witnesses. I have been a woman who loses things gracefully my entire life. I find, at the end, I'd rather be a woman who wins ugly."

* * *

He tried everyone else first.

Priya he found in the morning room, sitting with a cup of tea she hadn't touched, staring at the wall where the family portraits hung. When he told her the roads would close within hours, she stood so quickly her chair skidded.

"Take her. Take all of us. God, why are we even *here*—" She stopped herself, and Tamsin watched the fear move through her face like weather: fear of the house, fear of the answer, fear of what she knew and what her knowing made her. "She won't listen. She never listens. That's the whole tragedy of this family — we were all raised by a woman who doesn't listen."

"She listens," Tamsin said quietly. "She just doesn't obey."

Priya's hands twisted in her lap. "Then you take her yourself. Carry her if you have to. Because if she dies in this house—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "If she dies in this house, Mr. Sterling, I will have to live with what I didn't say soon enough, and I don't think I can carry two of those."

He wanted to ask her what she meant. He had wanted to ask for days. But her face closed like a door, and outside, the wind rose another register, and there was no time left for anything but logistics.

Aldous was in the library with a decanter, which was where Aldous always was, positioned equidistant between the liquor and the exits. When Tamsin laid out the situation — storm, sealed roads, the will's date falling tomorrow — Aldous laughed, a short unpleasant bark.

"You want to evacuate the house? On the night your mysterious paper says she dies?" He poured himself two fingers with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "Man, if I'd planned a murder, this is precisely the night I'd pick. Roads gone, power likely to fail, everyone trapped in a box with the body. Textbook." He drank, and his eyes over the rim of the glass were frightened and calculating at once. "Of course, I'd also pick a night when *I* had an alibi. Which I don't. So draw your own conclusions, counselor, but do it quickly, because I intend to sit in my room with the door locked and a bottle, and I suggest you do the same."

"You could help," Tamsin said. "Watch the stairs. Watch each other."

"We've been watching each other for three weeks, Sterling. It's the only thing anyone in this house does anymore." Aldous set down the glass. For a moment the drunken mask slipped and Tamsin saw the man underneath: old, broke, terrified, and — this was the part that unsettled him most — honest. "You want my advice? Get her out while the causeway's still dry. Whatever game she thinks she's playing, the other player isn't playing games."

Nell he found last, in the kitchen, checking the oil lamps and laying in matches with the methodical calm of someone who had kept this house through forty years of storms.

"They'll want the generator primed," she said, before he could speak. "I've done it. Candles in every room. Water's off the mains come high tide, so I've filled the baths." She looked up at him, and her weathered face held something almost gentle. "You're going to ask me to help you get her out."

"I'm going to ask you to help me keep her alive."

"Same thing, some nights." Nell tied off a matchbox and set it in the basket with the others. "But she won't go, sir. I've known her since before you were born, and she has buried a husband, a daughter, and every friend she ever let close, and she has never once run from a thing she could stand and face instead. It's the best and worst thing about her." She paused. "There's a cot in the east wing, next to her rooms. If you mean to guard her, guard her from there. And Mr. Sterling — the stair rail. The one that was repaired last month."

"What about it?"

"Nell checked it again this afternoon." Her voice dropped. "Someone's been at the brackets. New scratches in the paint. Old screws swapped for ones that don't match." She met his eyes. "Whatever happens tomorrow, don't let her use those stairs alone. Promise me."

He promised.

* * *

At seven, with the storm making its full argument against the windows and the lights stuttering once, twice, and holding, Tamsin made his final appeal.

He laid it out like a brief, because a brief was the only language he trusted: the sabotaged rail, the will's timeline, the vanished hour he couldn't yet account for, the drafting hand that matched documents Marcus had filed years ago. He did not name Marcus. He didn't have to. Lior listened from her chair with her hands folded and her face unreadable, and when he finished, the silence lasted long enough for the fire to shift and settle.

"You've built a case," she said at last. "A good one. Circumstantial, but good. Now tell me what happens if you're wrong."

"If I'm wrong, we've lost one night's sleep."

"And if you're right, and I leave, we've lost everything." She leaned forward. "Here is what you haven't considered, Mr. Sterling, because you are an honest man and honest men underestimate liars. If I flee tonight, my murderer does not stop existing. He simply waits. He has already written my death into law; do you imagine a hotel in Keswick is beyond him? But if I stay — if the trap springs and fails, in this house, with you watching — then we will have him. Not suspicion. *Him.* The rail breaks, and whoever weakened it must explain the hour it was done. The scene gets staged, and whoever staged it leaves traces in a house where I know every floorboard's opinion." Her voice dropped, went almost tender, which from Lior was more frightening than anger. "I am eighty-nine years old and I am dying, and this is the last useful thing I will ever do. Let me be useful."

"You're asking me to use you as bait."

"I'm telling you I *am* bait, and have been since the day that will was drafted. The only question is whether the fisherman is paying attention." She extended her hand, thin and strong, across the space between them. "Swear it properly. If anything happens to me despite everything — you finish it. You find the author. That's the retainer. That was always the retainer."

Tamsin looked at her hand and thought about his own name, inherited from a father who had signed papers Tamsin had never read, and how every life is lived inside some document or other. Then he took her hand. Her grip was astonishingly firm.

"I swear it."

"Good." She released him and settled back, and the imperious mask returned, but her eyes stayed on his a moment longer. "Now go check the stair rail again. And Mr. Sterling — thank you for arguing. No one has bothered to argue with me in years. It was almost like being loved."

* * *

He did not sleep.

Past midnight the storm reached its full voice, a sustained roar with the wind driving rain sideways against the panes, and somewhere around one o'clock the power failed as Nell had predicted, and the house went over to lamps and candles, every shadow doubling. Tamsin walked the corridors with a flashlight and a length of rope coil from the cellar, checking doors, checking windows, checking the stair rail with his fingertips — the new scratches, the mismatched screws, the bracket working loose under pressure that hadn't been applied yet but would be.

Tomorrow. *On or before the twenty-ninth day.*

He passed Marcus's door at two in the morning. A line of candlelight showed beneath it, steady, unmoving. He stood in the dark corridor listening to the storm and to his own heartbeat, and told himself that a man awake at two a.m. was a man as afraid as the rest of them.

He passed Priya's door: dark, silent. Aldous's: a mutter of radio, a bottle knocking softly against glass. Nell's room was empty — he found her below, seated on the bottom step of the main staircase with a lamp at her feet and a fire poker across her knees, keeping her own vigil, and she waved him off when he opened his mouth.

"Go and lie down, sir. I've got the stairs."

"It's my job to—"

"It's both our jobs tonight." She patted the step beside her. "Sit awhile, if you like. Storms are worse alone."

He sat. They listened together to the house groan around them like a ship, and neither spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the wind wasn't already saying better.

At some hour past three, Tamsin climbed back toward the east wing, past the landing where the rail waited with its loosened screws, and stopped.

Below him, faint through the roar of the storm, the front door bell rang.

Once. Twice. Three times, insistent, at three in the morning, on a night when every road to Blackthorn Grange had been under seawater since sundown.

Nell appeared at the foot of the stairs, lamp raised, her face drained white above the poker.

"No one can be out in that," she whispered. "No one *could* be."

The bell rang a fourth time.

And from along the corridor, unhurried, a door opened — and footsteps began to descend toward the hall.
