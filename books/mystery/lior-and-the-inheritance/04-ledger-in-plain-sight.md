## 4. Ledger in Plain Sight

The archive at St. Aldhelm's kept its own hours after dark, and Bram Winterbourne had long ago stopped pretending he didn't prefer them. By day the record room belonged to the parish — to Mrs. Tallow's vacuum cleaner droning through the nave like a persistent bee, to schoolchildren herded past the vestry on heritage tours, to the bishop's secretary telephoning about files that were always wanted yesterday. But at night the cathedral settled into itself. The radiators ticked and cooled. The stone drank up the last of the daylight and gave nothing back. And Bram sat at his desk beneath the one lamp he was permitted to leave burning, surrounded by five centuries of other people's business, and felt — as he had felt since he was a boy first allowed to touch the vellum with cotton gloves — that this was the closest a living man could come to sitting inside a held breath.

He had not touched either letter in three days.

They lay in the bottom drawer of his desk now, wrapped in acid-free tissue because even his suspicions deserved archival care, and every time he opened the drawer to retrieve something innocent — a spare nib, the tin of mints he pretended he didn't ration — he felt them there the way one feels a stone in a shoe. *I confess.* Two words, in a hand so controlled it might have been engraved. And beneath them, the promise: *find what hides in daylight, Mr. Winterbourne, and I will tell you the rest.*

*What hides in daylight.*

He had turned the phrase over like a coin whose face wouldn't resolve. Hiding places, properly understood, were dark things: safes, crypts, locked drawers, the spaces behind wainscoting where mice carried off the crumbs of history. An archivist knew darkness the way a fisherman knew water. But daylight — daylight was the enemy of concealment. Daylight was where you put something when you wanted it seen by everyone and read by no one.

Bram set down his pen, pulled the lamp closer, and went to fetch the donor ledger.

* * *

It lived on the open shelf by the reading table, because it was not, strictly speaking, an archival document at all. It was a public book — the great red-bound volume in which the cathedral recorded its benefactions, begun in 1889 and continued in a hand that changed decade by decade from copperplate to ballpoint to, latterly, Bram's own careful italic. Anyone could ask to see it. Tourists did, sometimes, idly, looking for their own surnames or for confirmation that wealth, once given, stayed given. School groups were shown the famous pages: the £10,000 from the Vellacott shipping family in 1911 that re-roofed the south transept; the anonymous gift of 1963, entered only as *A Friend of Music*, which bought the organ its new blower and occasioned forty years of speculation.

And there, in the final third of the volume, the Marlowe entries ran like a golden thread through the town's lean years. Edith Marlowe had been giving to St. Aldhelm's since before Bram took up his post — small gifts at first, then larger ones, then, after her husband's death, the great rolling bequests that had kept the roof on, the choir robed, the boiler lit, and — though no one said it aloud in Wetherholt — half the high street solvent. When the mill closed, it was Marlowe money that retrained the workers. When the cottage hospital lost its funding, a Marlowe wing rose over the old footprint. The town had named a bench after her, then a garden, then, after she died five years ago, the annual harvest festival itself. *Edith's Festival,* they called it now, without irony and without thought, the way one says the name of weather.

Bram carried the ledger to his desk and set it down with both hands, the way one handles a thing that has suddenly become evidence rather than furniture.

He began, deliberately, with the years he knew best — his own entries, the last decade — to warm his eye to the page. Then he worked backward. This was method, not superstition: read forward and you find what you expect; read backward and you find what you missed.

The amounts came at him in their patient procession. £250 for the Advent candles, 2016. £500 toward the guttering. £1,000, marked simply *for works*. He noted them without interest until, somewhere around the middle of the second hour, his eye snagged — the way an eye snags on a word repeated too often in a paragraph — on the shape of the numbers themselves.

£47. Then, two months later, £147. Then £247.

He sat very still.

Coincidence, said the reasonable voice, the voice that had served him well for thirty years of cataloguing and cross-referencing and gently correcting enthusiastic amateurs who believed every watermark was a secret map. Ledger-keepers round sums; a donor gives what she gives; the odd figure is the odd figure. He made himself continue backward, making himself bored, making himself disprove it.

But the pattern would not disprove itself.

Through 2014 and 2013 the trailing digits climbed in their quiet increments — £347, £447, £547 — each entry dated, each initialled *E.M.* in the margin in Edith Marlowe's upright, unornamented hand. Not all the gifts followed the sequence; there were ordinary round figures interleaved throughout, roof money, boiler money, honest money. But the strange ones came at intervals that were not quarterly, not seasonal, not tied to any feast in the calendar. They came, Bram realized with a slow cold settling in his stomach, at intervals of roughly six weeks. Irregular enough to pass unnoticed. Regular enough, once seen, to be unmistakable.

Six weeks. Or — he reached for the calendar pinned above the desk, the one marked with the cathedral's working year — sometimes seven. Sometimes five. Never aligned to anything liturgical.

He began writing the dates out on a fresh sheet, stripping away everything but the numbers, and it was while he was doing this — mechanically, the way one transcribes anything, the mind idling in low gear — that the other pattern surfaced.

The dates were days. Not gift-dates. *Days.*

He checked the first one against the parish diary, the daily log of services and works kept in the vestry and duplicated, thank God, in the archive. The date encoded in the first anomalous gift — £147, trailing digits 47 — fell on the fourth of July. The next, £247, on the twenty-fourth of August. The next on the seventh of November.

Fourth. Twenty-fourth. Seventh.

Not dates at all. *Initials.* The trailing hundreds digit and the trailing units digit, read together: D.G. A.M. G.N. He sat back, and the chair creaked in the silent room, and the sound seemed enormous.

Someone was being paid. Someone was being acknowledged, entry by entry, in a cipher so modest it hid in plain sight on a public shelf — initials disguised as shillings and pence, a name spelled out across three years in the language of generosity. And the final entry before the sequence broke off—

Bram turned the pages forward again, fast now, his heart going hard against his ribs, and found it where he had known it would be: the last of the strange gifts, entered the week before the crypt was sealed, the week before Edith Marlowe died.

**E.M. — £1,947.**

He stared at it.

E.M. Edith Marlowe. Of course. Every strange gift bore those initials; it meant nothing; it was simply how the ledger identified its donor. His eye slid off it, tried to slide off it —

— and caught.

Because the amount was not like the others. The others had been two-digit ciphers wearing the costume of currency: £147, £547, figures plausible enough for candle-money and guttering. But £1,947 was too large for the pattern and too precise for generosity. Nineteen hundred and forty-seven pounds. And if the last two digits were initials — 4, 7 — then the leading digits were not leading digits at all. They were a year.

Nineteen forty-seven.

Bram's mouth had gone dry. He wrote it out on the sheet, separating the components the way one separates a compound word to hear its parts: **1947 — D.G.** No. He checked the digit again, leaning close enough that his breath fogged the lamplight. One-nine-four-seven. Trailing pair: four, seven. D.G.

Except that E.M. stood in the margin, and E.M. was not D.G., and the whole elegant machine he had built in the last ten minutes shuddered on its axle. Unless — unless the initials in the margin were the disguise, and the amount was the truth. Unless every gift signed *E.M.* had been signing someone else's name in numbers. Unless Edith Marlowe had spent the last years of her life paying a person identified only as D.G., in sums dressed as donations, recorded in a book any stranger could read, because a book any stranger could read was the one place no one would ever think to look.

*What hides in daylight.*

Bram put both hands flat on the desk to stop them trembling. It was exhilaration, he told himself. It was only the old thrill, the same one that had come over him at nineteen when he'd first deciphered the marginal shorthand in the Vellacott account books — the sense of the world's lock turning under his fingers. He was an archivist. This was what archives were *for*: nothing written down is ever entirely hidden; paper keeps faith longer than people do.

But the feeling curdled even as he named it, because the next thought arrived unbidden and would not be sent away:

*I confess to the killing of a man in the crypt of St. Aldhelm's.*

A man. A specific man, killed in a sealed place, five years ago — the very week the strange gifts stopped. The very week the final entry read, in its buried grammar, *1947, D.G.* The very week the crypt's lock was changed.

He had checked that lock himself, two nights ago, with a torch and a bad conscience, telling himself it was routine maintenance. The new brass gleamed against stone gone black with a century of hands. Locks are replaced; cathedrals are full of replaced locks. But locks are also replaced *after* — after a breakage, after a loss, after a body.

Bram looked at the ledger entry for a long time. The lamp hummed. Somewhere overhead, in the dark triforium, a pigeon shifted and resettled, and the small sound fell into the silence like a stone into a well.

D.G. He had no idea who D.G. was. That was the honest truth of it, and he clung to the honesty the way a man clings to a rail in rough water. A cipher resolved is not a person found. He had letters, amounts, initials, a dead benefactor, a sealed crypt, and two anonymous confessions — and none of it yet amounted to knowledge, only to the terrible adjacency of knowledge, the sense of standing in a dark house with one's hand an inch from the light switch.

There was one more thing he could do tonight, and he made himself do it, because method was the only lantern he trusted. He went back through the ledger one final time, all the way back, past his own entries, past his predecessor's, into the copperplate of the 1990s, checking whether the cipher ran deeper than Edith Marlowe — whether some earlier hand had played the same game.

It had.

In 1988, in a different ink, a gift of £623, initialled R.V. In 1991, £826. In 1994, £119. He sat down slowly. The ledger was not a record of generosity. It was a correspondence — decades of it — conducted in the only language that never attracts attention: money, quietly given, publicly thanked, instantly forgotten.

And every correspondent, so far as he could see, had been a woman of substance giving to her church. Except that at least one of them — the one whose thread ended in a sealed crypt and a changed lock and a confession on his desk — had been saying something else entirely.

Bram closed the ledger. He did not return it to the open shelf. Instead he carried it to the cabinet of restricted holdings, unlocked it, laid the great red volume flat in the dark, and locked it again — a breach of protocol so small and so absolute that he heard himself justify it aloud in the empty room, in a whisper: *for safekeeping.*

Then he opened the bottom drawer, unwrapped the two letters from their tissue, and set them beside the sheet of figures on which the arithmetic of a hidden life lay exposed. Three documents. One hand? He looked at the confession's controlled engraving, at the promise's identical strokes, and then — newly, unwillingly — at the ledger's marginal initials, *E.M.*, upright and unornamented, and felt the ground tilt beneath the question he had not known he was asking. Whose hand had written *E.M.* in the margins? He had assumed Edith's. He had never once verified it.

He fetched the specimen file — the folder of signature cards and signed receipts every archivist keeps against fraud — and laid Edith Marlowe's authenticated hand beside the ledger's marginalia.

The letters matched. Of course they matched. The *E*s were identical, the *M*s identical, down to the small hesitation in the downstroke.

That was what frightened him. Because Bram Winterbourne had spent thirty years learning to read hands the way other men read faces, and the *E.M.* on the ledger page was not the writing of a woman in her eighties signing her final gifts. It was steady. It was young. It was the hand of someone practising an older woman's signature — and practising it well.

The lamp buzzed. Outside, Wetherholt slept under the fortune that kept it alive, streetlamps burning on Marlowe money, the hospital wing dark and warm on Marlowe money, the whole town dreaming in a house built on a ledger no one had ever thought to read.

Bram blew out the lamp and sat a moment in the dark, holding the edge of the desk.

Whoever D.G. was, someone had paid him — or paid *for* him — for years, in cipher, in a public book. And the last payment had been made the week a man died in the crypt and a nineteen-year-old heir collected a new key from the locksmith.

He needed to know who had been in Wetherholt in 1947. And there was exactly one person left alive who had tended Edith Marlowe's garden long enough to know everyone she had ever paid to forget.

At first light, Bram would go and see Silas Crane.
