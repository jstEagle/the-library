## 14. Agnes at the Door

Agnes Renner had spent sixty years learning the difference between a knock and a knock.

The postman knocked twice, briskly, already turning away. The vicar knocked like an apology. Children knocked with their fists because no one had taught them better. But this — three soft raps, spaced too carefully, followed by silence that had weight to it — this was a man who hoped she wouldn't answer.

She looked through the frosted glass of her front door and saw only a smudge of grey, tall and stooped, hunched against the drizzle as though the weather were his own fault.

Bram Winterbourne. Suspended archivist of St. Aldhelm's. The man who had come to her kitchen in spring asking gentle questions about Thomas, and whom she had sent away with tea and half-truths, and who had since become, according to the *Wetherholt Gazette*, the reason the restoration fund was frozen and three hundred pay packets hung in the balance.

She should not open the door. Every sensible instinct said so. Her sister in Taunton had said so on the telephone only that morning: *Agnes, love, that man is trouble wearing a cardigan.*

But Thomas had been trouble once, too, standing on this same step with rain in his hair and a scheme in his eyes, and she had opened the door for him every time he came.

She turned the latch.

He looked worse than he had in spring. Thinner through the face, greyer at the temples, and carrying — she noticed immediately, because she was a woman who noticed things, it was how she'd survived being a Renner — no bag, no coat over his arm despite the wet, nothing but himself and whatever he was holding behind his back.

"Mr. Winterbourne."

"Mrs. Renner." His voice was roughened, as if he hadn't used it much lately. "I'm sorry to come here. I know what they're saying about me."

"They're saying you burned down your own archive for the insurance." She let him stand in the damp a moment longer. "Which is nonsense, because you'd have made a better job of it if you had. That fire barely singed the curtains."

Something moved in his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first sign of life in him. "You saw the paper."

"I see everything. It's my chief occupation these days." She stepped back and held the door wide. "Well? Are you going to stand there dripping until the neighbours have something new to talk about, or are you coming in?"

* * *

Her kitchen was unchanged since spring, and Bram found that its sameness steadied him. The same blue willow pattern on the dresser. The same clock ticking with the patient insistence of a thing that had outlasted two wars and intended to outlast him. The same photograph on the mantel: Thomas at nineteen, in his verger's cassock, squinting into sunlight, caught mid-laugh by someone who must have been Agnes herself.

He set the bundle he'd been hiding behind his back on the table. His private file. Papers gone soft at the edges from handling, a notebook swollen with damp, photocopies curled like autumn leaves.

Agnes looked at it for a long moment before she spoke.

"You took it home," she said. "Before the fire."

"Yes."

"So when the record room went up, everything you'd found was safe in your house, and everything else is ash." She poured tea without asking, the way she did things — decisively, without negotiation. "And now the bishop thinks you did it, the town thinks you did it, and Julian Marlowe gets to stand about looking sorrowful while the fund stays frozen and everyone starves politely." She set his cup down hard enough to ring. "Sit down, Mr. Winterbourne. You look like a man who hasn't eaten since Tuesday."

He sat. He ate what she gave him — cheese and pickle, bread still warm, the ordinary miracle of it nearly undoing him after a week of service-station sandwiches and sleeping badly in his own house while strangers walked past his windows. He hadn't realised how hungry fear had made him until food appeared.

Agnes watched him eat with her arms folded, and when he'd finished she pulled out the chair opposite and sat, and folded her hands on the table between them like a woman settling in for a long siege.

"Now," she said. "Tell me why you've really come. And don't say the grounds records again, I'll pour this tea over you."

So he told her. Not all of it — he found himself holding back the encoded ledger, the arithmetic of payments, the shape of what Edith Marlowe had built — but most of it. The letters. Silas Crane's edelweiss. The gap in the burial registers where Thomas should have been. The locksmith's invoice, and Julian Marlowe, nineteen years old, collecting a new crypt key the morning after his grandmother's gardener stopped speaking to anyone but God.

Agnes listened without interrupting, which Bram would learn was her rarest gift. When he finished she was quiet for a while, looking at the photograph of her brother on the mantel.

"He never got a letter from Canada," she said at last. "I told you that in spring. Everyone said he'd write when he settled, and I believed them for about a year, and then I stopped believing and started counting." She turned her teacup slowly on its saucer. "Forty-one years, Mr. Winterbourne. I've been counting forty-one years, and everyone in this town has been very kind about my poor brother who ran off, and very careful never to meet my eye while they said it."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She fixed him with a look that had cowed schoolmasters and clergymen alike. "You said the crypt was locked with a new lock the week he vanished. You said young Julian collected the key himself. What you haven't said is whether you think that boy killed my Thomas."

Bram looked down at his hands. "I don't know."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." He heard his own voice crack on it, and pressed on before the crack could widen. "Silas Crane says Thomas fell. Says there was a confrontation over Mrs. Marlowe's ledgers, and Thomas fell, and Edith ordered the crypt sealed until God decided what to do about it. Silas carried the body down because—" He stopped.

"Because he loved her," Agnes said flatly. "Men do stupid things for love and call it loyalty afterwards. Go on."

"And Julian collected the key. Either because his grandmother told him to, and he was a frightened boy doing as he was bid — or because there was no boy about it at all." Bram rubbed his eyes. "His alibi, if you can call it that, is his own grief. Everyone remembers him weeping at the funeral. Nobody remembers where he was the night Thomas disappeared. Grief makes a wonderful curtain."

Agnes was silent again. Then she stood, went to the dresser, and took down a tin — a biscuit tin, faded roses on the lid — and brought it back to the table.

"My sister thinks I keep sewing things in here," she said. "I keep everything in here. Receipts, letters, the coroner's report on our mother, my wedding invitation to a man who died at Normandy." She lifted the lid and sorted through the paper archaeology of a life until she found what she wanted: a small spiral notebook, its cover soft with age. "Thomas's. He kept lists of everything. Jobs done, candles stocked, who owed the vestry sixpence. He was that sort of man. Orderly." Her voice caught, just slightly, and smoothed itself. "This is from his last month."

She pushed it across the table. Bram opened it with hands that were not quite steady.

The entries were in a neat, upright hand — times, amounts, initials. And there, in the final week, wedged between *polish brass — nave* and *order altar wine*, a line that made the hair rise on Bram's arms:

*Sat. Late. Lady M. night business again. Car at gate past 1. Told A. nothing.*

"Night business," Agnes repeated, watching his face. "That's what he wrote. I've read that page four hundred times and I still don't know what it means. But you do, don't you? I can see it in you."

"The ledger," Bram said quietly. "Payments to Silas Crane, dated on nights when the Marlowe accounts were supposedly audited. Whatever happened in that crypt, it wasn't the first strange night at that house. Thomas had been watching. He wrote down what he saw, and I think—" He stopped, because saying it aloud made it real. "I think watching is what killed him."

Agnes nodded slowly, unsurprised, and Bram understood that she had known, in the way that people who are lied to for decades always know — not the facts, but the fact of lying, its temperature, its weight in the air.

"There's something else," she said. "Since you've shown me yours." She refilled his cup though it was nearly full, an old hostess reflex, buying time. "That week Thomas went missing — the last week anyone saw him — I couldn't sleep. I was twenty-three and worried sick and I lay awake listening for his footsteps on the lane. And around one in the morning, maybe later, I heard a car go past. Slow. Then stop. Then idle." She looked at the window, at the rain. "We didn't get many cars idling outside our cottage at one in the morning, Mr. Winterbourne. Ours didn't run. So I got up and looked through the curtain, and I saw it parked down by the crypt gate. Big dark car, engine running, lights off. Someone sitting in it. Waiting."

"Did you see who?"

"No. Too dark, and I wasn't brave enough to go and look." Her mouth twisted. "But I knew the car. Everyone in Wetherholt knew that car. It was the Marlowe Daimler. Old Mrs. Marlowe's own car, with the little silver bird on the bonnet. And it sat there by the crypt gate, engine running, for the best part of an hour, and then it drove off slow, the way you drive when you don't want to wake anybody."

Bram sat very still. "Who was driving?"

"I assumed the chauffeur. Who else?" She met his eyes. "But I've had forty-one years to wonder, and here's what I wonder now. A chauffeur runs errands. A chauffeur doesn't sit outside a sealed crypt at one in the morning with the engine running unless someone inside the car has told him to wait. And the person who gave the orders in that family — well. She was seventy-eight and frail, but she was never frail in the ways that mattered." She paused. "Or the orders came from someone younger. Someone who'd just learned what his family was, and what it cost, and who'd do anything — *anything* — to keep it from costing more."

"Julian was nineteen."

"Nineteen-year-olds have driven cars for a hundred years, Mr. Winterbourne. And grief-stricken ones drive fastest of all."

* * *

Later — much later, the clock having ticked its way past ten and the rain having settled in for the night — Agnes made up the spare bed with sheets that smelled of lavender and cedar, and Bram stood in the doorway of her guest room feeling the strangeness of it like a second skin.

"I can't stay," he said. "If anyone sees—"

"If anyone sees, they'll say poor Agnes Renner has taken in that poor Winterbourne man, and about time somebody did." She handed him a hot water bottle wrapped in flannel, briskly, as if he were twelve. "You're suspended, not contagious. And you shouldn't be alone tonight. Men alone with their thoughts do foolish things. I've buried enough of them to know."

He almost smiled. Then it faded, and he stood there holding the bottle, and what came out of him next came out unplanned, the way true things do.

"Mrs. Renner — Agnes. Can I tell you something I haven't told anyone?"

She waited.

"I'm not sure I want to find the truth anymore." The words seemed to surprise even him; he said the rest quickly, before he could take them back. "When I started, it was simple. A letter, a mystery, a wrong to right. Thomas deserved a name on a stone. But now—" He looked down the narrow hallway toward the kitchen, toward the town beyond it, invisible in the dark but utterly present, the way Wetherholt was always present, breathing at the edges of everything. "The fund pays the wages of half this street. It pays the roof on the primary school. It pays Mrs. Pargeter's heart medication and the bus service to Millbrook and the flood defences that keep the lower lanes dry. Three hundred jobs. If what I found goes where truth is supposed to go — courts, newspapers, the bishop's lawyers — the endowment freezes permanently. Maybe dissolves. And this town doesn't survive that. I've done the arithmetic. I'm an archivist; arithmetic is what I *do*. There isn't enough town left after it."

Agnes said nothing for a long moment. The clock ticked. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Do you know what I did when Thomas disappeared?" she said finally. "I went to Edith Marlowe. Nineteen years old, straight up the drive to the big house, and asked to see her. She received me in that drawing room with the yellow silk chairs, and I asked her — I was rude, I was frightened, I asked her outright if she knew where my brother was." Agnes's voice was level, but her knuckles had whitened on the doorframe. "And she looked at me for a long time. Long enough that I saw her decide something. And then she said, *'My dear, some doors are locked because there's nothing behind them worth finding.'* And she had a cheque written for fifty pounds — fifty pounds, in 1958, to a shop girl — and told me it was a reward for information, and that I should use it to travel to Canada myself if I liked, and look for him."

"Did you take it?"

"No." Something flickered across her face — pride, regret, both wearing the same coat. "I was too proud. And I've wondered ever since which of us that cheque was meant for. Whether it was kindness, or hush money, or a test, or all three. Because here's the thing, Mr. Winterbourne." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped, not to a whisper but to the register people use for truths. "Edith Marlowe built the hospital wing. She built the swimming baths and the library and the roof over your cathedral and the wage packet in every pocket on this street. And she did it with money that — whatever else it was — passed through a dead man's keeping. This town has been eating off that grave for forty years. Me included. My pension comes from the charity trust. My sister's care home is paid from it. Half of me wants you to burn the whole rotten thing down and the other half knows I'd freeze with it."

She held out her hand, palm up, an old gesture, half plea and half challenge.

"So don't ask me whether you should want the truth. Ask yourself what it's *for*. Truth that feeds nobody is just another kind of hunger. Truth that keeps three hundred families alive might be worth its price — or it might be the most expensive lie ever told, and we're all of us paying instalments." She closed her fingers slowly. "I waited forty-one years to know what happened to my brother. Now I'm frightened of knowing. Both things are true. Welcome to being a person, Mr. Winterbourne. It doesn't get easier."

Bram took her hand. Her grip was stronger than it looked.

"Thank you," he said. "For the bed. For the notebook. For—" He gestured vaguely at everything: the kitchen, the kindness, the terrible clarity of her.

"Thank me by not deciding tonight," she said. "Deciding tired is how men ruin things. Sleep on it. And tomorrow—" She paused, and something shifted in her face, a thought arriving late but arriving whole. "Tomorrow, you should know: I wrote to Julian Marlowe last week. After the fund froze. An old woman's letter, telling him what I've just told you — that I remember the car, that I've always remembered the car, that I'd rather hear it from him than from a courtroom."

Bram went cold. "Agnes. What did he say?"

"He hasn't answered." She turned toward the kitchen to bank the fire, and her voice drifted back, matter-of-fact and terrible. "But he telephoned this afternoon, while you were standing in the rain. Said he'd like to call round. Tomorrow evening." She glanced at Bram over her shoulder, and her eyes in the lamplight were bright and unafraid and very, very tired. "Said there was something he'd been carrying a long while, and he thought it was time I heard it. So whatever you decide about truth, Mr. Winterbourne — I'd make up your mind by tomorrow. Because I rather think it's coming to find us either way."

* * *

Bram did not sleep.

He lay in the lavender-smelling dark under Agnes's spare quilt and listened to the rain, and the file of papers sat on the chair by the door like a sleeping animal he didn't dare approach. Through the thin wall he could hear Agnes moving below — the clink of a cup washed and set down, the small domestic sounds of a woman who had waited forty-one years and could wait one more night, though he suspected she was not moving slowly for patience's sake but because her hands, like his, needed something to do.

Once, near two o'clock, a car passed along the lane. Slow. He was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, heart hammering, at the gap in the curtains — but it was only the milk float making its rounds, headlights sweeping the ceiling, gone again. Ordinary. Harmless. The town going about its survival.

He thought of Julian Marlowe kneeling somewhere in the dark, weeding a grave-shaped flowerbed. He thought of Silas Crane dying by inches with an edelweiss on his sill. He thought of a cheque for fifty pounds and a locked door and a woman in yellow silk deciding, in one long look, what a grieving sister could bear to know.

*Some doors are locked because there's nothing behind them worth finding.*

And some, he thought, staring at the ceiling, are locked because of exactly what's behind them — and the lock was changed the week everybody started needing what the lie paid for.

Downstairs, the clock struck two. In less than twenty-four hours, Julian Marlowe would walk through Agnes Renner's door carrying whatever he had carried for forty-one years.

Bram lay back down and did not close his eyes.
