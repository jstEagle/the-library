## 8. Where the River Keeps Its Dead

The ledger's last entry was a set of coordinates, written in a hand so small it looked like an apology.

*Mill race, north bank, forty paces from the old sluice gate.* Beneath it, a date — two days before the funeral — and a single letter: *L.*

Theo spread the page flat on my kitchen table at three in the morning, his finger trembling against the paper. "The Hollis Mill closed in '89. Nobody's touched it since."

"Which is why nobody found him," I said. "And why somebody's been paying taxes on it for thirty years under a holding company."

I'd traced the holding company in twenty minutes. I always remembered numbers — account numbers, parcel numbers, the tax ID that looped back through two shell entities to a trust whose signature line bore the same looping *W* I'd seen on four forged burial documents. The Whitmore hand. The sheriff's family. The fourth chair, empty in every photograph, holding the deed to the ground where Lior Ashford had been sleeping all along.

"We go at dawn," Theo said.

"No," I said. "We go at dawn *with witnesses*. We photograph everything, we call the state archaeologist's office before we set foot on the property, we timestamp—"

"Juniper." He said my name the way you'd steady a ladder. "If we call anyone official, the call gets made, and by noon there's a backhoe on that site and a story about foundation work. We've seen what these people do with paperwork. They're better at it than the state will ever be."

He was right, and I hated it. I had spent my whole life believing that documents were armor — that if you wrote it down correctly, in the right order, with the right signatures, the truth would hold its shape. But I'd spent three weeks learning how these people used documents. They didn't break them. They *made* them. My mother had made one, once, or been made to, and it had cost her everything.

So we went alone, with a rented ground-penetrating radar unit in the back of Theo's truck and my camera around my neck, and I told myself it wasn't recklessness. It was evidence.

* * *

The Hollis Mill sat where the river bent north, a long gray skeleton of timber and rusted tin, its wheel long since collapsed into a fan of spokes half-swallowed by black water. The millrace still ran — a concrete channel choked with alder and silt, but running, because the river never stopped running, not for anyone.

Theo carried the radar like it was a sleeping child. I walked the ground first, photographing the approach, the gate, the padlock — new brass on a door that should have had none. I photographed the lock. I photographed the tire ruts in the mud, still holding rainwater, still holding the tread pattern of a truck with mud tires. I photographed the sky, overcast and featureless, because a featureless sky is still a timestamp when you're me.

"The sluice gate's that way," Theo said. "Forty paces."

We counted them together, out loud, like children. At forty we stood in a clearing where the alders had been cut back — recently, the stumps still pale — around a rectangle of ground that looked, to any eye, like nothing at all.

To the radar, it looked like everything.

I'd watched the technician run the machine twice in a training session six years ago, and my memory held the sequence the way it held everything: sweep, overlap, sweep. Theo handled the unit with the same careful hands he used for the ledger. The screen bloomed in grays.

First pass: nothing but river cobbles and old foundations.

Second pass, two meters east: an anomaly. A rectangular void, roughly six feet by two, at a depth of four feet.

Third pass confirmed it. And inside the void — I will never forget the shape of it, because I cannot forget anything — a human outline. Folded. On its side. Knees drawn up, the way you'd place a body if you were in a hurry and the box you'd promised everyone was already empty and buried under a marble stone in the Witness cemetery.

"Oh God," Theo said quietly. "That's him. That's — he's been *here*. Thirty years. While we put flowers on an empty—"

"Theo." I took his wrist. "Look at me. We're not done. Photograph the screen. Photograph the unit. Photograph the ground, the gate, the lock, the ruts. Then we call everyone. State police, archaeologist, the *press* if we have to. But first we make it undeniable."

His hands shook, and then they didn't. He photographed. I photographed. The radar hummed. The river ran. And I felt the thing I'd been chasing since the coffin came up light — the moment where the story stopped being a suspicion and became a fact with a body attached to it.

That was when I heard the water change.

* * *

You learn to hear water, growing up in a river town. You learn its ordinary voice — the low conversation it has with its banks, the gurgle at the culverts. What I heard from upstream was none of those things. It was a roar arriving, still distant, like a train you can't see yet.

"The headgate," Theo said, and his face went white. "Someone opened the headgate. The old flume — it still feeds the race when they release upstream —"

"How much water?"

"Enough. Juniper, *run*."

We ran. Not back toward the truck — the truck sat on the low side of the race channel, and the channel was about to become a river again for the first time in thirty years. Theo grabbed my arm and hauled me toward the mill itself, up the collapsed loading ramp, through a doorway whose door had rotted to lace. Behind us the roar arrived.

I turned once, at the doorway, and I saw it come — a brown wall of water shouldering down the concrete race, carrying thirty years of silt and branches and one of our radar cases, snatched up like a toy. It hit the clearing where we'd stood and swallowed it. Four feet down. The water would take the site down to six, maybe eight. Whatever was in that ground would be scoured, scattered, *moved* — and by tomorrow there would be a story about a fluke release, a dam maintenance error, an unfortunate accident at an abandoned mill, and if two investigators happened to drown in it, well. The river keeps its dead. That's practically the town motto.

We stood in the dark of the mill, soaked to the knees, listening to the water do its work.

"They knew we were here," Theo said. "The lock. The ruts. They were watching."

"Or they were already coming," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "Theo — the headgate is a mile upstream. Someone drove there and opened it *while we were sweeping*. They gave us twenty minutes to finish and leave. Or not leave."

He looked at me. "They let us start."

"They wanted to know how much we had." I lifted the camera. "Now they know. Which means, from this minute on, they know exactly what they have to do to us."

* * *

We came out the far side of the mill, through the loading doors, and climbed the bluff through alder tangles while the flood thundered below. My hands were steady. That was the strange part. My whole body had gone calm the way it does in a claim file when the fraud is finally legible — when every forged line snaps into the same sentence.

At the top of the bluff, in the gray light, I did what I do.

I photographed the flooded race. The headgate road. The tire ruts at the gate, now half-drowned but still legible — I could read the tread pattern from here, and I would still be able to read it in thirty years. I photographed Theo, mud to the eyebrows, holding the radar's surviving data card above his head like a match struck in a cave. The unit itself was gone. The screen images were gone.

The card was not.

"Backup," Theo said, breathless, half laughing, half something darker. "I made a backup the first pass. I always— my grandfather taught me that. *Always* make the copy before anyone can take the original."

"Then we have him," I said. "Depth, position, outline. Timestamped, geotagged, and duplicated to the cloud before they opened that headgate — because *I* make the copy too, Theo. I always make the copy."

We stood on the bluff, two drenched people holding a plastic memory card between us, and I felt the exhale come up from somewhere below my ribs — terror and triumph in the same breath, indistinguishable. They had tried to drown the truth and instead they'd signed it. A flood at the exact hour of our sweep was not an accident. It was a confession with a timestamp.

"They'll know we got out," Theo said. "They'll know we have the images."

"Yes."

"So what happens now?"

I looked down at the river, brown and churning, already smoothing itself back into innocence. Somewhere under that water, or scattered along a mile of bank, was Lior Ashford — and the people who put him there had just told us, in the only language they had left, how afraid they were.

"Now," I said, "we stop investigating in the dark."

The centennial gala was in six days. The whole town would be in one room. Every founder's descendant, every forged signature's heir, the sheriff, the mayor, the lawyers with their envelopes.

I put the camera away and started walking toward the truck.

"Now we do it in the light."
