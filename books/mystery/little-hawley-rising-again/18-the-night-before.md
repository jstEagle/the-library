## 18. The Night Before

The manor kept its own hours, and past midnight it stopped pretending to be a house at all. It became a body — breathing through the radiators, ticking in its joints, sighing in the walls where the plaster had begun to let go of its secrets. Juniper had worked in old buildings her whole career. She knew how they spoke. But Hawley Manor spoke in Cordelia's voice now, and every creak sounded like a woman saying *hurry*.

The study had become theirs over the past week without either of them deciding it. Wren had claimed the desk first, spreading her mother's gardening journals across the green leather in a fan of water-stained spines. Juniper had claimed the floor, because the floor was where the documents lived — the ledger, the dredged diary, the decoded confession, Crane's last letter, each one laid out in careful chronological order on sheets of butcher paper like a body on a table. Between them, on a card table dragged in from the morning room, sat the evidence of eleven years: photographs, timelines, a transcription of Mrs. Vane's testimony taken down in Juniper's smallest, most defensible hand.

Outside, the last of the greenhouse smoke had finally washed out of the sky. The rain had come in behind the fire, as if the night itself were trying to scrub the ash away, and now it fell against the tall windows in long silver threads.

"Read me the timeline again," Wren said. She had her mother's journal open on her lap but wasn't reading it. She'd been holding the same page for twenty minutes. Juniper had learned to read Wren the way she read documents — not the words, but the pressure of the hand behind them.

"April the second. Cordelia withdraws two hundred pounds from the estate account — the last of the ledger's entries. April the third, she writes the twin letters and gives them to Mrs. Vane with instructions that make no sense until now. April the fourth, she argues with someone at the pond — Vane heard the voices, Edmund heard the raised tone, and Tomas saw Alistair's study light burning until dawn. April the fifth, she is officially dead. Crane signs the certificate he had no right to sign." Juniper ran her finger down the line of paper. "And April the ninth, she buys a platform ticket at Little Hawley station, alive, four days after her own funeral."

"Alive," Wren said softly. "She was *alive*, and I stood at her grave in a dress that choked me, and everyone told me to be brave."

"I know."

"You don't. You can't." Wren closed the journal, not angrily — carefully, the way you close a door on a room you're not ready to enter. "But you're the only person in this house who's even trying to understand it, so I'll forgive you the platitude."

Juniper almost smiled. "That's the kindest thing anyone's said to me all week."

"It wasn't kind. It was accurate. There's a difference." But Wren's mouth had softened at the corner, and in the lamplight her eyes were the same grey-green as the pond in the photographs, the pond they'd dredged by lantern light while Edmund's footsteps circled the hedges like a bad conscience. "Again. From the top. If we're going to do this tomorrow, I want it seamless. I want it so airtight that Reginald suffocates on it."

Juniper began again. She was halfway through Crane's letter — *I chose duty to the living over justice for the dead, and I have hated myself with a thoroughness that would impress you* — when she heard Wren set the journal down and cross the room.

"Juniper."

She looked up. Wren was standing over her with the lamp behind her, so her face was half shadow and half gold, and her hands were doing the thing they did when she was frightened — folding, unfolding, finding work that didn't exist.

"Say the rest tomorrow," Wren said. "Tonight I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer it without a lawyer in the room."

* * *

Wren had rehearsed this. She would admit that later, in the privacy of her own head, where she admitted most things: she had rehearsed it in the bath, in the dark of her childhood bedroom with its unfamiliar-new wallpaper, walking the east corridor at three in the morning while the house groaned around her. She had rehearsed it the way she'd once rehearsed telling her father she was leaving for Lisbon — every sentence polished, every exit planned.

And now, standing over Juniper Eastwick with the evidence of a murder spread at her feet like autumn leaves, every rehearsed word had gone out of her head.

Juniper was looking up at her with those patient, careful eyes, waiting. That was the thing about her. Everyone else in Wren's life had always been reaching — grasping, demanding, taking. Juniper *waited*. She made waiting look like the most generous act in the world.

"Sit down," Wren said, because she couldn't do this with Juniper looking up at her. "Please."

Juniper gathered herself off the floor and sat on the threadbare sofa beneath the window, and Wren sat beside her, and the distance between them was the width of a legal pad and eleven years of Wren's own cowardice.

"Tomorrow," Wren said, "when you stand up at that dinner and say what you're going to say — Reginald will destroy you. Not maybe. *Will*. He told me as much on Tuesday, in that voice he uses, the one that sounds like a bank manager delivering bad news he secretly enjoys. Your license. Your firm. Crane's name dragged through whatever mud he can find, and Crane's name is the last thing of yours that still matters, isn't it? The thing you're still trying to redeem."

Juniper's jaw tightened. "He can try."

"He won't try. He'll *succeed*. He's had thirty years of practice at succeeding in rooms like that one." Wren turned to face her fully, drawing her knees up, making herself small in a way she despised and couldn't help. "So I'm asking you. Don't come. Give me the documents. Give me everything — the diary, the ledger, the timeline, Vane's statement. I'll stand up. I'll say it. I'm his daughter; I have standing you'll never have. And you keep your license, and your firm, and his good name, and you walk out of this story the way you walked into it. Invisible. Untouched."

The silence that followed was so complete Wren could hear the rain finding its separate rhythms — the window, the drainpipe, the pond, somewhere far off in the dark, filling and filling.

"Invisible," Juniper repeated at last. She said the word the way a doctor says the name of a disease.

"It's not an insult. It's a survival strategy. I've used it myself. Lisbon was invisible. Ten years of postcards and wire transfers and never once saying the word *murder* out loud — that was invisible, and it kept me breathing." Wren's voice cracked and she hated it, and pushed through anyway. "I'm offering you what I never had. An exit."

"I know exactly what you're offering me." Juniper's hands were folded in her lap, and Wren noticed — had been noticing for weeks, and noticing that she noticed — how expressive those hands were, how they argued and conceded and held their ground all on their own. "You're offering me the thing I built my whole life to have. The safe distance. The clean hands. The life where I stand at the edge of other people's catastrophes and file the paperwork."

"Isn't that what you want?"

"No." It came out of her quiet and absolute, like a stone dropping into that pond. "No, and I think you know it isn't, and I think that's why you're really asking. Not to protect me. Because you're afraid that if I stand up tomorrow, I stop being the woman who arrived here with a briefcase, and becomes the woman who burned her life down standing next to you. And then you'd have to decide what you feel about that woman. And you'd have to decide it out loud."

* * *

The rain had thickened. Juniper listened to it and felt the strangest sensation — that she had been holding her breath for eleven days, since the certified letter first arrived with its impossible date, and that Wren had just pressed a thumb to the centre of her sternum and said *breathe*.

She was right. That was the unbearable thing. Wren was right about all of it — about Reginald, about the disciplinary inquiry that would come regardless, about the way Juniper had spent her entire adult life perfecting the art of not being seen. Aldous Crane had taught her that too, though he'd never named the lesson. *The best lawyer in the room is the one they forget is in the room*, he used to say, pouring the tea he always poured, wearing the grey suits he always wore, burying a murder he always — no. Not always. Once. Once, and then forever.

She had built herself in his image and called it integrity.

"Tell me about the invisible life," Wren said. Not a challenge. An invitation. Her voice had changed — the sharpness set down like a weapon she'd decided, for tonight, not to need. "The real one. Not the CV."

Juniper looked at her hands. "I live above a stationer's on Fetter Lane. Third floor. The window looks at a brick wall, which I used to tell people I preferred, because light is a distraction." A breath of a laugh, entirely without humour. "I eat the same breakfast every day. I take the same route to chambers. I have a standing order at the same café, and the woman there knows my order but not my name. I've been invited to four weddings in the last six years and attended none of them. I have a photograph of Aldous on my desk and no photographs of anyone else anywhere in the flat. When I imagine the future, I imagine it as a well-organised filing cabinet, and I have never once opened the drawer marked *want*."

The words kept coming, quiet and terrible, a confession she hadn't known she was carrying.

"Until this house. Until a letter arrived from a dead woman, and I got on a train, and I don't think I've breathed properly since." She turned her head. Wren was very close, and the lamplight had found the wet at the corner of her eye, and Juniper thought: *this is the drawer. This whole catastrophic, beautiful, ruinous fortnight — this is what was in the drawer.* "You asked me not to come to the dinner. And what I heard was: go back behind the brick wall. Go back to being the woman they forget is in the room. And I can't. I did it once already — I did it for Aldous, I did it for eleven years without even knowing I was doing it — and I am not going to sit in a chair tomorrow and watch you carry this alone while I keep my clean hands. I'd rather lose the license. I'd rather lose all of it."

"Juniper—"

"Let me finish, because I've spent two weeks watching you be brave and I've been a coward about exactly one thing." Her heart was going like the rain against the glass. "I didn't stay at Hawley for the will. I didn't stay for the mystery, or for Cordelia, or even for Crane. I stayed for the way you say *accurate* instead of *kind*. For the way you read your mother's journals like they were letters to you specifically. For the fact that when you're frightened, you get *more* precise, not less. I have spent my whole life invisible, Wren, and then you looked at me — really looked, the way you look at evidence — and I have not wanted to be invisible for one single day since. So no. I won't stay away tomorrow. I'm done hiding. You are the first thing I have ever chosen in the open, and I'm not going to un-choose you in front of a dinner party."

The rain filled the silence. Wren didn't move, and Juniper's courage, which had carried her further than it had ever carried her in her life, began — quietly, professionally, like a lawyer watching her own case collapse — to prepare its retreat.

Then Wren reached out and took her hand. Not gently. With the grip of a woman who had spent ten years not touching anyone and had just decided to make up for it.

"You absolute, infuriating, *accurate* woman," Wren said, and her voice broke clean through, and she was laughing and crying in the same breath, which Juniper would learn was simply how Wren did the most important things. "I rehearsed a whole speech. I had *exits planned*. And you've just — you've stood up in the middle of my own argument and won it."

"I'm a lawyer. It's what we do at dinners."

Wren laughed again, wet and helpless, and then she leaned across the width of the legal pad that had separated them all night, and kissed her.

It was not a gentle kiss. It was the kiss of two people who had been circling a truth for eleven days the way they'd circled the truth about Cordelia — respectfully, methodically, terrified of what it would cost — and had finally, at three in the morning on the eve of everything, simply picked it up. Juniper's hand found the back of Wren's neck, and Wren's fingers tightened in hers hard enough to leave marks, and somewhere in the walls the old house sighed, long and low, like a held breath finally released.

When they broke apart, Wren kept her forehead against Juniper's.

"If we do this," she whispered, "we do it in the open. All of it. No more exits."

"All of it," Juniper said. "I've had enough of invisible."

* * *

They worked until the sky went from black to the bruised grey of a bruise healing. Wren transcribed; Juniper cross-referenced. The diary's final entries went into the folder in Cordelia's own hand — *He knows I know. If I drown, look to the pond* — followed by the ledger, the station photograph, Vane's statement, Edmund's confession about the burning papers, Crane's letter, and the second will. Juniper made three copies of everything and hid them in three places, because eleven years of other people's secrets had taught her that evidence, like truth, survives best when it isn't kept in one heart.

At some point Wren fell asleep sitting up, her head tipping onto Juniper's shoulder, the gardening journal sliding closed in her lap. Juniper sat very still and let her, and watched the rain stop, and watched the first thin light come up over the ruined greenhouse and the black water of the pond beyond it.

Tomorrow — today — the family would gather. Reginald would arrive polished and venomous. Edmund would come sober, which was its own kind of testimony. Alistair would be wheeled in against every medical warning, dying and lucid and unreadable. And at the toast, when the glasses rose to a dead woman everyone had agreed to misremember, Juniper Eastwick would stand up in front of all of them and stop being invisible forever.

She looked down at Wren, asleep against her shoulder, her mother's journal rising and falling with her breath.

On the card table, the twin letters sat where they had sat all night — the confession and the promise, side by side in Cordelia's coded hand. *He knows I know.* And beneath it, decoded at last: *to whoever finishes what was started, more than money.*

Juniper understood, in the grey hush before the household woke, that the promise had never been about the estate at all.

Down the corridor, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Someone in this house was already awake — and had been, Juniper thought, for some time.

The anniversary dinner was at seven.
