## 2. Alphabetized Secrets

The Crane & Associates archive occupied the entire lower floor of the building on Wren Street, and it was, Silas liked to say, the only honest room in Hawley.

He said it as a joke, mostly. But there was truth under it, the way there was truth under most of his jokes — a thin vein of ore he'd stopped mining years ago because the shaft went too deep. The archive did not flatter anyone. It did not forget a debt or soften a grudge or lose a deed because a family's name had grown inconvenient. It held what it held: forty-one years of wills, trusts, settlements, quiet transfers of land between quiet hands, all of it filed in his own system, which was not alphabetical so much as liturgical. Voss belonged between Vollmer and Wade, third drawer from the floor, second cabinet from the east wall, where the light from the boiler room vent fell at four o'clock in a long amber blade across the labels.

Silas descended the stairs at seven-forty that evening with Margaret Voss's retainer check folded in his breast pocket like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

Triple the fee. Paid in advance. *Prove my son will not be murdered.*

He had taken stranger commissions in his time — Hawley was a town that generated strange commissions the way marshes generated mist — but never one shaped quite like this. A prophecy, essentially. A woman had walked into his office carrying a date, a method, and a name, and asked him to unmake the future with paperwork. And beneath her certainty, beneath the tremor in her hands and the oxygen tube looped over her ears, there had been something else. Something he had recognized without wanting to.

She had looked at him the way people look at a lock they already know how to pick.

The stairwell smelled of cold plaster and old toner. He keyed the code into the archive door — 4-1-7-2, unchanged since 1998, because Silas Crane believed that a man who changed his locks announced to the world that he had something worth stealing — and the fluorescent tubes stuttered awake one after another down the length of the room, illuminating row upon row of gray-green cabinets like a congregation rising for the sermon.

He stood a moment in the doorway, listening to the building settle. Rain had started again outside, a fine persistent needle-rain that had been falling since October began, as if the sky itself were trying to wash Hawley clean of something and failing, season after season.

*Voss,* he thought. *V-O-S-S.*

His feet knew the way before his mind committed to it. That was the thing about forty-one years in one room: the body became an index. He crossed to the east wall, knelt — his knees registered their annual complaint — and drew open the drawer marked V–W.

Vollmer, Harold. Estate, 2009. Vollmer, Harold. Trust amendment, 2013. Vollmer, Ingrid. Then the hanging folders marched on in their neat manila rhythm, each tab aligned to the millimeter, each label typed on his 1988 IBM Wheelwriter in Courier Twelve, because Silas Crane did not trust labels written by hand; handwriting could be forged, but a typewriter's quirks were a fingerprint.

Vollmer, Ingrid. Vogel. Voight.

And then — nothing. Then Wade, Thomas. Estate, 2016.

Silas knelt there in the amber blade of the boiler-room light and felt the temperature of the room change, though the thermometer on the wall would have told him otherwise.

There should have been a folder between Voight and Wade. He could feel the absence the way a dentist feels a missing tooth with his tongue — not by seeing it but by the wrongness of the space, the way the remaining folders leaned fractionally toward the gap, starved of their neighbor's pressure. There should have been a folder there, and not just any folder. His memory, which he kept the way other men kept wine cellars — dark, cool, undisturbed — produced the image whole: a green folder, not manila, because in 2014 he had still been using the green stock for probate-adjacent matters before the supplier discontinued it. A green folder, tab reading VOSS, [REDACTED], ESTATE MATTER / CONFIDENTIAL SETTLEMENT, and beneath the tab, in his own handwriting on the intake card — his own, because some things he had never trusted to the machine —

He stopped himself.

Because that was the detail that would not stay submerged. The intake card for the Voss matter of 2014 had carried a name struck through in his own hand. Not whited out. Not torn away. Struck through with a single ruler-straight line of black ink, the kind of redaction a careful man performs when he wants the document to remain legible in every respect except the one that matters. He remembered the pen. A Parker Jotter, blue-black ink, the one with the hairline crack in the cap that leaked a smudge onto his ring finger every third Friday. He remembered the sound the ruler made against the cardstock.

He remembered doing it.

That was the horror of it, arriving not all at once but in stages, like cold water filling a boot. Anyone could have removed a folder from this room — the sheriff's office had keys, the cleaning service had keys, once upon a time even Eleanor Ashcombe's late husband had possessed a key, God rest his scheming soul. But no one else could have made *this* gap look like this. The remaining folders were squared and flush. The drawer ran on its rails without a hitch. Whoever pulled the Voss file had then re-seated the surviving folders with the same compulsive precision that Silas brought to everything, had closed the drawer on runners that he personally lubricated twice a year with a silicone cloth purchased from Hollis Hardware, and had left behind a silence so perfectly tailored to the room that only the room's author could hear it singing false.

Someone had edited his archive in his own hand.

Or — and here the thought turned over like a fish showing its pale belly — someone had edited his archive *as* him. With his codes, his habits, his silicone cloth, his ruler-struck line.

Silas rose, knees protesting louder now, and stood looking down at the drawer as a man looks down a well. The rain ticked against the high little window near the ceiling, the one that hadn't opened since the Clinton administration. Somewhere in the walls the boiler knocked twice and went quiet, embarrassed.

He did what he always did when the world misbehaved: he reached for the record.

His daily log lived on a shelf beside the sink in the archive's corner kitchenette — a series of black marbled composition books, one per year, going back to 1983, each page ruled and dated in his small upright hand. Access logs. Maintenance logs. A note of every person who had entered this room below the rank of partner, which was to say, below the rank of himself. If a folder had left this drawer, the leaving would have disturbed something — a dust shadow on the folder's neighbors, a shift in the drawer's weight, an entry missing from the log.

He found the 2014 volume and opened it to October.

The entry was there. Of course it was there. October 14th, 2014, 9:12 p.m., in his own hand: *Removed File V—[illegible]. Returned 11:40 p.m. Same night.* The name after the V had been blotted — not struck through this time but blotted, smeared with a thumb while the ink was wet, deliberately, unmistakably deliberately, because Silas Crane did not smear his ink. Silas Crane blotted his pages with blotting paper kept in the second drawer of his desk, cut to size, replaced quarterly.

He had sat in this room eleven years ago and destroyed his own record with his own thumb, and then he had gone home and slept, apparently, and woken up, and notarized things, and lived his tidy life, and the memory had lain down in the cellar of him like a body under floorboards, and tonight a dying woman had walked across those floorboards in her stocking feet and made them creak.

*You filed it,* she might as well have said. She hadn't said it yet. But he could hear it coming down the years toward him like weather.

* * *

Junie Okafor arrived at eight the next morning to find the lights already burning in the archive and her boss sitting on a stepstool in front of an open drawer with a composition book in his lap, looking like a man attending his own autopsy.

She had worked for Silas Crane for six years, ever since she'd come back to Hawley with a paralegal certificate and a plan to stay exactly two years before moving somewhere with a skyline. The skyline had failed to materialize, partly because of the pay, partly because of the work, and partly — though she'd have denied it under oath — because of him. Silas Crane was the kind of teacher who didn't know he was teaching, who corrected your citation format at seven in the morning and then quietly paid for your mother's hospital parking for three months and threatened to fire you if you mentioned it. Junie had catalogued his moods the way he catalogued deeds. There were perhaps four of them. This morning's face matched none of them.

"Mr. Crane." She set down her bag and her coffee and the paper bag of maple bars from the Donut Den, because it was Thursday, and Thursdays were maple bar days, and the world's architecture depended on such things. "You're sitting in the dark ages."

"I'm sitting in 2014," he said.

"Even worse." She came down the aisle, heels ticking on the concrete, and followed his gaze into the open drawer. She saw the gap. She was trained to see gaps; he had trained her. "Missing file?"

"Missing file."

"Which one?"

He was quiet long enough that she counted the boiler knocks through the wall. Three, this time.

"Voss," he said.

Junie frowned. "I don't know any Voss. Should I?"

"No," Silas said, and heard how the word landed, too fast and too flat, like a door closing on a hinge that needed oil. He watched her register it. She was twenty-nine years old and sharp as a box cutter, and he had spent six years sharpening her, and this morning he discovered the flaw in that project: he had sharpened her *against himself*. "A matter from before your time. Closed. I need to verify some details."

"What details? Maybe I can pull them from the docket index, or—"

"The index is fine," he said. "Everything is fine."

She looked at him. Really looked, the way she almost never let herself, because looking too directly at Silas Crane was like looking at the sun through a surveyor's instrument — you got the measurement, but it cost you something. What she saw made her take a half-step back and put her hand flat on the cool metal of the cabinet, as if steadying herself against a tremor.

"You're rattled," she said.

"I am not rattled."

"Mr. Crane, I have seen you get served with a complaint by a process server who tripped over your umbrella stand, and you apologized to *him*. I have seen you find a typo in a recorded deed from 1997 and drive to the county seat in a thunderstorm to fix it the same day. You are the least rattled human being in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts." Her voice gentled. "You're rattled now."

Silas closed the composition book. The sound it made was very small and very loud.

"Junie," he said, "when you file something in this room, who else knows the system?"

"Nobody better than me," she said. "But nobody knows it better than you."

"That's not what I asked."

She understood then. He watched her understand, watched it move across her face like cloud-shadow over a field. When you file something in this room, who else could make it look untouched? Who else could strike a name with a ruler and blot a log with a thumb and square the folders just so?

"Only you," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"And somebody did."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." Junie took off her glasses — she wore them only for close work, a affectation she'd adopted from him, God help her — and polished them on her scarf, buying time, thinking. He could see the machinery running: loyalty pulling one direction, sense pulling the other, and underneath both, the question she was too well-trained to ask first and too honest not to ask eventually.

She asked the honest one. "Is whatever's in that file going to hurt you?"

Silas looked down the long aisle of cabinets, forty-one years of Hawley sleeping in their gray-green drawers, the rain still needling the little window, the amber light beginning its slow crawl as the sun tried and failed to get through the weather. He thought about Margaret Voss's hands around the check, steady now that the money was spoken for, trembling only when she spoke of her son. He thought about a green folder he could describe down to the staple. He thought about a name struck through in blue-black ink by a man with a cracked Parker Jotter, and about how a man could carry a thing for eleven years without knowing he was carrying it, the way a town can carry a death.

"I don't know yet," he said. It was the truest sentence he'd spoken in a decade, and they both felt it.

"Then we find out," Junie said, putting her glasses back on. "Together. And Mr. Crane — if somebody's been in this vault wearing your habits, they'll be back. People like that always come back for the loose ends." She nodded at the drawer, at the gap, at the absence with its perfect edges. "So maybe we stop keeping the important things where they expect them to be."

Silas Crane looked at his clerk — his sharp, loyal, dangerous clerk — and felt something he had not felt since Margaret Voss walked into his office: the sensation of a plan forming that he had not authored. It was not entirely comfortable. But it was warm, the way a struck match is warm.

"First," he said, climbing off the stepstool and straightening his cuffs, restoring order to at least one small territory of the universe, "we establish exactly what else is missing. Every drawer. Every year. You take the even cabinets, I'll take the odd."

"And if we find more gaps?"

"Then we'll know this wasn't about one file," Silas said. "It was about a story. And somebody has been cutting chapters out of mine."

They worked through the morning while the rain kept on, and it was nearly noon, with the audit half done and two more absences already logged — a 2015 correspondence file, a 2016 deposition transcript, both with edges too clean — when the phone rang upstairs and Junie went to answer it, and came back down the stairs slower than she'd gone up, holding the message slip the way you hold something that might be live.

"It's the Pines Care Facility," she said. "For a Mrs. Margaret Voss." A pause. "Mr. Crane — the caller says Ms. Voss checked out three weeks ago. Family, they said. Her son arranged it." She looked up at him, and the amber light had reached the end of its crawl, and the archive seemed suddenly very large and very cold around the two of them. "But Mr. Crane — Daniel Voss called the front desk himself yesterday afternoon. Asking whether anyone had come asking about his mother." Another pause, and her voice dropped. "The caller said he sounded frightened. And he said if anyone asks again — tell them his mother never existed."
