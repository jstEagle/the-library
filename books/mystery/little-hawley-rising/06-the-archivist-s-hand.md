## 6. The Archivist's Hand

I have spent twenty-two years in the morgue of Little Hawley, and in that time I have learned that handwriting is the most honest thing a person ever produces. Fingerprints can be lifted. Alibis can be rehearsed. But a hand betrays its owner on every page — the way a person crosses a seven, whether they loop their ell or let it stand naked, where they press hard and where they trail off like a thought they'd rather not finish.

So I did what any coroner does with a body of evidence. I laid it out on steel and examined it.

The annotations on the 1924 drowning file were written in fresh ink — I could smell it, that faint iron-and-vanilla scent of a fountain pen recently filled — but the knowledge inside them was old. Whoever wrote them knew things that had never been committed to paper. He knew that the depth measurement taken at the millrace was recorded wrong by two feet. He knew that the coroner's original draft report had named Edmund Hawley as a witness before the name was struck through and replaced with "no witnesses present." He knew the drowned man had been wearing a wedding ring that never appeared in the evidence log.

And he knew these things in an investigator's cadence. The marginal notes weren't speculation. They were corrections. *No. Measured at 11 ft, slack tide.* *Ring entered county evidence, then removed. See ledger, Nov. 3.* This was someone who had sat with the file so long he'd stopped reading it and started remembering it.

I spent three evenings at my kitchen table with the file spread between my soup bowl and my lamp, cross-referencing against everything else I could get my hands on. That meant the town records. And there was only one place in Little Hawley where the town records lived in anything resembling completeness.

The archive. In the basement of the Carnegie building. Under the care of Theodore Vasquez-Zhou, town archivist, my foster brother, keeper of every scrap of paper this town had produced since before either of us was born.

I want to be honest about what happened when that thought arrived, because honesty is the one luxury this case has left me. I didn't think *Theo*. I thought: *someone with lifelong access to the archive*. I thought it the way you think about a stranger. It took me a full minute to understand that the stranger was sleeping forty feet from my childhood bedroom, eating breakfast at the table where I'd learned long division.

That's the trick of family. You can know a person your whole life and never once see him.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Theo, assembled coldly, the way I'd assemble any profile:

He had run the archive since 1989, when old Mrs. Pell retired and handed him the keys like a crown. Before that he'd volunteered there through high school, through college summers, through every year of a life that never quite left Little Hawley. He catalogued with a devotion I recognized because I shared it — the same need to make order out of the town's indifferent past, the same conviction that a record kept is a life defended.

He had also, I now realized, been the one who taught me to look closely. When I was nine and obsessed with the cemetery behind our house, it was Theo who walked me row by row and said, *Read the stones, Astrid. Everyone here was somebody.* I built my entire profession on a sentence my brother gave me.

And the annotations — I compared them against documents I'd seen over the years, permits Theo had typed, exhibit labels he'd lettered for the bicentennial display. The pen pressure matched. The peculiar flat-topped *t* matched. The way he wrote his numerals with a little foot on the one — matched.

It wasn't proof. Handwriting analysis done at a kitchen table by a coroner with a grudge against his own eyesight is not proof. But it was enough to make my hands shake while I washed the soup bowl, which I mention only because I dropped it, and the sound it made breaking was very small and very loud.

* * *

I did not confront him. Confrontation is for people with proof, and besides — some animal part of me wanted to be wrong. Wanted it badly enough that I did the next best thing instead.

I lied to my brother.

On Thursday evening I came home carrying a folder I'd dressed up to look official — photocopies, my own careful notes, and one fabricated detail slipped into the middle like a pill in meat. The detail concerned the boot in Edmund Hawley's coffin. I wrote that I'd sent fragments of the leather to the state lab, and that preliminary results suggested the boot postdated 1924 — that it was, specifically, a style sold by Calloway & Sons General Store, a line discontinued in 1931.

None of that was true. There was no lab. There was no result. There was only a hook, baited with the Calloway name, because Ruth Calloway's scrapbook had taught me something: whoever moved that boot wanted the story steered toward the founding family, away from the drowned man. A liar correcting the record would correct a lie too.

Theo was at the kitchen table doing a crossword, which he does every Thursday, in pen, because pencils are for people who expect to be wrong.

"Busy?" I asked, casual as a man carrying a bomb in a folder.

"Seven across is killing me." He looked up. My brother has gray in his beard now and reading glasses he refuses on principle, and he looked at me with the same open face he's had since we were boys. "What've you got?"

"Bicentennial paperwork," I said. "Ceremony follow-up. The county wants a summary."

"Ah." He took the folder, and I watched his hands — those careful hands, the ones that had turned pages in that basement for thirty-five years without tearing one — and I saw nothing. No tremor. No pause. He flipped through my fabrication the way he'd flip through a shipping manifest.

"The boot analysis is in already?" he said. "That was fast."

"State lab moves quick when the sheriff asks."

"Mm." He set the folder down, aligned its edges with the table edge, a small compulsive kindness to my sloppy work. "Calloway boots. Huh. Ruth will want to know that."

"You talk to Ruth?"

"Occasionally. She comes in asking after genealogy materials." He picked up his crossword again. "Everyone in this town is looking for somebody, Astrid. Most of them just don't know it yet."

I laughed. God help me, I laughed, and it sounded right, and I went upstairs, and I lay on my bed staring at the ceiling crack I've memorized over forty years, feeling like the worst kind of fraud — a man who lies well enough to fool the people who love him, and loves them anyway, which is either the tragedy or the point.

* * *

The false detail appeared on my desk Monday morning.

Not the whole lie — just the thread of it, pulled taut. Slipped under my microscope stand, the same delivery method as before, was a single photocopied page from the Calloway & Sons ledger, 1929–1931, showing the boot inventory. And beside the entry for the discontinued workman's line, in that familiar flat-topped hand, a note:

*Close. Wrong decade. Check the 1918–1922 ledgers — the founder bought twelve pairs for the quarry crews. Your lab result is a plant, little brother. Clever, though. I'm almost proud.*

*I'm almost proud.*

I stood in my morgue holding the page and felt the betrayal arrive not as a knife but as a slow flood, the way water finds every seam in a foundation. Because the note told me more than guilt. It told me he'd known instantly. It told me he read my fabrications the way I read corpses — fluently, sadly, seeing everything. And it told me he'd answered, which meant he wanted the game to continue. Which meant he wanted *me* in it.

Twelve pairs for the quarry crews. I checked. The 1918–1922 Calloway ledgers do exist; I drove to Ruth's that afternoon and she found the reference in her scrapbook within ten minutes, her finger steady on the column of figures. The founder had outfitted the quarry workers himself. Men who drowned in his water might well have worn his boots.

Which meant the boot in the coffin might belong to no one at all — or to everyone. A prop. A message. Theo hadn't planted evidence; he'd planted a *question*, dressed in period-accurate leather.

"You knew," I said to Ruth, not accusingly. Just tired.

She folded her hands on the scrapbook. "Astrid Zhou, I've been watching that boy restore dead men's names for thirty years. I watched him petition the county twice to reopen the 1924 file. I watched them deny him twice." Her eyes were dry and very old. "I didn't know what he'd do. But I knew what he wanted. Wanting's not a crime."

"No," I said. "It's just where crimes start."

* * *

I went home that night and ate dinner across from my brother and said nothing, and he said nothing, and we passed the salt like two men defusing something between bites. Afterward he washed dishes and I dried, our old division of labor, and the silence had a new texture — not comfortable anymore but *loaded*, a wire strung between us humming with everything unsaid.

At the door of his study he paused. "Good work on the ceremony summary," he said. "County accepted it."

"Thanks," I said.

"Astrid." He turned, and the hall light caught his glasses, and for a moment I couldn't read his face at all — couldn't tell if I was looking at the boy who taught me to read gravestones or the man who'd dug up a founder and scattered him like a rumor. "Whatever you find down there, in the records — remember that some things are buried twice. Once by the people who did it. And once by the people who loved the town enough to keep it quiet."

"Which kind are you?"

He smiled, and it broke my heart, because it was exactly the smile from the photograph on our foster mother's mantel, 1981, two boys squinting in the sun. "I'm the third kind," he said. "The kind that digs."

His door closed. I stood in the hallway a long time, listening to the house settle around its secrets.

Then I went downstairs, took a fresh sheet of paper, and began writing down everything I knew — dates, hands, ledgers, the boot, the quarry, the file under my microscope — in chronological order, in my own unmistakable hand. If this ended the way I feared it would end, one of us would testify, and it would not be a lie that saved us both.

Tomorrow I would take it to Sheriff Okonkwo. Tomorrow the case would stop being mine alone.

But tonight, folding the pages, I understood the shape of the trap I was standing in: whatever Theo had planned, he had planned it for an audience. And the only witness he trusted to tell the truth —

was me.
