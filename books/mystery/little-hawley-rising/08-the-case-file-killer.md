## 8. The Case File Killer

Dane Okonkwo's office smelled of burnt coffee and the particular despair of a man who had spent thirty years believing Little Hawley was a boring place to be a sheriff.

He'd spread the evidence across his desk like a tarot reading: photographs of the quarry, a warrant application half-filled out in his blocky handwriting, and the annotated pages from my morgue, sealed in evidence bags that fogged slightly with Dane's breath every time he leaned over them.

"Spring tide's Saturday," he said. "High water at 2:14 in the afternoon. Which is exactly when the bicentennial committee has scheduled the dedication ceremony at the quarry overlook. Two hundred people, a brass band, and the lieutenant governor's office sending a representative because somebody in the historical society can't say no to a photo op." He rubbed his eyes. "I want to take Theo into custody Thursday night. Quiet. Before he can turn whatever he's planning into a spectacle."

"And if he's already turned it into a spectacle?" I asked. "If the spectacle is the point?"

Dane looked at me the way he'd been looking at me all week—like I was a man carrying something fragile across ice. "You know him better than anyone, Astrid. Is he capable of that?"

I thought about Theo at nine years old, organizing our foster mother's recipe cards by decade because the disorder made him physically anxious. Theo at sixteen, standing in front of the town council with a binder full of citations to argue for restoring the mill pond path. Theo last month, sliding pages under my microscope with annotations so precise they read like love letters written in a dead man's hand.

"Yes," I said. "It might be the only thing he's capable of."

* * *

The problem with knowing someone your whole life is that you mistake the shape of them for the shape of the truth. I had spent forty years being the man who looked closely—at bodies, at ledgers, at the small dishonesties of decay—and I had somehow failed to look closely at the boy who shared my bedroom, my breakfast table, my entire manufactured childhood.

Thursday night came and went. Dane's deputies sat on Theo's house on Cutter Street from six o'clock onward, and at six-fifteen a neighbor called to report that the archivist's kitchen light was on and Theo was visible through the window, eating dinner, reading, apparently unaware or magnificently unconcerned that three cars full of law enforcement were parked along his street like a very polite ambush.

At seven, the light went out. At seven-twenty, Deputy Marsh knocked on Theo's door and got no answer. At seven-forty-five, they found the back door unlocked and the house empty except for one thing: on the kitchen table, weighted down under a river stone, a single page in Theo's handwriting.

Dane brought it to me personally. He didn't put it in an evidence bag. He just laid it on my examining table, next to where I keep the scale I weigh hearts on, which felt like a joke Theo would appreciate.

It said:

*Astrid — You were always better at endings than beginnings, so I'll leave this one to you. The quarry. Saturday. 2:14. Bring the file. Bring the truth. Bring yourself — you're the only witness who was ever real. — T.*

Below it, in smaller letters, almost an afterthought:

*P.S. Check the bicentennial program. Page 12. I got them to print it months ago. You'll want to see what the tide does.*

* * *

Page twelve of the official bicentennial program was titled "A Founding Reconsidered: New Evidence Regarding the Events of June 1924." It ran four hundred words, set in the same dignified serif as everything else, and it named Edmund Hawley, the drowned laborer, and the two county officials who signed the false report—the coroner and the archivist's daughter. My foster parents. It named them in print, in a document distributed to every household in Little Hawley, and nobody had noticed because nobody reads page twelve of anything until it's too late.

Theo hadn't just planned a reveal. He'd published it. Months ago, hidden in plain sight, waiting for the spring tide to make everyone look up from their programs at the right moment.

"He wants a crowd," Dane said when I showed him. He'd gone very still, in the way of large men absorbing bad news. "Two hundred witnesses means I can't quietly take him into custody without it becoming a scene. And if I wait until after—"

"After, there's nothing left to arrest him for hiding," I finished. "The secret will already be out. The arrest becomes a footnote instead of a headline. That's the design."

"You sound like you admire it."

"I sound like I know his handwriting," I said, and Dane had the grace not to push.

But here is what I didn't say, what I couldn't say even to Dane, because saying it would make it true in a way I wasn't ready for: the program page wasn't aimed at the town. The town was just the audience. Read the note again. *Bring the file. Bring the truth. Bring yourself.* Three imperatives, and only one of them was about evidence.

Theo didn't want a stranger from the lieutenant governor's office to stand at the quarry overlook and read page twelve aloud. He wanted me. The coroner. The son of the woman who signed the false report. The brother of the man who dug up the founder. He wanted me to stand in front of everyone and do the thing I had spent my whole life refusing to do: stop being invisible. Stop being the careful, quiet keeper of other people's endings and become, for five minutes, the beginning of something.

He'd built a trap out of love, and the trigger was me.

* * *

Friday night I did what I always do when I can't sleep, which is work. I laid out the original case file—the real one, the one Theo had been feeding me page by page like a man teaching a child to read—and I went through it with the attention I usually reserve for remains.

And that's when I saw it. The thing I should have seen weeks ago.

Every annotation Theo had left me followed a pattern. Not corrections exactly—additions. Each page he gave me contained one detail that existed nowhere else: the depth of the water where the laborer was found, the position of the boot, the weight of the stones used to sink him. Details only the killer would know. Details Theo could only have gotten from one source.

Edmund Hawley himself. Or rather, from wherever Theo had moved him.

"The quarry," I said out loud, to no one, to the fluorescent hum of my own morgue. "He didn't hide the body. He *placed* it."

Every annotated page had been a breadcrumb leading not to Theo but to the quarry. Not a confession trail—a tour. Theo had been walking me, step by step, to the exact spot in the flooded quarry where Edmund Hawley had waited ninety-nine years to be found, so that when the spring tide rose on Saturday afternoon, in front of two hundred people and a brass band, someone would have to go into the water and bring him out.

And Theo had arranged for that someone to be me.

I sat down on my stool and laughed, once, a short ugly sound that bounced off the tile. Because the worst part—the part that made my hands shake—wasn't the manipulation. It was that he was right. If I stood at that overlook and let the sheriff's divers do it, the story would belong to the county, to the warrant, to the machinery that had buried Wei Zhou's name in 1924 and buried mine in a stranger's house a generation later. But if I went into the water myself—if the coroner of Little Hawley pulled the founder's bones out of the flood with his own hands—then the truth would have a body attached to it. A witness who was real.

*Bring yourself,* he'd written. *You're the only witness who was ever real.*

He knew me. That was the trap. He knew I would rather drown than let the wrong person tell the right story.

* * *

Saturday morning, 11:40 a.m., the quarry overlook. The brass band was tuning in a way that suggested none of them had ever met each other before today. Folding chairs in white rows. A banner: LITTLE HAWLEY, TWO HUNDRED YEARS. The lieutenant governor's representative—a young woman with a clipboard and the expression of someone who had been promised a ribbon-cutting—stood near the edge, looking at the water.

The spring tide was already coming in. You could see it happening, the quarry lake climbing its own walls, swallowing the old pump-house steps one by one, the water darker than it had any right to be, as if it were remembering what it was for.

Dane found me by the railing. He was in his dress uniform, which fit him badly, and he spoke low, under the band's warming-up.

"Divers are suited up. Warrant's signed. The moment we have probable cause confirmed, we take Theo into custody and this becomes a police matter, clean and simple."

"Nothing about this is going to be clean," I said. "Look at the east path."

Theo was standing there. Not hiding. Standing in the open in his good gray suit, holding a leather portfolio against his chest the way our mother used to hold her hymnal, and he was watching the water rise with an expression I had never seen on him before—not triumph, not fear. Peace. The peace of a man who has finished a very long piece of work and is simply waiting for someone to check it.

"He's not running," I said. "He's not going to run. He's going to walk into that water at 2:14, in front of everyone, and he's going to hold that proof above the surface until someone comes to get it. And if it's a deputy, he'll hand it over and say nothing. But it won't be a deputy."

"Astrid—"

"Because he wrote the invitation to me." My voice did something embarrassing on the last word, and I steadied it. "Dane, if you send your people into that water, he'll give them the file and the story dies in a courtroom for two years. If you don't send anyone, he drowns proving a point, and the story dies with him. There's exactly one version of today where the truth survives, and in that version, I'm the one in the water."

Dane was quiet for a long moment. Below us, the band struck up something patriotic and slightly out of tune, and the crowd began to settle into their chairs, opening their programs, finding their seats, four hundred eyes drifting toward page twelve without yet knowing why.

"So don't go in," Dane said finally. "Let him float his little portfolio. We fish it out with a pole if we have to."

"That's not how he built it."

"How do you know?"

Because I'd checked the tide tables myself at 3 a.m., I didn't say. Because the spot where Theo would wade out—the old pump-house platform, just barely submerged at peak tide—was positioned so the portfolio would go under at 2:31 unless someone reached it by then. Because Theo had done the math on my hesitation, my conscience, my goddamn meticulousness, and he had bet everything on me being exactly who he knew I was.

"Because I know his handwriting," I said again.

I took off my jacket, folded it over the railing, and looked down at the rising water, where the pump-house roof was just going under, and where a man in a gray suit was beginning, calmly, to walk down the old quarry steps toward a drowned century.

The band played on. The crowd murmured. Somewhere behind me, Dane was shouting my name.

At 2:14, the tide reached its mark, and Theo stepped off the last dry stone into the flood, holding the truth above the water like a lantern.
