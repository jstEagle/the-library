## 9. The Trial Date

The call came at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Casper Sterling had spent eleven years in insurance investigation, and he had learned that institutions kept hours the way tide pools keep water — nothing moved before nine, and everything that mattered happened while someone was watching. A 6:47 a.m. call from a clerk at the Harrow Bay County Courthouse was not a courtesy. It was a signal.

"Mr. Sterling? This is Doreen Pruitt, clerk of court." Her voice had the flat efficiency of someone reading from a script she'd been given by someone else. "You're listed as a party of interest in a motion filed Friday. Judge Ellery Haskins granted it yesterday afternoon. The matter of Ashcombe versus the Estate of Ashcombe is reopened. Trial date's been set."

Casper sat up in the narrow bed at the marina inn, fog pressing gray against the window. "Set for when?"

"Six weeks. August fourteenth." A pause, the sound of paper being squared. "You'll want to come down and review the filing. It's public record now."

"I'd like that," Casper said. "Who filed it?"

Another pause. Longer. He could hear her deciding how much of the script to abandon.

"The motion came in anonymously," she said. "No counsel of record. Just the petition, notarized, with supporting documentation attached." She lowered her voice as if the walls of the courthouse might be listening from forty miles away. "Mr. Sterling, I've been clerk here nineteen years. I've never seen an anonymous filing granted. Not once. Judge Haskins read it for about ten minutes and signed it the same afternoon."

"What kind of documentation?"

"That's the thing," Doreen said. "It was good. Tide charts, coroner's supplemental notes, a chain-of-custody log I don't have any record of ever existing. Whoever put it together knew exactly what this office would need to see." The paper-squaring stopped. "Almost like they knew what we were missing."

She hung up before he could ask anything else, which told him she'd said more than she meant to already.

Casper sat on the edge of the bed and let his memory do what it always did — lay the facts out on the table of his mind like evidence bags, each one tagged and dated.

The letters had arrived eleven days ago. Both in the same hand — he'd confirmed the pen pressure match within minutes of opening them, the same heavy downstroke on the ascenders, the same hesitation break before the capital *A* in *Ashcombe*. One letter confessed. The other promised. He had come to Harrow Bay because of them, and Ruth Ashcombe had told him she wrote three, mailed to three investigators, as a test.

Only he had come.

And now, eleven days later, a judge had reopened a drowning that everyone in this town had agreed to remember wrong for a decade — based on documents so complete they included records that had been excised from the official file. Records only someone with institutional access could have pulled. Records that existed somewhere, intact, waiting.

He dressed quickly and went down to the inn's small dining room, where Marisol the owner set coffee in front of him without being asked. She still flinched, slightly, at his surname. He'd stopped trying to make conversation about it.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic," she said.

"I'm trying to figure out who wants something badly enough to build me a staircase to it," Casper said. "And what's at the top."

Marisol refilled his cup. "In Harrow Bay, the answer to the second question is usually the same," she said. "Daniel Voss."

"Not this time," Casper said. "Voss doesn't want this case open. Voss has spent ten years and, if Albrecht's ledger is any indication, a great deal of money keeping it shut." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit he'd never trained out of himself. "Somebody else is pulling the string. Somebody who wanted a trial date more than they wanted a confession read."

* * *

The courthouse smelled like floor wax and old paper, and Doreen Pruitt met him at the counter with the file already open, as though she'd been standing there since dawn hoping he'd arrive before her courage left.

"Read it here," she said. "I can't let it leave the building until counsel of record enters an appearance."

"There is no counsel of record."

"Then I suppose you'll be reading it here." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the ghost of complicity. She slid the folder across the counter and stepped back to her desk, ostentatiously occupied with a stapler.

The motion itself was two pages, typed, clean. *In re: The Death of Vivian Marguerite Ashcombe, deceased. Petition to reopen investigation pursuant to newly discovered evidence.* No name. The notarization block bore the stamp of a notary public in Tillamook County — two counties north, the same county where Albrecht lived drunk and afraid. Casper noted that and made himself note that he noticed it; coincidence was a currency that only spent well once.

Then the attachments.

He read them the way he read everything — once, completely, the pages photographing themselves into the archive behind his eyes — and then he read them again slowly, because the second pass was where the architecture showed.

Attachment A: tide charts for the night of June ninth, ten years ago, annotated in red ink. The annotations matched Nell Okafor's grandmother's cove exactly, and they matched the discrepancy Casper had already reconstructed from the excised pages of the original claim file: the water was forty minutes lower than the official timeline required. Vivian Ashcombe could not have drowned where and when the coroner's report said she did, unless the report was written first and the facts arranged after.

Attachment B: a supplemental note from Dr. Emory Callis, the coroner — undated, but referencing a "second vessel observed idling beyond the point" in language nearly identical to what the grandmother had given Casper in fragments, terrified of getting it right incorrectly. Someone had interviewed her. Recently. And written down what she actually said.

Attachment C: the chain-of-custody log. This was the one that made Casper's hands go still on the counter. It documented the transfer of a photograph — one photograph, described only as "image depicting the cove, post-incident" — from evidence storage to an unnamed custodian and back again, dated three years ago. Three years ago, someone had taken that photograph out, looked at it, and put it back. And then, apparently, sat on it.

Until now.

"Doreen," Casper said quietly. "The documentation packet. Was it mailed or hand-delivered?"

"Hand-delivered." She didn't look up from her stapler. "Manila envelope, no return address. Left with the night deputy on a Friday evening, which — " she finally glanced at him — "is when you deliver something if you want it sitting on the judge's desk first thing Monday morning, unfiltered by anyone else in this building."

"And the handwriting on the envelope?"

Her stapler stopped. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I've seen it before," Casper said. "Twice."

Doreen Pruitt looked at him for a long moment across the waxed floor of the Harrow Bay County Courthouse, and whatever she saw in his face made her decide to trust him, or to stop pretending she hadn't already.

"It was a woman's hand," she said. "Small, careful. Heavy pressure." She hesitated. "There was a name on the return address line, actually. Just no street address. The deputy didn't think anything of it."

"What name?"

"Ashcombe," she said. "Ruth Ashcombe."

* * *

Ruth's hospice room smelled of lavender and the antiseptic underneath it, the way all such rooms smell — sweetness deployed against the inevitable. She was smaller than she'd been nine days ago. The illness that had killed her mother was keeping its appointment with her daughter, and it was punctual.

Casper stood in the doorway holding a photocopy of the motion, and Ruth looked at it, and then at him, and began to laugh — a thin, delighted, terrible sound.

"You got the call," she said. "Of course you got the call. They always call you early, don't they? When they want you somewhere?"

"Ruth." He crossed to the chair beside her bed. "Did you file this?"

"I can barely file my own nails," she said. She held up her hands — trembling, the nails pale and ridged. "Look at me, Mr. Sterling. I'm dying on a schedule. Do I look like a woman who spent last week assembling a chain-of-custody log?"

"No," Casper admitted. "But your name was on the envelope."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. As if she'd been waiting to see whether he'd catch it, and was pleased, and frightened, in equal measure.

"My name gets used a lot these days," she said. "I sign things. That's mostly what I'm good for now — a living signature. There are people who find that useful." She turned her head on the pillow to face him fully. "You've figured out some of it. I can tell. You have that look my mother used to get, right before she said something that ended a dinner party."

"Tell me about the third letter," Casper said. "The one you were paid to mail and didn't."

Ruth was quiet for a moment. Outside the window, a gull hung motionless over the parking lot, riding wind Casper couldn't see.

"I told you I wrote three confessions," she said. "That was true. What I didn't tell you is that I only wrote two of them myself." Her voice dropped. "The third one came to me already written. With instructions. Mail it to a specific investigator — a name I didn't know — on a specific date. In exchange, my hospice care gets paid through the end, and my mother's grave gets a headstone that says what actually happened to her." She smiled, and it cracked halfway. "I kept it instead. I told you that too. Unopened. Because I wanted to know what was inside before I decided whether I was a courier or an accomplice."

"Has anyone asked you for it since?"

"Once. About a week ago. A phone call." Her fingers plucked at the blanket. "A woman's voice. Very calm. She said, 'Ruth, the timeline has accelerated. We need the letter mailed by Thursday.' And do you know what struck me, Mr. Sterling? Not the request. The word *we*. Like there was a committee. Like my dying was a project with stakeholders."

Casper felt the shape of it assembling in his mind — not the whole picture, but the load-bearing walls. "Ruth. The anonymous filing. The tide charts, the coroner's note, the custody log. Someone built a case file better than the county ever had. Someone who knew exactly which pages had been cut out of the original claim, because they're the ones who needed them back."

"Yes," Ruth said simply.

"Someone who could get your name onto an envelope without your knowledge. Someone who calls your hospice room and says *we*." He leaned forward. "Is it Daniel Voss?"

Ruth laughed again, softer this time, and shook her head with something close to tenderness.

"Oh, Mr. Sterling. No. Voss is the one this is being done *to*." She reached out and touched the photocopy in his hands, one trembling finger resting on the words *petition to reopen*. "Think about it the way you think about insurance fraud, since that's your trade. Who profits? Follow the money backward. Every fraud has an author, and the author is almost never the person holding the bag."

"Then who—"

"You keep asking who," Ruth said. "Ask *when* instead. Ask why now. Ten years, and the case opens six weeks before a trial date, timed so the evidence is fresh enough to use and the witnesses are old enough to be scared." Her eyes were bright and feverish and terribly clear. "Someone lit a fuse, Casper. And they sent you the match box, because they knew — they *knew* — you were the one investigator alive who couldn't walk away from your own mother's signature on a falsified alibi."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere down the hall, a cart rattled past.

"You knew about my mother," Casper said.

"I know what I was paid to mail," Ruth said. "And I know that whoever wrote that third letter knew things about your mother that I certainly never did." She settled back into the pillow, exhausted now, the clarity burning down like a lamp running low. "Go home, Mr. Sterling. Read the motion again. Count the days. Six weeks isn't a deadline — it's a countdown. And whoever set it doesn't want justice."

"No?" 

"They want a stage," Ruth said, closing her eyes. "Trials are stages. And every stage needs an audience that arrived believing they chose to come."

* * *

He walked back along the harbor road in the late-afternoon fog, and he made himself do the arithmetic out loud, because saying it aloud made it real and he needed it real.

Eleven days ago: two letters, one hand. A confession and a promise. He had read them as bait, and he had bitten, and he had told himself he was the fisherman.

Nine days ago: Ruth, confessing to writing three letters as a test. Only Casper came. He had read that as flattery aimed at his vanity, and it had worked, and he had told himself he understood the game.

Seven days ago: the excised file, his mother's certification signature, the vertigo of a record edited by institutional hands. He had read that as the motive — cover-up, exposure, threat — and he had begun building a case the way he always did, brick by brick, alone.

Five days ago: Albrecht, drunk and afraid, handing over the shape of the economy of silence. *They don't threaten you. They make you useful.*

And today: a judge, an anonymous filing, a trial date. Six weeks. Public, fast, irreversible.

Casper stopped at the rail overlooking the marina, the fog beading on the metal, and looked at the whole structure at once for the first time.

The letters hadn't been a confession. Confessions end things. These letters had started everything — had started *him*, specifically, moving toward Harrow Bay on a schedule he'd believed was his own. The excised file hadn't been leaked to expose Voss; it had been leaked to arm him. Albrecht hadn't been found by accident; a retired detective two counties over, in the same county as the notary stamp, was a waypoint, not a destination. Even Ruth — even Ruth's deathbed, her trembling signature, her unopened letter — had been positioned like furniture in a room designed for him to walk through.

Every door he'd opened had been unlocked. That was the detail his photographic memory kept returning to, turning it over like a coin that refused to show tails. In eleven years of fraud work, Casper had learned one law above all others: locked doors mean secrecy, but *unlocked* doors mean invitation. Someone had walked ahead of him through this entire case, unlocking everything, timing everything, and the trial date was not the finish line.

It was the starting gun.

Six weeks. Long enough for discovery to drag the ledger into the light, for subpoenas to reach the coroner and the harbor master and the council members, for the grandmother to take a stand and choose between fear and Nell's face in the gallery. Short enough that the photograph — the photograph in the custody log, the one taken after the drowning, the one showing a figure on the dock in a coat Casper was beginning to dread — would surface before whoever held it could decide otherwise.

Someone wanted all of it public. Fast. On the record, under oath, beyond anyone's power to bury twice.

Which meant the person who had built this machine — patient, meticulous, cruel in the way that only precision can be cruel — wasn't hiding from the truth.

They were curating it.

Casper stood at the rail until the fog thickened into dusk and the marina lights came on one by one, yellow smears in the gray. Then he took out his notebook and wrote a single line at the bottom of the page, beneath the tide charts and the dates and the names:

*The confession was the summons. The trial is the trap. Find out who needs me in the room.*

His phone buzzed against his chest.

An unknown number. A text message, five words long:

**Check your mailbox tonight.**
