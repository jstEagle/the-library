## 22. Belonging, Provisionally

The fog came in on a Tuesday, the way it always did in October, and Casper Sterling stood at the window of the room he still hadn't given up at the marina inn and watched it erase the harbor one boat at a time.

Six weeks since the arrest. Five since Voss's empire began folding like wet cardboard — the cannery assets frozen, the council seats vacated one resignation at a time, the coroner's pension clawed back with a bureaucratic efficiency that almost qualified as poetry. The estate claims were still working themselves through probate, which was the only reason Casper gave himself for staying. Someone had to inventory what Vivian Ashcombe had left behind, and the estate's interim administrator — a tired woman from the county who kept calling him "Mr. Stirling" no matter how many times he corrected her — had retained him as a consultant.

It wasn't entirely true that that was why he stayed.

He'd told himself the same story every morning for a month: *the work isn't finished.* And every morning the work got smaller. A signature here. A deed there. The slow arithmetic of a fortune being unwound and handed back to the people it had been taken from — the Okafor family among them, though Nell's grandmother refused to discuss money and had asked instead, through Nell, whether the town might finally take down the plaque at the marina that named Daniel Voss as its benefactor.

It came down last Thursday. Nobody held a ceremony. By Friday afternoon someone had already bolted up a new board where it used to hang, blank, waiting for whatever the town decided to be next.

Casper watched the fog eat the last of the fishing fleet and thought about how a place could be rebuilt the way a case could be closed: imperfectly, provisionally, one document at a time.

His phone buzzed on the desk. He didn't look at it right away. He'd learned something in Harrow Bay about letting things arrive at their own pace.

* * *

Ruth Ashcombe had died eleven days after the trial opened, in the hospice wing she'd worked in for twenty years, holding her mother's hand.

Vivian had been there when it happened. Casper knew because he'd driven Ruth's second letter — the confession, the test, the summons — to the hospice himself, and found them together in the small hours, two women who had spent a decade being dead to each other in different ways. He'd stood in the doorway and let them have the moment. Some evidence you don't collect. You just witness it.

At the funeral, Vivian had worn gray and stood apart from everyone, which was its own kind of honesty. She'd caught Casper's eye across the graveside and mouthed something he chose not to interpret. Later, settling the estate, he understood: she'd been mouthing *thank you*, but thank you hadn't felt like enough, so she'd said nothing out loud at all.

Her plea deal was still being negotiated. Manslaughter, accessory after the fact, obstruction — the lawyers were arguing about which decade-old sins attached to which statutes. She'd surrendered her passport, her accounts, and the truth, in roughly that order of pain. She would serve time. She seemed, in the one long conversation Casper had gotten with her since, almost relieved by the prospect.

"I spent ten years being a ghost," she'd said, in the visiting room, her hands flat on the table like a woman bracing against weather. "Do you know what that does to a person? You start to believe you deserve to be one." She'd looked at him then with eyes that were Ruth's eyes, older and harder-worn. "Your mother never believed that about me. That's why I trusted you with the letters. Not because she certified the alibi. Because before that — before any of it — she was the only person in this town who looked at me like I was still real."

Casper had gone home to the inn that night and sat with his mother's file for three hours. Not the sanitized version. The true one, reconstructed from Albrecht's ledger and the excised pages and his own memory, which never let anything go. His mother's certification signature sat on the false alibi statement like a wound that had finally been allowed to scab.

She'd shown Vivian kindness first. Then Voss had shown her the photograph. Then she'd spent twenty years carrying both.

Casper had filed the report under both their names — hers posthumously, his in ink — and the state had accepted it without comment, which was either justice or bureaucracy, and in Harrow Bay those had always been difficult to tell apart.

* * *

Nell Okafor found him at the pier on a Saturday morning, the way she found everyone eventually, because the pier was where the town did its thinking.

She was sixteen now, or nearly — she'd turned fifteen somewhere in the middle of the trial, and there'd been cake at her grandmother's house that Casper had been invited to and had actually attended, which surprised them all. She came down the planks with her hands in her jacket pockets and stopped an arm's length away, which was closer than she'd stood the first day, when she'd left him a note on the railing and vanished.

"You're leaving," she said.

"I'm wrapping up the estate work."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Casper admitted. "It isn't."

The fog had burned off early and the water was doing that thing it did on clear October days — going glass-flat and honest, showing you exactly what was underneath. Crab boats. Old pilings. The cove, far off, where everything had started and ended.

"My grandmother says you're the first adult who ever listened to me like I was a witness instead of a kid," Nell said. "She says that's rare. She says most people listen like they're waiting for you to stop."

"Your grandmother is a wise woman."

"She also says you eat standing up and you alphabetize your notes and you're weird." Nell shrugged. "I said all of that was fine."

Casper looked at her. She looked back, unflinching, the way she had on the pier the first day — except now he knew what that steadiness cost her, and what it was worth. She'd recorded Voss's confession from the bluff with shaking hands and hadn't dropped the phone. She'd sat through the trial's worst days in the front row of the gallery, close enough that her grandmother could see her, and had stayed seated when other adults had gotten up and left the room.

"What's your question, Nell?"

She pulled a folded paper from her pocket. Unfolded it. Held it out.

It was a page torn from a composition notebook, and on it, in careful block letters, was a list. *Observation log — Oct 3:* the tide time, the boat registrations at the marina, the color of Deputy Marsh's truck on four separate occasions, a stranger's license plate copied down outside the inn. Dated entries. Cross-referenced. Footnoted, more or less, in the margins.

"I've been keeping it since August," she said. "Since before you came. I didn't know what it was for. I just — noticed things, and writing them down made them stay put." She tapped the page. "You do this professionally. So. Teach me."

"It's not a profession you can apprentice into," Casper said. "There's no license. No school. Mostly it's just—"

"Noticing things and writing them down so they stay put," Nell finished. "Yeah. I read your report. All forty pages. Twice."

A gull screamed somewhere over the water, offended by something. Casper folded the page along its existing creases and handed it back.

"The first rule," he said, "is that observation is a discipline, not a talent. Anyone can notice. Almost nobody can notice *accurately*. Your memory will lie to you within an hour. Mine doesn't, which is a curse I wouldn't wish on anyone, but yours can be trained to lie less. Dates. Times. Write it down while it's happening, not after. Never trust a detail you didn't record."

Nell had produced a pen from somewhere. She wrote on her palm.

"Second rule. Observation without ethics is surveillance. You're not watching people to use them. You're watching to understand. The moment you cross that line, you're Voss."

She wrote that too.

"Third rule—" He paused. She waited, patient as tide. "Third rule is that nobody hires you to find the truth. They hire you to find a version of it. Your job is to know the difference and choose anyway."

"Is that what your mother did?"

The question landed clean, the way Nell's questions always did. Casper let it sit for a moment, out on the water, before he answered.

"She forgot the third rule," he said. "Or maybe she remembered it too well, and chose wrong once, and spent the rest of her life paying for it. Either way — yes. That's the lesson. That's the whole curriculum, if we're being honest. Everything else is technique."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a yes."

Nell grinned — quick, crooked, gone again in a second, banked like a match. She stuck out her hand and they shook on it, formally, absurdly, two people on a pier making a contract out of nothing but a notebook page and a shared suspicion that the world was worth looking at closely.

"One condition," Casper said.

"Name it."

"When your grandmother asks what you're learning, you tell her the truth. All of it. Including the parts about my mother."

Nell considered this. "She already knows. She remembers your mom. Said she used to buy penny candy at the harbor store and count the change twice." A beat. "Said she was the only investigator who ever asked her name before asking her questions."

Casper turned toward the water so Nell wouldn't see what his face did with that.

* * *

He took the first case that evening, and it was gloriously small.

Mrs. Okonkwo at the bait shop — not related to the Okafors, a point of some local pride and confusion — was convinced someone was stealing from her till. Three dollars here, five there, over months. The sheriff's office had told her to keep better records. Her son had told her she was imagining things.

Casper spent ninety minutes observing the shop from the counter of the diner across the road, drinking coffee he didn't want, and identified the thief before sunset: a raccoon, fat and unrepentant, entering through a gap in the screen door whenever Mrs. Okonkwo stepped into the back room. It had learned the sound of the register drawer.

He fixed the screen door himself. Charged nothing. Wrote it up anyway — full report, dates, times, methodology — because rules were rules, and because Mrs. Okonkwo read all three pages with the gravity of a woman reviewing a court verdict, and then framed the summary paragraph above the register.

*Subject: raccoon. Finding: guilty. Remedy: applied.*

When Nell saw it the next morning, she laughed until she had to sit down on a crate of herring.

"That's your first case? A raccoon?"

"The first case anyone hired me for in this town," Casper said. "And the first one I took because I wanted to, not because a letter summoned me or a corporation retained me or a dead woman's ghost needed avenging." He handed her a fresh composition notebook, bought that morning, the good kind with the sewn binding. "Log everything. Even the boring things. Especially the boring things. Boring things are where people hide."

She opened it to the first page and wrote, in her careful block letters:

*Case 001. Subject: raccoon. Apprentice: present.*

* * *

That night, the fog came back, and Casper stood at his window again and let it.

Down the hill, the lights of the town were coming on one by one — the diner, the bait shop, the Okafor house where Nell was presumably starting her log, her grandmother presumably supervising with tea and commentary. Farther off, past the dark shape of the shuttered cannery, the county jail where Voss awaited sentencing glowed with institutional indifference. And beyond that, invisible, the cove, keeping its water and its dead and its resurrected woman's secrets, waiting for the trial that would finally give it a legal ending even if it would never have a human one.

Vivian had written to him last week. One page, from the county facility, in handwriting whose pen pressure he recognized instantly — the same hand that had started everything, though she'd dictated none of it this time. Every word her own.

*Mr. Sterling,* it began, formal as a deposition, and then abandoned form entirely: *I don't know what happens to people like me after the sentence starts. But I know what happened to your mother, and I know she'd have wanted someone to keep watching this town after we're both gone. The girl with the notebook. Don't let her learn the third rule the hard way.*

He'd written back: *Understood. Case open. Indefinitely.*

Now he watched the fog roll in over the harbor and thought about belonging — how he'd spent his whole life treating it like evidence, something you had to prove, something that could be fabricated or falsified or excised from the record. How Harrow Bay had offered it to him in a handshake that flinched, and then tried to bury him with it, and then, somehow, in the wreckage, actually delivered.

Not granted. Chosen. Provisionally, like everything worth having.

His phone buzzed. A text from Nell, sent at 9:47 p.m., which was past her grandmother's curfew for electronics and therefore either an act of rebellion or urgency:

**found something in grandma's attic. old box. harrow bay historical society seal on it. addressed to V. ASHCOMBE, returned undelivered, 1998. should i open it?**

Casper looked at the message for a long moment. Then he smiled — quick, crooked, gone again in a second, banked like a match.

**Rule one,** he typed. **Record it before you touch it. Photograph the seal. Log the time.**

**then?**

He set the phone down on the windowsill, fog pressing at the glass, the whole town lit below him like a case file waiting to be read.

**Then,** he wrote, **we open it together.**
