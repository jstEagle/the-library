## 11. The Limping Woman

The Wexford Inn sat a quarter mile down the lake road from the Hotel Vesper, close enough to share the same gray water view and far enough away that its owner, Dolores Penn, had spent twenty years pretending the rivalry didn't sting her. Mara had chosen it for exactly that reason. If anyone in Vesper Hollow was going to have a camera pointed at the cemetery gates, it would be Dolores — who photographed everything and trusted no one, least of all the dead.

Mara arrived at nine in the morning, before the inn's breakfast rush, wearing the plain gray coat of a woman on an errand. She carried a tin of shortbread from the hotel kitchen, because Dolores could be bribed but never asked.

"Sebastian." Dolores looked up from her ledger, eyebrows climbing. "You've come down off your hill. Should I mark the date?"

"You should mark the date I bring you shortbread, which is now." Mara set the tin on the counter. "I need a favor, and I'd rather you didn't enjoy it too much."

"Those are my favorite kinds of favors."

"The cemetery gate is visible from your east windows. Your camera over the porch — does it catch the road?"

Dolores's eyes narrowed with the particular pleasure of a small-town woman scenting gossip. "It catches the gate, the fence line, and half the yew trees. Why? What's happened at the cemetery that concerns the Hotel Vesper?"

"Nothing yet," Mara said. "That's what worries me."

* * *

Dolores led her back to the little office behind the kitchen, a room that smelled of coffee and old carpet, where a monitor glowed above a stack of delivery invoices. The footage was kept ninety days, Dolores said, then wiped automatically — a policy she resented, since she believed history ought to be kept whether or not anyone asked for it.

"What dates?" she asked, hand hovering over the keyboard.

Mara had done the arithmetic three times that morning, lying awake in the blue hour before dawn with the cipher's numbers turning behind her eyes like beads on an abacus. The exhumation had been announced eleven days ago. Councilman Harrow's shaking hands, the permit filed quietly through the county, the notice tucked into the *Vesper Gazette* where only careful readers would find it. Eleven days. But if someone had learned of the exhumation earlier — if someone had needed to see the grave before the town did —

"Start twelve days back," Mara said. "Nights especially. Between midnight and four."

"That's a strange hour to watch a cemetery."

"It's the only honest hour," Mara said. "Nobody performs at three in the morning."

Dolores gave her a look that said she intended to unpack that sentence later, alone, at length, and began scrolling.

The footage unspooled in grainy green-gray silence: the gate, the fence, the yews stirring in wind. A raccoon made its drunken way across the frame on the fourth night. A pair of headlights swept past on the sixth. Mara watched with the patience of a woman who had spent ten years watching lobbies — the stillness of it, the discipline of attention without appetite. Most people watched for what they hoped to see. She watched for what was actually there.

On the ninth night, at 2:47 a.m., a figure entered the frame from the left.

Small. Hooded. Moving with a gait that Mara's body recognized before her mind consent to.

The left foot planted, and the right followed a half-beat late, a hitching drag, as though the ground owed the walker something and the walker had stopped expecting repayment. Mara had seen that limp across a hotel lobby when she was nine years old. She had heard it coming down a corridor the night her mother packed a single suitcase and told her to be brave, which was a thing mothers say when they mean *forgive me*. She had heard it in dreams for twenty-five years, always just around a corner, always gone when she turned.

"There," Mara said, and her voice came out level, which cost her more than screaming would have. "Stop there. Go back ten seconds."

Dolores paused the frame. On the screen, caught mid-stride beneath the porch light's weak amber spill, was a woman in a dark coat, face shadowed by her hood, one shoulder lower than the other in the way of bodies that have learned to carry themselves crooked around an old hurt.

"Someone walking to the cemetery at three in the morning," Dolores said slowly. "Nine days before they dug up Edmund Vale." Her eyes went from the screen to Mara and back again, doing the arithmetic small towns do best. "Sebastian. Who is that?"

"A guest," Mara said. "Of a kind."

She watched the figure reach the gate. It did not go over the fence — the fence was climbable; Mara had climbed it herself at fourteen, chasing nothing but the need to stand where her mother hadn't. The figure opened the gate with a key.

A key.

Mara felt the floor tilt gently under her, the way it did on the rare occasions the truth moved faster than she did. The cemetery gates were locked at dusk. The caretaker, old Mr. Brandt, held one ring of keys. The town council held another. And twenty-five years ago, when Edmund Vale had been lowered into the ground before half of Vesper Hollow wept into their good coats, the third key — the mortician's key, the one that opened both the cemetery gate and the receiving vault where coffins waited overnight for morning burials — had belonged to Ruth Amberdale.

The figure passed through the gate and out of frame. The timestamp read 2:51. Mara made Dolores keep playing.

Forty minutes. The camera held its green vigil over the empty road while Mara stood with her arms crossed so tightly her knuckles ached, and Dolores, mercifully, said nothing, though her silence had the texture of a hundred questions stacked like plates.

At 3:33 the figure returned. Slower now. It stopped inside the gate, in the full wash of the porch light, and pulled back its hood.

Mara's breath left her all at once.

Gray hair, cropped short. A face thinned by decades and weathered by whatever life had been lived away from here — but the bones were her bones, the set of the jaw was her jaw, the mouth that pressed itself flat against feeling was the mouth that had kissed her forehead in a funeral parlor smelling of lilies and formaldehyde, twenty-five years and one lifetime ago.

Ruth Amberdale stood on the screen, alive, looking directly into the camera — or so it seemed, so completely did her gaze hold the lens — and then she raised one hand. Not a wave. Something smaller. Two fingers touched to her lips and lifted outward, an old gesture, a mortician's farewell, the one Ruth used to give the grieving families when words would have broken the moment open.

Then she turned, limped back up the road, and was gone.

* * *

Mara did not remember leaving the Wexford Inn. She had some memory of Dolores saying *Sebastian, wait*, and of the shortbread tin being pressed into her hands, absurdly, the way one offers bread to the bereaved. She remembered the lake road, the water flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of dishwater, and her own footsteps keeping time with a sentence that repeated itself in her skull like a metronome:

She's alive. She's alive. She's been alive.

She walked past the hotel without entering it. She walked to the cemetery instead, because her feet had decided before the rest of her had, and let herself in through the gate — unlocked now, in these last days before the exhumation, the town too busy preparing its grief to lock anything.

Edmund Vale's monument rose ahead of her among the older stones, granite gone soft-edged with lichen, VALE cut deep enough to outlast sentiment. The grass around it was neatly kept. Too neatly. Mara crouched and saw it plainly: the turf had been cut and relaid within the season, the seams still faintly visible to a woman who had grown up learning how graves were dressed. Someone had opened this grave recently. Carefully. Professionally. And closed it again with the stitch her mother taught her — the mortician's seam, tight and even, the work of hands that respected the dead even when the dead weren't home.

Not robbing. Mara stood, knees cracking, and looked at the grave the way she'd learned to look at guests: not at what they showed, but at what they arranged.

Her mother had come here nine days before the exhumation. Had opened the coffin — or confirmed what she already knew — and sealed it again. Had stood vigil over an empty box for forty minutes in the dark, and then, knowing the camera was there, knowing everything about being unseen and choosing once, deliberately, to be seen — had shown her face.

Shown it to a camera in a rival inn's window, in a town where nobody watched that footage except Dolores Penn, and Dolores Penn would show it to exactly one person, because Sebastian had come asking.

*Oh, Mama,* Mara thought, and the thought cracked something in her chest that had been calcifying since she was nine years old. *You sent me a letter. You knew I'd come.*

Relief hit first, and it nearly took her to her knees. Twenty-five years of the worst arithmetic a child can do — the sum that always comes out *she left because of me, she died because of me, she chose the road over me* — and every term of it was wrong. Ruth Amberdale had not vanished. Ruth Amberdale had been *stationed*. Like a sentinel. Like a keeper.

And then the relief curdled, because the second arithmetic finished itself, and it was worse.

If her mother had been alive all these years — alive, and near enough to walk to the cemetery at night, near enough to know about the exhumation before the town did — then she had been near enough for everything else. Near enough for birthdays. For the flu Mara had suffered alone at nineteen. For the night the boiler burst and Mara had stood in the flooded basement of the Hotel Vesper, twenty-three years old and drowning in her inheritance, wishing for one adult in the world who belonged to her.

Near enough to watch. Near enough to choose, every single year, not to knock.

Mara pressed her palm flat against Vale's cold granite and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that her mother had not been hiding from the town.

She had been guarding it. Guarding *this* — the grave, the lie, the secret sleeping in an empty coffin. Twenty-five years of vigil over Edmund Vale's fiction. Which meant her mother had known all along what Mara had only just pried out of the ledgers: that Vale was not in the ground, that the death was staged, that the whole beloved history of Vesper Hollow rested on a coffin with nothing in it but air and a mortician's careful stitching.

Which meant Ruth Amberdale had helped bury the lie.

And had stayed — all these years, somewhere, close, close, *close* — to make sure it stayed buried.

Mara straightened. Down the hill, the Hotel Vesper waited with its hundred rooms and its century of ledgers, and in those ledgers, in her mother's own handwriting, a string of numbers still waited to finish telling what they knew. Room numbers. Dates. Amounts. A payment schedule that began the week after Vale's funeral.

Her mother had written those numbers. Her mother had kept the books of the secret — or known who did.

The grief was still there, vast and patient as the lake. But threaded through it now, inextricable, was purpose, sharp as a bone needle: find her. Find the woman on the tape. Find out which parts of the last twenty-five years were protection and which were simply abandonment wearing protection's coat.

Mara turned once more to the camera's distant eye on the Wexford Inn's porch, and — mirroring her mother, mirroring the gesture she'd watched forty times on the office monitor — touched two fingers to her lips and lifted them toward the hills.

*Message received,* she thought. *Now I'm coming to find you.*

Behind her, at the cemetery gate, a car she didn't recognize slowed to a crawl — a black sedan with government plates, moving at the speed of a man reading a map he shouldn't have. It didn't stop. It rolled on toward town, unhurried, and Mara stood very still in the October light and added a new column to her ledger of things seen.

Someone else was watching the watcher.
