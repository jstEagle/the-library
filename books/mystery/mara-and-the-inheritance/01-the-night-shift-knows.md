## 1. The Night Shift Knows

There is a particular hour in Graywater — somewhere between the last bar closing and the first bakery waking — when the town stops pretending to be anything other than what it is. The streetlights hum. The harbor slaps its pilings like a bored man drumming his fingers. And at Kavanagh & Sons Taxi, which has neither sons nor a Kavanagh currently driving, the dispatch radio sits on my desk like a sleeping animal I've learned not to trust.

My name is Zephyrine Kavanagh. Yes, really. My mother had a sense of humor and a book of obscure saints, and between the two of them I never stood a chance. Everyone calls me Zeph, except Dev, who calls me "the night guy," and Odette, who calls me "child," which is generous given that she's only got about twenty years on me and spends most of them pretending she doesn't.

I run the night shift. Have for six years. Before that I was a driver, and before that I was a kid doing homework in the break room while my mother drove the late fares home from the cannery. This company raised me as much as she did, and some nights I can't tell you where one family ends and the other begins.

The night shift knows things. That's not a boast; it's a job description. We know that Judge Hollis takes a cab to the dialysis clinic every Tuesday and Thursday and tips exactly nothing but always says "God bless" like it's legal tender. We know that Mrs. Okonkwo on Delancey Street waters her plants at three a.m. because she says the neighbors' cats are less judgmental then. We know which houses leave their porch lights on for nobody, and which marriages have gone quiet, and which kids are walking home from parties they shouldn't have been at.

And we know death. Not the way doctors know it, or priests, or cops. We know it the way dispatchers do: as addresses and times and the strange, careful voices people use when they call a cab to a hospital at four in the morning. In six years I have catalogued every kind of dying this town offers — the slow ones we drive to hospice, the sudden ones we drive away from, the ones that happen in the back seat with Odette holding a stranger's hand and telling him about her son in Vancouver until the breathing stops. I keep a mental ledger. It's not morbid. It's just true. Somebody has to keep watch over the hours when everything happens, and around here, that somebody is me.

* * *

The shift started the way good shifts do: with nothing.

Dev came through at nine-fifteen to hand off the day's complaints, which is his love language. Dev Kavanagh — no relation, though he'll tell you otherwise after two beers — owns the company the way old captains own ships: suspiciously, tenderly, and with a running list of things wrong with it that only he is allowed to say out loud.

"The number seven's brakes are singing again," he said, dropping a clipboard on my desk hard enough to wake the radio. "Tam says they're fine. Tam is twenty-three and thinks everything is fine."

"They passed inspection."

"Inspections pass cars. They don't make them safe." He glared at the board where the night's bookings hung like laundry. "Quiet night?"

"Harvest festival's over. Town's broke and tired. Should be dead."

"Don't say that word," Dev said, half-joking, the way he always does, and left. That's the thing about Dev — he jokes about death more than anyone I know, and flinches harder than anyone I know when it actually shows up. I'd filed that observation years ago under *owner, eccentric* and thought no more about it.

Odette came in at ten to swap out of her day car. She's sixty-one, drives like a prayer being answered, and has forgotten more about this town than the historical society ever learned. She made tea in the break room — she refuses to call it coffee, even when it is — and settled into the chair across from my desk without asking, which is how she does everything.

"You look tired, child."

"I slept."

"Liar. You look like your mother looked." She sipped. "Before."

Before. There was a whole country called Before, and my mother lived there for the last decade of her life — quiet, careful, checking locks twice, taking the long way past streets she used to cut through. I'd stopped asking why around the time I stopped believing the answers.

"Anything tonight?" she asked.

"Two hospital runs, a pickup at the VFW, and a woman who wants to be driven past her ex-husband's house at midnight. I told her we charge by the mile, not by the grudge."

Odette laughed, low and unbothered. Then Tam burst in from the lot with the energy of a golden retriever who'd found the keys to a truck.

"Zeph! Zeph, the scanner—" He caught himself, glanced at Odette, lowered his voice to a stage whisper that carried to the harbor. "The county scanner. Something's happening at the Merrick plot."

* * *

You have to understand what Aldous Merrick means to Graywater. He's not a man here; he's a load-bearing wall. Founder of the cannery that built half the town, donor of the library, the bandstand, the seawall. Dead thirty years this spring, buried with full honors, his name on more plaques than there are walls to hang them. Schoolchildren learn his biography before they learn their own addresses. There's a bronze statue of him by the harbor looking nobly out at the water, one hand extended toward the future, or possibly hailing a cab — Odette's theory, and I've never been able to unsee it.

So when Tam said the words *Merrick plot*, both Odette and I went still in the same instant, the way a house goes still when the floor creaks at an hour when floors shouldn't creak.

"What about it?" I asked.

"Exhumation." He was scrolling his phone, reading aloud as he walked. "Court order came through last week, sealed, apparently. Crews started digging at dawn. The scanner's been chattering all evening — county sheriff's got two units out there keeping the crowd back. Zeph, there's a *crowd*. At a cemetery. At ten o'clock at night."

"Why would they exhume at night?" Odette asked, and her voice had changed — flattened out, gone careful. I knew that voice. My mother had it too, in the country called Before.

"Doesn't say. Some lawyer thing. Oh — get this — it was paid for privately. Some party petitioned the court. Anonymous beneficiary, the scanner said." Tam looked up, glowing with the pure joy of a man holding information in a town starved for it. "They're going to open the coffin *tonight*. Like, within the hour. The radio guys are losing their minds."

I picked up the handset of our own dispatch radio, mostly out of habit, mostly to have something in my hands. Static moved across the channel like weather.

"Who pays to dig up a founder?" I said.

"Somebody who thinks he isn't there," Odette said quietly.

We all looked at her.

She shrugged, unrepentant, ancient, calm. "Graves are either full or they're empty, child. Nobody spends money finding out which unless they already suspect."

"That's grim," said Tam.

"That's Tuesday," said Odette.

But she didn't laugh, and neither did I, and Tam's grin faded into something younger and less certain. Outside the office window, the town lay dark and ordinary, and I felt — for no reason I could name, with no evidence but thirty years of other people's secrets passing through this room — the whole dark grid of it shift slightly, the way ice shifts on a lake before you hear the crack.

* * *

At eleven-forty, the calls started.

Not to us — to each other. But dispatchers hear the echo of every conversation in a town, because everyone who needs a ride needs a reason, and reasons come through my headset all night long.

A woman from Cedar Lane wanted a cab to the cemetery, then canceled before the car arrived. A man at the Anchor Inn asked if we drove out to Graywater Cemetery "this time of night" and hung up when I said yes. Old Judge Hollis called the office landline directly — he's one of maybe four people who still do — and asked for a car at midnight, destination unspecified, then changed it to "just drive, son, just drive the coast road a while." I put him with Odette, because the judge in a mood is Odette's specialty, and I heard her pull out of the lot at 12:04 with the old man murmuring in the back seat like a kettle coming to a boil.

Tam sat across from me, refreshing his phone with the devotion of the truly converted. "Nothing yet. Scanner says the crew hit the vault at eleven. Now they're — hold on." His face did something complicated. "Now they're saying the district attorney's been called. To a *cemetery*. At midnight."

"Maybe it's procedural," I said, because someone had to say it.

"Zeph. They called the DA."

In the corner of the office, the police-band receiver we're technically not supposed to have crackled, and a voice came through thin and official and shaken: *—all units, all units, be advised, the Merrick exhumation site is now closed to all personnel including press, repeat, closed—*

Then, quieter, meant for someone specific and caught anyway:

*—you're not going to believe what's in this box.*

The channel clicked dead.

Tam looked at me. I looked at Tam. Somewhere out on the coast road, Odette was driving a retired judge along the water in the dark, and I found myself wondering — really wondering, for the first time in a long career of wondering professionally — whether she'd taken that fare by chance or by instinct. Whether the old man had needed a witness, or a getaway driver.

"Zeph," Tam said slowly, "why would the DA need to see inside a coffin?"

"Because coffins are supposed to have people in them," I said. "It's sort of the whole arrangement."

"And if it doesn't?"

I didn't answer. The radio hissed. The clock over the door ticked toward one a.m., the hour my mother used to call the hinge of the night, when things swing open or swing shut. On my desk, under a coffee ring and a stack of trip sheets, sat the company key cabinet — forty brass keys, forty cars going back fifty years, each tag stamped with the same small mark: **K&S**. I'd looked at that cabinet ten thousand times and never once seen it.

Tonight, for no reason, I saw it.

The scanner woke again, one last fragment riding the static before the sheriff's office locked the channel down entirely: a deputy's voice, young, trying very hard to sound professional and failing completely —

*—tell the captain it's empty. Tell him the damn coffin is empty.*

Tam whispered a word their mother probably wouldn't approve of. I reached over and turned the scanner off, which is dispatcher-speak for *we'll know soon enough*, and we sat in the humming quiet of the office, two men and a sleeping radio, while out in the dark the town of Graywater turned over in its bed and began, without knowing it yet, to dream about its founder.

I should have gone home at two, when the shift thinned. Instead I pulled the trip logs for the week of April 1994 — don't ask me why; the hands moved before the mind voted — and found the binding cracked, the pages stiff, and one night torn clean out. Just one. A single missing sheet in thirty years of meticulous records, ragged at the spine, gone.

I sat there until dawn with the gap in my hands, feeling the town's attention turn like a tide, and understanding — the way you understand a storm by the pressure in your ears before the first drop falls — that whatever the coffin held or didn't hold, it had been down there waiting for me specifically.

The brass key cabinet gleamed under the fluorescent light.

Forty tags. One of them, I now noticed, older than the rest, stamped with a date.

April 1994.
