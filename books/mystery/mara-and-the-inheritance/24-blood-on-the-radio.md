## 24. Blood on the Radio

The thing about a quiet night shift is that it's never quiet. It's just quiet the way a held breath is quiet. You learn to hear the difference after enough years behind the dispatch console — the town breathing, the taxis murmuring their positions like beads on a rosary, the static between transmissions filled with everything nobody's saying. That Thursday night, the static had teeth.

Tam was on the couch under a blanket he pretended was for the cold, and I was pretending not to know he'd been sleeping there since the office got ransacked. The log was gone. The locker hung open like a pulled tooth. And somewhere out in the dark was a young man in a company coat who'd run into the fog with a stolen confession and a face like a man who'd finally stopped being afraid of the wrong thing.

"Dispatch, this is Odette. I'm dropping at the harbor hotel, then I'm done for the night. Zeph, your porch light's out again. A man can't live like that."

"Copy, Odette. I'll get right on the porch light. It's been on my list since the Reagan administration."

"Your list is a napkin."

"My list is a *system*."

It was the kind of exchange we'd had ten thousand times, the small liturgy of the night shift, and I held onto it the way you hold onto a railing in rough water. Because the radio had been wrong all evening. Not broken — wrong. A half-second of delay on transmissions that should have been instant. A hiss under channel four that Dev swore was the weather and I swore was someone breathing.

At 11:47, channel two opened and gave us the sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

It came in pieces. That's the thing people don't understand about distress calls — they don't arrive like a sentence. They arrive like wreckage washing ashore, one plank at a time, and your job is to stand on the beach and assemble a drowning man out of driftwood.

"—dispatch — cove road — he's — *copy, copy, dispatch* —"

Tam sat up so fast the blanket slid to the floor. "Is that Marcus?" The judge's grandson. The driver whose coat had been in our office, whose name I'd said out loud for the first time six hours earlier, whose taxi had gone off the board at nine with a passenger flag that never got logged.

"Unit twelve, say again your location," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because inside my chest something had begun to run.

Static. Then, closer to the mic, wet-sounding, breathless: "—the cove — he took it — Zeph, *he took the pages* — there's blood — not mine — I don't think it's mine —"

"Marcus. Unit twelve. Are you injured? Yes or no."

A pause long enough to drive a hearse through.

"Dispatch —" and then the channel folded, a sound like the sea closing over a stone, and channel two was just channel two again, empty and ordinary, as if the night had decided to pretend it hadn't shown us its teeth.

* * *

I did everything right. That's the part that still gets me. I pinged the GPS on unit twelve — dead last signal at the pull-off on the cove road, 11:31, sixteen minutes before the call. I called Marcus's cell — rang through to a voicemail greeting so cheerful it was obscene. I called Dev, who answered on the second ring because men like Dev sleep like cats, one ear open for the sound of the business dying.

"Cove road," I told him. "Marcus's cab. Get Odette. Bring the good flashlight. And Dev — call it in. Sheriff, ambulance, all of it. We don't get to handle this one ourselves."

He didn't argue. That frightened me more than the call had. Dev always argues. Arguing is how he loves you.

The cove road at midnight in April is a tunnel of black pines with the sea muttering somewhere below it, and when we came around the last bend with three vehicles' worth of headlights, unit twelve sat in the pull-off like a dropped toy. Driver's door open. Engine ticking as it cooled. Interior light on, burning its little yellow hole in the dark.

Odette reached the car first. She was in her coat over her nightgown and she didn't care, and I remember thinking that was the most Odette thing I'd ever seen, and then I stopped thinking anything because I saw the seat.

There was blood on the passenger seat. Not much. A smear, a handprint half-finished, like someone had grabbed for it and been pulled away. On the floorboard: the folder where Marcus kept his research — I knew it on sight, because I'd seen him carrying it around town for two years like a man carrying an unexploded shell, and everyone had assumed it was tax forms. The folder was there. The papers were not.

The confession's second copy. Aldous Merrick's last testament, the thing that could unmake five families and a town's founding myth, gone from the seat of an abandoned taxi with blood drying on the vinyl.

"Nobody touch anything," I said, and then, because the night deserved one honest sentence: "Too late for that. Nobody touch anything *else*."

* * *

The sheriff's deputy who took the scene was a kid named Brandt who'd been in Tam's graduating class and kept looking at the blood like it had personally insulted him. He asked his questions. I answered the ones I could and built careful, load-bearing walls around the ones I couldn't. No, I didn't know why Marcus would be at the cove at eleven at night. No, I had no idea what papers were missing. Yes, he'd seemed stressed lately. Who isn't.

Every lie sat in my mouth like a river stone. I was getting good at it. That scared me more than the blood did.

Odette found me at the guardrail, where the drop to Graywater Cove went down into a darkness that was already starting to feel like an accomplice.

"You know whose blood that is," she said. Not a question.

"I know whose blood it probably isn't."

"That's a lawyer's answer, Zeph."

"That's a dispatcher's answer. We deal in what the radio gives us."

She looked out at the water. The storm was still two days out, but the sea already knew about it; there was a heaviness on the horizon like a held breath, the same held breath as the office, as the town, as everything.

"Thirty years ago I washed salt water out of a car's floorboards and kept my mouth shut," Odette said quietly. "I told myself it was one night. One secret. That's how it starts, Zeph. You give it one night, and it gives you thirty years, and then one Thursday it puts blood on the seat of a car with your company's name on the door."

"Marcus ran into the fog," I said. "He's scared, not —"

"His phone's in the glove box."

I stopped. "What?"

Odette nodded toward unit twelve, where Brandt was now photographing the seat. "Deputy found it. Phone, wallet, keys. All in the glove box, all neat. Nobody flees into the fog and leaves their wallet *neatly*, Zeph. Either Marcus walked away from his own life on purpose —" she turned to look at me, and in the emergency lighting her face was all planes and shadow, the sage of the night shift stripped down to something older — "or somebody made sure he couldn't be tracked. And one of those is worse."

* * *

We didn't find Marcus that night. By three a.m. the cove road had a search party, by four it had floodlights, and by five it had the particular exhausted silence of a town that has remembered, all at once, that it knows how to lose people. The tide went out and came back in and didn't offer anything. I stood at the pull-off watching it and thought about my mother's letter in my jacket pocket. *The tide keeps what it's paid for.* I'd read that line as a warning about the past. Standing there, I understood it was also a warning about the present. The tide was being paid again. Tonight. In blood and paper.

Dev found me at dawn, sitting in the dispatch chair I'd come back to because I didn't know where else in the world to sit. He put a cup of coffee on the console without a word, which from Dev was a eulogy.

"Brandt called," he said. "They pulled the security footage from the gas station at the junction. One car on the cove road after eleven. Heading toward town, fast."

"Whose car?"

Dev's jaw worked. "You know whose car."

I did. I'd known since Odette said the word *neatly*. The judge's grandson had gone to the cove to meet someone, and that someone had taken the confession and left blood, and the only person in this town with a bigger stake in those pages staying buried than Marcus was the man who'd raised Marcus to believe his father died in an accident.

"He was trying to make the judge say one true sentence," I said. "That's all. That's all he ever wanted. And now —"

"And now you don't know what he wanted," Dev said, "because the person who has the pages decides the story now. That's what a confession is, Zeph. It's a loaded gun with somebody's name already written on the bullet."

I looked at the radio. Just a radio, sitting there, its little green light blinking its patient idiot blink. Six hours ago it had carried a man's voice in pieces. Somewhere out there, that man was either hiding, running, or feeding the water, and every one of those possibilities had the same root: thirty years ago, five friends wove a net of words around a drowning, and the net was finally, finally pulling tight around *them*.

"Dev," I said. "If the judge did this — if he took Marcus, if that blood —"

"Then we go to the sheriff."

"And say what? *Your honor's grandson stole a dead man's confession and now the dead man's confession is missing and also there's blood?* We'd be confessing to obstruction, tampering, and thirty years of knowing."

Dev sat down heavily in Odette's chair, which he never did, and put his face in his hands, and for a long moment the founder of the taxi company, the man who'd taught me everything I knew about the night shift, looked like what he actually was: a driver who'd moved a crate one bad night in 1994 and had been driving it ever since, in circles, in the dark.

"There's a storm coming Thursday," he said at last, muffled. "Fisher boys are saying it's the big kind. The kind that rearranges the bottom of the cove."

"I know."

"Whatever's down there, whatever Marcus was doing out there — after Thursday, it's not ours to find anymore. It's the water's."

"Then we've got two days," I said, "and one judge, and one notary who shakes when you say *Graywater*, and a folder full of nothing."

Dev lowered his hands. And I saw it cross his face — the thing I'd been afraid of since the locker, since the torn log, since the word *traitor* first put on its company coat. Not guilt. Arithmetic. The cold, tired arithmetic of a man calculating whether the people he loves can survive the truth, and what the truth costs, and whether there's a version of the ledger where everybody lives.

"Zeph," he said. "Before you go to the judge — before you burn the whole town down over some pages — there's something you need to understand about that night in '94. About what we each thought we were covering up." He looked at the radio, at the blood we couldn't see anymore but all could still feel. "Because I promise you, son. Whatever you think happened at that cove — it's worse. And it's also, God help us, *smaller*. Both at once. And the judge knows which."

The green light blinked. The static breathed. And out past the harbor, under a sky the color of a bruise coming up, the tide was already turning toward the storm — carrying, somewhere in its gray hands, either a living man or a finished story, and no way yet to tell which.
