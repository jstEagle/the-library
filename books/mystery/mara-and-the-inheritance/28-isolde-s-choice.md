## 28. Isolde's Choice

The offer arrived the way all respectable threats do: on cream paper, hand-delivered, with no return address and a faint smell of old money trying to pass for old friendship.

I read it twice at the kitchen table of the room I'd rented above the chandlery, because the first reading I assumed I'd misunderstood. The second reading I understood perfectly and wished I hadn't.

*The undersigned parties, in consideration of your withdrawal from all further inquiry into the matter of the Merrick exhumation and associated matters, extend the following: the full restoration of Aldous Merrick's name and standing in this community, the public acknowledgment of your paternity, and the assurance that no legal, civic, or financial impediment shall attach to you or yours in perpetuity.*

In perpetuity. The judge had drafted it himself — I recognized the cadence from the one time I'd heard him recite that alibi, word for word, like a psalm. He wrote the way men write when they've had thirty years to polish a sentence: everything smooth, everything load-bearing, nothing true.

*In consideration of your withdrawal.* As if I'd been considering. As if I'd walked across an ocean, spent my savings, and stood in a cemetery while a crowd gasped at a coffin full of river stones because I was *considering*.

I set the letter down and looked out the window at the harbor, where the water was doing that thing it does before a storm — going flat and gray and attentive, like a dog that's heard a word it doesn't like.

* * *

Let me tell you what I had, at that point, because I'd been keeping an inventory the way other people keep grudges.

I had thirty-one letters. My father's letters, unsent, unsigned, hidden in a second locker at the taxi company like contraband tenderness. Thirty-one years of a man writing to a daughter he'd abandoned and never once finding the courage to put a stamp on it. *Dear Isolde, you are four today. Your mother sent a photograph. You have my chin, which I am sorry about.* *Dear Isolde, you are nine. I watched you from across the street at the Christmas market and you were wearing a red coat and I nearly broke my own rule.* *Dear Isolde, I am dying, and I have watched the news of my own grave being opened, and I think it is the funniest and most just thing that has ever happened to me.*

I had the hotel room above the harbor, dust-sheeted and preserved, with the torn pages of a driver's log in the desk drawer and a confession addressed *to whoever finds the cove empty* — which turned out to be me, though I don't think he'd imagined it would be his daughter. Fate has a sense of humor. It's just not a kind one.

I had Zephyrine, who was at that moment — I knew, because he'd told me with the particular grimness he reserves for bad news — sitting across from Dev Kavanagh in the dispatch office while Dev explained, in fragments, the night he'd driven a crate he'd been told not to ask about. The found family was learning what it was built on. I'd watched Zephyrine walk into that office the way you walk into a room where someone you love is dying: already grieving, already braced.

And I had the offer on cream paper, which was worth more than all of it, if I'd wanted it to be.

* * *

Here is the thing about being abandoned that nobody tells you: you spend your childhood constructing a father out of absence, and the father you construct is always better than the real one, because absence has no bad habits. Mine was a man of mystery and regret, a founder in exile, a tragedy with my surname on it. I'd built him out of money orders signed by a stranger and a mother who went quiet whenever the Merrick name came up, and I'd built him so well that when I finally stood in that hotel room and read his confession — the struggle, the embezzled town funds, the partner whose death he'd spent a lifetime calling an accident — I felt, absurdly, *relieved*. At least he was real. At least the tragedy had bones.

The town, I was learning, had built the same father, only bigger. Aldous Merrick the benefactor. Aldous Merrick the library wing and the scholarship fund and the bronze plaque by the harbor steps. They'd had a hundred years — no, thirty, but thirty is enough — to make him a myth, and myths are load-bearing. That was the whole architecture of the offer: withdraw, and the myth stays standing, and you get to stand under it with your name restored. Daughter of the Founder. It had a ring to it. It would have looked lovely engraved on something.

I made tea I didn't drink and thought about my mother.

Not the mother in the letters — my actual mother, who had raised me on the money orders and never told me where they came from, who had died when I was twenty-two with a shoebox of unopened envelopes I'd only found last year. She'd known. I was nearly certain now she'd known, or guessed, and she'd chosen the money and the silence and my ignorance, and she'd made that choice out of love, and it had been the wrong choice, and I had spent my whole adult life paying the interest on it.

That was the arithmetic the judge's letter was asking me to do again. Take the money. Take the silence. Let them hand you a father made of bronze.

* * *

Zephyrine found me at the chandlery that evening, which surprised me less than it should have. I'd stopped being surprised by how thoroughly the taxi company saw this town. Dispatch hears everything; the dispatcher hears it twice.

"You got the letter," he said, before I'd even opened the door. He was holding two coffees from the machine at the office, which he handed over like a peace offering between nations. "The judge sent one to Dev too. Same paper."

"What did Dev do with his?"

"Used it to level the table in the break room." He sat down across from me, uninvited, which by then I'd stopped minding. "Tam thought it was very funny. Odette said the judge always did have too high an opinion of his stationery."

"And what do you think I should do with mine?"

"I think," Zephyrine said, "that you're going to do what you're going to do, and my job is to make sure you're not alone when you do it." He looked at the letter on my table without touching it. "But since you asked — I've spent my whole life around deaths. Dispatch. You catalog them, after a while. Every death in this town has come through that radio at some hour or other. And the ones that haunt me aren't the sudden ones. They're the ones where somebody knew something and sat on it for years, and by the time it came out, everyone who could have been helped by it was dead." He wrapped his hands around his coffee. "Your father wrote you thirty-one letters. That's thirty-one times he chose to be a coward. Don't you get to be the one who isn't?"

"That's a terrible way to talk about the dead man I came here to vindicate."

"You came here to *find* him. Vindication was the judge's idea." He said it without cruelty, which is the only way Zephyrine says anything sharp. "Isolde. The storm's coming in tomorrow. The fisher — Halloran — says the cove will give up or swallow everything in one tide. Whatever's still down at that eyelet, we get one chance at it. And the judge is handing out cream paper because he knows the clock's running. He's not buying your silence because he's generous. He's buying it because he's scared."

"Good," I said. "I want him scared. I've been scared my entire life on his friend's behalf and nobody sent me a letter about it."

Zephyrine almost smiled. "There she is."

* * *

I want to be honest about the night that followed, because this is the part of the story where I'm supposed to say I never wavered, and that would be a lie, and my father's whole crime began with a lie told to protect something he loved, so I have a professional interest in not repeating his mistakes.

I wavered.

I sat up until three in the morning with the letters spread across the bed like a hand of cards I didn't know how to play, and I thought about what publishing them would cost. Not me — I'd already lost the myth; that was spent currency. I thought about what it would cost the town. The library wing. The scholarship fund that had put three generations of Graywater kids through school, Zephyrine's mother among them. The bronze plaque. The story the town told itself about the kind of place it was. I thought about Dev, whose license was already hanging by a thread, whose whole life was that company. I thought about the notary, who had broken down and told us the alibi was the judge's handwriting on everyone's fear — that five friends had spent thirty years each believing the *others* had done the killing, bound together by mutual hostage-keeping, and that she was terrified of a blackmail she hadn't yet admitted the shape of.

I thought: I could take the name. I could take the restored father, the bronze one, and I could put the letters in a drawer, and in thirty years I could be the notary, shaking hands, keeping a promise that was really just a fear with good posture.

And then I thought about the shoebox. My mother's shoebox of unopened envelopes. Thirty years of a daughter not knowing, because two people who loved her decided she was better off not knowing, and how much of my life I'd spent digging out from under that particular mercy.

I am my father's daughter. I have his chin, which I am sorry about. But I decided, somewhere around three in the morning, that I would not be his heir in the way that mattered.

* * *

I didn't publish them the way the town expected things to be published. No press conference — this town would have eaten one alive. I did it the way my father had done everything: quietly, in a room above the harbor.

The harbor hotel had been closed for years, but the current owner was an elderly woman named Mrs. Pell who remembered my father as "the quiet gentleman in room nine who always tipped for the newspapers," and when I told her who I was, she sat down very suddenly on the lobby sofa and said, "Well. That explains the chin."

I asked her if I could put the letters on display. Room nine, door open, the desk as he'd left it, the letters under glass in the order he'd written them, from *you are four today* to *I am dying*. Mrs. Pell thought about it for exactly as long as it took her to finish her sherry.

"He watched over this town for thirty years and never let it see him," she said. "Seems only fair the town gets to watch him back."

So that's what I did. I spent the morning of the storm carrying letters up one flight of stairs in a fisherman's raincoat, arranging them on the desk under glass borrowed from the chandlery, and taping a sign to the door of room nine that said, in my own handwriting because I couldn't bear to have it typed:

*These letters were written by Aldous Merrick to his daughter between 1994 and this year. They were never sent. He was too ashamed to sign them, and I am not too ashamed to show them. Read them and know what he was: not the founder on the plaque and not the villain in the cove, but a man who did one unforgivable thing and spent thirty years writing love letters to the person he'd hurt most. This room is open. So is the door.*

Then I walked down to the harbor steps — past the bronze plaque, which I did not deface, though I'll admit I considered it — and I put a copy of the offer letter, the judge's cream paper with its *in perpetuity* and its *consideration of your withdrawal*, on the noticeboard next to the tide tables, with one line pinned beneath it:

*This was the price of my silence. I decline.*

* * *

The town found out the way towns find out: all at once, and with opinions.

By noon, room nine had a line down the hotel stairs. By two, the judge's clerk had come by to look at the noticeboard and gone pale in a way I found deeply satisfying. By four, Dev Kavanagh himself stood in the doorway of room nine, cap in his hands, reading the letter marked *Dear Isolde, you are eleven* — the one where my father wrote, *Dev Kavanagh drove the crate that night. He was a boy and he trusted us. Of everything I have done, corrupting that boy's faith is the thing I will answer for longest* — and when Dev turned around, his eyes were red and he didn't pretend they weren't.

"He was wrong about one thing," Dev said. "It wasn't corruption. It was a kid being too dumb to ask questions. That's not his sin. That's mine."

"Then you can put it down," I said, "the same way I'm putting mine down. In the open. It's lighter there. I checked."

He laughed — a wet, broken laugh, but a laugh — and he put his cap back on, and he said, "Your father would've hated this."

"Yes," I said. "And then, after a while, I think he'd have loved it. He was a man who wrote thirty-one letters. He knew what he wanted. He just couldn't do it. Somebody had to do it *for* him. That's what inheritance is, I've decided. Not the money. Not the name. It's finishing the sentences they couldn't."

* * *

Zephyrine found me at dusk on the harbor steps, the storm light going strange and green over the water, the first gusts pulling at the noticeboard behind us until my one-line declaration flapped like something alive.

"Halloran says the tide turns at four in the morning," he said. "Slack water, maybe forty minutes. After that, the storm owns the cove for two days, and whatever's still chained to that eyelet either comes up or goes down for good."

"Then we dive at four."

"You don't have to. You've made your point. The letters are out, the offer's on the board, the whole town is reading my father's—" he stopped, corrected himself, "—your father's handwriting. You could stop here and no one could call it quitting."

I looked at the water. Flat, gray, attentive. Somewhere under it, a chain and an eyelet and a crate with my family's whole truth bolted to the rocks.

"Zephyrine," I said, "my mother let other people decide what I should know, out of love, and it cost me twenty years. My father let shame decide, and it cost him thirty-one letters. The judge is letting fear decide, and it's costing him whatever's left of his soul. I am not letting a *tide* decide." I pulled the raincoat tighter. "Four in the morning. You dive with me or you hold the rope, but either way, you're there."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he did that thing he does — the small, grim nod of a man adding a name to a list he keeps of people he'd drive into a storm for.

"There's just one thing," he said, and his voice had changed, gone careful. "Before we go down. Halloran came by the office an hour ago. Someone's been at the cove tonight already — he saw a light out past the point, and tire tracks on the cove road, and the eyelet chain—" he paused, and I felt the bottom drop out of the evening, "—the padlock on the dive shed was cut. Isolde, I don't think we're the only ones who read the tide tables."

The wind came off the water then, hard, the first real blow of the storm, and somewhere out past the point, the cove began to speak.
