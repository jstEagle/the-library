## 5. Twelve Years of Silence

The county records office smelled like dust and radiator heat and the particular despair of paper that no one had touched in a decade. Lena liked it immediately. It was one of the few rooms in the world that made sense to her — everything filed, everything findable, everything waiting to be read by someone patient enough.

She signed the visitor log at 9:14 a.m., noting that she was the first name on the page for a Tuesday in March, which told her something about the office and something about herself, neither of which she wanted examined too closely.

"Parish records," she said at the desk. "St. Brannoc's, Millbrook-in-the-Vale."

The archivist — a woman in her sixties with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the unhurried air of someone who had outlived every deadline ever imposed on her — looked up without surprise. "Which years?"

Lena had thought about this on the drive over. "Start with 2010 through 2014. And I'll want whatever you have from the parish itself — newsletters, annual reports, minutes if they exist. Not just the deposited copies."

"The parish deposits what it chooses to deposit," the archivist said, with the mild finality of a woman stating a law of nature. "We keep what they give us."

"I understand." Lena smiled the smile she used on people whose cooperation she needed and whose suspicions she didn't want to trigger. "I'm doing some historical research. A family connection."

It wasn't even entirely a lie. She hadn't decided yet whether it was.

The archivist disappeared into the stacks and came back twenty minutes later pushing a trolley loaded with four grey archive boxes and two bound volumes. "Sign for the boxes. Pencils only. No food, no drink, and if you're going to photograph anything, use the flash off — the older newsprint can't take it."

"Pencils only," Lena agreed, and meant it in the way that she meant most rules: as a technicality she intended to honor while violating their spirit completely. She wouldn't photograph. She didn't need to. She never needed to.

She waited until the archivist retreated behind her desk, then opened the first box and began to read.

* * *

Her memory worked like this: she read a page once, and it was hers. Permanently. Not a photograph exactly — photographs fade, blur, sit flat on the surface of things. What Lena had was more like a second reading that happened inside her head whenever she called for it, complete and fresh, with all the texture of the original. She could look at a page she'd seen three days ago and notice things she hadn't noticed the first time, because the page was still there, still open, still lit by whatever light had been falling on it when she first looked.

Other people took notes. Lena took rooms.

She started with the annual reports, because institutions tell on themselves in the boring documents first. St. Brannoc's was a healthy parish in 2010 — membership steady, restoration fund growing, a capital campaign for the roof that had exceeded its target by eleven percent. The treasurer's reports were signed E. Voss, and even four years before Lena met him, Voss's numbers were immaculate. Too immaculate, maybe. She flagged it and moved on; tidy books were a thread to pull later, not now.

2011 was more of the same. The choir is mentioned often — a source of pride, forty voices strong, touring the deanery, singing at the diocesan festival. There are photographs. Lena studied them longer than she needed to. Rows of children in cassocks, squinting into summer light. She memorized faces without deciding to; it was simply what happened when she looked at people.

And then 2012.

The annual report for 2012 was thinner than its neighbors. Not suspiciously thin — parishes had lean years — but thinner in a specific way. The choir section, which had run half a page with photographs for two years running, ran four sentences with no photographs. The tone of the whole document shifted by a degree, the way a room shifts when someone leaves it. You couldn't point to what changed. You could feel it.

The four sentences said: *The choir continued its work under Mr. Fenwick's direction. We give thanks for the gifts of our young musicians. The parish mourns the loss of Thomas Ashby, aged fourteen, in June. Contributions to the memorial fund may be directed to the treasurer.*

That was it. Fourteen lines of typeset grief, compressed into four sentences, and then the document moved briskly on to the state of the gutters.

Lena sat back. In her head, the page stayed open, glowing faintly with attention.

*Mourns the loss.* Not *mourns the tragic loss*, not *our beloved Tom*. No tribute paragraph. No reminiscence from the choirmaster, who in 2011 had written warmly and at length about individual choristers by name. No mention of how he died. Annual reports loved a cause of death; causes of death gave the grief a shape the congregation could hold. Illness. Accident. *After a long struggle.* This report gave nothing, which was not the same as saying nothing. Giving nothing was a choice, and choices like that were usually made by committee.

She pulled the newsletters next.

* * *

The parish newsletter, *The Brannoc Bell*, was published monthly and archived in bound volumes, and it was, Lena reflected around noon, the single most incriminating genre of document ever invented. Newsletters were written by volunteers who believed no one would ever read them carefully, and they recorded, in cheerful type, everything an institution would later need to deny.

June 2012. The issue opened with the vicar's column — a different vicar then, a Reverend Pratt, gentle and long-winded — about the goodness of God in seasons of sorrow. No name given for the sorrow. Page two announced the postponement of the choir's summer tour, *due to circumstances beyond our control*. Page three listed service times. Page four thanked the volunteers who had provided refreshments after the funeral — no name attached to the funeral either, but funerals got refreshments, and there was only one funeral that month, because Lena cross-checked the burial register, which the archivist had fetched for her with the resigned courtesy of a woman watching a stranger do research properly.

Thomas James Ashby. Buried 22 June 2012. Aged fourteen. Officiant: Rev. G. Pratt. Cause of death, per the register's laconic convention: *drowned*.

July 2012 brought the newsletter back to full cheer with almost audible effort. The choir tour was cancelled outright. Mr. Fenwick's column thanked the choir for their resilience *in a difficult season*. And then, buried at the bottom of the events page, between a cake sale and a reminder about bell-ringing practice, a single line:

*The reservoir footpath remains closed following the incident. Please respect the barriers.*

Lena read it three times, though she only needed to read it once. The other two readings were for pleasure — the cold, clean pleasure of a pattern clicking shut.

The reservoir. The footpath. *Following the incident.* The parish knew where Tom Ashby died, and had known within weeks, and had told its congregation in the smallest available type — and then, in the annual report, had declined to say so at all. Institutions hid locations the way people hid alibis.

She wrote nothing down. She didn't need to. But she allowed herself, just once, the small vanity of closing her eyes and reciting the line back verbatim, punctuation included, because some pleasures were private and harmless and hers.

* * *

The coroner's file was harder to get and easier than expected, which should have warned her.

The archivist explained that coroner's records for concluded cases were transferable to the county archive after ten years, subject to redaction, and produced a folder with a form stapled to it. Lena filled in the form with a pencil, printed her name in careful block letters — L. PEMBERTON, INDEPENDENT RESEARCHER — and waited the forty minutes it took for a supervisor to authorize access to a file that had been sitting unopened since roughly the second Obama administration.

The folder was thin. That was the first thing. Death files for children were never thin — there were police statements, pathologist reports, witness interviews, maps, photographs. This one held perhaps thirty pages, and Lena felt the thinness in her chest before her brain caught up and named it.

She read everything twice anyway. Once fast, once slow. Then she closed her eyes and let the two readings merge into the permanent copy.

Cause of death: drowning. Location: the Millbrook Reservoir, eastern inlet. Date: 14 June 2012, time of death estimated between 8 p.m. and 10 p.m. Found: morning of 15 June, by a dog walker, at the water's edge near the sluice gate. Conclusion: accidental drowning. Contributing factors: none identified. Toxicology: negative.

The narrative statement, attributed to an unnamed *pol spokesperson*, ran four paragraphs and contained, as far as Lena could reconstruct, the entire investigative effort:

Tom Ashby had been seen cycling toward the reservoir on the evening of the 14th. He was known to fish there — a rod was found on the bank. The bank at the eastern inlet was steep and slick after rain. It was concluded that he had slipped while fishing alone after dark and entered the water. There were no witnesses. There were no signs of foul play. The family had been informed and asked for privacy.

Four paragraphs. No interviews quoted. No names of the officers involved. No mention of whether the bicycle had been recovered, or where it was found relative to the rod, or whether the boy had been wearing a life vest, or whether fourteen-year-olds fished alone after dark in the rain in June, or —

Lena stopped herself. She made herself stop, because this was the moment she knew too well from three previous cases, the moment where the hunger for a wrong thing to find started manufacturing one. She had learned, expensively, to separate the anomalies from the appetite.

So she did what she always did. She laid the facts out in the room of her mind and walked around them slowly.

Fact: the newsletter of July 2012 said the *footpath* remained closed. Footpaths were closed after deaths when the death occurred on or near the footpath, or when the ground itself was dangerous. If Tom had slipped from the fishing bank at the eastern inlet, the sensible closure was the bank, or the inlet. Closing the footpath suggested the path itself was implicated — that he had been walking it, not fishing from it.

But the police narrative put a fishing rod on the bank.

Two explanations, then. Either the rod was staged — placed after the fact to give the drowning an innocent grammar — or Tom had carried the rod along the footpath toward somewhere else, and the narrative had quietly converted *walking with a rod* into *fishing*. Both explanations required someone to have shaped the story. One required them to have shaped the scene.

Fact: the timeline. Time of death estimated between eight and ten p.m. Rain — the narrative said the bank was slick *after rain*. Lena pulled the weather record from her memory of the archive's own bound newspaper volumes, which she had skimmed that morning while waiting for the coroner authorization, because waiting time was reading time and reading time was never wasted. The regional paper for 15 June 2012 carried a weather roundup: heavy rain had begun at approximately 6:40 p.m. on the 14th and continued past midnight.

So Tom had gone out in the rain. Possible — boys did foolish things — but the narrative leaned on the rain twice, as explanation and as exculpation, *slick after rain*, nobody's fault, weather happens. And yet the newsletter closure order, issued within weeks, closed the *footpath*, dry-weather hazard or not.

Fact: the file was thin. Files got thin in two ways. Either there had been very little investigation, or there had been a great deal and most of it had been removed. Lena turned back to the front of the folder, to the transfer log — the archival chain of custody, the list of hands the file had passed through. Transferred from the coroner's office, 2023. Accession number. Condition noted: *complete*.

Complete. Thirty pages, for a child's death by drowning, logged as complete.

She had solved three accidents before this one, quietly, none of them ever reaching a courtroom, each ending in a settlement or a resignation or a silence that suited everyone including, sometimes, her. She knew the anatomy of a thinned file the way a locksmith knows the anatomy of locks. And she knew the feeling that arrived now, right on schedule, the one that had brought her to this dusty room in the first place: the thrill, bright and electric, of a lock beginning to turn.

Except this time the thrill went cold halfway through, because of the last thing in the folder.

A single sheet, undated, on parish letterhead rather than coroner's stock. It had been misfiled into the coroner's transfer — or filed there deliberately, which was worse — and it was a photocopy of a letter of condolence sent by the parish council to the Ashby family. Standard wording, warm and hollow. But at the bottom, above the typed signature block, a handwritten line in blue ink, added by whoever had signed it:

*We will make sure Tommy's name is kept safe among us.*

Not remembered. Not mourned. *Kept safe.* As if the name were a thing that might otherwise be lost, or found, or used.

Lena recognized the handwriting.

She had seen it yesterday, on the letterhead of the parish council's current meeting minutes, which Voss had left on his desk in plain sight during their interview, either carelessly or as bait, and which she had read in the four seconds it took him to pour her tea. The chairman's signature. Marcus Hale. The same forward-slanting capitals, the same distinctive crossed H with the little tail.

The man who promised to keep Tommy Ashby's name safe in 2012 was the man who chaired the parish council that Elias Voss was so desperately afraid of in the present tense.

Lena sat very still in the radiator hum, thirty pages spread before her, and let the cold finish arriving. The thrill was gone. What replaced it was older and quieter and much less comfortable: the recognition of a shape she'd seen before, in three other towns, wearing three other faces. A community that had decided, twelve years ago, what the truth was allowed to cost — and had paid it, together, and had been paying interest ever since.

She thought of Voss's rehearsed answers. Of Deenie's terror. Of the warden watching doors. Of a vicar due to die on Easter Eve, exactly as described.

*Kept safe among us.*

Someone in this parish had spent twelve years keeping a promise. And someone else, Lena suspected, had just spent twelve years waiting for an outsider to come and break it.

She gathered the folders, returned them to the trolley, and signed the log at 4:52 p.m. At the door, she paused.

"One more question," she said to the archivist. "The Ashby family. After 2012. Did they stay in the parish?"

The archivist looked at her over the beaded glasses for a long moment, the look of a woman deciding how much a stranger deserved to know.

"They stayed," she said. "The mother still comes to the early service, when she's able. Sits in the back row." A pause, delicate as a pencil mark. "And every year on the fourteenth of June, someone puts white flowers on the grave before dawn. Never signs the card."

"Does anyone know who—"

"Oh, we've our suspicions." The archivist began stacking her papers, conversation over. "This is a parish, dear. We suspect everything. We say nothing. That's rather the point of us."

Lena walked out into the grey afternoon with the whole file burning behind her eyes, every page, every gap, every handwritten line — and one new certainty settling into place beside the others, cold and patient as a stone at the bottom of a reservoir:

Before Easter Eve, she was going to have to talk to Tom Ashby's mother.
