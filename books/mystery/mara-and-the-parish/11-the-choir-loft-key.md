## 11. The Choir Loft Key

The key had been in Deenie's coat pocket for three days before she understood why she hadn't thrown it away.

It was small, brass, older than anything else on her ring — a stubby thing with a worn bow, the kind that opened a hundred doors in a building like St. Aldhelm's and none of them important. She'd found it the Tuesday after Vicar Crane's funeral, tucked into the seam of the choir loft bench where the upholstery had split, right where her fingers went every Sunday out of habit, checking the stitching the way she'd checked it since she was nine years old. Her thumb had found the gap. Her fingers had found the key.

She should have handed it to Elias Voss. That was procedure. Anything found in the church went to the treasurer, who logged it in the little green book that lived in the vestry cupboard. Deenie knew the book. She'd helped Elias re-ink its faded columns two winters ago, sitting shoulder to shoulder at the parish office desk while rain came down the windows like a curtain drawn against the world.

Instead she'd closed her hand around it and said nothing.

* * *

She told herself it was grief. Everyone grieved strangely; she'd said so herself, standing at the vicarage door with a covered dish she couldn't remember carrying there. Crane was dead — fallen, they said, down the tower stairs on Easter Eve, in the dark, in the storm — and the parish had folded around the loss like hands folding around a candle flame. There were casseroles. There were phone trees. There was Marcus Hale's steady voice at the council meeting saying, *We take care of our own*, and everyone nodding, everyone relieved to be told what to feel.

Deenie had nodded too. She always nodded. That was the trouble with being a curate: you became fluent in other people's comfort and illiterate in your own.

But the key wasn't grief. The key was a question, and questions were the one thing St. Aldhelm's had taught her never to carry openly.

So on Thursday evening, after the Maundy Thursday rehearsal emptied out and the last of the choir parents drove off through the wet lanes, Deenie climbed the narrow stair to the loft alone.

* * *

The loft smelled of dust and candle wax and old varnish, the smell of every year of her life. She'd sat up here at Tom Ashby's elbow for three singing seasons, back when they were eleven and twelve, their shoulders almost touching on the shared bench, both of them altos with more enthusiasm than pitch. Tom had been the better singer. Tom had been better at everything except keeping quiet, which was the thing that got him noticed by people he shouldn't have been noticed by — though Deenie hadn't known that then. At twelve you think the world is arranged for your benefit. At twenty-four you know better, but only if something forces the lesson.

Tom drowned in August 2012. The reservoir. An accident, they said. He'd gone swimming after choir practice, alone, at dusk, which everyone agreed was foolish and sad and very like Tom. Deenie remembered the funeral: the whole parish packed in, the hymns cracking halfway through, Mrs. Hale weeping into her sleeve while her husband stood straight as a pillar beside her. She remembered Marcus Hale putting his hand on her head afterward, heavy as a blessing, saying, *You hold onto the good memories, Deenie. Don't go digging up the sad ones.*

She had obeyed him for twelve years.

Now she knelt on the loft floor with a pocket torch between her teeth and worked her fingers into the seam behind the paneling where the bench met the wall — the same seam, she realized, the same exact place, because whoever hid the key had hidden it where only someone who sat in that spot every week would ever find it. Where *she* would find it. Or where Tom had meant to come back for it himself, and never did.

The panel gave with a sound like a held breath released.

Behind it: a cavity no deeper than a hymnal, lined with the pale raw wood the varnishers never reached. And inside, wrapped in a plastic bag gone brittle with age, a notebook. A school exercise book, marbled cover, the kind sold in packs of five at the stationer's in town. On the front, in ballpoint, in handwriting she knew the way you know your own name — because she'd copied it off homework sheets, off passed notes, off the flyleaf of a borrowed novel she still owned —

*T. ASHBY.*

Deenie did not open it immediately. That surprised her. She sat back on her heels in the dark of the loft and listened to the building settle around her, the pipes ticking, the wind testing the louvers, and she understood that once she read this book, every comfort anyone had ever handed her about Tom's death would have to be either true or a lie, and she would finally have to know which.

Her mother used to say Deenie could stand in a doorway forever, deciding. She'd inherited it from her mother, along with the alto voice and the tendency to cry at anthems.

She unwrapped the bag. She opened the book.

* * *

The early pages were ordinary, and that ordinariness nearly undid her. Choir schedules. A joke about Mr. Fenwick's conducting ("he counts us in like we're bombs"). A pressed flower — a poppy, flattened to tissue, from the war memorial garden, though Tom was the sort of boy who'd pick one just because it was red. Lists of hymn numbers with ratings out of ten. *All Things Bright and Beautiful: 3/10, boring but easy.* She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, too loud in the empty loft, and turned into something else halfway up her throat.

Then, near the middle, the entries changed.

Not gradually. All at once, the way weather changes. The handwriting tightened. The jokes stopped. And the dates began to cluster around the restoration fund — the fund she'd heard mentioned her whole life, the glorious project that had reglazed the east window and repointed the tower and bought the new boiler, the fund Marcus Hale chaired, the fund the parish was endlessly, proudly raising money for.

*Counted the collection bags again after Hale said not to. Numbers don't match the deposit slips. Off by £400 this week. Maybe I'm bad at maths. Except I'm not.*

Deenie's mouth went dry.

*I asked Mr. Voss about the slips. He went grey and said leave it, Tom, please leave it, and he looked scared, actually scared, so now I'm MORE sure. You don't tell a kid to leave it unless there's something to leave.*

Elias. She saw him suddenly across twelve years — the treasurer's office door half open, a boy's voice inside, and she'd assumed Tom was being scolded for loitering. She'd been fourteen. She'd walked past without stopping. She walked past it again now, in memory, and this time she stopped, and the stopping hurt like a stitch torn out.

*Initials in the ledger margins. M.H. next to the big withdrawals. Also initials I don't know — R.F.? Could be Father? No, Father wouldn't sign anything. Ask Deenie's mum? No. Don't get her near it.*

Deenie read that line four times. *Don't get her near it.* Her mother. Her mother, who sang in the alto row directly behind Tom for six years. Her mother, who had cried harder at the funeral than anyone except the Hales.

What had her mother known?

* * *

The reservoir entry was dated the ninth of August, 2012. Eleven days before Tom died.

*Met M.H. at the reservoir tonight. He said he wanted to "talk man to man" which is a stupid thing to say to a 12-year-old but okay. He said the fund numbers were complicated and adults handled them and if I cared about the choir I'd stop counting. He put his hand on my shoulder and it stayed there too long and I didn't like it. Then he said something I keep turning over:*

*"Everyone here loves you, Tom. That's a rare thing. But love only works when everybody keeps the same promises."*

*I said okay. I said whatever he wanted to hear. Because his hand was still on my shoulder and we were the only two people for a mile and the water was right there and black.*

*He smiled when I said okay. I didn't like the smile.*

Deenie set the book down on the loft floor with great care, the way you set down something that might go off.

The ninth of August. Eleven days later, Tom went swimming alone at dusk, which everyone agreed was foolish and very like Tom, except that Tom had failed his swimming badge twice and was frightened of deep water — frightened of it since he was small, since the holiday at the coast, since the incident his mother told stories about to make him laugh. Everyone in the choir knew Tom wouldn't swim in the reservoir. Everyone. It had been a running joke for years: *Tom and water, oil and vinegar.*

And the whole parish had stood at his funeral and sung *There Is a Green Hill Far Away* and called it an accident, and the coroner had called it an accident, and Marcus Hale had put his hand on her head and told her not to dig up the sad ones.

*Love only works when everybody keeps the same promises.*

They had kept them. God help them all, they had kept them. The casseroles after Crane's death, the phone trees, the hymns — the same machinery, humming along, the same hands passing comfort down the rows like a collection plate. The people who had dried her tears when Tom died. The people who had made her tea and told her he was in a better place and that time would soften it. Some of them had simply believed the lie. But some of them had built it. Some of them had signed it. And they had all, every one, watched a twelve-year-old girl grieve and said nothing, because the family came first, because the parish survived, because *we take care of our own.*

She thought of the new woman — Pemberton, the investigator, sharp-eyed and unsmiling, asking her questions outside the vestry with a politeness that felt like a scalpel. *You seem frightened of something, Miss Ashcombe. Is it the vicar, or is it the past?* Deenie had given her the curate's answer, the smooth one, the rehearsed one, and watched the woman file it away with those total-recall eyes, and had hated her a little for seeing through it.

She wasn't frightened of the vicar. She had never been frightened of the vicar. She was frightened of exactly this: of a notebook in a wall, of a boy's careful handwriting, of the sentence *don't get her near it,* of learning that the arms that comforted her had been the arms that held the water closed over her friend's head — or at least held the door shut while the water did its work.

* * *

She read until the light through the louvers went from grey to blue to nothing, and then she read by torchlight, and then she stopped, because the last legible entry was dated the eighteenth of August, the day before Tom died, and it was short:

*If anything happens to me it won't be an accident. I've put copies somewhere safe. I'm going to give it all to the police on Monday. I'm not scared anymore. Well. A bit.*

Monday never came.

Copies. Somewhere safe. Deenie sat in the dark with the torch cooling in her hand and thought: *the diary isn't the danger. The diary is the decoy. Somewhere in this church — or out of it — there is another set of these pages, and Marcus Hale has spent twelve years believing they were buried with Tom, and if he finds out they weren't—*

The pipes ticked. Below her, in the nave, a door closed — softly, carefully, the way a door closes when someone doesn't want to hear it close.

Deenie clicked off the torch and went cold all over, because she knew that care. She'd grown up in this building. She knew every footstep it owned, and the slow tread on the nave flags, pausing at the tower arch, listening upward toward the loft —

That was not the tread of a grieving parishioner.

That was a man checking whether the walls had given up what they'd kept.
