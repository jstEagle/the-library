## 18. The Bell Tower

I had counted the stairs twice before I understood why.

Forty-one steps from the vestry door to the first landing. Forty-two from the landing to the belfry floor. I knew because I'd climbed them at nine that morning with a torch and a measuring tape and the particular calm that comes to me when a plan is finished and all that remains is the waiting. The stone treads were worn into shallow bowls by two centuries of feet — choristers, ringers, verger's boys, one vicar who had come down them too fast on Easter Eve in the dark and never reached the bottom.

That was the point of the place. Hale would know it was the point. That was why I chose it.

The trap was simple, which is not the same as safe. At four o'clock I told Deenie — only Deenie, and I made her swear it on Tom Ashby's name — that the diary was hidden in the vestry, behind the paneling where Tom had kept his own secrets for twelve years. I told her I'd be back after evensong to collect it. I did not tell her that I had already moved it, that it sat now in a police evidence locker with DI Rourke's signature on the log, or that my phone lay in my coat pocket with an open line to Rourke's desk, screen dark, microphone live, battery at ninety-four percent.

I did not tell her because Deenie could not lie. I'd watched her try twice this week, and both times her hands went to her collar and her voice climbed half a tone. If Marcus Hale looked at her — and he would look at her; he looked at everyone — she would give me away with her face before she said a word.

So I sat alone in the belfry dark above eight bells and waited for the man who had killed a vicar and a chorister and called both of them God's will.

* * *

The bells hung above me like sleeping animals. Great bronze shapes, each with a name painted on the crown stock — I'd read them that morning: *Mara* among them, third from the left, cast 1891, which had meant nothing to me then and everything to me since I'd found my mother's maiden name in a dead boy's diary. I didn't look at her now. Looking at her felt like being watched.

Below, through the louvres, I could see the churchyard going blue with dusk. Evensong had ended twenty minutes ago. Cars pulled away. The floodlights over the porch clicked on and made the wet grass shine. It had rained again — it always seemed to be raining in this parish, as if the sky were trying to wash something and failing — and the smell came up the tower shaft: stone and rain and old rope.

At 8:52 the vestry door opened.

I heard it before I saw anything, because sound is the thing I keep best. The iron latch, the hinge with its dry note, the particular drag of the door's bottom edge on the mat. Then footsteps inside the vestry — unhurried, heavy through the heels. A man who owned the building. A man who had walked these floors for thirty years and never once checked whether anyone was watching.

The paneling creaked. A long silence. Then a laugh — short, quiet, almost admiring — and I knew he'd found the space behind the wood empty, and I knew he knew what the emptiness meant.

His footsteps crossed the nave. Stopped. He stood there for perhaps a full minute, and I imagined him looking up through the tower shaft the way you look up when you already know the answer.

Then he started up the stairs.

He did not hurry. He did not bring a torch. He climbed forty-one steps and turned onto the landing below me, and by the light coming faintly through the louvres I saw him clearly for the first time: Marcus Hale, chairman of the parish council, silver-haired, broad-shouldered, wearing a good coat that rain had beaded but not soaked. He looked like every photograph of every respected man in every parish newsletter I'd memorized. He looked like someone's grandfather. He looked like the reason my mother had never come back here.

"You moved it," he said. Not a question. His voice was warm. That was the worst part, how warm it was.

"It was never here," I said.

"No." He smiled up at me. "But you wanted me to think it was. Which tells me you're not the police. The police don't set traps; they fill out forms." He climbed the last steps slowly, and I made myself stay where I was, against the wall beside the belfry door, with the whole drop of the stairwell at my back. "You're Miss Pemberton. The insurance woman Voss hired. I've been reading about you. Three accidental deaths you unmade with nothing but your memory. Remarkable."

"Four," I said. "If Crane counts."

"Crane was a fall." He said it gently, the way you correct a child. "A terrible fall, on a stormy night, on stairs he'd climbed ten thousand times. Grief makes us see patterns, Miss Pemberton. So does ambition."

"And Tom Ashby?"

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt. Something older and more settled than guilt. "Ah," he said softly. "There it is. You found the diary."

"I found enough of it."

"Enough to be dangerous. Not enough to understand." He took another step toward me, and I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone behind me. My phone was in my right-hand pocket. I shifted my weight so my hand could reach it. "Let me tell you what actually happened, since you've earned some of it. Twelve years ago this parish was dying. Roof leaking, congregation halving, restoration fund bleeding out through a builder who quoted honest prices for dishonest work. And then there was money. Suddenly there was money. Grants, donations, a legacy nobody questioned. This church was restored top to bottom — the glass you admire, the roof, the heating, the choir stalls. Two hundred children have sung in that loft since. Weddings, funerals, christenings. A community that would have scattered like ash stayed together."

"At the price of a fourteen-year-old boy."

"At the price of one death against the slow death of everything else." He spread his hands, and the gesture was so reasonable, so practiced, that I felt the horror of it land somewhere under my ribs. "Tom Ashby stole from the fund. Small amounts, foolish amounts, a boy skimming the collection for cigarettes and bravado. I confronted him. He panicked. There was a struggle at the reservoir — I barely touched him, I swear to you, he slipped, and I stood there in the water up to my knees and I knew that if the truth came out, it wouldn't just be me. It would be the fund, the restoration, the diocese, the families who'd given their savings. Eleven people on that council knew pieces of it. Every one of them chose the parish. Every one of them has carried it. You want monsters, Miss Pemberton, and I'm sorry to disappoint you. You found a family instead."

"A family built on a drowned boy."

"A family built on forgiveness," he said, "which is rather the point of a church." He stepped closer, close enough that I could smell wool and rain. His voice dropped, went intimate, confiding. "Here is what happens next. The diary is gone — destroyed, I'll assume, though it doesn't matter; a dead boy's scrawlings prove nothing after twelve years. The vicar's death will be ruled accidental, because it was. Elias Voss will resign, quietly, for health reasons. And you will take whatever fee remains unpaid and go back to London, where no one knows your mother's name, and you will tell yourself you solved what could be solved. Or—" and here the warmth didn't leave his voice, which frightened me more than any threat could have, "—you stay, and you become the stranger who tore open a hundred grieving people to satisfy your own hunger, and you learn what this parish does with strangers. We are very kind, Miss Pemberton. Until we are not."

I let the silence sit. In my pocket, my thumb found the phone's edge.

"That's a beautiful speech," I said. "Have you given it before?"

Something flickered across his face. "What?"

"To the council. To Voss. To Crane." I pushed off the wall, one step, keeping the belfry door between us. "Crane knew, didn't he. Twelve years he buried it with the rest of you, and then something changed — Tom's diary, or Deenie asking questions, or just an old man's conscience finally getting loud. He was going to talk. So on Easter Eve, in a storm, the lights failed on exactly the stairs where a boy's death had been ruled an accident, and the parish got to bury its second secret in the same dark corner." My heart was going hard, but my voice came out level, and I clung to that levelness like a rail. "You didn't kill a vicar, Mr. Hale. You performed maintenance."

For the first time, the warmth drained out of him.

"You should have taken the offer," he said.

He moved fast for a big man — faster than I'd planned for, and I had planned for everything. His hand closed on my left arm and wrenched me away from the door, out onto the landing, and the drop opened beside us, forty-two steps of worn stone spiraling down into black, and for one white instant all I could see was Crane falling, the letter's exact words, *he will fall from the tower stairs in darkness*, and I understood with total clarity that I was standing in the exact geography of the prophecy.

I did the only thing my body knew how to do. I remembered.

Every detail, seized and held: the seam of his glove, the rain-smell of his coat, the number of steps to the turn, the loose tread on the seventeenth stair that I had marked with chalk that morning — *the seventeenth step rocks, left side, half an inch*. I twisted toward him, off balance on purpose, and drove us both backward across the landing, and when his heel came down on the seventeenth step the rock threw him, just half a second, just enough. I tore free. My coat ripped at the shoulder seam. I slammed the belfry door between us and threw the bolt — a rusted old slide-bolt, installed in 1891, and I blessed whoever had forged it.

He hit the door once, twice. The frame shuddered. Wood older than either of us groaned but held.

"Miss Pemberton." His voice through the door had gone quiet again, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Open the door. Whatever you think you've won tonight, you haven't. There are eleven of us and one of you, and no diary, and no witness—"

"There's a witness," said a voice below.

Hale went still. So did I.

Elias Voss stood at the foot of the tower stairs, holding a torch, and the light shook the way his hands always shook, but his voice didn't. He looked terrible — grey-faced, coat buttoned wrong, a man who hadn't slept since Easter Eve. He looked, for the first time since I'd met him, like exactly what he was: an honest coward who had run out of places to hide.

"Elias," Hale said pleasantly. "Go home. Whatever she's told you—"

"She hasn't told me anything." Voss started climbing. Slowly, one hand on the wall, the torch beam swinging wild across the vaulting. "I've known since the night Crane died. I did the books, Marcus. Thirty years of doing the books. I knew what the restoration money was the day it arrived, and I signed the accounts anyway, and I told myself it was loyalty. And when Aldous started talking about confession and restitution, I wrote that letter — the letter Miss Pemberton thinks I wrote to fake an alibi. I wrote it because I couldn't say it out loud. *He will die on Easter Eve.* I sent it to myself. I hired her—" his breath caught on the stairs, and he steadied himself, "—I hired an outsider, a stranger, someone with no family here, because everyone in this parish who could have exposed you was already inside the family. I needed someone the silence couldn't hold."

He reached the landing. The torch found Hale through the louvred gap of the door, and the two men looked at each other across twelve years.

"You killed a boy," Voss said. "And then you killed the only other man who prayed about it every single day. And you stood at his funeral and sang every hymn."

"I kept this parish alive—"

"You kept yourself alive," Voss said, "and you called it the parish. That's the trick, isn't it. That's always been the trick."

The door shuddered under Hale's shoulder. Once. Twice. The bolt was bending — I could see the screws working loose in the frame, little curls of old paint flaking down — and I backed away across the belfry floor with the bells looming over me, and my hand closed around my phone, and I did the thing I had promised Rourke I would do only if everything else failed.

I brought the phone out and held it up, screen glowing in the dark, the call timer running: 47:12.

"He's been listening since seven o'clock," I said. "Everything. The reservoir. The cover-up. Crane. Your speech about families." I looked Hale full in the face and gave him my own smile, and it wasn't warm at all. "You're right that there were eleven of you, Mr. Hale. You just forgot the twelfth person in the room is a detective inspector with a warrant and a grudge."

Hale stared at the phone. And I watched — I will always watch it, I will remember it at ninety — the moment the charisma failed him. The shoulders dropped a fraction. The eyes went from mine to the door to the black spiral of the stairs, calculating, and finding no exit anywhere in the arithmetic.

Below us, faint but unmistakable, tires on gravel. Doors. Voices. Blue light strobing up through the louvres, painting the bells in flashes, so that Mara herself seemed to swing silently on her wheel.

Rourke's voice came up the stairwell, flat and carrying: "Marcus Hale. Police. Come down the stairs slowly, and put your hands where I can see them."

Hale looked at me one last time. "Your mother," he said quietly, "would have understood."

"My mother," I said, "left."

And that — of everything, that — was what broke him. He came off the door like a man diving, not at me but past me, for the belfry window, for the parapet, for anything, and I caught his sleeve and the sleeve tore and he crashed against the frame of the great tenor bell and the bell spoke — one deep, enormous note that filled the tower and the churchyard and my bones — and then hands were on him, Rourke's officers, three of them, hauling him back into the light, and the note rolled away over the roofs of the town and faded into the rain.

Voss slid down the wall to the floor of the landing and put his face in his hands. I stood in the ringing dark with my torn coat and my shaking phone and I realized I was crying, which I almost never do, and that I didn't entirely know what the tears were for — Crane, Tom Ashby, my mother, or the strange, ruined, loyal, lying family of this parish that I had just helped to break open like a locked box.

Rourke climbed the last steps, took in the scene — the bent bolt, the torn sleeve, Voss weeping, Hale cuffed and silent — and looked at me for a long moment.

"Seventeenth step," he said.

"I chalked it this morning."

"I know. I watched you do it." He exhaled, and something in his face that had been flint for twelve years cracked, just slightly, into something almost like respect. "Interview rooms at the station, both of them. Statements tonight." He paused at the top of the stairs. "Pemberton. When you give yours — start from the beginning. All of it. Everything you remember."

I looked down at the phone in my hand, still connected, still counting, and thought of a hymn page in a dead boy's handwriting, and a nickname I hadn't grown up knowing was mine.

"Inspector," I said, "you might want to sit down."
