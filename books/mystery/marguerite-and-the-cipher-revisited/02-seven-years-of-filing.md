## 2. Seven Years of Filing

The county records annex smelled the way grief would smell if grief were kept in a filing cabinet: dust and toner, old paper gone soft at the edges, the faint chemical sweetness of the dehumidifier that ran day and night to keep the damp from eating the past alive. I had been coming here since I was nineteen years old. I knew the building the way other people knew their own kitchens. I knew which drawer stuck in August, which lightbulb flickered over the 1980s cabinets, which corner of the reading room caught the last of the afternoon sun and turned the dust to drifting gold, as if the records themselves were exhaling something holy.

I also knew, by heart, the contents of every claim file the town had ever generated.

Not approximately. Not in the way an experienced investigator knows a body of work — the general shape of it, the recurring names, the kinds of losses a place like Vasse Hollow tends to produce. I mean I knew them the way a monk knows his psalms. Every page. Every signature. Every coffee ring on every 1974 property appraisal. My mother used to say I remembered the way a camera does, that I didn't learn things so much as take them in whole, and that it was a gift, and that I should thank God for it. She said this the way you'd hand someone a knife and call it a present.

Seven years I'd worked claims in this town. Seven years of walking into kitchens where people had just lost a barn, a father, a livelihood, and asking them, gently, to tell me everything, and then carrying everything out of those kitchens in my head like water in cupped hands, never spilling a drop. The company loved me for it. The town tolerated me for it, the way a body tolerates a conscience — necessary, occasionally unwelcome, mostly ignored.

And so the morning after the exhumation, while Sheriff Dane Colter stood in the annex doorway with his hat in his hands and his face arranged into an expression of bureaucratic patience, I did not need to consult anything to tell him he was wrong.

"Grave robbery," he said. "Simple as that. Somebody got into the mausoleum years ago — decades, maybe — took whatever was valuable, and the family never noticed because nobody visits a founder's crypt except on Founders' Day, and on Founders' Day everybody's looking at the wreaths, not the coffin." He set his hat on the reading table as if staking a claim. "Rafael, that tomb's older than my granddad. Locks fail. Marble cracks. People are vultures. It's not a mystery. It's just time."

"Time doesn't sew," I said.

He blinked at me.

I pulled the photograph from my bag — the one I'd taken yesterday, before the coroner's people had closed the coffin back up, before anyone had thought to preserve the evidence properly, because no one but me had understood that there was evidence. The shroud, folded where the body should have been. Cream-colored linen, gone the color of old teeth. And along the hem, in thread that had once been red and was now the color of a dried wound, a line of embroidered letters.

*Marguerite Ashcombe.*

My mother's maiden name.

"Grave robbers don't embroider," I said. "And they don't leave the one thing in the coffin worth more than the silver they supposedly stole. Dane, that shroud was placed there deliberately. Recently. The thread wasn't faded the way the linen was. Somebody put my mother's name into Edmund Vasse's empty coffin within the last — I'd say ten years. Possibly five."

The sheriff looked at the photograph for a long moment. I watched him do the arithmetic every lawman in a small town does when an outsider's family name turns up in a bad place: how much trouble, whose trouble, can it be made to go away.

"Your mother's been dead seven years, Rafael," he said, not unkindly. "People do strange things with grief. Maybe somebody who loved her wanted her name near the founder. Sentiment."

"In a coffin that's been sealed since 1968?"

"Sealed coffins get opened." He picked up his hat. "I'll file it as desecration of a burial site. That gets us a report number, and a report number gets us nothing, and in six months it'll be a line in the year-end summary." He paused at the door. "You should let it be, is what I'm saying. Some files are better left closed."

He meant it as advice. I heard it as a dare.

* * *

Here is what I know about Vasse Hollow, and how I know it.

I know that in March 1961, Hollis Vasse — Edmund's eldest son — insured the east orchard for a figure so inflated that the company's regional office flagged it twice, and that the claim that came through that November, after a frost that the weather service recorded as two degrees too warm to have caused the damage, was paid in full anyway. I know the adjuster's name was Gerald Pruitt, and that his signature on the final settlement is different from his signature on the initial inspection report — the *G* looped differently, hurried, as if his hand had been guided. I know that Gerald Pruitt left town the following spring and that no one ever asked why, because the orchard money had gone into the ground of half the founding families, and gratitude makes a very effective fertilizer.

I know that in 1968 — the year Edmund Vasse died — the Vasse estate carried a personal effects policy that listed, among other items, "one strongbox, iron, contents undisclosed," valued at an amount that would have bought the whole east orchard twice over. I know that strongbox was never itemized in the estate inventory. I know that the claim for its "loss in transit" was filed nine days after the funeral by a lawyer whose office, according to the annex's own cross-reference cards, also handled the probate. The same lawyer. The claim was paid. I have read that file so many times the pages have gone soft as cloth under my fingers, and every time I read it I notice something new, the way you can stare at a word until it stops meaning anything and then, later, means too much.

I know that in 1972, a house fire on Cordwainer Lane killed a woman named Delia Marsh, and that the fire marshal ruled it accidental, and that the life policy paid out to a beneficiary whose name was blacked out with such care — not marker, but cut and replaced with an archival patch — that whoever did the blacking-out understood exactly how archives work. I know that patch was applied sometime after 1990, because the adhesive is a conservation-grade acrylic that wasn't in common use before then. Someone had gone back, decades later, and edited the past.

I know that the town clerk's office keeps a "restricted" drawer that is not locked but is simply never mentioned, and that in it are the claim files of the founding families, pulled from general circulation by a standing order no one living can produce in writing. I know that Corwin Ashe, the archivist, has a key to that drawer and a chair by the window and a habit of humming hymns while he works, and that when I was eleven years old and my mother died, it was Corwin who sat with me in this same reading room and taught me the filing system as if he were handing me a legacy. Alphabetical. Chronological. Cross-referenced. *A place for everything,* he used to say, *and everything in its place, and then nothing can hurt you.*

I believed him for a long time.

I know all of this the way I know the backs of my own hands. And yesterday, standing over an open coffin with the November wind coming through the mausoleum door like something with a job to do, I had understood — with the cold, clean shock of a number refusing to balance — that everything I knew was arranged. Alphabetized. Cross-referenced. *In its place.*

And that the one thing in its place that should never have been there was a shroud with my mother's name stitched in thread the color of a wound.

* * *

After the sheriff left, I stayed in the reading room and did what I do when the world refuses to make sense: I made sense of it anyway, by force.

I laid out the Vasse files on the long oak table in chronological order. Death claim, 1968. Strongbox claim, 1968. Estate probate cross-references. The mausoleum maintenance policy — the reason I'd been standing at that graveside at all, the policy the historical society had taken out two years ago to cover the crypt against structural damage, the policy whose terms required an inspection of the interior, which is the only reason anyone had opened that coffin in living memory. The insurance company had done what the town never had. We had looked inside the founder's grave because a policy rider told us to.

And it was empty.

I sat with that. I let my memory do what it does — the involuntary, total recall of it, the pages turning themselves behind my eyes. The mausoleum policy application, signed by the historical society's president, a Vasse descendant, a woman named Eleanor Vasse-Croy, whose signature I had seen before. Where. Where had I seen it before. The pages turned. The 1972 fire file. The archival patch. The conservation adhesive. The historical society's restoration grant application, 1991, co-signed by — Eleanor Vasse-Croy. The same hand. The same small, deliberate *E* with its crossbar like a closed gate.

I wrote it down anyway, on a legal pad, because Corwin had taught me that memory unrecorded is memory unaccounted for, and because the act of writing slows the recall down to the speed of thought, and thought, unlike recall, can be checked.

That was the comfort of it, you understand. That was the gift my mother gave me and Corwin refined: a mind where everything has a place. Seven years of files, and before that a childhood of them, and before that — I was beginning to suspect — something older, something sewn into the lining of the town itself, thread the color of a wound. I could close my eyes and walk the stacks. I could tell you the exact shade of rust on the 1958 flood claim photographs. I could tell you that the 1968 strongbox file is missing page six, and that the missing page's stub — the little tab of paper left in the binding when a sheet is removed — is still there, and that whoever removed it used a clean blade, and that the stub has yellowed exactly as much as its neighbors, which means the page was taken recently, not decades ago. Recently. By someone with access. By someone with a blade and a conservationist's patience and a hymn on his lips, or a clerk's, or a rival's.

The comfort of order, I have learned, is a room you can lock from the inside.

Because here is the other thing my memory gave me, the thing I had never said aloud, not to Dane, not to Corwin, not to Sol Reyes, who watched me across the annex parking lot that afternoon with the particular stillness of a man deciding whether to break a rule. Here is the thing: every secret in this town was insured. That is what a small town does with its sins — it doesn't confess them, it underwrites them. It prices the risk, sets the premium, files the loss, and pays out quietly to the people who must never speak. Seven years of files, and the files were not records of accidents. They were a ledger. A ledger of everything the founding families had ever needed buried, with claim numbers instead of names and settlements instead of silence, and I had spent seven years reading it cover to cover and calling it work.

And someone had read it too.

Someone had read it more carefully than the sheriff, more carefully than the company, possibly more carefully than me — someone who knew that the mausoleum policy required an interior inspection, someone who knew that the 1968 file was missing page six, someone who knew my mother's maiden name and where to put it for maximum effect. Someone who understood that in this town, the archives are not a record of the past.

They are the past. And they can be edited.

I stood at the window as the light went amber over the annex parking lot, and I watched Sol Reyes get into his cruiser and not leave, and I felt the cage close around me with a sound like a drawer sliding home. Everything in its place. Including me. Including my mother's name, stitched into a dead man's shroud by hands I could not yet see, filed under a claim number that did not yet exist.

There was only one person in this town who had touched more of these files than I had.

I took my coat off the hook. On the legal pad, under Eleanor Vasse-Croy's name, I wrote a single line:

*Who requested the 1968 strongbox file, and when?*

The annex's request ledger would answer that. The request ledger lived in the archive.

And Corwin Ashe had always kept the archive open late, for me. He always would.

I turned off the reading room light and walked into the dark of the stacks, toward the sound — faint, familiar, terrible — of a man humming hymns among the files.
