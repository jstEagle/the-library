## 7. The Man Who Reads Everything

The rain came in off the harbor that evening the way it always did in Vasse Hollow — sideways, patient, as if it had all the time in the world to wear the town down to its bones. Rafael Hartwell stood at her kitchen window with a cup of tea gone cold in her hand and three names written on a napkin she'd stolen from the Blue Anchor Diner two years ago.

Three names. That was all the world of the archive amounted to, when you boiled it down.

She turned the napkin over, though there was nothing on the back but a grease ghost shaped like the state of Virginia.

*Delia Marsh. Town clerk. Twenty-two years at the records vault.*
*Tobias Fenn. Independent adjuster. Had fought her for every contested claim since 2011.*
*Corwin Ashe.*

Her pen — she hadn't picked it up, but it was in her hand anyway, she noticed, the way a hand finds a knife in the dark — hovered over the third name without touching it.

Outside, the gutters ran loud. Somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, which in this town amounted to the same thing.

Rafael sat down at the table and made herself think like an investigator instead of like a daughter. It was a discipline, that. Like holding your breath underwater. You could do it only so long before your body betrayed you.

*Access.* That was the first filter. Whoever had staged the empty coffin — whoever had embroidered her mother's maiden name into a burial shroud with stitches fine enough to pass for Victorian work — needed more than access to the mausoleum. They needed access to *memory*. To the paper memory of Vasse Hollow: the claim files, the probate records, the correspondence, the sealed settlements, the whole sedimentary layers of what this town had insured against and then agreed to forget.

The archive lived in the basement of the municipal building, a room Rafael knew better than her own bedroom. She had grown up in it, in a sense — doing homework at Corwin Ashe's reading table while he moved among the shelves with his white cotton gloves and his soft voice, telling her that paper was the most honest thing people ever made, because they wrote it before they learned to lie well.

*Paper is honest,* he used to say. *People are just paper that learned to walk around.*

She had believed him for thirty years.

* * *

Delia Marsh first.

Rafael drove out to the clerk's house the next morning through a fog so thick the telephone wires appeared and vanished like piano strings being strung by invisible hands. Delia answered the door in a quilted robe, her gray hair pinned up with what looked like two pencils, and her eyes — sharp, small, unfooled — went immediately to the folder under Rafael's arm.

"If you're here about the Vasse exhumation," Delia said, "the answer's no comment, the answer's always been no comment, and the answer will be no comment at my funeral."

"I'm not here about the exhumation." Rafael stepped onto the porch, into the smell of wet cedar and old coffee. "I'm here about the will file. The codicil. Someone's requested it twice this year, and I want to know who."

Something moved behind Delia's face — quick, submerged, gone. The look of a woman deciding how much of the truth was safe to surface.

"Request logs are confidential."

"They're also public record under the state sunshine statute, section four, subsection nine. I've read it. I've read everything." Rafael let that sit. "You know I have."

Delia's jaw worked. Rain dripped off the porch eave between them, counting seconds.

"You come into my vault maybe twice a year, Ms. Hartwell," Delia said finally, "and you read like other people eat when they're starving. You know what I respect about you? Nothing. Absolutely nothing. But I'll tell you this for free, because your mother once helped me with a flood claim nobody else would touch." She pulled her robe tighter against the damp. "I didn't pull that will file for anybody. Both requests came through the after-hours drop box. Signed request slips, proper forms, proper authorization numbers. Somebody who knew our system better than I do."

"Who has authorization numbers?"

"Three people." Delia held up fingers. "Me. The archivist. And whoever stole a blank authorization pad sometime before 2009, which I have never proven and never stopped looking for." She looked at Rafael with those small bright eyes. "You want a fourth name? Ask yourself who taught you to read files the way you do. Because whoever requested that will reads exactly like you, honey. Exactly like you."

Rafael walked back to her car through the fog and did not start the engine for a long time.

* * *

Tobias Fenn kept an office above the hardware store, one window, a desk buried under other people's misfortunes. He was younger than Rafael by five years, hungrier by a factor of ten, and he greeted her with the smile of a man who smelled blood in the water and wasn't yet sure whose.

"Hartwell." He leaned back until his chair creaked. "Come to surrender the Kessler appeal?"

"Come to ask where you were on the night of October ninth."

Fenn's smile didn't fall, but it thinned, the way ice thins over a creek — still smiling, suddenly less trustworthy. "That's a strange question."

"The judge's crypt was opened between ten p.m. and four a.m. according to the groundskeeper's log. Someone with knowledge of the original Vasse interment replicated the staging exactly. Down to the orientation of the shroud folds. That's not something you learn from a newspaper, Tobias. That's something you learn from a case file. From *my* case files, some of them, which were never digitized."

"And you're asking me." He laughed, short and hard. "You've spent eleven years beating me on every claim worth more than a used car, and now there's something weird in the cemetery and I'm your first suspect?"

"You're not my first suspect. You're my second."

"Flattered." He came forward, elbows on the desk, and for a moment the hunger in him looked less like ambition and more like fear. "For the record, Hartwell — I was at the Anchor until midnight, forty witnesses, then home. And for the record twice — I couldn't stage anything that delicate. You know why? Because I don't have the patience. I skim. I always skimming. You're the one who reads every word." His voice dropped. "You're the one who *was taught* to read every word. So before you go digging through my alibi, maybe ask yourself why the person who raised you knows these archives better than God does."

Rafael left without answering. In the stairwell, the smell of wood varnish and sawdust rose around her, and she gripped the rail and thought: *he's right, that's the ugly thing, he's right about the reading.*

And the reading pointed home.

* * *

Corwin Ashe lived in a narrow house behind the Congregational church, in a garden gone deliberately wild, where foxglove crowded the fence and a stone birdbath wore a cap of moss like a green beret. She had brought him flowers. She noticed that about herself halfway up the walk — that she had bought flowers, instinctively, the way you bring an offering to an altar — and hated the instinct even as she obeyed it.

He opened the door before she knocked. He always seemed to know when she was coming; she had long ago filed it under *archivist's intuition*, that sixth sense librarians develop for footsteps in their stacks.

"Rafael." His face did the thing it always did — softened, gathered her in, made the doorway feel like shelter. He was seventy-four and moved carefully now, one hand on the frame, but his eyes were still the clearest things about him, pale gray, unhurried. "You look like a woman carrying weather. Come in before it breaks."

Inside, the house smelled of tea and binding glue and the faint vanilla of aging paper — the smell of her entire childhood. Every wall was shelved. Not archives, exactly; Corwin Ashe did not keep official records at home. But books, ledgers, orphaned documents he'd rescued from floods and estate sales, the flotsam of a town's paper life, curated with the tenderness other men reserved for grandchildren.

He made tea without asking. He always made tea without asking. He set the cup before her and folded his hands and waited, because he had never once in thirty years pushed her to talk before she was ready.

That patience, which had felt like love her whole life, now looked like something else. A man accustomed to waiting for documents to surface. A man comfortable in the silence between entries.

"The judge," she said. "Harlan Vasse-Pryce. His crypt."

"Yes." Grief moved across his face like a cloud shadow, genuine, she was almost sure it was genuine. "A terrible business. He was a difficult man, but no one deserves to be — rearranged." He chose the word carefully. Archivists chose words carefully.

"Did you know him well?"

"As well as anyone. He came to the vault every March, on the anniversary of the '68 settlement. Never said why. Just sat with the boxes for an hour and left." Corwin sipped his tea. "Guilt keeps its own calendar. I've seen it in the files a hundred times — claims filed on anniversaries, policies dated to birthdays. Paper remembers what people try to forget."

"You've said that before."

"I say most things before." He smiled gently. "It's the privilege of age."

Rafael set down her cup. Her hands were steady. She had trained them to be steady in rooms full of grieving beneficiaries, and she trained them now.

"Corwin. The will file. The codicil. Two requests this year, both through the after-hours drop box, both with valid authorization numbers. Delia says only three sets exist. Hers. Yours." She watched his face. "And a stolen pad from 2009."

The silence lasted four seconds. She counted. She always counted.

"You think I requested it?" He didn't sound wounded. That was the terrible part — he sounded *interested*, the way he sounded when a rare document crossed his table. "Rafael. Child. I've had legitimate access to that file for fifty years. If I wanted to read Edmund Vasse's will, I would simply walk downstairs and read it, the way I've read everything in that vault since before your mother was born." He refolded his hands. "Why would I sneak into my own library?"

"Because you wouldn't. Which means either someone forged the authorization—" 

"—or the third possibility," he said quietly, "which you haven't said out loud yet, though you've been carrying it since the coffin opened. I can see it sitting on your shoulder like a crow."

Rafael's throat tightened. "Which is?"

"That whoever did this learned the system from someone inside it. Learned the staging from the interment records. Learned the stitch patterns from the seamstress shop's surviving invoices. Learned the cipher's numbering scheme from the 1960s claim forms." His clear gray eyes rested on her, kind and bottomless. "Someone taught all of that to somebody. That's the third possibility, isn't it? That the thief was *apprenticed*."

The rain arrived then, all at once, hammering the foxglove flat against the fence, and neither of them moved.

"I should go," Rafael heard herself say.

"You should eat something first. There's soup." He was already rising, already moving toward the kitchen with his careful gait, already assuming — as he had assumed for thirty years — that she belonged in this house, at this table, inside the warm circumference of his care. "Sit. Whatever storm you're tracking, it'll keep. Paper keeps. That's the whole point of paper."

She sat. She ate the soup. It was good; it was always good; he had been feeding her since she was eleven years old and feral with grief, and every spoonful was a small argument against suspicion.

But when she carried her bowl to the sink, she passed the shelf by the window — the shelf where he kept his personal notebooks, forty years of them, cloth-bound, spines numbered in his own fair hand. Volume Thirty-One lay open where he'd left it that morning, weighted with a river stone.

She did not mean to read it. She told herself afterward that she did not mean to.

But her eye — the eye he had trained, the eye that read everything — caught the last line of the morning's entry, written in ink still fresh enough to shine:

*She is close now. Closer than Marguerite ever got. I must decide what she is ready to carry, and what I must carry for her.*

Rafael put the bowl in the sink. She thanked him for the soup. She kissed his papery cheek at the door and let him hold her shoulders for a moment, his hands light as leaves.

"Whatever it is," he said against her hair, "you won't have to carry it alone. That's a promise I made a long time ago, to someone who couldn't hear me anymore."

She drove home through the rain with the wipers going and her heart going harder, and she did not let herself cry until she was parked outside her own house, alone, where the only witness was a cold cup of tea and a napkin with three names on it.

She picked up her pen. Over the first two names she drew clean lines.

Over the third, her hand hovered — hovered the way it had hovered for two days — and then, instead of crossing him out, she wrote beside it, small and precise, in the margin where investigators write the things they cannot yet prove:

*Volume Thirty-One. Morning of the 14th. He knew I'd come before I knocked.*

Then, beneath it, the sentence that changed the shape of everything:

*What did Marguerite get close to?*

Outside, the rain kept falling on the town that Edmund Vasse had built and someone was unbuilding, file by file, grave by grave — and somewhere in a narrow house behind the church, a gentle old man was writing in a notebook, deciding what she was ready to know.
