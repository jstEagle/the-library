## 13. The Third Staging

The call came at 4:17 in the morning, and I knew before Sol said a word.

Not because of anything in his voice — his voice was careful, stripped of everything, the way voices go when they're carrying something heavy across a long distance. It was the hour itself. In seven years of reading this town's claim files I had learned its grammar of catastrophe: fires happen at two in the morning, drownings at dusk, heart attacks over Sunday supper. And staged deaths — theatrical, deliberate, meant to be found by one specific person — those happened in the small hours, when the town's attention was thinnest and the message could arrive undiluted.

"Rafael." A breath. "It's Dolores Quintero. The town clerk."

"Dead?" I asked, because that was the question the pattern demanded, and even as I said it I understood it was the wrong question, that he would not have called me with this particular care if it were the right one.

"No," Sol said. "That's the thing. She's alive. But Rafael — you need to come see where they left her."

* * *

The Vasse Historical Society occupies the old counting house on Meridian Street, a brick building with a clock face frozen at eleven minutes past four — some failure decades ago that no one ever paid to fix, so that the town's official memory now lives inside a building that believes it is permanently almost dawn. They had found Dolores there at three, or rather the night deputy had found her, answering an alarm that turned out to be tripped deliberately, from inside, by a hand that wanted witnesses.

She was sitting up when I arrived, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of dust and camphor, drinking tea someone had sweetened too heavily. Her glasses were missing. Without them her face looked unfinished, younger, the eyes naked and darting. There was a bruise blooming along her jaw like ink dropped in water, and her wrists, when she moved them, told the story of being bound — not tightly, I noticed. Not cruelly. Bound the way you'd bind something you didn't want damaged.

"Dolores," I said, crouching to her level. "It's Rafael Hartwell. Can you tell me what happened?"

Her gaze found mine and held it, and what I saw there frightened me more than any empty coffin had. Not shock. Recognition. She had been expecting this — or something shaped like this — for longer than she'd admit.

"He knew my mother's maiden name," she whispered. "He used it. Nobody's used that name since she died."

* * *

They had left her in the display room.

I had been in that room before, years ago, on some civic errand I no longer remembered — a long gallery lined with glass cases holding the town's relics: Edmund Vasse's surveyor's compass, the original plat maps drawn in iron-gall ink, a wedding dress from 1902 gone the color of weak tea. And at the far end, dominating the room the way a period dominates a sentence, stood the display coffin.

It was a Victorian piece, walnut and brass, built in the era when funeral parlors kept such things on hand the way pharmacies kept tonics — a demonstration model, an advertisement for the inevitable. The Historical Society had filled it with mannequin hands and mourning cards and a placard about Victorian death customs. Tourists photographed their children pretending to sleep in it. The town had made a toy of its own mortality, which is either healthy or obscene depending on how well you've slept.

Tonight the coffin was empty. Truly empty — the props removed, the placard taken down, the interior wiped clean and waiting. And beside it, laid out on the floor with a precision that made my skin crawl, they had arranged Dolores Quintero on her back, arms at her sides, ankles together, head tilted slightly toward the coffin as though listening to it. Around her, in a careful ellipse, someone had scattered dried rose petals — brown, ancient, the kind that crumble to dust if you breathe on them wrong. Petals from a preserved funeral wreath. I recognized them later; there was a wreath in the archive from Vasse's 1968 memorial service, sealed in a case, catalogued under *Commemorative Ephemera*. Someone had opened that case. Someone had taken flowers from a dead man's memorial and arranged them around a living woman to make her look like a corpse rehearsing.

On her chest, folded once, they had left a single sheet of paper.

Sol handed it to me in an evidence bag, but I barely needed the plastic between us. I had read enough of this killer's handwriting — if it could be called handwriting, this typescript, this clean archival voice — to know its cadence the way you know a caller's breathing.

The note read:

**CLAIM NO. 3 — PENDING.**

**You are reading faster than expected. That is good. That is necessary. But you are reading the wrong pages, Miss Hartwell, and time is a finite resource in every file.**

**Dolores Quintero processed the probate transfer in 1994. She knows what passed through her hands. Ask her what she filed and did not read.**

**Or stop. Walk away. Close your mother's folder and let the dead keep their arrangement. The choice has always been yours. It was hers first.**

No signature. No threat against Dolores beyond the staging itself — and yet the staging *was* the threat, wasn't it? Here is what we do with people who stand between you and the truth. Here is the shape waiting for you.

But it was the last line that I read six times, standing in that cold gallery while deputies photographed rose petals and Sol watched me with the particular stillness of a man trying not to hover.

*It was hers first.*

Hers. Whose? Dolores's? My mother's?

Marguerite's?

The clock above us all said four minutes past eleven, forever. Outside, actual dawn was still an hour off, and I felt time compress around me like a fist closing — the sense, familiar now and terrible, that I was being managed. Steered. Every discovery I'd made, every door that had swung open, had opened onto a corridor someone else had built.

And now the builder was telling me, politely, to hurry up.

* * *

Dolores talked in the county hospital's family lounge, under fluorescent light that made everyone look embalmed. She held her tea in both hands and stared into it as if the leaves might arrange themselves into an answer.

"I never met him," she said. "The man. Woman. I don't know. I was locking up — I always lock up late on Thursdays, the microfilm needs the machine cooled down before you shut it off, it's —" She caught herself, embarrassed by the detail, and I wanted to tell her not to be. Detail was the only religion I'd ever practiced. "Someone was already inside. He must have come through the loading dock. He put something over my mouth and when I woke my wrists were taped and he was just — tidying. That's the word I keep coming back to. Tidying. Like a librarian who found books out of order."

"What did he say?"

"Almost nothing. He asked me one question." Her hands tightened on the cup. "He asked me if I remembered the Quimby transfer. 1994. The probate materials that came through when the estate finally settled the last of the Vasse trusts. And I told him the truth, which is that I process hundreds of transfers, I couldn't — and then he said—" Her voice frayed. "He said, *'You stamped them received. You stamped them without reading them. Everyone in this town stamps and doesn't read.'* And then he put the tape back over my mouth and arranged me on the floor like I was — like I was an exhibit. He adjusted my hands twice. Twice. Like he wasn't satisfied with the composition."

Sol wrote it all down. I let him, because my mind had gone somewhere else entirely — back to Chapter Four of my own investigation, to the probate lawyer's office, to the will with its backward-counting codicil, and to the fact that someone had requested the will's file twice this year.

The Quimby transfer. 1994. Twenty-six years after Vasse's supposed death and burial. What passes through probate twenty-six years later? Trusts maturing. Contingent bequests activating. Documents that were sealed at the founder's death and unsealed on a schedule he set himself.

A will that counts backward. A codicil with a numbering scheme borrowed from insurance claim forms. And a clerk who stamped it *received* without reading it, because everyone in this town stamps and doesn't read — everyone except the man who reads everything.

"Dolores," I said gently. "Do you know where those transferred documents went after you stamped them?"

She looked up at me, and for a moment the naked eyes behind the missing glasses steadied.

"That's the thing, Miss Hartwell. That's why I've been sick about this for two days — yes, I do, I figured it out last night lying on that floor with nothing to do but think. Those boxes came through my office and went straight to the archive vault. To records retention." She swallowed. "To Corwin Ashe."

* * *

I didn't go home. I went to the diner on Canal Street and sat in the corner booth with the files spread out under my coat, because home felt surveilled now, and maybe it had always been surveilled, and maybe the man who raised me had a key.

Let me say plainly what that sentence costs me to write, even here, even now. Corwin Ashe taught me to read. Not letters — my mother saw to letters before she died — but *reading*, the deep kind, the kind where you understand that a document is a person wearing paper. He'd sit me on a stool in the archive at eight years old and hand me claim files like fairy tales. *Look what people insure against, Rafa,* he'd say. *Floods, fire, betrayal. Look how honest they get when money's involved.* He taught me that the truth keeps receipts. He taught me order as a form of love.

And now Dolores Quintero lay in a hospital bed with a bruised jaw, arranged beside an empty coffin, because of what passed through his hands in 1994.

Unless it hadn't. Unless the clerk was mistaken, or misdirected, or — and this thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome — unless the whole staging was designed to point me at Corwin precisely because I'd trust it. The killer knows I'm closing in. The killer knows how I think, because the killer has read every archived case file, including mine. If I were staging a scene to steer me, wouldn't I stage it toward suspicion of the man I already half suspected? Wouldn't the perfect false trail wear the face of my fear?

I pressed my palms against my eyes until I saw constellations. This was the trap within the trap: I could no longer trust my own deductions, because my deductions had been trained on a curated archive. He had rewritten decades of records in his own hand. If history itself was forged, then my instincts were forged instruments too.

But the note had said *hurry*. And whatever else was manipulation, urgency had the ring of truth. Killers who want you to stop don't tell you to speed up. Killers who want you to finish something —

*Finish something.*

I took out the copied page I'd pocketed from the founding-family descendant — the hush-money ledger line, the payment to *M. V—* dated nine months before my birth — and laid it flat under the diner's buzzing light. Then I took out my notes on the cipher, the backward-counting numerals, the coordinates I hadn't yet fully resolved. Somewhere under the old seamstress shop was a second coffin and a confession clause, and somewhere in the 1994 transfer boxes was whatever connected them, and somewhere in the archive vault was the man — or the ghost of a man — who had spent his whole life arranging other people's truths into compositions he found satisfying.

My phone buzzed. Sol.

*"Rafael. Just got the lab report on the rose petals from the Historical Society."* A pause I didn't like. *"They're not from the memorial wreath. The wreath's still sealed — Ashe signed out the key log showing it untouched. These petals are fresh-dried. Somebody dried them recently. Within the last month."*

Within the last month. Since my investigation began. Since the exhumation, the shroud, the second empty grave — since the moment the killer started composing scenes for an audience of one.

He wasn't reusing the past. He was making new relics. Building the archive forward, adding to the collection, and every addition was addressed to me.

I looked at the ledger page, at the cipher notes, at the cold coffee going bitter beside them, and I understood the shape of the next hours with terrible clarity. The note had given me a deadline dressed as a choice. *Ask her what she filed and did not read. Or stop.* But there was a third option the note hadn't listed, the option it was engineered to prevent: ask what was *in* the boxes. Go to the vault tonight, before he could tidy again, before he could rearrange the composition one more time — and read the 1994 transfer myself.

Every hour I waited was an hour he had to work. Every file I touched, he'd know I'd touched. Fine. Let him watch. Let him see me coming.

I gathered the papers, left money for coffee I hadn't drunk, and stepped out into the blue-black hour before dawn, where the fog off the canal had swallowed the streetlights whole. The archive was twelve minutes away on foot. I walked fast, and behind me the whole town seemed to hold its breath — every dark window a case file, every closed door a stamp that said *received, unread*.

At the corner of Meridian, under the dead clock's frozen almost-dawn, I stopped.

The Historical Society's loading dock door stood open six inches, swinging gently in the fog, and from inside the building came the small, patient sound of someone turning pages.
