## 17. The Confession File

The forgery took me three nights.

Not because the writing was hard — I had spent seven years reading Corwin Ashe's hand in the margins of every file in that vault, his careful annotations in soft pencil, *verified*, *cross-referenced*, *see also* — but because a lie, to be believed, has to be built with more love than the truth ever was. The truth gets filed. The lie gets curated.

I worked at my kitchen table under a single lamp, the shade pulled low so the light fell only on the page and not out the window at the dark street where anyone might be watching. Old habit. New necessity. Somewhere between the second empty grave and the third staging — poor Mr. Okafor waking in the funeral home's display room with his wrists taped and an empty coffin yawning beside him like a mouth — I had stopped being the investigator and become the quarry. If I was going to be hunted, then I intended to leave a trail worth following. A trail with teeth.

The document I was building was beautiful. That was the shame of it.

I had started with the paper itself. Modern stock would never do; the vault's oldest files carried a particular cream-colored bond from the Hollis Paper Company, discontinued in 1974, and I had found two dozen blank sheets of it years ago tucked inside a water-damaged box of unfiled claims — the kind of orphaned material no archivist ever catalogued, or so I had believed until this week. Now I wondered if Corwin had left them there for me, the way he'd once left penny candy on my schoolbooks. Everything he did had been a gift. That was what made him dangerous.

I aged the sheets in the oven at two hundred degrees, watching through the glass as they turned the color of old teeth. I brewed black tea and brushed it along the edges with a pastry brush, let it pool in the creases of the fold lines I'd pressed with a bone folder. The smell filled the kitchen — tannin and heat and something almost like blood — and I sat with my hands flat on the table and breathed it in and felt nothing. That was the strangest part. I kept waiting to feel sick, the way you feel sick before you cheat at cards, before you sign a name that isn't yours. Instead there was only a clarity so clean it rang, like the air after the first hard frost. I was hunting the hunter. Every craft I owned was aimed now, and aim is its own kind of peace.

The confession itself I composed in Edmund Vasse's voice, which meant composing it in Marguerite's world, which meant composing it in mine.

*I, Edmund Vasse, being of sound mind and unsound body, set down here what was done to me in the autumn of 1968...*

I wrote it longhand first, practicing the founder's slanted, impatient script from the letters I'd photographed in the probate archive — the same hand that had written *my darling M.* across forty years of correspondence, the hand that had numbered his codicil backward the way claim forms used to be numbered, the hand whose loops I could reproduce in my sleep because they were, by blood, half my own. Then I copied it out twice more until the ink flowed without hesitation. Hesitation shows. Forgers are caught by their pauses, the little dry spots where a liar stops to think. I made myself stop thinking. I let Vasse's fury move my wrist: the accusation left deliberately incomplete, the killer's name burned away with a match — a neat charred oval, chemically produced, that would read to any examiner as deliberate destruction by the dying man himself.

Because that was the hook. Not the name. The absence of it.

Corwin Ashe had read every file in that town's history. He knew, better than anyone living, that a document naming the killer outright would end things — would go to Sol Reyes, to the district attorney, to the light. But a confession with the name destroyed? A confession that said *the one who counted the stitches* and nothing more? That was not an ending. That was a door left open. And a man who had spent his whole life curating other people's unfinished business — who had killed twice to complete what he called Marguerite's *unfinished claim* — would not be able to bear a door left open. He would have to come see what was behind it himself.

I sealed the trap with details only the killer would recognize as bait.

I wrote that the confession had been found *inside the lining of the shroud*, folded into eighths, discovered when I unpicked the hem to count the stitches. The stitch count had never been made public. Only two people alive knew it: me, and the man who had quoted it back to me over chamomile tea in his own kitchen, gentle as a lullaby, while my whole understanding of him came apart like wet paper. If word reached Corwin that the shroud had given up a second secret, he would know exactly how I'd found it — and he would know, too, that the shroud was still in evidence storage, and that whatever else it held, he had missed.

Grief makes people careless. So does certainty. He had been certain of me since the day I was seven years old and he taught me to alphabetize. He thought he knew what I would do with a confession: take it to the sheriff, follow procedure, close the file. He had raised me to close files.

He had never raised me to write one.

* * *

Planting it was the easier half.

I could not put the forgery into the official evidence chain — Sol had already fought his captain to keep me on the periphery of the case, and anything I logged would be photographed, examined, and eventually exposed, and I would not make a felon of the one honest man standing beside me. No. The file had to travel the way all true secrets traveled in this town: informally, breathlessly, from mouth to ear, until it reached the one person who listened to everything.

So I let it leak the way water leaks — finding the cracks that were already there.

On Thursday morning I sat in Denny's Diner in the corner booth with the probate lawyer, Whitcomb, a nervous man with reading glasses on a chain and a talent for hearing more than he was told. I let him see the edge of a folder in my bag — just the edge, the cream-colored corner, the red string tie of the old Vasse estate boxes. I let him watch me close the bag a beat too quickly.

"Still working the will?" he asked, casual the way men are casual when they've already been asked about you by someone else.

"Working something," I said, and stirred my coffee, and looked tired, and looked excited, and looked like a woman carrying something she couldn't quite hold. "You know, Whitcomb, sometimes the paperwork tells you more than the people do."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means Edmund Vasse wrote more than one will." I said it quietly, into my cup, and then I laughed at myself, waved it off, changed the subject to the weather. But I watched his face do the arithmetic. *More than one will.* By Friday afternoon that sentence would be in three kitchens and a barbershop, and by Friday night it would be in the archive, because Corwin Ashe had coffee with Whitcomb every Saturday morning and always had, for thirty years, and had probably chosen those mornings for exactly this reason. The whole town was a filing system. I had simply learned to write to it.

The second leak went through the funeral home. I asked young Peter Okafor — still shaking, still sleeping with the lights on since his father's ordeal — whether the shroud from the Vasse exhumation had been returned to the family yet, and I asked it wrong on purpose: *has anyone come to look at it besides me?* He said no. I said, "Good. Keep it that way," and touched his shoulder, and left him holding the sentence like a hot coal. Peter would tell his father. His father would tell the board. The board included Alma Vasse-Drayton, and Alma had been offering me money to go away since chapter nine of this affair, and Alma telephoned Corwin Ashe whenever she was frightened, which was always.

And the third leak — the truest one, the cruelest — I delivered in person.

* * *

I brought flowers to the archive on Saturday.

Chamomile and feverfew from the stand outside the hardware store, wrapped in brown paper, because Corwin grew chamomile in a window box and had taught me its name when I was small enough to ride on his shoulders through the stacks. I stood outside the records annex for a full minute before going in, holding the bouquet against my chest like a shield, feeling the cold clarity settle over me one more time, the way you check a knot before you rappel.

He was at his desk under the green lamp, sleeves rolled, restoring a ledger with rice paste and a brush finer than anything a surgeon owns. He looked up and his whole face opened — that was the unbearable thing, that it still opened, that the love in it was real even now, maybe realer than anything else in the room.

"Rafael." My name in his mouth sounded the way it had sounded when I was eight and had lost my mother's locket down a storm drain and he'd knelt in the rain with me until we found it. "You shouldn't be working. You look exhausted."

"I can't sleep." True. Let the true things carry the false ones; they bear weight better. "I keep reading files in my head. It won't stop." Also true. Also, precisely, the performance.

He set down his brush and gave me his full attention, the way he always had, as though I were the only record in the room worth keeping. "Tell me."

So I told him a story shaped like a confession of my own. I told him I hadn't been able to let the shroud go. I told him I'd gone back to evidence storage Tuesday night with Sol's reluctant permission and unpicked the hem — *I counted them again, Corwin, four hundred and six, I had to know* — and found something sewn into the lining. I watched his hands very carefully as I said it. They went still on the desk. Perfectly still. A man who had quoted that number to me across his own kitchen table understood instantly what I was telling him: that the shroud had spoken, and that it had spoken to me alone.

"Found what?" he asked. His voice was gentle. Gentle as a lullaby. Gentle as a lid closing.

"A paper. Folded into eighths. Water-stained, tea-colored, old bond paper." I let my own hands shake a little — grief is the best disguise for acting, because it isn't acting. "It's a confession, Corwin. In Vasse's hand. Dated October 1968. He describes being poisoned — slow, over weeks — and he names—" I stopped. Pressed my knuckles to my mouth. "The name's burned out. Someone got to it, or he did. There's just a scorch mark and a phrase. *The one who counted the stitches.*"

Silence. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the stacks a dehumidifier clicked off, and the quiet that followed was enormous.

"That's evidence," he said at last, softly. "You'll turn it over to Deputy Reyes."

"Of course." I smiled, and the smile hurt my face, and I let it show that it hurt. "Monday morning. I just — I wanted one person to know first. One person who'd understand what it means. After everything the archives gave me, it felt right that the archives should hear it back."

Something moved behind his eyes then — I saw it, I catalogued it the way I catalogue everything, photographic and merciless — something that was not surprise and not doubt but *appetite*. Brief as a fish turning under ice. He embraced me at the door, and I stood in the circle of his arms among the smell of rice paste and old paper and chamomile, and he said into my hair, "You were always the best of her, Rafael. Whatever happens Monday — remember that you were loved."

"I know," I said. And walked out into the cold without looking back, because looking back is how hunters become prey.

* * *

That night I sat in my truck across from the annex until two in the morning and watched the green lamp burn.

He came back after hours. Of course he did. I saw his shape cross the frosted window, unhurried, a man going to check on something precious. He was in there forty minutes. When he finally emerged and locked the door and walked away down the empty street with his collar turned up against the wind, I gave him ten minutes and then I let myself in through the coal chute Sol had shown me — the deputy who had been ordered to take me off the case and had instead handed me a flashlight and said, *off the record means I don't know where you are, so don't get dead.*

The archive smelled of him: paste, paper, chamomile, the faint camphor of the preservation cabinets. I did not need the flashlight. I knew this room the way I knew my own heartbeat, and I went straight to the shelf where the Vasse estate materials lived, and I found what I knew I would find.

My forged confession was gone from the folder where I'd reported leaving it — I had never actually placed it there, but Corwin believed the evidence log said otherwise, and belief is its own architecture — and in its place, in the folder, lay a single sheet of the archive's own cataloguing paper. On it, in his soft pencil, in the hand that had annotated thirty years of other people's tragedies:

*Verified. Cross-referenced. See also: the mausoleum, tonight.*

He had taken the bait and answered it. He wanted to meet where it had all begun, among the tombs, where the first empty coffin waited like a stage set for the last act. He thought he was coming to finish Marguerite's claim.

I stood in the dark among the shelves he had tended like a garden, holding his pencil note, and I felt the cold clarity ring through me like struck glass. Tomorrow night, the mausoleum. He would bring whatever he'd decided I must never publish. I would bring Sol, hidden, and the truth, hidden deeper.

I slipped the note into an evidence sleeve with steady hands.

Let the file read: *bait taken.*
