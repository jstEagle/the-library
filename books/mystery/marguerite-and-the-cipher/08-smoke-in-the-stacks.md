## 8. Smoke in the Stacks

The fire started, as far as I could ever reconstruct it, in the back room where I kept my past.

I want to say I smelled it first. That would be tidy—the old detective's nose catching trouble before his eyes did. But the truth is less flattering and more human: I was at the front counter with a cup of tea going cold beside me, reading a decoded fragment of Marguerite Vale's handwriting for the fourth time, when the smoke alarm in the back hallway gave a single experimental chirp and then, insultingly, went quiet.

I remember thinking: *the battery's dying.*

That's what twenty years of quiet does to a man. It teaches him to explain away the first warning.

* * *

It was a Tuesday in late October, the kind of gray afternoon that made Hollow Creek feel like a town holding its breath between seasons. Rain had been threatening since morning without committing. The shop was empty except for me—Mrs. Aldous had come and gone at eleven for her romance paperbacks, and a college kid had browsed the poetry section for an hour without buying anything, which I'd noted at the time because that's what I do now, note things, catalog small movements of ordinary life like a man building an alibi out of trivia.

The atlas was in the back room. So were my case files—two decades of notes from the Vale investigation, the coroner's statement transcribed in my own hand, the name Elias Thorn written three times on three different pages as if repetition could make it mean more. After the old man's house had been ransacked two nights ago, after he'd sat across from me in his kitchen with his hands shaking around a coffee mug and said, *They paid me, Isandro. God forgive me, they paid me and I signed anyway*—after all that, I should have moved everything. I knew I should have moved everything.

But moving it felt like admitting how afraid I was. And I had spent so long being unafraid that fear felt like a kind of defeat.

So instead I did what foolish men do. I bought a fireproof document box on Monday—a small one, inadequate—and told myself I'd transfer the important papers "this week." The atlas stayed wedged behind the local history section where it had lived since the night Tamsin and I cracked the first layer of the cipher. My files stayed in the filing cabinet with the sticky drawer. And I sat at my counter drinking tea and reading about three founding families whose fortunes had bloomed like mushrooms on a grave the same autumn Marguerite Vale stopped existing.

The alarm chirped again. Twice this time.

I set down the page.

Here is what I noticed in the thirty seconds that followed, and I list these details not because they matter to anyone but me, but because noticing is the only prayer I know:

The smell came before any visible smoke—hot dust, burning glue, the particular sweetness of old paper catching. It drifted under the door of the back room like something patient.

The light through the front windows had changed. Not darker—*oranger*, though the sky outside was still the color of dishwater.

And the college kid from the poetry section was gone. Had been gone, I realized, for some time. I hadn't heard the bell over the door.

I stood up. My tea tipped. I didn't catch it.

* * *

The back room door was warm before I touched the handle. I knew what that meant—I'd spent eleven years as a detective and attended enough fire-scene walkthroughs to recite the rules in my sleep—but knowledge and instinct are different animals, and my instinct that day was to open the door, because the atlas was behind it, and the files, and twenty years of a woman's secret handwriting that someone had already tried once to erase by ransacking an old man's home.

My hand was on the handle when the window at the room's far end burst inward—or outward, I couldn't tell which—and the sound was enormous, a cough from the throat of the building, and heat rolled through the gap in the door like a living thing shoving past me.

I stepped back. Behind me, a voice said, sharp and close: "Isandro. *Isandro.*"

Tamsin. She'd let herself in with the key I'd given her a week ago—given her casually, I remembered later, as if handing over a spare key weren't itself an act of trust I hadn't managed with another human being in fifteen years. She stood in the hallway in her rain-damp jacket, camera bag still slung across her body, and her face had gone the color of the sky outside.

"The back room's gone," I said. Stupidly, calmly. As if reporting weather.

"I can see that." She grabbed my sleeve. "Come *on*."

"The atlas—"

"Isandro." Her grip tightened. For a moment she wasn't the young journalist who'd cornered me on the riverbank with her notebook full of questions; she was something harder, someone who had grown up fast in ways I was only beginning to learn about. "It's wood and paper. You're not."

She pulled, and I went. That's the whole truth of it. I have replayed those nine seconds a hundred times—the distance from the back room door to the front of the shop, the smoke already threading along the ceiling toward us, the sound of the shelves in the history section beginning to tick and pop as their spines heated—and every time, I see my own feet moving away from the thing I wanted most to save, and every time, I understand that Tamsin Cole saved my life with nine seconds and four words.

We came out through the front onto the sidewalk. The cold air hit my face and I realized I'd been breathing smoke, that my throat felt scoured, that my eyes were streaming. Behind us the windows began to glow.

"Call it in," I said.

"Already dialing." She had her phone out, and then she paused, and looked at me, and said quietly: "Your neighbor called it in two minutes ago. I saw her on the corner when I walked up. There were already people coming."

People were coming. Hollow Creek was assembling on the opposite sidewalk in its gray coats and its umbrellas just starting to open against the first drops of rain, and they stood there watching my bookshop burn with expressions I will spend the rest of my life trying to read. Concern, some of them. Genuine concern—Mrs. Aldous with her hand pressed to her mouth, the boy who worked at the hardware store running toward the hydrant. But others—others simply watched. Watched the way you watch something scheduled. Watched the way Aldous Merrick had watched me across the gala ballroom three weeks ago, glass of wine held at chest height, nodding slowly, as if confirming an entry in a ledger.

I turned away from them and looked at my shop.

* * *

The fire department arrived in seven minutes. By then the back room was fully involved and the flames had found the gap between the floorboards and were traveling the old joists like veins filling with poison. Firefighters in yellow helmets went in and came out. A lieutenant with a soot-striped face asked me questions I answered in a voice I didn't recognize as mine—when had I last been inside, was anyone else in the building, was there anything hazardous stored in the rear.

"Yes," I heard myself say. "Paper. Twenty years of it."

He wrote that down without looking up.

Tamsin stood beside me the whole time, and neither of us spoke. What was there to say? We both knew what this was. We had known since the ransacking of Elias Thorn's house that the watchers would eventually become actors, that a town built on silence would defend its silence the way a body defends itself against infection—with fever, with fire, with whatever it took to burn the intruder out.

At 4:40 in the afternoon, standing on the wet sidewalk while my livelihood breathed smoke into an October sky, I felt something I hadn't felt since the case ended my career: rage with nowhere to go. It filled me like water fills a sealed pipe. I thought of Marguerite Vale, wherever she was—if she was—living her hidden life, writing her coded entries because she could not stop writing, and I understood suddenly, viscerally, in my scorched throat and my stinging eyes, what it costs to be erased. This was what erasure looked like. Not a dramatic moment. Just a Tuesday. Just a room where your true self was kept, and men willing to burn it, and everyone on the sidewalk watching with their umbrellas half-open, and no one saying the obvious word.

Arson. Nobody said arson. The word hung unspoken over the whole street like the smoke itself.

* * *

By six o'clock the fire was out. By seven, the crowd had thinned to stragglers, and the rain had finally committed, coming down soft and steady on the blackened shell of my back room, hissing in the ruins. The lieutenant found me sitting on the curb—I don't remember sitting down—and crouched next to me with his helmet under his arm.

"Preliminary says origin point was the rear wall," he said. "Electrical's possible. Old wiring, damp season." He paused. His eyes flicked up to mine, and for one bare second, the official language fell away. "But there's a burn pattern on the floor that doesn't look electrical to me, Mr. Sterling. Looks like accelerant, poured low. I'm flagging it. Whether the flag goes anywhere—" He straightened, and the official face came back down like a shutter. "That's above my pay grade."

Above his pay grade. In Hollow Creek, everything worth knowing was above somebody's pay grade. That was the architecture of the place. That was how it had worked for twenty years.

When he'd gone, Tamsin sat down next to me on the curb. She handed me a coffee from somewhere—the diner, maybe; someone had brought a thermos—and we drank in silence while the firefighters packed up and the ruined shop dripped.

"The atlas?" she finally asked.

"Gone. The cabinet too. Everything in the back room." I stared at the wet ash. "Everything except—" I reached into my jacket and drew out the fireproof box. Small, inadequate, bought too late. Inside it: the pages I'd decoded, transcribed in my own hand. Three sheets of paper. The sum total of what I'd saved.

Tamsin looked at the box, then at me, and laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it, just disbelief wearing humor's clothes. "Three sheets."

"Three sheets."

"And the original diary?"

My stomach dropped through the curb, through the earth, all the way down to the river where the search had ended twenty years ago. Because the original diary—the actual book, Marguerite's handwriting, the thing itself—had never lived in the back room. I had moved it Sunday night, after Thorn's ransacked house, after my sleepless reckoning with the fact that they knew about Thorn and therefore might know about me. I had put it somewhere no one would think to look, wrapped in plastic, in a place I hadn't yet told Tamsin about, because telling her meant writing her name deeper into a story that was getting people hurt.

"They don't have it," I said.

Her head turned slowly. "You moved it."

"I moved it."

"Isandro Sterling." She set down her coffee and pressed both hands over her face, and her shoulders shook—not crying, laughing again, that same hollow laugh. "You absolute— you let me think—"

"I let *them* think," I said. "That was the idea."

She lowered her hands and looked at me, and beneath the exhaustion and the soot-smudge on her cheekbone there was something new in her expression. Respect, maybe. Or recalibration—the journalist reassessing her source, discovering the retired detective still had a detective somewhere inside the ruin of him.

"They burned your shop," she said quietly, "for nothing."

"For something." I nodded at the smoking doorway, at the black water still dripping off the gutted shelves. "They burned it to tell me they could. That's not nothing. That's a message with a return address." I looked down the street, toward the darkening hills, toward the big house on them with its lights just coming on, one by one, like eyes opening. "Aldous Merrick wants me to understand that paper burns and witnesses burn and old men's houses get torn apart, and that the only thing that survives in Hollow Creek is silence."

"So what do we do?"

I turned the little fireproof box over in my hands. Three sheets of decoded cipher. A dead woman's living words. Somewhere out there, in whatever narrow life she'd built, Marguerite Vale was hearing about a fire in a bookshop—if she heard anything at all—and she would know exactly what it meant. They've escalated. They're done watching. Run further. Go quieter. Disappear so completely that even the diary stops.

Unless.

Unless she was tired. Unless twenty years of bees and routine and coded sentences had worn her down to the last thin layer of herself, the way fire wears through a wall, and what waited on the other side of that last layer was a woman who wanted, more than safety, to exist.

"Tamsin," I said. "The entries we decoded. The second layer. The mill references."

Her eyes sharpened. "The defunct mill on Kestrel Road. What about it?"

"There was a phrase I couldn't resolve. 'Where the water remembers the wheel.' I thought it was a location. I think now it might be an instruction." I stood, joints aching, throat raw, and looked at the wreck of the only place in Hollow Creek that had ever felt like mine. "Tomorrow we go to the mill. Tonight—" I glanced at the three sheets in the box, at the rain falling on the ashes of twenty years of careful, alphabetized, invisible living, "—tonight I tell you where the diary is. Because if they'll burn a building over three sheets of paper, Tamsin, then hiding things from each other is a luxury we've finished paying for."

She stood beside me. Down the street, a car passed slowly—too slowly—and its headlights swept across us and the ruined shopfront, and did not stop, and I memorized the plate anyway.

Old habits. The only inheritance I had left.

Above the blackened doorway, warped by heat but still legible, the shop's painted sign hung by one screw: *Sterling's Books — Est. 2003.* It swayed in the wet wind, creaking, refusing to fall.

Neither, I decided, would I.
