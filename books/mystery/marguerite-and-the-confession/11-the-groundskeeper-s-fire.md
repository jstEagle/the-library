## 11. The Groundskeeper's Fire

The fire file arrived at my chambers on a Tuesday morning in a cardboard box that smelled of mildew and old paper, and I knew before I opened it that it would change the shape of the case.

I had requested it three days earlier — a routine pull from the county records office, one line in a letter: *all documentation pertaining to the fire at Vane House, March 1996.* Fires at great houses are common enough; they generate paperwork the way bodies generate witnesses. I expected an incident report, a coroner's finding, perhaps a photograph or two of scorched brickwork. What I did not expect was for the clerk to telephone me personally, her voice carrying the particular caution of a woman who has handled a document she wishes she hadn't.

"You'll want to see this one yourself," she said. "It's been requested twice before, you know. Once in '96 by the insurers. And once in 2004."

"By whom?"

"That's the thing, Mr. Coldharbour. The second request came with no name attached. Just a solicitor's reference number." She paused, and I heard paper turning. "And the number's been dead since 1998."

I thanked her, hung up, and sat for a moment in the grey light of my office, listening to the radiator tick. A dead firm requesting a dead woman's fire. The case had been doing this to me for weeks now — offering me doors that opened onto rooms already occupied.

* * *

The file itself was thin, which was the first wrongness. A fatal fire should have generated a small forest: witness statements, forensic reports, correspondence between the coroner and the constabulary, insurance assessments. This was barely two inches of paper, and half of it was boilerplate. The incident report ran four pages. The coroner's verdict — *accidental death by misadventure, cause of death smoke inhalation* — ran one paragraph. There were no photographs. There were no witness statements beyond a single interview with Aldous Vane, conducted eleven days after the fire, in which he said he had been in London and could offer nothing useful.

Eleven days. In my experience, when a death is genuinely unremarkable, it is documented within forty-eight hours. Eleven days means someone took their time deciding what the document would say.

The victim was Tomas Bell, aged thirty-four, employed as groundskeeper at Vane House for — and here I set down my pen and read the line again — *six weeks*.

Six weeks. He had been hired in late January of 1996 and died on the ninth of March, in the gardener's cottage at the edge of the estate's north wood, in a fire the report attributed, without elaboration, to "a faulty paraffin heater."

I am not a suspicious man by temperament. Suspicion is a solvent; it dissolves everything it touches, including judgment. But I have spent thirty years reading documents, and documents have accents, and this one was speaking with its teeth clenched. The report named the responding officer as PC D. Ellery, who had retired in 1997 and who, according to the records office, could not be located. The fire investigator's certificate was photocopied so many times it had gone soft as cloth. And stapled to the back of the file, out of chronological order, as if someone had added it later and hoped no one would notice —

— was Tomas Bell's contract of employment.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened when I read that contract, because precision is all I have left now.

It was a standard domestic employment agreement, the kind any country solicitor keeps in a drawer. Name of employer: Marguerite Vane. Name of employee: Tomas Bell. Position: groundskeeper, full board, cottage provided. Salary modest. Terms unremarkable.

The signature block bore two signatures. Bell's was a hurried scrawl, the signature of a man signing quickly because he wanted the job more than he wanted to read the terms.

Marguerite Vane's signature was not hurried. It was confident, looping, unmistakably hers — I had by then spent enough hours with her diary and her old correspondence to know her hand the way one knows a face across a crowded room. The capital M with its long descending tail. The Vane crossed with a single decisive stroke. It was, to all appearances, authentic.

The date beneath it read **14 January 1996**.

Marguerite Vane disappeared on the third of March, 1994. She was declared dead in absentia twenty-two months later. And on the fourteenth of January, 1996 — nearly two years after her vanishing, ten months after her legal death — she signed a contract hiring a man who would be dead of a convenient fire within seven weeks.

Someone with access to her identity had been operating openly. Not hiding. Not forging in the dark. Signing documents, filing them, letting them sit in county records where anyone might find them — which, apparently, almost no one had, because no one was looking, because everyone already knew Marguerite Vane was dead, and the dead do not hire gardeners.

Except, I thought, setting the page flat under the desk lamp, the dead do not sign either. And that signature was no forgery. I had seen forgeries for thirty years; they hesitate. They are drawn rather than written. This hand moved like water finding its level.

Which meant one of two things. Either Marguerite Vane had been alive and present in January 1996, six weeks before her employee burned to death in her own gardener's cottage —

— or someone had spent years learning to be her, and had used that skill to put a living man into a wooden building and lock the question of him behind a faulty heater.

* * *

I drove out to Vane House that afternoon, because there is a limit to what paper can tell you and I had learned, over these weeks, that the house itself kept better records than the county ever had.

The north wood was bare in the way only English woodland is bare in late autumn — black trunks, wet leaf-rot, a silence that felt administered rather than natural. The gardener's cottage stood at the end of a track that the trees had begun to reclaim. It had never been rebuilt. That alone told me something. An estate like Vane House maintains its buildings the way a body maintains its skin; a ruined structure at the property's edge, left to rot for thirty years, is not neglect. It is a decision. Someone had decided the cottage should remain exactly as the fire left it — a monument, or a warning, or a grave marker.

I stood in the doorway, ducking under the police tape that had long since faded to the color of old bone, and let the room speak.

Fire investigators read rooms the way I read contracts, and though I am no investigator, thirty years around coroners' courts teaches you the grammar. The fire had started low and west, in the corner where a paraffin heater might plausibly have stood — the scorch pattern fanned upward and outward in the classic V. But the floorboards near the origin had burned *through*, deeper than a heater fire typically manages, and the burn was uneven, pooled in places, as if the fuel had been liquid and generous. Paraffin heaters spill. But they spill what's in them, and a heater holds a gallon at most.

There had been more than a gallon in that corner. I would have bet my reputation on it.

Against the far wall, a stone hearth, swept clean even now by three decades of rain through the broken roof. On the sill above it, wedged in the mortar, a small object caught the light. I worked it free with my penknife.

A brass door key, green with verdigris, its bow stamped with a maker's mark and a number. Not a cottage key — too fine, too heavy. The kind of key that opens a good lock on a good door. I turned it over twice, found nothing else, and put it in my breast pocket, telling myself it was evidence and not a talisman, and knowing I was lying.

Tomas Bell had died in a locked room, then. Or a room made to look like one.

* * *

Aldous Vane was in the orangery when I returned to the house, doing something elaborate and unnecessary to a lemon tree. He had aged badly since our first meeting — or perhaps he had simply stopped performing youth, which amounts to the same thing. When he saw me his trowel went still.

"The north cottage," I said, without preamble. "You were in London the night Bell died."

"I told the police that."

"You told the police that eleven days after the fire. Why the delay?"

He set the trowel down with exaggerated care, buying time the way guilty men buy time — by being very busy with their hands. "Shocked, I suppose. These things scramble you."

"They scrambled you for eleven days specifically?"

"Orsen." He wiped his palms on his apron and looked at me directly, and I will grant him this: whatever else Aldous Vane was, he was not a coward. "You've been here six weeks and you've turned the place inside out. So I'll ask you plainly, since you won't ask yourself. Do you think my sister burned that man alive?"

"I think," I said, "that your sister signed his employment contract two years after she vanished, and I think you know whether that signature is real, because you've known since 1996 whether it's real, and I think that knowledge is why you've been drinking at ten in the morning every day since I arrived."

The color left his face in a slow tide. Behind him, through the orangery glass, the gardens lay flat and grey under a sky the color of dishwater.

"She wouldn't have," he said quietly. "Whatever she did — however she did it — she wouldn't have killed anyone. You don't understand what she was protecting."

"Then help me understand."

But he only shook his head, once, slowly, like a man refusing a meal he was starving for, and picked up his trowel, and turned back to the lemon tree as if I had already gone.

* * *

Cressida Hale was waiting for me in the hall.

I had not announced myself. Her car was not in the drive. Which meant she had come while I walked the north wood, entered a house where she had no current right of entry, and positioned herself in the entrance hall like a piece of furniture that had always been there. She wore grey wool and pearls and an expression of such perfect composure that I understood, finally, why the alibis from 1994 had been airtight. Composure like hers is not a temperament. It is a discipline, maintained daily, the way some women maintain their nails.

"Mr. Coldharbour. You've been to the cottage."

It was not a question, and I did not treat it as one. "You knew Bell?"

"I met him once. At the Christmas drinks, January '96. Quiet man. Good with roses." She smiled faintly. "Terrible shame about the heater."

"His contract bears Marguerite's signature. Dated January 1996."

"Yes. I heard." She examined a fingernail. "The estate's affairs were in some disorder after her disappearance. Documents get signed. Executors act. Imogen's trustees, perhaps."

"Imogen was eight years old in 1996, Miss Hale, and her trustees were Aldous and you. Are you telling me you signed that contract?"

The pause lasted perhaps a second and a half. In that second and a half I watched Cressida Hale decide, with the efficiency of long practice, exactly how much truth the moment required.

"No," she said. "I'm not telling you that."

"What are you telling me?"

She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and for the first time since I'd met her, the discipline flickered — not broke, but flickered, like a lamp in a draft. "I'm telling you that you're digging in a churchyard, Mr. Coldharbour, and the dead don't appreciate it. Tomas Bell was hired by someone who wanted him at Vane House. He died six weeks later. If you keep pulling that thread, you will find out who hired him. And I promise you" — her eyes held mine, and beneath the pearl-grey calm there was something moving, quick and cold, like a fish under ice — "you will wish you hadn't. Some fires are accidents. Some are messages. Be sure you know which one you're standing in."

She gathered her coat from the hall chair and left through the front door, and I stood alone in the marble corridor listening to her engine fade down the drive, aware that my heart was going harder than a lawyer's heart ought to go, and aware of something else besides.

She hadn't asked me what I'd found at the cottage. She'd told me what I'd find if I kept looking. People only issue warnings about things they can see coming.

* * *

That evening I spread the fire file across my hotel desk beside the photocopies of the diary, and I did what I do best, which is to say I stopped thinking like a lawyer and started thinking like the case.

Timeline. Third of March, 1994: Marguerite vanishes. All three beneficiaries produce alibis polished to the minute. Autumn 1995: declared dead in absentia. Fourteen January 1996: Marguerite Vane — or her hand — hires Tomas Bell. Ninth of March 1996: Bell dies in a fire that a two-inch file calls accidental, eleven days pass before the sole beneficiary statement is taken, and the investigating constable retires within eighteen months and cannot afterward be found. Two thousand and four: an unnamed party with a defunct solicitor's reference requests the same file I now held.

Someone had been maintaining this story for thirty years. Pruning it. Watering it. Keeping the cottage unrebuilt, keeping the file thin, keeping the questions unasked. The diary entries that continued past the vanishing had seemed impossible to me once; now they seemed merely consistent. Whatever force had kept Marguerite Vane's handwriting alive in the world had also kept her name alive on contracts, and had done it openly, patiently, with the confidence of someone who knew that no one audits the paperwork of the dead.

But Bell changed the arithmetic. Bell was not paper. Bell was a man with a face and a history and, presumably, people who had known him — and he had been placed at Vane House like a chess piece, and removed just as deliberately, six weeks later, by fire.

Either Marguerite had hired him and someone had killed him — which meant she had spent thirty years knowing it, and the diary's cold surveillance had a motive I hadn't yet dared name.

Or someone else had hired him in her name, and killed him, and had been signing her hand for decades — which meant the person maintaining the diary was not remembering Marguerite Vane.

They were becoming her.

I looked at the brass key lying on the desk beside the lamp, green and patient in the light. It fit some good lock somewhere — a study, a strongbox, a door in a house I hadn't found yet. Bell had carried it, or it had been left where he'd died, wedged into the hearth like a message in a bottle thrown backward through thirty years.

*If you are reading this, you are already inside it,* the diary had said.

I turned the key over once more, and noticed — for the first time, beneath the verdigris, where my thumb had worn the corrosion away — a tiny engraving on the bow, so small I needed the lamp's magnifier to read it.

Two letters. **M.V.**

And beneath them, a date. Not 1996.

**March 1994.**

The night she vanished.
