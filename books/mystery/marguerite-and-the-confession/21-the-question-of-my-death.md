## 21. The Question of My Death

The letter came on a Tuesday, in an envelope the color of old bone, and Imogen knew before she opened it that her mother was alive.

She couldn't have said how she knew. There was nothing in the handwriting — the address was typed, the postmark local. But she had spent thirty years learning the shape of an absence, the way a sailor learns the shape of a coastline by what isn't there, and something in the weight of that envelope against her palm told her the absence had ended. Something had arrived to fill it.

She sat at the kitchen table of her flat in London — the flat she'd kept even after Vane House became hers in all but name, because some distances you maintain on principle — and she read the letter twice.

*Dear Miss Vane,* it began, and already it was wrong, because no one called her Miss Vane; she had been Imogen Hale-Carrow for twenty years, hyphenated into someone else's family tree. *I am writing to inform you of certain developments in the probate of your mother's estate. Mr. Coldharbour will wish to speak with you at your earliest convenience. I would advise you to prepare yourself for information of a personal nature.*

It was signed by a solicitor's clerk whose name meant nothing. It said nothing at all. And yet Imogen set it down on the table with hands that were not quite steady, and looked out the window at the rain coming down over the rooftops of Camden, and thought: *she's alive.*

Thirty years. Thirty years of a closed coffin with no body, of a funeral conducted over photographs, of being the girl whose mother vanished — which was somehow worse than being the girl whose mother died, because death at least had the decency to be final. Vanishing left a door open. Vanishing meant that every woman on a train platform with dark hair seen from behind was a small heartbreak waiting to happen. Vanishing meant that when Imogen turned eighteen and the birthday cards stopped coming, she grieved a second time, and this grief had no grave either.

She made tea she didn't drink. She called Orsen Coldharbour from the landline, because some calls you make standing up.

"Miss Carrow." His voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful when he has been carrying something heavy across ice. "Thank you for calling."

"You know why I'm calling."

A pause. Rain against glass, both ends of the line. "I think I do."

"She's alive." Not a question. She heard him breathe.

"Yes," he said. "She's alive."

Imogen sat down at the table without meaning to, her knees simply deciding for her. The tea went cold beside her hand. "Tell me," she said, and then, before he could begin: "No. Don't tell me. Not on the phone. I'll come tomorrow."

"There's something else you should know first," Coldharbour said, and there it was again, that terrible gentleness lawyers learn, the gentleness of men who deliver verdicts for a living. "There are things about the case you'll want to hear from me rather than from a newspaper. But I want you to understand — before you come — that knowing everything may cost you something. That's not a warning against coming. It's only that I've learned, these ninety days, that truth is not free. Someone always pays for it. I'd like you to decide, with open eyes, whether you're willing to be the one who pays."

Imogen looked at the letter, at its bone-colored envelope, at the typed characters so carefully impersonal.

"I've been paying my whole life," she said. "I'd like to see the bill itemized."

* * *

Vane House in autumn was a different animal than Vane House in any other season. In summer the ivy softened it, greened the scars in the brickwork; in winter the frost made it architectural, austere, almost noble. But in autumn it looked like what it was — a large house that had outlived its purpose, standing in grounds gone bronze and wild, its windows reflecting a sky the color of dishwater.

Imogen had been inside perhaps a dozen times since childhood. Her uncle Aldous lived there still, rattling around in the east wing like a coin in a jar, and Cressida Hale had occupied the guest cottage for as long as Imogen could remember, arriving each Christmas with wrapped gifts and a smile that never quite reached the architecture of her face. Imogen had always assumed the house would pass eventually to Aldous, or be sold, or fall down with dignity. She had never once imagined it might pass to her.

Coldharbour met her at the door. Up close he looked like a man who had been through a long illness of the mind — sharp still, but worn thin, the way a blade wears when it has been used to cut something harder than itself.

"Miss Carrow."

"Imogen is fine. We've exchanged enough pleasantries over ninety days, Mr. Coldharbour. Just tell me."

He studied her for a moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something in his own. He led her through the hall — past the staircase where a six-year-old girl had once sat listening to a lullaby drift down from a locked study, sung in a voice that wasn't quite her mother's — and into the morning room, where the light came gray and even through tall windows, and where two chairs had been placed facing each other like witnesses.

He told her all of it.

He told her about the will that shouldn't exist, dated four years after her mother walked out of the world. About the diary in the locked study whose entries continued for a decade after the disappearance, in ink that hadn't dried. About the alibis, perfect as rehearsed theater — Aldous at his club, Cressida at a conference in Bath, each corroborated to the minute by witnesses who never wavered in thirty years. About DS Renwick, dying in a cottage by the sea, handing over a half-burned notebook and a single word: *witness.* About the payments — thirty years of them, marked M.V., maintenance — flowing from Cressida Hale's accounts like blood from a wound that never scabbed. About the fire in 1996, and Tomas Bell, the groundskeeper whose employment contract bore Marguerite's signature dated after the world had agreed she was dead.

And then he told her the rest. That Marguerite had witnessed a murder in 1994 — that Cressida Hale had killed Marguerite's business partner, a man named Edmund Roach, whose death had been ruled a heart attack, and that Marguerite, understanding the alibis were untouchable and the law would fail her, had chosen instead to become a ghost. To vanish so completely that no one could kill the witness, because no one knew the witness existed. That Tomas Bell had kept her diary alive for her, writing entries in her hand, watching Vane House for decades, until someone burned him in his bed for getting too close. That Marguerite had waited thirty years for the statute to run down like a clock in a locked room, and had used Orsen Coldharbour — used the will, used the diary, used *her*, Imogen — as the mechanism of her revenge.

That three days ago, in the morning room where Imogen now sat, Cressida Hale had confessed to everything, believing herself beyond the law's reach, while a recorder turned in Coldharbour's pocket. That Cressida was in custody now, charged with concealment, the old alibis unraveling thread by thread, Renwick's pressure traced back to her late husband's bench.

"And my mother," Imogen said, when he finished. The words came out flat, strange, like coins from another country. "Where is she now?"

"That," Coldharbour said, "is complicated. Legally speaking, Marguerite Vane remains dead. There is a provision in the will — a contingency I drafted under her instruction — that permits the question of her death to be resolved ambiguously. If it is resolved ambiguously, she can remain legally dead. No charges for false identity. No charges for the extortion. She walks away clean, under whatever name she's been wearing, and no one ever finds her unless she wants to be found."

"But I could find her."

"Yes," he said quietly. "You could. She hasn't left the county. I believe — though she would deny it — that she wanted you to have the choice. Whether to know where she is. Whether to go."

The rain had started again, ticking softly against the windows, and Imogen sat very still in the gray light and felt something enormous move through her — not joy, she noticed, and not relief, but something older and stranger, like a tide returning to a shore it had abandoned.

"When?" she asked. "When did she decide? When did she choose the diary, the will, the whole machine of it?"

"The pact with Bell dates to 1995. The will was drafted in 1998."

"So for twenty-seven years." Imogen's voice didn't rise. That was the strange thing; she felt it should rise, should break, should do all the things voices did in films, and instead it stayed level as a frozen lake. "For twenty-seven years she was forty miles away, or four hundred, and she sent me birthday cards, and she let me grow up believing she was dead or worse, and she did it — why? Because if she loved me openly, someone would have used me to find her. Because loving me was the one thing that would have gotten her killed."

"Yes," Coldharbour said. "I believe that's the shape of it."

"And now Cressida is caught, and the statute has run, and she's safe — and only now, only *now*, am I allowed to know she's alive." Imogen stood. Her legs held. "Where is she, Mr. Coldharbour?"

He wrote an address on a card and set it on the table between them, and did not push it toward her, and did not look away.

* * *

She drove for two hours without deciding to drive. That was how it felt afterward, looking back — the car simply going, the A-roads unspooling, the hedges blurring green and brown, her hands on the wheel and her mind somewhere else entirely, in a locked study in 1994, in a voice singing a lullaby that wasn't quite right, in a kitchen in Camden where a six-year-old girl had asked her father when Mummy was coming home and been told *soon* by a man who knew it was a lie.

The cottage was on the coast road above a cove, whitewashed, low-roofed, with a garden gone soft and salt-blown. A lighthouse stood on the headland beyond, white and patient. Imogen recognized it from a photograph in her mother's study — she had seen it a hundred times as a child and never known where it was, and here it was, real, exactly where a watermark had promised.

She parked at the gate. She did not get out.

Through the window she could see a woman moving in the kitchen — gray-haired now, broader than the photographs, wearing a cardigan the color of oatmeal. She was doing something ordinary. Washing a cup, maybe. Setting it on a rack. The gestures of a life that had continued, day after day, for thirty years, while Imogen grew up in the negative space of it.

Marguerite Vane — or whoever she was now; the alias registered to the cottage belonged to a dead man named Bell, and Imogen supposed her mother had taken even that, had taken the murdered man's name the way she'd taken everything else, wearing the dead like armor — moved out of view and back again. Imogen watched her and felt the strangest thing, which was nothing. A vast, ringing nothing, where thirty years of longing had been.

She had imagined this moment her whole life. Every version of it ended the same way: her mother opening a door, and Imogen running, and the hole in her closing like water over a stone. She had imagined fury too, in her darker hours — screaming at her, demanding to know how, *how* — and she had imagined forgiveness, in her better ones, the two of them sitting at some table like this one, thirty years pouring out of them like sand.

But now, at the gate, with her hand on the latch, Imogen understood something she hadn't understood in the morning room, or in the car, or in thirty years of unfinished grief.

Her mother had built her absence the way an architect builds a cathedral — deliberately, load-bearing, every wall essential. The vanishing wasn't a wound in their lives. It was the structure of them. Imogen was who she was *because* of the absence: the woman who didn't trust doors that opened easily, who kept her own study locked, who had learned from a lullaby sung in the wrong voice that love and deception could share a throat. If her mother stepped out of the absence now, smiling, holding out her arms — she wouldn't be filling the hole. She'd be demolishing the house Imogen had built inside it, the house that was, in the end, the only home her mother had ever left her.

Some absences can't be repaired. They can only be honored.

And there was more, a colder thing beneath it, which she made herself look at directly: her mother had chosen the cards. Had chosen eighteen years of unsigned envelopes, postmarked nowhere — close enough to touch, never close enough to hold. That wasn't protection alone. Protection would have been silence. The cards were a leash, thin as thread, kept slack for eighteen years in case it was ever needed. And the will — the will that had made Imogen heir to everything, conditional on a stranger solving the mystery of her mother's death — even that had been a mechanism, a gear in a machine of revenge, and Imogen had been turned by it like everyone else. Loved, perhaps. Used, certainly. Both at once, in proportions no one would ever be able to measure.

She could not un-know that. And she could not sit across a kitchen table from the author of it and pretend the arithmetic came out warm.

Imogen took the card Coldharbour had given her out of her coat pocket. She looked at the address written on it in his precise lawyer's hand, and then she looked at the cottage, at the gray-haired woman in the oatmeal cardigan who had once sung lullabies in a voice not quite her own, and she did the hardest thing she had ever done, which was nothing at all.

She put the card back in her pocket. She got in the car. She drove away, and she did not look in the mirror, because looking in the mirror would have been a kind of seeing, and she had decided — with a clarity that felt like both devastation and peace — not to see.

* * *

The estate passed to her formally in November, the question of Marguerite Vane's death entered into the record as *resolved*, which was, Imogen thought, the most elegant lie Orsen Coldharbour had ever told, and she had watched him tell several.

Aldous moved out within the month, to a flat in Truro near his daughter, weeping with what might have been relief. Cressida Hale's trial was set for spring; Imogen followed it in the papers and felt, reading the headlines, only a distant chill, like weather happening to someone else. The guest cottage stood empty. The groundskeeper's cottage stood empty. The house stood, as it had always stood, full of rooms nobody needed.

Imogen moved in — not all at once, but the way water moves into dry ground, slowly, by capillary action. She changed nothing. This became her policy, and then her creed. The furniture stayed where it had stood since before she was born. The dust sheets in the west wing stayed draped over their ghostly shapes. The portraits stayed crooked in the way they had always been slightly crooked, and she found she liked them crooked; straightened, they would have been strangers' faces, but crooked they were simply the house's own expression, familiar as a scar.

She burned nothing. People expected her to, she sensed — the daughter avenging herself on the past, bonfires on the lawn, catharsis by flame. But fire was what had killed Tomas Bell, and fire was what her mother had used to erase herself, and Imogen wanted no part of that economy. Things that happened should leave records. Even painful things. *Especially* painful things. She had spent thirty years grieving a woman with no body and no grave, and she would not manufacture a second disappearance just because the first one had been explained.

Except for one room.

The study at the top of the stairs — the room from which the lullaby had drifted, the room where the diary had waited thirty years in its locked drawer — Imogen kept locked. She had the key, of course. Coldharbour had given it to her with the rest of the estate's papers, turning it over in his palm for a moment before passing it across, as if it weighed more than brass.

"You could go in," he'd said. "It's yours now. Everything in it."

"I know," Imogen had said. "That's why I won't."

Because the study was the one place in the house where her mother still existed as she had been — not the gray-haired woman washing cups above a cove, not the architect of alibis and birthday cards, but the younger woman from the photographs, the one who had signed contracts and written diaries and planned, in that room, the vanishing that would define her daughter's life. As long as the door stayed shut, that woman stayed intact — suspended, uncontradicted, hers. Open the door and she'd become an inventory: desk, chair, cold hearth, empty drawer. Imogen had lost her mother twice already. She declined to lose her a third time to daylight.

So the door stayed shut, and once a week she climbed the stairs and stood outside it, hand flat against the wood, the way you'd stand at a grave. Some people prayed at graves. Imogen listened. For the lullaby, though she never heard it. For anything, though there was never anything. The listening was the ritual. The silence was the offering.

Winter came. Snow lay on the wild gardens, erasing the paths, and Imogen learned the house's sounds — the tick of cooling pipes, the wind worrying the chimney, Aldous's old dog barking at nothing in the far fields. She hired no staff. She answered her own door. On Christmas Eve she lit fires in the morning room and the hall and left the study cold, and stood at the bottom of the stairs with a glass of wine, looking up at the locked door, and raised the glass to it slightly, the way you'd salute a ship on the horizon going the other way.

*Goodbye*, she didn't say. *Or hello. Or thank you. Or how dare you.* All of them, none of them. The absence had finally been given its true shape, and its true shape was this: a door, a key in her pocket, and a woman on the wrong side of both, choosing, every day, to leave it closed.

On the last night of January, a letter came. Bone-colored envelope, typed address, no return. Imogen stood in the hall with it in her hands, snow melting off her boots onto the flagstones, and knew — the way she had known with the first one — what it was before she opened it.

Inside was a single sheet. No salutation. No signature. Only a line of handwriting she recognized the way you recognize your own heartbeat, a hand confirmed by graphologists and thirty years of blackmail letters and one diary that would not stop writing:

*I won't write again. But the study drawer is not empty anymore.*

Imogen stood a long time in the cold hall, the letter trembling very slightly in her hand, the fire crackling behind her, the stairs rising into shadow toward a door she had sworn never to open.

Then she took the key from her pocket, and began to climb.
