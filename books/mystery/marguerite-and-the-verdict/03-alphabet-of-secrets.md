## 3. Alphabet of Secrets

The letter sat on my kitchen table all night, face down, like a card I was afraid to turn.

I made coffee at six the way I always did—two scoops, tap water, the mug with the chipped handle that says WORLD'S OKAYEST DISPATCHER in letters that have faded to a whisper—and I did not look at it. I fed the cat. I watered the plant on the windowsill that has survived twelve years of night shifts through sheer indifference to being watered. I stood at the sink and watched the sun come up over the Bellhaven water tower, and I told myself, in the flat professional voice I use on drunks at two a.m., that there was an explanation.

People's handwriting changes. People imitate handwriting. Grief plays tricks; grief is famous for it. My mother had been dead eleven months and four days, and dead women do not write letters confessing to murders they had no possible connection to, postmarked from a town forty miles away, addressed to daughters they hadn't spoken to in person since 1997.

Except.

Except that I had spent twelve years listening to this town confess itself one fare request at a time, and if there was one thing Bellhaven had taught me, it was that the impossible is just the improbable wearing a nicer coat.

I turned the letter over at 6:47 a.m., because that was the minute shift change used to happen at Bellhaven Taxi back when my mother still worked dispatch before me, and because rituals are how cowards find their courage.

*Dear Felix,* it began, in that looping, leftward-leaning hand I would know anywhere, the hand that had written my name on lunch bags for years, on birthday cards, on the visiting-room sign-in sheets I still kept in a shoebox under my bed.

*I killed Edmund Voss. You deserve to know why. Look where we always looked.*

No signature. No date beyond the postmark. Just those two sentences in blue ink, in my mother's handwriting, sitting on my table like a loaded gun someone had left as a housewarming gift.

*Look where we always looked.*

My mother and I had exactly one place we always looked, and it wasn't a place at all. It was a habit. Every Sunday phone call, every visit through the glass at Halloway Correctional, she ended the same way: *Check your corners, sweetheart.* It started when I was small and she worked nights and the world felt full of men who hadn't learned to knock. By the time I was grown it was a joke between us, a benediction, a way of saying *I love you* without having to say it, because Ruth Fairweather treated direct emotional statements the way other people treat expired milk.

Check your corners.

I checked my corners. And then I went to the shoebox.

* * *

Here is what the town of Bellhaven knows about Ruth Fairweather, or what it knew before everyone politely forgot: she was arrested in the spring of 1998 for embezzling eleven thousand dollars from Halloway Feed & Seed, where she'd worked the counter for nine years. She pled guilty. She served eight years. She came home, took a job folding linens at the Bellhaven Motor Lodge, and died in her sleep last March at fifty-eight, in the little apartment over the laundromat on Delancey Street, three weeks after her last Sunday call to me.

Here is what I know, which is both more and less: my mother could not embezzle eleven dollars. Not because she was honest—she was honest, ferociously so, in the way of people who have watched dishonesty eat something they loved—but because Ruth Fairweather counted everything. She counted the change in her purse twice. She counted the steps to the bus stop. She once returned a dollar-fifty to the gas station because the clerk had given her too much change for a lottery ticket she hadn't even won. The woman treated arithmetic like a religion and theft like a mortal sin, and the story the courts told about her taking eleven thousand dollars in small unmarked increments over fourteen months was a story with the structural integrity of a soap bubble.

I said this to her public defender. I said it to the judge, in a letter. I said it to my mother herself, through the glass, in the second month, gripping the receiver so hard my knuckles went white.

"Ruthie," I said—I called her Ruthie when I was angry, because she hated it—"you didn't do this."

She looked at me through the glass with an expression I spent twenty-five years learning to read and never quite mastered. It wasn't guilt. It wasn't innocence. It was the look of a woman doing long division.

"Felix," she said, "some numbers don't work out clean. You round down and you move on."

"That's not math, Mom. That's surrender."

"It's survival." And then, softer, in the voice she saved for the ends of calls: "Check your corners, sweetheart."

She wouldn't say another word about it. Not in eight years of visits, not in eleven months of freedom, not ever. When I brought it up she changed the subject to my job, my apartment, whether I was eating anything that hadn't been microwaved into submission. She asked about the fares. God, how she loved hearing about the fares. She'd been a dispatcher herself, nights, for Bellhaven Taxi, from 1985 until the arrest—the same chair I sit in now, the same headset, the same window overlooking the same parking lot with its three light poles and its permanent oil stain shaped like Portugal. Taking the job after she went inside wasn't sentimentality. It was necessity; it was the only night work in town that didn't require standing. But somewhere along the way it became inheritance, and neither of us ever named it out loud.

So I told her about the fares. The doctor who took cabs to the cemetery every second Tuesday and never visited any grave but one. The woman on Kestrel Street who ordered taxis to the train station every time her husband left for his brother's, and taxis home two days later, and never once let the drivers carry her bag. The man who paid cash, always cash, always to the county courthouse, always tipping like an apology. My mother listened to all of it with her chin propped on her fist and her eyes half-closed, and sometimes she'd ask a question that seemed small and wasn't.

"Which road does he take?" she'd say. Or: "Does he look at the driver, or past him?"

And I'd answer, because I didn't yet understand that my mother was teaching me to read people the way she read ledgers—for what was entered, and for what had been carefully, deliberately left out.

* * *

The shoebox lived under my bed, which will tell you everything about how well I processed her death. Inside: forty-one letters, one for roughly every six weeks of her sentence, each in the same looping leftward hand, each signed *Love, Mom* with a small flourish on the M that looked like a bird taking off.

I laid them out on the kitchen floor in chronological order, kneeled among them in my bathrobe like a woman praying to a very paper god, and compared.

The letter on my table matched. That was the first thing, and the worst thing. Not similar. Not close. *Matched.* The same pressure on the downstrokes. The same way the R in *Ruth* hooked inward like a question mark deciding against itself. The same slight upward drift across a line, as though the words were climbing toward something. Handwriting analysts talk about slant and spacing and letter formation, but what they mean is that handwriting is a body. It's the way a particular wrist moves, a particular thumb presses. You can fake a signature. Nobody fakes a body.

Whoever wrote the confession letter had either been writing in my mother's hand for decades, or—

Or.

I put that word away. I was very good at putting words away. Twelve years of dispatch teaches you to hold terrible sentences at arm's length: *my husband isn't breathing*, *there's someone in my yard*, *the car went off the bridge at Miller's Road*. You hold them steady, you do the job, you fall apart later or you don't.

But alone in my kitchen at seven in the morning, holding my dead mother's handwriting up to the light like a counterfeit bill, the arm's length collapsed.

Because here was the thing I couldn't put away: the letter knew things. Not big things—small ones. It called me Felix, which everyone did. But it called me *Felix* with no period after it, and my mother never used periods after names, ever, in forty-one letters, because she said a name should be allowed to run. The ink was blue. My mother wrote in blue ink exclusively and considered black ink "funeral colors." The paper was cheap lined paper torn from a spiral notebook, with the little fringed edge left intact, and my mother tore pages out of notebooks with the fringe on, always, because she said the perforations were a scam invented by paper companies to sell more notebooks.

These were not facts available to the general public. These were facts available to maybe three people alive, and one of them had been cremated in April of last year while I stood in a gray dress holding an urn too heavy for what it contained.

I sat down on the kitchen floor among the spread-out letters, in the middle of my own alphabet of secrets, and I laughed—one short, ugly syllable with nothing behind it—and then I did what I do instead of crying, which is make lists.

Fact: Edmund Voss was murdered—or died, the police were still using the polite word—on the fourth of October.

Fact: this letter was postmarked the fifth of October, the day after his funeral, from Millbrook, forty miles east.

Fact: my mother died last March and could not have written it.

Fact: it was written by her hand anyway.

Fact: *look where we always looked.*

I got up, washed my coffee mug, dried it, put it away. Fed the cat again, who accepted this bribe without comment. Stood in front of the window watching the morning traffic on Delancey—a school bus, a man jogging with the desperate rhythm of someone newly divorced, Mrs. Okonkwo walking her terrier past the laundromat where my mother used to fold towels for minimum wage and tip the owner for the privilege of the shift.

And then I thought about corners.

*Check your corners, sweetheart.* All those years I'd heard it as love, or superstition, or the residue of whatever fear had lived in my mother before I was old enough to see it. But my mother didn't waste words. She counted them like change. If she'd spent thirty years telling me to check my corners, it was because there was something in the corners worth checking.

And if the letter was a map—if *look where we always looked* meant anything other than a cruel joke played by someone with a photocopier and a grudge—then the corners of my mother's life were the places nobody had ever thought to search. A guilty plea that fit her like someone else's coat. Eight years of silence about a crime she couldn't have committed. A daughter raised on fare stories like vitamins. A job handed down like a key.

I gathered the letters off the floor, all forty-one, squared their edges, and put them back in the shoebox. Then I took the confession letter and slid it into a plastic sleeve I'd bought years ago for protecting old taxi permits, because apparently some part of me had always known I'd end up handling evidence in my own kitchen.

One more fact, and it was the one that made my hands go cold around the sleeve: the letter hadn't come to my apartment by chance. Whoever sent it knew where I lived. Knew my name. Knew my mother's hand well enough to wear it like skin. And had chosen me—not the police, not the newspaper, not the grieving Voss family—to receive a murderer's confession, as if I were the natural next of kin for it.

As if killing Edmund Voss were a family business, and I'd just inherited my share.

I looked at the clock. Nine-twelve in the morning. I had slept, technically, and I was due back at the dispatch desk at ten tonight, and between now and then lay fifteen hours of daylight, which had always struck me as the least trustworthy part of the day.

In the shoebox, beneath the letters, there was one more thing I hadn't taken out in a year: a single sheet of yellow legal paper in my mother's hand, found in her apartment after she died, folded into eighths and tucked into her Bible at Proverbs. I'd assumed it was a grocery list. I'd been afraid to check.

I unfolded it now, and smoothed it flat on the table next to the confession letter, and set the two hands side by side.

They matched too. Of course they matched. But the grocery list—which was not a grocery list, unless my mother had recently developed a need for *2:14, Miller Rd, V.M., DNR*—was dated October 3rd.

Twenty-two years ago.

Two days before the night a schoolteacher named Alice Brennan was killed on Miller's Road, in a hit-and-run the county closed in nine days flat, and eighteen years before anyone thought to connect the quietest dispatcher in Bellhaven to the richest family in it.

I put on my coat. Some corners, it turns out, you have to go check in person—and the first one was a funeral home on Chestnut Street, where tomorrow afternoon the whole town would gather to cry, or not, over Edmund Voss.

I intended to be in the back row, counting.
