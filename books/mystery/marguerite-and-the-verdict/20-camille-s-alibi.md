## 20. Camille's Alibi

Camille Voss called the dispatch office at 9:47 on a Tuesday morning, which told Felix two things before a single word was spoken. First, that Camille was an early riser—Edmund Voss's daughter would not sleep past dawn if her whole life had been spent waiting for the house to wake up. Second, that she was calling the taxi company instead of showing up at the door, which meant she wanted a record. Camille Voss did nothing without wanting a record.

"Bellhaven Taxi, this is Felix."

"Miss Fairweather." A pause, the kind people use when they're deciding how much of themselves to spend. "I'd like to book a fare. To the county records office. And I'd like you to drive it yourself."

Felix looked at the board, at the three cars idling in the lot, at the coffee going cold beside her elbow. "We can have Marcus there in ten minutes."

"I'm aware of your staffing arrangements," Camille said, and there was something almost dry in it, a humor that had been locked in a drawer for years and only occasionally taken out to be dusted. "I said I'd like *you*."

Felix should have said no. She knew that even then, some part of her brain filing the instinct away like a fare slip: *decline, 9:48 a.m., reason unknown.* But the last week had taught her that every instinct she owned was suspect, that Ruth Fairweather's daughter was apparently constitutionally incapable of walking away from a mystery, and so instead she heard herself say, "Twenty minutes."

"Eighteen would be better," Camille said, and hung up.

* * *

Camille was waiting on the front steps of a small brick house on Delaney Street that Felix had driven past four thousand times without once seeing. That was the thing about Bellhaven—the town kept its secrets not behind walls but in plain view, tucked into houses so ordinary they became invisible, the way a dispatcher's voice becomes invisible to the people who call her every night. Camille Voss lived on Delaney Street. Nobody knew that. Nobody talked about Edmund Voss's daughter at all, which in Bellhaven was its own kind of exile.

She was smaller than Felix expected. Silver hair cut short and practical, a wool coat buttoned to the throat despite the mild morning, hands folded around a leather handbag like it might bolt. She got into the back seat the way people get into churches—carefully, respectfully, as though the vinyl might object.

"You drive," she said, settling herself, "the way my father used to walk through his own factory floors. Like you own everything you can see."

"That's just good posture," Felix said, pulling out. "Where to?"

"Records office. But take Miller Street. Not the highway."

Felix took Miller Street. It added six minutes and they both knew why Camille wanted them—because Miller Street ran parallel to Miller's Road, close enough that a person could look out a window and see the place where everything had started, without ever having to stand on it.

For the first mile, Camille watched the storefronts go by with the expression of someone inventorying a life she'd been evicted from. Then, without preamble:

"I want to tell you where I was the night my father died."

Felix's hands went very still on the wheel. Ten and two. Model citizen grip. "Okay."

"Because I know you've been asking questions. Don't look surprised—you have a face for it, Miss Fairweather. You listen the way my mother used to listen to the radio, like the answer might come out of the speaker if she held still enough. Julian mentioned you. He does that now, mentions people, the way other men mention weather." Her mouth thinned. "So. The night my father died, I was at the Bellhaven Inn, at the charity dinner for the library roof. I arrived at six-fifteen. I sat at table nine, between Doctor Prasad and the Hollis sisters. I left at eleven-forty. There are forty people who will swear to it, photographs exist, and the innkeeper remembers my coat because he complimented it."

The recitation came out smooth as poured cream. Every time stamped, every name placed, the whole evening laid out like silverware at a formal dinner. And Felix, who had spent twelve years listening to people explain themselves at two in the morning—drunk men explaining their marriages, frightened women explaining their bruises, teenagers explaining the smell in the car—all of whom lied, all of them, but lied *loosely*, with gaps and stumbles and the beautiful human mess of improvisation—felt the wrongness of it before she could name it.

It was too clean. Nobody remembered their own evening like a train schedule. Nobody's alibi arrived pre-assembled.

"You rehearsed that," Felix said.

The silence from the back seat lasted exactly long enough to be honest.

"Yes," Camille said finally. "Three times. Once to the mirror, twice to my lawyer. He insisted. He said that in a family like mine, the innocent person needs an alibi more than the guilty one, because nobody investigates the guilty—they investigate whoever has something to gain, and after the will changed, that was me." A breath. "My father left me forty thousand dollars and the lake house. Julian got everything else. Do you understand what that means, in a town like this? It means half of Bellhaven believes I killed him for spite, and the other half believes I killed him because forty thousand wasn't enough. My lawyer says rehearsed is better than stumbling. My lawyer may be right. But you caught it in under a minute, so perhaps he's wrong about the general public."

Felix turned onto Court Street, the records office rising ahead, gray and indifferent. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to find out anyway. You're the type." Camille leaned forward slightly, and Felix caught her eyes in the rearview mirror—pale, steady, tired in a way that sleep didn't fix. "And because I need to know whether you believe me. Not the alibi. Me."

* * *

Felix parked in the shade of a sycamore that dropped little helicopters onto the windshield all summer. She didn't get out. Neither did Camille. The meter of the conversation, if conversations had meters, was still running.

"Here's what I know," Felix said, and gave herself permission to spend a little of the hoard. "Your father died on a Thursday night. Somebody canceled a pickup from Voss Manor that evening using an account code that belonged to my mother—who'd been dead a year. The confession letter that showed up afterward was written in her handwriting by somebody who knows it intimately. And the account code was known by exactly three living people: me, Harlan Boyd, and whoever Ruth trusted enough to teach it to. So when you ask if I believe you, what I actually need to know is simpler. Did you know my mother?"

Something moved across Camille's face—not guilt, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone hearing a name they'd buried under other names.

"I knew *of* her," Camille said carefully. "Ruth Fairweather. The woman who went to prison for the Kessler embezzlement. My father spoke of her exactly once, and I remember it because he never spoke of servants or drivers or anyone beneath his notice, and yet he spoke of her with—" she searched, "—caution. Like mentioning a dog that had bitten someone. 'That Fairweather woman knows things,' he said. That was the whole sentence. My father did not say sentences like that about women who merely stole money."

"And you never asked him what she knew?"

"I was fifty-one years old, Miss Fairweather, and my father had been teaching me not to ask questions since I was four. You don't unlearn it in a year. You don't unlearn it ever, really—you just get old enough that the not-asking starts to hurt." She looked down at her folded hands. "That's what Julian inherited, you know. Not the money. The not-asking. Except with him it curdled. With him, the questions stopped being forbidden and started being *dangerous*, and he made sure everyone understood the difference."

Felix turned off the engine. The sycamore helicopters ticked against the glass. "Your alibi. Walk me through it again. Not the schedule—the details. What did you eat?"

Camille blinked. "What?"

"At dinner. Table nine. What did you order?"

A pause, and then, slowly, the rehearsal began to crack—not collapsing, but softening, becoming human. "The salmon. No—I ordered the chicken, and I regretted it, because the Hollis sister nearest me had the salmon and it smelled extraordinary. I sent back the chicken. They brought the salmon. Which means the kitchen has a record too, if anyone cares to check, which they won't, because who audits a salmon?" Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first Felix had seen on her. "You're testing me."

"I test everybody. It's the job. People lie on the phone all night long—about where they are, who they're with, why they need a car at three a.m. to a address they won't say out loud. The lies aren't the interesting part. The interesting part is *which* lies. A man lying about an affair gives you a false address but a true time. A woman lying about fear gives you a true address and a false calm. Everybody lies a little, Miss Voss. The trick is learning what each person's particular little lie is protecting."

"And mine?" Camille asked quietly. "What is my little lie protecting?"

Felix considered her honestly. "Fear. Same as most people. You're terrified that if you don't have every minute accounted for, you'll end up like my mother—convicted of a story someone else wrote." She paused. "But there's one thing in your schedule that doesn't fit, and I'd like you to tell me about it before I tell you what it is."

Camille went very still. "Go on."

"The library dinner ended at eleven. The program says so; I checked the paper. But the inn's parking log—and yes, we keep tabs on lots our cars wait in—shows your car didn't leave until twelve-oh-five. An hour. An hour between the last toast and your taillights. Where were you for that hour, Miss Voss? Because that hour is either the hole in your alibi, or the truest part of it, and I genuinely don't know which."

* * *

Camille Voss sat in the back of cab forty-seven—for Felix had taken the old forty-seven out that morning, out of habit, out of superstition, out of whatever you call the feeling that certain vehicles have become characters in your life—and for a long moment she looked out the window at the records office like it had personally disappointed her.

Then she laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, startled out of her.

"An hour," she repeated. "Of course. Of course there's a log. My lawyer never thought to ask about the *parking*." She pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. "Fine. Fine. I'll trade you truth for truth, since that appears to be the currency you deal in. I stayed behind because I was drinking coffee I didn't want and working up the courage to do something. My father had called me that afternoon. The day he died. He wanted to meet Sunday. He said—" her voice caught, steadied, "—he said, 'Camille, I've been a coward for thirty-five years and I'm running out of time to stop.' I thought he meant the will. I thought he'd finally decided to fix it, to put things right, whatever *right* meant to a man like him. So I sat in that parking lot for an hour trying to decide whether to call him back and beg him to do it, or call him back and beg him not to, because I was afraid of what he'd break fixing it." Her hands tightened on the handbag. "And while I sat there deciding, someone made the decision for him."

The sycamore seeds drifted down. Somewhere across the street, the records office clock struck the quarter hour, indifferent as everything official in Bellhaven.

"So your alibi has a hole," Felix said slowly, "and the hole is the one part that's true."

"My lawyer will be devastated," Camille said dryly. Then the dryness fell away, and what was underneath was older and rawer. "Miss Fairweather. Felix. I need to ask you something and I need you not to perform for me, because God knows I've done enough performing for both of us this morning." She leaned forward. "The account code. Your mother's code. You said three people knew it. You, Boyd, and one other. Have you considered that the third person might not have needed Ruth to *teach* them anything? That they might have simply... read it? Off documents, off letters, off anything she touched in thirty years of correspondence?" A beat. "Have you considered that the person who forged those letters learned her hand somewhere legitimate? Someone she *wrote to*?"

Felix felt the ground shift under the cab's parked wheels.

Letters. Ruth had written letters for a year—from prison, to Felix, weekly, dutifully, lovingly. Letters full of fares and codes and stories that were lessons. Letters that someone with access, patience, and a reason could have studied the way a forger studies a signature.

"Who had access to her letters?" Felix asked, and heard her own voice go flat and careful, the dispatcher's voice, the one that doesn't shake.

Camille held her gaze in the mirror. "That, Miss Fairweather, is the question I hoped you'd already answered. Because I've spent two weeks asking it about my brother, and every road I walk down ends at the same terrible door: Julian visited your mother. Near the end. Twice, that I know of—he used the family lawyer's name on the visitor log, but lawyers bill by the hour and ours billed nothing that spring." She opened her handbag, drew out a folded paper, and held it between the seats like a peace offering or a grenade. "I shouldn't give you this. My lawyer would faint. But you strike me as a woman who keeps better records than any lawyer in this county."

Felix took it. A photocopy of a prison visitor log, dated fourteen months ago. Two entries, both under the name *Aldous Wren, Esq.* Both signed out at times that matched nothing on Wren's billing calendar—Camille had annotated the margins in small, furious pencil.

And beneath the second entry, in the log's cramped official hand, a note Felix had seen once before and never placed until this moment: *Visitor declined to sign ledger; staff noted resemblance to prior visitor of same description.*

Prior visitor. Prior description. Someone had come to see Ruth Fairweather before Julian did. Someone whose face rang a bell with the prison staff.

Felix stared at the page, and the morning seemed to tilt, gently, like a cab taking a corner too fast.

"Camille," she said. "This note. Who else visited your mother—your *father*—in the last year of her life? Think. Anyone. Even once."

Camille frowned. "Father never visited anyone. He barely left the manor." But then she stopped, and Felix watched the same cold realization move through her that was already moving through Felix's own chest, because Camille had grown up in that manor too, and she knew its rhythms, and she knew that Edmund Voss had taken exactly one standing appointment outside its walls, every month, for thirty years, and had always told the family it was the cardiologist.

"He drove himself," Camille whispered. "He never let anyone drive him. He said doctors required privacy." Her eyes came up, wide and pale. "Felix. What if my father wasn't seeing a doctor?"

Outside, the sycamore seeds kept falling, patient as snow, covering everything equally—the cab, the courthouse, the road that led, eventually, to Miller's Road and everything buried along it. Felix folded the visitor log into her coat pocket, over her heart, where she was starting to keep all the dangerous papers, and put the key back in the ignition.

"One way to find out," she said. "Prison visitor logs go back thirty years. And I know exactly who to call to pull them."

She was reaching for the radio when her phone buzzed against the dashboard. Unknown number. She answered the way she answered everything, flat and professional—*"Bellhaven Taxi"*—and heard breathing on the line, and then a voice she knew, gentle and reasonable and utterly wrong, saying:

"Felix. I hear you and my sister have been comparing notes. I'd hate for Camille to get hurt by association. Some passengers, you know, shouldn't be picked up at all."

The line went dead.

Felix sat very still, engine idling, Camille watching her face in the mirror and reading there everything Felix couldn't say out loud.

"Well," Camille said at last, into the silence. "I suppose that settles who the third visitor was."

"No," Felix said, turning the wheel toward the prison road, toward thirty years of logs and one name she was suddenly afraid to find. "It settles that we're out of time. Hold on, Miss Voss. This fare's about to run the meter hot."
