## 6. The Anniversary Guest List

By the third morning at Ashcombe House, Vesper had learned the house's language.

It spoke in drafts and door hinges, in the particular groan of the third stair from the landing, in the way light moved through the long gallery like a slow tide. It spoke through Ruth's footsteps—brisk on service floors, softer near Marguerite's wing—and through the silences that pooled in certain rooms the way water pools in a hull with a slow leak. Every hotel has its own dialect, and every great house is only a hotel that stopped pretending to welcome strangers. Vesper had spent fifteen years learning to listen. Ashcombe was fluent, and it was saying something she didn't yet understand.

She rose before six, as she always did, and dressed in the dark grey uniform of an event coordinator—a costume she wore now with the ease of long practice and the discomfort of a woman who knew exactly what costumes cost. In the Aurelia, she had been the one who saw through staff; here, she was staff, and the seeing had to be done sideways, through reflections in silver trays and half-heard conversations carried on the wind off the terrace.

The anniversary dinner was four days away. The house had begun to fill with the machinery of celebration: crates of crystal from London, a florist's van idling by the gatehouse, two men on ladders replacing bulbs in the chandelier of the great hall where thirty years ago—or so the family story went—Edmund Delacroix-Ashford had last raised a glass among the living.

Vesper stood at the service window of the kitchen with a clipboard she barely needed, watching the caterer's crew unload their van, and let the morning come to her.

* * *

Julian Delacroix arrived at the catering table at nine-forty, precisely when the head chef was deepest in his inventory and least able to be interrupted. Vesper noted the timing the way she noted everything: without appearing to note it at all. She was folding napkin samples at the far end of the terrace, her back half-turned, her ears doing the work her eyes could not.

"—and the seating plan stays exactly as I sent it," Julian was saying. His voice had the polished warmth of a man who had spent decades making demands sound like favors. "Mrs. Delacroix-Ashford's chair faces the window. No one sits to her immediate right. That place setting comes out."

"Out?" The chef's voice, younger, uncertain. "You said forty covers."

"I'm saying thirty-nine now." A pause, the soft clink of something being set down on the table between them—an envelope, Vesper thought, or something shaped like one. "Consider it a bonus for your discretion. My aunt's health is delicate. She tires of conversation. If anyone asks about the empty place, it's for a guest who cancelled. Understood?"

A silence long enough to have a texture. Then the chef: "Understood, Mr. Delacroix."

Vesper folded another napkin into a shape she wasn't looking at. An empty place at Marguerite's right hand. Removed at Julian's instruction, not Marguerite's. She turned the fact over as she turned the linen—carefully, by its corners. Who had been meant to sit there? And who had decided, thirty years later, that they no longer would?

She risked a glance. Julian was walking away across the lawn, phone already at his ear, his other hand sliding something into the inner pocket of his blazer. He did not look back at the catering table. Men like Julian never looked back at transactions; looking back was for people who feared being seen. He was afraid of nothing this morning.

But he had not counted the napkins. He had not noticed the woman folding them, because to Julian Delacroix, staff were furniture that occasionally spoke. It was the single greatest gift his class had ever given her, and she accepted it with both hands.

* * *

Colette was harder.

Where Julian performed, Colette hid, and hiding required different instruments. Vesper tracked her by absence: the sister-in-law was missing from breakfast, missing from the terrace, missing from the drawing room where Sefu Okonkwo sat with his laptop and his lawyer's quiet, conducting business as though the house around him were merely weather. It was Ruth who gave Colette away—not by word but by direction. Vesper watched her carry a tray of untouched sherry up the east stairs and turn, at the landing, toward the old nursery corridor, and Vesper filed the route away.

She found the room an hour later by smell before sight: smoke, sweet and chemical, the ghost of burned paper. It seeped under a door at the end of the corridor, a door painted the same cream as the wall, designed to be forgotten.

Vesper did not open it. Opening doors was what amateurs did. Instead she continued past at a housekeeper's pace, unremarkable, and descended the back stair, and let herself out onto the kitchen garden path where the burn barrel stood—the one the groundskeeper used for clippings. It held ash. Not garden ash. Paper ash, fine and white, still warm at the center when she passed close enough to feel it, with fragments at the edges that hadn't fully surrendered: a corner of heavy cream stock, the kind used for personal correspondence; a sliver of blue ink, the tail of a letter, perhaps an *f*, perhaps an *l*; and one fragment larger than the rest, no bigger than a stamp, bearing three words in a hand she recognized from the confession letter—recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water.

*My dearest Edmund.*

Marguerite's handwriting. Burning letters written to or by a drowned man, thirty years after he drowned.

Vesper straightened, smoothed her apron, and walked back inside with her heart beating a rhythm she hadn't permitted it since she'd signed the contract. Above the nursery corridor, a window opened and closed. She did not look up. Looking up was how invisible people became visible.

* * *

They came for her room that evening while she was at dinner.

Not her dinner—staff ate at seven in the basement kitchen, and Vesper had made herself punctual and dull, eating quickly beside a florist's assistant who talked endlessly about peonies. She had left her quarters locked. She had left them *looking* locked, which was different: the hair she'd pressed into the jamb of the wardrobe, the thread laid across the seam of her suitcase, the pencil mark aligned with the floorboard crack beneath the bed. Fifteen years in hotels had taught her that locks kept out honest people and nothing else, and that the only reliable security was evidence of entry.

She climbed the servants' stair at eight-twenty and felt it before she saw it: the air of her room had changed. Occupied spaces have a stillness, and hers had been stirred.

The thread was gone. The hair was gone. The pencil mark had been realigned—nearly perfectly. Nearly. Whoever had searched her quarters was good, better than good, but they had replaced the pencil a degree off true, and they had not known to check the lining of the curtain hem, where Vesper kept the one thing she never left behind: the second letter, Marguerite's contract, folded into a rectangle the size of a communion wafer.

Still there. Her hands shook as she confirmed it, and she stood in the middle of the small neat room and let them shake, because there was no one to see, and because terror deferred is terror doubled.

Someone had gone through her clothes. Someone had lifted her mattress, checked beneath the loose board she herself had found loose on her first night—had found the same hollow she had, and left it looking untouched. Someone knew she was worth searching, which meant someone knew she was more than event staff, which meant—

Which meant she had failed. Or someone else had spoken. Or the house itself had told on her, the way houses do, the way Ashcombe seemed determined to do, leaking its secrets through walls and ash barrels and empty chairs.

She sat on the edge of the bed in the dark and made herself think like a concierge instead of a frightened woman. Who had access? Ruth, obviously—but Ruth had warned her off, and warnings were the currency of people who wanted you gone, not robbed. The groundskeeper. One of the caterer's crew, though their vans left each night. Sefu, who moved through the house like a man auditing it. Julian, who had the entitlement to enter any room and the arrogance to leave it imperfectly. Colette, who smelled of smoke and sherry and grief.

Or Marguerite herself. Dying upstairs behind a closed door, directing her final production with whatever strength remained. Vesper considered this last possibility longer than the others. The confession letter had been a summons. The contract had been bait. Perhaps the search was simply the next act in a play whose script only one person had read.

But the pencil mark had been moved by a careful hand, and Marguerite, Vesper suspected, was past careful. Marguerite was past everything except finishing.

Below her window, across the gravel sweep, the great hall glowed with workmen's lights, the chandelier half-lit, the long tables taking shape for a dinner that would seat thirty-nine where forty had been planned. Somewhere in that arithmetic was a name. Somewhere in the ash barrel was a letter. And somewhere in this house was a person who had stood over her belongings today, touching her things, reading her the way she read guests—looking for what she hid.

Vesper lay down without undressing and listened to the house settle around her, beam by beam, like an old animal arranging its bones.

For fifteen years she had survived by being unseen. Tonight, for the first time since the Hotel Aurelia had taught her the trade, Vesper Okafor understood what it was to be the one observed—and how the observed must feel, every day, in every lobby, under every polite glance: flayed, exposed, known.

Tomorrow she would find out who had opened her door.

And tomorrow, she promised herself in the dark, she would stop pretending the empty chair didn't have a name. Because at breakfast she had heard Ruth mention it, just once, in passing, to the florist—*mind the east end, that's where Mr. Edmund always sat*—and Vesper had done the arithmetic no one had asked her to do.

Forty covers. Thirty-nine settings. Marguerite's right hand, empty.

Thirty years ago, Edmund Delacroix-Ashford had drowned within sight of this house. Now his brother-in-law was paying to keep his ghost out of a chair.

Vesper closed her eyes and let the fear become fuel.

In the morning, she would ask Ruth—who had been a maid in this house in 1994—exactly what she remembered about the night of the storm.
