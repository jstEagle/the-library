## 13. Vivienne Disappears

I woke at six-forty to a silence that was wrong.

You learn the sound of a hotel at night — the pipes ticking, the elevator's old bones settling, someone's television murmuring through two walls of plaster. The Verlaine had been humming along beneath me for four nights now, and I'd learned its voice the way you learn a sleeping child's breathing. This morning it was holding its breath.

I dressed without sitting down. I don't know how to explain that except to say my body knew before I did. I was in the corridor with my shoes half-laced when I passed Vivienne's door — 407, the room Orsen had assigned her herself, away from the stairwell, away from the service lift, chosen like a chess move — and saw the maid's cart standing outside it, untouched, its linens still folded in their plastic.

At seven the maids worked the fourth floor. Always. Orsen had told me that on the first night, in that flat, unhurried way of hers, as if she were reading me a train schedule. *The floor is watched. Nothing happens on the fourth floor without the desk knowing.*

The cart hadn't moved. It was 6:47.

I knocked. Nothing. I knocked again and said her name, and the word came out smaller than I intended, the way words do when you already know the answer to the question they're shaped like.

The door was unlocked.

* * *

Her room looked like a departure. That's the only way I can describe it, and I've spent three days trying to find a better one. The bed was made — not slept-in-and-straightened, but *made*, corners tucked with a crispness Vivienne never managed, because Vivienne had never once in her life made a bed. The bathroom towel hung squared on its bar. Her toiletry bag sat closed on the shelf, zipped, aligned with the edge of the counter like an exhibit.

Everything tidy. Everything staged. My sister had lived out of that room like a woman under siege for four days — clothes over chairs, case open, papers fanned — and now it looked like a photograph in a brochure.

Except her coat was on the hook behind the door.

It was October. She'd arrived wearing that coat, grey wool, the one she'd saved three months' salary to buy the year our father died, because she said a Wren ought to look like she owned something even when she owned nothing. She would not have left it. Not in this city, not in this cold, not voluntarily. You could have staged every other object in that room and I would have doubted; the coat I believed instantly, completely, the way you believe a wound.

On the desk, weighted under the water glass, was a note in her handwriting.

*Gone ahead. Don't follow. Will explain everything soon. — V.*

I stood there reading it until the letters stopped meaning anything. Because I knew her hand better than anyone alive, and it was hers, and that was exactly what frightened me. Anyone who'd seen her sign the hotel register could copy it. Anyone who'd watched her write. And someone had been watching her — we all knew that by now, we'd all felt it, the pressure in the corridors, the sense of a script being followed by someone patient enough to wait thirty years between lines.

Orsen Marchetti was already at the front desk when I came down. Of course she was. I don't think the woman sleeps so much as powers down in intervals, like a lighthouse. She took one look at me — I must have been grey, I must have been shaking, I could feel my own hands doing something independent of my will — and she didn't ask what was wrong. She lifted the gate in the counter and said, "Room 407," and walked.

She read the note once. She picked up the coat, held it, pressed the wool against her cheek briefly — checking, I realized later, whether it was damp, whether it smelled of river or rain or petrol or anything at all. It didn't. Then she opened the wardrobe, knelt, and looked under the bed, and did a dozen small methodical things while I stood in the doorway falling apart quietly.

"The attaché case," she said finally.

"Gone."

"You're certain."

"It was under her bed every night. She told me — she told me if anything happened, the first thing to check was the case." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated myself for it. "It's gone, Ms. Marchetti. The case is gone and she's gone and the note says—"

"The note says nothing." She set the coat down with strange gentleness. "A woman leaving voluntarily takes her coat. A woman packing takes her own papers, not just the ones worth stealing. And your sister has not willingly written a full sentence since she arrived — she types everything, she made the lawyers initial their copies, she trusts nothing to handwriting she hasn't controlled." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes were the color of a winter harbor. "This note was written by someone who studied her but doesn't know her. Which means it was written fast. Which means whoever took her wanted us calm for as long as possible."

"Then she's alive."

"For now, yes. Alive is the point. Alive is the entire point." Something moved behind her face then, some private arithmetic completing itself, and for a moment she looked less like a concierge than like a woman standing at a door she'd nailed shut years ago. "Mr. Wren. Sit down."

"I can't—"

"*Sit down.*"

I sat on the edge of the maid's cart. She let that pass, which from Orsen Marchetti was an act of mercy.

"There is something you haven't told me," she said. "There has been since the night you hired me. I've let it go because I thought it was shame, and shame keeps. But shame doesn't keep anymore, Mr. Wren. Your sister is gone, and whatever you're carrying is now evidence."

And there it was. The moment I'd been dreading since the day I walked into this hotel with a retainer and a lie of omission folded into my pocket like a knife I kept forgetting I was holding.

So I told her.

* * *

"Three weeks ago I got a letter," I said. "No return address. Typed. One page."

"What did it say?"

"That my sister would be murdered before the month was out. That the police would call it an accident, the way they always did in our family. And that if I wanted the truth about 1994 — the real truth, provable truth — I should bring her to the Hotel Verlaine and hire the concierge."

The silence went on long enough that I heard the boiler cycle somewhere below us.

"Say it again," Orsen said. Her voice hadn't changed at all. That was the terrifying part. "The last part."

"'Bring her to the Hotel Verlaine,'" I repeated. "'And put the matter in the hands of the woman at the desk.'"

"And you didn't think to mention that the person paying you to prevent a murder had been *assigned to you by the person threatening one?*"

"I thought—" I heard how thin it sounded even as I said it. "I thought it was a test. I thought maybe the letter-writer was testing whether I'd actually do it, whether I loved her enough. People send letters like that. Cranks. And the alternative was going to the police, and the police—" I laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "Ms. Marchetti, my family has been going to the police about accidents for thirty years. My uncle drowned and they wrote *accident* in a file and closed it. My father died asking questions and they wrote *heart failure*. When your whole life teaches you that the official door leads nowhere, you stop knocking on it. The letter offered me the one thing no one else ever had: a way in. So I took it. I brought her here. I paid you. And I told myself the letter was a crank right up until tonight."

"Who posted it?"

"Local stamp. City postmark. Hand-delivered to the box, the carrier said."

"Have you kept it?"

"In my room. In a book. I didn't trust the safe — the lawyers use the safe."

Something flickered across her face at that — *the lawyers* — filed away at speed. She crossed to the desk telephone, lifted it, and dialed a single digit with one finger, never taking her eyes off me.

"Léonie. Fourth floor, 407, and the service corridor after. Who signed in overnight, who used the freight lift, when did the night porter do his rounds — exact times, please." A pause. "Yes. Now."

She set the receiver down and looked at me again, and this time what was in her face wasn't anger. It was worse. It was pity.

"Do you understand what you did?" she asked, not unkindly. "Someone chose you, Mr. Wren. Out of everyone who loves that woman, someone selected you — measured you, found you frightened and loyal and slightly gullible, and used you as a delivery mechanism. You didn't bring your sister to safety. You brought her to the address on the invitation."

"I know." My eyes were burning. "I know. I'm sorry. God, I'm sorry — I'll do anything, I'll—"

"Then stop apologizing and start remembering." She pulled a chair around and sat facing me, knees nearly touching, close enough that I could see the fine tired lines around her eyes. "The letter. Every detail. Paper, fold, font, phrasing. Anything odd?"

I closed my eyes. The letter swam up: the cheap paper, the elite typewriter face, the flat declarative sentences. And then—

"One thing. At the bottom. After the signature space — there wasn't a signature, just a line left blank — there was a number. Small, typed in the corner. '412.' I assumed it was a reference number. Case number, something administrative."

Orsen Marchetti went very still.

Not startled-still. Still the way water goes still right before it freezes. I watched her knuckles whiten on the chair arms, watched her breath go shallow and deliberate, and I understood that I had just said something that reached into her past and closed its hand.

"Room 412," she said quietly.

"Is it— does it mean something?"

"It means," she said, rising, "that whoever wrote your letter knows this hotel the way I do. Better." She was already moving, snatching her key ring from the board behind the desk, and there was a new register in her voice, low and rapid, the voice of a woman running calculations too fast to speak them all aloud. "Léonie will bring the logs. The freight lift was serviced last Tuesday — I remember because the mechanic complained about the gate latch, which means it stops between floors if you know the trick of it. And the night porter, Bertrand, drinks his coffee at five-forty in the office with the door shut, which leaves twenty minutes unwatched."

"You think she's still in the building."

"I think," Orsen said, pulling on her coat, "that a man who stages departures wants time. Time to move her, or time to make us search the whole city while she's fifty meters away. And I think he made one mistake." She stopped at the foot of the stairs and looked back at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, Orsen Marchetti smiled — a thin, cold, absolutely fearless expression that made me understand, finally, why three dead people's secrets had come apart in her hands.

"He sent his letter to the wrong concierge."

She threw me the spare master key.

"Fourth floor," she said. "Start with the linen room. And Mr. Wren — touch nothing, and if you hear the freight lift move, you run to me, not to it."

I took the key and ran for the stairs, and behind me I heard her speaking into the telephone in a voice I'd never heard her use — not the desk voice, not the careful, neutral voice of the woman who solved murders between check-ins, but something older and harder, a voice with thirty years of grief banked behind it:

"Inspector Lachaire. Wake up. It's Orsen Marchetti — and this time you're going to listen to everything I say."
