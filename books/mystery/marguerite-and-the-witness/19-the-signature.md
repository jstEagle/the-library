## 19. The Signature

They had dressed the room for a funeral.

That was Vivienne's first thought when they brought her up, past the sealed door on the sixth floor that the staff pretended not to see, into the old attic suite where the Hotel Verlaine kept its history like a body in a wardrobe. Dust sheets over the furniture — but freshly laundered dust sheets, corners pressed, as if someone had anticipated her arrival and wanted the room to look respectable. A writing desk by the dormer window. A single lamp with a green glass shade. On the desk, squared to the edge with a precision she recognized from thirty years of estate paperwork, lay a document.

And beside it, a pen.

Not just any pen. A fountain pen, black lacquer, gold nib, the kind her father had used for signatures and nothing else. The kind Silas Renner had always carried for him, uncapped and ready, standing at his shoulder like a second shadow.

"You remember it," Renner said. It wasn't a question.

"I remember everything about my father's death," Vivienne said. "Which is more than I can say for you."

He smiled at that — a small, unhurried smile, the smile of a man who had been patient for three decades and could afford another hour. He was older than she'd expected. In the photographs from the estate file he had been a gray presence at the edge of every family gathering, and she had assumed age had finished the job of erasing him. But here, under the green lamp, he seemed less erased than distilled. Silver hair combed straight back. A cardigan the color of wet slate. Hands folded, clean, quiet hands.

"Sit down, Miss Wren," he said. "You've had a long night."

"I've had a long thirty years."

"Yes." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that was the worst thing yet — that he could say *yes* to her grief as if he shared it, as if they were two mourners at the same grave instead of the gravedigger and the last name on the stone. "Sit anyway."

She sat. Not because he asked. Because her legs had opinions of their own, and because the chair was between her and the window, and the window was the only thing in this room that touched the outside world. Through the dormer glass she could see a slice of Paris roofline, chimney pots, a smear of gray morning light. Somewhere below were six floors of hotel, a concierge named Orsen Marchetti who watched everything, an inspector who might or might not believe anything, and her brother Aldous, who had lied to her, who had brought her here on the strength of an anonymous letter and called it protection.

If she ever got out of this room, she was going to kill him herself. Slowly. With paperwork.

"Tea?" Renner asked.

"No."

"You should keep your strength up."

"I should keep my wits about me," Vivienne said. "Strength is for people who intend to survive by force. I intend to survive by being insufferable."

Something flickered across his face — not annoyance. Appreciation. That frightened her more than anger would have. Anger meant she was surprising him. Appreciation meant he had already accounted for her.

"The document," he said, turning it so it faced her. "You'll want to read it. You're your father's daughter; you'll read every line. I'd think less of you if you didn't."

It was the codicil. She knew it before she touched the paper — she'd spent two years in probate litigation and could smell a codicil the way other people smelled rain. Her father's final amendment, executed but never filed, contingent on a living signature: hers. The estate would pass entire, unencumbered, outside the reach of Odile and Tomas Ferrand and their endless claims. All of it hers — provided she signed while alive, of sound mind, in the presence of the executor.

Provided she signed *here*, in this room, in this building, before tomorrow noon.

"And if I don't sign?" she asked, though she already knew. She'd read the will a hundred times. She'd read it in bed, in airports, in the waiting room of a notary in Lyon. If she died intestate as to the codicil — if no living signature existed by the deadline — the estate reverted to the 1994 distribution. The distribution built on her uncle's drowned body. The distribution that had made her father rich and everyone else complicit or silent.

"If you don't sign," Renner said, "the money goes where it has always been going. And you—" he paused, choosing the word with a craftsman's care, "—will have had an accident. I'm told the stairs in this hotel are treacherous. Margot Sennet could tell you, but alas."

Vivienne kept her face still. Margot. Her father's oldest counsel, the woman who had taught her contract law over burnt coffee and contempt. They had found her at the bottom of the service stairs four days ago, and the police had said *drink*, and Vivienne had known — the way you know a forged signature, instantly, in the gut — that Margot Sennet had not had a drink in eleven years.

"You killed her," Vivienne said.

"I arranged her circumstances," Renner said. "There's a difference. There is always a difference. That difference is the whole of my profession."

*Your profession.* Not law. Not execution of estates. Something older and quieter: the making of accidents. She thought of the boathouse letters hidden in the lodge — proof that her uncle Étienne had been murdered over the will, proof she had carried across half of France in a locked attaché case until someone took it from her room. Proof that had cost Margot her life, probably, and might yet cost Vivienne hers.

But the letters weren't the only copy anymore. That was the thought she held onto, the splinter of light she'd wedged into the dark. Orsen Marchetti had read them. Whatever else happened in this attic, whatever happened to Vivienne Wren, the concierge of the Hotel Verlaine now knew what had happened at the lakeside lodge in 1994. You couldn't unread a dead man's handwriting. You couldn't un-know a murder.

Unless you killed the knower too. Hence, presumably, the fresh dust sheets.

"Before I sign," Vivienne said, "I have questions."

Renner sighed — a small, theatrical sigh, the sigh of a man indulging a child at bedtime. "Of course you do."

"Then let's begin with capacity." She pulled the document toward her and did not pick up the pen. "A codicil requires the testator's intent, but the beneficiary's signature must be given freely and without duress. I am currently, by any reasonable definition, under duress. Imprisoned in a sealed suite, threatened with staged death, following the actual staged death of my attorney. Any court in France would void this signature before the ink dried."

"A court would have to hear about it," Renner said mildly.

"A court will hear about it. I've made arrangements."

"I know about your arrangements, Miss Wren. The copies. The letters. The concierge." He said the name without emphasis, which somehow gave it enormous emphasis. "I've had thirty years to learn how arrangements fail. Arrangements are made by people, and people are unreliable. People fall down stairs. People leave candles burning. People sign documents they shouldn't and then can't be reached to correct the record."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere far below, muffled through floors of old stone, a telephone rang and was answered. Vivienne fixed her eyes on that sound — the ordinary world, still operating, still full of switchboards and witnesses — and made herself breathe.

"Then we come to my second question," she said. "Witnesses. A codicil acceptance requires attestation. Two witnesses, present, uninterested parties. Where are yours?"

"In the corridor."

"Names?"

"You'll meet them at the appropriate moment."

"That *is* the appropriate moment. Now. Because if those witnesses are interested parties — if either of them stands to gain under the 1994 distribution — then the signature is defective, and we both know it, and you've wasted a kidnapping on an invalid instrument."

For the first time, something moved behind Renner's eyes. Not fear. Recalculation. He turned his head slightly, the way a man does when listening to a clock, and Vivienne understood that he was doing arithmetic too — hours, deadlines, the statute closing at noon tomorrow, the narrow window in which a living signature still mattered.

"Caspian Hale," he said at last. "And a notary retained through the firm. Neither benefits under the prior distribution."

"Caspian Hale sold me to you."

"Caspian Hale was purchased by Margot Sennet, who was, in turn, managed by me. He's a young man with expensive tastes and no core. They're common. You'll find one in every generation." He said it without malice, the way a gardener says *aphids*. "He's waiting downstairs with the notary. They'll come up when we're finished with preliminaries."

"Preliminaries," Vivienne repeated. "Good. I love preliminaries. Let's do several."

* * *

She had learned stalling from the best — from Margot, ironically, who used to say that time was the only weapon the weak side owned, and that a lawyer who surrendered an hour without charging for it deserved to lose. So Vivienne charged. She questioned the codicil's execution date. She demanded the original will for comparison. She noted that the paper stock differed from her father's stationer's stock and required an explanation — which Renner provided, smoothly, correctly, infuriatingly — and then she disputed the explanation on procedural grounds she half-invented, and made him rebut her, and rebutted the rebuttal.

Hours were what she was spending, and she spent them like a woman burning furniture to keep a fire going, and she watched Renner watch her do it, and neither of them said the true thing aloud: that every hour she stole was an hour in which something outside this room might change. A switchboard. An inspector. A concierge who noticed everything.

Once, mid-objection, she heard it — faint, through the dormer glass, the clank of the old service lift in the wall behind the paneling. The dumbwaiter shaft. Her heart stumbled. She kept talking about paper stock, about the 1994 revision clause, about her uncle's initials on a page that had never borne them, and all the while some cold bright animal part of her brain screamed: *someone is in the walls, someone is listening, hold him here, hold him here.*

She didn't look at the paneling. She had learned that much from watching prey animals — you never look at the thing that might save you.

"You're very good at this," Renner said eventually, and there was real weariness in it now, the first honest note he'd struck. "Margot said you would be. She said, *the girl argues like her father and stalls like me.* He was proud of you. Did you know that? In the last year, especially. After the diagnosis. He used to say the estate would be safe because you read everything."

"He was right," Vivienne said. "I read everything. Including the file on my uncle's death. Including the coroner's photograph of the boathouse steps. Including a letter my father wrote in 1996 that he never sent, that I found after his funeral, that begins *Silas, I know what you did and I have decided to live with it, but if you ever touch my daughter—*" She let it hang. "He knew. From almost the beginning. And he paid you, didn't he? For thirty years. Hush money dressed as executor's fees. My inheritance is blood money with interest."

Renner was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone soft and terrible, the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried politely for decades.

"Your father was a coward," he said, "but he was a punctual coward, and I respected him for it. Your uncle was neither. Étienne wanted to tear up the will and give it all to charity and a woman in Annecy nobody talks about. Thirty years of a family's future, and he wanted to give it away in an afternoon because he'd grown a conscience." He unfolded his hands, refolded them. "I didn't kill him out of greed, Miss Wren. I killed him out of bookkeeping. Someone had to balance the ledger."

"And my mother's maid," Vivienne said quietly. "The one who died in the fire that same year. The one whose daughter works at your front desk. Was she also bookkeeping?"

The silence lasted three seconds. She counted them. In those three seconds, Silas Renner's face did something it hadn't done all night: it aged. Ten years fell onto it at once, like silt settling.

"You've been reading," he said.

"I've been reading everything. I told you."

"Then you understand why the girl at the desk cannot be permitted to keep reading." He rose, unhurried, and crossed to the desk, and picked up the black lacquer pen, and held it out to her across the document. "It's nearly ten. The witnesses are downstairs. Noon tomorrow is not a suggestion; it is arithmetic. Sign, and you walk out of this hotel alive, wealthy, and free — I give you my word, and my word, whatever you think of me, has never once been broken. Refuse, and I stop negotiating."

Vivienne looked at the pen. Then at the document. Then at the window, where the gray light had thickened toward morning, and somewhere beneath her, six floors down, a bell sat on a counter beside a photograph of a woman who had died in a fire to keep this man's ledger balanced.

*Stall like me,* Margot had said. Well. Margot had also taught her the oldest trick in probate, the one used by heirs and fraudsters and desperate women since ink was invented: *never sign the page in front of you until you've counted the pages behind it.*

"There's a problem," Vivienne said.

Renner's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "What problem?"

"Exhibit schedules." She flipped the codicil over, frowning, letting irritation climb into her voice — genuine irritation, which helped, because she genuinely was irritated, at all of it, at every man in her life who had treated her inheritance as a game board. "This codicil incorporates the 1994 distribution by reference. Which means the signature page has to be attached to a complete, paginated instrument. This copy is missing its schedule of assets — page nine. Without it, the incorporation fails, the whole codicil is arguably void, and you've wasted your evening. I need the complete set. From the firm's records. Tonight."

"The schedule exists upstairs in my office. Third floor."

"Then send for it."

"It's nearly ten o'clock. The staff—"

"Are employed by you," Vivienne said. "Send for it. Or we sit here until dawn arguing about whether page nine is load-bearing, and noon comes, and you find out exactly how much arithmetic you've lost."

She saw him weigh it. She saw the clock behind his eyes tick against the clock on the wall. And she saw, with a surge of triumph so fierce it nearly broke her composure, that he believed her — because she was right, because the flaw was real, because she had built her life on reading everything, and men like Silas Renner always underestimated a woman who read.

He went to the panel by the door and pulled the bell cord. Footsteps sounded in the corridor. Low instructions, in French, in that flat executor's cadence: *the 1994 file, the complete schedule, bring it up yourself, tell no one.*

When he turned back, Vivienne had the pen in her hand.

"Ah," Renner said. "You've reconsidered."

"I'm going to sign," Vivienne said. "Right now. While you wait for your page nine. Watch me."

She drew the document toward her, found the signature block, and wrote her name — *Vivienne Anne Wren* — in the careful, legible hand her father had drilled into her at age eight. She felt the nib catch on the good paper. She felt Renner lean in, satisfied, the ledger balancing itself in his mind.

And she felt, underneath the desk, her left hand doing the only work that mattered: turning back three pages, to the revocation clause, the page that undid everything — and signing *that* page too, quickly, in the same hand, the same name, so that the instrument now bore two signatures and only one of them was the one Silas Renner had asked for.

Under the doctrine Margot had loved to teach over burnt coffee — *an ambiguity construed against the drafter* — there were now two signature pages in play, and the wrong one was dated first, and proving which signature governed would take lawyers weeks. Weeks past noon tomorrow. Weeks past the statute. Weeks in which Vivienne Wren intended to remain inconveniently, stubbornly alive.

Renner straightened up, took the codicil, glanced at the signature page, and smiled — thin and correct and utterly unaware.

"There," he said. "Was that so difficult?"

"Not at all," said Vivienne, folding her hands over the page she had really signed, hiding it beneath the schedule of preliminaries like a card palmed at a table. "I find that once I've decided, I'm very decisive."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the old service lift clanked again — closer now, rising.

And Silas Renner, gathering his papers, paused with his head tilted, listening to it, and for the first time all night he looked less like an executor and more like a man who has heard a sound in a house he thought was empty.
