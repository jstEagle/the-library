## 2. A Body in Borrowed Clothes

The call came at 6:47 in the morning, which was the first wrong thing. Nobody called Yara Okafor before seven unless something had burned down or someone had died, and the cathedral had not burned.

"Mr. Okafor?" A woman's voice, clipped, unfamiliar. "This is Detective Brandt, Halden City Police. Are you at home?"

"I'm at the cathedral." He was, in fact, standing in his small flat above the archive with one shoe on and a cup of tea going cold in his hand. "Is something wrong?"

"We'd like you to come down to the canal dock on Verlaine Street. Can you do that?"

He could have asked why. He thought about asking why. But there was a quality to her voice — not sympathy, exactly, and not suspicion either, though he would learn later that it had been both — that made questions feel like a luxury he couldn't afford. "I can be there in twenty minutes."

"Don't touch anything when you arrive," she said, and hung up.

Yara stood for a moment in the gray light of his kitchen, listening to the building settle around him. Below him, through the floor, he could hear the cathedral waking: the boiler's cough, the first sacristan's keys, the pigeons arguing in the gutter of the north transept. The ordinary machinery of an ordinary Tuesday. He put on his other shoe and drank the tea anyway, because whatever was waiting for him at the canal dock, it would go better warm.

* * *

The dock at Verlaine Street was a stub of rotted timber and old mooring rings, the kind of place the city kept meaning to renovate and never did. The canal ran brown and slow behind the tannery district, carrying its cargo of leaves and shopping carts toward the lock gates. By the time Yara arrived, there were two police cars, an ambulance with its lights off, and a blue tarp over something at the water's edge.

A young officer tried to stop him at the cordon. Yara showed him nothing because he had nothing to show — no badge, no reason, no identity that mattered to anyone — but Detective Brandt appeared from behind the ambulance and waved him through, studying him as he came.

She was younger than her voice had suggested. Mid-thirties, maybe, dark hair pulled back so tightly it looked architectural, a notebook already open in her hand even though she wasn't writing anything. Her eyes did something Yara recognized and distrusted: they went over him the way he went over documents. Cover to cover. Looking for the forgery.

"You're the archivist," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yara Okafor."

"Do you know why you're here?"

"No."

"Then let me ask it differently." She turned and walked toward the tarp, and after a moment he followed, because what else was there to do. "Do you own a wool overcoat? Charcoal gray. Brass buttons. There's a repair on the left cuff — someone re-stitched it by hand, badly."

The morning tilted.

Yara stopped walking. He was aware of the canal smell, the diesel and the rot and the cold iron tang of the water, and he was aware, very precisely, of his own heartbeat, which had gone strange and loud.

"Yes," he said. "I do."

"When did you last see it?"

"It's—" He caught himself. *It's in my closet.* That was what he would have said yesterday. That was what he had said to himself on Sunday, hanging up his good coat after the restoration gala, except that when he'd reached for it Monday morning to walk to the market, the hook had been empty. The coat gone. The scarf too — the long gray scarf Agnes had given him three Christmases ago, the one with the dropped stitch near the fringe that he'd never had the heart to fix. He had searched the flat. He had searched the archive office. He had concluded, with mild irritation, that he had mislaid them somewhere between the vestry and the parking court, and he had resolved to look again this weekend.

That resolution sat in his chest now like a stone.

"The coat is missing," he said. "Since Sunday night. And a scarf."

Brandt wrote nothing down, which somehow frightened him more than if she'd filled the page. "Walk with me," she said.

She lifted the corner of the tarp herself, without gloves, which told him she'd already looked and already knew what was under it and was watching his face while he learned it too.

The man was perhaps fifty, heavyset, with the pallor of someone who had been in cold water for a long time. His hair was thinning and dark, plastered across his forehead. There was waterweed in his collar. His face was swollen past recognition by anyone who hadn't known him well, and Yara didn't know him at all — and yet something about him snagged, some half-remembered shape, a man he might have passed in a corridor or seen across a courtroom gallery years ago, gone before he could fix it.

And he was wearing Yara's coat. Charcoal wool, brass buttons, the bad stitching on the left cuff. The scarf was wound twice around the dead man's neck, tucked into the lapels, neat as a gift.

Yara heard himself breathing. He made himself stop.

"That's mine," he said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Both pieces."

"Yes," said Brandt. "We know. There was a dry cleaner's tag inside the collar. Okafor. We matched it to your address within two hours." She let the tarp fall. "Here's my problem, Mr. Okafor. This man has been in the water since sometime Sunday night. Your coat went missing Sunday night. You reported nothing stolen, told no one, and when I called you this morning you were ready to come down here in twenty minutes without asking a single question."

"I'm asking questions now."

"Not enough of them." She closed the notebook. Not writing. Still not writing. "Who is he?"

"I don't know."

"You're sure?"

"I've never seen his face before in my life." And then, because lying badly was worse than saying less: "Something about him is familiar. I can't place it."

Brandt studied him for a long moment. The canal lapped against the pilings. Somewhere behind them, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"Ferris Lund," she said finally. "That's our man. Does the name mean anything?"

It didn't. Yara shook his head, and watched her watch him shake it, and understood that every honest thing he said from this moment forward would be weighed against every dishonest thing she believed he might be capable of, and that the scale had been built before he ever arrived.

"How did he drown?" Yara asked.

"He fell in. Or was helped. The post-mortem will tell us more." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "You understand I have to ask where you were Sunday night."

"At the cathedral. The restoration gala. I worked it until nearly midnight — I was cataloguing the donations afterward. Then home."

"Anyone see you leave?"

"The night porter locked up after me. Father Anselm— " He stopped. The name was seven years buried; it had come out of some reflexive drawer of habit, the litany of names he no longer permitted himself. "The porter. Marek Dusek. He'll remember."

"Marek Dusek," Brandt repeated, writing it down at last, and Yara felt the small relief of having produced a fact like a man producing a receipt. "We'll talk to him. Mr. Okafor — don't leave the city. Don't leave the cathedral grounds, ideally. And I'd like the key to your flat."

"My flat?"

"And your closet. If the thief took the coat from your closet, we want to know how they got in. Locked door? Window?" She paused. "You do lock your door, Mr. Okafor?"

"Always," he said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

* * *

They let him walk back alone. He took the long way, along the canal path, because he needed the movement and because he did not trust his face yet. Invisible men survive by controlling their faces. For eleven years at St. Verena's, Yara Okafor had been furniture — the quiet archivist who shelved baptisms and funerals and confirmations, who fetched the 1911 property dispute file for visiting lawyers, who nodded in corridors and was forgotten before the nod finished. Invisibility was not something that happened to him. It was a discipline. He had practiced it the way monks practice silence, and it had kept him safe through things the city preferred to forget.

Now a detective had his name in a notebook. Now a dead stranger wore his coat.

At the footbridge he stopped and gripped the rail and made himself think, because fear was a fog and thinking was the only lamp he owned.

Facts. Ferris Lund had drowned Sunday night wearing clothes stolen from Yara's closet. Either Lund had taken them himself — which required getting into a locked flat above a locked archive, in a building full of people, during a gala — or someone else had taken them and put them on Lund. And nobody dressed a dead-adjacent stranger in another man's coat by accident. Clothes were evidence. Clothes pointed. Whoever had done this had wanted the police to come looking for Yara Okafor, and had wanted it specifically, deliberately, with the patience of someone who knew that an invisible man, once noticed, stays noticed.

Which meant whoever had done this knew him. Knew his habits, his closet, his name. Knew him well enough to hate him, or fear him, or both.

The canal slid past beneath the bridge, brown and patient, keeping its secrets the way it had kept Lund's until Sunday night spat him out.

Yara thought: *What did I ever do to anyone?*

And then, because he was who he was, he thought the second thing, the truer thing, the thing that cracked his routine open like ice on a pond:

*Nothing. Which means this isn't about what I did. It's about what I know.*

Three files sat in the locked crypt below his office. Three deaths the city had ruled accidents and Yara had quietly, privately, proved otherwise — solved on paper, in the margins, in a hand no one had ever read. He had never taken them to the police. Taking them to the police meant becoming visible, and visible men got hurt, and besides, all three cases were closed, sealed, mourned over and forgotten. What good would it do the dead to unbury them?

He had told himself that for years. Standing on the footbridge with a dead man's borrowed warmth still on his mind, Yara Okafor admitted, for the first time, that it had always been an excuse. He had stayed hidden because hiding was comfortable. And comfort, it turned out, had a price, and the price had just washed up at the Verlaine Street dock wearing his name in its collar.

Someone knew about the files. Someone had decided the archivist was dangerous.

Or — colder thought, arriving late and staying — someone wanted the archivist destroyed before he could become dangerous again.

* * *

By afternoon, the cathedral knew. Of course it did. Two uniformed officers had come through the cloister asking for Marek Dusek, and Marek, bless him and doom him, had confirmed everything within the hour: yes, Yara had left near midnight, alone, coatless — *coatless*, Yara noted, filing the word away with a chill; if Marek remembered him leaving without the coat, then the coat had already been taken by midnight, which narrowed the theft window to the gala hours themselves — and yes, he'd seemed perfectly normal, quiet as ever.

Quiet as ever. By evening it was being said in the refectory with a different intonation entirely.

He felt it in the way conversations paused when he entered the sacristy corridor. In Sister Bernadette's smile, which arrived a half-second too late. In the dean's handshake at vespers, firm and brief, the handshake of a man handling something fragile that might also be contaminated. No one accused him of anything. No one had to. Suspicion in a close community moves like damp through plaster — silent, spreading, showing itself only in stains.

Only Agnes met his eyes. Old Sister Agnes, ninety-one and sharp as a paper cut, caught him in the ambulatory after compline and held his sleeve with fingers like bird claws.

"They're saying a drowned man wore your coat," she said, without preamble.

"People say many things."

"They're saying you killed him." She said it flatly, the way she announced hymn numbers. "I don't believe it. But Yara — listen to me." Her grip tightened. "Whatever this is, it isn't random. Nothing in this building has been random for a very long time." And then she released him and shuffled off toward the convent door, leaving those words hanging in the candle-smell air like incense that wouldn't settle.

Yara stood alone in the ambulatory, beneath seven centuries of painted saints, and turned her sentence over.

*Nothing in this building has been random for a very long time.*

He should have dismissed it as the rambling of a tired old woman. Instead he found himself descending, past his office, down the worn steps to the crypt, unlocking the inner door with the key on the chain under his shirt, and standing in the cold among his private shelves with the lantern lit, looking at the three boxes that contained everything he had never told anyone.

The oldest box was at the bottom. He hadn't opened it in four years. Its label, in his own careful hand, read:

**REYES, ANSELM J. — d. 14 March. Ruled accidental.**

Seven years ago next month. A judge. A fall from the bell tower. A verdict everyone accepted because accepting it was easier than the alternative.

Yara set the box on the table and stood with his hand flat on the lid, listening to the cathedral breathe above him, feeling for the first time in eleven years like a man whose margins had been invaded — like a footnote promoted, violently, to the main text.

Somewhere out in the dark, a killer was walking free in the knowledge that Yara Okafor existed. And the only answer Yara had — the only weapon, the only shield, the only way back to invisibility — was buried in this box, in a case the whole city had sworn was closed.

He lifted the lid.
