## 7. Trial Date Set

The memo came down at 8:47 on a Tuesday morning, and Ilse Brandt read it twice before she let herself feel anything about it.

*In the matter of the Crown v. Okafor, Yara: trial date is hereby set for the sixteenth of November, Division Four, Judge Halloran presiding. The prosecution will be prepared to proceed.*

Six weeks. She turned the page over as if the back might say something different. It didn't. It just had a routing stamp and the signature of an assistant prosecutor named Duvall whom she'd met exactly once, at a retirement lunch, where he'd spent forty minutes explaining to her why juries distrusted men who were too quiet.

She set the memo down on her desk and looked out the window at the canal, gray-green in the October light, the dock where they'd pulled Tomas Lindqvist's body still cordoned off with tape that flapped in the wind like something trying to get free.

Six weeks was fast. Not impossibly fast — drowning cases with physical evidence could move that quickly when the docket cooperated — but this wasn't a case where the docket had cooperated. This was a case where someone upstairs had decided it *would* cooperate, and Brandt had been a detective long enough to know the difference between momentum and a push.

She picked up her phone, put it down, picked it up again.

"Brandt." Her captain's voice through the door before she'd even knocked. "You got the memo?"

"I got the memo."

"Come in."

Captain Roesch was a broad, careful man who kept his office like a museum of other people's decisions — framed commendations he hadn't earned, photographs of him shaking hands with officials whose names changed every election cycle. He was standing behind his desk with his reading glasses pushed up into his thinning hair, and he did not sit down, which meant this was going to be one of those conversations conducted at parade rest.

"You have concerns," he said.

It wasn't a question. He'd read her preliminary report, or at least the summary Duvall's office had let survive the trip upstairs.

"I have questions," Brandt said. "The clothes are ours — I'll give the prosecution that. The coat and scarf came from Okafor's closet, no question. Fibers match, the tailor confirmed the alterations, and there's no record of them being sold or donated. Somebody took them from his room and put them on a dead man. That part holds."

"And?"

"And everything else is air." She counted it off, because counting things off was how she kept herself honest. "No witnesses placing Okafor at the dock. No water in his lungs consistent with contact — we never even established he'd been near the canal that night. His timeline has a gap, yes, but a gap isn't evidence, it's an absence of evidence. The forensics on the body itself point away from him if anything — Lindqvist had defensive bruising on both forearms, and Okafor's file shows an old wrist injury; the medical examiner flagged it as *inconsistent with the struggle pattern*. I put that in my report. I don't know how much of it made it upstairs."

Roesch sat down then, slowly, the way a man sits when he's already decided how the conversation ends and is just arranging the furniture.

"The ME's flag is noted," he said. "Duvall's office has reviewed it."

"Reviewed it and what?"

"And concluded it doesn't preclude the theory of the case." He laced his fingers over his stomach. "Ilse. You found the clothes. You built the connection. Six weeks ago nobody in this building knew Yara Okafor existed, and now we've got a suspect with means, access, and a two-hour hole in his night. That's not nothing. That's a case."

"It's a *shape* of a case," she said. "Somebody drew it around him after the fact."

"Or he's exactly what he looks like. A quiet man with secrets who finally left a fingerprint on something." Roesch's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was worse than if he'd shouted. "Here's what you're not seeing, because you're too close to it. November sixteenth isn't arbitrary. That's the week of the Reyes anniversary proceedings. The estate, the inquest reopening, the whole civic apparatus — half the city's legal establishment is going to be in Division Four's orbit that month. The prosecutor wants this wrapped before it starts. A clean conviction in the drowning case, tidy narrative, no loose threads wandering into the anniversary and making everybody nervous."

"So it's political."

"It's a *calendar*," Roesch said. "Calendars exist whether you like them or not. Your job is the evidence. Their job is the schedule. Don't confuse the two."

Brandt looked at the window. The tape on the dock had come loose on one end and was streaming out over the water.

"Whose idea was the date?" she asked. "Specifically. Did it come from Duvall, or did it come from above Duvall?"

Roesch smiled without any warmth in it. "That's not a question you get answered."

"That's an answer," she said.

He let that sit for a moment. Then: "Is there anything in your investigation you haven't given the prosecution? Anything you're holding because you think it points somewhere else?"

And here was the moment. She felt it open under her like a trapdoor. Because there *was* something — several somethings. The way Okafor had sat across from her in that interview room, offering nothing, absolutely nothing, with the composure of a man who had rehearsed silence for years. The private files she suspected he kept, the ones he'd all but admitted existed by refusing so cleanly to deny them. And the thing she hadn't written down anywhere, the thing she'd told no one: that when she'd shown him the photograph of Lindqvist's face, his reaction hadn't been guilt or fear or performance. It had been *recognition*. Brief, buried, but real — the look of a man seeing a name from an old list.

"No," she said. "Nothing I'm holding."

Roesch nodded, satisfied, and dismissed her with a tilt of his chin toward the door. "Six weeks, Ilse. Make them count."

* * *

She didn't go back to her desk. She went down to records and pulled the Lindqvist file, and then, on an impulse she didn't examine too closely, she pulled the Reyes file too — the seven-year-old closed case, the judge who fell from the bell tower, ruled accidental by a coroner's court that had taken eleven days to reach a verdict everyone involved seemed relieved to reach.

She spread them side by side on a table in the back of the records room, under the buzzing fluorescent tube that flickered whenever the elevator ran.

Lindqvist. Former clerk of the Supreme Court, seconded to the Reyes chambers in the last year of the judge's life. Resigned abruptly six months after the fall. Drifted through three jobs, developed a drinking problem, ended up living in a rooming house near the canal. No family close enough to claim the body quickly. A small man who had gotten smaller.

Reyes. Anselm Reyes, sixty-one, appellate judge, fell from the cathedral bell tower on the night of March third. Found at dawn by the sexton. Coroner's verdict: accidental, misjudged footing, railing possibly degraded. Case closed in under two weeks.

Two files, seven years apart, connected by one dead man who used to work for the other dead man, and by a coat stolen from an archivist's closet.

Brandt sat with that for a long time.

Here was what bothered her, and she made herself say it plainly in her own head, because plainness was the only discipline she trusted: if Yara Okafor had killed Lindqvist, then the smartest thing he could have done — the *only* smart thing — was to make the death look like nothing at all. An accident, a heart attack, a drunk falling into the canal. Instead, someone dressed the corpse in Okafor's own clothing and dumped it twenty meters from the cathedral's front steps, in full view, on the morning of a restoration gala. That wasn't a killer covering himself. That was someone *pointing* — loudly, deliberately, with a flair for theater that fit none of the thin, dry, careful archivist she'd interviewed.

Unless Okafor was clever enough to murder a man and frame himself on purpose, betting that the frame would be so obvious it would collapse. Which was a bet only a genius or a madman would place, and Brandt had met both, and Okafor was neither. He was something rarer. He was a man who knew things.

And men who knew things made other men nervous.

She thought about the trial date again. Six weeks. Before the anniversary proceedings. *A clean conviction, tidy narrative, no loose threads.* She'd heard Roesch say it as scheduling logistics, but now, sitting between two dead men's files, she heard it differently. If Okafor went to trial and was convicted in early November, then whatever Okafor knew would be sealed behind a guilty verdict — discredited, filed, finished. Nobody would listen to a convicted murderer's theories about a judge's death. The anniversary proceedings would pass clean. The benefactors would smile for the cameras. The cathedral would ring its bells.

Or — the other possibility, the one she liked less — Okafor really was guilty, and the timing was pure coincidence, and she was constructing conspiracies out of calendar entries because the alternative was admitting the case was thin and she'd helped build it.

She closed both files and carried them back to the shelf, and on the way out she stopped at the duty desk.

"Has anyone requested the Reyes file recently?" she asked the clerk, a young woman named Pfeiffer who tracked access logs the way other people tracked celebrity gossip.

Pfeiffer scrolled. "You, just now. And—" she squinted— "huh. Three weeks ago. One request, flagged as internal research."

"Who?"

"That's the odd part. The request came through the prosecutor's liaison office. Signed authorization, but the name field's blank. Just a routing number." Pfeiffer looked up. "Want me to chase the routing number?"

"Yes," Brandt said. "Quietly."

* * *

That evening she drove past St. Verena's without stopping, which was a thing she'd started doing and couldn't explain to herself. The cathedral stood against the darkening sky like a ship beached in the middle of the city, its scaffolding wrapped in sheeting for the restoration, floodlights turning the stone the color of old bone. Through the open doors she could see candles, the tail end of evening Mass, a handful of figures bent in the blue gloom.

One of them, she was nearly sure, was a tall thin man moving along the side aisle with the unhurried gait of someone checking locks.

She didn't stop. Stopping would mean telling him about the trial date, and she wasn't ready to give him that — partly because it was procedure to let counsel deliver it, and partly, though she hated admitting this even to herself, because she wanted to see what he did with the information. Whether the news reached him through a lawyer's mouth or through the cathedral grapevine. Whether he panicked or planned.

But as she idled at the corner, waiting for a light that had already gone red for the cross traffic, her phone buzzed. A message from Pfeiffer, sent earlier and buried under two work calls:

*Routing number traced. Request originated from the office of Asst. Prosecutor Duvall. Authorization signed — initials T.V. No T.V. on staff. Checking further.*

T.V.

Brandt stared at the letters while the light changed twice behind her and a taxi honked and swung wide around her car. Two initials, meaningless, except that she'd spent the afternoon reading seven-year-old documents, and somewhere in the Reyes estate paperwork — she was almost certain, almost — she'd seen those same two initials on a conservator's certification, some technical annex about the tower railings.

The light changed a third time. Behind her, the taxi gave up and went around.

Someone inside the prosecution's orbit had pulled the Reyes file three weeks before Lindqvist's body washed ashore wearing a dead archivist's coat. Someone with initials, with access, with a reason to know what a quiet man in a crypt might have been quietly collecting for years.

Brandt put the phone facedown on the passenger seat and drove home the long way, past the canal, past the dock, past the tape still flapping in the dark like something trying to tell her something.

Six weeks, Roesch had said. Make them count.

She intended to. Just not, she was beginning to suspect, in the direction anyone upstairs had in mind.
