## 14. The Second Body

The call came at 5:47 in the morning, and Ilse Brandt knew before she answered it.

Not because of the hour — homicide didn't keep business hours, and neither did she — but because of the voice on the other end. Officer Renk had been on the job eleven years and had never once called her before six unless something was wrong in a way that couldn't wait for the briefing room. His voice had that particular flatness cops developed when they were trying not to feel a thing yet.

"St. Verena's," he said. "Convent annex, third floor. A nun. Sister Agnes Weiss. Fell from her window."

"Fell."

"That's what it looks like."

Brandt sat up in the dark of her apartment, phone pressed to her ear, and looked at the case file open on her nightstand. Yara Okafor's name stared up at her from the top page. *Prime suspect*, someone had written in the margin, and beneath it, in her own handwriting from three days ago: *Doesn't fit.*

"Nobody touch anything," she said. "I'm twenty minutes out."

She dressed in the dark, which was its own kind of confession about how often she'd been doing this lately — dressing at five a.m., chasing a case that had started as a drowned man in borrowed clothes and had somehow become the strangest thing she'd worked in fifteen years. She poured coffee she wouldn't drink and left it cooling on the counter like an apology.

Outside, the city was that gray color that comes just before dawn, when the streetlights are still on but ashamed of it. St. Verena's rose against the sky at the end of Kessler Street, all spires and buttresses, seven hundred years of stone pretending it would stand forever. She'd grown up four blocks from it. Her mother had lit candles there after her father left, one every Sunday for two years, until the candles became a habit and then a memory and then nothing at all. Brandt hadn't been inside since she was nineteen.

Now she was going back as a cop, with a body waiting.

* * *

Sister Agnes Weiss lay in the narrow garden between the convent annex and the cathedral's north wall, and even from the top of the fire escape Renk had rigged, Brandt could see what was wrong with the picture.

She descended the stairs slowly, cataloguing as she went. The nun was small — maybe eighty pounds of her, ninety at most — and she lay on her back among the herb beds, arms at her sides, almost neat. One slipper had come off. Her habit was undisturbed. Her face, turned slightly toward the wall, wore an expression Brandt could only describe as surprised, though whether by the fall or by something else, no expression could say.

"Window's the third one up," Renk said, pointing. "Latched from the inside, mostly. We think she leaned out too far — maybe watering the box? There's a planter."

"There's a planter," Brandt repeated.

It was empty. Bone-dry soil, cracked, no plants in it, no watering can anywhere on the sill or the floor below the window. She stood in the little room — a cell, literally, a bed, a desk, a crucifix, a shelf of books — and looked at the window latch. Old iron, painted over so many times the mechanism barely moved. She tried it. It took both hands and real force to open, and when it opened, it opened only halfway, jamming at some internal corrosion, leaving a gap of perhaps nine inches.

Nine inches. Sister Agnes had been, according to the mother superior, five feet one inch tall and frail as communion wafer.

"You tried this?" Brandt asked Renk.

He nodded. "Had to jimmy it. Thought it was painted shut."

"So did I." She stepped back from the window and looked down at the garden, doing the geometry. The drop was thirty feet. The wall below the window was sheer. For Agnes to have fallen, she would have had to climb onto the desk, force the corroded latch, wedge herself through a nine-inch gap, and then lose her balance watering an empty planter with a watering can that did not exist.

Or someone had helped her through that gap, from behind, with hands that were strong and patient and unhurried.

Below, the scene techs were moving around the body with the careful choreography of people who'd done this too many times. Brandt watched them and felt the old cold settle into her chest, the one that arrived whenever a scene was too tidy. Accidents were messy. Accidents involved chaos, half-measures, things knocked over and left where physics put them. This garden was arranged. The body lay where a story wanted it to lie.

Same as the canal dock. Same as the drowned man in the borrowed coat.

"Get me everything from her room," she said. "Books, papers, correspondence. And find out who else has a key to this wing."

* * *

By eight, she had the beginnings of an answer, and it was worse than she'd expected.

The convent annex kept a spare key in the sacristy office — one key, hanging on a board with forty others, unlogged, unmonitored, available to any member of cathedral staff who walked past. Which meant everyone. Which meant no one. The dean, Father Emmerich, confirmed it with the weary honesty of a man who had stopped defending his institution's sloppiness years ago.

"We are a church, Detective, not a bank," he said, standing in the sacristy doorway with his hands folded. He was tall, silver-haired, with the kind of face that made you want to confess things. "Half the diocese passes through these halls. If you're asking whether we can account for every key, the answer is no, and I'm sorry for it."

"I'm not asking about keys, Father. I'm asking about Sister Agnes. When did anyone last see her?"

"Yesterday evening, at compline. Seven o'clock. She sang — she always sang, even when her voice gave out on the high notes. Then she went up, as she always did." Emmerich's composure flickered, just once, around the eyes. "She was the oldest of us. Ninety-one. She'd been here sixty-three years."

"Did she have enemies?"

The question landed the way it always landed on clergy — with genuine bafflement, followed by a longer pause than the bafflement warranted. "Enemies. No. But she remembered things, Detective. That was her gift and her burden. Sixty-three years of remembering."

"What kinds of things?"

"The kind the rest of us are permitted to forget." He said it gently, almost kindly, and then he closed the subject like a door. "I'll pray for her. I'll pray for whoever did this, if someone did. Will that be all?"

It wasn't all — Brandt had a dozen more questions, starting with Judge Anselm Reyes and ending somewhere dark — but she recognized a wall when she leaned against one. She thanked him and walked out into the cloister, where morning light was coming through the arcade in long gold bars, and found herself standing in front of a memorial plaque she hadn't noticed on the way in.

*In memoriam: The Hon. Anselm Reyes, 1949–2018. Judge, benefactor, friend of St. Verena's.*

Seven years ago. The case everyone swore was closed. The case her prime suspect had pulled files on within days of being arrested for a drowning that made no sense.

And now the woman who remembered things had fallen from a window that barely opened.

* * *

At ten, the lab called with the preliminary findings, and Brandt listened with the phone wedged against her shoulder while she read Okafor's file for the fourth time.

Bruising on both upper arms, consistent with restraint. Petechial hemorrhaging suggesting pressure applied to the neck prior to death — post-mortem or peri-mortem, pending autopsy. No defensive wounds, which meant either she never saw it coming or she knew the person who came. Trace fibers on the window sill, gray wool, currently unidentified.

Gray wool. Brandt looked across her desk at the evidence photo from the canal: Yara Okafor's missing coat, dark charcoal wool, draped over the drowned clerk like a signature.

"It fits him," said Captain Dornau, who had appeared in her doorway without knocking, the way he did when politics were involved. He was holding the morning brief like a shield. "Wool fibers matching his coat. He had access — he works there, he knows every corridor, every key board, every blind spot. He interviewed with her twice last week; the mother superior confirms it. And he's already charged in one death."

"He's charged in a death he didn't commit," Brandt said.

Dornau set the brief down on her desk with exaggerated care. "Ilse."

"Captain."

"You've got a soft spot. I've seen it. The quiet little archivist with the sad eyes and the filing system. Fine. Be soft on your own time. On my time, you follow the evidence, and the evidence is walking toward him in a straight line."

"The evidence is being *arranged* toward him in a straight line." She stood, because sitting made this feel like a subordinate conversation, and she was past caring whether he noticed. "Two staged scenes, Captain. Identical methodology. Same theatrical instinct — the costume, the window, the story each scene wants to tell. Do you know what that means? It means our killer is building a narrative. Frame jobs aren't panic. They're authorship. And Yara Okafor, whatever else he is, spent seven years writing private reports nobody ever read. He doesn't perform. He hides. The man in that cell—" she tapped the file, "—has spent his whole life making sure no one ever looks at him. He is physically incapable of staging a crime scene designed to attract attention. It's not in his grammar."

Dornau studied her for a long moment. Outside his office window — no, outside hers; he'd migrated into her territory, which was itself a message — the bells of St. Verena's began to ring for mid-morning mass, and she watched the sound move through him like weather.

"You've been to the tower file," he said quietly. "Reyes."

"I've read it."

"Then you know what reopening that inquest does. You know whose names are in the margins of that restoration fund. The trial date is set, Ilse. Six weeks. The anniversary proceedings, the estate, the press — the Commissioner wants this clean and closed before any of it starts. A dead nun makes that harder. A second dead nun makes it impossible. So we close it fast, and we close it on the man the evidence points to, and we let the courts sort out the poetry later."

"And if the courts hang an innocent man because we were in a hurry?"

Dornau picked up his brief. "Then the courts will have company. Half this city's been hanging innocents for years — ask your archivist; apparently he keeps a list." At the door he paused, without turning. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I don't think he did it either. But thinking isn't my job anymore. It stopped being my job around the same time it stopped being enough."

He left. Brandt stood alone with the bell-sound still fading and understood that she had just been given permission to doubt, privately, forever, and forbidden to act on it, publicly, at all.

She had crossed lines before. Small ones. A report softened here, a timeline rounded there. Every detective had a drawer full of those. But she looked down at Okafor's file — at the photograph of the thin, stooped man who catalogued dead people's names for a living and solved their murders as a hobby and told no one, ever, because telling someone would mean being seen — and she understood that the line in front of her now was not small.

* * *

She went back to the convent annex at dusk, alone, without logging the visit.

The room had been released by the techs; the mother superior had asked, in a voice like wet paper, whether she might begin putting Agnes's things in order. Brandt had asked for an hour first. The woman had agreed and withdrawn, and now Brandt stood in the dead nun's cell with the window open behind her and the city lights coming on beyond the wall, and she searched.

Not for evidence — the techs had taken the evidence. She searched for the thing Agnes would have wanted found, because Brandt had spent two days reading Yara Okafor's private notes, photocopied from his seized files, and she had learned his grammar: the way he thought about people who kept secrets, the way he believed secrets were always, somewhere, addressed to someone. *Every concealment is a letter,* he'd written once, in a marginal note that had kept her up half the night. *The question is only who it's addressed to, and whether they'll live long enough to read it.*

Agnes had remembered things. Agnes had talked to Okafor twice. Agnes had fallen from a window two days later.

Which meant Agnes had known she might fall, and nuns who survived sixty-three years inside an institution learned to write letters.

Brandt went through the shelf of books. Breviary, catechism, a biography of the cathedral's patron saint, a novel with a broken spine. Hymnals — three of them, and the third one was wrong. Not wrong in any way she could name at first; wrong the way a staged crime scene is wrong. Too clean. Too centered on the shelf. The other two leaned against it like bookends.

She opened it. Hymn 214, the margins dense with Agnes's tiny, upright hand — but not musical annotations. Numbers. Dates. And beside hymn 214, underlined twice, a phrase that meant nothing to Brandt and therefore meant everything:

*"The truth shall be restored to its proper place."*

Verdict language. She'd know it anywhere — the formal cadence of a judgment read aloud. Reyes's judgment. She copied the phrase and the numbers into her notebook with hands that were not quite steady, closed the hymnal, and put it back exactly as she'd found it, between its bookends, facing out.

Then she stood a moment longer in the cold little room, looking at the window with its corroded latch, and let herself feel it — the grief, the urgency, the anger. An old woman who sang off-key at compline had held a piece of the truth for seven years and had died passing it on, and the machinery of the state was going to grind up a gentle man for it unless someone broke the machine.

Brandt clicked off the lamp, let herself out, and stood in the dark cloister breathing the smell of stone and incense.

Six weeks to trial. A killer inside the walls. A coded letter in a hymnal, addressed — she was suddenly certain — not to her, but to the man in cell four of the county detention block.

She needed to talk to Yara Okafor. Off the record. Tonight.

And she needed to decide, before she did, which side of the line she was standing on.
