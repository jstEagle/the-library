## 16. The Man Who Signed In

The ledger didn't want to give up its secret. It wanted to be a guest book — that was all. A leather-bound register with gilt corners and the cathedral's crest stamped in fading gold, sitting behind glass in the archive's locked case like it had never done anything wrong in its life.

I'd been staring at it for three hours.

The gala ledger was a ritual object as much as a record. Every benefactor who passed through the west doors on restoration night signed their name beneath the previous signer's, a cascade of ink going back decades — bishops, judges, developers, the occasional minor royal slumming it among the saints. The night of the gala two months ago, forty-one names were entered between six o'clock and midnight.

Forty-one names. But I'd counted the coats in the cloakroom log twice now, cross-referenced against the catering invoices, against the valet tickets, against the security camera stills Brandt had grudgingly let me see through the one-way mirror of her suspicion. Forty-one signatures. Thirty-nine guests.

Two people had signed in who never walked out into that candlelit nave. One of them, presumably, was my drowned man — the former court clerk whose name I still couldn't say without tasting river water: Elias Wren. He'd been there. He'd signed the book in his small, careful clerk's hand, the same hand that had transcribed seven years of Judge Anselm Reyes's rulings.

And the other —

I turned back to the page. The signature sat there in confident, flowing script: *T. Vey.*

Tomas Vey. The cathedral's conservator. The man who had spent eleven years keeping the fabric of St. Verena's from falling apart — the stone, the glass, the gilding, the bones of the building itself. Everyone loved Tomas. He had the gentle competence of a man who fixed things, who moved through the crypts and scaffolds and sacristies with a lantern and a soft word for the masons. When the bell tower railing needed repair after Reyes fell, it was Tomas who restored it. When the judge's estate papers needed cataloguing for the diocesan archive, it was Tomas who handled them.

When my office was searched three weeks ago and my private files vanished, only three people knew where they were kept.

I had spent those weeks telling myself the third person was impossible. That Tomas Vey, who had held my ladder on the scaffolding when I nearly slipped cataloguing the triforium, who brought coffee to the night watchmen, who once spent an entire afternoon helping me decipher a water-damaged burial register — that Tomas could not be the shape moving through the margins of my life, taking my coat, drowning a clerk, killing Sister Agnes.

But the signature was right there. And the more I looked at it, the more it refused to behave like the others.

* * *

Forgery is a strange art. The amateur thinks it's about copying. The professional knows it's about rhythm. Every hand has a music to it — the way the pen accelerates through certain letters, hesitates before others, where the loops close and where they drift open. You can fake the shape of a name. You cannot easily fake its tempo.

I am not a handwriting expert. I am an archivist, which means I have spent twenty years reading dead people's hands until their rhythms became familiar as voices. I knew Elias Wren's hand from his court transcripts — the clerk's discipline, every letter standing at attention. I knew Anselm Reyes's hand from his signed verdicts, heavy and impatient, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the page.

And I knew Tomas Vey's hand from eleven years of conservation reports filed in this very archive. Condition assessments. Treatment proposals. Invoices for lime mortar and bronze fittings. Hundreds of pages in that same flowing script now sitting in the gala ledger.

I pulled the conservation file at nine o'clock that morning. By noon I had spread forty pages across my desk under the good lamp, and I did what I always do with documents: I listened.

Vey's authentic hand had a particular habit. His lowercase *e* — look at enough of them and you start seeing it — carried a slight backward lean, a hesitation in the eye of the loop, as if the letter were glancing over its shoulder. It appeared in *Verena*, in *restoration*, in *necessary*, dozens of times across hundreds of pages, always the same glance, always the same shoulder.

The gala signature had no such glance. Its *e*s stood perfectly upright, perfectly confident, perfectly round. Whoever wrote *T. Vey* in the ledger that night had studied the shape of the name but not its soul. They had copied the calligraphy from a treatment report — probably one of the framed ones hanging in the conservator's own office, where any staff member with access could study it.

Except.

I set down my magnifier and pressed my palms flat against the desk, because here was the thought that made the room tilt: if someone was forging Vey's signature, then either Vey was a victim of forgery — or Vey was the forger, signing himself in under a hand slightly too perfect, the way guilty men overcorrect.

And there was a third possibility, the one that had been circling me since Agnes died, since her note surfaced in the hymnal with its coded date and its phrase from the Reyes verdict: that the signature was forged *by* Vey to establish an alibi he shouldn't need, and the slight imperfection was deliberate — a flaw planted so that anyone who examined it would conclude, as I almost had, that someone else had faked it.

That was the kind of mind I was dealing with. A mind that restored railings and altered wills and understood that the best disguise for a lie is a well-made truth with one visible crack.

I sat with that for a long time while the light moved across the floor and the cathedral above me went about its business — tourists, rehearsals, the vacuum cleaner droning through the nave like some patient sea creature. Then I got up, locked the ledger back in its case, and went to find out everything the archive knew about Tomas Vey.

* * *

What the archive knew was considerable, because Vey was meticulous. Eleven years of reports, each one a small masterpiece of professional prose. And buried inside them, the pattern I should have seen years ago, would have seen if I hadn't been so carefully not-looking at the Reyes file:

Tomas Vey had been the last conservator to work on the bell tower before Judge Reyes fell.

The timeline assembled itself with the terrible smoothness of something already built, waiting only to be noticed. Seven years ago, in the spring, Vey submitted a condition report on the tower's exterior gallery — the iron railing corroded, he wrote, several stanchions compromised, repair recommended within the fiscal year. The diocese approved it. Vey scheduled the work.

Judge Reyes died in October of that year. Fell from the gallery, the coroner said, leaning over an unstable railing to look at the view; the railing's deterioration was noted in the finding, almost apologetically, as though the building itself bore some share of the blame.

One week after the death, Vey filed a completion report. Railing repaired. New mortar, new stanchions, galvanized fittings. The work, he noted, had been accelerated in light of the accident.

Accelerated. After. Not before.

I had stood in that tower three weeks ago and found the fresh mortar myself, dated by its chemistry and its tooling to the week after Reyes fell. At the time I read it as evidence of staging — a railing weakened, a judge lured, a fall arranged, and then a hasty repair to destroy the proof. Now I saw the other layer: Vey hadn't just repaired the railing. He had *documented* the repair, created a paper trail establishing that the railing was sound *after* the death and deteriorated *before* it, so that any later investigation would find exactly what the coroner found — an old man, an old railing, a tragic accident. The conservation report wasn't a record. It was an alibi, cast in the most trustworthy material known to institutions: paperwork.

And the estate papers. I pulled them next, the Reyes estate catalogue Vey had prepared for the diocesan archive — and felt the cold move up my arms. Because here was the document trail I'd reconstructed in the crypt records, the altered will, the embezzlement Reyes had discovered in the restoration fund days before he died. Every page of that estate file had passed through Tomas Vey's hands. He had catalogued it, boxed it, indexed it. He had been the sole custodian of the dead judge's final intentions, and no one — not the diocese, not the courts, not the archivists who came after — had ever thought to check his work.

Why would they? Tomas Vey fixed things. Tomas Vey was the man you trusted with the building's bones.

* * *

I don't know how long I stood in the stacks holding that estate file before I realized my hands were shaking.

Not fear, exactly. Or not only fear. It was the specific tremor of a worldview failing — the sound a wall makes just before it decides to come down. I had built my whole strange life inside these walls on a single premise: that the cathedral was the safe place, the honest place, the one institution in the city that kept its promises in stone. I had solved my quiet cases in its margins believing that whatever rot existed in the city outside, here the archives told the truth.

But the rot had learned to write conservation reports. The rot had a lantern and a soft word for the masons and eleven years of trust banked like capital. The rot had signed itself into a gala under a borrowed hand and drowned a court clerk in the canal and pushed an old nun through her own window, and every step of the way it had used the cathedral's credibility as its weapon.

Elias Wren. I made myself think about him properly, finally — not as a body in my coat but as a man. A court clerk who had worked the Reyes trial, who must have known something about the estate, about the fund, about the numbers that didn't add up. Who had come to the gala — why? To talk to someone? To confront? To sell what he knew? And who had ended up in the canal wearing my clothes, which meant someone had planned his death around making me wear it. Someone who knew my closet, my habits, my invisibility — and understood that the perfect frame for an invisible man is to make him suddenly, catastrophically visible.

Only a handful of people knew me that well. Staff. Inner circle. The people who moved through the back corridors the way I did.

The list had been short for weeks. Now it was one name long.

* * *

There was one more thing, and I found it the way you find the last piece of anything — accidentally, tiredly, reaching for something else.

Vey's earliest file. His hiring record, eleven years back. I'd skipped it before because hiring records are bureaucratic sediment, but tonight I opened it, and there was his application, his references, his portfolio — and a line in the diocesan appointment letter that stopped my breath.

*Tomas Vey is recommended for the position of Conservator by the Office of the Hon. A. Reyes, in recognition of his exemplary restoration work on the courthouse annex.*

Anselm Reyes had hired him. Had vouched for him. Had brought him inside these walls with a judge's signature and a judge's confidence — and then, seven years later, had discovered what the restoration fund was hiding, and had died falling from a railing that Tomas Vey repaired one week too late.

Or one week exactly on time.

I closed the file. The lamp hummed. Somewhere far above, the bells began the hour, and the vibration came down through the stone into the soles of my feet like the building clearing its throat.

It fit. All of it fit — the forged signature, the tower, the will, the drowned clerk, Agnes's window, my stolen coat. Eleven years of access, of trust, of being the harmless man with the lantern. Vey had done to the cathedral what I had done to the city, only inverted: I had hidden in plain sight to find the truth, and he had hidden in plain sight to bury it. We were the same animal. That was the thought I couldn't shake, walking the long dark corridor back toward my office with the files locked away behind me. He was what I might have been, if my gift had pointed the other way.

And he knew where I kept my files. He had stood in my office. He had touched my things.

Which meant he knew how close I was. Which meant the next move was his, unless I made it mine first.

I reached my door and stopped.

The light under it was wrong. My office sat dark when I left it — I'm an archivist; I notice lamps — and now a thin gold line lay across the corridor stones, steady, patient, waiting.

Someone was in my office. And there was only one reason Tomas Vey — because I was past doubting now, past the decency of alternatives — would come to the archive at this hour, with the bells striking and the corridors empty.

He wasn't looking for the will anymore.

He was looking for me.
