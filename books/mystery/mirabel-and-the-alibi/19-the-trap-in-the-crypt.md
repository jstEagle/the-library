## 19. The Trap in the Crypt

The lie had to be small enough to be believed and heavy enough to be stolen.

That was the problem with bait: it had to look like something a man would kill for twice. I spent two days on the wording alone — not the words themselves, but the shape of the carelessness around them. A secret doesn't travel as gossip. It travels as a mistake.

So on Tuesday morning I made my mistake.

I left the crypt door unlocked. Not open — unlocked, which was worse, which was an invitation dressed as an oversight. Anyone who knew me would know that Yara Okafor did not forget locks; therefore anyone who found the door unlocked would understand that something inside mattered enough to be guarded badly.

Inside, I rearranged. I pulled the Reyes estate box from its shelf in the D-section and set it on the reading table, lid off, folders fanned out like a hand of cards someone had walked away from mid-game. On top of the stack I placed a single sheet — a photocopy of a probate inventory, 2018, Judge Anselm Reyes, with one line highlighted in yellow:

*Original instrument of last will and testament — held pending authentication.*

It was true, technically. That was the beauty of it. The original will *did* survive; I'd found it three days earlier, misfiled under a conservator's accession number that should never have touched estate papers at all. Tomas Vey's own numbering system, his own initials in the margin of the intake log. He had handled the will after Reyes died. He had filed it where no one would ever look — or he had meant to destroy it and never gotten the chance, and now it sat in my hands like a live coal.

I didn't read it in the crypt. I read it in the archive office with the blinds down, and when I finished I sat for a long time listening to the radiator tick, because what the will said was simple and enormous: Anselm Reyes had known. Days before he fell from the bell tower, the judge had amended his testament — not the distribution, nothing so crude — but a codicil, four paragraphs long, directing that certain documents in his possession be delivered to the state attorney general upon his death. Documents concerning the restoration fund. Documents concerning signatures.

Vey hadn't just killed him. Vey had buried the proof that killing him was necessary.

And the codicil named no executor. It named the cathedral itself — *delivery through the office of the archivist.* Seven years ago there had been no archivist worth the name. There had been a part-time volunteer who alphabetized hymnals. The instruction had gone into the dark like a coin into a well.

Until me.

I put the photocopied inventory on top of the folder stack, turned off the lights, locked the crypt properly this time — loudly, so the lock's complaint carried down the corridor — and went home to wait.

* * *

Waiting is a discipline. People think stakeouts are about patience, but patience is only the surface tension. Underneath, waiting is fear wearing a calm coat.

I waited badly for the first night. I sat in my flat above the sacristy with the lights off and rehearsed every version of what might happen, and each rehearsal ended with me dead in some new inventive way, because that is what imagination does when you feed it terror at midnight. By two a.m. I had catalogued seventeen deaths and one arrest, and none of them were useful, so I got up and made tea and did what I always do with fear: I organized it.

If Vey came, he would come quietly. He would come between the hours when the night porter made his rounds — I'd checked, the rounds ran at eleven and four, a ninety-minute window of institutional blindness that Vey, with twenty years in the building, would know better than anyone alive. He would bring tools, because the crypt's inner cabinet was locked and he'd assume I kept the will there. He would be careful. Careful men are slow men, and slow men can be watched.

He would also be angry when he understood what he'd walked into. That was the part I couldn't organize. That was the part I kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: Tomas Vey had drowned a man in a canal and smothered a nun in her sleep and dropped a judge off a tower, and all three times he had been alone with someone smaller than himself. I was planning to be the fourth person alone in a room with him. My whole plan rested on the assumption that being right would protect me.

Being right had never once protected anybody. It had only ever made them easier to find.

But there was no one to tell. That was the arithmetic I'd done a hundred times since Agnes died. Brandt believed me — I thought she believed me — but anything I told her officially became evidence, and evidence became disclosure, and disclosure became a defense attorney's motion and a sealed file and six months of procedure while Vey's friends in the benefactors' circle arranged things the way they'd arranged everything for seven years. Emmerich was bound by whatever Vey had said to him in the confessional — I could see it in the old man's face now, that terrible locked grief, a priest carrying a confession like a stone in his chest. And everyone else in the cathedral loved Tomas Vey. He restored their saints. He gilded their memories.

So it would be me and the tombs. It was almost fitting. I had spent ten years keeping company with the dead; it seemed right that they should stand witness when I finally asked one of them for justice.

* * *

Thursday night came down cold and clear, the kind of autumn night where the city's noise rises instead of sinks, every shout and siren bouncing off the low clouds. I entered the crypt at half past ten through the stair from the ambulatory, moving without light until my eyes adjusted to the emergency strip glowing green along the floor.

I had prepared the room the way I'd prepare any exhibit: everything positioned to tell a story. The Reyes box still open on the table. The false ledger — a decoy notebook I'd aged with tea and written up in a fair imitation of my own hand — tucked into the inner cabinet, visible through the mesh door, its spine labeled in block letters: *REYES — ORIGINAL WILL & CODICIL.* A chair drawn up at the reading table as if I'd been working late and stepped out. My coat hung on the hook by the door.

My actual coat was under the table, wrapped around my legs, along with my actual self.

It was not a comfortable place to wait. The space beneath the reading table was a knee-high crawl behind a panel of retired shelving, and I had measured it against my body the way you measure a coffin, because that comparison arrives whether you invite it or not. I sat on a folded blanket with my back against cold stone and my knees drawn up and a view, through a gap between shelf frames, of the whole lower gallery: the table, the cabinet, the door, the green line of the exit strip.

Above me, the bishops slept. Cardinal Alois Merk, d. 1961. Bishop Emmerich the elder, no relation, d. 1943. Names I had catalogued so many times I knew their chisel-cut letterforms by touch. I found their presence steadying, which is either piety or pathology, and I've stopped trying to determine which.

At eleven-fifteen the night porter's footsteps crossed overhead, unhurried, the squeak of his left shoe fading toward the west transept. Then silence, the specific layered silence of a cathedral at night — stone holding its breath, pipes ticking somewhere in the walls, the vast dark above the nave doing whatever vast darks do.

Then, at twelve minutes past midnight, the crypt door opened.

I heard it before I saw anything: the soft hydraulic sigh of the closer, cut short — someone catching the door before it sealed, easing it shut by hand. A careful man. My heart went from resting to hammering in perhaps two beats, and I pressed my palm flat against my sternum like a child, as if I could hold the organ quiet through sheer embarrassment.

Footsteps. Measured, even, the tread of a man who has walked these floors for decades and knows exactly which flagstones sing. He passed within four feet of my hiding place. Through the gap I saw him in silhouette against the green strip: medium height, broad-shouldered, the particular posture of a craftsman — weight settled, head level, hands loose and ready at his sides. He wore gloves. Of course he wore gloves.

Tomas Vey stood in the middle of my crypt and looked at the room the way I imagined he looked at damaged altarpieces: assessing, total, missing nothing.

He saw the open box. He saw the chair. He saw the coat on the hook.

He did not see me. Twenty years of habit told him the archivist worked days and lived above the sacristy, and the coat said *he'll be back,* and the unlocked door from Tuesday said *he's careless,* and people believe what the evidence tells them because the alternative is unbearable. I have built a life on that principle. Watching it work from inside a hiding place, aimed at me, was like watching myself in a distorting mirror.

Vey went first to the reading table. He lifted the highlighted inventory sheet, held it close to his face, and I heard him exhale through his nose — one sharp breath, satisfaction or contempt, impossible to tell. He set it down precisely where it had been. Then he moved to the inner cabinet, produced something from his jacket — a slim case, the whisper of metal on fabric — and went to work on the lock.

Here is what I had not fully planned: what I would do when he actually touched it. In my rehearsals I always stayed hidden until he had the decoy ledger in hand, then revealed myself with the door blocked and my phone already recording. Clean. Controlled. The fantasy version.

What happened instead was that I heard the lock give — a soft click that sounded, in that silence, like a bone breaking — and something in me refused to stay folded in the dark. Because the lock was mine. The cabinet was mine. Ten years of my life were in those shelves, and this man had killed my colleague and drowned a stranger in my coat and he was picking my lock with the ease of long practice, and I stood up.

Not gracefully. My knees had gone rigid and my foot was asleep, and I came up out of that crawl space like something exhumed, knocking the panel aside, grabbing the edge of the table for balance. The lamp I'd rigged — a plain desk lamp on a timer, meant to blind and disorient — clicked on directly into both our faces.

Vey didn't flinch.

That was the worst moment of the entire night, worse than what came after. He simply turned his head toward the light, eyes watering slightly, gloved hands still resting on the cabinet mesh, and looked at me with an expression of mild professional disappointment, as if I were a joint in a fresco that had failed inspection.

"Yara," he said. Quietly. Conversational. "You should have stayed under there."

"The will is real," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The codicil. Reyes directed it to the archivist's office. You missed it because seven years ago this office was a broom closet." I took one step sideways, putting the table between us, angling toward the corridor door. "It names the fund. It names the signatures. It's already copied, Tomas. Three copies, three places. Killing me makes it worse."

Something moved across his face then — not doubt. Calculation. I watched him do the arithmetic, watch him arrive at the answer I had feared he'd arrive at: that a man like me, a man with no family, no allies, no standing, a man already charged with murder, was not credible. That copies could be found. That dead men's claims could be dismissed. That the machine that had buried Anselm Reyes could bury me too, and had, in fact, already started.

"You're not a cop," he said. "You're not even a witness anymore. You're a suspect. Do you know what happens to a suspect's little ghost story?" He closed the cabinet door gently, reverently, the way he handled other people's treasures. "It becomes evidence of obsession. Your lawyer will weep."

"I don't need a lawyer. I need the inquest." I kept moving, another half-step, the corridor door now eight feet away. "Monday. Everything goes on the record. The mortar dates. The forged signature. The codicil. You can't forge your way past a sworn transcript, Tomas. There are too many pages."

For the first time, he stopped being gentle.

He came around the table fast — faster than a man his age should move, the speed of someone who has done violence before and no longer finds it strange — and his hand caught my shoulder and spun me, and my hip hit the corner of the reading table hard enough to white out my vision for a second. The lamp went over. The Reyes box slid, folders spilling across the flags. His other hand found my throat, not squeezing yet, just *there*, the way you hold a document you're deciding whether to keep.

"You were supposed to drown quietly," he said, and his voice had changed entirely, flattened into something with no craftsmanship in it at all. "You were supposed to be a sad little accident. Instead you get a trial, a detective, a *cause.*" His grip tightened. "Agnes wouldn't stop either. Sweet old woman, and she looked at me across the refectory like she could see straight through—"

He stopped. Realized what he'd said. And I watched — felt, through his fingers, literally felt — the exact moment Tomas Vey understood that he had just confessed to murder in front of a witness.

His face did something terrible. It went calm. Deeper calm than before, the calm of a man past decisions, and he shifted his grip from my throat to shove me backward, hard, into the shelving. The frame came apart the way old oak does — reluctantly, then all at once — and I went down under it, a shelf edge raking across my brow, and the world went red and ringing.

Through the blood in my eyes I saw him crouch, pick up the fallen lamp cord, and begin winding it around his fist with neat, economical turns.

"No copies," he said. "No codicil. No archivist. We go back to Tuesday, Yara. You never found anything. You were never down here."

He took one step toward me.

And the crypt door opened.

Not eased. Not caught. Thrown wide, with all its weight, and the sound it made against the stone wall was the loudest thing I had ever heard. Green light spilled down the steps, and in it stood Detective Ilse Brandt, sidearm drawn, badge held high, shouting words I barely processed — *police, drop it, hands* — and behind her, impossibly, Father Emmerich, ancient and upright, his voice cracking across the tombs: *"I heard him! God forgive me, I heard every word!"*

Vey froze. The lamp cord slipped from his fist.

Later — much later, in statements and corridors and one long conversation over terrible coffee — I would learn how it happened: that Emmerich had spent three days dying of his conscience, that he had finally gone to Brandt not with a confession but with a question, *if a man tells you something in the seal, may I act on what I know without saying how,* and that Brandt, who broke rules the way other people break bread, had said yes before he finished asking. That she had put a wire on the dean and staked the cathedral for two nights, and tonight, hearing Vey's voice rise, she had run out of patience forty seconds sooner than wisdom advised.

Forty seconds. I lay under the broken shelving with my blood on the flagstones of Cardinal Merk's tomb and understood that I had lived or died by forty seconds, and began, very quietly, to laugh.

Brandt reached me first. She knelt, pressed her hand flat against my shoulder — the same gesture, I realized dimly, that I'd used on my own heart two nights ago — and said, "Stay down, Okafor. You're safe."

Above us, officers were pulling Tomas Vey's arms behind his back, and he was not resisting, and he was not speaking, except once, as they turned him toward the stairs. He looked down at me — at the blood, at the scattered folders, at the codicil lying face-up on the stone between us, Reyes's handwriting exposed to the light for the first time in seven years — and he said, softly, almost wistfully:

"It was a beautiful forgery, you know. The best work I ever did."

"That's why it unraveled," I told him. "You signed it too well."

They took him up into the dark. Brandt helped me sit up. And Emmerich, kneeling beside me on the cold floor with tears running freely down his old face, whispered the words I would carry into Monday's courtroom like a lit candle:

"He waived the seal, Yara. Last night, in writing. Everything he told me — *everything* — you can say it out loud now."

Monday. Two days. The whole buried truth, finally legal, finally speakable — if I lived long enough, and stayed free long enough, to say it.
