## 21. The Anniversary Trial

The courtroom had been a chapel once.

I knew this because I had catalogued it — one of the first entries in my first year at St. Verena's, when the diocese deconsecrated the old annex and sold it to the city for a courthouse. The stone arches were the same. The acoustics were the same. Seven hundred years of prayers had soaked into these walls, and now they carried instead the dry liturgy of objections and oaths. Somewhere beneath the judge's bench, I suspected, there was still a foundation stone with a cross cut into it. Institutions rarely buried their God. They just built over him.

I sat in the dock on the morning of the reopened inquest into the death of Judge Anselm Reyes, wearing a suit Detective Brandt had told me to buy because mine made me look "like a man with something to hide." She was not wrong. For seven years I had dressed to be overlooked. Now every eye in the room was on me, and I understood, finally, what my drowned man in borrowed clothes must have felt in his last minutes: visibility as a kind of drowning.

The gallery was full. Half the cathedral's benefactors' circle had come to watch me hang — Aldous Merrin of the restoration fund, Canon Prebendary Lutz, two members of the Reyes estate trustees, all of them in dark wool and darker expressions. Father Emmerich sat near the back, his hands folded, his face arranged into the particular stillness he wore when he was praying for something he wasn't sure God would grant.

And in the front row, directly across from the dock, sat Tomas Vey.

He looked wonderful. That was the thing about Vey that had taken me so long to see — he looked *wonderful*, always, the way a restored painting looks wonderful, every crack filled, every color deepened, until you forget you are no longer looking at the original. He wore mourning grey, as befitted a man attending an inquest into a death he had spent seven years calling an accident. He caught my eye and gave me a small, pitying smile, the smile of a craftsman watching his work hold.

The coroner, a tired woman named Dr. Halloran who had inherited this case like a cursed heirloom, called the proceedings to order.

"The inquest into the death of Anselm Reyes is reopened," she said, "on the petition of the Crown, following new evidence. Mr. Okafor, you are present both as a person of interest in related proceedings and as a witness. Do you understand that anything you say may be used against you?"

"Perfectly," I said. "That's rather the point."

A rustle went through the gallery. Halloran looked at me over her glasses and decided, wisely, to let it pass.

* * *

They put me on the stand first, which was the prosecution's mistake, though I suppose they can be forgiven for not knowing it. Crown Counsel Deverell was a careful man with a careful file, and his file said: Yara Okafor, archivist, suspect in the death of Sister Agnes Weiss, owner of clothing found on a drowned court clerk, keeper of private files on deaths he had no business investigating. His file did not say what I intended to give him.

"Mr. Okafor," Deverell began, "you'll confirm for the court that Judge Reyes died on the fourteenth of October, seven years ago, from injuries consistent with a fall from the cathedral bell tower."

"I'll confirm that's what the record says."

"You dispute it?"

"I'd like to walk through it," I said. "If the court will indulge an archivist. We're tedious people. We like to start at the beginning."

Halloran waved a hand. Go on.

"The beginning," I said, "is a will. Judge Reyes rewrote his will eleven days before he died. Everyone in this room has been operating for seven years on the version lodged with the estate — the version that left the Reyes land easement to the cathedral restoration fund, cleared the way for the easement vote, funded the tower restoration, and burnished the reputation of a certain conservator sufficiently to earn him a bishop's patronage and, eventually, an appointment recommendation." I let my gaze travel, briefly, to Merrin, to Lutz, to Vey. "That will is a forgery. Or rather — it is a forgery of a forgery. The original survives."

Deverell's careful composure flickered. "Where?"

"In the crypt of St. Verena's," I said, "filed under the wrong accession number, by design. Judge Reyes hid it himself. He knew someone inside the cathedral was embezzling the restoration fund — he'd found the discrepancies in the ledgers days before he died. He hid the true will where he thought only one person would ever look." I paused. "Me. Though he didn't know my name. He just knew the archivist reads everything."

"That's a remarkable story," Deverell said. "Do you have evidence, or only narrative?"

"I have the will itself, authenticated, delivered to this court yesterday under seal. I have the ledger discrepancies Reyes annotated in his own hand — margins dated the ninth of October. And I have mortar."

"Mortar."

"Mortar," I agreed, and I watched Tomas Vey's smile hold perfectly still, the way a restored painting holds still when you bring the lamp too close. "The bell tower railing was repaired the week after Reyes fell. Fresh mortar, applied by a professional, matched to the historical mix. A railing doesn't need repairing after a man falls *over* it. It needs repairing if a man was pushed against it — hard — or if it was loosened beforehand and had to be quietly fixed before anyone looked too closely. The repair order was signed by the conservator's office. Signed by Tomas Vey."

Vey's counsel was on his feet. "My client performed routine maintenance—"

"At two in the morning," I said, "six days after the death, on a railing that appears in no other maintenance log for the preceding nine years? Sister Agnes remembered the lights in the tower that night. She told me so before she died. She was frightened, and she asked me to stop digging, and I am sorry — truly sorry — that I didn't stop soon enough to keep her alive. But she also left me something. A note, coded, in the hymnal we both loved. It names the date of the tower repair. And it quotes a phrase from the Reyes verdict — a phrase only someone who had handled the case papers would know. Someone like the court clerk who drowned in the canal wearing my coat. Someone like the conservator who processed the estate."

The gallery had gone very quiet. Even the benefactors had stopped pretending to check their watches.

* * *

Deverell recovered enough to try to take it back. That was his job, and I respected him for doing it competently.

"This is all circumstantial," he said. "You are, I remind the court, a man facing charges of your own. You had access to the tower. You had access to Sister Agnes. Your fingerprints were found on her window latch."

"They were," I said. "Planted. I've never touched that latch — you'll find no prints of mine anywhere else in that room, which is itself the anomaly. Someone lifted them from something else I handle daily. My suggestion, Counselor, would be the crypt ledger. Page forty-one. Check the smudge pattern against the latch impression. I'd have done it myself, but apparently I'm not considered a reliable investigator these days."

Someone in the gallery laughed — one short bark, quickly strangled. It sounded like Brandt. She was standing at the back by the doors, arms crossed, watching me with the expression she wore when she was letting me dig the hole and waiting to see whether I climbed out of it or was buried in it.

"And the drowned man," Deverell pressed. "Elias Wren, former court clerk. Wearing your clothes. Explain that."

"Gladly. During the restoration gala — the night my clothes were taken — the guest ledger records a signature: *T. Vey, arriving 9:40 p.m.* Except Tomas Vey arrived at 7:15. I know because he gave a speech; the parish newsletter printed the time. The 9:40 signature is forged — the pen pressure differs, the terminal stroke on the *y* is wrong, and I have compared it against forty-one authentic Vey signatures in the conservation files. Why forge yourself into a party you attended? To create a window. While 'Vey' was supposedly still at the gala, the real Vey was upstairs in the staff wing, taking clothes from my closet — clothes that would later dress a dead man and point the police at the archivist nobody knew. Premeditation, Counselor. Written in ink."

I turned, then, deliberately, and looked at Tomas Vey for the first time since taking the stand.

"You killed the judge," I said, "because he found your thefts. You killed Elias Wren because he recognized the forged will — he'd filed the original, and when the anniversary proceedings were announced, he started asking questions. You killed Sister Agnes because she remembered the lights. And you tried to kill me last week in the crypt, and would have succeeded if the tombs of dead bishops weren't quite so crowded. Every death for seven years has been housekeeping. Yours is a very tidy mind, Mr. Vey. It's why the forgery was good. It's why it wasn't perfect. Nothing handmade ever is."

"My client—" Vey's counsel began.

"Actually," said a voice from the back of the room, "my client will speak for himself."

Every head turned. Father Emmerich was standing.

* * *

I had begged him, three nights ago, in the sacristy, with the candles burned down to stubs. I had told him everything — the trap in the crypt, how Vey had cornered me among the tombs, how I had escaped with a split lip and a bruised rib and the knowledge that Vey knew the original will survived. And Emmerich had gone white and said, in a voice I had never heard him use, *"He came to me. After that night. He came to me for confession."*

And I had said: *Then tell them.*

And he had said: *I cannot. The seal is absolute.* And then, after a long silence, in a whisper: *Unless he releases me. Unless he waives it.*

"He never will," I had said.

"No," Emmerich had agreed. "But arrogance is a sin, Yara. And sins have a way of arranging their own punishment."

Now, in the courtroom, Emmerich walked slowly down the aisle while the coroner demanded to know who he was and by what right he addressed the inquest. He produced a document from his cassock — a single sheet, witnessed, notarized, dated six days ago.

"A waiver of the seal of confession," he said, "signed by Tomas Vey, in my presence, before two witnesses. He believed, when he signed it, that it was a formality — a condition of a charitable bequest arrangement he was negotiating with the diocese. He did not read it carefully. He was, I think, accustomed to documents being what he made them." Emmerich's voice did not shake, though his hands did. "He confessed to me the murder of Judge Anselm Reyes. In detail. The loosened railing. The meeting in the tower. The fall. And afterward — the clerk, the sister, the archivist. He confessed because he believed no one could ever hear it. He waived the seal because he believed it would never matter."

He laid the paper on the coroner's bench.

"I have kept this man's secrets for six days," he said, "and I have kept this cathedral's secrets for thirty years, and I want the court to understand what it costs me to set them down here. St. Verena's sheltered me. It sheltered this young man when he had nowhere else. But a church that protects murderers to protect its own name is not a church. It is a tomb with better lighting." He turned and looked at Vey, and his voice broke at last, gently, like old mortar. "You should have read the document, Tomas. You always assumed no one checks the archives. You forgot that some of us were raised on exactly that assumption."

Vey stood up.

It was the only time in seven years I saw the restoration fail — the cracks showing through, the paint lifting. He looked around the courtroom as if searching for the flaw in the frame, the seam in the canvas, the place where he could insert a tool and pry the whole scene apart. There wasn't one. The will was real. The mortar was dated. The signature was forged. The confession was waived. And the archivist — the invisible man, the margin-dweller, the ghost in the stacks — had spent seven years learning to read exactly the kind of room Vey had built.

"Tomas Vey," Dr. Halloran said, "the court requires you to remain. Officers—"

He ran. Of course he ran. Men who restore things cannot bear to watch themselves decay. He made it four steps toward the doors before Brandt, who had been standing by those doors for precisely this contingency since eight that morning, stepped into his path with the unhurried calm of a woman who had hidden evidence from her own captain to make sure this moment stayed possible.

"Tomas Vey," she said, snapping the cuffs on, loud enough for the whole gallery, "I'm arresting you for the murders of Anselm Reyes, Elias Wren, and Sister Agnes Weiss. You do not have to say anything. Frankly, I'd prefer you didn't. You're terrible at it."

As they led him past the dock, Vey stopped. Just for a moment. He looked at me the way a forger looks at the original — hatred and awe in equal measure.

"How long?" he asked. "How long have you known?"

"Seven years," I said. "I just didn't know I knew. That's the trouble with archives, Mr. Vey. Everything in them is already true. It's only waiting for someone to open the drawer."

They took him out. The sound of the doors closing behind him echoed off the old chapel arches, and for a moment — just a moment — the courtroom sounded like the cathedral again.

Dr. Halloran adjourned until afternoon for formal findings. Nobody doubted them. As the gallery emptied, Merrin and Lutz slipped out the side door, already talking in low urgent voices about damage control, about the fund, about *surviving this* — the conspiracy's roots would take longer to pull than its flower. That fight was coming. But it would be fought in daylight now.

Brandt lingered by the dock. She looked at me sitting there in the suit she'd made me buy, and something almost like a smile crossed her face.

"Not bad," she said, "for a man with something to hide."

"I don't anymore," I said.

"We'll see," she said, and left.

Father Emmerich found me last. He sat down heavily beside the empty dock, an old man in a room that had finally stopped holding its breath.

"They'll ask you to stay on, you know," he said. "After today. The diocese, the police, the newspapers. Everyone will want the archivist who solved it. You could be invisible anywhere else in the world, Yara. Here, you never will again."

I looked up at the stone arches, at the seven hundred years of prayers soaked into the walls, and I thought about the crypt below us, quiet now, my files returned to their drawers, my solitude intact and yet somehow no longer sufficient.

"Yes," I said. "I know."

Outside, the bells of St. Verena's began to ring the hour — the same bells that had rung, unanswered, over a dying judge seven years ago. This time, everyone heard them.
