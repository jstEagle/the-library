## 1. Postmarked After the Grave

The letter arrived on a Thursday, which felt appropriate. Thursdays were the days I did the accessioning—new acquisitions logged, catalogued, shelved—and there is something about the ritual of recording things that makes the universe eager to hand you one more item you never asked for.

I found it in the archive's external post tray, wedged between a parish's request for nineteenth-century marriage certificates and a flyer for a roof-appeal concert. It was addressed to *The Archivist, St. Aldhelm's Cathedral* in a small, upright hand that had been written slowly, the way people write when they want to be legible to God. No return address. Postmark dated the day after Archdeacon Hollis's funeral.

I stood in the archive doorway holding it for longer than was dignified. The archive smells of vellum and cold stone and, faintly, of the lavender sachets Mrs. Petch keeps slipping into the map drawers despite my repeated instructions—a smell I have come to think of as the smell of other people's endings, since most of what crosses my desk arrives because somebody has died.

Hollis had been dead eleven days. The cathedral was still wearing its grief like an ill-fitting cassock: too long in the sleeve, catching on doorframes. The official account of his death was simple and everyone repeated it with the relieved fluency of people reciting something they've memorized so they won't have to think about it. He had gone down into the crypt after hours—unusual but not unheard-of; Hollis was forever chasing some genealogical fancy or other—had suffered a fall on the worn steps, and had not been found until morning, by which time the cold down there had done what cold does.

An accident. A sad accident. An accident with a funeral attached.

The verger, Tom Beddoe, was charged with nothing yet, but he was accused of everything already—the thefts from the vestry strongbox that Hollis had apparently confronted him about, the missing endowment papers, the general atmosphere of suspicion that settles on a man once the Dean has used the word *irregularities* in his hearing. His trial was set for six weeks hence. In the meantime he'd been suspended, and he came to the cathedral only at odd hours, moving through the cloisters like a ghost who hadn't been told the house was sold.

I am, by profession and temperament, a person who stays out of such weather. I have spent thirty years perfecting the art of being furniture. People tell archivists things they would never tell their priests—where the bodies are filed, so to speak—and the price of that confidence is that you must never appear to be listening. I am very good at not appearing to be listening. It is my single marketable skill.

So of course I opened the letter. Curiosity is the one vice I've never managed to file away.

*The Archivist,* it began.

*I write to you because you are the only person in this building who deals honestly with paper.*

That should have flattered me. Instead it made my neck prickle, because it meant someone had watched me long enough to form a theory about me, and theories about me are not something I encourage.

*Archdeacon Hollis did not fall. He was pushed, and I know it, and I cannot say more without endangering myself and others. He was close to something. Closer than anyone understood. If you want proof of what he found, look where he looked last. And understand this: before the trial ends, there will be another death unless the truth comes out first.*

There was no signature. No date beyond the postmark. The ink was blue-black, ordinary, bought anywhere. The paper was good quality—not expensive, but good; the kind of laid stock the cathedral office orders by the ream.

I read it four times. Then I did the thing I always do with documents that frighten me: I put on white cotton gloves, as though the danger were contamination rather than content, and read it a fifth time under the raking light I use for watermarks.

There was no watermark. Of course there wasn't. But the light did show me something better: the impression of a second sheet, pressed against this one while the ink was wet. Writing transfers, faintly, mirror-reversed, when two pages are stacked carelessly. Most of it was illegible—a smudge of intention—but along the bottom edge I could make out three words, backwards, in that same careful hand:

*burial — register — margins*

I sat down on my stool and looked at the long wall of shelves where the burial registers live, forty volumes spanning three centuries, each one a ledger of everyone St. Aldhelm's had ever committed to the ground. Hollis had spent the last year of his life down here two or three evenings a week. I had assumed he was indulging his hobby—tracing some obscure cousin, feeding his appetite for the cathedral's past. Everyone had assumed that. Hollis was the sort of man whose enthusiasms were so constant they became invisible, like the hum of the heating pipes.

He had been looking at burials. And whoever wrote to me knew it.

* * *

I told no one. This required no discipline; telling people is the part I find difficult.

But I began, that same afternoon, to do something I have never done in thirty years of service: I started reading the collection not as a custodian but as a detective. There is a difference. A custodian asks, *what is this?* A detective asks, *why is this here?*

I pulled the burial register covering the years Hollis had visited most often—I checked the sign-in book, another small betrayal of my own rules—and carried it to the reading table by the north window, where the light is honest.

At first glance it was exactly what it appeared: names, dates, plot numbers, in the crabbed hands of four successive incumbents. But Hollis had been here. I know his habits the way I know the habits of every researcher who uses this room—the way he cracked his knuckles before writing, the particular green of his fountain-pen ink, the way he made his marginal marks: tiny, precise ticks beside entries that interested him, so small that a casual reader would take them for ink flecks or worm damage.

There were dozens of them. More than a hobby would warrant.

And beside certain entries—perhaps one in twenty—he had written dates in the margin. Not burial dates. Baptism dates. Small, neat, and wrong, somehow, though I couldn't have said why at first. Then I could: because a baptism date ought to precede a birth by days or weeks, and several of these preceded the recorded births by months. Years, in one case. As though children had been baptized before they existed.

Or as though someone, long ago, had needed those baptisms to exist on paper, and had entered them where no one would ever think to check—because who audits the dead? Nobody reads burial registers for fun.

Except Hollis. Except, now, me.

I worked until the light went grey and the radiators clicked off, copying marginalia into a notebook I kept inside my cardigan pocket, feeling two things at once with uncomfortable intensity. The first was dread—the cold, specific kind, the kind that says *you are reading something someone killed to protect.* The second was worse, because I am sixty-one years old and thought I'd finished with it: a thrill. The reluctant, shameful thrill of purpose. For thirty years I have kept other people's secrets in order and called it a life. Now a secret had arrived on my desk with my name on the envelope, and beneath the fear, treacherously, I felt awake.

Before I left, I returned the register to its shelf and stood a moment in the dark aisle, listening to the building settle.

The letter had said *look where he looked last.* I had assumed that meant the registers. But Hollis had signed out of the archive on the night he died at 9:40 p.m., and the crypt steps are directly below us, and there is one volume he had requested twice that week that I had not yet examined—the oldest burial register, the one from the founding years, the one whose margins, I now remembered with a lurch, were said to contain notes in the founder's own hand.

Someone had watched Hollis. Someone was watching me. And somewhere in this building, folded into centuries of the dead, was a truth worth killing for—addressed, at last, to me.
