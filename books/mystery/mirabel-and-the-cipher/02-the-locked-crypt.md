## 2. The Locked Crypt

Canon Merrick Hale kept his office in the northwest tower, a room that smelled of beeswax and old radiators, and he received Ada the way he seemed to receive everything — with a warmth so practiced it had worn smooth as a stair rail.

"Ada Kavanagh." He rose from behind a desk buried under parish correspondence and extended both hands, as if greeting a returning pilgrim. "The archivist himself. Sit, sit. Tea? There's a kettle that predates both of us but it still boils."

"No tea, thank you, Canon." Ada took the chair opposite. He had rehearsed this on the walk over from the archive annex, three versions of the request, each one calibrated to sound like routine business rather than what it was: a man asking to be let into a room the church had spent fifty years pretending didn't exist.

Hale sat back down and folded his hands. Up close he was older than his photographs on the cathedral website suggested — mid-seventies, perhaps, with a face like a map that had been folded too many times along the same creases. His eyes were pale blue and very steady. Ada had catalogued enough death masks in his career to know what genuine calm looked like on a human face, and Hale wore something adjacent to it. Adjacent was the word that stuck.

"I'll come straight to it," Ada said. "I'm processing the Ellery estate materials. Funeral ephemera, condolence correspondence, the usual. And I've hit a gap in the record."

"A gap?"

"Burial location. The interment register lists St. Verena's, of course, but there's no grave assignment. No plot number. No section." He had checked twice, then a third time at two in the morning, standing in the cold of the stacks with a pencil flashlight because he hadn't wanted to turn on the overheads. "Every burial in this cathedral for two hundred years has a plot number. Except his."

Hale's expression did not change, which was itself a kind of information. "Ah," he said. "Yes. That would be because Mr. Ellery was interred in the crypt."

"The crypt has been sealed since 1974."

"It has."

"Then how—"

"There's an access stair from the ambulatory," Hale said mildly. "It's been kept functional for exactly this purpose. Interments by special arrangement. The crypt remains consecrated ground even if it's closed to visitors. The structural report was quite dire — vaulting instability, subsidence in the northeast pier. The diocese felt it would be irresponsible to admit the public." He smiled, and the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "The dead don't mind the dust, Ada. It's only the living we have to protect."

Ada let a silence sit between them long enough to become its own question. "I'd like to see where he's buried. Professionally. To complete the record."

"I'm afraid that won't be possible."

The refusal came without hesitation, without apology, without even the courtesy of a pause to consider it — and that was the detail Ada carried out of the room later, turning it over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. A man asked an innocent question usually hesitates while he decides whether the answer costs him anything. Hale had answered before the question finished landing.

"The engineer's report," Ada said. "Is it in the diocesan archive? I'd be happy to consult it, just to understand the scope of—"

"These things are older than both of us." Hale stood, signaling the audience had concluded, and came around the desk to lay a hand briefly on Ada's shoulder. His grip was light and dry and very warm. "You do important work down there among the papers. Truly. But some doors are locked not because they hide anything, Ada. Some are locked because they're dangerous. You'd want us to keep the crypt gate locked even if you were standing on the other side of it, wouldn't you?"

He said it kindly. He said it like a blessing.

Ada walked back across the close with the wind pushing at his coat and the phrase lodged in him like a splinter: *even if you were standing on the other side of it.* As if Hale had already imagined him there. As if the canon had run the scenario — Ada, below ground, on the wrong side of the gate — and found it worth mentioning aloud.

In the archive annex, Ada made tea he didn't drink and pulled the 1974 file.

It was thin. That was the first wrong thing. A structural collapse serious enough to seal a cathedral crypt should have generated a small forest: engineering assessments, mason's surveys, insurance correspondence, faculty petitions to the diocese, consistory court rulings. Ada had processed dozens of comparable closures at other churches. They produced paper the way wounds produced blood.

St. Verena's produced eleven pages.

A single engineering letter, dated March 1974, on letterhead from a firm Ada had never heard of, declaring the northeast pier unsound and recommending immediate closure. No survey drawings. No second opinion. No follow-up inspection, ever — not in fifty years, not once, though every other sealed structure in the diocese had been reassessed at least twice. A consistory court minute noting the closure was approved. And a memo, typed on cathedral stationery, instructing that the crypt register be withdrawn from public access and held "under key of the Keeper's office."

*Under key of the Keeper's office.*

Ada read the phrase four times. In cathedral administration, the keeper of the crypt was traditionally the sexton — a maintenance role, keys and candles and drainage. But the memo capitalized the word. *the Keeper's office.* Someone in 1974 had thought of it as a title.

He photocopied all eleven pages, then photographed them too, because photocopies could be lost and photographs lived in three places at once. He was aware, doing it, that he was behaving like a man who expected the originals to disappear, and he noted the behavior the way he noted everything — filed, dated, cross-referenced — and declined to examine why.

* * *

The burial registers lived in the strong room, a windowless vault off the north transept where the temperature stayed at a constant sixteen degrees and the air tasted of stone. Ada worked there alone on Thursdays. He had requested Thursday access to the interment volumes ostensibly to complete the Ellery file; actually because Thursdays were when Edmund Voss, the sexton, cleaned the nave, and Voss's vacuum could be heard through two walls, which meant Ada would hear him coming.

He told himself this was ordinary caution. Archivists were cautious. It was practically liturgical.

Volume IX covered 1960 to 1985, entries in a succession of hands — three different curates, then a long unbroken stretch in a single script that ran from 1971 to 1979, neat, upright, faintly ecclesiastical, each entry ruled and numbered with a care bordering on devotion. Ada had catalogued enough handwriting to know when someone loved their work. Whoever wrote these entries had loved them.

Ellery's interment was recorded on the third-to-last page of the volume. Dated last month. Buried in the crypt, per the special arrangement. The same hand as 1979 — the same hand, unaged, or aged so little it made the back of Ada's neck prickle. Fifty years, and the penmanship identical: the same narrow ascenders, the same flat-topped *e*, the same habit of finishing each entry with a small diagonal tick, like a signature of satisfaction.

But that wasn't the impossible thing.

The impossible thing was further up the page. Because the crypt register — the withdrawn one, the one held under key of the Keeper's office — had apparently been continued in the same volume, interleaved without comment among the ordinary burials, and forty years ago, in that same devoted hand, there was an entry:

*No. 311. ELLERY, Thomas. Interred in the lower crypt, east range. Date of death: 14th of October, 1984.*

1984.

Thomas Ellery had died last month. Ada had catalogued the funeral ephemera himself — the order of service printed with the date, the condolence cards, the coroner's release form. Fourteen October of *this* year. He had held the paper. Paper did not lie to Ada Kavanagh; people lied, institutions lied, but paper simply recorded what someone had chosen to set down, and the choice itself was always evidence.

And here, forty years before Ellery died, someone had written his name into a burial ledger. Written it in advance. Numbered it. Given him a place in the east range of a crypt that had been sealed since before the ink dried.

Ada sat very still in the cold of the strong room and listened to the distant sound of Edmund Voss's vacuum moving along the nave, patient as tide.

There were explanations. Of course there were. A clerical error — a name entered twice, a coincidence of names, an Ellery father and son. He went looking for the father immediately, because that was the responsible hypothesis, the one a professional pursued before letting his mind go anywhere interesting. He pulled the death registers for 1984. He searched the surname Ellery through the whole decade.

There was no other Ellery. Not in the deaths, not in the marriages, not in the baptismal rolls back to 1900. Thomas Ellery appeared in the records of St. Verena's exactly twice: once in 1984, dead and buried in a sealed crypt, and once last month, dead and buried in the same.

Unless, said a voice in the back of Ada's head, the voice that sounded uncomfortably like his brother Daniel's, amused and a little cruel — unless the man who died last month wasn't the man who died in 1984. Unless the name was the thing being reused. Names got recycled all the time in this building. He'd spent six months cataloguing the memorial brasses; half the benefactors commemorated on the walls had died in parishes they'd never lived in, under dates that didn't survive cross-checking. He'd assumed sloth. Archives were full of sloth.

But sloth didn't number its errors. Sloth didn't rule its mistakes in a loving hand and tick them complete.

Ada copied the 1984 entry by hand, then photographed the page, then photographed the page again from a different angle in case the first photograph failed, and it was somewhere in the middle of the third exposure that he understood he was no longer completing the Ellery file. He was building a case file. For whom, he couldn't have said. Against whom, likewise. He only knew that the letter in his desk drawer — the confession postmarked the day after a dead man's funeral, claiming murder, pointing downward — had named a proof sleeping beneath the crypt, and now the registers themselves were whispering the same direction.

*Down,* said the paper. Everything said down.

He returned Volume IX to its shelf, squared it precisely with its neighbors, and signed the access log. As he wrote his name he noticed, above it, the previous signature in the log. Edmund Voss. Yesterday evening, after hours. Purpose of access, in the sexton's careful print: *routine check, humidity.*

The strong room's hygrometer read forty-nine percent. It had read forty-nine percent for the six years Ada had worked here, because Voss maintained the climate system with the tenderness of a man tending an aquarium.

Routine. Everything here was routine. The vacuum at ten o'clock, the hygrometer at forty-nine, the crypt sealed for fifty years on eleven pages of paper, a burial registered four decades early in a hand that never aged.

Ada put on his coat and walked out through the cloister, and at the mouth of the ambulatory he stopped — just for a moment, just long enough to look at the plain oak door set into the north wall, iron-hinged, paint-blistered, fitted with a lock considerably newer than the door. The stair behind it went down to the ambulatory passage and the crypt gate beyond.

Someone had polished the door's handle. Not the door — the handle. Worn bright in a small oval, the way metal gets when a hand finds it often.

Ada stood in the cold draft rising faintly from the seam beneath the door — a draft, he realized, which meant air, which meant the crypt breathed, which meant whatever was down there was not entirely entombed — and he thought about the tick at the end of each ledger entry. The little diagonal stroke of completion. Forty years of endings, each one signed off with a small satisfied mark.

He went back to the annex, unlocked his desk, and took out the confession letter. Then he opened the donor plaques database — the memorial brasses, the wall tablets, the giving records he'd spent half a year cataloguing and never once questioned — and began, with the cold methodical patience that was the only inheritance his brother had ever left him, to look for a cipher.
