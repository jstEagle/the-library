## 13. Vann's Confession Tape

Teo came to the depot at 2:14 in the morning, which was the hour I had privately designated as the only honest one left in Marrow Creek. The streetlights hummed their sodium hymn, the coffee in my thermos had gone to tar, and the switchboard sat quiet in a way that felt less like peace and more like held breath.

He didn't knock. He'd stopped knocking three weeks ago, when we stopped pretending he was just a drifter who liked late fares. He let himself into the dispatch office with the ease of someone who had learned doors from the wrong side of them, and he was holding a manila envelope against his chest the way you hold something that might still be warm.

"You're going to want to sit down," he said.

"I'm always sitting down," I said. "That's the job."

"This is different."

Everything was different now. Since the reservoir, since the bicycle came up out of the shallows with its zip-tied handlebars and its concrete-encrusted frame, since I'd stood on the drought-cracked bank with lake water soaking through my boots and understood that Corinne Ashe had not drowned — she had been *sunk*. Everything since then had been different, and worse, and closer.

I sat down anyway. Old habits of obedience die hard even in women who trust no one.

Teo set the envelope on the desk between us but kept his hand flat on top of it, like it might bolt. Up close I could see his hands were shaking. Teo, who had walked into my depot the first night with the loose-limbed calm of a boy who'd slept under bridges in four states and feared nothing but staying put — his hands were shaking.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"I've been sitting on it for two years." He swallowed. "It's why I came back at all. It's why I sent the booking."

The prepaid request. *Confirm that Elias Vann dies of natural causes on October 9th, or refund nothing.* The instruction that had unraveled fifteen years of my careful, ordered silence, thread by thread, until the whole sweater of my life lay in pieces on the floor. I had hated him for it once, briefly, before I understood it. I wasn't sure I'd fully stopped hating him for it. Grief makes strange instruments of us all.

"Vann recorded it himself?" I said.

"He recorded it in 2011. Before the council run. He was scared of Merrick then — properly scared, before Merrick got sick and everyone started talking about him in the past tense. Vann wanted insurance." Teo's jaw worked. "My mother cleaned houses. She cleaned Elias Vann's house every other Thursday for eleven years. When she died, I found this taped inside the lining of her sewing box. She never knew what it was. Or maybe she did, and that's why she kept it where she kept everything she couldn't throw away."

His mother. Another name I hadn't known until recently, another life folded into the lie like a letter into an envelope. I thought about a woman vacuuming around a councilman's desk while a cassette sat in a drawer three feet away, humming its secret, year after year.

"Play it," I said.

* * *

The tape deck in the dispatch office was ancient — a dual-well unit I used to dub call recordings onto cassettes back when the company still bothered with paper trails, back when Aldous Merrick still walked the depot floors and insisted that everything be logged, everything be kept, because *records are leverage, Miss Oyelaran, and leverage is the only currency that doesn't inflate*. I fed the cassette into the well with fingers that were steadier than they had any right to be.

The hiss came first. That long oceanic hiss of tape finding its speed, and beneath it, faintly, a clock ticking somewhere in a room I would never see. Then a chair creaking. Then a voice I knew from a hundred public meetings, from campaign ads, from the ribbon-cutting photographs in the *Marrow Creek Courier* — smooth, baritone, practiced. But stripped here of its audience, it sounded smaller. Sounded like a man alone in a room, talking to a machine because no living person could ever hear it.

"This is Elias Vann. October the fourth, 2011. If you're listening to this, then either Aldous Merrick or Chief Dornan has decided I'm expendable, and I want the record to show—"

A pause. A long breath drawn through the nose, the sound of a man rehearsing courage and finding the script shorter than he'd hoped.

"I want the record to show that I know what happened to Corinne Ashe."

My hand found the edge of the desk without my telling it to. Teo had gone very still beside me, the way people go still when a wound they've carried for years is finally about to be touched.

"The official report says accidental drowning. I wrote the supplemental findings myself. I was a junior clerk then, twenty-six years old, ambitious, and Aldous Merrick called me into his office and explained — patiently, reasonably, the way he explains everything — what the town needed. What the *families* needed. He said grief needs a shape, and accident is the kindest shape there is." A dry, terrible laugh. "He made it sound like mercy. That's the thing about Aldous. He can make anything sound like mercy."

On the tape, the clock ticked. In the office, neither of us breathed.

"She wasn't alone out there. She was driven to the reservoir. Someone drove her up Route 9 that night — September 22nd, 2009 — and she went into the water while that person waited by the car. I know because I filed the timeline, and the timeline has a hole in it shaped exactly like a taxi. Dispatch records for that night show a car assigned to the reservoir road that was never dispatched. Fare number—" he recited digits, and my blood went cold, because I knew those digits, I had stared at that gap in my own ledger a hundred times over fifteen years and told myself it was a clerical error, a mis-keyed entry, the small human sloppiness that creeps into any system — "that fare was logged out and logged back in ninety minutes later with no passenger, no destination, and no driver named in the margin."

"Because the driver was named somewhere else," I whispered. "Somewhere that got cut out."

Teo looked at me. Neither of us touched the machine.

"Merrick paid for the silence. Dornan buried the investigation — I watched him do it, I watched him close the file in under six days, six days, for a drowned child. And the driver—" Vann's voice tightened here, coiling, and I heard for the first time the fear underneath the ambition, the fear that had made a coward record his sins in an empty room — "the driver took her to the water. Whatever happened at the shoreline, whatever argument or panic or — God knows what — the driver was there. The driver drove her there, and the driver drove away, and the driver has been on Merrick's payroll ever since. Every month. Fifteen years of envelopes. I've seen the disbursements. I know the account."

The tape hissed. The clock ticked. And Elias Vann, mayor-track councilman, keeper of the town's most profitable secret, said the words I would carry out of that room like a stone in my chest:

"The driver's name is Ruthie Calloway."

* * *

The tape ran on for another eleven minutes. Vann rambled — dates, amounts, the layout of Merrick's second ledger, which I had already found and already read, page by damning page, in the dead founder's archive. He talked about the night itself in fragments, secondhand, things he'd pieced together from the file rather than witnessed: Corinne alive when the car left the depot, Corinne's bicycle strapped to the rack so no one would ask how she'd gotten home, Corinne going into the water while the taillights idled at the tree line.

But it was the last minute that mattered. The last minute, when the practiced voice cracked down the middle and became just a frightened man's voice:

"If anything happens to me, whoever finds this should know it wasn't accident and it wasn't suicide. Dornan keeps a standing fare to the reservoir every Friday. Ask him what he carries in the envelope. Ask Ruthie Calloway what she was told the night of September 22nd, 2009. Ask her whether she believed it. I don't think she did. I think that's why she never spent the money. I've seen her accounts too."

Click. Hiss. Silence.

I sat in the dark of the dispatch office with the tape deck's little counter glowing green — 00:23:47 — and felt hope arrive in my chest like a live wire, electric and dangerous and utterly indifferent to what it might burn.

We had it. Not all of it — Vann's confession was partial, hedged, built on files rather than witness — but it named names. It tied Merrick to the payment, Dornan to the burial, and the driver to the water. It was a thread, and if you pulled a thread in Marrow Creek, the whole garment came apart. I knew that now. I'd been pulling threads for weeks and watching the town unravel faster than I could catalogue it.

"We take this to the state police," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, rusty with purpose. "Not Dornan. Not anyone local. State investigators, tonight, first light at the latest. We copy the tape, we keep the original, we—"

"Zeph."

Teo's voice stopped me the way a hand on the arm stops someone walking in their sleep. He was looking at the tape deck, at the cassette turning slowly behind its plastic window, and his face had done something complicated — the joy draining out of it even as the vindication settled in, leaving behind something older and colder.

"You heard the name," he said.

"Of course I heard the name."

"Say it."

I didn't want to. Saying it made it real, and making it real meant the wire in my chest would start burning through everything it touched. But Teo had lost a sister to this town's silence; he had earned the right to make me say ugly things out loud.

"Ruthie Calloway," I said.

Ruthie Calloway, who had worked the day shift at Marrow Creek Taxi for nineteen years. Ruthie Calloway, who kept a dish of peppermints on the counter for the schoolkids and remembered every regular's usual route without checking the book. Ruthie Calloway, who had covered my shifts when my mother died — when the woman I claimed was my mother died, the funeral I'd invented, the grief I'd performed because a dispatcher with no dead was a dispatcher with no history — who had brought me a casserole I hadn't eaten and sat with me in the break room saying nothing, because Ruthie understood that sometimes company is just silence with witnesses.

Ruthie Calloway, who was downstairs right now. Who came in at five every morning to relieve the night board, whose car was probably already in the lot, whose keys were probably already jingling in her pocket in some kitchen across town where she was drinking her first coffee of the day and getting ready to come be kind to me again.

"She's still driving," Teo said quietly. "She never stopped. She's the one who taught you half your routes."

"And she's been paying for it," I said. "Every month. For fifteen years. Vann said she never spent the money — the hush money, the envelopes. If she never spent it, then where—"

I stopped. Because I knew where. Everyone in the depot knew where. There was a fund at Marrow Creek Taxi that nobody discussed, a discretionary account Ruthie administered with maddening vagueness, that had quietly covered medical bills for drivers' families, tuition for a mechanic's daughter, a new furnace for the widow who cleaned the offices. Fifteen years of blood money laundered into casseroles and furnaces and school shoes, distributed by a woman trying to give back, penny by penny, what she could never give back at all.

"Oh, Ruthie," I said, to the empty room, to the hissing tape, to nobody.

Teo reached out and clicked the deck off. The reels stopped turning. In the sudden silence I could hear the building around us — the refrigerator in the break room, the buzz of the sign outside, the old bones of the depot settling — and underneath it all, faintly, the sound of a car pulling into the lot. Headlights swept across the office window and slid away toward the garage.

4:51 a.m. Right on schedule. Nineteen years of right-on-schedule.

Teo looked at me, and I looked at the door, and we both understood at the same moment that the confession we'd been praying for hadn't ended anything. It had just given the story a face — a kind, tired, peppermint-and-coffee face that would walk through that door in nine minutes and greet me by name.

"What do we do?" Teo whispered.

Above us, on the stairs, a floorboard creaked.

Ruthie was early.
