## 2. Guest Register, 2004

The hotel's basement smelled the way old paper smells when it is losing its argument with time—dust and vinegar and something faintly sweet, like fruit rotting behind a wall. Ulyana Voss went down the back stairs at six in the morning, before the day staff arrived, before the lawyers' cars came back to circle the cemetery like crows that had learned to wear suits. He carried a flashlight, a thermos of black coffee, and twenty years of a very particular skill: how to be somewhere without being there.

The archive room had no lock worth the name. A brass knob, a door that swelled in humid weather, a sign reading STAFF ONLY that everyone ignored. Inside stood eleven gray ledger boxes on steel shelving, one per year from the hotel's opening through 2011, when the ledgers went digital and the past became something you could search instead of touch.

Ulyana pulled the box marked 2004 and set it on the worktable under the bare bulb.

He told himself he was doing inventory. The estate's attorneys had requested "all records pertaining to Edmund Hale" as part of the land dispute—the exhumation had turned a property squabble into something with teeth, and now every document with the founder's name on it was evidence or liability depending on which lawyer held it. Inventory was plausible. Inventory was invisible work.

But inventory did not require him to open the box at the bottom first.

* * *

The guest registers were leather-bound, each page ruled in faded blue, each entry written by whichever clerk had worked the desk that night. Ulyana knew them all. Doreen, who looped her g's. Marcus, whose handwriting collapsed after nine p.m. Old Semyon, the night man before him, who dotted his i's like knife wounds.

He started with October 2004 because he had always intended to start with October 2004, and lying to himself about it felt like a courtesy they extended each other.

The fire at the Quill place had been the second week of September. Everyone in Harrow's Bend knew the date the way they knew their own birthdays—not the year, just the season, the way the maples went red early near the burn scar. Mara Quill, bookkeeper for the Hale family concerns, witness for the state in the land-fraud case that had quietly evaporated, dead in the fire along with everything she owned. No body recovered intact enough for viewing; closed casket; a headstone by November. Ulyana had been twenty-six then, three months into the job, learning the shape of the town the way you learn a swimming pool—by keeping to the shallow end.

He turned pages. September. The register thinned after the fire, as if grief had checked out of the whole town at once. Then October, and the hand changed—Doreen's loops, Marcus's collapse—and then:

**Oct. 3 — Rm 12 — E. Hale — cash — no forwarding.**

Ulyana set down the flashlight.

Edmund Hale had been in Lisbon on October 3rd, 2004. This was not rumor. This was documented, photographed, celebrated—a feature in the *Gazette* two years later, the founder abroad securing the Portuguese shipping contracts that had doubled the family fortune, standing on a dock in a cream suit with his arm around a man Ulyana did not recognize. The dates were public. The dates were the kind of thing schoolchildren got quizzed on during centennial week.

And yet here, in Room 12, the room facing the cemetery—the room the quiet guest had requested all those years ago, the detail that had been scratching at Ulyana since the coffin came up full of stones—someone had signed *E. Hale* three days after the fire that killed the woman who was going to testify against the Hales.

Cash. No forwarding address. No phone number taken, because important people didn't need to leave numbers.

Ulyana sat down on the steel stool and looked at the entry for a long time. The bulb buzzed. Somewhere above him, the hotel woke up—pipes knocking, the elevator's slow asthmatic climb—and he let the sounds wash over him while the thrill moved through his chest, that clean cold thrill of *noticing*, the only drug he'd ever allowed himself. For most of his life noticing had been the whole of his survival. You noticed which guests watched the staff too long. You noticed which questions were questions. You noticed, and you said nothing, and you stayed.

But this notice had teeth in it. Because either someone had signed a dead founder's name in his hotel—

—or the founder hadn't been dead yet, and the grave was wrong, and the fire was wrong, and the woman under the headstone by the burn scar had never been Mara Quill at all.

His coffee went cold in the thermos cup. He didn't drink it. He copied the entry onto a card from the front desk stock, in his own careful hand, and then—he wasn't sure why, except that twenty years of instinct made him do it—he copied it again and slid the second card into the lining of his shoe.

Then he kept going. If October 3rd existed, other days might too.

* * *

They didn't. Not exactly. But almost.

October 4th: no entry. October 5th: a salesman from Dayton, Room 7. October 6th: **E. Hale**, same hand, same clerk—Semyon's knife-dot i's—Room 12 again, cash again. And on the 9th, a final line in Semyon's writing, smaller than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page:

**E. Hale — paid thru month — do not disturb.**

Do not disturb. Ulyana read the phrase four times. It was a standard notation. Clerks wrote it a hundred times a year. But Semyon had pressed it into the paper like a man leaving a message in wet cement, and Semyon had retired in 2006 and moved to his sister's place upstate, and Semyon had died in 2011, which Ulyana knew because concierges know things, because knowing things was the job.

So the message had outlived its messenger. That was usually how messages survived.

Ulyana worked until ten, photographing nothing, copying everything, returning each ledger to its exact position with its spine angled exactly as he'd found it. He was halfway through cross-referencing the 2005 payroll records—looking for a housekeeper named Mira, though he hadn't admitted that to himself yet—when he found the thing that ended the morning.

The 2004 box was wrong.

Not the ledgers. The box itself. The tissue paper lining the bottom had been torn and re-laid, hastily, by someone who cared about appearances more than archaeology. Two staples in the lid's interior had been replaced with newer ones, shinier. And tucked into the seam where the box met the shelf, so faint he nearly missed it, a crescent of gray dust that had been disturbed and never quite restored.

Someone had gone through these records before him. Recently. Carefully. And they had put everything back almost perfectly—which meant they were good, which meant they would be back, which meant the almost was the only gift Ulyana was going to get.

He thought: *the lawyers.* Of course the lawyers had combed the archive; they'd subpoenaed half the town. But lawyers took documents. They photocopied loudly and left sticky notes and billed by the hour. They did not restaple lids in the dark.

He thought: *Cassandra Hale,* arriving tomorrow, according to the reservation book, under a name he'd already flagged. He thought about the way powerful families searched a place—not for what was there, but for what might someday be there.

He put the box back. He climbed the stairs. He was careful to step on the fourth stair, the loud one, so that anyone listening would hear an ordinary man coming up from an ordinary errand.

* * *

Marek Osei found him at noon, in the corridor outside the linen room, holding a folded newspaper he clearly had no intention of reading.

Marek had managed the hotel for thirty-one years, longer than Ulyana had been alive in any identity. He was a broad, deliberate man who communicated primarily through sighs and the placement of objects, and right now he placed himself squarely in Ulyana's path.

"You were in the basement," Marek said.

"I was doing inventory. For the estate request."

"Mm." Marek looked down the corridor both ways, which he never did, because Marek did not believe anything down a corridor could threaten him. "A man came Tuesday night. After close. Said he was from the estate's document service."

"We don't have a document service on file."

"No," Marek agreed. "We don't. I gave him coffee anyway. He asked whether we still kept the paper registers." A pause, freighted. "I told him we burned them."

Ulyana waited. Lying to Marek had never once worked, and Marek knew Ulyana knew it, and between them this had calcified into a kind of honesty.

"He wasn't from any estate," Marek said. "I've met estate people my whole life. Estate people have business cards and bad shoes. This one had good shoes and no card, and when he left he got into a car with county plates that I'd bet my pension are stolen." He folded the unread newspaper smaller and smaller. "So. Whatever you found down there—you found it second."

The corridor hummed. Downstairs, the front doors opened and let in the sound of the town: hammers on the dinner pavilion going up on the green, a truck delivering chairs, somebody laughing too brightly. Centennial week. The whole town rehearsing its love for a man whose coffin had come up empty.

"Marek." Ulyana kept his voice at housekeeping volume, flat, unremarkable, the voice of a man discussing towels. "In 2004. Did we have a guest in Room 12?"

Marek's face did something complicated and final, like a door closing on well-oiled hinges.

"In 2004," he said, "we had a great many guests, and I was younger, and you weren't here, and the less either of us remembers about that year, the longer we both keep our jobs." He pushed the newspaper into Ulyana's hands as if it were a tip. "There's a family coming tomorrow that has eaten hotels like ours for breakfast. When they ask you questions—and they will ask you questions, that's what they're for—you will be boring. You will be furniture. You have always been excellent at furniture, Voss. Be furniture now."

"And if I can't?"

Marek looked at him for a moment with something that was almost tenderness and entirely fear.

"Then remember," he said, walking away, "that whoever searched that basement already knows what you know. The only question left is whether they know you know it."

That night Ulyana stood at the front desk until two a.m., watching the cemetery gate across the road, where the excavated earth lay in a neat brown rectangle under floodlights the lawyers had ordered installed. The hole waited. The town slept. And in Room 12—empty, made up daily, facing the dead—someone's absence sat so heavily it was practically a registration.

He took out the card from his shoe lining and read the embossed ghost of Semyon's pen on the page he'd copied from memory: *do not disturb.*

Too late, Ulyana thought. Somebody had disturbed. And tomorrow, Cassandra Hale would arrive to find out who.
