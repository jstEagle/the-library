## 1. The Locked Crypt

There were three hundred and eleven steps between the archivist's office at St. Vellum's Cathedral and the crypt below, and Emrys Ashgrove had counted them so many times over the years that the number had stopped being a count and become a kind of prayer. Down past the vestry, where the choir robes hung like patient ghosts. Past the boiler room, where old Mr. Tredwell's radio murmured hymns to itself even when no one was listening. Past the shelf of misfiled parish registers that Emrys had been meaning to reorganize since 2019 and would probably be meaning to reorganize until the heat death of the universe.

The steps themselves were worn into shallow bowls by four centuries of feet, which meant that descending into the crypt was less like walking and more like being gently swallowed.

Emrys carried the inventory ledger under one arm, a lantern in one hand — the crypt's electric lights worked on a schedule known only to God and the diocesan electrician, and neither had been consulted recently — and a thermos of tea in the other, because routine without tea was merely ritual, and Emrys drew a firm line between the two.

"Good morning," Emrys said to the dark, as always.

The dark, as always, said nothing back. But it felt polite about it.

* * *

The crypt of St. Vellum's was not the romantic sort you read about in novels — no cobwebbed sarcophagi, no dramatic shafts of moonlight. It was long and low and orderly, lined with stone shelves where the coffins of the cathedral's worthies rested in neat rows, each with a small brass plate: name, dates, and in several cases a Latin motto that some Victorian stonemason had clearly translated wrong on purpose, out of spite. DEO GRATIAS, read one, above a man who had sued his own brother over a pew. Emrys had looked up the translation years ago and had been quietly delighted ever since.

Inventory day came once a quarter. It was Emrys's favorite task in all the vast, dusty machinery of the cathedral archive, though they would have denied this under oath. There was something deeply soothing about walking the rows with the ledger open, confirming that everything was exactly where it had been three months ago, that death had remained punctual and tidy, that nothing down here had moved except the dust, which moved everywhere and apologized for nothing.

Row one: the bishops. All present. Row two: the deans, past and occasionally embarrassing. Row three: benefactors, including Dame Hester Quill, whose brass plate promised she "GAVE LIBERALLY AND EXPECTED NOTHING," a claim Emrys found touching precisely because it was so obviously false — she'd left instructions in her will that her coffin be checked annually, and it was the only reason row three got inventoried at all.

Emrys paused at her shelf, as always, and gave the coffin a small nod.

"Still here, Dame Hester. Still checking."

Somewhere overhead, the morning service began — the faint, woolly sound of the organ filtering down through the floor like music heard through a wall of sleep. Emrys checked their watch. Ten minutes past nine. The verger, Mr. Calloway, would be lighting the candles he should have lit at five past, and Dean Merridew would be pretending not to notice, and the whole ancient clockwork of St. Vellum's would grind through another Sunday exactly as it had ground through ten thousand Sundays before it.

Emrys wrote *all present, rows 1–3* in the inventory ledger, in the small, precise hand that had won them a penmanship prize at fourteen and exactly nothing else in life.

Then they reached row four, and the inner vault, and stopped.

* * *

The inner vault sat at the far end of the crypt like a full stop at the end of a very long sentence. It was a square chamber of darker stone set into the wall, older than everything around it — older than the cathedral proper, some said, though Emrys had never found documentation either way, which for Emrys was roughly equivalent to saying it didn't exist. Its door was iron, banded with hinges gone green as pond water, and it had one lock.

That lock had been Corwin Ashgrove's business, and nobody else's, for thirty-one years.

Corwin Ashgrove — no relation, despite the surname, a coincidence that had caused no end of confusion among visiting clergy — had been Archivist of St. Vellum's for three decades before his death eight months ago, and he had been, in every way that mattered, the architect of Emrys's entire mind. He had taught Emrys how to read a marginal note the way other people read faces. How to know that a gap in a register was sometimes more eloquent than an entry. How to sit with a document until it confessed. He had hired Emrys at twenty-three, offhand, almost carelessly — *"You alphabetized my chaos, I suppose you may as well stay"* — and then spent twenty years teaching them, patiently, obliquely, never once saying anything as naked as *I am proud of you*, because Corwin believed that praise, like strong drink, ought to be administered in small doses or not at all.

He had also kept the key to the inner vault on a chain inside his waistcoat, next to his heart, and had told Emrys — once, laughing, in a way that hadn't quite been a joke — *"When I'm dead, you'll find it's the one thing I haven't left you."*

He had been right. The will had been generous: his books, his desk, his absurd collection of inkwells, his small house on Candlemaker's Row. But the vault key had not appeared among his effects. Not in the house. Not in his office. Not, Emrys had eventually concluded, anywhere on this earth, unless it had been buried with him, which would have been exactly the sort of theatrical gesture Corwin would have found hilarious from beyond the grave.

For eight months, the vault had simply stayed shut, and Emrys had simply let it. There were inventories enough. There was work enough. And there was, if Emrys was honest — which they tried to be, at least in the privacy of their own skull — a certain comfort in leaving one door closed. A man deserved his secrets. Even dead men. Perhaps especially dead men; the living at least had the decency to change their minds.

But today, standing in front of the vault with the lantern raised, Emrys did not feel comfortable. Emrys felt the particular prickle at the base of the neck that Corwin had taught them to trust above all other instruments.

*"The archive will tell you when something is wrong,"* he used to say. *"Not in words. In weather."*

The weather was wrong.

Emrys stepped closer and held the lantern up to the lock.

It was a heavy thing, nineteenth-century, brass gone brown, with a keyhole shaped like a keyhole in a child's drawing — round head, long body. And across its face, where the metal should have been smooth with disuse, there were scratches. Fresh ones. Fine, bright lines in the old patina, as if someone had recently attempted to pick it — recently, and badly, and more than once.

Emrys stood very still.

Down here, the only sounds were the distant organ, the tick of the lantern cooling, and their own heartbeat, which had picked up the pace without being asked.

Someone had been trying to get into Corwin's vault.

* * *

Now — and here is the thing about Emrys Ashgrove that everyone at St. Vellum's knew and no one understood — most people, finding fresh pick-marks on a sealed vault, would do one of two things. They would either tell someone immediately, or they would convince themselves they'd imagined it and go have a nice cup of tea.

Emrys did neither. Emrys did what Corwin had trained them to do, which was to treat the anomaly as a document.

They knelt, carefully, knees complaining, and examined the marks properly. Three distinct attempts, by the spacing. Two with a thin tool — a knife blade, perhaps, or a tension wrench — and one, the shallowest, with something broader and flatter, a screwdriver maybe, wielded by someone who lost patience. The bright metal of the scratches said recent. Days, not months. The rust-dust that had collected in the deepest grooves said not yesterday, either.

And beneath the lock, on the stone threshold, in the gray film that gathered on everything down here within a fortnight, there was a scuff. A heel-mark, pointing away from the door. Someone had crouched here, worked at the lock, failed, and left.

Emrys took out a handkerchief — clean, because inventory days were the one day Emrys remembered to carry a clean handkerchief — and touched nothing. They measured the scratch positions against the lock face by eye, noted the direction of the scuff, the approximate size of the heel (smallish; a woman's shoe, or a small man's), and the fact that whoever it was had come alone, because there was only one set of marks and the dust doesn't lie, not down here, where nothing disturbs it but spiders and the occasional ambitious mouse.

Then Emrys stood, brushed off their knees, and did the second thing Corwin had trained into them, the thing that had slowly, over twenty years, turned a mild-mannered archivist into something the police of this county would one day have opinions about.

They opened the private ledger.

* * *

The private ledger lived in Emrys's satchel, in a oilcloth sleeve, and it looked — deliberately — like nothing at all. A plain school exercise book, the kind sold in stationery shops for ninety pence, with a marbled cover going soft at the corners. Inside, however, it was something else entirely. Twenty years of entries in Emrys's precise hand: observations, timelines, transcriptions, small mysteries noticed and pursued and, in a number of cases that Emrys did not like to think about too directly, solved.

Because that was the other thing about Emrys Ashgrove, the thing that existed nowhere but in these pages: over the years, deaths had occurred in and around St. Vellum's parish, and the authorities had filed them under headings like *misadventure* and *accidental*, and Emrys had looked at the files — Corwin had ways of getting access to files, in those days, ways he'd never explained — and had seen what the filings missed. And once you've seen what a filing misses, you cannot unsee it. You can only write it down, and keep writing, and tell no one, because who would believe an archivist? Who would believe a person whose entire vocation was being overlooked?

So the ledger grew. Quietly. Invisibly. Like moss.

Emrys uncapped their fountain pen — Corwin had insisted on fountain pens, *"ballpoints are for people in a hurry to be forgotten"* — and turned to a fresh page, and began.

*Inventory day, autumn quarter,* they wrote. *Inner vault, St. Vellum's crypt.*

*Observed: three separate attempts upon the vault lock, undated but recent (est. 1–3 weeks). Tools varied; skill poor; persistence notable. Single individual. Small build. Left no other trace. Did not succeed.*

Then Emrys paused, pen hovering, and added the question that had been sitting in their chest since they'd first seen the bright scratches:

*Question the first: why now? The vault has been sealed for eight months. Whatever sleeps behind that door has slept through two full quarters of inventory. What changed?*

*Question the second:* — and here the pen slowed — *who knows what's in there? Because I don't. Corwin never told me. Which means whoever is scratching at that lock knows something about Corwin Ashgrove that his own protégé does not.*

Emrys stared at that last line for a long moment. The organ upstairs had gone quiet; the service must be reaching the sermon, that sacred interval during which half the congregation contemplated lunch. The lantern hissed softly. Somewhere in the walls, water moved through pipes with the sound of a slow, indifferent digestion.

It was strange, Emrys thought, how grief worked. Eight months, and they could still walk past Corwin's office and expect, for half a heartbeat, to hear the clink of his teacup against its saucer, that precise porcelain punctuation of his mornings. They still caught themselves saving things to tell him — a funny misprint in the burial register, a letter from a genealogist hunting a bigamist ancestor. The habit of him was deeper than the fact of his absence.

And now someone was picking at the one lock he'd kept, the one inheritance withheld, and Emrys found that the grief had acquired a new and unwelcome flavor underneath it. Not sorrow. Suspicion. As if Corwin had left behind not just a vault but a question, and the question had finally grown loud enough to be heard.

*"You'll understand when I'm gone,"* he'd said once. Another of his not-quite-jokes. Emrys had laughed at the time.

They weren't laughing now.

* * *

Emrys finished the entry, blotted it, closed the ledger, and stood a while longer in front of the vault door, doing the thing they did best, which was nothing at all, very attentively.

The iron door gave back nothing. It was a door that had made peace with being shut. Green hinges, brown lock, and behind it — what? Parish records, probably. Corwin had called it "the deep stack," casually, the way a man might refer to a cupboard of old boots. Deeds, leases, the cathedral's more tedious legal history. That was the official answer, the one given to the diocese, to visitors, to Emrys.

But Corwin had kept the key against his heart. People did not carry deeds and leases next to their hearts.

Emrys thought about the heel-mark again. Small. Careful. Someone who had come down three hundred and eleven steps into the cold and the dark, alone, to spend minutes — how long? ten? twenty? — crouched at a lock they couldn't open, and then had climbed back out and told no one and tried again later, and again after that. That wasn't curiosity. Curiosity gives up after one attempt. That was need. That was someone who knew there was something behind this door that mattered to them personally, urgently, and who could not wait much longer.

And that, in turn, meant the attempts would continue. Whoever it was would come back — better tools, maybe, or better patience — and Emrys would very much like to know when.

So Emrys did a third thing, the thing that would have made Corwin raise one eyebrow in a way that managed to convey both approval and the warning that approval was not the same as endorsement. They took a small packet of fine ash from the satchel — sieved fireplace ash, kept for exactly this purpose, dusted along the thresholds of certain shelves to reveal browsing hands — and laid a thin, invisible-gray veil of it across the stone before the vault door, and another across the top step of the stair, where any visitor from above must pass.

Then they packed up the thermos, the ledgers, the lantern, and climbed back toward the light, counting the steps on the way up out of habit and losing count, for the first time in years, at two hundred and six.

* * *

Upstairs, the world resumed its ordinary shape. Sunlight through the great west window lay across the nave floor in colored puddles. The service was letting out; the vergers' bell rang its cheerful, brainless peal. Mr. Calloway was snuffing candles with the air of a man performing surgery. Dean Merridew stood at the door shaking hands and radiating benevolence like a well-insulated stove.

"Ah, Ashgrove!" the dean called, spotting them. "Down in the catacombs again? You'll catch your death down there."

"Almost certainly, Dean," Emrys said pleasantly, and meant nothing by it, and thought nothing of it, and smiled the small smile of a person whose entire life is built on the principle that the safest place to hide a thing is in plain sight, mildly agreeing with everyone.

But as Emrys walked back down the cloister toward the archive, satchel on shoulder, tea gone cold in the thermos, one thought kept turning over and over like a coin spun on a table, refusing to fall.

Three attempts. Recent. Patient. Personal.

And beneath the questions, quieter but colder, a fourth — the kind of thought Emrys usually only permitted after dark, with the office lamp low and the ledger open:

*Whoever is scratching at that lock thinks the vault holds a secret worth having. And the only person who ever knew what it holds is eight months dead.*

*Which means somebody has been reading Corwin Ashgrove's secrets. And if they've read his...*

Emrys unlocked the archive office, went in, and did not turn on the main lights.

The private ledger came back out of the satchel. The pen came back out of the pocket. And on the page beneath the day's entry, in handwriting slightly less steady than usual, Emrys added a final line:

*Note to self: check whether anything else of Corwin's has been disturbed. Papers. Desk. House. If someone is looking for what he kept, they will not have stopped at the vault.*

Outside, the afternoon sun lay warm and innocent on the cloister stones. Inside, an archivist sat down at a dead man's desk and began, methodically, to search it — and in the third drawer, beneath a folder of correspondence about gutter repairs, found a pressed white lily that Emrys was quite certain had not been there last week.
