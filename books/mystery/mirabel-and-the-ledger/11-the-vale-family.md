## 11. The Vale Family

### Emrys

There is a particular silence in a county records office, and Emrys Halloran had spent enough of their life in the cathedral's crypt to know it intimately: the silence of paper that has not been touched in a long time. Paper forgets it was ever meant to be read. It settles into itself like an old dog by a fire, and it resents being disturbed.

Emrys disturbed it anyway.

"Vale," they said to the clerk at the front desk, a young woman with reading glasses pushed up into her hair like a bird's nest she'd forgotten about. "Serafin Vale. Disappearance, twenty years ago. I'd like the file."

The clerk looked at Emrys the way people often did—first with polite attention, then with the slow dawning realization that the person before her was somehow *less* than expected. Emrys had cultivated that effect for years. Cardigan the color of wet cardboard. A face designed by committee to be forgotten. It was, they had always felt, a professional advantage.

"Serafin Vale," the clerk repeated, tapping at her keyboard. "That'll be a closed file. Missing persons, never resolved. You'd need to be next of kin or—"

"Or law enforcement," Emrys said, "or acting in concert with law enforcement, which I am. Detective Sergeant Vance of the St. Vellum's inquiry is expecting me to pull this."

It was, strictly speaking, true. Vance had said, *Go pull the file, I'll be there in an hour, don't touch anything without gloves,* which Emrys chose to interpret as expectation.

The clerk hesitated, then produced a gray box with the reluctance of someone handing over a sleeping infant. "Sign here. And don't remove anything from the reading room."

Emrys signed, carried the box to a table beneath a window that hadn't been cleaned since the previous administration, and put on white cotton gloves with the reverence of a priest vesting.

The file was thin. That was the first thing Emrys noticed, and it made their stomach tighten. Twenty years of a girl's absence, and the county had needed only a quarter-inch of paper to hold it. A missing persons report filed by her mother, Margaret Vale, eleven days after Serafin stopped coming home—eleven days, Emrys noted, because the mother had first assumed her daughter had gone off with friends, and then assumed she'd run off with a boy, and by the time she assumed the worst, the trail had gone cold and the cathedral had finished its restoration and everyone had stopped looking.

A photograph, clipped to the first page. A young woman with dark hair cut blunt at the jaw, a smile that was half amusement and half dare, as though she'd been interrupted mid-thought by the camera and found it funny. She looked, Emrys thought with a lurch, like someone who would have found Corwin Ashgrove's careful silences unbearable. Someone who would have poked at them. Someone who would have *opened the wrong drawer.*

*She found the fraud in the restoration fund,* Emrys reminded themself. *She was nineteen.*

The report listed Serafin's effects as reported by her family: a coat, a satchel of books, her scholarship correspondence. Not listed—because the family didn't know she had it—was whatever she'd taken from the cathedral offices in her last week alive.

Emrys turned a page and found the next-of-kin line. *Brother: Rhys Vale, b. 1971.* An address in Marlowe's Cross, forty minutes by the slow bus. And beside it, in a different ink, a later annotation: *NKA — moved, no forwarding.*

No known address. But Emrys had spent twenty years learning how paper lied, and paper lied the same way people did: not by vanishing, but by going quiet. Rhys Vale had not vanished. He had simply stopped updating his file.

Emrys copied the address, the birthdate, and—because habit was a kind of prayer—the file number, into the ledger. Then, after a moment's thought, they copied the photograph too, in words: *Dark hair, blunt cut. Smile like a locked door she expects you to open yourself.*

The clerk looked up as Emrys returned the box. "Find what you needed?"

"No," Emrys said honestly. "But I found where to look."

### Vance

Idris Vance had a theory about brothers of missing girls, and it was not a kind one. In his experience—and he had eleven years of it, most of it spent in the unglamorous middle distances of other people's tragedies—the brother was either the first suspect or the last man standing, and the difference between the two was usually nothing more than whether he'd cried at the right press conference.

So when the Marlowe's Cross address resolved to a small terraced house with a workshop sign in the window—*V. VALE, PAPER & BINDING CONSERVATION, BY APPOINTMENT*—Vance sat in his car for a full four minutes and rehearsed the conversation like a man rehearsing a difficult toast.

The bell over the door was an actual bell, brass, older than Vance. Inside smelled of wheat starch and old glue and something faintly sweet, like the ghost of leather. A man was bent over a workbench under a lamp, tweezers in hand, coaxing a fragment of torn vellum back into alignment with the patience of a surgeon and the tenderness of a parent.

He didn't look up. "If you're the woman from the historical society, I told Margaret—Mrs. Fenwick—I told her the parish register can't be rushed. It's eighteenth century and it's been through a flood. It'll take what it takes."

"Detective Sergeant Vance," Vance said. "St. Vellum's inquiry."

The tweezers stopped.

For a long moment the man didn't move at all, and Vance watched the stillness travel through him like frost through a windowpane. Then he set the tweezers down with exaggerated care, the way you put down a thing you're afraid you might throw, and turned.

Rhys Vale was fifty-three and looked sixty, or perhaps he looked fifty-three and simply carried sixty years of a particular expression around with him. He had his sister's dark hair, gone gray at the temples in a way that suggested it had happened early and all at once. He had his sister's jaw. He did not have his sister's smile. Whatever door that smile had once been, it had been bricked over.

"You're investigating Tomas Pell," he said. "The boy in the baptistry. I read about it."

"I'm investigating several things."

"Then you'll want to know I restored the cathedral's manuscripts. Twelve years ago. You'll have found that out already, or the archivist will have—the new one. Halloran." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "Yes. I can see it. You've got the look of someone who's been talked at by Halloran. They do that. They make you feel like the interesting one and then you realize they've memorized your face for later."

Vance blinked. "You know Emrys Halloran?"

"I restored Corwin Ashgrove's private papers." Vale said it flatly, a fact laid on the table like a dead bird. "Twelve years ago. For six weeks, his personal archive sat on that bench you're dripping on. Every letter, every note, every—whatever else he kept. I bound three of his ledgers myself. New spines. He was very particular about the spines."

Vance felt the room tilt slightly, the way it did when a case quietly changed shape. "His ledgers."

"His ledgers." Vale took off his apron, folded it, set it down. "Sit down, Detective Sergeant. You're going to want to sit down, and I'm going to want to make tea, because I've been waiting twelve years for someone from the police to walk through that door, and I refuse to have this conversation standing up like it's an accident."

### Emrys

Emrys arrived forty minutes later to find Vance sitting on a workshop stool that was clearly not built for sitting, holding a cup of tea he hadn't drunk, wearing the expression of a man who has just learned the floor of his own house is hollow.

"You're late," Vance said, with relief.

"The bus was slow and I stopped to buy biscuits, because I've learned that grief and interrogation both go better with biscuits." Emrys set a paper bag on the workbench and surveyed the room—the lamp, the vellum, the man. "You're Rhys Vale."

"And you're the one who found her." It wasn't a question. Vale's voice had gone very quiet. "Or—no. Not found. You're the one who's been *looking.* Twenty years, and the police file never grew by so much as a comma, and then a boy drowns wearing someone else's clothes, and a detective starts pulling files that have been shut since I was thirty-three. Someone's been looking. I'd bet the workshop it's you."

Emrys set down their bag. "Why would you bet that?"

"Because I've read your ledger."

The workshop went very still. Even the lamp seemed to hold its breath.

"Carefully," Vale said, raising a hand. "Carefully, before you do whatever it is you're about to do. Twelve years ago I restored Corwin Ashgrove's private papers. Six weeks, his archive, my bench. I'm a conservator, Detective Sergeant—reading what passes through my hands is not a habit, it's the job. You can't mend a document you don't understand. And in the margins of one of those ledgers—his, the older one—I found annotations in a second hand. Small ones. Dates. Initials. A pressed lily, once, marking a page." He looked at Emrys. "Your hand. Your dates. Your initials. E.H. I've had twelve years to wonder who E.H. was, and why Corwin Ashgrove was keeping a second record of the same deaths he'd already closed in his own book—except his book said *accident* and yours said *question.*

"So no, I haven't read your ledger, not the physical object. But I've read its shadow. I know how you think, Emrys Halloran, because I've spent twelve years thinking the same way, using the same methods, because they were his methods. And I know—" his voice cracked, and he let it, with the discipline of a man who had practiced letting it crack precisely there, "—that whoever taught you to keep that ledger was the last person my sister ever met."

Emrys sat down slowly on the second stool. "Tell me about Serafin."

And Vale told them.

He told them about a girl who'd won a scholarship to study archival restoration at nineteen, who'd been so bright their mother used to say the house was warmer when Serafin was in it. He told them about the summer she worked on the cathedral's restoration project—the ledgers, the fund accounts, the contractors' invoices—and how she'd come home one evening in July with a folder of photocopies and an expression he'd never seen on her before. Not fear. *Certainty.* The certainty of someone who has found the loose thread and knows, absolutely, that the whole sweater will come apart if she pulls.

"She said the restoration fund was a sieve. She said someone senior was routing payments to a firm that didn't exist—shell company, double invoicing, the whole repertoire. She said she'd taken it to someone at the cathedral she trusted, and that he'd told her to leave it, that she didn't understand what she was dealing with." Vale's hands were flat on the workbench, pressing down as though the bench might otherwise float away. "She laughed when she told me. That's the thing I can't—she *laughed.* She said, 'Rhys, if I'm right, this goes all the way up, and if I'm wrong, I've made a fool of myself over arithmetic.' She wasn't afraid. She was nineteen and she was *right,* and being right made her feel immortal."

"When did you last see her?"

"July the fourteenth. She went back to meet him again—she wouldn't say who, said it wasn't fair to name him until she was sure. She never came home." He finally picked up his tea and drank it, cold, without noticing. "The police said runaway. The cathedral said nothing at all. My mother died still setting a place for her at the table. And I—" He stopped. Started again. "I became a conservator because of her. She's the one who loved old paper. I figured if I got good enough at it, someday her file would come across my bench. It never has. Instead his did."

"Corwin's," Emrys said.

"Corwin's." Vale looked at them both. "And here's what I need you to understand, because I can see you've already decided what I am, Detective Sergeant, and I'd like to correct the record while it's still cheap to do so. Yes, I read his papers. Yes, I learned the methods. Yes, for twelve years I have been following my sister's trail through the only map anyone ever made of it—the map inside that man's head, transcribed. I know about the bell-ringer. I know about the gardener. I know about the visiting scholar. I know Halloran solved all three while the county filed them under *weather* and *drink* and *heart failure.* I know the ledger better than its own author does, because I've had twelve years and a conservator's memory."

"Did you kill Tomas Pell?" Vance asked. No warmth in it, no apology.

"No."

"Where were you on the night of the fourteenth of this month?"

And here—Vance saw it, and Emrys saw Vance see it—Rhys Vale hesitated. Not long. A second, perhaps two. The hesitation of a man consulting a memory he does not like.

"I was at home," he said. "Alone. Working."

"Can anyone confirm that?"

"I'm a fifty-three-year-old man who mends books in a terraced house in Marlowe's Cross. No one can confirm anything I've done since 1997." He met Vance's eyes. "But I didn't kill that boy. And I'll tell you something else for free, since you've come all this way and the tea's terrible. Whoever did kill him wanted you looking at me. That staging—the clothes, the baptistry, the theater of it—that's not grief. I've had twenty years of grief and I've never once wanted an audience for it. That was someone *performing.* Someone who read the same files I did and wanted the story to end with the dead girl's brother in the dock."

Emrys opened the biscuit bag and set it on the bench between them, an offering, a truce, a small human thing in a room that had gone cold. "Mr. Vale," they said gently. "The marginal annotations you found. The second hand. Describe them for me exactly. The slant of the letters. The pressure. Everything you remember."

Vale frowned. "Why? You wrote them."

"Indulge me," Emrys said, and their voice had gone very quiet, the voice they used in the crypt, the voice that paper seemed to trust. "Because last night I reread my own ledger, and I need to know if the hand in your memory matches the hand in mine."

Vale described it—the slight backward lean of the E, the H with the high crossbar, the dates written in a cramped shorthand only Corwin had used. And as he spoke, Emrys felt something shift in their chest, cold and slow, like a stone turning over at the bottom of a well.

Because it wasn't their hand. It had never been their hand.

It was Corwin's. It had been Corwin's all along—and Corwin had been dead for a year.

"Mr. Vale," Emrys said, standing, gathering the ledger, keeping their voice level by an act of will that cost more than they'd ever admit. "When exactly did you last look at those marginal annotations?"

"Twelve years ago. Why?"

"Because," Emrys said, "I think someone has been writing in my book since before I ever opened it—and I think they're not finished yet."

Vance was already reaching for his coat. Outside, the light was going the color of old vellum, and somewhere across town, in a cathedral that had kept its secrets for twenty years, the statute clock ticked down to eight days.
