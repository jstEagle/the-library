## 12. An Invitation in Ash

There is a particular hour—half past four, when the cathedral offices empty and the light goes the color of weak tea—when Emrys Ashgrove does their best thinking. Not because the thinking is better then, but because it is finally permitted. All day the archivist belongs to the building: to researchers wanting the parish registers of 1887, to the dean wanting a document that will make his fundraising letters sing, to Mrs. Okonkwo from the gift shop wanting to know whether the cathedral has ever owned a stuffed owl (it had; 1974; regrettable taxidermy; Emrys had produced the invoice with the solemnity of a man presenting a relic).

But at half past four, Emrys belongs to the ledger.

They kept it at home, of course. That was the first rule Corwin Ashgrove had ever taught them, back when Emrys was a nervous volunteer alphabetizing burial records and Corwin was still alive and still, as far as Emrys knew, merely eccentric rather than monstrous.

"A record that lives where the public walks," Corwin had said, tapping the archive's reading room table, "is a record anyone can read. Keep your true work where only you can find it."

Emrys had taken this for professional wisdom. It was, they understood now, something closer to a confession.

So the ledger lived in the flat above the vestry, in the third drawer of the writing desk, beneath a false bottom made from the lid of a cigar box, beneath a folder labeled ELECTRICITY — MISC., which was the most boring phrase Emrys could construct, and Emrys was very good at boring phrases. It was a professional skill. Whole careers had been built on less.

Which meant that when Emrys came home on the evening after the lily, climbed the forty-one stairs, hung their coat on its hook, and opened the third drawer, the cold that went through them was not the ordinary October draft off the river.

The drawer was open two inches.

Emrys always closed it. They were an archivist; closing things was not a habit but a sacrament. They could count on one hand the times in six years the drawer had been left ajar, and each time had been their own fault, discovered within minutes, corrected with a small private penance.

They stood in the doorway of their own sitting room for a long moment, one hand still on the light switch, listening to the flat breathe. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, the verger's television murmured through the floor like a beehive dreaming. Nothing else moved.

"Right," Emrys said aloud, because saying *right* aloud was another ritual, a small flag planted against fear. "Right."

They crossed the room and pulled the drawer fully open.

The ledger sat where it always sat. Same position, same angle, spine toward the window. But the ribbon bookmark—which Emrys kept at the page of Tomas Pell, because you do not look away from the newest grief—had been moved. It lay now at a page near the back, a page that should have been blank.

Emrys did not touch it. First rule of archives: before you move a thing, you record how it lies. They took out their phone and photographed the book, the drawer, the desk, the room, hands steadier than they had any right to expect. Then, and only then, they lifted the ledger into the lamplight.

The entry was there. In their handwriting.

Not *like* their handwriting. *In* it. The small forward lean of the letters, the way the crossbar of the *t* drifted slightly upward like a bird taking off, the idiosyncratic loop on the descender of the *g* that a handwriting analyst had once, years ago, called "distinctive enough to hang a man." Someone had studied that hand until they could wear it like a coat.

*Fourth accident,* the entry read. *St. Vellum's crypt, inner vault. Friday, the 24th, eleven o'clock at night. Come alone. Bring the book.*

Below it, in the same borrowed hand, smaller:

*You taught me everything I know about noticing. Notice this.*

Emrys set the ledger down on the desk with exaggerated care, walked to the kitchen, filled the kettle, and watched it with the fixed attention of a person who needed very much to watch a kettle. Their reflection floated in the dark window over the sink: a thin, grey-templed figure in a cardigan the color of porridge, holding a mug they did not remember filling.

"You're in my home," they told the reflection. "You've been in my home. You've read my—" and here the sentence failed, because what the intruder had read was not a diary exactly, not a confession exactly, but the closest thing Emrys had to a self. Six years of deaths the world had mislabeled, catalogued in a hand that was, apparently, easy to steal.

The kettle boiled. Emrys made tea they would not drink. This too was ritual.

* * *

The question that kept them up—not pacing, pacing was undignified, but lying on the sofa in their coat because the bedroom suddenly felt like a stage set—was not *who*. The who would come. The question that kept them up was *when*, and worse, *how often*.

Because here was the thing Emrys could not stop turning over, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth: the killer had been inside the flat more than once. The clothes stolen from Emrys's closet during Corwin's funeral—that had required access. The ledger entry required access again. And between those two visits, nothing else had been touched. Not the silver, such as it was. Not the small tin of emergency biscuits on top of the fridge. Not the photograph of Corwin and young Emrys on the archive steps, Corwin mid-laugh, Emrys mid-blush, both holding the same enormous folio like a shared organ.

Nothing had been disturbed except the things the killer wished to speak through.

That was the terrible intimacy of it. A burglar takes. This visitor *left*. Had arranged. Had chosen the ribbon's new resting place deliberately, had written *bring the book* knowing Emrys would understand that the ledger itself—their life's secret work—was now a piece of correspondence, passed back and forth like notes in a classroom. Emrys wrote in it; the killer answered. A conversation conducted in a dead woman's borrowed voice and a living stranger's stolen hand.

"It's not intimacy," Emrys informed the ceiling. "It's violation wearing intimacy's coat." The ceiling declined to comment. The radiator ticked on, faithful as ever.

And yet some traitorous part of them, the part that had spent a lifetime invisible and had never entirely decided whether invisibility was armor or wound, noted that someone out there had read every page. Every case. The bell-ringer, whose death Emrys had proven was no fall but a push, quietly, without telling anyone, because the man's widow had suffered enough and the truth would have changed nothing except adding a trial. The gardener, poisoned by his own employer over a boundary dispute so petty Emrys had almost admired it. The visiting scholar, whose "heart attack" had followed a very specific argument about a forged charter. Each solved in ink, never in court. Each a small private justice that existed nowhere but in this book.

Someone had read all of it. Someone knew Emrys completely—or thought they did—and had found the knowledge useful enough to kill with.

"There are worse eulogies," Emrys said to the ceiling, and then, horrified at themselves, got up and put the kettle on again.

* * *

Friday morning, Emrys did not go to Vance.

This deserves explanation, because Emrys was aware it looked bad. A reasonable citizen, finding a threatening note in their own home, would telephone the police immediately. But Emrys had spent three days being interviewed by Detective Sergeant Idris Vance, and had learned two things about him: he missed nothing, and he forgave nothing. If Emrys reported the entry, Vance would ask why the ledger existed at all. He would ask about the bell-ringer, the gardener, the scholar. He would ask why Emrys had said nothing about any of it while a chorister lay drowned in borrowed clothes. And Emrys—who had spent twenty years under Corwin's tutelage learning to notice, and one week learning that noticing without acting was its own kind of crime—did not have answers that survived daylight.

Also—and Emrys admitted this to themselves with distaste—the note said *come alone*. And some stubborn, foolish, Corwin-trained corner of their mind wanted to know what happened next. Wanted to see the hand that wore theirs.

"No," Emrys said, buttoning their coat. "No, we are not doing that. We are going to tell Vance. We are going to tell him today, after work, like a responsible adult."

They told Vance on Saturday.

In fairness, there were intervening events. There was Mrs. Okonkwo and the matter of the 1974 owl, which had somehow escalated. There was the discovery that someone had also, at some point, entered the archive office and squared the papers on Emrys's desk—a thing no one noticed unless they were Emrys, but Emrys noticed, and the noticing felt like a hand briefly resting on the back of their neck. And there was the small matter of Emrys spending Thursday night sitting upright in an armchair facing the door with a heavy brass paperweight on their lap, feeling extremely silly, until dawn made silliness official.

By Saturday the arithmetic had changed. Eleven o'clock Friday week. Nine days. Whatever was planned for the crypt's inner vault—the vault sealed with a lock only Corwin could open, the vault Emrys had catalogued as an anomaly in the ledger's very first entry—would happen soon, and Emrys would not meet it unprepared or unaccompanied.

Vance came to the flat because Emrys asked him to, and because, Emrys suspected, he wanted to see the drawer himself. He stood in the middle of the sitting room with his hands in his pockets, taking in the porridge cardigan on its hook, the photograph of Corwin, the tin of emergency biscuits, with the expression of a man updating a file.

"Show me."

Emrys showed him. The drawer, the false bottom, the ledger, the entry. Vance read it twice. He did not touch the book—he'd brought gloves, of course he'd brought gloves—but he bent close, nose nearly to the page, and was silent for a long time.

"This is good," he said at last.

"I'm sorry?"

"The handwriting. It's genuinely good. I've seen forgers with thirty years inside who couldn't match the slant, let alone the—" he tapped the air above the *t*-crossbars, "—the lift. Whoever wrote this didn't trace it. They *wrote* it. Means practice. Means time. Means they've had samples of your hand for a while." He straightened up and looked at Emrys with something that was almost sympathy and almost accusation, and was in fact both at once, which Emrys was learning was simply Vance's face. "How long have you kept this book?"

"Six years."

"And in six years, how many people have seen it?"

"One," Emrys said. "Me."

"Then either you're lying, or your forger has been in this flat more than once, for long stretches, unhurried." Vance turned a slow circle, taking in the window over the sink, the door, the forty-one stairs. "Who has keys?"

"The verger has a master key to the vestry stair. Mrs.—the sacristan has one. The dean has one he's never used, I think. And Corwin had one, but Corwin is—" Emrys stopped.

"Corwin is dead," Vance finished. "Keys get copied. Doors get left unlocked." He looked at the ledger again, and his jaw worked, and then he said, in a different tone, quieter, the tone of a man setting down something heavy: "You know what this entry is, don't you? It's not a threat. Threats say *or else*. This says *come*. It's an invitation. Whoever wrote it wants you there Friday, wants you watching, wants—" he paused, choosing, "—wants an audience."

"Then perhaps I shouldn't come."

"Oh, you'll come." Vance said it mildly, terribly. "Because if you don't, they'll come to you, and next time it won't be ink. But you won't come *alone*, and you won't come *blind*, and you won't come the way they expect." He took out his notebook—an actual paper notebook, which endeared him to Emrys against all sense—and clicked his pen. "Now. Start from the beginning. The lock on that inner vault. Who else could open it besides a dead man?"

Emrys opened their mouth to say *no one*, and stopped, because the honest answer was longer than two words, and because somewhere in the last week they had begun to suspect that every honest answer in St. Vellum's Cathedral was longer than two words.

"That," they said, "is a story about my mentor. And I warn you, Sergeant—it doesn't end well for him."

Vance clicked his pen once more and settled onto the sofa like a man prepared to stay past midnight.

"Those," he said, "are the only stories worth the paperwork."

Outside, the light went the color of weak tea, and somewhere below them, in the dark under the crypt, behind a lock built for one dead man's hand, whatever waited for Friday waited too.
