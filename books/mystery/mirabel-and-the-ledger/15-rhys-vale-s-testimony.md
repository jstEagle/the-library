## 15. Rhys Vale's Testimony

*Emrys*

The interview room at the station had the particular charm of a place designed by someone who hated both furniture and hope: a table bolted to the floor, three chairs that had surrendered their cushioning sometime in the previous decade, and a window so high and narrow it might have been an apology for a window.

Rhys Vale sat on the far side of it with his hands folded, patient as a man waiting for glue to dry. He was perhaps sixty, with the long, careful fingers of someone who had spent a lifetime handling things older and more fragile than himself. There was paper dust in the creases of his knuckles. Emrys noticed such things; it was the whole trouble with them.

"You restored the manuscripts," Emrys said, taking the chair opposite. "At St. Vellum's. The Corvinus psalter, the dean's charters."

"Twelve years ago." Rhys's voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of a man accustomed to working in silence. "And four years ago, briefly, some private papers belonging to Archivist Ashgrove. Water damage. He wanted them stabilized before he donated them."

"He never donated them."

"No," Rhys agreed. "I gathered he changed his mind."

Vance stood by the door with his arms crossed, radiating the studied boredom of a man who believed every word out of everyone's mouth was a small crime. He'd insisted on this arrangement—Emrys present, officially as a consultant, unofficially because Vance had decided, with visible reluctance, that Emrys read people the way other people read weather.

"Mr. Vale," Vance said, "where were you on the night Tomas Pell died?"

"At my workshop. Working."

"Can anyone confirm that?"

"My workbench can. It doesn't gossip."

Emrys felt something twist—not fear exactly, but its cousin. Because Rhys said it pleasantly. He said everything pleasantly. And yet there was a stillness under the pleasantness, the stillness of a lake over something sunk.

"Tell us about your sister," Emrys said quietly.

And the stillness moved. It was like watching frost crack across glass—sudden, total, and revealing everything beneath.

* * *

*Rhys*

Serafin had been the clever one. Everyone said so, and they'd all meant it kindly, which somehow made it worse, because kindness from adults who were about to fail you has a particular taste to it, like sugar on medicine.

"She won a scholarship at sixteen," Rhys said. He found his hands had come apart and were doing something with each other, some old fidget he thought he'd buried. "Classics. She used to say the dead were better company than the living because they couldn't interrupt. She was joking. Mostly."

The archivist—the young one, Halloran, who watched him the way a lighthouse watches a coastline—leaned forward slightly. "She worked here? At the cathedral?"

"For the restoration fund. Cataloguing, transcribing. She was nineteen." He stopped. Started again. "She found something in the accounts. She wouldn't tell me what, exactly. She said it was 'a beautiful fraud'—her phrase—which tells you everything about Serafin. She wasn't angry about it. She was *delighted*. She thought she'd caught it, you see. Thought the world worked the way her books did, where the clever person finds the clue and the truth comes out and everyone claps."

"And then?" Vance asked. His voice had lost its boredom somewhere around *beautiful fraud*.

"Then she went to meet the archivist. Corwin Ashgrove. She trusted him—everyone did. He was famous for being decent. She told me, the morning she died—" his voice snagged on the word, and he let it hang there rather than pretend it hadn't, "—the morning she disappeared, she said, 'If I'm not back by supper, tell Father I've finally met a mystery worth my time.' She was laughing when she said it."

The room held very still. Even the radiator seemed embarrassed to tick.

"I reported her missing," Rhys said. "They called it a runaway. Nineteen-year-old girls run away, they said. She'll turn up, they said. My father searched until his heart gave out. My mother until hers followed. And I—" He looked down at his careful hands. "I learned patience. Twenty years of it. You can restore anything with enough patience. Vellum. Paper. A reputation. A trail."

"Had you been inside the cathedral crypt before last week?" Vance asked.

"No."

"Did you kill Tomas Pell?"

"No."

"Then why does your alibi have holes in it?"

Rhys looked up. For the first time, the pleasantness fell away entirely, and what was underneath was not menace but exhaustion—deep, geological, twenty years deep. "Because the truth is worse than a lie, Sergeant, and I've learned to prefer lies. They're lighter to carry."

* * *

*Vance*

Vance had interviewed forty-one murderers in his career, or forty-two if you counted Mrs. Okafor, which he privately did, though the magistrates had disagreed. He knew the taxonomy. There were the ones who performed grief, overselling it like market traders. There were the ones who showed nothing, which meant either sociopath or shock, and the difference took time to find. And there were the rare ones whose grief was real and whose guilt was also real, and those were the ones that kept him up at night, because sympathy was a door and killers knew how to knock on it.

Rhys Vale was knocking.

"So let's hear the truth, then," Vance said, "and we'll weigh it against the lie."

"The night Pell died, I left the workshop at nine. I came to the cathedral. I stood outside the baptistry door and I watched him rehearse alone—he stayed late sometimes, the boy liked the acoustics when nobody else was there—and I watched him for perhaps ten minutes, and then I left."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to hate him." Rhys said it flatly, without decoration. "He wore clothes that belonged to Mr. Halloran. I'd seen them before—I watch Mr. Halloran, the way I've watched everything connected to Corwin Ashgrove for twenty years. And I thought: here is a boy who is loved, who is given things, who walks into a cathedral and is welcomed. And I thought about Serafin walking into this same building at nineteen and never walking out. So I stood in the dark hating a child for being alive, and then I was ashamed of it, and I went home."

Vance glanced at Halloran. The archivist's face had gone the color of good vellum.

"That's not an alibi," Vance said. "That's a confession to loitering with intent to resent."

"It's the truth. Which is why I lied. Who admits to standing in a churchyard hating a drowned boy? Not any jury I'd want."

"And the clothes in your possession now," Vance pressed. "Tomas Pell's clothes. The ones taken from Mr. Halloran's closet. How do you explain having them?"

For the first time, Rhys hesitated. It was a small hesitation—a breath held half a second too long—but Vance had built a career on half-seconds.

"They came to me," Rhys said finally. "In a parcel. No note."

"When?"

"The day after the body was found."

"Who sent it?"

"I don't know."

"You didn't go to the police with a parcel containing a murdered boy's clothing?"

"I went to the cathedral instead. To return them. Quietly. I assumed—" He stopped, and Vance watched him decide, in real time, how much truth the room could hold. "I assumed whoever sent them wanted me found with them. I've spent twenty years learning how evidence behaves. Evidence is never innocent. It's always *placed*."

Halloran made a small sound. When Vance looked over, the archivist was staring at nothing, lips moving faintly, the way they did when a ledger entry was rearranging itself behind their eyes.

"Someone's framing you," Vance said.

"Someone is framing *someone*," Rhys corrected gently. "Whether it's me remains to be established. I'm aware I'm the obvious answer. Obvious answers are usually wrong in restoration work—you learn that early. The stain that looks like water damage is almost never water damage."

"What is it, then?" Vance asked. "Usually."

"Ink," Rhys said. "Someone trying to hide what they wrote."

* * *

*Emrys*

They walked out into the grey afternoon together, Vance and Emrys, past the noticeboard with its curling flyers for a bake sale and a bell-ringing recruitment drive that had clearly been optimistic.

"He's lying about something," Vance said. "The parcel, maybe. Maybe all of it."

"Yes."

"But you believe him about Serafin."

Emrys considered. The wind coming off the square smelled of rain and roasted chestnuts from the cart by the war memorial, and somewhere a busker was murdering a hymn with an accordion. The world, indifferent as ever, carrying on.

"I believe he loved her," Emrys said. "That part isn't performable. I've read enough false testimony to know the difference between a story someone has rehearsed and a wound someone hasn't. The parcel, though—" They frowned. "A parcel arrives the day after the body is found. Containing the clothes. Sent to the one man in the county with motive, means, and a grudge. That's not a frame-up, Detective. Frame-ups are quiet. This is a *summons*. Someone wants us looking at Rhys Vale, and they want us looking hard, and they want it now—"

"—before the statute runs out," Vance finished grimly. "Eleven days. Whoever killed Pell needs the case closed fast and closed wrong. Rhys convicted in the papers, if not the courts, and everyone goes home satisfied while Serafin Vale stays a runaway forever."

They walked a few steps in silence. Then Emrys said, slowly, the thought assembling itself as they spoke: "Detective. The parcel. Old banknotes paid to the verger. Clothes stolen during Corwin's funeral. Someone who moves through this cathedral like they belong to it. Someone who knows the files well enough to stage from them, and knows *me* well enough to know I'd notice."

"You've got a name forming," Vance said. "I can see it. Say it."

But Emrys shook their head, because the name forming wasn't a name at all—it was a shape, a habit of mind, a way of leaving traces that only another keeper of ledgers would recognize. And the shape was horribly familiar. It fit a hand they had held. It fit a voice that had taught them to catalog what others overlook.

"Not yet," Emrys said. "First I need to reread the ledger. All of it. There's something in the margins I've been reading wrong."

Vance gave them a look that in a less restrained man would have been an eye-roll. "The margins."

"The margins," Emrys confirmed, already turning toward the cathedral, toward the dark and the shelves and the book that had begun, without permission, to answer back. "Everything important in that building lives in the margins, Detective. Corwin taught me that."

It occurred to them only later, halfway across the square, that they had just said *Corwin taught me that* without flinching—and that the lesson itself might be the cruelest thing he'd ever left behind.
