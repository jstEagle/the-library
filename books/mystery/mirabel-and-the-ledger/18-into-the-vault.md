## 18. Into the Vault

**Emrys**

The key had been in front of me for twenty years, which was, in fairness, exactly the sort of place I would have put it.

Corwin's last cipher had decoded to a single sentence, written in his careful archivist's hand: *The vault opens with the ledger's first word, and the hour of her name.* The ledger's first word was *Serafin*—I had written it there myself, in the first entry I ever made, before I understood what I was recording. And the hour of her name. Serafin. I had spent an entire evening turning that over before I saw it: the cathedral's bells rang the Angelus at noon and six, and the six o'clock peal began, as it had for a century, with the note the old ringers called *Sera*—the cracked bell in the north tower, the one Corwin had refused for years to have recast. *Sera-fin.* The finish of Sera. Six o'clock.

So at ten minutes to six on the second-to-last day of the statute, I stood in the crypt with a lantern, a crowbar I hoped I wouldn't need, and Detective Sergeant Idris Vance, who was pretending he wasn't nervous by inspecting the mortar between the stones as though it owed him money.

"You're sure about this," he said. It wasn't a question so much as a man rehearsing his doubts aloud so they'd behave themselves.

"The cipher was unambiguous."

"The cipher was written by a man confessing to manslaughter through a riddle. Forgive me if I'd have preferred a signed statement and a solicitor."

"He was an archivist," I said. "We don't sign things. We *file* them."

Vance gave me a look that had, over the past three weeks, evolved from suspicion into something I was almost willing to call affection, the way a stone evolves into something almost willing to call a cushion. "If there's a body down there, this becomes a crime scene. If there isn't, this becomes the most elaborate practical joke in the history of the diocese, and I arrest you for wasting police time. Either way, one of us is having a very bad evening."

"Corwin never joked," I said. "It was his single failing as a human being."

The lock on the inner vault was a thing of beauty, which I had always resented. Brass and iron, older than the cathedral's last restoration, with a mechanism Corwin had once told me—over tea, in the offhand way he dispensed everything important—was "a little puzzle for whoever deserves it." I had spent years assuming I didn't deserve it. Now I understood he'd meant something quite different. He'd meant *whoever reads the ledger the way I taught them to.*

There was no keyhole. There was a dial, and around the dial's rim, in letters worn nearly smooth, a word: *INCIPIAT.* Begin.

I set the ledger on a crate, opened it to the first page, and turned the dial letter by letter. S-E-R-A-F-I-N. The mechanism inside made a sound like an old man clearing his throat before a difficult conversation. Then nothing.

"The hour," Vance said. "You said the hour of her name."

I looked at my watch. Two minutes to six. Above us, muffled by stone and earth, the cracked bell began to speak—*Sera*, the flat, mournful note, once, twice, three times—and on the third stroke, deep in the door, something released with a click I felt in my teeth rather than heard.

The door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently. Recently. I noticed that the way I noticed everything, the habit Corwin had built into me brick by brick, and I noted it in the ledger of my own mind with a small, cold asterisk: *someone has been here.*

"Emrys," Vance said quietly, and put a hand on my shoulder. "Let me go first."

For once in my life, I let someone.

* * *

**Vance**

The vault was small—no more than eight feet by ten—and dry, which told me someone had maintained it. Dry stone, dry air, a shelf along one wall. On the shelf: a wooden box, long and narrow, and beside it a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that I recognized before Emrys said a word, because I'd spent three weeks staring at photographs of it.

The clothes. Tomas Pell had died in these clothes. The navy jumper, the grey trousers, the shoes with the scuffed left toe. Here they were, folded with a care that made my skin crawl, sitting in a locked crypt under a cathedral, two feet from what was very likely a second body.

And sitting on the crate beside the box—not hiding, not crouched in ambush, just *sitting*, the way a man sits in a church when he's run out of anywhere else to be—was Rhys Vale.

"Detective Sergeant," he said. His voice was wrecked. He'd been crying for a while, I thought, the way you can tell in a man who doesn't do it often: the redness gone past, the voice sanded down to gravel. "I was hoping you'd come. Both of you. I've been waiting since yesterday."

"Stand up slowly," I said, and Emrys made a small sound beside me—not disagreement, exactly. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Two people who had spent their lives reading rooms had both read this one at the same moment, and the room said: *no danger here. Only the end of something.*

Rhys stood up slowly. His hands were empty. He nodded at the oilcloth bundle. "I brought them back. I should have brought them back weeks ago. I know what it looks like. I know what it *was*. I took them from your closet, Mr. Ashgrove, at the funeral, and I have hated myself for it every day since, and I did it anyway, because I needed the police to open the vault, and I needed them to open it *before Thursday*."

"The statute," Emrys said. Not a question.

"The statute." Rhys laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh. "Twenty years I've been chasing my sister. Twenty years. And the one thing I could never outrun was a calendar."

* * *

**Emrys**

He told us the whole of it there in the vault, with Serafin's box between us and the door, and I will set it down as he told it because he has earned at least that much.

He had been nineteen when she disappeared. The police had closed the file in eleven days—*runaway*, the word that closes more cases than any other—and his mother had died inside three years of not believing it, and Rhys had spent the rest of his life doing what I did, only without a cathedral to hide in. He'd followed the restoration fund. He'd found the fraud—she'd found it first, in the last weeks of her life, and had told him, in the way of sisters, half-boast and half-secret, that she was going to *fix* it. He'd traced her last day to this building, to this man Corwin Ashgrove, and he'd gotten himself hired as the conservator who restored the manuscripts precisely because the conservator gets to read everything.

"I didn't know what I was looking for," he said. "Only that it would be in his papers. People hide things in their papers. It's the one honest thing about dishonest people." He looked at me. "Then I found the margins. Someone had been studying him the way I was studying him. Annotations in the files. Dates cross-checked. A whole invisible mind at work. I thought—I swear to you, I thought for months that *Corwin* had a second self, some archivist-ghost keeping watch on his own crimes."

"It was me," I said. "The annotations were mine."

"I know that now." He passed a hand over his face. "I read your ledger, Mr. Ashgrove. I'm not going to pretend I didn't—I picked the lock of your office the second week of November and I have regretted it and relied on it in equal measure ever since. The bell-ringer. The gardener. The scholar. Three deaths the police called accidents, solved by a person who never told a soul. And I thought: *there it is. That's how it's done.* You solved them with patience and paperwork and the assumption that everyone lies about something small. So when I needed the vault opened—when I found Corwin's confession fragment and knew where she was but couldn't prove it, couldn't get a warrant, couldn't get *anyone* to listen before the statute ran out—I used your methods. His methods. They were always his methods, weren't they? He taught you. I just read the homework."

The clothes. The staging. The drowned boy in my own garments, arranged so precisely that even Vance—who trusts nothing, least of all precision—had been pulled along the grooves of the thing. It had been a message written in a language only two people could read, and I was one of them.

"You let us think it was you," I said. "The false alibi. The old banknotes to the verger. You built yourself into the frame."

"Because a suspect gets investigated, and an investigation gets warrants, and warrants get you into the vault eventually." He shook his head. "It was working. It was working too slowly. So I wrote the fourth entry myself, in your hand as best I could manage, and gave you the date and the door, because I thought—if you're half of what the ledger says you are, you'll solve it faster from the inside."

"You used a dead boy's death," Vance said, and his voice had gone flat and hard, the voice I'd heard him use exactly once before. "Tomas Pell. Nineteen years old. You turned a child's drowning into a stage prop."

And here Rhys Vale did the thing that I will remember for the rest of my life, which is that he did not flinch, and he did not excuse himself, and he did not say the words *I had no choice*. He said, "I believe someone else killed him. I believe someone used my trap and put a boy in it. And I believe that because I have asked myself every hour since Tuesday whether I could have done it, and the answer is that I could not, and I have never been so grateful for my own smallness of soul."

"The verger's payment," Vance said. "The old banknotes."

"Mine. I paid him to leave the door unlocked. That part is true and I'll swear to it. But I never touched Tomas Pell, and I never asked anyone to, and the clothes—" his voice broke, finally, on the word, and he steadied it the way you steady a ladder, with both hands and your whole weight— "the clothes I took from a closet, not from a boy. Someone else put them on him. Someone who wanted you to find me, Detective. Someone who *needed* the statute to expire with Serafin still a runaway. Ask yourself who."

I was already asking myself. I had been asking since I noticed the oiled hinges.

* * *

**Vance**

I opened the box.

I've opened a lot of boxes in twenty-two years of police work and I will tell you the truth, which is that it never becomes routine, and I have stopped wishing it would. Inside, wrapped in linen that someone had chosen with care, were the remains of a young woman, and beside them—placed there deliberately, I'd stake my career on it—was a small brass plaque, engraved in an archivist's hand:

*SERAFIN VALE. 1979–2001. SHE WAS RIGHT.*

Not *she was murdered*. Not *forgive me*. *She was right.* Corwin Ashgrove had sat with his victim's bones for twenty years and the epitaph he chose was the fact of her discovery. Emrys read it and made a sound I will not describe, because it wasn't mine to hear, and I turned my back and gave them both the room.

Rhys knelt by the box and put his forehead against its edge, and for a long time the only sound in the vault was the cathedral above us settling into evening, stone ticking as it cooled, the building keeping its own time.

When he could speak, he said, "Thursday. Tomorrow at midnight it expires. Twenty years, and she goes back to being nobody's daughter, nobody's sister, a file with a rubber stamp on it." He looked up at me, and his eyes were terrible and clear. "So here is what I'm asking, and I'll ask it on my knees if it helps. Don't arrest me for the staging tonight. Arrest me tomorrow, after. Give me one day to see her case *opened*. I've waited twenty years. I can wait one more day. But if you take me now, the story ends with me, and she stays a runaway forever."

And I would like to report that I said something procedural and correct. What I actually did was look at Emrys, because somewhere in the last three weeks the two of us had developed the bad habit of deciding things together, and Emrys was staring at the plaque with an expression I'd learned to read as *there is a fact here we have not found yet.*

"Rhys," Emrys said slowly. "The verger. You paid him in old banknotes."

"Yes."

"When?"

"The Sunday after Corwin's funeral. The ninth."

"The clothes were taken at the funeral," Emrys said. "Forensics confirmed it. You took them at the funeral. You paid the verger two days later. Both true." He turned, and I watched the whole architecture of it rise behind his eyes. "But Rhys—the fourth entry. The invitation in my ledger. You wrote it the night of the eleventh."

"I did."

"The verger says someone came to him on the *twelfth* and asked him again about the crypt door. Asked whether he'd locked it that night. A second person, checking. He thought it was you, coming back. He described a tall man in a cassock." Emrys's voice had gone very quiet, the way it goes when he's found the loose thread and is afraid to pull it in case the whole garment comes away. "You don't wear a cassock, Rhys."

"No," Rhys said. "I don't."

The three of us stood in the vault, in the lamplight, around the bones of a girl who had been right about everything, and above us the cracked bell rang the quarter hour—*Sera*, flat and mournful—and I watched Emrys Ashgrove arrive at the thought that I was already halfway to myself.

Someone else had read the ledger. Someone else had walked the trap after them and made sure the door was open and put a boy in borrowed clothes, and they had done it wearing a cassock, in a cathedral where only a handful of men were entitled to wear one.

The dean had been at Corwin's funeral. The dean had signed the restoration accounts for twenty years. The dean had everything to lose on Thursday at midnight, and one day left to lose it in.

"Vance," Emrys said, "how do you feel about working through the night?"

I checked my watch. "I feel," I said, "like we've got about thirty-one hours."
