## 20. Ledger Against Ledger

*Emrys*

There is a particular kind of lie that requires more care than the truth. The truth, after all, has been sitting in my ledger for years, patient as a stone. A lie must be built from scratch, and it must be built to bear weight.

I sat at my desk in the archivist's office—the office Corwin Ashgrove had kept before me, with its green-shaded lamp and its smell of vellum and old glue—and I prepared to commit forgery.

Not of a document. Of an *entry*. Which is, in the trade of lies, something rather more intimate. An entry in my ledger carries my habits in it: the way I date things first and reason second, the small cross I make when a fact is corroborated, the way I write *see also* when one thread touches another. Anyone who had studied me long enough to steal clothes from my closet and stage a murder from my pages would know these habits. So the false entry had to be perfect in all the ways that mattered—and imperfect in exactly one.

That imperfection was the hook.

"Say it back to me," Vance said from the doorway, arms crossed, looking like a man who had eaten something that disagreed with him. He'd been disagreeing with this plan for two days. "Slowly."

"The entry will suggest that Corwin hid evidence in the inner vault," I said. "Specifically, evidence concerning the restoration fund. Dated entries, financial records, correspondence. Enough to end a career and open a prosecution."

"And why would our killer believe it?"

"Because it's what *they* would do." I turned the ledger toward him. "Look at how they've operated. Tomas dressed in my clothes—evidence pointing at me. The verger paid in old banknotes—evidence pointing backward in time. Whoever we're dealing with doesn't destroy evidence. They *curate* it. They leave trails they've composed. So when they read an entry saying Corwin left a box of documents in the vault, they won't think *trap*. They'll think *of course he did*. Hoarding is what archivists do. Concealment is what Corwin did. It's the most believable sentence ever written about that man."

Vance came closer and looked down at the page without touching it, the way he looked at everything in my office—as though it might be armed.

"And the flaw you're leaving in?"

"Here." I tapped the date line. "The entry is dated three days ago. But the ink—" I held up the pen I'd used, a cheap ballpoint with blue-black ink that sat on top of paper instead of sinking into it. "This ink is less than forty-eight hours old. Under magnification, anyone who knows paper could tell. And the dean, if it's the dean, has spent twenty years around restoration documents. He'll know."

"So you're giving him a reason to come tonight," Vance said slowly, "instead of a reason to stay away."

"I'm giving him a reason to come *hurriedly*. If the entry is fresh, then the vault was opened recently—which means the evidence might not have been found by anyone else yet. Fresh ink means fresh opportunity. He can't wait for the statute to expire and tidy up at leisure. He has to come now, tonight, while he believes the box is sitting there waiting to be discovered by someone with a conscience."

"Which you are," Vance said dryly, "famously not."

"I'm famously invisible," I corrected. "It's different. Invisible people are never suspected of anything, which is precisely why I've spent thirty years being underestimated, and precisely why it's going to work."

He grunted. From Vance, a grunt was a round of applause.

We had spent the afternoon composing the entry together, which was itself an education. Vance thought like a policeman: motive, opportunity, chain of custody. I thought like an archivist: provenance, condition, cross-reference. The forged entry needed both. It cited a "correspondence file, C.A., undated but internal references place it 2003–2004"—the years Serafin Vale's fraud discovery would have ripened into danger. It mentioned a strongbox "under the third shelf, north wall, beneath the misfiled diocesan returns." It even included one of my little crosses next to a note that the dean's signature appeared twice in the correspondence.

"That last bit's cruel," Vance had said, almost admiring.

"It's necessary. Guilt needs a name attached or it won't move."

Now, as evening settled over the cathedral and the lamps along the cloister came on one by one, I made the final adjustment. I wrote the entry in my own hand, because it had to be my hand—I was the only person whose ledger it plausibly was—but I wrote it slightly faster than I usually write, with the small impatience of a man recording something urgent. Then I left the ledger out on my desk, closed but not locked, exactly as careless as I have never once been in my life.

It felt like leaving my front door open in a storm.

"You'll be in the crypt?" Vance asked.

"Behind the columbarium wall. There's a recess where the memorial plaques are stored before installation. I can see the vault door and the stairs."

"And I'll be behind the vestry screen. Two officers outside, plainclothes, at the chapter house door. Nobody comes in or out without us knowing." He paused. "Emrys. If he brings anything—a crowbar, a blade, anything—you do not move. You are bait with a pulse, nothing more. Say it."

"I am bait with a pulse."

"Again, with feeling."

"I am bait," I said, "with a pulse, and a profound appreciation for remaining so."

He almost smiled. It was, I reflected, remarkable how far we had come from the man who had stood over Tomas Pell's body and looked at me as though I were the most interesting suspect he'd ever met. Suspicion, given enough time and enough shared secrets, ferments into something like trust. Or perhaps we had simply learned each other's habits the way one learns a colleague's handwriting—well enough to forge it, well enough to forgive it.

"One more thing," I said, as he reached the door. "The recorder."

"On the shelf above the misfiled diocesan returns. Battery good for nine hours. It picks up everything within twenty feet." He tapped his ear. "And I'll have mine. Between the two of us, the man couldn't sneeze without documentation."

After he left, I sat alone for a moment with the lamp green above the open ledger, and I spoke to Corwin the way I sometimes did, silently, in the grammar of old habit.

*You taught me that every record is a promise,* I thought. *Tonight I'm breaking one on purpose. I hope you'd understand that some promises are made to be broken by the right person.*

The ledger lay open on the desk, my forgery gleaming wetly under the lamp. Somewhere in the cathedral, a door closed. Footsteps crossed the cloister—light ones, unhurried, belonging to no one I knew.

Or perhaps belonging to no one at all. Cathedrals creak and settle at night like old ships. I had told myself that for years.

I put out the lamp and went down to keep my appointment with the dark.

* * *

*Vance*

The thing nobody tells you about stakeouts in a cathedral is that God provides excellent acoustics and terrible seating.

I'd wedged myself behind the vestry screen at ten past eleven, and by half past my knees had filed a formal complaint. The screen was carved oak, nineteenth century, with saints on it who regarded me with expressions I chose to interpret as encouragement. Through the gaps between their haloes I could see the mouth of the stair down to the crypt, a black rectangle at the far end of the ambulatory. Emrys had gone down an hour ago, quiet as dust settling, and now there was nothing to do but wait and listen to the building breathe.

Cathedrals breathe. That's not poetry; it's physics. Stone takes the day's heat and gives it back slowly, timbers shift, and somewhere overhead a bell swings a half-inch in the wind and knocks against its fittings like a spoon against a pot. I'd grown up in a terrace house where you could hear the neighbors' telly through the wall, and I'd thought I understood noise. St. Vellum's taught me there was a difference between noise and sound. Sound had intention.

At quarter past midnight, the side door off the cloister opened.

I hadn't heard a key. Later, forensics confirmed what I already suspected: Prendergast had a copy of the cloister key cut years ago, back when he was still rising, back when convenience mattered more than caution. Powerful men collect keys the way other men collect regrets—one at a time, each one seeming reasonable at the moment.

His shoes were rubber-soled. That told me plenty too. You don't wear rubber soles to a prayer meeting. You wear them when you plan to be unheard.

He crossed the ambulatory in the dark with the confidence of a man walking his own hallway, which in fairness he had done for eleven years. Dean Marcus Prendergast. Sixty-one years old, a career built on fundraising galas and restored stonework and the phrase *responsible stewardship*, and underneath it all, according to the file Emrys and I had assembled across two kitchen tables and one very long evening, a hole in the restoration fund the size of a human life.

He stopped at the head of the crypt stairs.

For a full minute he stood there, and I confess my heart did something unprofessional. Because in that minute, any sensible man turns back. Any sensible man thinks: *this is absurd, I am the dean of a cathedral, I am standing in the dark with a stolen key preparing to burgle my own crypt.* The whole architecture of his life should have collapsed under him right there.

Instead, he took out a torch, shaded it with his hand, and went down.

I gave him ninety seconds. Long enough to reach the vault corridor. Short enough that he wouldn't be deep into whatever he intended. Then I moved, saints watching me go, and took the stairs myself, keeping to the wall where the steps don't announce you.

The crypt at night is a country of its own. The emergency lighting painted everything the color of weak tea. I came around the corner of the columbarium wall and saw him at the vault door—Prendergast, in a dark overcoat, kneeling with the torch in his teeth, working at the lock with a thin strip of metal. Not panicking. Methodical. A man who had researched locks the way he researched everything: just enough to be dangerous.

Beyond him, half-hidden in the recess behind the memorial plaques, I could see the edge of Emrys's shoulder. Still. Patient. Bait with a pulse, holding position.

Prendergast got the outer door open—it wasn't the famous lock, just the corridor gate—and slipped inside to the inner vault. And here was the beautiful, terrible thing: the inner vault's legendary lock, the one only Corwin could open, had been standing unlocked since the night Emrys opened it and found Serafin Vale. We'd relocked it, of course, for appearances. But Prendergast didn't know the lock's history. He assumed it would fight him. When it gave at the first turn of the pick, I watched something go through his posture—not relief. Recognition. As if the ease of it confirmed something he'd suspected for twenty years.

*Corwin kept it open,* he must have thought. *Corwin always meant to come back to it.*

People build stories around evidence the way water builds channels around stones. Give them a shape and they'll pour themselves into it.

He knelt at the third shelf, north wall. Moved the misfiled diocesan returns—actual returns, actual misfiles, because Emrys insists authenticity matters even in a lie—and lifted out the strongbox we'd placed there that afternoon. A deed box, brass-cornered, bought from an antique shop for twelve pounds. Inside: printed correspondence, aged overnight in Emrys's oven at low heat, bearing the dean's own signature beside figures that would curdle a bishop.

Prendergast set it on the floor, opened it, and read.

I stepped out of the dark.

"Evening, Dean."

He didn't startle, which surprised me until I understood: he'd been expecting this moment for twenty years. Some other version of it, anyway. His face went through surprise, calculation, and arrived at a kind of weary dignity, the expression of a man deciding which of his faces to wear.

"Sergeant Vance." He closed the box calmly. "This is a private visit. I trust the cathedral's security arrangements haven't deteriorated so far that—"

"There's a recorder running," I said. "Has been since you picked that lock. It's picked up your voice, your torch, and your breathing, and in about four minutes it will pick up whatever you decide to say next. Choose carefully."

His eyes flicked to the columbarium recess. "Is Ashgrove back there? Of course he is. The little ghost." Something in his voice changed—not fear, exactly. Contempt, worn smooth by years of never needing to hide it. "You know, for thirty years I walked past that man in the cloister and never once thought of him. That was always the joke of it. We keep the archivists like we keep the mice. Quiet, useful, invisible."

"He heard that," Emrys said, stepping out of the recess.

I'll give the dean this: he didn't flinch. But his hands, holding that box, tightened until the brass corners bit white lines into his fingers.

"Mr. Ashgrove. Or should I say Miss—or is it—" He smiled thinly. "Forgive me. I've never bothered to learn."

"No," Emrys said, in that mild voice that I had learned, over these weeks, to hear the steel underneath. "You never bothered to learn anything about the people below you. That's rather the point. Serafin Vale learned about you, though. She learned about the fund, and the invoices, and the company your brother-in-law owned. She was nineteen, Dean. She brought her findings to Corwin Ashgrove because she trusted the church, and he sent her away, and she came back, and—" Emrys stopped. Steadied. "You know what happened. You've known for twenty years. You've said the offices for her disappearance. You lit candles for her."

"She was a runaway," Prendergast said. The words came out smooth, rehearsed, a sermon fragment. "A troubled girl from a troubled family. I prayed for her then and I pray for her now."

"Then pray loudly," I said, "because the recorder's getting all of it."

And that's when it happened—the moment I'll be writing up in my report for weeks, the moment I already know will define this case. Prendergast looked at the box in his hands, at me, at Emrys, and I watched him do the arithmetic. Recorder or no recorder, he was a powerful man with lawyers and friends and twenty years of practice at making trouble disappear. One conversation in a crypt, however recorded, was survivable. He'd survived worse. He'd survived *her*.

So he smiled, and set the box down gently, like a man returning a borrowed book, and said:

"Sergeant, whatever fiction Mr. Ashgrove has written into that little diary of theirs, you'll find no court in this county gives it five minutes. Forged entries, planted boxes, a disgraced archivist with an obvious grievance. I almost admire it. It's very nearly competent." He straightened his coat. "Now. Unless you're arresting me—and do check with your superintendent before you try—I'll say goodnight."

He walked toward the stairs. Past me. Past Emrys, close enough that I saw Emrys's hands curl and then deliberately flatten.

And Emrys let him go.

That was the plan—we'd agreed, argued, agreed again—but watching it happen was like watching a fish swim back into deep water. At the foot of the stairs Prendergast paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back down the length of the crypt at the two of us standing among the dead with our little box and our little machine.

"Oh, and Sergeant," he called softly. "Do give my regards to Rhys Vale. I understand grief makes people do extraordinary things. Perhaps he'll confess to something before Thursday. It would save everyone such unpleasantness."

Then he was gone, footsteps fading up the stairs, the side door closing behind him with a click as gentle as a lid.

The crypt went quiet. The tea-colored light hummed. After a moment, Emrys exhaled, long and slow, like someone setting down something heavy.

"'Before Thursday,'" Emrys repeated. "He knows the statute expires Thursday. He's telling us he knows we know."

"He's telling us he thinks it doesn't matter," I said. "That whatever we've got dies with the deadline." I looked at the strongbox on the floor, the forged signatures inside, the whole elaborate trap sprung on a man who'd simply stepped over it and kept walking. "Emrys. Talk to me. Did we just lose?"

But Emrys was smiling. It was a small smile, tired and absolutely certain, the smile of a person holding a card they'd been keeping face-down for days.

"No, Sergeant. We just learned something worth more than an arrest." Emrys picked up the recorder, pressed stop, and held it a moment in both hands, the way you'd hold a bird. "He came for the box. He touched it. He opened it and read his own name in it and *put it down*—which means he believes the real evidence, whatever Corwin actually kept, is still somewhere he hasn't checked. And he told us his deadline is Thursday, which means Thursday is when he stops being careful."

"Stops being careful how?"

Emrys looked up the stairs, toward the nave, toward the great dark vaulting where the cathedral kept its secrets the way the ledger kept its entries.

"Tomorrow," Emrys said, "we find out what Corwin's cipher says about where he really hid his confession. Because if the dean is coming back for it before Thursday—"

The emergency light flickered, steadied.

"—then we have one day to get there first."
