## 21. The Statute Expires

The clock in the Chapter House had been wrong for as long as I had worked at St. Vellum's—seven minutes fast, a fact I had recorded in my ledger on my first day and never corrected, because there is something useful about a clock that lies to you gently. Tonight it said eleven minutes past midnight, which meant it was actually four minutes past, which meant we had twenty hours and some change left before the statute of limitations closed over Serafin Vale's death like water over a stone.

I sat in the dark of the crypt antechamber with a cup of tea that had gone cold an hour ago, and I waited for Dean Ambrose Fennick to come destroy his own history.

He did not disappoint me. He never had. That was rather the whole tragedy of the man.

* * *

It had taken three days to build the trap, and the building of it had been the strangest work of my life, because for once I was not recording the truth but manufacturing it. Vance had been uneasy about it from the start.

"I've spent twenty years putting people away for lying," he'd said, standing in my office with his coat already on, which was how he stood whenever he disapproved of something but intended to do it anyway. "Now you want me to lie them into confessing."

"Not lie," I'd said. "Curate. There's a difference. A ledger is only ever a selection of what's true. We're simply selecting very carefully."

"That's exactly what a liar would say."

"Yes," I'd agreed pleasantly. "It's also exactly what an archivist would say. The professions overlap more than either would like to admit."

He had glared at me, then sat back down, which from Idris Vance constituted a signed confession of agreement.

The fabrication itself had been delicate work. I wrote a new entry in my own hand—a skill I had, these past weeks, learned to fear—and dated it convincingly, describing how Corwin Ashgrove had hidden "the original documents" beneath the floor of the inner vault: correspondence, bank records, a letter naming names. I described the dean's fraud in terms specific enough to terrify and vague enough to be deniable. Then I let the entry be seen.

That last part was the true artistry, and it belonged to Rhys Vale, of all people. He came to my office on the second day, pale and hollow-eyed, still under suspicion for Tomas Pell's murder, and he said, "You need someone he trusts to see it. He'll never believe your book. But he believes in books. It's why he killed her." His voice cracked on the last word. "Let me be the one who finds it."

"You shouldn't be anywhere near this," I said.

"Neither should you, by any sane measure." He almost smiled—the first time I'd seen him do it, and it transformed his face into something startlingly like Serafin's, if the photograph in Corwin's cipher could be believed. "She would have wanted to be in the room for this. Let me stand where she can't."

I let him. I have made worse decisions. I am not certain I have made many better ones.

So Rhys "discovered" the entry while cataloguing in my office with the door conspicuously ajar, and he was seen to leave in visible agitation, and by evening the dean had requested an emergency meeting of the finance committee, and by nightfall he had stopped sleeping, if the light burning in the deanery window meant anything, and it always had.

* * *

Now it was past midnight and the dean came down the crypt stairs carrying a torch and a crowbar, wearing a dressing gown over his clothes like a man who had told himself this wasn't really happening and had almost convinced himself.

I switched on the lamp.

"Good evening, Dean," I said. "Or good morning, technically. The clock up top runs seven minutes fast, so don't trust it."

He made a sound like a hinge. The crowbar dipped. Behind him, at the top of the stairs, Vance stepped out of the shadow of the pillar where no one had bothered to look, because no one ever looks at pillars; that is the first lesson of archives and the last lesson of stakeouts.

"Dean Fennick," Vance said. "Fancy meeting you here. With a crowbar. At one in the morning. In a sealed vault."

"I—I heard noises," the dean said. "Intruders. I came to investigate."

"In a cashmere dressing gown," I said. "With a crowbar you took from the gardener's shed, third hook from the left. You'll want to put it back before Mr. Osei notices; he counts his tools. Everyone here keeps ledgers, Dean. Even the gardeners. Especially the gardeners."

Something went out of him then—not all at once, but the way a candle goes out when the wax is spent, guttering twice before the dark takes it. He set the crowbar against the wall with exaggerated care, as though tidiness might still save him, and lowered himself onto the step as if his legs had filed their resignation.

"You were going to find it eventually," he said. "The old man's little hoard. I've known for twenty years that it existed."

"Known?" Vance moved down the stairs, notebook out. "Say more about that. Slowly."

And the dean, who had spent thirty years teaching boys to recite Latin and forty years managing money that was not entirely his, began to talk. I think he had been waiting to talk for two decades. I think the talking was almost a relief. Guilt is a debt too, and debts compound, and his had reached the point where the interest alone was crushing him nightly.

"Serafin Vale came to me in the spring of—" he paused, and I supplied it.

"2003. April. The seventeenth, according to the restoration fund's minute book. You struck her question from the minutes. I've read the originals."

He stared at me. "How—"

"It's what I do," I said. "It's what I've always done. Someone has to write down what everyone else agrees to overlook."

"She found the discrepancies," the dean went on, as if I hadn't spoken, as if the words had been queuing behind his teeth for twenty years and now shoved forward all at once. "Small ones. Rounding errors. Payments to contractors who didn't exist. She was clever—brilliant, really, the way she'd traced the shell companies—and she came to me first, out of respect, she said. Respect." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "I offered to fix it quietly. She said no. She said the cathedral belonged to the poor of the parish as much as to anyone, and she was going to report it. She was going to go to the police on Monday."

"And?" Vance said, though we both knew.

"And I went to Corwin Ashgrove." The dean's hands turned over in his lap, palms up, an unconscious gesture of appeal—to us, to God, to the dark. "I thought he'd handle her. Reason with her. He was the archivist; he knew everything about everyone; I assumed he'd know how to make a frightened girl feel safe. I told him where she'd be Sunday evening—she walked the river path after evensong, always alone. I only meant for him to frighten her. To remind her what families endure when scandals break. I swear that's all I meant."

"But he panicked," I said quietly. "And so did she. And there was water nearby, and ice, and a girl who slipped—or was pushed, it hardly matters now—and a man who chose silence over truth, and then spent the rest of his life paying for it in the only currency he had."

The dean looked at me with something like horror. "You talk as if you loved him."

"I did love him," I said. "That's rather the point of everything."

"And the boy," Vance said. "Tomas Pell."

The dean flinched as if struck. For a moment I thought he would deny it, and I confess my heart dropped—for if he denied Tomas, then Rhys still hung in the balance, and I did not think I could bear to have been wrong about the man who wept among stolen shirts.

But the dean was finished with denial. Denial had been his life's work and it had bankrupted him.

"The boy found the fund records while cataloguing the music library," he said. "Old invoices. He was going to ask questions at the chapter meeting—he told the organist so, and the organist told me, because the organist tells me everything, the man is a sieve with a salary." He passed a hand over his face. "I couldn't let him ask. Not with the anniversary coming. Not with the statute—" He stopped himself, but too late.

"With the statute expiring," Vance said flatly. "Eleven days. You needed the boy quiet until the clock ran out, and then you dressed him in borrowed clothes and drowned him in our own baptistry, and you framed it to point at the one man in this city with a reason to hate this cathedral."

"I pointed it at whoever your files pointed at," the dean said, with the ghost of his old pedagogical precision. "I read the same files anyone reads. The Vale brother's grievance was public record. I merely... arranged the evidence to agree with it."

"You staged a murder from an archive," I said. "You used records as a weapon. Do you understand that's the most obscene thing a person can do with paper?"

"I understand," the dean said, "that I am going to prison for the rest of my life, and that none of it—not the fund, not the fraud, not even the girl—needed to happen, if any of us, at any point in twenty years, had simply told the truth." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and utterly without self-pity, which was somehow worse than pity would have been. "You kept a ledger, they say. Of all the deaths. Why? What did you imagine you'd do with it?"

And I opened my mouth to answer—and that is when Vance held up his hand, and the whole crypt went silent as a held breath.

Because the dean had just confessed. Fully, specifically, to two murders, in front of a detective and a witness, and I should have felt triumph, and instead I felt the cold arrive, the cold I had been dreading for days: none of it mattered. Not legally. The statute expired at midnight tomorrow. Serafin Vale's killer would walk free forever, protected by the one law even a perfect ledger cannot overturn. Twenty years of patience, and the law itself would close the book.

"Get it in writing," Vance said quietly. "Everything you just said. Signed, witnessed, dated tonight. It won't convict you of her death. But it matters. Truth matters even when the law doesn't want it."

The dean nodded, hollowed out, and asked for a pen.

* * *

We gave him one. Vance produced the standard statement forms from his coat—he had carried them for three days like a talisman—and the dean wrote in the antechamber by lamplight, his handwriting deteriorating page by page from the beautiful italic he taught the choristers to something ragged and human. I watched him sign. I watched Vance witness. And I watched the clock, because I could not help it. Ten hours. Nine.

It was somewhere in the eighth hour, near dawn, that I finally broke.

I went down to my office and I took out Corwin's last cipher—the one I had decoded two nights ago, the one that had led us to the vault, to Serafin's remains wrapped in the altar cloth he had chosen for her himself, to the confession written in a hand gone shaky with age and remorse. I had read it four times. Each time it hurt in a new place, the way a broken bone hurts differently when the weather changes.

*If you are reading this,* Corwin had written, *then I failed to say it aloud, which will surprise no one who knew me. I killed Serafin Vale. Not with malice—with cowardice, which is malice that arrives late. I have spent my life teaching you to see what I could not bring myself to name. Consider this my final lesson: the truth is the bequest. Everything else was only wrapping.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, almost an afterthought, almost a joke—which Corwin absolutely would have intended as a joke:

*I have done what I could to make this usable. See the notary.*

See the notary.

I had read those words four times and taken them for sentiment. An old man's whimsy. But Corwin Ashgrove did not write afterthoughts. Every marginal note I had ever found in his papers had turned out to be load-bearing.

I sat very still in my chair while the idea assembled itself around me, piece by piece, the way a solution always does—never arriving, only assembling.

Corwin had known he was dying. He had known the statute was running. He had known that a confession scrawled in a private cipher would be worth nothing in a court of law. So he had done the thing he had spent fifty years doing, the thing he had taught me without ever explaining why: he had made the record official. Dated. Witnessed. Sealed.

I ran—not walked, ran, which at my age and dignity is its own kind of emergency—up the tower stairs to the notary's office. Mrs. Okonkwo was already there, because Mrs. Okonkwo was always already there; I believe she predated the cathedral. She looked at my face and said, "The Ashgrove deposit. Finally. He made me promise not to release it until someone came asking with the right question, and he made me memorize the question, and I have been waiting two years to hear it, so please ask quickly, I'm not as patient as I perform."

"What question?" I breathed.

"He said you'd come asking whether the confession was *admissible.*" She smiled, and for once it was not the smile of a woman enduring the clergy; it was the smile of a woman who has won a bet placed decades ago. "And I am authorized to tell you: yes. Yes, it is. Dated the ninth of March, two years ago. Witnessed by me and by Judge Aldous—retired, but not deceased, whatever the choirboys say. Sealed under notarial certificate. The date of the offense, the date of the confession, the full admission, signed in the presence of two witnesses whose testimony is independent of any statute." She slid the sealed packet across the desk. "He said to tell you: *the law counts from discovery, lad, when the discoverer comes forward within the year. Read the exception clause. I did. Twice.*"

The exception clause.

I stood there holding two years of dead man's foresight in my hands and I laughed—actually laughed, alone in the notary's office at dawn, loud enough that Mrs. Okonkwo raised an eyebrow, which from her was applause. Corwin had confessed within the legal meaning of discovery; the clock had reset the day he sealed that packet; and the exception required only that the crime be reported within one year of discovery by someone acting to bring it to justice. Two years had passed—but the discovery that mattered, the discovery by *me*, had happened six days ago.

Six days. Inside the window. Hours inside, by the time the paperwork crawled through, but inside. Corwin had calculated it. He had counted the days of my grief before I could bear to open his final letter, and he had built the deadline around my heartbreak, generous margins and all, because he had known me for thirty years and knew exactly how long I would take.

He had scheduled his own redemption around my mourning. It was the most Corwin thing imaginable. I both blessed him and resolved to haunt him back someday.

* * *

Vance took the news the way he took all good news: suspiciously.

"A notarized confession from a dead man, discovered by his protégé, timed to the hour of the statute's expiry." He rubbed his eyes. "If I put this in a report, I'll be reassigned to traffic."

"If you put it in a report, you'll close a twenty-year homicide and a fresh one in the same week."

"That too." He looked at the sealed packet for a long moment. "Your mentor planned this from the grave."

"He planned everything from everywhere. Death barely slowed him down. I intend to be equally inconvenient when my turn comes."

"I don't doubt it for a second." He straightened the packet on the desk, aligning its edges with the blotter, a small nervous ritual of his I had catalogued months ago. "The Crown Prosecution will want statements. From you. From Rhys. From the notary. It'll take all day."

"We have all day," I said. "We have until midnight. And for the first time in twenty years, midnight is on our side."

At half past nine that morning, Detective Sergeant Idris Vance walked into the county courthouse with a sealed confession, a living murderer's signed statement, a set of remains identified as Serafin Vale, and a folder of bank records older than some of the officers who received them. By noon, Dean Ambrose Fennick was charged with the murder of Tomas Pell. By two o'clock, after a prosecutor read the exception clause three times and made two phone calls and poured himself a coffee with the reverence of a man handling relics, he was charged with the murder of Serafin Vale as well.

I was not in the courtroom. I was in the crypt, sitting on the steps beside the opened vault, where Serafin's altar cloth lay folded and waiting to be carried up into the light. Rhys sat next to me. Neither of us spoke for a long time.

"She used to say records were promises," he said finally. "That writing something down was a way of refusing to let the world pretend. I thought she was being naive. She was nineteen; I forgave her for being right about everything."

"She sounds like someone I'd have liked."

"She'd have liked you." He looked at the vault, at the dark that had held his sister for twenty years. "You kept her alive in ink. All this time, in a book nobody read."

"Somebody read it," I said. "Somebody always reads it. That's the trouble with ledgers, and the hope of them."

Down the corridor, the cathedral bells began the noon peal, and the sound came down through the stone and filled the crypt the way water fills a cup—completely, without asking. Twenty years of patience, and the ledger's long arithmetic had finally summed. I took out my pen, opened the book to a clean page, and wrote the closing line of the longest entry I would ever make:

*Serafin Vale. Found. Named. Avenged by patience, by paper, and by a dead man who kept his promise late. Case closed.*

Then, because the habit of a lifetime dies hard, I added the time, the temperature, and the fact that the Chapter House clock was still seven minutes fast.

Some things, after all, should survive even justice.

I closed the ledger—and froze, pen still in hand, because there on the cover, in pencil so faint I must have missed it a hundred times, someone had written a single word in a hand I knew better than my own:

*Mirabel.*
