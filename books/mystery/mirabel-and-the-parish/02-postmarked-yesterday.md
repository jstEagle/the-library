## 2. Postmarked Yesterday

The morning after Edith Mirabel's funeral, I did what I always do on the first working day after a burial: I updated the ledger.

Not the official one — the county has its own forms, its own boxes, its own cheerful bureaucratic vocabulary for the end of a human life. *Decedent. Remains. Disposition.* As if we were talking about a shipment of lumber. No, I mean my ledger, the one I've kept since my first week in this office, eleven years ago: a plain composition book in which I write, in my own cramped hand, every person the parish has given back to the ground since I took the job. Name, dates, where they lay, and one line — just one — about who they were.

*Edith Mirabel, 1931–2024. Row C, under the cedar. She knew every tide table by heart and pretended not to know every secret by heart.*

I'd hesitated over that second clause. Then I'd written it anyway, because the dead don't read my ledger, and the living who might object are exactly the ones who should.

I was still capping my pen when the mail slot clacked and something landed face-down on the tile.

Here is what you need to know about mail in this parish: there isn't any, not really. The coroner's office gets a quarterly invoice from the county, an occasional subpoena with the wrong middle initial for my surname, and — in November — approximately four hundred catalogues addressed to the previous coroner, who died in 2016 and whose name, Mr. Hollis, apparently lives on in the hearts of the people who sell commemorative knives. A letter arriving the morning after a funeral is not a letter. It is an event.

I let it lie there for a moment, the way you let a wasp lie still on the windowsill, telling yourself it's dead.

Then I picked it up.

The envelope was cheap, the kind sold in boxes of five hundred at the office supply store two towns over. No return address. My name and title typed — typed, not printed, and I want you to hold onto that detail, because I did — in a font that had the slightly bruised look of an old machine. And in the upper right corner, where a stamp should have been, a postage meter mark, the kind the postal annex in Harwick uses for bulk mailings.

The postmark read the day *after* the funeral.

Which meant the letter had been in the mail, traveling toward me, while we stood in the rain at Row C and lowered Edith Mirabel into the ground. While Father Ambrose said the words over her. While Harold Ashford held an umbrella over Celeste Delacroix and neither of them looked at the coffin, not once, not even when the straps creaked.

While I recited her name and put my ledger away in the drawer where I keep the things I'm not ready to think about.

I opened it with a letter knife that had belonged to Mr. Hollis, because some rituals you inherit whether you want them or not.

* * *

Inside was a single sheet, typed on both sides, the ink ribbon fading toward the bottom of the second page the way ribbons do when someone doesn't want to stop and change them. No salutation. No signature. Just this:

*I am writing to tell you that Edith Mirabel did not drown by accident on the night of June 14, 1994. She was murdered. I did not kill her, but I know who did, and I know why, and I have known for thirty years. I am dying, or I am a coward, or both — I no longer distinguish. The parish seal on this letter will tell you this comes from inside. Do not trust the families. Do not trust the church. Do not trust the water. She could not have drowned where they found her, and someone in this parish has known that for three decades and said nothing. You of all people should look. You, Quentin. Look at what your name is sitting on.*

I read it three times.

The first time, my eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off anything unbearable — I absorbed the shape of the accusation without absorbing the accusation, the way you can drive home and remember none of the road. The second time, I read it properly, and my hands did something interesting, which was to become very cold and very steady at the same time, as if my body had already decided how it wanted to handle this and was simply waiting for the rest of me to catch up.

The third time, I read it like a coroner. Which is to say: I read it like a man looking for the lie.

*She could not have drowned where they found her.* That was not a sentence a crank writes. Cranks write about the government, about frequencies, about their neighbor's fence line. Cranks do not know where Edith Mirabel's body was found, because that detail never appeared in the paper. The *Gazette* ran eleven inches in June 1994 — I checked the archive years ago, out of professional boredom, the way you check a lock you're sure is locked — and the article said she drowned "in the bay," which covered roughly four hundred acres of possibility. The specific location — the shallows off the old boathouse, thirty feet from the pilings — lived in the police file. A file that was closed twice. A file that a private citizen would need a very good reason to have read.

*Do not trust the families.* The parish had, at the time of Edith's death, exactly two families that anyone meant when they said *the families*: the Ashfords and the Delacroixes. Founding families, both. The kind of families whose names are on the church windows and the marina sign and the plaque by the seawall, and whose names, if you said them in certain company, produced a small respectful silence, like the pause after a hymn.

*You, Quentin. Look at what your name is sitting on.*

That was the sentence I kept returning to, and I'll tell you why it frightened me more than the others. The others were an accusation. This one was a *summons*. Whoever wrote this knew me. Not my title — me. Knew that my name would mean something to me, though I couldn't yet have told you what. It read like a hand reaching out of 1994 and closing around my collar.

I sat there for a long time with the letter flat on my desk, and outside the window the parish went about its morning, which is to say it performed its morning: the bakery van, the gulls arguing over the harbor trash, Mrs. Okafor power-walking her loop with the grim efficiency of a woman training for a war only she believes in. The parish is very good at performing itself. I'd always found that comforting. I'd always thought: *here is a place that knows what it is.*

I looked at the letter again and thought: *here is a place that knows exactly what it is, and has hired very good help to keep it that way.*

Then I did the thing I should have done first, and turned the sheet over, and held it up to the light.

* * *

The watermark was faint — a crosshatch of lines and, at the center, a small device I recognized the way you recognize a face across a crowded room before you've placed it. I got out the magnifier Mr. Hollis had also left me, because that man never met an instrument he didn't want to bequeath, and I looked properly.

A seal. A circular seal, pressed into the paper during manufacture, not stamped on afterward. A ring of text too fine to read at arm's length, and at the center, a stylized flame over an open book.

I knew that flame. Every child in this parish knows that flame. It's on the banner in the nave. It's embossed on the hymnals. It's on the letterhead that Father Ambrose uses for the parish newsletter, the one that arrives monthly with the tide charts printed in the footer, because Edith Mirabel used to compile them and after she died — after she died the first time, in 1994 — no one had the heart to drop the feature, so it just kept running, thirty years of tide charts, a dead woman's column.

The paper was church stationery.

I want to be precise about what that means, because precision is the only weapon I own. It doesn't mean the letter came from the church. Stationery gets stolen, borrowed, hoarded. Edith herself had a ream of it in her house — I knew that the way everyone knew it, the way you know things about a town you've lived in your whole life, by osmosis and without thinking. Anyone could have typed this letter. The postmark was Harwick, twenty miles away, which meant anyone could have mailed it.

But the *stock* — the weight of it, the watermark, the particular cream color the church office had ordered from the same supplier for as long as I'd been alive — the stock said that whoever typed this confession had stood in the parish office, or in a room furnished from it, and fed a sheet of it into a typewriter.

A typewriter. In 2024.

I looked at the lettering again. The slightly bruised font. The uneven impression — heavier on the left of the platen, lighter toward the margin, the signature of a machine with a worn carriage. I'd seen that kind of impression before, in the parish newsletter's back issues, in the typed order-of-service inserts from the nineties that the church archive still kept, because the church archive kept everything, because the church in this parish had never once believed that the future would fail to need its records.

Someone had walked into the present day carrying a piece of 1994, and used it to tell me that 1994 was not what I thought it was.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I record it in full because I am, whatever else, a man who believes in the record.

I did not call Sheriff's Deputy Rosa Vane, who is the closest thing this parish has to an honest badge and who would have wanted to know immediately. I did not call Father Ambrose, who baptized me, married exactly no one I know, and buried my mother with a kindness I have never fully repaid. I did not call anyone.

I put on my coat, drove to the Harwick postal annex, and asked to see whoever handled the metered mail.

The woman at the counter had the wary competence of someone who has spent thirty years explaining to the public that no, she cannot tell them where a letter came from. I showed her my county credentials, which opened doors the way credentials do, and I asked my question carefully: a metered letter, postmarked the day after yesterday, no return address — was there any way to know which account had paid the postage?

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "You're the coroner over in — what is it, the parish?" And when I nodded, she said, "My sister lives out that way. Went to the funeral yesterday, matter of fact. Whole town turned out." She pulled a logbook toward her, and I understood that she had already decided, for reasons that had nothing to do with my credentials and everything to do with Edith Mirabel, to tell me.

"Meter account's a church account," she said. "The Catholic parish, out your way. They mail their newsletter through us. Bulk rate." She turned the log around so I could see the line. "Somebody ran a single letter through the bulk meter the day before yesterday. Doesn't happen. You don't use a bulk meter for one letter unless you don't want to buy a stamp under your own name."

"Who runs the meter?" I asked, though I knew.

"Church office," she said. "Whoever's minding the desk. It's a shared code." She paused, and her voice dropped into the register people use when they're about to hand you something heavy and want credit for the gentleness. "You want to know the funny thing, though? The meter run was logged at 4:12 in the afternoon. I know because it's the only single-piece run that day, and I noticed it when I balanced the books. 4:12 in the afternoon. And the funeral — my sister said it was a morning service."

The service had ended at eleven. The reception at the Ashford house had run until two. I had spent the afternoon at my desk, updating my ledger, writing Edith's name in my careful hand, believing — with the whole, warm, foolish confidence of a man who thinks he knows his town — that the day's strangeness was behind me.

At 4:12, while I was capping my pen, someone had fed a sheet of church stationery into a machine older than I am and typed out a murder.

* * *

I drove back with the windows down even though it was cold, because I wanted the parish in my face. I wanted to look at it. The fields going gold at the edges, the church spire doing its patient impression of a finger pointed at heaven, the harbor lying flat and silver and innocent as a lie told well. The marina sign: ASHFORD & DELACROIX WHARF — EST. 1948. Two names, bolted to the sky over the water where Edith Mirabel had gone into the water and not come out, thirty years ago, while everyone I had ever loved or trusted agreed, in their way, to call it an accident.

I had lived here my whole life. I had buried a hundred and six of its people — a hundred and seven, now — and I had recited every one of their names, because names were how I kept faith with this place, and this place had kept faith with me. That was the bargain I thought we had.

The letter sat on the passenger seat in its envelope, and I found I couldn't stop glancing at it, the way you can't stop looking at a door you've just noticed is ajar in a house you thought you knew every room of.

*Look at what your name is sitting on.*

What was my name sitting on? My mother's house, empty these six years. My office, with its ledger and its inherited knives. A grave in Row D that I visit on Sundays and speak to in two languages, one of them dead. What could a stranger mean by that? What could my name — Zhou, the only one of its kind in the parish register, the name of a woman who came here in 1968 and never left and never quite — 

I stopped the car on the bridge, which you're not supposed to do, and I sat there, and I let the thought arrive, because it was going to arrive whether I let it or not.

The letter writer knew my name meant something. Not *Zhou* the way the parish meant it — politely, with a small pause, the way you handle a word you learned late. They meant it the way *I* meant it, privately, at night. They meant my mother. And they meant that whatever happened to Edith Mirabel in the water off the boathouse in June of 1994, my mother's name was somewhere underneath it, the way a name is underneath a stone, the way a body is underneath water.

You cannot un-know a thing like that. I want to be clear about that, because people talk about knowledge as if it were a door you could close. It isn't. It's water. It comes in under every door, and it rises, and it rises.

I drove the rest of the way home. I locked the letter in the office safe, under the ledger, because I am a coroner and we keep things under other things; it's practically the job description.

And then I sat down at my kitchen table with a glass of the whiskey I keep for occasions I hope never to have, and I did the arithmetic that was going to govern the rest of my life, though I didn't know it yet.

Thirty years. A statute of limitations, if the letter was true, that would not wait forever. A confession with no signature. A seal with no owner. A typewriter with a worn carriage, sitting somewhere in this parish tonight, in a drawer or a closet or a rectory, patient as a grave.

And somewhere out there, the hand that had fed the paper in — dying, or a coward, or both — waiting to see whether I would look.

I drank the whiskey. I washed the glass. I went to bed and lay in the dark listening to the harbor breathe, and I thought about the tide charts in the footer of the parish newsletter, thirty years of them, Edith's column running on without her, the water going in and out, in and out, keeping its own ledger, keeping everything.

The mail comes early in this parish. I would be at the slot at eight tomorrow, and every tomorrow after.

But the next morning, before the mail came, the phone rang — and it was Edith's lawyer, and he said, in a voice carefully sanded of all expression, "Mr. Zhou. I'm afraid I have some news about the Mirabel estate. You'd better sit down. It seems she's left you the house."
