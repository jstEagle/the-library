## 16. The Confession's Hand

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a typewriter. I don't mean the quiet of an unused machine—dust on the keys, ribbon gone brittle as autumn leaves. I mean the silence a typewriter keeps about what it has typed. A gun remembers nothing. A knife forgets. But a typewriter holds its evidence in the very teeth that made it: every letter strikes the paper with the same small violence, and every letter wears down in its own particular way. Type a thousand pages and your machine develops a fingerprint no forger can fake and no owner can erase.

I learned this from a forensic examiner named Dr. Imelda Cho, who consults for three counties and treats documents the way other people treat wounded animals—with patience, gloves, and a certain grim tenderness. She had examined two of my corrections back when I was still allowed to call them that. When I carried the confession letter to her office in a plastic sleeve, she looked at it the way you'd look at a bird with a broken wing.

"Typed," she said. "Not printed. Somebody wanted it to look old."

"Somebody *is* old," I said. "That's the problem. Everybody in this parish who could hold a grudge for thirty years learned to type on something like this."

She spread the letter under her magnifier and was quiet for a long time. Outside her window, the county courthouse clock did its slow civic duty. Inside, I watched her read a dead woman's murder confession—or rather, someone's confession *about* a dead woman's murder—and I thought about how strange it is that paper can outlast intention. Whoever typed this letter meant it to arrive, to accuse, to detonate. They had not considered that it would also sit still long enough to be studied.

"Elite model," Dr. Cho said finally. "Smith-Corona, early eighties. The lowercase *e* has a lifted serif—the typebar's been bent and straightened at least once. The *r* strikes slightly heavy on the left stroke. And there's a characteristic drift to the right margin, increasing over the length of a line, which suggests a worn platen knob." She sat back. "This machine has been used hard and maintained badly. That's good news for us. Machines that are loved are machines that are replaced. Machines that are neglected stay put for decades."

"And the bad news?"

"The bad news," she said, tapping the parish seal pressed into the bottom of the page—a deep violet stamp, the dove and the wave, the emblem of St. Brigid-by-the-Water, "is that this tells me the author had access to church property. Which narrows your suspect pool to roughly everyone who's ever attended a funeral there."

"I was afraid you'd say that."

* * *

The church office of St. Brigid-by-the-Water smells the way all church offices smell: mimeograph ghosts, old coffee, candle wax, and the faint sweet rot of hymnals that have absorbed thirty years of rain-soaked congregations. I had been in that office perhaps a hundred times—for records requests, for funeral logistics, once, memorably, to retrieve my own mother's death certificate, which Father Ambrose had filed with a neatness that now struck me as its own kind of testimony.

Father Ambrose had been in St. Anne's Hospital for eleven days by then. Pneumonia, then something the doctors wouldn't name but spoke about in the lowered voices they reserve for endings. His office had been kept as a shrine of sorts—nobody at St. Brigid tidies a dying priest's desk—but Mrs. Okafor, the parish secretary, let me in with the weary permission of a woman who had decided some weeks ago that whatever Quentin Zhou was doing, it was between him and God.

The typewriter sat on the side table under a window, where it had presumably sat since the Reagan administration. A Smith-Corona Elite. Gray body gone yellow at the top, a strip of masking tape on the back bearing Ambrose's handwriting: *OFFICE — DO NOT BORROW.* The ribbon was fresh. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing that made the back of my neck prickle. A dying man does not change his own ribbon. A hospitalized man certainly doesn't.

I didn't touch it. Thirty years of reading crime scenes had taught me that the most dangerous thing you can do with evidence is have hopes about it. I photographed it instead—top, sides, the tape, the ribbon spools, the serial number stamped into the frame—and drove the photographs straight to Dr. Cho.

She called me the next morning at seven, which for Dr. Cho constituted unseemly excitement.

"It's your machine," she said. "Or rather, it's the confession's machine. The bent *e*, the heavy *r*, the platen drift—it's a match across every measurable character. Quentin, whoever typed that letter sat at Father Ambrose's typewriter."

I stood in my kitchen holding the phone and looking out at Parish Lane, where Edith Mirabel's house sat empty under a sky the color of dishwater. Somewhere down that street, someone had walked into a dying priest's office, fed a sheet of church stationery into his machine, typed a confession to a thirty-year-old murder, pressed his seal into the paper, and mailed it.

"Why?" I said aloud, though not to Dr. Cho.

"That's above my pay grade," she said. "But here's something below it. The hospitalization timeline. You said he went in on the ninth?"

"The ninth. The letter was postmarked the tenth."

"Then either he typed it before he went in—which is possible, if a dying man spent his last free morning confessing a murder he may or may not have committed—or somebody else typed it while he lay in a ward four miles away." A pause. "You'll want to know which day the ribbon was changed. Ribbons keep impressions, you know. If you can get me that ribbon legally, I might be able to recover everything typed on it recently. It's like a palimpsest. The last few thousand characters are usually readable if you know how to look."

I thanked her, hung up, and stood there a moment longer, doing the arithmetic that was making my hands cold.

A trap within a trap. That was the phrase that kept arriving, uninvited, like a bill collector. Because here was the shape of it: the confession had come to *me*. Not to the sheriff's department—not to Rosa Vane, who had jurisdiction and, by then, a growing sympathy for my cause. Not to the newspaper in the next county. To the coroner's office of a parish so small that our office shared a parking lot with the feed store. Someone had wanted this letter in *my* hands, specifically, at *this* moment—three weeks into an investigation that was already making the Delacroixs nervous, one week after Celeste had convened her council and turned the parish's face against me.

Either the author was an ally sending me ammunition.

Or the author was setting me up to be found holding it.

Consider how it would look, if you were Celeste Delacroix and you wanted this to end. Your enemy the coroner produces an anonymous confession accusing your family of murder. The confession is traced to the typewriter of a beloved dying priest. What follows? Not truth. Chaos. A suggestion that poor Father Ambrose, in his final delirium, had fabricated a scandal—or worse, that Zhou himself had forged the whole thing, using his access to the church office, to smear the founding families of a parish that had only ever been good to him. The audit of my office was already underway. A petition with two hundred signatures was circulating at the hardware store. All it would take was one expert witness willing to say the letter couldn't be authenticated, and I would go from investigator to fabricator in a single news cycle.

Unless the letter was genuine. Unless Father Ambrose really had typed it, in the window between deciding to die honestly and being admitted to St. Anne's.

Two possibilities, and both of them required me to do exactly what I was already doing. That's the elegance of a trap within a trap: every road leads to the same room.

* * *

Rosa came by my office that evening, after hours, carrying two coffees from the gas station and the expression of a woman who has stopped sleeping well. She set one cup on my desk without asking and dropped into the chair across from me, and for a while we just listened to the building settle.

"You've heard," she said.

"Heard what?"

"Ambrose is asking for you." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the desk, a habit of hers when she was choosing words carefully. "The nurses say he won't eat until he talks to you. He told them—" she hesitated, "—he told them you'd know what it's about. Do you?"

I thought about the typewriter with its fresh ribbon. About the seal. About a man carrying someone else's sin for thirty years, refusing even now to break the confessional, and what it might cost a man like that to find a door around the seal rather than through it.

"I think," I said, "that he's been trying to tell the truth in the only way his conscience would allow. And I think someone else found the door he left open."

Rosa frowned. "Explain that to me like I'm a deputy and not your friend."

So I laid it out. The typewriter match. The ribbon changed days before a dying man would have had reason or strength to change it. The postmark falling squarely inside his hospitalization. The letter arriving at my desk like a gift-wrapped grenade.

"Or," Rosa said slowly, "he dictated it. From the hospital. Called Mrs. Okafor, or one of the volunteers, asked them to type something up and mail it. People do that. Dying men tidy up."

"With his personal seal? Rosa, that seal stays in his desk drawer. He's told me himself—he doesn't trust anyone else to use it. Said a seal is a promise you make with wax and ink, and promises shouldn't be delegated." I rubbed my eyes. "Whoever typed that letter knew where the seal was. Knew how he loaded the paper. Knew the machine well enough to sit down and produce a full page without a single correction mark—did I mention that? No strikeovers, no lifted letters. A practiced typist, or someone who rehearsed."

"Rehearsed," Rosa repeated. "On what?"

"On other paper. Paper that's gone somewhere I haven't found yet." I looked at her. "Your father's effects. The box you gave me. Is there anything in it from the church? Correspondence, memos, anything typed?"

She was quiet for a moment, and I watched her do what she always did when the case touched her father: flinch, then lean in anyway. "There's a memo," she said. "Sheriff's department letterhead, 1994, requesting the boathouse records be sealed. Typed. I never thought about what typed it."

"Sheriff's departments don't own typewriters with bent lowercase e's."

"No," she said. "But churches lend them. My father was at St. Brigid every week of his life. If he needed something typed up clean and official, he'd have—" She stopped. Her face did something complicated. "He'd have asked Ambrose. Or Ambrose's secretary. Or just used the office machine after Mass, the way half the parish probably did. Quentin, that machine might have typed half the official history of this town."

"Which means," I said, "the machine can't tell us who wrote the confession. Only that the author had a key to the church office, steady hands, and a reason to want me holding it."

Rosa leaned forward. "There's a third option you're dancing around."

"Say it, then."

"Ambrose typed it himself. Before the hospital. He knew he was going in—maybe he'd been diagnosed, maybe he just felt it coming. He wrote the confession because it was the only way around his own vow. He couldn't speak what Edith told him in the confessional, but a letter isn't the confessional. He sealed it and mailed it to you because you're the coroner and you're the heir and you're the son of Lian Zhou, and he knew—God help him, he *knew*—you were the only person in this parish who wouldn't stop." Her voice had gone rough. "And then he got sick faster than he planned, and now he's lying up at St. Anne's watching the thing he set in motion turn into a weapon pointed at you, and he can't say a word about any of it because the seal still holds. Even now. *Especially* now."

We sat with that. The heater ticked. Down the hall, a faucet dripped its patient metronome.

"If that's true," I said finally, "then the letter is genuine, the evidence chain is clean, and the only person who can confirm it is a priest who's forbidden to confirm it."

"And if it's not true," Rosa said, "if someone used him—if someone sat in that office while he was coughing his lungs out four miles away—then that person knows this office, knows this parish, knows *you*, well enough to build a trap with your name on the trigger." She stood, drained her coffee, and set the cup down with a click that sounded final. "Either way, you need to see him. Tonight, if the nurses will allow it. Because there's one more thing, Quentin, and I've been sitting on it all day trying to decide whether it means anything."

"Tell me."

"When I pulled the visitor log at St. Anne's—informally, off the books, don't ask—there was one name that made me stop." She pulled a folded sheet from her jacket pocket and smoothed it on my desk. "Three visits this week. All during evening hours, when the staff runs thin. Signed in as 'parish pastoral care.'"

"Which means what?"

"It means nobody signed their name." She tapped the entry. "But the night nurse described her. Older woman, silver hair, pearls. Brought flowers and stayed forty minutes. Quentin—" Rosa's voice dropped, though we were alone in the building, "—the nurse said she recognized her from the garden society newsletter. She said it was *Mrs. Delacroix.*"

I looked at the log. At the careful, anonymous hand. At the word *pastoral* doing its quiet, terrible work.

Celeste Delacroix, visiting the dying priest whose typewriter had accused her family. Sitting at his bedside for forty minutes at a stretch, with flowers, with pearls, with whatever she says to people when she is arranging them.

Was she comforting him? Threatening him? Searching his office for the evidence he'd left behind? Or simply keeping watch over a fire she hadn't lit but intended to control?

"I'm going to see him," I said.

"Tonight?"

"Tonight. Before whoever 'pastoral care' is comes back." I reached for my coat, and my hand wasn't quite steady, and I decided not to be embarrassed about it. "Because there are only two people alive who know who typed that letter, Rosa. One of them is visiting the other one with flowers."

"And if Ambrose can't talk?"

"Then I'll sit with him anyway," I said. "Sometimes the truth gets said best by the people who can't say anything at all. You just have to know how to listen."

Outside, the fog was coming in off the water the way it always did, erasing the parish lane by lane, and somewhere in that soft gray blindness a car started—an engine turning over in the dark near the church, catching, and pulling away slow, like something that had finished its work for the evening and was in no hurry to be seen leaving.
