## 21. The Reading of the Names

The parish hall had not been this full since the Bicentennial Pie Auction of 2011, an event remembered chiefly for the incident involving Harold Ashford's rhubarb entry and a wasp. Tonight there were no pies and no wasps, though I suspected several people would have preferred either to what they'd come for.

They filled every folding chair and lined the walls, and still more stood outside in the October cold with their collars up, listening through propped doors like a congregation locked out of its own church. The Delacroix pew—there had always been a Delacroix pew, unofficially, the way there had always been a Delacroix everything—sat conspicuously empty except for one person: Celeste's sister-in-law Marguerite, eighty-one and deaf as a post in her left ear, who had come, she told me on the way in, "to see what all the shouting is about." There had been no shouting yet. I intended to keep it that way.

Rosa Vane stood at the back in civilian clothes, arms crossed, watching the crowd the way she watched traffic stops—not nervous, just counting exits. Father Ambrose was not there. He had died eleven days after giving me his half-confession, gently, in his sleep, at St. Anne's Hospital, and the parish had buried him with full honors and a eulogy that mentioned his forty years of service and did not mention the typewriter in his office or the seal pressed into a stranger's confession. I understood. Eulogies are the last courtesy we pay the dead, and nobody wants to spend it on the truth.

I stood at the front beside a folding table. On the table: Edith Mirabel's letter, in its plastic sleeve, because I am a coroner before I am anything else and evidence is evidence even when you're about to read it aloud to two hundred people. Beside it, a single sheet of paper—the amendment to the cemetery registry—and beneath the table, where no one could see it yet, a stone.

Not the finished marker. The blank one. The mason had promised me the engraving by Thursday; tonight I would only show them what a name looks like before it's cut, which is to say, like everyone else.

Mayor Pro Tem Douglas Wren called the meeting to order at seven o'clock sharp, banging a gavel he had clearly borrowed from somewhere and was enjoying far too much. He was a decent man in the way of most parish men—decent right up until decency became expensive. He looked at me over his reading glasses with an expression that said, plainly, *Quentin, don't do this*, and I looked back with an expression that said *you asked me here*, because technically he had. The petition demanding my removal as coroner had required a public hearing, and public hearings require a venue, and the venue required someone to speak. They had expected me to defend myself.

Instead I held up Edith's letter.

"This will take about twenty minutes," I said. "Some of you will want to leave. The doors are open. No one will think less of you." A small lie. Everyone would think less of everyone, whichever side of the door they ended up on. That was the arithmetic of truth in a small place; I had learned it the hard way, over forty days, and I had stopped pretending otherwise.

Douglas started to say something procedural. I began to read.

* * *

Edith wrote the letter in June of 1994, nine days before she died, in the careful cursive of a woman who had been a schoolteacher and never quite stopped grading herself. My voice carried badly in the hall—I'm a coroner, not a lector—so I read slowly, and the slowness did its own work. You could hear the building settle. You could hear people stop breathing.

She described the night of the hit-and-run the way she must have described it to herself a thousand times, trying to make the numbers come out different. Tomás Reyes, walking home from the Ashford marina along the water road, no lights, no shoulder, a car moving fast enough that the sound reached her porch before the headlights did. She described running down. She described holding him. She was very specific about that—she wanted whoever read this someday to know that someone had held him, that he had not died alone on wet asphalt while five respectable people arranged their stories. She described Harold Ashford getting out of the driver's side with his dinner jacket still on, and Celeste Delacroix arriving ten minutes later in a second car, calm as Sunday, taking charge the way she took charge of everything: the flower show, the restoration fund, the disposal of a man who had no family within four hundred miles and therefore, in Celeste's arithmetic, no weight.

Then the pact. Five names. Edith listed them without commentary, which was somehow worse than commentary: Ashford. Delacroix. Ambrose. Lian Zhou. Mirabel. My mother's name sat in that list like a splinter I'd been carrying for weeks and finally turned toward the light. Edith wrote that Lian had argued—had stood on the water road and said *we cannot do this*—and then, at dawn, had signed anyway, because Celeste had made the cost of refusal clear in the quiet way she made all costs clear. Edith did not forgive her for signing. But she wrote: *I have thought for thirty years about whether I would have been braver, and I have concluded that I would simply have been afraid differently.*

And then the last part. The reason for the letter. She had gone to the boathouse on the night she died to tell Celeste she was done—that she had copied the ledger pages, that she had written everything down, that the pact was over whether the other four consented or not. She wrote: *Celeste did not shout. Celeste has never shouted in her life. She put both hands on my shoulders, the way she does when she is about to say something kind, and then the water came up fast and I understood, too late, what her hands were for.* The letter broke off mid-sentence there, in a smudge, and resumed on the next page in a shakier hand: *If I drown, it was not the tide. Check the tide charts. Check the ledger. I am sorry to leave this to a stranger, but the strangers are the only ones left who didn't sign.*

There was a rustle when I read my mother's name—a real, physical rustle, like wind through the hall—and another, longer one, when I read the sentence about Celeste's hands. In the third row, Marguerite Delacroix leaned over to her neighbor and said, at full deaf-person volume, "Well. I always liked Edith better anyway," which got the only laugh of the night, brief and shocked, the laugh people use to keep from doing something else.

When I finished, I folded the letter closed and let the silence sit for a moment, because I had learned from Rosa that silence is a tool and not just an absence.

"Tomás Reyes was thirty-four years old," I said. "He picked apples at the Ashford orchard for three seasons. He sent money home to his daughter, Lucía, who is now forty-one and lives in Corbett County, and who received a money order from Edith Mirabel every March for thirty years. He has been buried in our cemetery since September of 1994, in the unmarked section, under number 47, because the men who killed him owned the ground he was buried in." I let that land. "Tonight I'm filing the amendment. His name goes in the registry. And on Thursday, if the weather holds, he gets a stone."

I lifted the blank granite so they could see it. It wasn't dramatic. It was heavy and gray and cold, and I set it on the table with a thud that sounded, in that hall, like a verdict.

"That's all I have," I said. "The floor is open."

* * *

What followed lasted ninety minutes and I will not pretend to give you all of it. Grief in a crowd behaves like water finding cracks; it goes everywhere and takes the shape of whatever contains it.

A man named Pete Calloway—third generation orchard, Ashford's foreman once upon a time—stood up first and said, "My father worked for Harold thirty years. You're telling me he built his life on top of—" and couldn't finish, and sat down, and that unfinished sentence did more damage than any finished one could have. A woman from the garden society demanded to know why I hadn't brought this to the council privately, "like a civilized person," and Rosa, from the back, said, "Because the council knew in 1994, ma'am," and the woman sat down too. Someone accused me of desecrating Edith's memory. Someone else pointed out that Edith had done the desecrating herself, in her own handwriting, thirty years ago, and had merely been waiting for someone with a spine to read it.

The fractures showed themselves the way fractures do—not dramatically, but in the small migrations of bodies. When Douglas called for a vote on a resolution condemning my "unauthorized disclosure," about sixty people walked out. They went quietly, in ones and twos, coats gathered, eyes fixed on the middle distance, the way people leave a church during a sermon that has found them out. I recognized some faces. The Ashfords, what remained of them, led the exodus—Harold himself did not come; his lawyer had advised against it, and for once Harold had taken good advice. The Delacroix name was represented only by Marguerite, who stayed, and who voted, loudly, against the resolution, on the grounds that she had never trusted Celeste's potato salad and this confirmed everything.

But here is the thing I want to set down accurately, because the historians of this parish—if it survives having historians—will get it wrong: more people stayed than left. Not many more. Maybe forty stayed against the sixty who walked. But they stayed. They stayed through the vote, which failed, nineteen to nothing with a great many abstentions from people suddenly fascinated by their shoes. They stayed while I filed the amendment at the table by the door, and a surprising number of them lined up afterward—not to congratulate me, most of them, but to look at the registry page, to see the name *Tomás Reyes* sitting in the record between Ruthie Okafor and Gunnar Pedersen, ordinary as anything, a man restored to the list of the dead. An old apple picker named Sam Ferro touched the page with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, and said, "I knew him. Three seasons. He taught me a song." Then he cried, briefly and without apology, and went home.

Catharsis, the Greeks promised us, is cleansing. Nobody mentions that it's also a demolition. You don't get the clean without the wrecking. Walking out to the parking lot afterward, past clusters of people arguing in the cold in low, urgent voices—families split down the middle, I could see it, husbands and wives standing slightly apart from each other like diplomats from rival nations—I felt the thing I had been chasing for forty days and dreading: not triumph. Relief, thin and exhausted, like the feeling after setting down something you've carried so long your arm has forgotten its own shape.

Rosa fell into step beside me. She had the box of her father's effects under one arm; she'd brought it to the meeting and never needed it. The originals had already gone to the district attorney, along with her resignation letter, which the sheriff had refused to accept and which she had resubmitted that morning with a note reading *third attempt, please take it seriously.*

"You know half this town will never speak to you again," she said.

"I know."

"And the other half will only speak to you about this, forever, until you're the guy who ruined the pie auction forever."

"There was a wasp involved in that," I said. "I contain multitudes."

She almost smiled. Then she nodded at the granite slab in my arms. "Thursday?"

"Thursday. Ten o'clock. If you're coming, wear boots. The unmarked section floods."

"Quentin." She stopped at her car and looked at me across the roof, and for a moment she was sixteen again, standing in her father's kitchen while he drank himself silent over a file he shouldn't have signed. "Thirty years, this town kept that grave empty on purpose. Empty is easier. You're making them carry the weight of a whole man." She opened her door. "Good. That's the job."

* * *

I did not go straight home. I drove out to the cemetery, because I always do, because names are heavier at night and I wanted to set mine down somewhere soft.

The fog was coming off the water road—the same road, widened now, paved, with a memorial bench some civic group had installed in 2009 that did not mention why it was needed. I walked the rows reciting them, as I always do, the dead of this parish, my dead, my congregation of stone: Ruthie Okafor, Gunnar Pedersen, old Doc Halloran, the Okafor twins, Father Ambrose, new in his plot with fresh-turned earth like a scar. Edith Mirabel, three rows from the water, her stone reading BELOVED TEACHER AND FRIEND, which was true and insufficient, the way most stones are.

At the edge of the unmarked section I stopped. Number 47 lay flat under the grass, indistinguishable from the ground around it, which had been the entire point for thirty years. I set the blank granite down on the frost-stiffened grass beside it, and I crouched, and I said the name out loud to the dark, because a name spoken alone at midnight is a rehearsal, and Thursday I would need to get it right in front of everyone.

"Tomás Reyes," I said. "Tomás Reyes. Tomás Reyes."

Three times, the way you say a thing you mean to carve into stone.

Behind me, gravel crunched. I turned, expecting Rosa, expecting a deputy, expecting God knew what. Instead, halfway down the row, a figure stood at the gate with a flashlight clicked off, holding it against her chest like something she'd rather not be seen carrying. Small, wrapped in a wool coat too warm for the season, gray-haired, upright, unmistakable.

Marguerite Delacroix had followed me out. She stood at the cemetery gate in the fog, looking at the blank stone, and then at me, and she raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. More like a question.

"Well," she called, in that voice that had never met a volume dial. "Are you going to invite me over, or do I have to stand here freezing?"
