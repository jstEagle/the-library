## 3. What the Water Kept

The county records annex smelled the way old paper dreamed of itself: dust and glue and the faint vinegar sweetness of degrading acetate. Paloma had signed in at 6:40 a.m., before Ruthie arrived, before anyone could ask her what she was doing down in the basement where the cold cases slept in gray boxes with their spines facing the wall.

She hadn't told Ruthie about the letter yet. She wasn't sure why. Or she was sure, and the reason was ugly: because Ruthie would look at her the way she'd looked at her yesterday in the cemetery—*some stones shouldn't be turned*—and Paloma wasn't ready to defend herself out loud. Not until she knew whether there was anything worth defending.

The box was where it should have been. HALLOWAY, MIRABEL A. — CASE NO. 04-1187. The cardboard had gone soft at the corners, bloomed faintly brown, as if the file itself were bruising after all these years.

She carried it upstairs to her office instead of reading it down there. Superstition, maybe. Or the simple animal need for daylight when she opened it. She set it on her desk beside the confession letter, which she'd brought in a ziplock bag like evidence, though technically it belonged to no case at all. Not yet.

Paloma made coffee she didn't drink. Then she untied the string on the box and let eleven years fall open in her hands.

* * *

The first thing she saw was her own signature.

Younger, rounder, the letters leaning forward like a woman in a hurry to be done. *P. Vasquez, Deputy Coroner, Marrow Creek County.* Below it, the countersignature of Dr. Emory Ladd, who'd been coroner then and had retired two years later to a golf community in Scottsdale, taking his tremor and his shortcuts with him.

She remembered that day. That was the terrible thing—she actually remembered it. The third week of September, eleven years ago, air still holding summer by the fingernails. Mirabel Halloway had come out of the quarry pond at dawn, pulled by two teenagers who'd gone out early to smoke where their parents couldn't see them. Paloma had been twenty-nine, three years into the job, and the Halloway name had arrived at the scene before she did—arrived in the form of Sheriff Dunmore's hand on her elbow, low voice, *the family would appreciate discretion, they've been through enough.*

She'd told herself at the time that discretion was compassion. She'd told herself a lot of things.

The autopsy report ran nine pages. Paloma read them slowly, the way she'd learned to read only later in her career—backwards first, from conclusion to observation, so the conclusion couldn't do the observing for her. It was a trick Emory had never taught her. It was a trick she'd taught herself the year after this file was closed, which meant that everything in these pages had passed through the hands of a woman who didn't yet know how to distrust a tidy story.

Cause of death: asphyxia due to drowning. Manner: accident. Blood alcohol: 0.11.

She stopped there. Read it again. 0.11.

The bonfire party—the one the whole town still described in the same soft-focus language, *everyone had a few beers, it was a warm night, kids will be kids*—had run, according to every witness statement in the box, from roughly eight in the evening until Mirabel was last seen around eleven-thirty. Four witnesses placed her at the fire. Two of them—Tommy Reyes among them, though his statement was thin, nervous, half a page long—said she'd been drinking beer. One said wine coolers. Nobody said much of anything else, because nobody had been asked much of anything else.

But 0.11 was not a night of casual beer at a bonfire. Not for a woman of Mirabel's build—one hundred thirty-one pounds, noted right there in the report in Paloma's own younger handwriting. To reach 0.11 and still be walking, talking, arguing—there was a witness statement in here, she remembered now, someone had heard raised voices near the water—that woman would have needed to drink hard and fast, the equivalent of five, six drinks in under two hours, and then navigate a quarter mile of rutted quarry track in the dark without stumbling once before entering the water.

Or she hadn't drunk it herself.

The thought arrived without permission and sat down in Paloma's chest like a guest who intended to stay.

* * *

She turned to the external examination findings and made herself read every line, including the ones young Paloma had clearly decided meant nothing.

Contusions, left upper arm, medial aspect: two, roughly oval, 2.5 cm and 3 cm, patterned, consistent with fingertip pressure. Contusion, right upper arm, posterior aspect: 4 cm, irregular. Petechial hemorrhages in the conjunctivae. Abrasions on both wrists—"consistent with post-mortem movement against substrate," the report said, in Emory Ladd's dictated cadence, transcribed by young Paloma's typing fingers.

Fingertip bruises on the inside of both arms. The kind you get when someone grips you from the front, hands closing around your arms, either to hold you up or hold you still. And wrist abrasions explained away as the body drifting against rocks—as if bodies drifted with their wrists forward, as if the quarry pond bottom were a cheese grater arranged conveniently at both pulse points.

Paloma laid her own hands flat on the desk and looked at them. These same hands, eleven years younger, had examined that body. Had pressed the skin around those bruises, noted their age—perimortem, the report said, inflicted at or near the time of death—and then written *manner: accident* at the bottom anyway.

What had she told herself? She searched for it honestly, the way you probe a sore tooth. She'd told herself that drunks grab each other. That a tipsy girl stumbling on rocks could absolutely present with grip marks if someone tried to catch her. That petechiae happened in drownings sometimes, that struggle was its own explanation, that the Halloways were grieving and the town wanted peace and the water was right there, so obviously, so conveniently full of answers.

She'd told herself that a rich family's tragedy was none of her business beyond the paperwork.

Shame came up through her like groundwater, cold and rising, finding every crack.

* * *

By ten o'clock she had the file spread across her desk in chronological strata and a legal pad filling with notes in the tight handwriting she used when she didn't trust herself to be calm. Ruthie knocked twice, saw the spread, and hovered in the doorway with a manila folder of her own.

"County board faxed over the quarterly forms," Ruthie said. Her eyes went to the box spine. To the name. Back to Paloma. "You're really doing this."

"I'm reading a file, Ruthie."

"You're reading *that* file." Ruthie came in far enough to close the door behind her, which she only did when she wanted to say something she didn't want the hallway to hear. "Paloma. I walked that cemetery with you yesterday. I let you have your morning. But I'm telling you as your deputy and as somebody who loves you—Daniel Halloway is dead. Whatever he wrote, he wrote it with a dying brain or a cruel one, and the person it hurts most is standing right there." She nodded at the photograph clipped inside the file's front cover: Mirabel at seventeen, school-picture smile, hair the color of dark honey. "You're going to dig all this up and pour it back over her family for nothing."

"For nothing," Paloma repeated. She turned the report around and slid it across the desk. "Read page four."

Ruthie read. Paloma watched her face do what faces did when they encountered restraint bruising in a drowning case: nothing at first, the professional blankness, and then the small involuntary tightening at the mouth.

"Drunks grab each other," Ruthie said finally, but she said it the way people repeat a phrase they've been handed and don't believe.

"Both arms. Symmetric. Perimortem." Paloma tapped the blood alcohol line. "And explain this to me. Explain how a hundred-and-thirty-pound woman gets to point-one-one on beer at a bonfire and still walks a quarter mile downhill in the dark."

"People do it every weekend."

"Not cleanly. Not without a single stumble, a single scraped knee, a single torn hem—and there's no clothing damage in this report except the wrists." She heard her own voice sharpening and made herself stop. Made herself breathe. "I'm not saying I know what happened. I'm saying I don't know, and I signed my name to a version where nobody had to find out."

Ruthie set the report down carefully, aligning its edges with the desk edge, a tidiness that was really a retreat. "Eleven years ago you were following Emory's lead and the sheriff's request. That's not a crime, Paloma. That's called being twenty-nine."

"It's called being willing." The words came out quieter than she expected, and truer. "There's a difference between being fooled and wanting to be fooled. I wanted the easy answer. The Halloways wanted the easy answer. The town wanted it. So we all held the door for each other and called it grief."

Ruthie stood there a moment longer. Something moved behind her eyes—not agreement, something more like recognition, quickly shuttered. "Just—whatever you think you're opening, open it slow," she said. "Some doors have people standing behind them."

Then she was gone, and Paloma sat with the echo of that, wondering why it sounded less like caution than like confession.

* * *

She spent the afternoon pulling the peripheral records. Weather logs from the regional station: the night of the fourteenth had been clear, calm, wind under five miles an hour. No storm to explain a slip. No rain to explain mud on shoes that might account for the absence of other injuries.

Then the witness statements, and this time she read them not for what they said but for what they'd been allowed to say. Every interview was short. Half a page, most of them. Sheriff Dunmore's office had conducted them all, and Dunmore's questions survived only as paraphrase, but the shape of them was visible in the answers like footprints in snow: *Did you see Mirabel leave? Did she seem drunk? Was anyone fighting?*

Nobody had asked Tommy Reyes why his statement was the shortest of all, when he was the last one to see her alive.

Nobody had asked why Mirabel's car was still at the quarry when she supposedly walked there alone at night—a detail that appeared nowhere in the file, but which surfaced now in Paloma's memory with the awful clarity of something noticed and shelved: the little green Civic parked at the gate, unlocked, her purse on the seat. Who leaves their purse to walk to the water?

A woman who means to come back. Or a woman who isn't walking anywhere under her own power.

Paloma put down the statements and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations. Eleven years. Eleven years of Mirabel Halloway lying in the ground under a stone that said *Beloved Daughter and Sister*, while the truth lay in a basement box saying *look at me, look at me,* and Paloma had walked past that box a thousand times on her way to other files, other deaths, other families who didn't have lawyers and galas and a foundation named after their generosity.

Her younger self had signed off. There was no undoing that. But shame, she thought, lowering her hands, was only useful if it curdled. If it soured into something you could act on. Otherwise it was just another way of keeping the grave quiet—grieving your own failure instead of fixing it.

She picked up the phone and dialed the state repository, requesting certified copies of everything: the autopsy, the toxicology workup from the state lab, the scene photographs. Then she pulled the quarry property records, because a thought had begun forming, cold and specific: the pond had been dredged and regraded two years after Mirabel died—for a development that never got built. The landscape of the original scene no longer existed. Which meant that whatever the water kept, it kept it in paper, and in the bodies of the living.

One more call. She hesitated over it, pen hovering, then dialed the number for the Halloway house that she'd known since childhood, the way everyone in Marrow Creek knew it, the way you know the number of the big house on the hill without ever expecting to use it.

Elena answered on the fourth ring. Her voice was careful, smoothed flat, a voice holding itself very still.

"Mrs. Halloway. This is Paloma Vasquez. I'm sorry to bother you so soon after—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm reviewing some records related to Mirabel's case. Routine. I wonder if I might come by and ask you a few questions."

The silence on the line lasted three seconds. Long enough for Paloma to hear, beneath the widow's composure, something shift—a held breath, a decision being made in real time.

"Of course," Elena said. "Tomorrow morning. Come at ten." A pause, and then, softer, almost too quiet to catch: "There's a desk in Daniel's study. I've been meaning to ask someone about it."

The line went dead before Paloma could ask what she meant.
