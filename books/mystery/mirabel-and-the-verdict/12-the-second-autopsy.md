## 12. The Second Autopsy

The request went through channels that had no names attached to them.

Paloma had learned, over nineteen years in the coroner's office of Marrow Creek County, that bureaucracy was not merely a nuisance—it was a language, and like any language it could be spoken softly or loudly, precisely or vaguely, depending on what you wanted heard and by whom. A formal exhumation order for Daniel Halloway would have echoed through three offices before lunch. It would have reached Cordelia Halloway before the coffee in her porcelain cup went cold.

So Paloma did not file an exhumation order. She filed a *records request*—a routine administrative review of a death certificate issued within the county's jurisdiction, standard practice following the receipt of new documentary evidence in an adjacent matter. She cited the confession letter without naming its contents. She signed nothing that required her signature to be witnessed. And she drove forty minutes to the state regional archive in Calloway herself, on a Tuesday morning when the fog sat so low on Route 9 that the world seemed to exist only in a corridor ten feet wide, her headlights carving through it like hands parting a curtain.

Ruthie hadn't come in that day. Ruthie hadn't really *come in*, in any meaningful sense, for two weeks—not since the confrontation in the break room, since the words *your mother worked for them* had landed between them like something dropped from a great height. Paloma had left voicemails. Two of them. Both polite. Both hollow as tin. She told herself she'd stopped calling because Ruthie needed space, but the truth was uglier: Paloma was glad, in some small shameful chamber of herself, that Ruthie wasn't there to see what she was about to do. Glad there would be no witness. Because if Daniel Halloway's body told her what she suspected it might tell her, then everything changed—not just the old case, not just the town's tidy memory, but the shape of the danger itself. A confession written by a dead man was a mystery. A dead man who may have been *made* dead was a warning.

The archivist at Calloway was a young man with wire-rimmed glasses and the unhurried manner of someone who had never been lied to by anyone important. He wheeled out the transfer box marked HALLOWAY, D. — 2024 — MC COUNTY CORONER'S OFFICE, set it on the reading table, and left her alone with it under the hum of fluorescent lights.

Paloma washed her hands before she opened it. An old habit, half superstition, half respect. You handled the dead clean, even when all you were handling was paper.

* * *

Daniel Halloway had died on the fourteenth of March, at home, in the big house on Wren Hollow Road where he and Mirabel had grown up. The responding paramedics noted he was found in his study, seated in his leather chair, a book open face-down on the armrest. Elena had called it in at 6:47 in the evening. She said she'd gone up to change for dinner and come down to find him unresponsive. No signs of struggle. No disturbance. A man who simply stopped.

The original autopsy had been performed by Dr. Anselm Petty, the contract pathologist the county used when the caseload overflowed—which was always—and Paloma read his report twice through, slowly, the way you reread a letter from someone you loved after they've died, looking for what you missed the first time.

Cause of death: acute myocardial infarction. Cardiac arrest secondary to coronary artery disease. Daniel had been fifty-eight, overweight in the soft way of men who drank two fingers of good scotch every night and played golf in a cart. His arteries were, per Petty's own gross description, "moderately diseased." Not catastrophically. Moderately. There was a word doing quiet work in that sentence, and Paloma circled it in pencil and stared at it for a long time.

Because here was the thing she knew, and knew deeply, from twenty years of standing over bodies while other people guessed at their stories: moderate disease kills people eventually, and sometimes suddenly, but it does not usually kill them *cleanly*. A man dying of a massive infarction claws at his chest. He vomits. He falls, or he slides, or he grips the desk hard enough to bruise his fingertips. The body fights, even when the mind has given up. But Petty's report described no vomitus, no petechial hemorrhaging beyond what was expected, no evidence of the violent final struggle between a heart and its ending. Daniel Halloway had died, according to every line of this report, the way people die in novels: quietly, mid-chapter, book still open.

And then there was the toxicology.

The initial screen had come back negative for the standard panel—alcohols, opiates, amphetamines, benzodiazepines, cocaine. Negative, negative, negative. A tidy row of negatives, each one a little door closing. But Paloma noticed that the panel had been run at Meridian Labs—the same commercial lab the county had used for eleven years, the same lab whose annual contract renewal was approved by the county board, chaired these days by Marcus Halloway-Prue, Cordelia's nephew. She noticed, too, that the blood draw had been taken nine hours post-mortem, which was within protocol but far from ideal, and that the sample volume logged was notably small.

She noticed one more thing, and this was the thing that made her sit back in the plastic chair and press both palms flat against the table as though the room had tilted.

In the microscopic section, buried in Petty's description of the cardiac tissue, was a single sentence: *"Focal areas of myocyte vacuolization noted; etiology unclear; possibly artifactual."*

Possibly artifactual. The great shrug of pathology. *We saw something strange and we decided not to look at it.*

Paloma had spent enough evenings with veterinary toxicology journals—leftovers from a phase years ago when she'd nearly lost a malpractice suit over a livestock poisoning case—to know what myocyte vacuolization could mean when it wasn't artifact. Certain compounds didn't leave fingerprints on a standard screen because they weren't looking for them. Cardiac glycosides. Oleandrin. Digoxin analogues. And—her pencil stopped moving—one class of compounds she'd only ever read about in the context of industrial accidents: the cardenolide derivatives developed by Halcyon Therapeutics, the crown jewel of the Halloway Family Foundation's pharmaceutical portfolio, the company whose stock certificates hung framed in Daniel's study like hunting trophies.

Halcyon had a research campus outside Marrow Creek. Half the town worked there or had a cousin who did. They made heart medications. That was the irony, if irony were the right word for it—she wasn't sure anymore that anything in this county deserved so gentle a name. They made drugs that strengthened failing hearts, and somewhere in their pipeline, in the literature she'd once skimmed at 2 a.m., were experimental compounds that could stop a healthy-seeming heart in a way that looked, to a moderately diseased heart's autopsy, exactly like the disease winning.

A compound like that wouldn't show on a standard panel. It would require a targeted assay—expensive, specialized, ordered only when someone specifically thought to ask.

Nobody had asked.

* * *

She drove back through fog that had thickened into something almost solid, wipers useless against air that had turned to milk. Her phone buzzed twice on the console. The first was a calendar reminder: *Gala planning committee — declined.* The second was a text from an unknown number, six words long:

*Some files are better left closed.*

She pulled over at the overlook above the quarry road and sat with the engine running and her hands at ten and two, breathing the way she'd taught herself to breathe during her residency, when a pediatric case had broken something in her that never fully knitted. In through four. Hold. Out through eight. The fog pressed against the windshield like a palm.

They knew. Of course they knew. She had been careful, but careful in Marrow Creek meant only that your footsteps took slightly longer to trace. The records request would have rippled. The archivist would mention it to someone; someone would mention it to Marcus; Marcus would mention it over dinner, and Cordelia would set down her fork and say, in that voice like cooled honey, *Let me handle the doctor.*

But here was the arithmetic that mattered, and Paloma turned it over and over as the fog swallowed the road below: Daniel Halloway had mailed—or caused to be mailed—a confession to Mirabel's drowning. Three days later he was dead. Officially of his heart. Possibly of a compound manufactured by his family's own company, administered by someone with access to his home, his scotch, his evening routine.

If the confession was true—if Daniel had killed his sister eleven years ago—then his death was revenge, or conscience catching up, or an heir tying off a thread. If the confession was false—a plant, a manipulation—then his death was something else entirely, something colder: the silencing of a man who knew too much, dressed in the costume of a confession to point suspicion in a pleasing direction.

Either way, someone had decided that Daniel Halloway's voice was worth more dead than alive.

And either way, the list of people who knew what Paloma now knew was very short, and getting shorter felt like something the wrong people might consider a solution.

* * *

She didn't go home. She went to the office, because home was a cottage on Larkspur Lane with thin walls and a landlord who mowed Cordelia Halloway's lawn on Saturdays, and because the office had a lock she trusted more than most things in her life these days.

At midnight, with the county building dark around her and the radiator ticking like a patient clock, Paloma laid out both cases side by side on her desk—the old file and the new one—and let them speak to each other across eleven years.

Mirabel, twenty-four, drowned in the quarry pond on the last night of June. Blood alcohol of 0.19, per the original report. Bruising on both wrists and the left upper arm, consistent—Paloma had written, at thirty-one years old, with a hand that wanted very much to go home—"consistent with post-mortem recovery efforts." Recovery efforts. As if the men who'd pulled her body out had gripped her wrists hard enough to bruise deep tissue, in the dark, through water. She had known better. Some part of her, even then, had known better, and she had signed the ruling anyway because the sheriff signed it first and the Halloway attorney reviewed it second and her mortgage was due on the first of the month.

Daniel, fifty-eight, dead in his chair. Moderate arterial disease. Vacuolized myocytes. A tox screen full of doors that had never been opened.

Two autopsies. One family. One pattern: a death that fit the story everyone needed, examined just shallowly enough to keep fitting.

Paloma got up and stood at the window. Below, Main Street lay empty under the streetlamps, wet-looking in the fog, the marquee of the Orpheum Theater glowing faintly with next month's community calendar. Somewhere out there was Elena Halloway, in the house on Wren Hollow Road, sleeping alone in a bed beside a man-shaped absence, having sworn to mail a letter upon her husband's death and then—perhaps—made sure of the death herself. Somewhere out there was Cordelia, seventy-six years old and sharp as cut glass, who had offered Paloma tea and a version of history so smooth it went down without swallowing. Somewhere was Tommy Reyes, two counties away, silent behind a changed name. Somewhere was Ruthie, not answering her phone.

And somewhere—this was the thought that finally made her cold, colder than the fog, colder than the office at midnight—was whoever had access to Halcyon's compounds. Because those substances didn't walk out of a research campus. They were logged, locked, inventoried. Which meant the person who used one either stole it, which would leave a trail, or *signed for it*, which meant the trail led somewhere with a title on the door.

Paloma returned to her desk and began, methodically, to copy everything. Scanner humming, page by page, both files, plus her own notes, plus photographs of every document onto her personal phone—redundancy upon redundancy, the paranoia of a woman who had finally understood that the truth was not merely unwelcome in Marrow Creek but *insured against*, with premiums paid by people who could afford anything.

By 3 a.m. she had a single page of conclusions, handwritten, because handwriting couldn't be hacked:

1. D.H.'s death inconsistent with natural MI presentation (no terminal struggle; clean presentation).
2. Myocyte vacuolization = possible cardenolide exposure. Standard tox insufficient. Recommend targeted assay for Halcyon-class compounds.
3. Confession postmarked 3 days before D.H.'s death. Sequence: confession → death. Silencing is plausible.
4. Compound source likely internal to Halcyon/foundation.
5. I may be on a list.

She looked at number five for a long time. Then she folded the page into thirds and slid it inside the false bottom of her desk drawer, beneath the spare toner cartridge, where she kept the things she wasn't ready to be found holding.

The clock on the wall said 3:17. Outside, the fog was lifting off Main Street in slow rags, and the first gray suggestion of dawn was touching the roofline of the courthouse across the square—the courthouse where, eleven years ago, a younger Paloma Vasquez had raised her right hand and testified that Mirabel Halloway's death was an accident, and meant it, mostly, and slept fine afterward, mostly, for eleven years.

Her phone lit on the desk. Not a text this time. A call. From the number that had sent the warning, hours ago.

It rang once. Twice. On the third ring, Paloma picked it up and said nothing, and after a moment a voice said nothing back—just breathing, slow and deliberate, and then, almost gently, a woman's voice, familiar in a way that made her stomach drop before her mind caught up:

"Doctor Vasquez," Elena Halloway said. "I think we should talk. Before the anniversary. Before they decide it's too late for talking."

The line went dead.

Paloma sat in the blue dark of her office, the copied files stacked neat as headstones beside her, and understood that whatever came next, the clock had already been running for a long time—and she had only just heard it start.
