## 16. Tommy's Testimony

The truck stop sat at the junction of Route 9 and the county line, a slab of sodium light in a sea of dark pines, and Paloma had been sitting in her car outside it for forty minutes watching the pay phone.

She'd found him the way she'd found everything lately—by refusing to stop. The name change had cost him nothing but a trip to the courthouse in Ashford County: Thomas Reyes, born Marrow Creek, now Thomas Rye, employed as a night mechanic for a freight company that ran refrigerated trailers up and down the eastern seaboard. His coworkers, when she'd called the depot posing as an insurance verifier, said he was reliable, quiet, ate his meals alone. One of them said he didn't like to be touched on the shoulder. Startled easy.

He'd missed their meeting yesterday. She'd waited two hours at the diner off Exit 14 with a cup of coffee going cold and a folder going soft in her hands, and when he hadn't come she'd driven back through the rain telling herself it was over, that she'd pushed too hard, that a man who changed his name and flinched from shoulders did not come back for second invitations. Then, at one in the morning, unable to sleep, she'd pulled the file again and noticed what she should have noticed before: the address he'd given her dispatcher was fake, but the truck route wasn't. Route 9 passed within four miles of Marrow Creek. Twice a week, Thomas Rye drove past the town that had made him into someone else.

So she waited by the pay phones, because a man who'd gone dark would not answer a cell phone registered to a dead name, but a man driving a route twice a week might still call his mother from a pay phone where no one could trace the number.

At 11:52, a rig rolled in, air brakes sighing, and a man climbed down from the cab. He was thirty now, thick through the shoulders in a way the boy in the yearbook photo had never been, but she knew him by the way he moved—head down, scanning the lot once, twice, the old habit of a kid who'd learned early that being seen was expensive. He fed coins into the pay phone, dialed from memory, and said something she couldn't hear into the receiver.

Paloma got out of the car and walked toward him across the wet asphalt, and she watched him see her in the chrome trim of the ice machine, watch his whole body go rigid, the receiver still at his ear.

"Tommy," she said. "Please."

He hung up without saying goodbye. "I told you I couldn't do this." His voice was low, roughened by eleven years of cigarettes she could smell on his jacket. "You shouldn't have come here. You shouldn't have found me."

"I know."

"You don't." He looked past her at the highway, at the long dark throat of it running north. "You don't know anything about what you're doing. That's the worst part. You think you're the only one who ever tried."

The words landed harder than any threat had. She thought of Ruthie's face in her office, bitter and frightened: *You dig only when it's safe.* She thought of the notes on her windshield, polite as funeral flowers. "Then tell me," she said. "Tell me what happened to the others who tried."

Tommy Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Behind them, the truck stop hummed its fluorescent hymn; a driver inside laughed at something on the television. Somewhere out there, Mirabel Halloway lay under a stone that said *Beloved Daughter and Sister*, which was true and insufficient and both those things at once.

"There's a booth inside," he said finally. "By the windows. I'm not talking in your car. I'm not talking anywhere with a door."

* * *

He ordered coffee he didn't drink and sat with his hands wrapped around the mug like it was something that might be taken from him. Up close, under the bad light, she could see the boy still living in his face—the lanky fourteen-year-old from the bonfire photographs, the one standing at the edge of every group shot, half a step outside the circle even then.

"I've been driving past that town for six years," he said. "This route. Twice a week. And I have never once stopped. Do you understand what that takes? There's a turnoff at mile marker 31 where you can see the water tower, and I keep my eyes on the road like my life depends on it, because it did. It does." He turned the mug a quarter rotation. "You want to know why I left? Everybody in Marrow Creek has a story. My mother tells people we moved for my father's work. My father was dead eight months by then. She tells people anyway, because the story is easier than the truth, and in Marrow Creek everybody's got an easier story."

"The Halloways paid your family's debts," Paloma said quietly. "Your mother's hospital bills."

His laugh had no humor in it at all. "My sister's leukemia. Nineteen ninety-eight through 2001. You want to know what that costs? Nobody in my family ever said the number out loud, but I did the math when I was older. It was more than my father made in five years laying pipe. And then Mirabel died, and my mother came home from the Halloway house—she cleaned for them, Tuesdays and Fridays—and there was an envelope on the kitchen table with all of it in it. Every bill. Paid. Plus enough to move us somewhere the bills couldn't find us again. And a note in beautiful handwriting." His jaw worked. "*With our deepest gratitude for your discretion.* Gratitude. Like we'd done them a favor. Like my sister's life was a service they'd purchased."

"And you saw something," Paloma said. "That night at the quarry."

Tommy closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he let them be wet, which told her more than tears would have—he'd spent eleven years rationing this, and he was done rationing.

"It was the end of June," he said. "The bonfire was dying down. I'd been down by the water with some of the younger kids, skipping rocks, and I went up the path to get a soda from the cooler, and they were up there. At the edge. Daniel and Mirabel." He said the names like they were stones he'd carried so long he'd forgotten they were heavy. "They were arguing. Not yelling—that's the thing nobody understands. It was quiet. That's how I knew it was real. Kids yell. Grown-ups who are really angry get quiet, and this was the quietest fight I ever heard. He had her arm. Her upper arm. And she kept pulling it, trying to get loose, and he wouldn't let go, and she said—I'll never lose this, I've tried for eleven years to lose it—she said, *'You don't own me. None of you own me. I'm ending it, Danny. All of it.'*"

Paloma's pen had stopped moving. She hadn't noticed. "Ending what?"

"That's what I thought. I thought she meant the party, or the argument, or—" He shook his head slowly. "But I've had eleven years in trucks to think about it. She didn't mean the night. She meant the *trust*. The money. All of it. She was going to dissolve it or give it away or walk out with nothing, I don't know exactly—but I know what it did to him. His face. I was fourteen and I knew what I was looking at, and I've spent my whole adult life pretending I didn't. He looked at her like she was burning down his house."

"What happened then?"

"Somebody came up the path—one of the cousins, I think, I heard voices—and I ducked behind the rock pile like a coward, and when I looked again they were walking toward the water. Toward the edge. And I told myself it was fine, they were just talking, families talk, and then—" His voice caught and steadied. "And then maybe twenty minutes later I saw Daniel coming back up the path. Alone. And he was wet to the elbows."

The truck stop noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Paloma sat very still.

"Wet to the elbows," she repeated.

"To here." Tommy touched his forearms below the elbow. "Sleeves pushed up. And his shirt was damp at the front, like he'd leaned against something wet. And he walked past me—he didn't see me, I was behind the rocks—and he stopped and stood there looking at the fire, and he was breathing like a man who'd been running. And then Cordelia came and put her hand on his shoulder and said something to him, and he nodded, and they both smiled at somebody walking by, and that was it. That was the whole thing. Two minutes. I was fourteen years old and I watched a murder get folded up and put away like laundry."

"Why didn't you say anything?" Paloma asked, though she already knew, though the knowing was the grief of it. "Tommy. You were a child. If you'd come to anyone—"

"To who?" He spread his hands. "To Sheriff DeWitt, whose campaign the Halloways funded? To the coroner's office?" He didn't look at her when he said it, and the not-looking was worse than looking. "To you? Paloma, I'm not saying this to hurt you. But everybody in that town knew which doors opened and which doors didn't. My mother knew. Your family knew—you think I didn't hear it, growing up? *The Vasquezes owe the Halloways.* It was the water we all swam in. So when the envelope came, my mother took it, and I let her take it, and we moved, and I changed my name, and I told myself for years that staying quiet was the price of my sister being alive. And maybe it was. Maybe it was a fair price. But you asked me once if I'd testify." He finally met her eyes. "Here's what I need you to understand. It wasn't fear that kept me quiet. Fear, I could've lived with. It was that they didn't threaten us. They never had to. They just paid, and we just accepted, and that was the whole machinery. A whole town bought off one debt at a time. The doctor who signed things. The lawyer who lost papers. The coroner who—" He stopped himself, jaw tight.

"Say it," Paloma said.

"The coroner who was young and grateful and wanted to believe the best about people she loved." He said it gently, which somehow made it land like a blow. "I used to hate you. Did you know that? For years. And then I grew up and got tired, and I realized hating you was just another way of not looking at the water tower. You weren't the machine, Paloma. You were a part in it, same as my mother's envelope, same as my silence. We were all parts."

Outside, a rig pulled out, headlights sweeping the window, and for a moment his face was lit and then dark again, like a slide changing in a projector.

"They'll come for you now," he said. "Not with threats—they don't do threats, that's amateur hour. They'll come with kindness. With offers. With your sister, maybe, or your job, or some favor you forgot you owed. Cordelia doesn't break legs. She settles accounts." He pulled a napkin toward him and wrote something on it—a date, a time, a rest area number. "If it goes to court, I'll testify. I've decided. I decided yesterday, actually, which is why I didn't show—I sat in the parking lot of that diner for an hour trying to talk myself out of it, and I couldn't decide, so I drove my route instead. But I'm deciding now." He slid the napkin across the table. "One condition. When you write it all down—and you will, it's all over your face—write my sister's name in it somewhere. Not what she cost. Just her name. She didn't ask to be the reason a town stayed quiet."

Paloma took the napkin. Her hand was steady, which surprised her. "What was her name?"

"Lucia." He almost smiled. "She's a nurse now. Oncology. She spends her days paying forward a debt she doesn't know exists."

* * *

Paloma sat in her car in the emptying lot for a long time after his rig pulled north, the heater ticking, the napkin on the passenger seat like a small white flag of surrender—or a summons.

*Wet to the elbows.*

It fit. God help her, it fit everything—the restraint bruising on Mirabel's forearms, the blood alcohol that didn't match the witnesses, the entry point at the quarry that the water itself had argued against. Daniel had gone in after her, or held her under, or dragged her from the shallows where she'd struck her head—some version of it, some terrible version—and then walked back up the path with the pond still on his skin, and his aunt had smoothed his collar and aimed his smile at the crowd, and the machinery had done the rest. Eleven years of it. Envelopes and signatures and easier stories.

Her phone buzzed against the console. Unknown number. She almost didn't answer.

"Dr. Vasquez." A woman's voice, warm and unhurried, and Paloma knew it before the introduction came—had heard it at every memorial, every gala, every Sunday service where the Halloway pew sat front and center. Aunt Cordelia. "I understand you've been doing some traveling. Some interviewing. I'd hate for all that effort to go to waste." A pause, silk over steel. "Come to tea on Thursday. I have a version of events I think you'll find... comforting."

The line went dead before Paloma could answer.

She looked out at the highway, at the long dark road running north past mile marker 31, past the water tower, past the town that had bought its peace one envelope at a time. Then she picked up the napkin, folded it carefully, and slid it into the file beside Lucia's unnamed debt and Mirabel's unquiet grave.

Thursday, then. Tea with the spider in the center of the web.

She started the engine and drove south, away from the light, carrying the only weapon anyone had ever given her that couldn't be bought: a witness who had finally stopped keeping his eyes on the road.
