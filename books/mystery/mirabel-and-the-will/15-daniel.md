## 15. Daniel

The locket sat on Wren's workbench for three days before she could touch it again.

She had tried to work. That was the discipline of her life — hands busy, mind quiet. There was a half-restored sloop in the boatyard, her hull stripped to bare ribs like something waiting to be reborn or buried, and Wren stood over her each morning with sandpaper and steam and the particular mercy of wood that only wanted shaping. But every time she bent to the task, the locket's chain caught the light, and the light was the same light as 1994, and her hands stopped.

So instead she sat at the kitchen table of her cottage with the envelope Edith Marlowe had left her, and she read it a second time, and then a third, because the third time she finally saw what grief had blurred.

*You were the child of the tide, and I was your witness.*

Not *I am sorry for what happened.* Not *forgive me for my part in it*, though that sentence was there too, further down, in ink gone brown at the edges like old blood. It was the first line that mattered. *Your witness.* As if Edith had been standing on the shore all along, watching Wren drown slowly for thirty years and calling it protection.

Wren set the letter down. Her tea had gone cold again; she noticed she'd made it and never drunk it, the way she'd been making tea and not drinking it for three days, small ceremonies of a body trying to remember its own rhythms while the mind was elsewhere, thirty years elsewhere, at the tide pools in the rain.

She had been fourteen. She knew this the way you know your own height — not as information but as fact worn smooth by handling. What she did not know, what she had spent three decades not knowing with the ferocious dedication of a woman bailing out a boat, was what came after the scream.

There had been a scream. She was almost sure of the scream.

And there had been Daniel Voss, fourteen too, all elbows and fury, who had come to Greywater that summer the way weather comes — suddenly, and changing everything. His mother rented the blue house on Channel Street and mostly wasn't in it. He was supposed to be a nuisance. He was supposed to be the townie girl's summer torment, the rich-adjacent boy who'd break crab traps and leave them tangled on the mudflats. Instead he had found her at the tide pools on the third day of July and said, with the shocking directness of children, "You're always alone. Why are you always alone?"

Nobody had ever asked her that. Nobody had asked it since.

Wren pressed her palms flat against the table until the wood bit back. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, rain came off the harbor in long grey sheets, and the gulls wheeled and complained, and the world went on being ordinary, which felt like an insult.

*I was your witness.*

Witness to what? That was the question she'd been circling for days like a gull over a fish shoal, unable to dive. Because there were two possibilities and they could not both be true, and one of them would unmake her.

Possibility one: Daniel Voss drowned at the tide pools in the summer of 1994, and Wren Calloway — fourteen years old, wild-haired, motherless in every way that counted — had watched it happen, or caused it, or failed to stop it, and Edith Marlowe had arrived afterward and built a wall of silence around the child so high and so thick that Wren had lived inside it ever since without knowing the walls were there.

Possibility two—

Possibility two was the birthday cards.

She hadn't thought about the birthday cards in years. They belonged to the strange warm stratum of her childhood, the part that didn't hurt: the summers after, when Edith had taken her in the way Edith took in everything stray — the winters she'd let Wren sleep in the window seat of Marlowe House when her own mother's boyfriends made the cottage unbearable, the hot chocolate with too much nutmeg, the way Edith had taught her knots and constellations and how to swear properly in two languages. And once — once, when Wren was maybe sixteen, snooping where teenagers snoop, looking for nothing — she had found a drawer in Edith's desk full of birthday cards. Dozens of them. Thirty years of them, some of them, which couldn't be right, because Edith had bought them all in advance, hadn't she, the way organized people did?

They were signed *Edie*. Every one. In handwriting that changed over the decades — the loop of the E tightening, the letters going smaller and more careful as the hand aged — but always *Edie*, and always addressed to the same name on the front, a name Wren had seen and registered and, with the magnificent self-preservation of the young, immediately forgotten.

Or not forgotten. Buried. Filed in the same flooded room as the scream.

Wren got up so fast her chair went over backward. She didn't right it. She went to the coat hooks, took down her oilskin, and walked out into the rain without closing the door behind her.

* * *

Marlowe House stood at the end of the shingle spit like a ship run aground and lovingly maintained by no one, its shutters peeling, its chimneys leaning, its garden gone to seed since Edith died — except that it hadn't gone to seed, Wren saw now, crossing the wet grass. Someone had been cutting back the rosemary. Someone had tied the dahlias to stakes. The beds were weedy but not abandoned, tended with the irregular devotion of a person with other things on their mind.

The lawyer's car was parked under the lean-to. Ansel Sterling. The stranger from the city with Edith's name in her will and a face like a closed ledger. Wren had watched her from the boatyard these past weeks, the way everyone in Greywater watched everyone, and had catalogued her the way she catalogued hulls: sound construction, hidden damage, riding low in the water. Carrying something heavy.

Good, Wren thought grimly, letting herself in through the garden door Edith had never once locked for her. Let her carry it. Let us both carry it.

The hall smelled of dust and beeswax and, faintly, of the lilies someone kept replacing on the table — fresh flowers in a dead woman's house, week after week, a tenderness nobody claimed. Wren stood in the dimness dripping rainwater and listened. A floorboard settled upstairs. Or didn't. The lawyer might be anywhere in this labyrinth of a house, and Wren found she didn't care, found that shame had burned clean through courtesy the way a fire burns through a deck.

Edith's study was where it had always been, at the back, overlooking the garden, the desk with its green leather top and its brass lamps and its drawers that locked with small satisfying clicks. The key had lived in the second drawer down for as long as Wren had known the house, because Edith believed locks were for people with something to hide, and had said so while hiding things in unlocked drawers her whole life.

The drawer stuck. Then gave. Inside: rubber bands, spare keys, a tin of peppermints gone soft with age — and the birthday cards.

Wren knelt on the cold floorboards and lifted them out into her lap like something rescued from water.

There were more than she remembered. Forty, fifty, sixty — bundled loosely by decade with string gone brittle. She untied the first bundle with fingers that wouldn't quite obey her, and the cards fanned across her knees, and she looked at the names on their fronts, and the room tilted.

*Daniel. My darling Daniel. Danny-boy. To Daniel, with all my love, Edie.*

Every card. Every year. From his fourteenth birthday — the card dated July 1994, never sent, its envelope still sealed, a birthday he had apparently not lived to have — through his fifteenth, sixteenth, twentieth, thirtieth, up to a card dated just last year, addressed to *Daniel, wherever you are*, in a hand gone shaky as a spider's walk.

Daniel Voss had birthdays for forty years in this drawer, and Wren had grown up in this house, and no one had ever told her.

Her breath came wrong. She put a hand against the desk to steady herself and her elbow caught the lamp, and the lamp rocked, and beneath it she saw the edge of paper that shouldn't be there — a photograph slid flat under the base, hidden the way Edith hid things, in plain sight of anyone who knew her and invisible to anyone who didn't.

Wren pulled it out.

Two children on the shingle beach, squinting into sun. A boy with a cowlick and a gap-toothed grin, holding up a crab like a trophy. A girl beside him, hair full of salt wind, laughing so hard her eyes had disappeared. She knew the girl's laugh from the inside. She knew the crab. She remembered the day — the last good day, the day before the quarrel, before whatever had happened at the pools happened — and she had thought the photograph lost forever, because she'd never owned it, because who had taken it?

Edith. Edith had taken it. Edith had kept it under her lamp for thirty years, next to forty unsent birthday cards for a dead boy, and had looked at it every evening of her long careful life, and had never once said to Wren, *do you remember him?*

Unless.

Unless there was nothing to remember. Unless the boy in the photograph had not stayed dead.

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do — fully formed, fully armed, refusing to be unbuilt. Wren sat down hard on the study floor among the scattered birthdays and held the photograph at arm's length as if distance could make it lie to her.

Daniel Voss didn't drown.

It was insane. It was impossible. The town had mourned him; she remembered the candles in windows, the search boats, the way her own mother had gripped her shoulder too hard and said *you weren't down there, were you, tell me you weren't down there* — and Wren had said no, had lied, had not known why she was lying, only that Edith's hand on her other shoulder had tightened like a warning, like a promise.

But if he hadn't drowned — if Edith had staged it, hidden him, raised him here, in this house, behind these walls, while Wren slept in the window seat ten feet away —

The rage came up through her like bilge water, sudden and filthy. All those years. All those nights she'd lain awake hearing footsteps overhead and told herself it was the house settling. The locked north rooms. The second place setting some mornings, which Edith had laughed off — *an old habit, I grew up in a big family* — while the porridge steamed and Wren ate alone. The boy's clothing she'd once found in a cedar chest, outgrown, salt-stiffened, and been told were donations awaiting collection. Donations. Thirty years of donations.

She had been a child of the tide. Edith had written it herself. And another child had lived in the dry rooms above her head, fed and schooled and celebrated with a drawer full of birthdays, while Wren got the window seat and the silences and, eventually, the lifelong conviction that she had done something so terrible at the tide pools that even the woman who loved her could only save her by never speaking of it again.

*You were the child of the tide, and I was your witness.*

A witness protects. A witness also chooses. Edith had chosen, every day for thirty years, and Wren understood with a clarity that felt like cold water filling her lungs exactly what had been chosen and what had been paid for it: the boy got the family. The girl got the guilt.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty study, to the dead woman's careful handwriting, to the laughing children in the photograph. "No, you don't get to—" But get to what? Get to be dead? Edith had managed even that on her own terms, tidy as a filed alibi.

Wren gathered the cards. She meant to throw them in the fire. She stood at the cold hearth with the first bundle raised and her arm shaking, and she could not do it, because underneath the rage was the other thing, the thing braided through it tight as tarred twine: relief. Vast, shameful, dizzying relief. If Daniel lived, then she had not killed him. Whatever had happened at the pools — the shove, the slip, the scream, the blood or the water, the memory that came apart in her hands whenever she reached for it — it had not ended him. Some frightened fourteen-year-old girl in 1994 had not destroyed a boy. She had carried a corpse in her chest for thirty years and the corpse had gotten up and walked away and eaten breakfast in the next room.

She lowered the cards. She sat back down on the floor and cried the way she hadn't cried since she was fourteen — ugly, gasping, graceless, alone in a dead woman's house with the rain coming down and the sea working at the shingle outside like something patient trying to get in.

When it was done, she wiped her face on her sleeve, and the lawyer was standing in the doorway.

Ansel Sterling held a folder against her chest and looked at Wren the way people look at a fire they've just discovered in an adjacent room. Rain-light moved on her face. Neither of them spoke for a long moment, two strangers in a dead woman's study, surrounded by evidence.

"You found the drawer," Ansel said finally. Not an accusation. Something worse — recognition.

"He didn't drown." Wren's voice came out scraped raw. She held up the photograph, the two laughing children, the trophy crab. "Tell me I'm wrong. You've been digging for weeks. Tell me I'm wrong."

The lawyer didn't answer fast enough, and her not-answering answered everything, and then she crossed the room and knelt — actually knelt, on the dusty boards, in her city clothes — and laid the folder open between them like a hand of cards that could not be taken back.

"I need to show you something," Ansel Sterling said. "And then I think we both need to go to the hospice in Corndale. Because there's a letter there addressed to *the lawyer, when she comes* — and after today, Wren, I'm starting to believe it was written for both of us."

Outside, the tide was turning. Wren could hear it, the long slow exhale of the water giving back what it had borrowed, and for the first time in thirty years she was afraid of what it might return.
