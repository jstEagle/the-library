## 16. Where Is Now

The ledgers were kept in the bottom drawer of Edith's desk, beneath a false lining of yellowed newspaper, and they smelled of camphor and time.

Ansel had been at Marlowe House for eleven days now. The thirty-day condition sat over her like weather. She had learned the house's rhythms — the way the pipes knocked at dawn, the way gulls argued on the chimney at dusk, the way the light came through the parlor windows in long amber bars that made even dust look deliberate. She had also learned to work with the curtains half-drawn. Someone in Greywater was still watching her; she'd seen the curtain twitch across the lane twice this week, and Silas's burned shed stood like a black tooth at the edge of Edith's garden, a reminder that curiosity here had a price.

But the ledgers — the ledgers were worth the price.

There were four of them, bound in green cloth gone soft as felt, their spines cracked from handling. They spanned 1994 to 2019. And they were not household accounts. Ansel knew bookkeeping when she saw it, and this was bookkeeping — careful, columned, annotated in Edith's small upright hand, each entry initialed twice, once in ink and once, later, in pencil, as if she had come back years afterward to confirm her own arithmetic.

The payments began in March 1995.

*To the account,* Edith had written, never naming it. *Annual. As agreed.*

Five hundred pounds the first year. Then seven hundred. Then a thousand, rising by steady increments until, in the last decade, the figure settled at six thousand annually — paid every March, without fail, for twenty-four years. Ansel turned the pages slowly, feeling the paper's grain under her fingertips, watching a sum of money move through the decades like a pulse.

Someone was alive. Someone had been alive, and fed, and kept — quietly, invisibly, for twenty-five years after a boy officially drowned.

*Daniel Voss.*

She said the name aloud to the empty study and heard how strange it sounded, a name spoken in a house where it had never once been spoken above a whisper — if it had been spoken at all.

* * *

The bank records took three days to pry loose, and Ansel used every tool her profession gave her: letters of administration, a notarized request, two phone calls during which she deployed the flat, unhurried voice she saved for opposing counsel. Her partner, Devi, would have told her she was burning billable hours on a dead woman's obsession. Devi had told her exactly that, in fact, on Sunday night, and Ansel had held the phone out over the kitchen counter toward the window, where the sea was doing its gray endless arithmetic against the seawall, and said nothing until Devi sighed and asked what she'd found.

"A boy who didn't drown," Ansel said.

"And you're sure?"

"I'm sure someone was paid to stay gone."

The trail, once she had it, was almost insultingly clean. Edith Marlowe had been more careful than any criminal Ansel had ever encountered, but care leaves its own sediment. The annual transfers had moved from a Greywater building society to a private bank in Jersey, then from Jersey into an account held under a name Ansel didn't recognize — *D. Marsh* — and from there, disbursements. Rent, mostly. Small, regular, unremarkable. A life being funded the way you'd fund a plant on a windowsill: just enough water, never enough to draw attention.

Until five years ago.

Then the pattern changed. The final transfer was larger — a lump sum, eighteen months of payments consolidated — and it went not to rent but to an institution. Ansel read the payee line four times, her tea going cold beside the keyboard.

*St. Brendan's Care Home & Hospice. Harrowgate Bay.*

Two counties up the coast. Four hours by car, less if the fog stayed off the road.

She sat back in Edith's chair — Edith's chair, she was sitting in a dead woman's chair in a dead woman's house reading a dead woman's secrets, and some part of her noted that she no longer found this strange — and let the hope rise in her chest, warm and dangerous.

He might be alive. Daniel Voss, missing since the summer of 1994, certified dead by a weeping old doctor, mourned by no one official and remembered by everyone unofficially — he might be lying in a bed at St. Brendan's right now, sixty-some years old, breathing.

And then the other thought arrived, the one the numbers had already whispered and her heart refused to hear:

Why would Edith stop paying five years ago? Why consolidate, why close the account, unless—

Unless there was no one left to pay for.

Ansel looked at the ledger's last page. In the margin, beside the final entry, Edith had written a single word in pencil so faint Ansel had to tilt the page toward the lamp:

*Enough.*

It could have meant anything. It could have meant *the debt is paid.* It could have meant *he is at peace.* It could have meant nothing at all, one old woman's shorthand to herself.

Or it could have meant *it is finished,* the way you say it at a graveside.

* * *

She drove north the next morning.

The coast road climbed away from Greywater through country that grew progressively more resigned — the hedgerows scrubbier, the villages thinner, the sea visible only in cold silver flashes between hills. Ansel drove with the ledger photocopied and folded in her bag and the name *D. Marsh* written on the back of her hand like a schoolgirl's crib note, which was absurd, and which she did anyway.

Harrowgate Bay announced itself first by smell — diesel and brine and frying batter — and then by sight: a promenade of shuttered amusement arcades, a beach of dark shingle, and above the town, on a rise of wind-bent pines, a long low building of pale brick with a chapel spire behind it. St. Brendan's. Even from the road it looked like a place designed for waiting.

Inside, it smelled of lilies and disinfectant, an alliance of scents that seemed to cancel both out. A receptionist with kind, tired eyes looked up from a crossword.

"I'm looking for a resident," Ansel said. "Possibly a former resident. He may have been admitted around five years ago, under the name Marsh. D. Marsh."

The receptionist's pen hovered. "Are you family?"

Here it was, the question again, the question that had followed her since the will. *Who are you to him? Who are you to her?*

"I'm the executor of the estate that funded his care," Ansel said, and laid her credentials on the desk like a winning hand.

It took forty minutes, two phone calls to an administrator, and a promise signed in triplicate before she was led down a corridor of framed seascapes — all of them painted by residents, the dates penciled on the backs, some signed with names, some with only initials — to a small office where the administrator, a brisk woman named Mrs. Okafor, opened a leather-bound admissions register.

"D. Marsh," Mrs. Okafor read. "Admitted March of that year. Private room, fees settled in advance." She glanced up. "That's unusual, you know. Settling in advance. Most families argue about the invoices."

"Who settled them?"

"The same source throughout. A solicitor's trust, I believe. There was a letter on file from the payer — instructions regarding his records, his privacy." Mrs. Okafor closed the register gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping person. "Mr. Marsh was with us four years."

*Four years.* Five minus four. The arithmetic landed in Ansel's stomach like a stone.

"He died," Ansel said. Not a question.

"Four years ago this spring." Mrs. Okafor's voice softened by a degree. "He was a quiet gentleman. Liked the window seat. The staff were fond of him."

Ansel stared at the register, at the neat entry, at the dates bracketing a life that had officially ended in 1994 and had actually ended here, in a pale brick building above a shingle beach, four years ago — while Edith Marlowe was still alive, still walking her cliff path, still writing birthday cards in a hidden room.

Still paying. No — still *having paid.* The account had closed a year after his death. Which meant Edith had known. Which meant Edith had come here, or written here, or at least received word here, and had folded the ledger shut and written *enough* and carried that knowledge alone back down the coast to Greywater, to a house full of hidden rooms and unsent grief.

"His records," Ansel said. "I'll need everything. Admission notes, medical history, next of kin."

Mrs. Okafor hesitated. "Next of kin is listed as—" she checked a card "—E. Marlowe."

Of course it was. Ansel closed her eyes briefly. Even at the end, even four counties' distance from it, the thread ran back to Marlowe House. Everything ran back to Marlowe House.

"There's something else," Mrs. Okafor said, and now her briskness had a different texture, almost apologetic. "When a resident passes, personal effects go to the listed kin. Miss Marlowe collected most of them herself — she came up on the train, I remember, the week after. But there was one item she instructed us to retain. Written instruction, signed, sealed. She said—" Mrs. Okafor paused, recalling, and her brow creased slightly, as if the words had waited thirty years for this exact visitor. "She said, *keep it until the lawyer comes.*"

The corridor hummed its fluorescent hum. Somewhere down the hall a television murmured to an audience of one.

"She said that?" Ansel asked. "Those words?"

"Near enough." Mrs. Okafor rose. "We keep such things in a locked cabinet. If you'll give me a moment."

Ansel stood alone in the little office while the woman's footsteps receded. Through the window she could see the shingle beach, the gray water working at it patiently, patient as a lawyer with a lifetime to prepare a case. *Keep it until the lawyer comes.* Edith had known. From the beginning — from the will, from the confession letter, from the very first line of whatever design she'd begun drafting decades ago — Edith had known that a lawyer would eventually stand in this room, asking these questions, following this money like a blood scent.

Edith hadn't just hidden a boy. She had built a relay. Every document, every payment, every sealed envelope was a baton passed forward through time, handed from one keeper to the next, all of it aimed at this moment: Ansel Sterling, standing in a hospice office, waiting for a cabinet to open.

Mrs. Okafor returned carrying a shoebox.

Not an archive box, not a legal exhibit box — a shoebox, scuffed at the corners, tied with string gone brittle, and heavy in the way that only paper and metal and memory are heavy. On the lid, in Edith's small upright hand, the same hand that had ruled the ledgers, was written a single line:

*For the lawyer, when she comes. Then it belongs to the tide's child too.*

Ansel untied the string with fingers that were not quite steady.

Inside: photographs. Dozens of them, spanning decades — a gangly teenage boy on the rocks above the tide pools, squinting into sun; the same boy older, laughing on the porch of Marlowe House; a young man holding a fish up proudly; birthdays, Christmases, a whole hidden life documented in snapshots, each one dated on the back in pencil. Beneath the photographs, wrapped in a scrap of blue cloth, lay a locket — tarnished silver, tide-pitted, its chain snapped. Its twin, Ansel thought instantly. Wren Calloway had pulled its twin from the rocks.

And at the bottom of the box, folded into quarters and softened by handling, a letter. The envelope bore no stamp because it had never needed one. It had traveled thirty years inside a shoebox to reach this desk.

It was addressed in Edith's hand:

*To the lawyer, when she comes.*

Ansel held it and did not open it. Not yet. Some documents, she knew from long practice, changed everything that came after them, and you got exactly one chance to be the person you were before you read them.

Outside, the tide was turning. She could see it from the window — the slow reversal, the sea gathering itself to take back everything it had lent the shore.

She broke the seal.
