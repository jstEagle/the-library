## 2. Entries After the End

The Grand Meridian's storage room lived in the basement, past the wine cellar and the dead boiler that no one had ever funded a replacement for. It smelled of cedar blocks, old lavender sachets, and the faint mineral ghost of a hundred years of rain tracked in on guests' coats. Noor had spent enough hours down there to know it by heart — the way you know a friend's breathing.

She signed herself in at 6:47 p.m., after her shift ended but before the night porter arrived, because what she was about to do was not something she wanted witnessed.

The lost-and-found ledger sat on its lectern like a small bible. She had kept it for six years, ever since Salim handed her the keys and said, *You notice things, Noor. Make that useful.* Every item logged: date found, location, description, disposition. Umbrellas. Reading glasses. A prosthetic leg (claimed, gratefully, by a man from Oslo). A wedding ring left deliberately in Room 904 by a woman who did not want it anymore — Noor had mailed it back with a note that said only *He doesn't deserve the symbolism either*, and received a postcard from Lisbon a month later that she still kept in her desk drawer.

Item 4471, logged March 3rd. *One diary, navy leather, gold-edged pages, initials C.M. embossed on cover. Found in the Palm Court, under table 12, 11:20 p.m. Unclaimed.*

Unclaimed because Celeste Marchand had vanished on February 26th.

Noor had not made the connection when she logged it. That was the thing that gnawed at her now, walking down the aisle between shelving units with her flashlight beam sweeping the dust. She noticed everything — she prided herself on it — and yet a diary belonging to a woman who had disappeared four days earlier had passed through her hands and she had filed it like an umbrella. She'd been busy. There had been a wedding, a diplomatic delegation, a pipe leak on seven. Excuses stacked up like wet towels, and none of them held weight.

Detective Okafor's card was in her pocket. She had told him she'd check her records. She had not told him she already knew exactly which shelf the diary lived on.

Box 4471 sat at shoulder height, third aisle, between a crate of abandoned ski equipment and someone's forgotten tax documents. She lifted it down, set it on the worktable beneath the bare bulb, and stood over it for a long moment before opening the flaps.

The diary looked exactly as she remembered. Navy leather gone soft at the corners, gold edges dulled where thumbs had worn them. The elastic band had snapped; the pages were held shut by nothing but their own compression. She could smell the paper from here — ink and hand cream and something faintly floral, jasmine maybe, the kind of scent a woman wore rather than bought.

*Don't,* said the careful voice in her head, the one that sounded like Salim. *This is evidence. Call the detective. Hand it over. Close the loop.*

But another voice, quieter and older, said: *You logged this before anyone knew she was missing. Which means no one has read it who wasn't supposed to. And if there's anything in here about why Celeste Marchand walked out of the Grand Meridian and into nowhere —*

Noor put on a pair of cotton gloves from the restoration kit — habit, ritual, a way of making the moment formal — and opened the cover.

* * *

The handwriting hit her first.

It was lovely. Slanted, confident, loops on the ascenders like a fountain pen user's, though the indentations said ballpoint. It was the kind of handwriting you saw on wedding invitations. And it was wrong.

Noor read guests' handwriting the way sommeliers tasted wine — professionally, constantly, without meaning to. Registration cards, thank-you notes slipped to the front desk, complaint letters, the scrawled *do not disturb* notes housekeepers brought down. She had read thousands of samples, and she knew the tells: how people crossed their sevens, whether their dots floated or punched, where the pressure thinned at the end of a stroke because the writer was hurrying toward the next word.

Celeste Marchand's registration card was still in the archive box downstairs. Noor had pulled it on her lunch break, along with the folio from her stay. The real Celeste wrote fast and slightly cramped, her letters leaning hard rightward like wind-blown grass, her t's crossed with a single impatient slash that often overshot the stem. Her i-dots sat close to the letter, almost touching. She dotted her j's too. Most people didn't bother.

The diary's handwriting leaned rightward too. But the t-crosses were neat, contained, perfectly centered. The i-dots floated high and round, placed with care. And there were no j-dots at all — every j naked, forgotten, in a way that real writers almost never managed across two hundred pages. Forgetting once was human. Forgetting consistently was forgery.

Noor turned pages slowly, the bulb buzzing overhead.

The early entries felt true. February 14th: *The concierge here — Adeyemi — noticed my coat was damp before I did. Either she's brilliant or I've become transparent.* February 19th: *Dinner alone again. The waiter assumed I was waiting for someone and I let him. It was easier than saying no one is coming.* Entries like that made Noor's chest ache in a way she distrusted, because they were exactly the kind of thing a lonely woman staying eleven days in a grand hotel would write, and also exactly the kind of thing a forger would write if he wanted the reader to feel sorry for her.

Then February 26th. The last day anyone saw Celeste Marchand alive.

*Checked out this morning. Or almost. I have one more thing to do and then I'm gone. If I'm honest I'm frightened, but I've been frightened for weeks and it hasn't stopped me yet. The truth is heavy but it's mine to carry. One last meeting. Then home.*

Noor checked the folio. Celeste Marchand had never checked out. Her bill was settled by corporate card through March 1st, her key returned unused, her suitcase — according to Marta, who had serviced the room — gone from the closet, the bed neatly made, the room looking *like she'd evaporated*, in Marta's phrase. People who intended to leave checked out. People who intended to come back left things behind. People who were taken left nothing at all.

And then, three days later, the diary — which should have been in that evaporated suitcase, or in a safe, or anywhere but under a table in the Palm Court — appeared in the hotel's most public room, waiting to be found.

Noor turned to the entries dated after the disappearance, and her mouth went dry.

February 28th. *I needed rest more than I knew. I've decided to stay away a while longer. Some debts can't be paid from a distance.* March 2nd. *I keep thinking about the twelfth floor. Why does nobody go up there?* March 4th. *I saw him today. He looked older. Softer. Almost kind. Maybe I was wrong about everything.*

A dead woman's handwriting, narrating a life she wasn't living. Someone was authoring Celeste Marchand — keeping her alive on paper, moving her around the city, giving her thoughts, doubts, sightings. Building a record. And a record built after the fact meant someone expected the record to be read. Expected questions. Expected a detective, eventually, holding this diary under a lamp and concluding that Celeste Marchand had simply wandered off on her own accord, alive as late as March 4th, erratic, perhaps unwell, certainly not murdered in late February.

An alibi written in a dead woman's hand.

Noor sat back. The bulb hummed. Somewhere above her, the hotel went on with its evening — the elevator chimes, the muffled thunder of luggage carts, a banquet crowd laughing through three floors of marble. All that ordinary noise, and underneath it this: a forged life, page by page, being assembled in her own building.

She forced herself to be systematic. Errors. Forgers always left errors, because they forged what they thought mattered and missed what actually did.

March 4th again: *I saw him today.* Seen where? A person writing their own diary wouldn't need to say — but a forger leaving a trail would want locations. She flipped back. March 2nd: *the twelfth floor*. The twelfth floor had been closed since before Noor's time, sealed behind a service door that even the master keys didn't open. Why would a forger point there? Unless he wanted the diary to lead somewhere — or unless he was careless, letting his own preoccupations bleed into his victim's voice.

There were smaller errors, and these were worse. On February 21st, the diarist wrote: *The barman with the silver hair remembered my drink. Old Fashioned, rye.* Noor had worked the evening bars for two years before the front desk. The silver-haired barman was Tomas, and Tomas was famously, militantly anti-rye — he'd refused to stock it since a bad batch in 2019, served bourbon only, and said so to anyone who ordered otherwise. Celeste's actual folio showed charges from the bar: *Old Fashioned — bourbon.* The forger had invented a plausible detail instead of checking the true one. He hadn't been in the bar. He'd only imagined it.

And on February 24th: *Rain all day. Watched it from the window while the city drowned.* Noor pulled the weather log she kept out of pure habit — she noted weather daily because guests asked, because umbrellas moved, because patterns were patterns. February 24th had been cloudless, dry, one of those false-spring days that filled the Palm Court at lunch. The forger had guessed. Guessing was a confession.

Someone inside the hotel, or very close to it. Someone who knew Celeste's habits broadly but not precisely. Someone with access to the Palm Court on the night of March 3rd, and access to a diary that should have vanished with her.

Noor closed the diary and pressed her gloved palm flat against the cover, as if taking a pulse.

Here was the rule, the one she'd built her whole quiet career on: *notice, log, return.* Find the owner, restore the object, take the tip or the gratitude, and walk away clean. Six years without breaking it. The rule was what made her trustworthy; the rule was what made her safe.

She thought about Detective Okafor's face in the lobby that morning — tired, procedural, already half-convinced that Celeste Marchand was a woman who'd walked away from her own life, the kind of case that solved itself by going cold. He would get the diary eventually. Of course he would. But he would get it after Noor had read every page twice, cross-referenced every date against her logs, and understood the shape of the lie — because whoever wrote this book had made one fatal miscalculation. They had planted it in *her* lost-and-found. In her ledger. Under her initials.

They had made it hers.

Noor took off the gloves, folded them into her pocket, and wrapped the diary in a linen napkin from the restoration shelf. She did not sign it out of the ledger. Instead, she took out her pen and amended entry 4471 in her small, precise script: *Disposition pending verification.*

Off-book. Her first case that belonged to no one but her.

At the top of the basement stairs she paused, the diary against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and allowed herself one honest admission: she was not doing this for Celeste Marchand, whom she had met exactly once, in passing, near the elevators. She was doing it because someone had used her hotel as a stage and her ledger as a prop, and because a woman's voice was being written by a stranger's hand, and because underneath all the professional language she was already, helplessly, fascinated.

The elevator carried her up toward the light. Through the brass doors' seam she watched the floors tick past — lobby, two, three — and thought about the twelfth floor, sealed and dark, that a forged diary kept pointing toward like a finger.

Tomorrow she would find out who had keys to a floor that officially had none.

Tonight, she would read the diary again.
