## 4. What Marta Remembers

The trolley's wheel squeaked on the twelfth-floor corridor, the same note it had made for eleven years, and Marta Vukovic had learned to hear it as a kind of permission. *You may pass. You have always passed. No one stops the woman with the linens.*

She did not clean the twelfth floor. Nobody cleaned the twelfth floor. That was the point of it — a floor folded shut like a letter no one wanted to read, its guest rooms sealed behind fire doors painted the same cream as the walls, its service elevator keyed to three cards in the whole building. The Grand Meridian had nine hundred rooms and Marta could walk every one of them blindfolded, but the twelfth floor existed for her only as a corridor of doors she dusted on the outside and never opened.

Except one.

At the far end, past the ice machine that hadn't hummed since 2014, there was a service door — steel, unmarked, the kind guests never saw. Once a week, on Thursdays, Marta parked her trolley beside it, knelt down as if adjusting the wheel that always squeaked, and slid a canvas bag through the gap beneath the door. Rice. Tinned fish. Bread from the staff kitchen, the good rye, not the white they gave the night shift. Once, a bar of dark chocolate wrapped in a page torn from a crossword book.

Once a week, eleven years. She had never once seen a hand take it. She had only ever seen, the following Thursday, that the bag was gone.

*You are feeding a ghost,* her sister Ljuba would say, if Ljuba knew, which Ljuba did not. And Marta would answer — to Ljuba, to God, to the squeaking wheel — *I am feeding my conscience. It eats more than he does.*

* * *

His name had been Ilya Kessler, and in the spring of the last good year, he had been the sort of man the Grand Meridian made housekeepers feel useful to. Not rich — the rich were a weather system, you simply dressed for them — but precise. An accountant who stayed four nights a month, always room 1207, always the same: two pillows, no turndown, coffee at seven, and a habit of saying *thank you, Marta* in a voice that made the word sound like it had been baked fresh.

He worked for Voss Holdings. Everyone on the staff knew the name the way sailors know the sea — Edmund Voss had bought the hotel in the eighties, kept the penthouse like a crown kept in a case, and let his money move through the building so quietly that half the staff believed they worked for the Meridian itself. They did not. They worked for the twelfth floor, even when they didn't know it.

Ilya Kessler had been Edmund Voss's numbers man. And in October of that year, Marta had found him in the linen room on eleven — her linen room, her kingdom of pressed sheets and lavender sachets — sitting on a stack of bath towels with his glasses off and his face in his hands.

She should have left. Housekeepers learn early which sorrows are furniture and which are guests'. But he looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he said, "Marta. Do you ever count things?"

"Every day," she said. "Two hundred sheets, four hundred pillowcases."

"And do they ever come out wrong?"

"Sometimes." She kept folding. It was easier for people to talk to your hands than to your face; she'd known that since Sarajevo. "Then you find where the error is, and you fix it, or you write it down so someone else can't pretend it isn't there."

He laughed then — a strange, cracked laugh, like ice under weight. "That's what I've done," he said. "I've written it down."

She didn't ask what *it* was. She wishes, now, at seventy-one, with knees that complain and a conscience that complains louder — she wishes she had asked. Because three weeks later, Room 1207 burned.

* * *

They told the staff it was electrical. A fault in the wall behind the wardrobe, the fire marshal said, tragic, the guest had died of smoke inhalation before the flames reached him. There was a funeral notice in the paper, small, with a photograph of a younger Ilya, and Edmund Voss sent flowers — an enormous wreath, white lilies, with a card in gold ink: *A faithful colleague, deeply mourned.*

Marta had stood in the lobby looking at those lilies and felt something turn over in her chest, slow and cold, like a body rolling in water.

Because she had been on twelve that morning. Not inside — never inside — but delivering the special hangers Mr. Voss liked, the wide wooden ones, to the service landing. And she had seen Ilya Kessler come out of the penthouse door with his coat over his arm and his face gray as dishwater, and she had heard Edmund Voss behind him say, in the pleasant voice he used for donors and diplomats, "Paper burns, Ilya. People burn better."

She had thought, for years, that she had imagined it. That grief and steam and the smell of lilies had rearranged themselves into a sentence no man would say aloud. She had told herself this while the fire trucks came, while the smoke curled up the east shaft, while the hotel hummed around its wound like a hive around a crushed bee.

But then the twelfth floor closed. Not renovated — *closed*. Sealed doors, killed elevators, a line drawn through the floor plan in someone's fountain pen. Hotels don't seal floors over wiring faults. Hotels seal floors over graves.

And Marta, who counted everything, began counting the things that didn't add up. The ashes report she'd glimpsed on Salim's desk, listing accelerant findings that vanished from the file by Monday. The way Adrian Voss — young then, beautiful, cruel in the effortless way of men who have never been told no — appeared at the staff entrance the day after the fire, asking which housekeeper had access to twelve. The way Marta's own name had been in his mouth when another maid mentioned it later: *the Bosnian woman, she goes up there.*

She had gone home that night and taken the canvas bag from the pantry shelf, the one she used for the market, and filled it with rice and tinned fish and bread, and she had carried it up to the sealed service door at the end of the dead corridor, and she had knelt, and she had pushed it through the gap.

She had not let herself think about why. Thinking was a luxury for people whose hands didn't shake.

* * *

Now it is Thursday again, and the wheel squeaks, and Marta kneels.

Eleven years of Thursdays. She has grown old in the gap between the bag leaving her hands and the bag disappearing. She has buried a husband, married off a niece, survived a hip operation and two changes of general manager, and every Thursday she has fed whatever lives behind the steel door — a ghost, a fugitive, a guilt, a hope. She stopped trying to name it around year five. Naming things gives them edges, and edges can be taken from you.

Today the bag holds rice, sardines, rye bread, and — because the girl at the pharmacy owed her a favor — a small bottle of aspirin and a tube of toothpaste. She slides it through. The gap is narrower than it used to be; the building settles, the door swells. One day soon it will not fit at all, and then what? Then nothing. Then she will keep kneeling at a door that cannot be fed, the way you keep visiting a grave.

She is rising, hand on the trolley, when she hears it.

Not a sound from behind the door. She has never heard a sound from behind the door — that silence is part of the arrangement, the unspoken contract between them: *I feed, you stay dead.* This is a sound from the corridor. Footsteps. Soft-soled shoes, unhurried, coming up the dead corridor of a dead floor where no living person has business being.

Marta's heart does something old and wartime in her chest.

"Noor?" she says, before she can stop herself — because of course it's Noor Adeyemi, standing in the dimness by the shuttered ice machine with her concierge's calm and her eyes that read people like ledgers, holding something against her chest the way you hold a bird you're afraid will startle.

"The wheel squeaks," Noor says quietly. "It's been squeaking for eleven years, hasn't it, Marta. Every Thursday."

Marta looks at the canvas bag, already swallowed by the dark beneath the door. Too late to be anyone but what she is.

"I don't know what you mean," she says, in the voice she used at checkpoints, and hates herself for it, because Noor is not a checkpoint, Noor is the closest thing this hotel has to a daughter.

Noor steps closer. In the light from the stairwell door, Marta sees what she's holding: a diary, green leather, a ribbon marker. Celeste Marchand's diary, the missing woman's diary, the one from the photographs the detective showed everyone at briefing. And Noor opens it to a marked page and turns it toward her, and says, in a voice gentle as a sheet laid over a face:

"There's an entry here about a grocery bag. Rice, sardines, rye bread. Left at a sealed door on the twelfth floor." A pause. "Written in Celeste Marchand's handwriting, Marta. Dated last week."

The corridor is very cold. Somewhere below, the hotel breathes on, nine hundred rooms, none the wiser.

"That's impossible," Marta whispers. "Celeste Marchand disappeared a month ago."

"Yes," says Noor Adeyemi. "So either she wrote it before she went — which means she knew about you and your door. Or someone else wrote it after." Her eyes go to the steel door, then back, steady and terrible. "Either way, Marta — whoever wrote it knows exactly what you've been doing every Thursday for eleven years. And I think it's time you told me who's behind that door."

The wheel of the trolley gives one small squeak, like a thing asking permission.

Marta finds she has none left to give.
