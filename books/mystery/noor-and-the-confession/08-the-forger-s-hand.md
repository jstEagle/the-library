## 8. The Forger's Hand

The diary had been lying to Noor for three days before she understood it was also telling her the truth.

She sat in the back office after midnight, the book open under a desk lamp, surrounded by printouts of hotel logs she'd pulled before her access was quietly revoked. The entries after Celeste's disappearance were good. That was what had unsettled her from the start — too good, the kind of handwriting that had been studied rather than remembered. But forgery always failed at the edges, and Noor had spent eleven years reading guests the way accountants read ledgers: not for what they wrote, but for where their hands trembled.

Celeste Marchand wrote left-slanted, quick, impatient with her own loops. She abbreviated. She never wrote *hotel* when *the Meridian* would do — she'd told Noor so herself, laughing over coffee in the lobby, weeks before she vanished. And she dated everything twice, once in numerals and once spelled out, an old habit she'd called "insurance against my own memory."

The forged entries did none of this. They were careful, complete sentences. They said *hotel*. They used single dates.

And they made mistakes — but not the mistakes of a careless man. The mistakes of a deliberate one.

Noor spread six pages across the desk and read them as messages instead of lies.

*Entry one:* "I saw him on the twelfth floor today." Celeste had never been on the twelfth floor; the elevator didn't stop there. But the entry gave a date — March 9th — and the twelfth floor's service corridor had been unsealed for exactly forty minutes that afternoon, according to a maintenance log Adrian Voss himself had signed off. A window. An invitation to look.

*Entry two:* "He keeps the tapes near the water." Nonsense, unless you knew the old linen room on twelve shared a wall with the abandoned kitchen, whose pipes still ran. Ilya Kessler had been living beside running water for ten years. Someone who knew that had written the line — someone who wanted Noor to find him, or wanted whoever came looking to be seen finding him.

*Entry three,* dated four days ago: "I am tired of being sick. The penthouse is quiet. The doctors are kind. I will rest here until I am myself again."

Noor read it five times.

There was no record of Celeste checking out. There was no hospital admission under her name anywhere in the city — Noor had paid a favor at St. Vincent's to confirm it. But there *was* a catering order, two days old, for the Voss penthouse: one tray, soft foods, marked *guest recovering — do not disturb.* And there was a pharmacy delivery, signed by Adrian's housekeeper, for a sedative prescribed to *C. Marchand.*

A fictional breakdown, authored in the same hand as the fictional diary. Adrian wasn't hiding Celeste from the world. He was writing her out of it — one plausible entry at a time — so that when his father died and the auditors came, there would be no witness, no confession, no woman carrying either. Just a sad story about a guest who lost her mind and wandered off.

Noor closed the diary and pressed her palms flat against the pages, as if she could hold Celeste through the paper.

*The penthouse,* she thought. *Top floor. His father's floor.*

Then she got angry, because anger was easier than fear, and started pulling logs.

* * *

Adrian Voss moved through the Grand Meridian like a man who owned the air in it, which, in every way that mattered, he did. But ownership leaves fingerprints. Noor had logged them all.

His key card showed the twelfth-floor service door accessed seven times in the past month — always between two and four a.m., always alone. It showed the penthouse elevator used nightly. It showed, on the night Celeste disappeared, a gap of ninety minutes where his card went dark entirely — no doors, no elevators — which meant he'd used the freight stairs, the ones without readers, the ones only staff and ghosts knew about.

And it showed something else. Something that made Noor sit very still in the humming dark of the office.

On the night of March 9th — the night the forged diary pointed her toward the twelfth floor — Adrian's card had opened the service door at 2:14 a.m. and again at 3:51. Ninety-seven minutes watching a sealed corridor. Waiting to see who would come.

Nobody had come. Noor hadn't decoded the clue yet. But now she had, and if she went to twelve, she'd be walking into a trap already nine days old.

Unless the trap had been meant for someone else. Unless the forger wanted the diary found — wanted *her* to find it — because Adrian wasn't the only one staging this story.

Noor stared at the ceiling. *Someone is authoring Celeste.* She'd assumed it was Adrian. But Adrian's forgeries were designed to erase. These errors were designed to reveal. Two hands in one book. A forger within the forger.

Which raised a question she didn't want to ask: who else knew enough to write Celeste's life?

Her office door opened.

Salim stood in the doorway, coat still on, rain on his shoulders, holding two paper cups of the terrible lobby coffee he pretended not to drink. He looked older than he had eight days ago, when security had walked him out with a box of his things and a suspension letter citing "unauthorized disclosures."

"You changed the lock code," he said.

"You taught me to change the lock code."

"I taught you well." He set a cup on the desk beside her, an offering, and lowered himself into the chair across from hers like a man sitting down to a confession of his own. "You've been pulling my old logs. I still get copies. Old habit."

"Reading other people's mail is against hotel policy," Noor said.

"So is forging a dead woman's diary," Salim said quietly, "but here we are."

* * *

He told her in the flat voice of a man who had rehearsed this in the shower for a week and still hated every word.

Eight years ago, the Grand Meridian had nearly gone under. Bad loans, a fire on twelve that the insurance company had fought tooth and nail, a winter with the ballroom dark and half the staff laid off. Edmund Voss had bought the debt quietly, through three shell companies, and then — instead of gutting the hotel, as everyone expected — he'd kept it alive. Kept *them* alive. Marta's pension. Salim's job. The health plan that had paid for the surgery when Salim's wife's heart began to fail.

"There was one condition," Salim said. "Once a month, I send Mr. Voss a summary. Incidents. Complaints. Anything unusual. Anything... searching." His eyes went to the diary on the desk. "You think I didn't know what I was doing? You think I slept? Every report, I told myself: this keeps two hundred people employed. This keeps Marta off the street. One letter a month, and the family leaves us all alone."

"And when I started asking questions?"

"I stopped mentioning your name." He said it simply, and it landed harder than any apology could have. "Three reports ago, I started lying to a dying man to protect you. Then someone above me noticed the omissions. That's why I was suspended, Noor. Not for leaking. For *not* leaking. Adrian wanted me gone so he could put his own eyes behind that desk." He nodded at her chair. "You're sitting in the spot they built to watch you."

Noor's hands were cold around the cup. Eight years. Eight years of *good morning, Salim*, of lessons in the back office, of the man who'd taught her that a concierge's first loyalty was to the truth — and he'd been filing monthly reports to the family that had erased a man's life and hidden another inside her walls.

"You let me believe—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Marta cried in the linen closet, Salim. She's been feeding a ghost for ten years and crying about it, and you knew. You *knew* what happened on twelve."

"I knew a fire and a death certificate," Salim said. "I didn't know Ilya was alive until Tuesday, when your key card touched the service door and Adrian's phone lit up like a Christmas tree. And then I had a choice." He leaned forward. "Report it, and buy back my job with a dead man's location. Or come here, tonight, in the rain, and burn down the last thirty years of my career."

The coffee had gone cold. Noor set it down anyway, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't." He said it without flinching. "Trust isn't owed, it's earned, and I spent mine. So don't trust me. Verify me. Here." He slid a folded paper across the desk. "Adrian's schedule for the gala Saturday. His private elevator codes — he rotates them Thursdays, and tomorrow is Thursday, so use these fast. And the name of the doctor who signed Celeste's 'recovery' order. He falsified records for the Vosses once before, in '09. He'll fold the moment anyone says the word *malpractice*."

Noor didn't touch the paper. "This could be the trap. Walk me into the penthouse wearing your leash."

"It could be," Salim agreed. "That's why you check every number on that page yourself before you use it. That's why Marta watches the freight stairs and I stand in the lobby where the cameras can see me being nowhere near you." He met her eyes, and for the first time all night he looked less like her manager and more like the tired, stubborn man who'd hired a sharp-eyed nineteen-year-old and told her she could read people better than any detective. "I can't give you back the trust I broke. But I can spend what's left of my name buying you one clean shot at that penthouse. Take it or leave it."

Noor looked at the paper. At the diary. At the man who had been both her teacher and her jailer, sometimes in the same breath.

"The blackout gala," she said slowly. "Saturday. The whole grid goes down for the generator switch at eleven — I filed the work order myself in September."

Salim almost smiled. "I know. I taught you to file work orders."

"Eleven minutes of darkness," Noor said. "Every camera blind. Every lock on battery fallback, which means the penthouse elevator defaults to *open access* — Adrian's codes won't matter, but neither will his locks."

"And in those eleven minutes," Salim said, "a missing woman has to be found, a dying man's tape has to surface, and two hundred employees have to keep pretending they don't know anything at all."

Outside, the rain thickened against the windows, and somewhere far below, the city glittered on, unaware.

Noor picked up the paper at last and folded it into her pocket, next to the diary's final forged line, the one she'd read twenty times and only now truly heard:

*"I will rest here until I am myself again."*

Rest, written by a forger. Held, by a man who thought he was writing her ending.

"He's keeping her calm," Noor said. "Sedated. Compliant. Which means Saturday night, when the lights go out and the drugs wear thin, she'll be awake in the dark — and scared."

"Then you'd better reach her before Adrian does," Salim said. "Because I've read his calendar, Noor. Saturday morning, he's scheduled a visitor for the penthouse. A doctor. For a *final assessment*."

Noor felt the floor tilt beneath her, the way it does when a case stops being a puzzle and becomes a clock.

"A final assessment," she repeated. "Or a transfer. Once she's moved, we lose her."

"Eleven o'clock Saturday," Salim said. "Eleven minutes. After that—"

"After that," Noor said, standing, "we don't knock. We take the stairs."
