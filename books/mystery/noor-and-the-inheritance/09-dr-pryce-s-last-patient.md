## 9. Dr. Pryce's Last Patient

The rain had stopped by morning, but the town still dripped. Noor walked the length of Harbor Street with their collar up and the brass key's impression still ghosting against their palm, though the key itself now lived in a plastic sleeve in their coat's inner pocket, where it pressed against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

Gull Point Care Home sat at the end of a gravel lane, a low building the color of driftwood, its windows fogged at the corners with salt. Someone had planted mums along the walkway in September; they had gone brown and papery, holding their dead blooms like small fists. Noor paused at the door, breathing the smell of the place through the threshold—disinfectant cut with something sweeter, apples maybe, or the memory of apples—and felt the old reluctance settle over them. Homes for the elderly were archives of a kind. Every room a file that would close soon, every resident a witness whose testimony expired on no fixed schedule.

They had learned that lesson too late once already. Ravi Chandra had died in Fairview Correctional with his story still locked inside him.

Inside, a receptionist with reading glasses on a beaded chain looked up from a crossword. "Visiting hours don't start till—"

"I'm not here to visit a resident," Noor said. "I'm here about Dr. Alden Pryce. He was a patient here."

The crossword pencil stilled. "Dr. Pryce passed in April."

"So I've been told. I'd like to speak with whoever cared for him. His nurse, if possible."

The receptionist studied Noor the way people in Millbrook had begun studying them lately—with that flicker of recognition, the slight narrowing, *the one from the bookshop who asks questions*. "Marta Okafor?" she said finally, then caught herself. "No. Sorry. You're the other one. The writer." She set down her pencil. "Marta kept to herself mostly, toward the end there. I can see if she'll talk to you. She does puzzles in the sunroom most mornings."

*Marta.* Noor filed the name away and waited while the receptionist made an internal call, spoke low into the receiver, hung up. "Sunroom's through there," she said. "Left past the dining room. She knows you're coming."

* * *

Marta Ellery was a small woman folded into a wicker chair by a window that looked out on the brown mums and the gray sea beyond them. She was knitting something the color of oatmeal, needles clicking with metronomic patience, and she did not stop when Noor entered. She only glanced up, took their measure, and went back to her work.

"You're the bookseller," she said. "Noor. Like the light."

"That's right."

"People keep saying you've been asking about Dr. Pryce." The needles clicked. "I was his nurse for eleven years. Toward the end, just nights. He didn't sleep much. Men like him never do—they spend their whole lives outrunning something and then wonder why they can't lie still when it finally catches up." She looked out at the water. "Sit down, if you're going to sit. I don't like talking up at people."

Noor pulled a chair from beside a rubber tree that had seen better decades. Up close, Marta's hands were remarkable—steady, precise, the knuckles swollen but sure. Hands that had turned a thousand patients, checked a thousand pulses, and remembered everything.

"Tell me about his last months," Noor said.

"Which part? The part where he got sick, or the part where he got scared?"

Noor went very still. "He was scared?"

Marta set the knitting down at last, across her lap, both hands flat on it as if pinning it in place. "Dr. Pryce was not an easy man to know. Proud. Fussy about his tea, his newspapers, his schedule. But he wasn't a coward. I've sat with dying men my whole career, and I can tell you which ones are afraid of death and which ones are afraid of something else. He wasn't afraid of dying. His heart was bad; he knew the arithmetic better than any of us. What he was—" she searched for the word, found it, delivered it flatly, "—was *watchful*. Starting in the fall, he changed. Started asking me odd things. Whether visitors had signed in. Whether anyone had called for him. He had me check his window locks twice a night, and I did it because it cost me nothing and it bought him a little peace, or so I thought."

"When did it start exactly?"

"October. Last October." Her eyes came up and held Noor's, and there was something in them—not warning exactly, but a kind of weary recognition, one professional recognizing another person doing the same grim arithmetic. "Right around the time the cemetery paperwork started moving. The seawall project. He asked me to help him write a letter about it. To the county. Requesting the relocation review be expedited."

Noor's pulse ticked up a notch. "He requested it himself."

"He insisted on the wording. Made me read it back three times. He wanted the Halloway plot moved *first*, before any others, and he wanted it done quietly, without public notice." Marta's mouth thinned. "I thought it was sentiment. An old man wanting to tidy up an old grave before the sea took it. It wasn't until later I understood he wasn't tidying anything. He was checking."

"Checking whether the coffin was empty."

"I don't know what was in that coffin." But her hands had gone still on the knitting, and the silence around the words had a shape to it, the shape of a thing she suspected and refused to hold. "What I know is this: six days before he died, he had a visitor. And after that visitor left, Alden Pryce was a different man."

* * *

The sunroom clock ticked. Somewhere down the hall a television murmured its daytime consolations. Noor leaned forward.

"Tell me about the visitor."

"She came in the evening. Not visiting hours—she came anyway, and the girl at the desk let her through, because—" Marta's lip curled slightly, the first open expression of feeling she'd shown, "—because some people don't get asked for ID in this town. They get *ushered*. She was well dressed. Silver hair, done properly, not the home-perm kind. Tall for a woman her age. Carried herself like the whole building belonged to her family, which I suppose parts of it do." Marta's eyes narrowed at the middle distance, retrieving. "And she wore a pin on her lapel. Little gold thing. I noticed it because it caught the hallway light when she turned. A scale, I thought at first. Justice scales. But when I mentioned it later to Alden, he corrected me. Said it was a gavel. Judge's pin."

The word landed in the room and stayed there.

"A judge," Noor said carefully.

"A judge's *pin*. I'm telling you what I saw and what he said. Make of it what you do—I know you will, everyone says you make things of things." She resumed knitting, but the needles moved faster now, agitated, betraying her. "Alden wouldn't see her at first. Told the desk to say he was asleep. She waited anyway. Sat in that lobby for two hours like she had all the time in the world, reading a magazine she never turned the pages of. And when he finally came down—or she went up, I never learned which—they talked behind his closed door for forty minutes. I brought the tea tray, because that was my job, and I knocked, and the voice that said 'come in' wasn't his voice. It was hers. She answered for him. She poured the tea herself, thanked me by name—my name, which she had no business knowing—and asked me to leave the pot."

"What did you hear? Through the door."

"Nothing useful. That's what frightened me, afterward. Forty minutes and I couldn't tell you a single sentence. Just the rhythm of it. Him talking fast, high, the way men talk when they're explaining themselves. And her—" Marta shook her head slowly. "Her voice never rose once. Low and even, like a tide going out. He did all the drowning in that room, and she just... presided."

"And after?"

"After, he was finished. Not dead—his heart held on another six days—but finished. He gave me an envelope the next morning. Asked me to mail it if anything happened to him, and I mean *anything*, not just the expected. I mailed it the day after he passed, to the address on it. Then he stopped eating much. Stopped asking about his window locks. One night he said to me, clear as anything, 'Marta, some debts you pay your whole life, and the interest is worse than the principal.' I asked him what debt. He smiled at me—the saddest smile—and said, 'Ask the lady with the pin.'" The needles stopped. "Three nights later, his heart quit between two and four in the morning, quiet as a lamp going out. Natural causes, the doctor wrote. And maybe it was. His heart was genuinely bad. But I've watched enough hearts quit to know the difference between a body giving up and a man deciding to stop fighting, and Alden Pryce decided."

Noor sat with that. Outside, the tide was turning; they could feel it in the quality of the light on the water, the slow reversal of everything.

"The envelope," Noor said. "Where did you send it?"

Marta's needles resumed, slower now, deliberate. "That's the question you actually came to ask, isn't it." She looked at Noor for a long moment, and whatever she saw in their face seemed to decide something. "I sent it to the county records annex. Attention: sealed personal effects, estate of A. Pryce. There's a process for it—lawyers' letters, probate forms. Took me weeks of phone calls to figure out how to do it properly, because I promised him properly." She set the knitting aside entirely. "Whether it's still there, I couldn't say. That annex is a drafty old building full of paper nobody's read in thirty years. Paper burns easy."

The words dropped into the room like stones into still water.

"Burns easy," Noor repeated.

"Fires happen," Marta said mildly. "Especially around buildings nobody loves. Especially around buildings that have recently been asked inconvenient questions." She picked up her yarn again. "You should go. Visiting hour's nearly over, and I've said more than I meant to. But bookseller—one thing more, since you'll ask anyway if I don't give it to you."

Noor waited.

"Alden kept a photograph in his nightstand drawer. Old, creased in quarters. Four families standing on the steps of the founding hall, 1961 or so, everyone smiling like church. He'd look at it some nights when he couldn't sleep. And the last week, he took it out and turned it face-down. Every night. Face-down, like he couldn't bear to look at them, or couldn't bear for *them* to look at him." Marta's voice dropped. "There were five people in that photograph, you understand. Four couples. And one of the women in it was wearing the same little gold pin."

* * *

Noor walked back down the gravel lane with the sea wind pushing at their back, and the feeling that settled over them was not fear exactly, or not only fear. It was the specific vertigo of the hunter who looks over their shoulder and finds the tracks running both directions.

They had spent three weeks believing they were following a trail. Now the trail had a shape they recognized: it looped. It led back. Edmund Halloway requesting his own grave opened. Alden Pryce requesting the paperwork, then dying within six months, watched and hollowed out by a visitor with a judge's pin. A letter mailed into the dark. A fire waiting, perhaps, in a drafty building full of thirty years of paper.

Someone was walking the same path ahead of them, clearing it—or salting it.

At the top of the lane, Noor stopped and looked back at Gull Point, its windows catching the late light. Somewhere in that sunroom, Marta Ellery was knitting oatmeal-colored yarn into something that would probably never be finished, and Noor understood suddenly why she had talked: not for justice, not for curiosity, but because she had spent eleven years watching a man drown by inches and had been unable to throw him anything but tea.

*I mailed it the day after he passed.*

Sealed personal effects. Estate of A. Pryce. County records annex.

Noor turned toward town, already calculating—the annex closed at five, tomorrow was Saturday, Reyes could pull strings but might not, and every hour the paper sat in that dry old building was an hour it could become ash. They reached for their phone to call Miriam, to warn her, to ask her to help pull the probate index—

And stopped.

Because across the street, parked in the shade of the shuttered chandlery, sat a car Noor knew. Dark sedan, county plates, spotless despite the salt spray. And leaning against its fender, arms crossed, watching the care home with an expression Noor had last seen on the face of someone guarding a door, stood a tall woman with silver hair done properly.

She wore a small gold pin on her lapel.

She turned her head, found Noor looking at her, and smiled—a low, even, tidal smile—and raised one hand, not in greeting but in acknowledgment, the way one acknowledges an opponent who has finally arrived at the table.

Then she got in the car and drove away, unhurried, leaving Noor standing in the road with their heart hammering and one thought ringing clear as a struck bell:

*She knows exactly where I've been. Which means she knows exactly what I know. And if Pryce's letter is still in that annex—*

It was already Monday's problem, and Monday was six days after a man had started counting his window locks.

Noor ran for the bookshop.
