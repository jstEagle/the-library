## 1. The Diary That Kept Writing

The rain had been coming down in that particular Greywater way all morning — not falling so much as hanging, a grey suspension between sky and street that made the whole town feel like something submerged. Kestrel Ravensworth stood behind the counter of Ravensworth's Books and watched it through the window, a cloth in one hand and a cup of tea gone cold in the other, and thought about nothing at all, which was the closest he could get these days to thinking about nothing that mattered.

Eleven months since he'd handed in his warrant card. Fourteen months since the Okafor case had ended the way those cases always ended — with a conviction that felt like a bandage over a wound nobody wanted to look at, and with Kestrel standing in the car park afterward realizing that the thing he'd spent thirty years doing had hollowed him out and left a shape behind. The bookshop had been his wife's idea, back when there'd been a wife, and now it was his, and it was dying at a pace he found almost restful. He dusted the same shelves every day. He alphabetized sections no customer had browsed since the flood of '09. He let the bell above the door ring for people buying paperbacks with cracked spines, and he told himself this was peace.

It was a Tuesday in late October when Dev Anand came in without an umbrella, wet to the shoulders, carrying a padded envelope the way a man carries something he'd rather not have touched.

"You're not a client," Kestrel said. "Clients call first."

"I'm not a client." Dev set the envelope on the counter between a stack of unpriced Victorian sermons and the till that stuck. He was sixty now, silver at the temples, still wearing the barrister's pinstripes even though he'd left chambers for a solicitor's back office a decade ago. He looked at Kestrel the way he used to look at juries he was about to disappoint. "I need you to do nothing. That's the favor. I need you to do absolutely nothing with this."

"And you brought it here because I'm famous for doing nothing."

"I brought it here because you're the only man I know who's bored enough to break his word." Dev pulled his coat tighter, water ticking onto the floorboards. "It came through the office. Anonymous courier, no return address, addressed to me personally. Inside —" he tapped the envelope once, lightly, as if it might go off — "a diary. And a note in the same hand as the diary. It says, *You were on the record in 2014. You said the Adeyemi case was closed too fast. Finish it or pass it on.*"

Kestrel's hand had stopped moving on the cloth. He noticed this the way he noticed all the old reflexes now — late, and with a kind of embarrassment, like catching himself talking to someone who'd died. "Noor Adeyemi," he said. "The girl who ran away."

"The girl who ran away," Dev agreed. "Eleven years ago. Except the courier's note is dated three weeks ago, Kestrel, and it's written in the same hand as the last entries in her diary. Which is why I'm standing in your shop getting rained on instead of taking this to the police."

"Because the police closed it."

"Because the police closed it *well*. That's worse." Dev looked at the window, at the drowned street. "I'm too old and too careful. You were always too young and too careless. One of us should be neither." He pushed the envelope an inch across the counter. "Do nothing. I mean it. Or do what you're going to do, and don't tell me until it's over."

Then he was gone, the bell ringing behind him, and the rain closed over the street again, and Kestrel stood alone with the envelope as if it had been left there for him all along and Dev had merely been the delivery mechanism of a fate already in motion.

* * *

He didn't open it that night. He told himself it was prudence, but prudence had a particular texture — the flat, deliberate patience of a man who knows exactly what's in a room and is choosing the order of his entrances. This was something softer and more cowardly: he left the envelope in the back room, under a copy of *The Shipping Registries of the Severn Estuary, 1978–1992* that had been gathering dust since he'd bought the lot from an estate sale, and he went upstairs and slept badly and dreamed of water.

The bookshop had been a shipping office once, before it had been his wife's dream, before it had been his. The building remembered. On damp nights the walls gave off a smell of brine and old paper, and the floorboards settled with sounds like footsteps in another room. He'd lived above it for nine years and had never once, in all that time, felt haunted. Tonight he lay awake listening to the building breathe and thought: *eleven years.* Noor Adeyemi had been nineteen. He'd read the file the week she vanished, before retirement, before all of it — a missing persons report that came across his desk and went out of it again inside a day, because there was nothing there. A girl from the coastal estates, no car, no money, a note to her mother saying she was sorry, and a town full of people who shrugged. Runaways were the sea of policing; every force lost hundreds of them a year and grieved none of them individually. He'd signed the closing memo himself. He remembered the pen.

In the morning he made tea, opened the shop out of habit, and then, with the door still locked against customers who wouldn't come, he took the envelope out from under the shipping registries and opened it.

The diary was a small hardback, navy cloth gone soft at the corners, a cheap thing from a stationer's with a ribbon marker gone grey. The cover had been written on in white paint-pen in a looping, confident hand: *N.A. — PROPERTY OF N. ADEYEMI — IF FOUND, READ IT ALL.* He turned it over twice before opening it, the way he used to handle evidence, and then made himself a fresh cup of tea, sat down behind the counter where he could see the door, and began at the beginning.

The early entries were a nineteen-year-old's diary and no mistake — exams she was sitting, a friend called Priya who was "being weird," a mother she loved with a fierce, specific tenderness (*Mum made jollof even though it's just us, she does that when she's worried, I don't know what she's worried about, I know what she's worried about*). There was a job at a garden centre. There was a boy she didn't name, only *he's not right for me and I keep seeing him anyway, there's something in his eyes when he looks at the water.* Kestrel read with the part of his brain that had never retired, the part that catalogued without being asked: the boy, the water, the worry the mother couldn't hide. He was four pages in before he caught himself leaning forward, elbow on the counter, tea forgotten, and he sat back and laughed at himself, one dry note in the empty shop.

Then he reached the last week of entries, and stopped laughing.

The handwriting changed. Not gradually — that was the thing he kept returning to, turning the pages back and forth under the shop's grey light. It didn't drift or deteriorate. Mid-sentence, in the middle of an entry dated the 14th of March, eleven years ago — the day after Noor Adeyemi had, according to every record in the county, walked out of her mother's house and out of the world — the hand simply *stopped being hers.*

The sentence read: *I keep looking at the harbour and thinking about what I saw when I was nine, and I think someone is following me home from —*

And there the loop of the *g* in *following* became, without a break in the ink, without a lifted pen, a different letter entirely. A different architecture of the alphabet. The letters after that were smaller, more upright, pressed harder into the page, the writing of someone older, someone who had learned penmanship in an era of fountain pens and discipline. The same diary. The same ink, near enough. The same date.

Kestrel read the entry that followed three times.

*She is safe. She asked me to keep writing so that no one closes the file. If you are reading this, then she trusted someone, eventually, or I am dead, or both. The night of the 9th of March, thirty years before the night she disappeared, four men stood on the quay at Voss House and swore to the same story. Three of them are dead now, and the papers called it accident, accident, misadventure. She saw what bound them. She was a child on the water and she has been running from it ever since. The fourth man's name is in these pages. So is mine.*

Kestrel set the diary down very carefully on the counter, aligning its spine with the edge of the wood, because his hands had developed a fine tremor he did not want to see.

* * *

He spent the afternoon not reading it, which was its own kind of reading. He priced a carton of donated paperbacks. He served two customers — a woman buying a gift for a grandson who liked trains, and old Mr. Fenwick, who came in every Thursday to stand by the poetry and talk about his late wife, and who today, sensing something, cut his visit short for the first time in nine years. Kestrel locked up at four and climbed the stairs to the flat and took down the box he kept in the wardrobe, behind the winter coats, the box he'd sworn in front of his wife's memory never to open again: his own notebooks. Thirty years of them. The private record of a public career, the cases the files had flattened into paperwork and his hand had kept alive in pencil.

He found the Adeyemi memo from 2014, one page, his own signature at the bottom. *No evidence of foul play. Subject left voluntarily. Recommend no further action.* He read it and felt the specific shame of a man discovering he has been a piece of someone else's machinery without ever hearing the gears.

Then he went back down to the shop, past closing, with the lights off and the rain still hanging in the street outside, and he read the diary properly, cover to cover, twice, by the light over the till. The second hand wrote in it for eleven years. Short entries, months apart, then years apart, then — in the last three weeks — daily. The entries never said where Noor was. They never said who was writing. But they kept the ledger of everything else: the three deaths, dated and annotated (*the papers said he fell. He was afraid of the water. Everyone in this town knows he was afraid of the water*); the name *Voss* appearing and reappearing like a reef under a tide; and once, near the end, in the newest ink, freshest and blackest on the page, a single line that Kestrel read aloud to the empty shop just to hear how it sounded in a human voice:

*She wants to come home. She is waiting to be sure that coming home won't kill her.*

He sat back in the dark. The bell above the door stirred in a draught and didn't ring. Somewhere down the street a gull complained at the rain, and the harbour, three roads away, breathed its cold salt breath through the town, and Kestrel Ravensworth felt the thing he'd been mourning for fourteen months come back to life in his chest with a heartbeat like a fist against a door.

He knew what this was. He knew what it cost. The last three cases had taken his marriage, his reputation, and his faith in the uniform, in that order, and he had retired a man with nothing left to lose, which everyone had told him was freedom.

But this case had a living girl in it. A girl who wanted to come home and couldn't, because coming home might kill her — and because somewhere in the town where he sold secondhand books to old men, the people who'd bought eleven years of her silence were still, this very month, this very week, paying for it.

He turned to the last page. One line, dated three days ago, in the older hand, the hand that had kept writing after the girl stopped:

*If the file is open again, they will come for whoever opened it. I am sorry. I chose you because you signed the paper.*

Kestrel closed the diary, and in the dark of his dying bookshop, he began, quietly, to laugh — the laugh of a man who has just understood that the trap was built for him personally, and that he is going to walk into it anyway.
