## 4. Alibis Woven Thirty Years Ago

The first witness was already dead, which Kestrel took as a kind of answer.

Gerald Mowbray, sixty-one at the time of his passing, had spent thirty years as a harbormaster in Greywater Bay and eleven years as a name on a list Kestrel had pulled from the county archive at seven in the morning, before Marisol arrived to open the shop. The file on Mowbray's death was thin in the way files get thin when nobody wants to look too closely: cardiac event, found in his boat, alone, no witnesses. The same phrase kept recurring through the Voss-adjacent deaths like a watermark. *Alone. No witnesses.* As if the whole coast had agreed, once and for all, to stop seeing things.

Kestrel drove out to Greywater Bay with the diary locked in the boot of his car and a thermos of coffee going cold beside him. He told himself it was due diligence. He told himself he was only confirming that Mowbray was dead, that this thread ended here, that he could go home and shelve the diary behind the poetry section where no one would ever ask about it again.

He didn't believe himself. He hadn't believed himself since the night he'd first read Noor Adeyemi's handwriting change mid-sentence — the letters slurring from careful schoolgirl loops into something tighter, older, more afraid, without so much as a paragraph break to mark the transition. A hand taking over a life mid-word.

Mowbray's widow still lived in the low stone house above the harbor, its garden gone wild with salt grass. She was small and iron-haired and she looked at Kestrel's card for a long time before inviting him in, though not because she recognized him. Because, she said, she'd been waiting thirty years for someone to come.

"I knew you would," she said, pouring tea neither of them drank. "Eventually. Someone always comes asking about Gerry, but they always ask wrong. They ask what kind of man he was. You're going to ask me where he was."

Kestrel said nothing. He'd learned long ago that silence was a door people walked through on their own.

"The fourteenth of June," she said. "Thirty years ago. Everyone asks about the fourteenth of June eventually." Her hands were steady around her cup; only her voice trembled. "He came home at three in the morning smelling of brandy he doesn't drink and fear he does. He sat in that chair" — she pointed, and Kestrel saw the chair still angled toward the window, still facing the door, as if Gerald Mowbray had spent his last years watching for someone to come up the path — "and he told me we might have to move away. He wouldn't say why. He never said why. But I'll tell you what he did say, Detective, because I've kept it thirty years like a stone in my shoe."

She set down the cup. Her eyes were dry and terrible.

"He said: *We said what the man told us to say. All four of us, word for word.* And then he went to bed, and in the morning he was a harbormaster again, and he never spoke of it, and every year on the fourteenth of June he got drunk alone in his boat until the year the boat killed him."

*All four of us.* Four witnesses. One alibi. Word for word.

Kestrel asked her, gently, if her husband had ever mentioned the others by name. She shook her head. Then she paused, halfway to the kitchen, and turned back with an expression Kestrel had seen on witnesses a hundred times — the look of someone deciding whether to hand over the last thing they own.

"There was one name," she said. "He said it once, in his sleep, near the end. *Tulliver,* he said. *Tulliver knows it wasn't an accident even then.*"

The name landed in Kestrel's chest like a dropped anchor. Dennis Tulliver. Second on the list. And according to the oncology ward at St. Brendan's, dying of it.

* * *

St. Brendan's smelled of antiseptic and cut flowers doing their futile best. Tulliver was in a corner room with a view of the parking lot, propped upright against pillows that seemed to be holding him together more than any medicine was. He was seventy-four and looked a hundred, yellowed and shrunken, but his eyes — when they fixed on Kestrel — were still sharp enough to draw blood.

"You're the detective," he rasped, before Kestrel had finished introducing himself. "Ravensworth. The one who quit. My daughter reads your shop's newsletter, God help me. She mentioned you'd been asking after old names." He coughed, wet and long. "I told her to tell you to stay away. She's a good girl. She didn't pass it on, did she."

"She did not."

"Of course she didn't. Nobody passes on the warnings anymore. That's how we got into this." He gestured at the chair beside the bed with two fingers, as if lifting something heavy. "Sit. I haven't got the wind for a long conversation, so let's not pretend you came about the weather."

Kestrel sat. He laid the photocopies on the blanket — the alibi statements, all four, dated the fifteenth of June, thirty years ago. Tulliver looked at them the way a man looks at an X-ray of his own fracture.

"Mowbray's widow talked to you," he said. Not a question.

"She did."

"She always was the honest one. It's why Gerry drank." Tulliver closed his eyes. For a moment Kestrel thought he'd drifted off, but when they opened again they were wet. "You want to know about the night. Everyone wants the night. But the night isn't the story, Ravensworth. The story is the morning after, when a man in a grey suit came to each of our houses separately and told us what we'd seen."

"The fixer," Kestrel said. "Edmund Voss's solicitor."

"Halloran." The name came out of Tulliver like a splinter. "Aldous Halloran. Neatest man I ever met. Never raised his voice, never wrote anything down, never threatened anybody — that was the genius of him. He just explained. Very patiently. He explained to me that accidents happen near water, that men drink, that grief makes families say strange things, and that a man with a young family — I had three girls, Ravensworth, three girls under ten — ought to think carefully about which version of the evening he wanted his daughters to grow up inside." His mouth twisted. "He didn't threaten my girls. He just made sure I understood they existed. That they were *reachable*. That everything I loved was reachable, and that the difference between a tragedy and a catastrophe was whether I remembered the evening correctly."

"And you remembered it correctly."

"I remembered it exactly as instructed." Tulliver laughed, and the laugh cost him; he had to wait out a spasm before he could go on. "Word for word. We all did. Halloran gave us the same sentences — I know because years later, drunk, Mowbray recited his statement to me in a pub car park and it was mine, Ravensworth, *mine*, down to the bloody commas. Four men who'd been nowhere near each other that night, telling the same story like a choir. You don't do that by accident. You do that by rehearsal."

Kestrel leaned forward. "Rehearsed when? Where?"

"Voss House. Two nights before we signed. Halloran sat us in the long room with the bad portrait and walked us through it like a stage manager. *You left the party at eleven. You drove the coast road. You saw nothing unusual.* We said it back until it was smooth. Until it sounded like memory." Tulliver's gaze slid to the window, to the parking lot, to some middle distance thirty years deep. "And the worst of it, the part I've carried like a hot coal, is that it worked. It worked so well that *I* started believing it. Thirty years of telling a lie turns it into furniture. You stop noticing it's there. You build your whole house around it."

"What happened that night, Mr. Tulliver? What really happened?"

The old man was quiet for so long that a nurse came in, checked a bag, checked a monitor, and left without either of them acknowledging her.

"I don't know all of it," he said finally. "That's the truth, and it's also the coward's truth, because I could have known. I chose not to. There was a boy — a dockworker's son, sixteen, worked the late shift on the Voss pier. He saw something that night. Something that meant the fourteenth of June wasn't what the paper said. Halloran mentioned him only once, obliquely, the way he did everything — said the family had 'settled matters' with all the young people who'd been present." His jaw worked. "*Settled.* With a child."

"But the boy—"

"Was never found." Tulliver's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Not arrested, not paid off, not moved away. *Never found.* Halloran used those exact words, and I've thought about them for thirty years, and I want you to understand what they mean coming from Aldous Halloran. That man was precise. If he'd meant the boy was silenced, he'd have said silenced. If he'd meant hidden, he'd have said hidden. He said *never found* — and then he looked at me, just for a second, with something I'd never seen on that neat face before." Tulliver swallowed. "Regret. Or maybe warning. And he said: *Some things are safer unfound.*"

The room hummed with the sound of machines keeping a man alive against his body's argument. Kestrel felt the vertigo rising, the same vertigo from the ledger's pages — the sense of standing on a floor that someone else had built over a hole.

"Do you have a name?" he asked. "The boy. Do you have his name?"

"No. Only that he was a dockworker's son, and that the other men called him—" Tulliver stopped. Coughed. Started again, slower, each word costing him. "Mowbray called him *the little ledger-keeper*, because the boy was always writing things down. Times. Boat numbers. Who came and went. Sixteen years old and he kept records like a customs officer. If anyone alive knows what happened on the fourteenth of June, Ravensworth, it's whatever became of that notebook."

*A ledger.* The word rang through Kestrel like a struck bell. Payments, threats, a standing annual sum labeled N.A. — and somewhere beneath it all, a boy who wrote everything down, who saw everything, and who was never found.

Or never *allowed* to be found.

Tulliver's hand crept across the blanket and caught Kestrel's sleeve with surprising strength. Up close his skin was paper over bone.

"One more thing," he whispered. "The girl. The Adeyemi girl who vanished. Mowbray's wife says you've got her diary." His eyes bored into Kestrel's, wet and burning. "Then you've read the entries that aren't hers. So ask yourself the question I should have asked thirty years ago: who else keeps a record like that? Who else writes down times and boat numbers and who comes and goes?" His grip tightened. "The boy kept a ledger, Detective. And somebody's been paying N.A. every single year since. You find out what N stands for, and you'll find your missing girl sitting next to a ghost."

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old pewter, and the drive back along the coast road — the same road, Kestrel realized, the same road from the rehearsed alibi — was slow with fog rolling in off the water. By the time he reached the bookshop the lights were on and Marisol was in the doorway, arms crossed, wearing the expression of someone who had spent the day not asking questions and had run completely out of patience.

But it wasn't Marisol who made him stop on the pavement.

It was the envelope taped to the shop's front door. Heavy cream stock. No stamp, no postmark — delivered by hand. Inside, a single sheet of expensive paper, typed:

*Mr. Ravensworth — Some inquiries are best conducted with an awareness of their cost. Ask Dennis Tulliver what his curiosity bought his daughters. — A well-wisher.*

Kestrel stood in the cold reading it twice, three times, while behind him the fog thickened and the harbor bells began to ring, and somewhere out in all that grey, he understood, someone had just told him they were watching — and that they knew exactly how far he'd gotten.

Someone who signed their threats the way Aldous Halloran must have signed everything.

Neatly. Politely. And never, ever, in their own hand.
