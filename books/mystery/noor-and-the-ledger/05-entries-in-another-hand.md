## 5. Entries in Another Hand

The bookshop after closing had a different heartbeat. Marisol knew it the way she knew her own — the tick of the radiator, the settling groan of shelves that had stood since before either of them was born, the particular silence of a building full of paper waiting to be read. During the day the shop belonged to customers and to Kestrel's low voice answering questions about maritime history he'd learned solely to sell books about it. At night it belonged to her, and to him asleep in the back room, and to the diary.

She hadn't meant to take it out again. That was the lie she'd been telling herself for three days now, and she was getting tired of it. She took it out every night. She waited until the floorboards above stopped announcing Kestrel's restless circuits — up at eleven, pacing, down at midnight, the creak of the cot that was too short for him — and then she unlocked the glass case behind the register where he kept it, because Kestrel Ravensworth locked everything away from everyone including himself, and she carried it to the reading nook by the window with both hands, like something surgical.

*Noor Adeyemi's Diary*, said the label in Kestrel's careful block capitals. *EVIDENCE — DO NOT HANDLE.*

Marisol handled it.

She was seventeen and she had been covering his shifts for two weeks without being asked, which in her private accounting made her not a thief but a stakeholder. He pulled old case files out of a lockbox under the till and thought she didn't notice. He came back from visiting a dying man with his coat smelling of hospital antiseptic and sat staring at nothing for an hour, and thought she didn't notice that either. Marisol noticed everything. It was the one thing she'd ever been praised for, back when anyone bothered praising her, and she had decided — quietly, stubbornly, the way she decided most things — that noticing was going to be what she did with her life.

So she noticed this: the handwriting changed.

Not between entries. Not at some tidy page break where a person might put the pen down and pick it up years later. Mid-sentence. She had found it on her second night with the diary, on page 141, an entry dated the ninth of October, eleven years ago — three weeks *after* Noor Adeyemi had officially vanished off the face of the county. The entry began in a small, slanted hand, letters leaning like they were hurrying somewhere:

*I keep thinking about the gulls. How they sound like crying but they aren't. They're just hungry. I think that's most of what people mistake for grief—*

And there, in the middle of the word *grief*, the hand changed. The letters went upright. Wider. Slower, as if written by someone thinking hard about each shape before committing ink to it. The sentence finished itself in the new hand as if nothing had happened:

*—but that they are only hungry, and the sea gives them what it gives them, and they take it.*

Marisol had read that sentence maybe forty times. She could recite it. And every time, the same cold slid down the back of her neck, because there were only two explanations and she hated both of them. Either Noor Adeyemi's hand had changed mid-word — impossible, or nearly — or someone else had picked up the diary after Noor stopped writing, and had kept writing *as* her, and had done it so carefully that the seam only showed once, in a moment of carelessness or exhaustion or grief of their own.

Someone was keeping the file alive.

That was the phrase Kestrel used, muttering over the case files, not knowing she heard through the door he never quite closed. *They stitched the alibi together. They kept the file alive.* He meant the Voss family, the fixer, the machinery of money that turned witnesses into puppets and murders into accidents. But Marisol had started to suspect the phrase cut both ways. If the powerful could manufacture a record, then someone else could too. Someone had taken up a dead girl's diary and written in it for eleven years — dated the entries correctly, matched the voice, the references, the private jokes — all so that if anyone ever opened this book again, the story would still be moving forward. Still alive. Still waiting for a reader.

Her.

She was the reader. Eleven years of patient forgery, and the audience was a teenager in a cardigan sitting cross-legged under the window lamp at one in the morning, eating vending-machine crackers and taking notes in the margins of a school notebook.

* * *

The late entries were strange in other ways too. Whoever wrote them knew Noor intimately — knew that she called rain *the grey visitor*, knew that she counted stairs when she was nervous, knew that she'd once broken her wrist falling out of a rowan tree and still couldn't watch birds land on branches without flinching. But the writer got things subtly wrong, the way a portrait gets things wrong. Noor's early entries were messy, exuberant, full of crossed-out lines and doodles in the margins — little ships, mostly, and once a lighthouse with a beam drawn as furious yellow scribbles. The later entries were clean. Composed. Too composed, like a letter written knowing it would be read by police.

And they repeated themselves.

Not word for word. The writer was better than that. But certain images came back around like gulls over a harbour. The smell of diesel and salt. A road that ran along cliffs with no guardrail. And always, always, the light.

*The light comes around every twelve seconds. I've counted. Twelve seconds dark, then the sweep, white to gold across the water. You can set your heart by it.*

That was entry 158, dated four months after the disappearance.

*Twelve seconds. I timed it against my pulse tonight. My pulse is faster. That felt like winning.*

Entry 171.

*Some nights the fog eats the light whole and you have to trust it's still turning in there, faithful in the dark, keeping its stupid perfect time. I try to be like that. Faithful in the dark.*

Entry 190. Entry 203. Entry 217, the last one, dated eight days ago — eight days, while the diary sat in a lawyer's evidence box and Kestrel told Marisol the trail was thirty years cold.

Eight days ago, somebody had written in a dead girl's hand: *Faithful in the dark. Still counting. Still here.*

Marisol put the diary down on her knees and pressed her palms flat against her eyes until she saw constellations. Her heart was doing something complicated — part terror, part the electric joy she usually only got from finishing a book she loved, that feeling of a world opening under her feet. Because this wasn't a metaphor. She'd spent enough nights with Kestrel's case files spread out on the counter, pretending to shelve biography, to know that lighthouses were geography. There were only so many lights on this coast. Twelve seconds was a signature. Somewhere along those grey cliffs, a light was turning right now, every twelve seconds, faithful in the dark, and someone was standing near it writing in a stolen diary, keeping a promise or a vigil or a trap.

She got up and went to the travel section, because of course Kestrel had a whole shelf of coastal guides, the man had never met a body of water he didn't want to read about, and she pulled down the Admiralty list of lights with hands that weren't quite steady. There it was, halfway down the page, in dry official prose: *Cape Merrow Light — character: Fl W 12s.* Flashing white, twelve seconds. Forty miles north. The guidebook called it automated, decommissioned as a staffed station in 1987, the keeper's cottage sold into private hands.

Private hands. Somebody owned a lighthouse nobody visited, on a cliff nobody drove past, forty miles from a town that had agreed, very thoroughly, to forget Noor Adeyemi.

Marisol copied the details into her notebook, then looked at what she'd written and felt a cold, sobering drop of fear arrive at last, punctual as the light itself. This was real. This wasn't shelving books and covering shifts and feeling useful. This was the kind of knowledge that got the last person who held it ruled a runaway. She thought about the dying man Kestrel had gone to see, and how he'd come back quieter, older, carrying a name like a hot coal. She thought about Celia Voss, whose photograph she'd found tucked in one of the case files, beautiful and brittle as ice about to crack.

If the Voss family had spent thirty years and uncounted money burying one night, what would they spend on burying a girl who'd seen it? What would they do to a bookshop assistant who'd figured out where she was?

Marisol closed the notebook. Then, because she was who she was, she opened it again and underlined the lighthouse twice.

Above her, the floorboards creaked. Kestrel, turning over in a sleep that never seemed to hold him. She looked at the diary, at the last entry's final line — *Still here* — and understood suddenly, completely, why the writer had kept going all these years. Not to deceive. To signal. Every forged entry was a flare fired over eleven years of darkness, waiting for exactly one pair of eyes to look up.

The writer hadn't been hiding Noor.

The writer had been leaving a trail *to* her.

Marisol slid the diary back into its case, locked it, and stood in the dark shop with her heart hammering out a rhythm of its own — faster than twelve seconds, faster than it should be, terrified and exhilarated and absolutely certain of one thing: tomorrow, somehow, she was going to have to tell Kestrel Ravensworth that the case wasn't cold at all.

It was flashing white, every twelve seconds, forty miles north.

And she was already reaching for her coat.
