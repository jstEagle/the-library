## 6. The Coroner's Closet

The house told on itself the way a body does.

Elodie Fairweather had spent twenty years reading the small betrayals of rooms — the chair angled wrong, the dust disturbed in a clean line, the smell of another person's breath lingering where it shouldn't. She read her own house that way now, standing in her kitchen at seven in the morning with cold coffee going colder in her hand, and the house said: *someone has been here.*

Nothing was missing. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. The silver still sat in its drawer — she checked, though checking made her feel like a madwoman. Her mother's rings were in the dish by the sink. The cash in the coffee can above the fridge, forty dollars for emergencies, untouched.

Burglars took things. This person had taken nothing.

They'd only looked.

* * *

She should have called Dane Okafor immediately. She knew that the way she knew ligature patterns and lividity windows — automatically, professionally, without needing to think. Instead she stood in her kitchen for four full minutes doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish.

The victim at the mill had been dressed in her clothes. Not similar clothes. *Her* clothes — the charcoal sweater with the pulled thread at the left cuff, the dark trousers, the scarf with the E.F. monogram her sister had given her three Christmases ago. Elodie had inventoried her closet twice since the body came in, once at midnight and once at four a.m., both times telling herself she was being thorough and both times knowing she was being afraid.

The count was short. Sweater, trousers, scarf, one pair of boots, size eight, scuffed at the heel. Five items gone from a locked house in a town where people still didn't lock their doors because locking your doors was an accusation against your neighbors.

Five items. And now an empty house that felt *handled*.

She set down the coffee, went to the hall closet, and opened it the way she opened everything now — slowly, braced, cataloguing before she saw.

* * *

The closet was a museum of her own ordinariness. Winter coats in dry-cleaner plastic. A box of tax records she kept meaning to shred. The vacuum. On the top shelf, the garment bag that held the dress she wore to funerals and verdict anniversaries alike.

She took the bag down and unzipped it and understood, with a lurching, physical nausea, how long this had been happening.

Inside the bag, folded into the dress's tissue paper, was a slip of paper — a dry-cleaning ticket, dated six weeks ago. Six weeks. She remembered that day: she'd dropped off the funeral dress and two blouses at Halloway's Cleaners on a Tuesday, picked them up Thursday, hung them here, zipped the bag, and hadn't opened it since. Which meant the ticket had been placed after Thursday. After she'd put the house to bed for the night. After she'd slept ten feet away, behind a door she'd started locking only last year, out of a vague unease she'd never bothered to examine.

Someone had come into her home while she slept, opened this closet, tucked a receipt into her funeral dress like a flower pressed between pages, and left.

Not a burglar. A curator.

Elodie sat down on the hallway floor with the garment bag across her knees and let the shaking come, because there was no one to see it and because holding it in would cost more than releasing it. She shook for maybe ninety seconds. Then she wiped her face with the heel of her hand, stood up, and did what she did with bodies: she worked.

* * *

She went through the house room by room, coroner's method applied to her own life, and what she found was not ransacking but *reading*. In the bathroom, her prescription bottle had been turned label-out; she always left it label-in. On her nightstand, the novel she'd abandoned in March lay open to page 212 instead of the 87 where she'd quit it. Small adjustments. A hand moving through her days, straightening them, learning them, the way you'd learn a script before performing it.

The clothes. God, the clothes. They hadn't been grabbed in a panic. They'd been selected. The killer had stood in front of her open wardrobe — or she'd worn them and he'd taken them from the hamper, which was worse, which meant he'd touched the warm fabric against his face — and chosen an outfit for a dead man the way a dresser chooses for an actor. Dress him as her. Put her on the corpse. Make every investigator who knelt beside that body kneel beside *her*.

She thought of Dane's face when she'd identified the monogram. He'd tried to keep it professional and failed, and she'd loved him a little for failing, and hated herself for noticing at a crime scene.

Now she picked up the phone and called him.

"Dane. My house has been searched. Weeks ago, I think. Maybe longer."

Silence on the line, then the sound of a car door, an engine turning over. "Don't touch anything else. I'm ten minutes out."

"I've already touched everything," she said. "I live here. I'm sorry. I'll try not to be sorry next time."

"Next time," he repeated, flat. "There isn't going to be a next time, Elodie."

She looked down the hallway toward her bedroom, toward the bed where she'd slept through someone's careful hands. "You can't promise that," she said, "and we both know it," and hung up before either of them had to say anything truer.

* * *

Dane arrived with Deputy Ruiz and a camera kit and the particular controlled anger of a man who takes other people's violations personally. He walked the house without touching, jaw tight, and when he reached the bedroom he stopped in the doorway for a long moment.

"You slept here," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I sleep here most nights."

"How long do you think?"

Elodie pointed at the dry-cleaning ticket, bagged now. "At least six weeks. Probably longer. The book on my nightstand was moved — I stopped reading it in March. Someone turned pages." She heard how it sounded and pressed on anyway. "He wasn't stealing. He was studying me. Blocking his scene."

Ruiz, young and pale, asked the question Dane wouldn't. "Why you? Why not break into the sheriff's office, or—"

"Because I'm part of it," Elodie said. "Whatever it is. He dressed his victim in my clothes. He wants me inside the frame, not outside looking in." She thought of the sealed files, the fingerprints of a man dead twenty years, the pages rewritten so subtly you'd miss it unless you were looking for the seams. "And I think he believes I already am."

Dane crouched by the nightstand, examining the novel without touching it. "We can put a car on the street tonight."

"A car on the street tells him we know. Then he goes quiet, or he accelerates, and I don't know which is worse." She wrapped her arms around herself, then forced them down — she refused to stand in her own bedroom looking like a victim. "I want to know what else he left. People who stage scenes always leave something. It's the whole point of staging."

It was Ruiz who found it, an hour later, and it was the last place anyone would have looked, because it was the place Elodie herself would have looked last.

Her microscope case.

* * *

She kept the case in the spare room she used as a study, under the window, near the reference texts. It was old — Ruth Calloway had given it to her when Elodie finished her certification, a battered steel case with foam cutouts shaped to the instruments like a coffin fitted for very small bodies. Ruth had said something then, some joke about carrying your trade with you, and Elodie had carried it through two counties and fifteen years without ever thinking of it as anything but a tool.

She opened it now because it was hers, because it was the one object in the house no stranger could have any reason to touch, and because dread had taught her all morning that the untouchable places were exactly where he'd go.

The instruments were undisturbed. Forceps in their slot, scalpel handles aligned, the scale calibrated and capped. She was closing the lid when her thumb caught the edge of the bottom foam tray and lifted it slightly out of true.

Underneath, where the case's false bottom hid spare blades and old calibration weights, lay a photograph.

She didn't pick it up right away. She stood over it the way she'd stood over the drowned man at the mill, letting the details arrive one at a time, because the first second of seeing was the only honest second and after that you started managing yourself.

The photograph showed a woman asleep. Dark hair loose across a pillow. One arm flung above her head, the throat exposed, the mouth slightly open — the total defenselessness of genuine sleep, the kind you couldn't fake for a camera. The angle was high, from the doorway or from standing beside the bed. The lighting was the blue-gray of three in the morning.

The woman was her.

Elodie's hands were steady. She noted that with distant clinical interest, the way she'd note an absence of rigor mortis. Her hands were steady and her heart was slamming and somewhere far away Dane was saying her name, saying it twice, and she reached into the case and turned the photograph over.

On the back, in small, precise, unhurried handwriting — the handwriting of a man who wrote reports, who filed things, who believed in the record:

*you closed it too.*

* * *

"What does it mean?" Dane asked. "Closed what? You've never closed a case in your life. You're famous for it. Okafor's office has a running joke—"

"I know what they say about me." She was sitting at her kitchen table again, the photograph in a bag between them, and her voice came out level in a way that scared her more than shaking would have. "It's not about my cases, Dane. It's about *the* case. Twenty years ago. Harlan Voss."

His face did something complicated. "That file's sealed."

"I know. I've been reading it. His prints match our mill victim — a man who's been dead two decades. The file's been altered. Pages rewritten." She laid it out for him piece by piece, the vertigo of the archive, the sense of ground shifting, and watched him move from skepticism to something colder. "And now this. *You closed it too.* Written to me. About a case I was never involved in."

"Unless," Dane said slowly, "he thinks you inherited it. Ruth Calloway signed Voss's autopsy. Suicide. You trained under Ruth. You took over her office, her files, her—" He stopped.

"My whole practice," Elodie finished quietly. "My whole way of seeing a body and believing what it says. He's not accusing me of closing a file. He's accusing me of inheriting the lie. Of being the next pair of hands on the same machine." She looked at the photograph through the plastic, at her own sleeping face, and the violation curdled, finally, all the way through, into something with heat in it. "He watched me sleep, Dane. For months. He learned which side of the bed I favor. He knows I leave the bathroom light off. And he decided I was worth educating rather than killing, which means he needs something from me."

"Or he's saving you for last."

"Yes," she said. "That too."

Dane pushed back from the table and stood at her window looking out at the ordinary street, the ordinary morning, Mrs. Pell watering her hydrangeas two houses down, and Elodie watched him understand, in real time, that the town they'd both grown old in had a second skin and they'd just begun peeling it.

"We need Ruth," she said.

Dane turned. "Ruth Calloway? She's—"

"Dying. I know. I visited her in June and she held my hand and told me I had good hands and neither of us mentioned that she signed a suicide ruling on a man whose skull was fractured." She stood, took her keys, took her coat. "I didn't know about the fracture then. But I knew Ruth lied about something, that June. I've known it for two months and I told myself grief makes people strange." Her voice hardened. "I'm done telling myself things."

"Elodie." Dane caught her arm at the door, gentle, and for a moment neither of them performed their jobs. "Whoever this is, he wanted you to find that photo. He put it where only you would look. This is him talking to you."

"Then I'm going to answer," she said, "in person," and stepped out into the morning light that no longer felt like safety, driving toward the one witness who could tell her what she'd inherited — and what, twenty years ago, her mentor had buried.
