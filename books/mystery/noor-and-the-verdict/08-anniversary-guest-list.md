## 8. Anniversary Guest List

The invitations went out on a Tuesday, which Dane Okafor thought was fitting. Nothing about the Merrick Verdict Anniversary Dinner had ever happened on a day that meant anything. It floated through the calendar like a manor house adrift in a flood — untouchable, ornate, and older than everyone standing around it.

By Wednesday morning, his desk at the sheriff's office looked like a stationery store had exploded.

"Two hundred and forty confirmed," said Deputy Alvarez, dropping another stack of RSVP cards beside the coffee ring that had lived on his blotter since spring. "And the thing's not for three weeks."

"People plan for this the way other towns plan for Christmas," Dane said.

"Worse. My mother started hemming her dress in July." Alvarez paused at the door. "You want me to keep pulling the guest list cross-references?"

Dane didn't answer right away. He was looking at the card on top of the stack — heavy cream stock, embossed scales of justice in the corner, gold-leafed lettering that read *Mr. and Mrs. Aldous Merrick request the honor of your presence...* Except it wasn't signed by Aldous anymore. Hadn't been for years. The old judge was ninety-one now, mostly a name on a marquee, wheeled out each October like a parade float. The invitations came from Marcus.

*Mrs.* — that was the part that snagged him. The town still couldn't say *Merrick Verdict Anniversary Dinner* without a certain shine coming into their voices. Twenty years of toasts to the night Justice Aldous Merrick convicted Daniel Cole of murdering Harlan Voss, and nobody in this town had ever once asked aloud what Dane had asked himself a hundred times since Elodie pulled that drowned stranger out of the mill pond: *what if the verdict was the crime?*

"Yeah," he said finally. "Keep pulling."

Alvarez left. Dane spread the cards across his desk like a dealer laying out a losing hand.

He'd been mapping connections for six days now, ever since Elodie called him at two in the morning with the fingerprint match that had rearranged his understanding of the case: their mill pond victim wasn't just unidentified. He was *un-dead*. A man named Gregory Pell, declared dead twenty years ago this month, dead the same week as the verdict, dead on paper in a file someone had gone back and quietly rewritten.

Dead men don't drown twice. So somebody had spent two decades keeping Gregory Pell alive off the books, and then killed him wearing a coroner's clothes.

Dane drew a line on the butcher paper he'd taped to his office wall. In the center he wrote **PELL**. Around it, he wrote the names of everyone who'd known him, employed him, or claimed to have grieved him. Then, because the pattern wouldn't leave him alone, he started a second column: **FIRST DINNER GUESTS**.

That was the thing about this town. The first anniversary dinner — the one held a year after the verdict, when the wound was still fresh enough to taste — had been small. Intimate. Forty-one people in the Merrick ballroom, according to the society page clipping Ruth Calloway had kept in her files and Elodie had photographed before the old woman's memory dissolved entirely.

Forty-one names. And Dane was finding them everywhere.

Gregory Pell's landlady? At the first dinner. The foreman of the jury that convicted Daniel Cole? Guest of honor's table. The court clerk whose handwriting had been all over those altered archive pages — the clerk who died last spring with no investigation and less mourning? Not only at the first dinner. He'd *catered* it. Young man then, ambitious, taking notes.

Dane stepped back from the wall and looked at the shape of it.

Every person close to Gregory Pell — every single one he could document — had been in that room the night the town first toasted its own clean conscience. And now they were dying, or hiding, or receiving letters that quoted testimony no record contained.

His phone buzzed. Elodie.

"You've seen the invitations," she said. No hello. She never bothered.

"Whole town's seen them. They're practically streetlights."

"I got mine today." A pause that had weight in it. "Seat card says *Coroner*. Front table."

"Marcus Merrick wants you where the cameras can see you."

"Marcus Merrick wants me where *he* can see me," she corrected. "There's a difference, and I think you know it."

He did know it. That was the problem with working a case with Elodie Fairweather — she was always three deductions ahead of his comfort level. He turned back to the butcher paper, phone wedged against his shoulder.

"I've been mapping the guest lists," he said. "Both dinners. First one and this year's."

"And?"

"And the overlap is making my skin crawl. You remember who catered the original dinner?"

A beat. "The Vane family. Before they opened the shop on Delancey. Why?"

"The father took minutes. Voluntarily. Sat in the corner with a legal pad the whole night like he was deposing the canapés." Dane touched the name he'd written there — **E. VANE** — and felt the same cold slide in his stomach he'd felt when he realized the archive alterations matched a dead man's hand. "Elodie, everybody connected to Pell was in that ballroom twenty years ago. Everybody. His employer, his neighbors, the cop who 'found' his suicide note. It's not a coincidence. It's a *roster*."

Silence on the line. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when she was holding something very still so it wouldn't bite her.

"Iris Vane works at the print shop now," she said. "She printed this year's invitations."

Dane closed his eyes. Of course she did. Of course the daughter of the man who'd documented the first dinner had put her hands on the paper for the second one. The killer — if Iris was the killer, and the handwriting analysis pointed that way like a weathervane in a gale — hadn't just inherited her father's access. She'd inherited his *seat*. His view of the room.

"She's not attending," Dane said slowly, working it through. "I checked the RSVPs. No Vane, no plus-one. But she printed two hundred and forty invitations, and she knows every name on the list better than the family does."

"Then she doesn't need an invitation," Elodie said. "She needs a uniform. Catering staff don't get seat cards, Dane. They get access."

He stared at the butcher paper. Forty-one names from the first dinner, and threaded through them like wire through meat: Pell, the stenographer Marjorie Quist — already dead, dressed in Ruth Calloway's coat — the deputy who'd written the original report, the newspaper editor who'd buried the discrepancies, and at the center of it all, golden and untouchable, the Merrick name.

"It's symmetry," he said. "Whoever's doing this isn't killing randomly. They're recreating the room. Every victim is a correction to something that happened at that dinner or in that trial. We're not chasing a murderer, Elodie. We're chasing an editor."

"A very patient one."

"Twenty years patient." He pulled the marker cap off with his teeth and wrote at the bottom of the first-dinner column, circled twice: **TOMAS REYES**. "Bookbinder was there too. Youngest person in the room. Which means if the pattern holds—"

"—he's next," Elodie finished. "Or last. Depending on how you read it."

Dane felt the horror settle over him properly then, the way weather settles. Not the sharp shock of a body. The slow dread of a design. Someone had taken the worst night in this town's history and was rebuilding it piece by piece, correcting errors, silencing witnesses, restoring the record to some version only they could see. And the final version — the completed text — required a witness.

"Tomas won't talk," he said. "I've tried twice. He looks at me like I'm the ghost."

"He'll talk to me. Eventually." A sound on her end — papers, the creak of her chair. "Dane. There's something else. The seating chart for this year's dinner leaked to the *Gazette* yesterday. Front table, center. Next to the judge himself."

"Who?"

"Seat card says *Witness*."

The line went quiet between them, both of them arriving at the same place at the same time, the way they always did, the way that scared him more than any body ever had.

"They're going to do it at the dinner," Dane said. "In front of two hundred and forty people. They're going to finish it in the room where it started."

"Then we have three weeks," Elodie said. "And Dane — we're not going to be able to stop it by standing outside the doors."

He knew that too. He hung up and stood alone in his office, looking at forty-one names and two columns and one word circled in red at the bottom of the page, and understood with terrible clarity that the most dangerous room in the county was the one this town loved best.

Outside, the first of the season's leaves came down past his window, gold as gilt lettering, falling exactly on schedule.

* * *

He drove out to the Reyes place that afternoon, mostly because sitting still had become impossible.

The bookbinder's cottage sat at the end of a gravel lane, windows shuttered against a sun that was still warm. Dane knocked three times. From inside, a voice like dry paper said, "Go away, Sheriff. I'm binding."

"Binding what?"

A long pause. Then, grudgingly: "Hymnals. For St. Brigid's. Go away."

"Tomas. Two minutes."

The door opened on a chain. One eye, red-rimmed, appeared in the gap. Behind it, the workshop smelled of glue and leather and something else — fear, maybe, which Dane had learned has a smell, sour and close, like a room shut up too long with a body in it.

"You got your invitation," Dane said.

The eye flinched. Actually flinched, the way a man flinches from a struck match.

"I don't go to dinners."

"This year you do. Seat card says *Witness*." Dane held up the photograph he'd gotten from the Gazette leak — the seating chart, front table, center. "You understand what that means? Because I think you do. I think you've understood since the letters started."

The chain didn't come off. But the eye stayed.

"Twenty years ago," Dane said quietly, "you were in that ballroom. Youngest person there. And whatever you saw that night — whatever you were paid to forget — somebody remembers it better than you do. Somebody's been reading your silence like a book, Tomas. And they're almost finished with the chapter."

"I never saw anything." The voice cracked down the middle. "I told the police that. I told everyone that."

"You told everyone what you were paid to tell them. That's different." Dane leaned closer to the gap, close enough to see the tremor in the lid of that single eye. "Here's what I need you to hear. The woman doing this — and I believe it's a woman, and I believe you know exactly who — she doesn't want money from you. She doesn't want your confession. She wants your *ending*. Written in her hand, in her scene, at her dinner. You're not a guest at that table, Tomas. You're the closing sentence."

The eye closed. When it opened again, wet, the chain rattled — not coming off, but loosening, a half-inch of new light between door and frame.

"Why are you telling me this?" Tomas whispered.

"Because in three weeks, either you run, or you help us end it. Those are the only two pages left in your book." Dane straightened. "Think about which one you want written down."

He walked back down the gravel lane without looking behind him, but he heard it — faint, unmistakable — the sound of the chain sliding free, and a door held open just wide enough to let the evening in.

* * *

Back at the office, the light had gone long and orange. Alvarez had pinned the final guest list to the corkboard, two hundred and forty names in neat rows, and Dane stood before it with a cup of coffee gone cold in his hand, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all day.

Cross-referencing the current list against the first dinner's forty-one. Names that appeared on both. He'd expected a dozen, maybe fifteen — the judge's circle, the old guard, the ones who never missed a toast.

He found thirty-eight.

Thirty-eight of the forty-one original guests would be in that ballroom three weeks from Saturday. The other three were dead — Pell, Quist, and the deputy who'd written the original report, heart attack, four years back, unremarkable, uninvestigated.

Uninvestigated. The phrase was starting to feel like the town motto.

Dane set down the cup and picked up the marker. On the butcher paper, under **FIRST DINNER GUESTS**, he drew a box around the entire column. Then, beneath it, he wrote the number that had been forming in his chest all day like a bruise:

**38 OF 41 PRESENT. 3 CORRECTED.**

The ballroom wasn't a venue. It was a stage being reset. And somewhere in this town, someone was rehearsing.

His phone lit up on the desk. A message from Elodie, no greeting, just the way she worked:

*Ruth Calloway kept a second copy of everything. Found her hidden autopsy report. Blunt-force trauma. Voss didn't drown — he was beaten first. Meet me at the diner at eight. Bring the guest list.*

Dane read it twice. Then he looked at the butcher paper, at the thirty-eight names, at the word *corrected*, and felt the last comfortable assumption of the case fall away beneath him.

They weren't investigating a murder anymore.

They were investigating a *revision* — and the original draft had been written by the most beloved man in the county.

He grabbed his jacket and the rolled-up guest list, and went to meet her, driving too fast through streets already strung with bunting for a celebration that suddenly looked, to him, like a rehearsal for an execution.
