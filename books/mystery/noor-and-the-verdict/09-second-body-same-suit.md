## 9. Second Body, Same Suit

The call came at 6:14 in the morning, and I knew before Dane finished the first sentence.

Not because of what he said — he said almost nothing, just my name twice and an address — but because of the pause before it. People who bring you ordinary bad news start talking immediately. People who bring you the other kind wait, as if the words might dissolve if they hold them long enough.

"Elodie. We found another one."

"Who?"

Another pause. Longer this time. "Marguerite Boone."

I sat down on the edge of my bed without deciding to. Marguerite Boone. Retired court stenographer. Thirty-one years behind a steno machine, transcribing every word spoken in the Harlan County courthouse, including — I realized with a slow, cold turn of my stomach — every word spoken in the trial of the man who was convicted of killing Harlan Voss twenty years ago.

"Where?"

"The boathouse at Kettle Lake. Her granddaughter was supposed to pick her up for church."

I dressed without turning on more lights than I had to. There is a superstition in this work — mine, not anyone else's — that a body deserves to be met by someone who has already woken up fully, who has left the soft country of sleep behind and crossed over into the flat, clear land where you can look at anything. I made coffee anyway. My hands were steady. I made a point of noticing that they were steady, the way you'd check the locks on a door you already knew was locked.

The scarf was still missing from my closet. I had stopped telling myself it wasn't.

* * *

Kettle Lake in October is a gray coin set in a gray palm. The boathouse leaned out over the water on pilings gone soft with decades of damp, and the county sheriff's tape snapped and whispered in a wind that smelled of cold iron. Dane met me at the tape, and I saw the look on his face before he said anything else.

"It's bad," he said. "Not the body. The body's — she went in the water, same as the man at the mill. But the staging, Elodie. You need to see it before I tell you about it, because I don't think I can describe it right."

Inside, the boathouse smelled of motor oil and lake rot. Marguerite Boone lay on the planked floor with her arms arranged at her sides, palms down, in a posture so formal it looked rehearsed — which, I would understand within the hour, it was. She was dressed in a long wool coat, dark green, double-breasted, with brass buttons gone dull and one button missing at the third position.

I stopped walking.

I knew that coat. I had seen it a hundred times hanging on the hook behind Ruth Calloway's office door, back when Ruth was county coroner and I was her shadow, her apprentice, her almost-daughter. Ruth had worn it every winter for twenty years and then, when she retired, had given it to the church rummage sale because Ruth never kept anything she was finished with.

"Where did the coat come from?" I asked. My voice came out flat. Professional. I was proud of it and ashamed of it in the same breath.

"We don't know yet," Dane said. "But that's not the part. The part is the paper."

He nodded toward a small table against the far wall — a card table, the kind you'd set up for a church supper. On it lay a single typed page, and beside the page, a steno machine. Marguerite Boone's own machine, I'd learn later, taken from her house, its ribbon replaced, its keys dusted and polished like an instrument prepared for a recital.

I put on my gloves and picked up the page.

It was a transcript. Or the beginning of one. It was formatted exactly the way a court transcript is formatted — the case caption, the line for the presiding judge, the line for the court reporter. The date at the top was October 14th, twenty years ago. The body of the page was a fragment, a few lines of testimony, and I read them twice because the first time my eyes refused to make sense of them.

It wasn't testimony from the Voss trial. It was testimony from *this* case — from the inquest into the man at the mill, the man whose lungs had filled with mill water and whose collar had hidden a ligature bruise. An inquest that hadn't happened yet. A transcript of an examination I had not yet performed, in my own voice, describing my own findings, down to the millimeters.

"Elodie." Dane's voice, careful. "The timestamps. Look at the timestamps."

I looked. In the margin of the page, beside each line, someone had noted the time — not the time of the supposed inquest, but precise, granular timestamps of the kind that appear in an official court record only because the court reporter's machine logs them automatically. 10:42:07. 10:42:31. 10:43:15. The kind of detail Marguerite Boone would have generated without thinking, thirty-one years of seconds accumulating in her machine like silt.

And then I saw the last line, and the timestamp beside it, and I understood what Dane couldn't describe.

The final entry was dated tomorrow.

* * *

We worked the scene for four hours. I examined Marguerite Boone on the planked floor of the boathouse with the wind worrying the tape outside, and everything in me that was procedure held steady while everything underneath it screamed.

Cause of death, pending autopsy, appeared to be drowning. No ligature marks this time. No defensive wounds. Her lungs, when I opened her at the morgue that afternoon, were full of Kettle Lake water, and there was nothing in her blood but the mild sedative her doctor had prescribed for sleep — a therapeutic dose, nothing more. She might, by every measure I could find, have simply walked out onto the boathouse dock in the dark, in a dead woman's coat, and stepped into the water.

Except for the coat. Except for the machine. Except for the page.

I sat in my office with the transcript photocopy spread out beside the original case file I'd pulled from the archive — the Voss file, the sealed one, the one that was breathing, the one whose pages had been rewritten in ways too subtle to catch unless you knew what the truth had been. And I did what I do, which is line things up and look at them until they confess.

Here is what I found.

The staging of Marguerite Boone's death was not a copy of the Voss case. It was a *correction* of it.

In the original file, the discovery of Harlan Voss's body had been logged at 7:03 a.m. — a time that never made sense, because the fisherman who reported it had testified he found the body at first light, which in mid-October comes closer to 6:40. Twenty minutes, unexplained, unexamined. Twenty minutes in which, the file implied, someone had been alone with the body.

Marguerite Boone's body had been found at 6:14 a.m. — the exact time, I checked it three times, that the corrected timeline would require if the fisherman's testimony were taken at his word and the log entry treated as the error it probably was.

In the original file, the clothing Voss was found in had been described with a single missing detail — the file noted a wool coat but omitted its color, an omission so small that no one had caught it in twenty years, and I only caught it because Ruth had once told me, in one of her late-career rambles, that the coat had been green, and that she'd had to argue to get even that much into her report before it was cut.

Marguerite Boone had been dressed in a green coat. The third button missing — and in Ruth's suppressed report, the one she'd hidden away and that now lived in a firebox in my closet, there was a line about a missing button on the original garment, a detail that never made it into the official file because it didn't fit the suicide narrative.

The killer wasn't reading the file.

The killer was *performing* it — and where the file was wrong, the killer was making the scene right. Correcting the record with bodies. Restoring the details that had been cut, supplying the timestamps that had been fudged, dressing the dead in the true version of events that the official version had buried. Every element of the staging matched not the file as it existed but the file as it *should have been* — as it would have been if the original investigation had been honest.

And that meant one thing, and I sat with it in my dark office while the coffee went cold beside me, and I let it mean it.

The killer had access to the truth. Not the file — the file was a forgery in progress, a document someone was actively editing. The killer had access to what the file had been *before* the edits, or to the people who remembered what it had been. Ruth. Marguerite Boone. The court clerk who'd died last spring. The small circle of people who had touched the original record and could have remembered what it said.

Marguerite Boone had transcribed the original trial. Every word. She would have known, better than anyone alive, exactly what the record said and exactly where it lied. A stenographer's memory is a machine memory. Thirty-one years of seconds.

She hadn't been killed because she knew too much.

She'd been killed because she *was* the record — the living, breathing, accurate version of it — and the killer was replacing the living record with a staged one, correcting history by eliminating everyone who could testify to the errors, and leaving behind, at each scene, a beautiful, typed, timestamped substitute.

A transcript that couldn't recant. A witness made of paper.

I looked at the final line of the page again. The entry dated tomorrow. My own voice, describing my own findings at an inquest that had not yet occurred, timestamped to the second.

The killer was writing me into the record. Not as an investigator. As a *character* — a role in a restoration, a part in a performance that had been running for twenty years and was approaching its final act. And the final act, whatever it was, was scheduled for a date I already knew by heart.

October 14th. The anniversary. The night of Judge Aldous Merrick's famous verdict, and the dinner the town threw every year to celebrate it.

This year's dinner was in five days.

I picked up the phone to call Dane, and then I set it down, because there was one more thing I needed to do first, and it needed to be done alone.

I drove to the church on Hill Street, to the rummage room in the basement where the donated clothes hung in rows by size, and I asked a nervous volunteer in a cardigan if she remembered a green wool coat, double-breasted, brass buttons, sold sometime in the last few months.

She did. She remembered it because of who bought it.

"Sweetest young woman," she said. "Quiet. Wore gloves the whole time, even inside. Paid cash." She smiled at the memory of good manners. "She said it was for her mother. Said her mother had one just like it, years ago, and lost it, and had never gotten over it."

"When was this?"

"Beginning of September, I want to say. Maybe late August." She tilted her head. "Why, honey? Was it stolen?"

"No," I said. "It was returned."

I walked back out into the October light and stood on the church steps with my hands in my pockets, and I let myself think the thought I'd been circling for two days, the thought shaped like a woman wearing gloves.

Whoever bought that coat had been planning this since before the man in my clothes went into the mill pond. Months. Patient, archival, exact. Someone who shopped for a dead woman's coat the way a director shops for a costume, and who had stood in a church basement being *sweet* while she did it.

Someone I might already have met. Someone who might have smiled at me.

Five days to the dinner. One transcript line already written in my voice, dated tomorrow.

I got in my car, and I did not drive home. I drove to the county archive, because if the killer was performing the file, then the file was the script — and I wanted to know, page by page, timestamp by timestamp, exactly how much of it had already been rewritten, and what scene was still missing from the record.

The answer, when I found it, was waiting for me in the margin of the final page, in handwriting I recognized from twenty years of reading Ruth's reports.

A single word, underlined twice.

*Witness.*
