## 13. Bait on the Hook

I didn't sleep. I sat at my kitchen table with the photograph face-down under a coffee mug, as if covering it could undo what it showed — me, asleep in my own bed, my mouth slack, my hair across the pillow like something spilled. *You closed it too.* The handwriting was small and neat and patient. The handwriting of someone who had all the time in the world.

By four a.m. I had stopped being afraid and started being angry, and by five I had stopped being angry and started thinking, which was the most dangerous stage of all, because that's when the shape of it came to me.

The killer didn't want to hide from me. That was the thing I'd been missing since the mill, since the scarf, since the place setting for two laid out on my table with the napkin folded the way I fold mine. Every scene had been addressed to me. Not to Dane, not to the town, not to the ghost of Harlan Voss. To me. The clothes from my closet. The photograph. The annotation — *you closed it too* — which wasn't an accusation so much as an invitation. A casting call.

He wanted me in the story. And if you want someone in your story, you leave them a part to play.

So I would play it.

* * *

Dane arrived at my house at seven, before his shift, carrying two coffees and the particular exhaustion of a man who has spent the night reading twenty-year-old court transcripts. He'd been doing it every night since the stenographer's body surfaced. He looked like he'd aged into the job he never wanted.

"You look terrible," he said, handing me a cup.

"You should see the other guy."

"I did. He's dead, dressed in your clothes, and floating in a mill pond." He lowered himself into the chair across from me, and his eyes went to the mug covering the photograph, and stayed there. "You kept it out."

"I needed to look at it."

"Elodie."

"Dane." I set my coffee down. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you start arguing, because you're going to argue, and I only have enough patience for one argument this morning."

His jaw tightened. "That's not promising."

"The killer wants me implicated. That's the design. Think about it — the clothes, the photograph, the note. If this ever goes to trial, what does the prosecution see? A coroner whose wardrobe shows up on two corpses. A coroner who received personal communications from the killer and didn't report the photograph for three days. A coroner whose mentor falsified the original autopsy. Every thread runs through me. He's not taunting me. He's building a case against me."

"We won't let it get that far."

"You can't control a trial. You can't control a grand jury. You can't even control the press conference you're going to have to hold when the third body turns up, because there will be a third body, Dane, unless we change the script." I leaned forward. "And that's what I want to talk about. Changing the script."

He knew. I watched him know. His face went carefully blank, the way it did in interviews with suspects, and I understood that whatever I said next, he was already preparing his refusal.

"He's finishing the file," I said. "Every murder corrects something in the original record. The stenographer's death matched the transcript down to timestamps nobody outside the courthouse could know. He's restoring the case the way he thinks it should have gone. So we give him a page to write."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. I can see it in your face, Elodie, and I'm telling you no." He stood, paced to the window, stood there with his back to me. Through the glass, morning was coming up gray over the rooftops of a town that still believed its judge was a saint. "You're going to say we announce the case is closed. Accidental drownings. Misadventure. Let the town relax, let him think he's won, let him come to you to finish the story — because the last correction, the final entry in his little restoration project, is you."

"It's the only way he surfaces," I said. "He's fluent in us, Dane. He knows our procedures, our timelines, our filing systems. He's been inside the archive; he may still be inside the archive. Any trap we build out of official channels, he'll smell coming. But this — this isn't procedure. This is theater. And he respects theater. It's the only language he speaks."

"And the price of the ticket is you standing alone in a dark house waiting for him."

"Yes."

He turned around. For a moment neither of us said anything, and the silence between us had a different weight than it used to — heavier, warmer, more frightening than anything the killer had left on my table. We had been circling something for months. Two people who ate lunch together too often, who called each other first, who had learned each other's silences the way you learn a language by living in it. Neither of us had named it. Naming it felt like it would cost something we couldn't afford while a killer was rewriting our town.

"If he comes," Dane said quietly, "he comes to kill you. You understand that. The witness dies at the dinner. The coroner who closed the case — you're the loose end now. You're the one entry he hasn't corrected yet."

"I've thought about that."

"Have you? Because from where I'm standing it looks like you've thought about everything except what it would do to—" He stopped himself. Pressed his knuckles against the windowsill. "To the people who'd find you."

The room got very quiet.

"That's not fair," I said.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. A man drowned wearing your scarf, Elodie. A woman died in Ruth Calloway's coat. Fair left the county weeks ago." He came back to the table but didn't sit. He loomed, and Dane Okafor never loomed; he was a big man who made himself smaller on principle, and watching him fail at it told me exactly how scared he was. "Give me another option. Give me surveillance on your house, a wire, a tactical team in your guest room. Something. Anything but you as the hook."

"A team means radio chatter, parked cars, boots on gravel. He hears a car door and we lose him forever, and then he goes to ground until after the dinner, and Tomas Reyes dies in front of two hundred people while we're chasing shadows." I held his eyes. "You know I'm right. You hate that I'm right, but you know."

"I know you're tired," he said. "I know you haven't slept, and I know grief makes people reckless, and I know that photograph is sitting under that mug like a wound you keep picking at. This isn't strategy. This is you trying to bleed on his terms instead of yours."

"Maybe both things are true." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Maybe I am reckless. Maybe I want him to come. But wanting doesn't make the plan wrong. Dane — he's already chosen me. The only question is whether I meet him on my feet or find out what he wants by waking up to a third body wearing something else of mine."

He sat down finally, slowly, like a man lowering himself into cold water. He wrapped both hands around his coffee cup and stared into it.

"Say the plan," he said. "All of it. No gaps."

* * *

I laid it out piece by piece, the way I'd lay out a body on the table — everything visible, nothing hidden, because the fastest way to lose Dane was to let him find a detail I'd withheld.

Tomorrow, at ten a.m., I would stand in front of the county courthouse and give a statement. I would announce that after exhaustive review, both deaths were consistent with accidental drowning — misadventure, the reports would say, a tragic coincidence of water and circumstance. I would express condolences to the families. I would thank the sheriff's department. I would close the folder on camera, visibly, deliberately, and walk away.

The announcement would be real enough to survive scrutiny for seventy-two hours. Dane would file genuine closure memos — thin ones, defensible ones, the kind a tired department produces when it wants to believe a bad thing is over. The town would exhale. The anniversary dinner would proceed without a cloud over it. And somewhere in the dark, a man who had spent months building toward a final correction would read the news and feel the record snap shut with me holding the stapler.

Which meant he had to come to me. Not to finish the file — to finish *me*. The coroner who closed it. The last uncorrected error.

For seventy-two hours after the announcement, I would live exactly as I always lived. Same lights, same routines, same creaking stair. No cameras, no wires, no backup within earshot — because the moment he sensed an audience, he'd vanish, and we'd never get him again. Dane would run the response: a perimeter held far enough back to be invisible, a signal protocol, a hard line he'd insist on and I'd pretend to accept.

"And if he doesn't come?" Dane asked.

"Then we've lost nothing but our credibility, and honestly, Dane, how much of that do we have left?"

"And if he comes with a partner?"

"Then we learn something new."

"And if he comes and you're wrong about the script — if he doesn't want to confront you, if he just wants you dead, quietly, from behind, before you ever see his face?" His voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a hairline fracture in a wall built to hold. "Then this whole elegant theory dies with you in your kitchen and I spend the rest of my life knowing I stood in this room and let you talk me into it."

I reached across the table and put my hand over his. His hands were cold. Mine weren't much better.

"You won't let me," I said. "That's the safety mechanism. Not the perimeter, not the protocol. You. You'll break the plan the second it starts going wrong, and we both know it, and I'm counting on it. I'm giving you veto power disguised as a contingency. That's the deal, Dane. That's the only version of this I'll agree to."

He looked at my hand on his for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough.

"You know what the worst part is?"

"Tell me."

"The worst part is that it's good. It's the best idea anyone's had since the mill, and I hate it, and I'd follow it off a cliff, because it's yours." He turned his hand over beneath mine, so we were palm to palm, and neither of us moved, and the morning light crept across the table between the coffee cups like something alive. "Twenty years ago, people in this town saw what they wanted to see and called it justice. I keep thinking — if I let you do this, and it goes wrong, I'm just another person who watched and said nothing."

"You're not watching. You're standing next to me."

"Next to you." He exhaled, almost a laugh, entirely without humor. "Elodie Fairweather, asking me to stand next to her while she hangs herself out as bait. There are easier ways to fall in love with somebody."

The words landed in the quiet room and neither of us took them back. That was the thing — neither of us took them back. He didn't soften it into a joke, and I didn't pretend I hadn't heard. We just sat there with it, palm to palm, two people who had run out of reasons to be careful with each other and hadn't yet found the courage to stop.

"Ask me again tomorrow," I said finally, gently. "If we're both still here, ask me again."

"I'll ask you tonight," he said. "Before you do this. I want it on the record — not the county's record. Ours."

* * *

He left at eight to draft the closure memos, and I stood in my kitchen and looked at the photograph under the mug, and then I lifted the mug and looked at it properly, one last time, the way you look at a face before a long journey.

*You closed it too.*

"Not yet," I told the empty room. "But ask me nicely tomorrow, and I'll close anything you want."

I took down the calendar on my wall — the old paper kind, the kind Ruth Calloway used to swear by — and drew a single line through tomorrow's date. Then, in the square marked for the day after, I wrote one word in pencil, light enough to erase:

*Bait.*

Somewhere across town, in a house I couldn't picture, the killer was already rehearsing his next scene. By tomorrow night, he'd believe the curtain had come down.

I intended to be waiting in the wings.
