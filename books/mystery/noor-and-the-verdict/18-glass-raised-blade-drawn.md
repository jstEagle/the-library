## 18. Glass Raised, Blade Drawn

### Elodie

The Merrick estate had never looked so much like a stage set.

Elodie stood at the edge of the great lawn as the light went amber over the hills, watching two hundred guests in their finest arrive along the gravel drive — councilmen and schoolteachers, the bank manager and his wife, half the jury pool from twenty years ago grown gray and prosperous. Strings played from a platform near the rose garden. Waiters moved through the crowd with trays of champagne, white-jacketed, anonymous, faces half-hidden under their visors.

That was the thing she kept coming back to. The faces.

She'd spent three days memorizing Iris Vane's photograph until she could draw it from memory — the narrow jaw, the mole below the left ear, the way one eyebrow sat higher than the other, giving her a permanent expression of polite skepticism. Dane had pulled her DMV records, her employment history, the single mugshot from a disorderly conduct charge at nineteen. Elodie had studied that face the way she studied wounds: looking for what it would look like altered.

Now she watched the catering staff and waited for the wound to show itself.

"You're hovering," Dane said at her shoulder. He wore his dress uniform, which he hated, and it made him stand like a man wearing someone else's skin. "You're supposed to be a guest. Guests don't count silverware."

"I'm not counting silverware."

"You're counting something."

"Staffing ratios." She lifted her glass without drinking. "The Vane family employed four caterers for events this size. The company Iris's father used — Halloran's — is running tonight. They listed eleven staff on the contract. I've counted fourteen."

Dane was quiet a moment. "Three extra."

"Or two extra and I miscounted once." She turned her head slightly, smiling for anyone watching, speaking through the smile. "Tomas is seated at table six, aisle side, like we planned. Two exits he can reach in under ten seconds. You have men at both."

"And if she doesn't go for him at dinner?"

"Then she goes for him after, when he's alone, and we lose our only chance to take her in front of witnesses." Elodie watched a waiter cross the lawn with a tray held perfectly level. "She didn't spend twenty years learning this town's rhythms to kill a man quietly in a parking lot. She wants the record corrected publicly. The toast is the ceremony. Tomas is the correction."

The toast. Seven o'clock, Marcus Merrick would rise at the head table and raise a glass to his father — the judge who had given Harlan County its proudest verdict, who had convicted the drifter everyone knew was guilty, whose name was carved into the courthouse lintel. Two hundred glasses would rise with his. And somewhere in that choreography, Iris Vane intended to write her final line.

Unless Elodie wrote it first.

"Drink your champagne," Dane said quietly. "You look like a coroner."

"I am a coroner."

"Not tonight. Tonight you're a woman at a party who has nothing on her mind but the shrimp." He touched her elbow, brief, warm, gone. "Breathe, Elodie. We only get one shot at this."

She drank. The champagne tasted like nothing at all.

* * *

### Tomas

Table six was too far from every exit.

Tomas Reyes had walked the distances himself, twice, yesterday afternoon while the tent crew strung lights, and both times the arithmetic came out wrong. Aisle side, Elodie had said. Ten seconds to the garden door. But ten seconds was a lifetime if someone was walking toward you with purpose, and all evening he'd been counting purposes — waiters drifting past, guests rising to greet friends, the slow tidal movement of two hundred people who all seemed, to his fraying nerves, to be converging on him.

He'd worn his good suit. It felt like a costume for his own funeral, which, he understood now, was exactly what it was meant to be. The seat card at his place read *Mr. T. Reyes* in beautiful copperplate, and beneath it, small enough that only he would notice, someone had added a second word in the same hand:

*Witness.*

He hadn't told Elodie about the card. He should have. His hands wouldn't stop shaking long enough to hold a phone steady, and some cowardly part of him had whispered that if he said it aloud it became real, and if it stayed unspoken maybe the evening would pass like all the other evenings of his careful, purchased life, and tomorrow he could go back to gluing spines and telling himself the story he'd told himself for twenty years: *I saw nothing. I was paid to see nothing. Nothing is what happened.*

The soup arrived. He couldn't tell you what kind.

At the head table, old Judge Merrick sat propped in his chair like a monument to himself, ninety-one years old and still sharp-eyed, still handsome in the way marble is handsome. Beside him, Marcus — silver-templed now, beloved, the man who'd built the children's wing at the hospital and coached Little League and shaken every hand in the county. Marcus caught Tomas looking and smiled at him. A warm smile. A host's smile.

Tomas looked down at his soup and thought: *you were there too. You dragged him. Your father pointed and you carried.* He'd told Elodie that much in her kitchen, whispering it like a prayer he was ashamed of. She'd believed him. That was the terrible thing — she'd believed him instantly, kindly, as though belief cost her nothing, and then she'd asked him to sit at this table and be bait, and he'd said yes, because saying yes was the first brave thing he'd done since he was nineteen years old, and he was already regretting it with his whole body.

A waiter refilled his water glass. Young, efficient, gloved.

"Thank you," Tomas murmured.

The waiter said nothing. But there was something in the set of the shoulders, the angle of the head under the visor, that made the cold climb up Tomas's spine and settle at the base of his skull like a hand.

He did not look up. He stared at his water glass and watched the surface tremble with his own pulse, and he understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own death certificate, that the waiter was standing very slightly too close, and was not leaving.

* * *

### Dane

From the service corridor windows, Dane Okafor could see everything and do nothing, which was the exact shape of hell as far as he was concerned.

Two deputies worked the kitchen entrance. Two more covered the garden gate. He had the guest list cross-referenced, the seating chart memorized, the timeline pinned: seven o'clock toast, seven-fifteen main course, and somewhere inside that window Iris Vane would make her move or she wouldn't, and either way the next forty minutes would decide whether Harlan County got its reckoning or buried the truth a second time, deeper, with better manners.

His radio crackled. Deputy Chen, at the kitchen door: *"Staff count confirmed. Fourteen badged, twelve verified against Halloran's roster. Two unknowns. One male, working the east tables. One—"*

"One what?"

A pause that lasted three seconds too long.

*"One hasn't been seen since setup. Badge found in the dish pit."*

Dane closed his eyes. An abandoned badge meant a disguise discarded — or exchanged. Iris Vane hadn't come to the dinner as a stranger. She'd come as one face among fourteen, and she'd already shed the skin they were looking for.

"Chen. Pull the kitchen photos from the check-in camera. Now. Send them to my phone."

*"On it."*

He moved back into the ballroom proper, unhurried, a sheriff enjoying a community event, scanning the white jackets. Twelve verified staff. If one badge was in the dish pit, then either Iris was one of the twelve he'd cleared — using a real employee's identity, which meant the real employee was somewhere they'd never find her — or she'd swapped badges with the missing worker and was moving among them right now, gloved and visored and invisible.

His phone buzzed. The check-in photo loaded.

He stopped walking.

The woman at the check-in table, badge number nine, was not Iris Vane. Rounder face, older, a real Halloran employee named Dolores Ketch he'd vetted personally. Which meant badge nine belonged to Dolores, and Dolores was fine, and the badge in the dish pit was—

He counted the white jackets on the floor again. Thirteen visible. Fourteen badged.

One of them was a ghost.

* * *

### Elodie

Seven o'clock came down over the lawn like a lid.

Marcus Merrick rose at the head table, and the string quartet softened, and two hundred conversations died mid-sentence the way they always did in this town when a Merrick stood up. Elodie stood near the rose trellis with an untouched plate, positioned — deliberately, she reminded herself, deliberately — with a clear line to table six.

Tomas sat rigid as a bound book. His water glass trembled. She'd been watching him for an hour the way she watched bodies on her table: cataloging distress. Rapid blink rate. No food touched. Hands hidden in his lap. He was terrified, and terror was legible, and legible terror was dangerous because Iris Vane could read it too.

Then Elodie saw the waiter.

Not by the face — the visor shadowed it — but by the hands. Every other server on the lawn carried trays with the flat, bored competence of people doing a job they'd done a thousand times. This one carried the tray like a prop. The grip was too precise. The posture was too straight. And the walk — Elodie had spent twenty years studying how human bodies moved through space, living and dead, and this body moved like someone performing the memory of walking rather than walking.

Heading for table six.

Her heart went slow and enormous, one beat filling whole seconds. *Don't run to him. Running tells her you know. Everything depends on her believing the stage is hers.*

The waiter reached table six. Set a fresh glass beside Tomas's full one. Bent — slightly, courteously — to adjust the placement.

And spoke. Elodie was too far to hear the words, but she saw Tomas's face change, saw the blood leave it, saw his hands come up off his lap and press flat against the tablecloth as if steadying the world.

*Now,* said every nerve she had. *Move now.*

But before she took a step, another figure crossed her sightline.

Marcus Merrick.

He was moving through his own party with a host's easy grace, laughing at something, clapping a shoulder, and his path bent — casually, inevitably — toward table six. Toward the waiter. Toward Tomas. And as he passed within arm's reach of the white-jacketed figure, he did something so small that no one else on that lawn could possibly have caught it.

He nodded.

One nod. Almost a bow. The nod a man gives a colleague. An accomplice. A craftsman whose work he has reviewed and approved.

And then Marcus smiled — at the waiter, at Tomas, at the beautiful evening — and raised his voice to carry across two hundred guests:

"Ladies and gentlemen. If you'd all raise your glasses."

* * *

### Tomas

The waiter's lips had barely moved. Three words, delivered with the tray still level, the courtesy still perfect:

*"Your turn, witness."*

Tomas's whole life arranged itself behind those words — the envelope of cash pressed into his nineteen-year-old palm on the night road, the decades of silence, the shop with its glue-pots and its locked back room where he kept every letter they'd ever sent him, because he'd never been able to throw away evidence of his own guilt. *Your turn.* As if it were a queue. As if justice were a line he'd been standing in wrong for twenty years.

And then Marcus Merrick had nodded at the waiter, and the world tilted.

Not approval of the murder — worse. Recognition. The nod of a man confirming a schedule. Marcus knew. Marcus had always known, and here, in front of everyone, under the strings and the candlelight, he was signaling to the killer that the program was proceeding on time, and Tomas understood with a lurch that the trap Elodie had built had a second layer inside it, one none of them had mapped: this wasn't just Iris's ceremony.

It was the family's.

"Glasses up," Marcus called warmly, "for my father — for the finest judge this county ever produced — and for the verdict that taught us all what justice looks like—"

Two hundred glasses rose. The sunset caught them, hundreds of small fires.

The waiter's right hand slid off the tray and into the jacket pocket.

Tomas did not think. Thinking was for men with time. He threw his chair backward and dove for the garden door, and behind him he heard the crystalline ring of a dropped champagne flute, and Elodie Fairweather's voice cutting across the lawn like a blade through silk:

"SHERIFF! TABLE SIX!"

* * *

### Elodie

Everything happened in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

The waiter spun — fast, trained, cornered — and for one instant the visor tipped up in the motion and Elodie saw the face beneath: the narrow jaw, the high eyebrow, the polite skepticism of Iris Vane, except there was nothing polite left in it. Her hand came out of the pocket holding something short and dark, and she wasn't reaching for Tomas anymore.

She was reaching for Elodie.

Because Elodie had broken cover. Because Elodie had shouted. Because Iris Vane, mid-performance, had just heard the one sentence that unraveled twenty years of design — and recalculated in less than a second, the way killers do, the way surgeons do, choosing the nearest threat.

Dane was thirty feet away and closing, deputies converging from the garden gate, two hundred guests rising in a single rustling wave of confusion and champagne. None of them would arrive in time. Elodie knew the geometry of it the way she knew anatomy, and she knew, with strange calm, that she had perhaps four seconds and a dessert fork.

Iris came at her low and economical, no flourish, a woman who had rehearsed this the way she rehearsed everything — in transcripts, in margins, in red ink.

Elodie stepped inside the reach instead of away from it.

It was the only move she had, and it almost worked. Iris's blade-hand swept past her shoulder; Elodie got a fistful of white jacket, got her weight behind it, drove them both backward into the rose trellis. Thorns raked her wrist. Somewhere very close, Dane was shouting her name, and somewhere farther, old Judge Aldous Merrick was being wheeled rapidly out of harm's way by his son — his son, who glanced back once at the struggle with an expression Elodie would carry to her grave.

Not alarm.

Assessment.

Then Iris's knee came up, brutal and precise, and the trellis broke, and they went down together into the roses as the first screams began, and above them the toast hung unfinished — two hundred raised glasses trembling in the amber light, waiting for a verdict no court in the country would ever have the courage to deliver.
