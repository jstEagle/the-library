## 7. Clerk of Secrets

The typewriter gave itself away on a Tuesday.

Ansel had spent the weekend with both letters flattened under glass on his dining table, the way he'd once studied contract language for hidden contingencies. He wasn't a handwriting man — that was for the forensic people in the city — but he was a documents man, and documents had habits. The letters were typed, which struck him first as an affectation and then as a signature. Nobody under sixty owned a typewriter. Nobody who owned one used it by accident.

He photographed each character at high resolution and laid them side by side on his monitor. The lowercase *e* had a chipped crossbar, a small white notch where ink refused to land. It appeared in *witness*, in *drowned*, in *confess*. The capital *H* leaned two degrees left of true. The ribbon was fabric, not carbon — you could see it in the soft bleed at the edges of every stroke, the way the letters sat slightly fat on the page like men who'd been drinking since noon.

Fabric ribbons meant maintenance. Maintenance meant an institution.

Monday morning he called three office-supply houses within fifty miles and asked, casually, who still serviced typewriters. Two of them laughed. The third, a repairman named Dill in Corvallis, went quiet when Ansel described the font.

"That's a Smith-Corona Electra 210," Dill said. "Heavy old girl. I know of maybe four in the county still in service." A pause. "One of them belongs to the county."

"The county what?"

"Records room. Harrow Bend annex. They use it for certified copies — death certificates, court transcripts, anything that needs to look official. State keeps threatening to digitize. Clerk won't let them."

Ansel thanked him and sat for a long moment with the phone warm against his ear.

Marla Voss had been town clerk for twenty-six years. She'd stamped Ansel's bar admission paperwork. She'd processed the deed when he bought his house on Delacroix Street. She was a small, precise woman with reading glasses on a beaded chain and a reputation — gently held, never stated outright — for knowing where everything was buried in Harrow Bend, because Marla had buried most of it herself, in triplicate.

He told Noor he had an errand. She looked at him the way she'd been looking at him all week, cataloguing, and said only: "Take pictures. If she lets you."

* * *

The county records annex occupied the ground floor of the old federal building, a limestone block from 1938 with a flagpole out front and a smell inside of cold paper and floor wax. Ansel signed the visitor log at 10:14 a.m. — he noted the time because he noted everything now; paranoia had become a filing system — and found Marla Voss behind her counter, feeding a document into a machine that clattered like distant artillery.

The Smith-Corona sat on a steel desk to her left, its platen shiny with age, a fresh black-and-red ribbon spooled inside.

"Ansel Okonkwo," she said without looking up. "You want a certified copy of something or you want to stand there smelling my toner?"

"I wanted to ask about your typewriter."

Her hands stopped. Not dramatically — Marla didn't do dramatic — but they stopped, the way a clock stops, and the silence in the records room got very large.

"What about it?" she said.

"It has a chipped *e*. Lowercase. Crossbar's broken on the type slug." He set both letters on the counter, face up, side by side. "So does this correspondence."

For a long moment Marla Voss looked at the letters the way a doctor looks at an X-ray of something she already suspected. Then she took off her glasses, folded them, hung them on their chain, and came around the counter.

"Not here," she said. "Break room. And bring those."

* * *

The break room was twelve feet square with a coffee pot older than Ansel's career and a window overlooking the parking lot. Marla shut the door, checked the hallway, and then sat down across from him with the posture of a woman preparing to give a deposition she'd rehearsed for years.

"You're going to accuse me of writing these," she said.

"Are you?"

"No." She said it flatly, without the little performative outrage innocent people deploy. "But I know who could have. Sit down, counselor. This is going to take longer than your parking meter allows."

Ansel sat. He did not touch the coffee.

"Twenty-six years," Marla began, "I have kept this county's records. Every birth, every death, every deed, every lien, every case file that passed through the circuit clerk's office before they consolidated upstairs. You know what I learned in twenty-six years?"

"That paper is patient."

She almost smiled. "That paper is *for sale*. Not by me — don't look at me like that, we'll get there. By the system. A record is only as permanent as the person paid to keep it. And everybody in this town eventually wants something kept less permanently."

Ansel felt the floor tilt a degree. "What are you telling me, Marla?"

"I'm telling you there is a market. There has always been a market. A DUI that never gets entered. A judgment that gets misfiled into a box nobody opens. A name spelled wrong on a deed until the title's cloudy enough to choke on. Men — always men, mostly — come down here with cash or favors or a sad story, and for twenty years I made things... quieter." She laced her fingers on the table. "I'm not proud. I'm also not pretending I invented it. I'm the last link in a chain that goes back to before this building had plumbing."

"And the Haddad file."

Marla didn't flinch. "Danny Haddad. Three years ago. Ruled accidental drowning, closed in eleven days." She recited it like a docket entry. "That file came through my hands twice. Once when it was opened. Once after."

"After?"

"After it was edited." She met his eyes. "Somebody brought me the coroner's report and asked me to make a substitution. Original pages out, new pages in, same case number, same date stamps — I have a way of aging paper, don't ask, it involves tea and a toaster oven and I'll deny it under oath. The replacement report read cleaner. Fewer questions. The original went into my personal archive."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "Your personal archive."

"Every case. Duplicate copies of everything, going back decades. Four filing cabinets in my garage, counselor, organized better than anything upstairs. If a document ever got 'lost' in this county, I kept the ghost of it." She tapped the two letters. "Which is why I know I didn't write these. Because whoever did wrote details that exist in exactly one place — the *edited* Haddad file. The version upstairs. The version the public saw."

Ansel leaned forward. "You've read the letters' contents?"

"I read everything that crosses my counter, and you logged in carrying them like a man carrying a live grenade." She pushed the letters back toward him. "Whoever typed these knew what the falsified report says. Which means they either made the edit, ordered the edit, or read the edited file after it went back upstairs."

"Who accessed it?"

Marla's laugh was short and bitter. "Access logs? Ansel. I *am* the access log. And my log says the Haddad file was pulled six times in three years." She counted on her fingers, an old habit, nails cut square. "Deputy Reyes, when he requested a review and got stonewalled. The district attorney's office, routine. A private investigator hired by the Haddad family — the sister, I'd wager, though she used a Portland firm. And three times by Gerald Prentiss."

"Gerald—" Ansel stopped. His predecessor's name sat in his throat like a stone. "Gerry died last year. Heart attack."

"He did." Marla's eyes were unreadable. "Eleven days after his third pull of the Haddad file. Isn't that a coincidence."

The break room hummed. Somewhere out front, the Smith-Corona clattered through another certified copy, its chipped *e* stamping the official record of Harrow Bend.

"Marla," Ansel said carefully. "If I asked you to open your archive — if I asked for the original Haddad coroner's report —"

"I'd tell you no."

"Why?"

"Because the moment it leaves my garage, whoever buried it knows it surfaced, and then you're not an investigator anymore, you're a body." She stood, straightened her cardigan, and put her glasses back on — the clerk again, the institution again. "But I'll tell you something for free, because Danny Haddad was nineteen and because I'm tired. Gerry Prentiss didn't pull that file three times to read it. I watched him on the camera. Third time, he photocopied the whole thing. Took him forty minutes. And when he walked out, he wasn't carrying it like a lawyer."

"How was he carrying it?"

"Like evidence," Marla said. "Like a man holding something over somebody else."

She opened the break room door and paused, back to him.

"One more thing. The letters. Whoever wrote them wanted you digging — that part's obvious, even to me. But think about *who benefits* from a lawyer in this town suddenly pulling threads. Somebody wanted the thread pulled, Ansel. Somebody's been waiting three years for a man stubborn enough to pull it." She glanced over her shoulder. "And somebody else has been waiting just as long to make sure he doesn't finish. Ask yourself which one typed with a chipped *e* — and which one is watching you right now."

* * *

Ansel drove back to the office with the windows down and the letters locked in his briefcase, doing what he did best: building lists.

Suspects. The coroner who'd signed the false report — Dr. Pell, retired now but still playing golf at the country club every Thursday, still beloved. Whoever had commissioned the edit — someone with money or leverage over Marla's market, which narrowed to half the town council and two deacons. Gerald Prentiss, dead a year, who had copied the file like a man collecting ransom. And the letter writer, who owned or had access to a county Smith-Corona, which meant a county employee, a night visitor, or a thief with excellent taste in obsolete machinery.

Four cabinets in a garage full of ghosts. A dead lawyer who might have been blackmailing a killer. A confession written by someone who'd read the lie instead of witnessing the truth.

Noor was waiting in his office when he got back, sitting in his client chair with her arms crossed, and the look on her face told him she hadn't spent the morning idle either.

"Before you say anything," she said, "you need to see what I found in Gerry Prentiss's dead storage."

She was holding a banker's box, and her hands were shaking.
