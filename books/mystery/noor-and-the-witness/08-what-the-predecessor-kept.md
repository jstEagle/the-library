## 8. What the Predecessor Kept

The dead storage room sat at the back of the firm like a held breath.

Noor had noticed it her first week — a door off the copy room with a brass plate reading ARCHIVES, always locked, always cool to the touch, as if the building kept one room in permanent winter. Ansel had given her the key on Tuesday without ceremony. "Everything before 2019 is mine to sort," he'd said. "Everything after is yours." He hadn't understood what he was handing her. She doubted he understood yet himself.

She went in after hours, when the office emptied and the cleaning crew's vacuum droned two floors down. She told herself it was diligence. Cataloging the Grange estate meant understanding Grange, and understanding Grange meant understanding every hand that had touched his papers — including the hands that came before Ansel Okonkwo's.

Gerald Mott. That was the predecessor's name. Ansel spoke of him rarely, and when he did, it was with the flat affection people reserve for the deceased: *Jerry trained me. Jerry liked things filed a certain way. Jerry dropped dead of a heart attack at sixty-one, right there at his desk.* A year ago this October. Ansel had inherited the practice the way you inherit a coat from a father — too big, still warm, smelling faintly of someone else's cigarettes.

Noor clicked on the fluorescent tube overhead. It stuttered twice before committing.

Row upon row of banker's boxes, labeled in Gerald Mott's cramped architect's hand: estate names, years, matter numbers. The alphabetized world Ansel loved had been built on these bones. She ran her finger along the spines until she found GRANGE, E. — MATTER 4471 — and beside it, unexpectedly, a second box with no label at all, just a strip of masking tape gone amber with age.

It was lighter than the others. It rattled.

* * *

Inside were notebooks. Eleven of them, cheap composition books with marbled covers, the kind schoolchildren use. No law firm kept its secrets in composition books unless it wanted them deniable, and Gerald Mott had been a lawyer for thirty-five years; he knew exactly what deniable looked like.

Noor pulled a chair from the corner and sat down under the buzzing light.

The early notebooks were mundane — client gossip, billing grudges, a running scorecard of Harrow Bend's petty corruptions. *Marla V. will "lose" anything for $500, double if it involves a divorce.* Noor's breath caught. She'd half-suspected the clerk; seeing it named in a dead man's handwriting made her stomach drop anyway. *Sheriff's dept: Colm R. clean but naive. Coroner: see separate file.*

See separate file. There was no separate file. Not in this box.

She turned pages faster. Then, in the fourth notebook, dated three years back — the autumn Danny died — the handwriting changed. Tighter. Angrier. The pen pressing through the paper.

*I pulled the Haddad file today because the mother keeps calling my office. Woman won't quit. Read the coroner's report against the deputy's incident report and they don't match. Blood alcohol listed at .19 — but the tox screen request form is dated four days AFTER the report was signed. You can't test blood that hasn't been drawn yet. Either the laziest paperwork in county history or somebody wrote the ending first.*

Noor's hands had gone cold. She read it again. *Somebody wrote the ending first.* Danny hadn't drowned drunk. Danny had been made to look like he drowned drunk, and the paperwork had been assembled backward, like a lie built from the roof down.

The next entries came quicker, the dates crowding each other:

*Met with the coroner, D. Pruitt. Asked him about the tox timing. He sweated through his shirt. Told me the river takes what the river takes and I should think about my practice. Threat or advice? Both.*

*Pruitt came to MY office tonight. Uninvited. Sat where Ansel sits now. Said he knows about the Voss arrangement, said if I go to the state bar everyone learns what this firm has buried over the years. Then he smiled and said drowning boys don't have lawyers. Left an envelope. Inside: photos of me leaving the Blue Flamingo with a woman who isn't my wife. He's been collecting. He collects everything. That's how he thinks — files on people instead of cases.*

*He wants money now. Monthly. Says it's cheaper than my reputation. God help me, I paid. First payment October 14. Set up a cash account under the name G. Whitfield. If anyone finds this, know that I hated myself more each month than the month before.*

Noor set the notebook down. Her eyes were burning and she let them. Three years she'd believed the town had failed Danny out of laziness, indifference, small-town rot. She hadn't let herself imagine something worse: that someone had known, immediately and precisely, and had chosen to feed on it. That her brother's death hadn't been covered up so much as *monetized* — first by Marla Voss's quiet erasures, then by a blackmailer who'd turned the cover-up into an annuity.

And then Gerald Mott had died of a heart attack at his desk, and the payments had stopped, and the man collecting them had lost his income and his audience in the same stroke.

* * *

The last notebook was different. Thinner. The handwriting shakier, the dates closer together — the final months before his death.

*I saw the doctor. He says what I already knew. My heart is a bad tenant and it's going to skip rent one of these days. So I'm writing this like a will, because nobody else will say it.*

*I've thought for a year about whether Pruitt could have done something to hurry me along. I don't believe it. I checked my pills, I checked my food, I checked everything, because that's the kind of man I've become. But here's the thing that frightens me more: I don't need him to have done it. He only ever needed to wait. Men like Daniel Pruitt don't kill what they can outlast.*

*One more thing. The letters. I've started getting letters. Typed. Same machine as the old case files — I'd know the font anywhere, I've read ten thousand of them. They never threaten. They ask questions. Do I remember the river that night. Do I remember what Danny Haddad was carrying. Whether I sleep well. It's a game to him. He doesn't want money anymore — he has enough. He wants to be KNOWN. He wants someone clever enough to find him and too compromised to turn him in. He's been testing me for months to see if I'll dig.*

*I won't give him the satisfaction. And I won't dig either, because I already know what I'd find, and I'm tired, and my heart can't take the truth.*

*Whoever inherits this office: I'm sorry. Check the box without a label.*

Noor closed the notebook and sat in the cold hum of the archive room, grief and fury arriving together like weather fronts colliding. Gerald Mott had known. Had known and paid and shrunk and finally chosen to die quietly rather than fight — and had left the whole poisoned inheritance to a meticulous young lawyer who trusted the filing system the way other men trusted scripture.

The letters weren't new. The letters were a *sequel*. Daniel Pruitt had run this game once before, on a dying man too frightened to play. Now he'd dealt the same hand to Ansel Okonkwo — younger, cleaner, unblackmailable — and the six-week deadline wasn't a statute-of-limitations countdown at all.

It was a test. *Will this one dig?*

And somewhere across town, the man who signed death certificates was waiting to see the answer, watching the office the way he'd watched Gerald Mott's desk lamp burn late into the night.

Noor photographed every page with shaking hands, re-shelved the box exactly as she'd found it, and was reaching for the light switch when she heard it — the front door of the firm opening downstairs, unhurried, familiar footsteps crossing the reception area toward the copy room.

Toward the archives door.

Footsteps she knew.
