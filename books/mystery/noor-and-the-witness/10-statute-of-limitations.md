## 10. Statute of Limitations

**Ansel**

The reading was scheduled for ten o'clock on a Tuesday, which Ansel chose because Tuesdays in Harrow Bend were the days nothing happened — and therefore the days no one would expect something to.

He had dressed for it the way he dressed for closings: gray suit, blue tie, shoes polished to a dull shine. But under the left cuff, taped flat against his forearm, sat the recorder, a slim black thing Noor had bought with cash at a pawn shop in Corvallis. The red light was off. It didn't need to be on yet. It only needed to be ready.

The conference room held six chairs and a long table scarred with ring-stains from forty years of coffee. Ansel had arranged the codicil — his codicil, his forgery, his sin — in a green folder at the table's center, squared to the table edge with a precision that was, he admitted, its own kind of tell. Noor stood by the window, arms crossed, watching the parking lot through the blinds like a woman counting soldiers.

"He's here," she said.

Ansel didn't ask how she knew. He'd stopped asking how she knew things three weeks ago, when she'd identified the make of a tire from a photograph of a mud impression and hadn't looked up from her coffee while doing it.

Dr. Emmett Sorel came through the front door at 9:52, exactly eight minutes early, the way a man arrives when he wants to seem casual and has rehearsed the seeming. He wore his good jacket, the corduroy one with the leather buttons, and he carried a leather satchel that Ansel knew — because he'd inventoried the man's life down to his dental records — contained a legal pad, two pens, and nothing else. A man bringing tools to a reading he had no business attending.

"Dr. Sorel," Ansel said, extending a hand. "I didn't list you among the interested parties."

"Town is small, Mr. Okonkwo. I signed the death certificate on half the people in that file cabinet of yours. I like to know how they left their affairs." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had signed off on an accidental drowning three years ago and a convenient heart attack a year before that, and it had never once wavered.

"Of course," Ansel said. "Please. Sit."

* * *

**Noor**

Noor had rehearsed this moment for three years, and in every rehearsal she had cried, or screamed, or put her hands around a throat. In the actual moment she did none of those things. She stood by the window and watched Emmett Sorel's hands.

That was the thing no one told you about grief. It made you a scholar of the small. She knew Danny's hands better than her own — the scar on the left thumb from the bike chain, the way he drummed his fingers when he was thinking, the way he'd held his cigarette cupped against the wind that last October night, standing on the bank of the Willamette slough while she yelled at him from the porch to come inside, Danny, it's freezing, Danny.

The coroner's hands were folded on the table now, patient, proprietary. Hands that had turned her brother over on a steel table and written *consistent with accidental immersion* and gone home to dinner.

Ansel opened the folder. "Before we begin, a housekeeping matter. The codicil references a document recovered from Mr. Grange's effects. A photograph."

Noor saw it happen — the small stillness in Sorel's shoulders, the half-second where his breathing went from automatic to deliberate. Three years of reading that body across a morgue table had taught her the grammar of stillness. He hadn't known about the photograph. He'd known about the *codicil* — the forged one, the bait, the thing Ansel had let slip to Marla Voss, who had let it slip to everyone, because that was what Marla did. But the photograph was new. The photograph was the one true thing in a room full of lies, and it landed on him like a dropped glass.

"What photograph?" Sorel asked, and his voice was perfect, and that was how she knew.

* * *

**Ansel**

Ansel slid the photograph across the table. It was real — Grange's own, pulled from the shoebox in the dead storage, the night river gray and grainy, two figures on the bank, one face turned away. He had made a copy for the sheriff's deputy waiting in his car and kept this one here, under his hand, like a card he was tired of holding.

Sorel looked at it for four seconds. Ansel counted. Then Sorel laughed, a short dry sound, and said, "That could be anyone. That could be a shadow."

"It could," Ansel agreed. "Except for the watch. See the glint on the second figure's wrist? Grange documented everything. He documented the watch because he documented everything, and the watch is a Breitling with a cracked crystal, and you've worn it to every board meeting in this county for six years. There's a photograph of you wearing it in the *Gazette*, March of the year Danny Haddad drowned."

The room went very quiet. Through the blinds, Noor had stopped watching the parking lot. She was watching Sorel now, and her face had the terrible calm of a person who has stopped hoping for anything.

"You've made an accusation," Sorel said.

"I've made an observation. You're free to leave."

And here was the thing Ansel had counted on, the thing he'd understood only after reading the predecessor's notes, after Marla's confession, after six weeks of letters in a dead man's typewriter font: Sorel didn't want to leave. Leaving was what guilty men did. Sorel was not, at bottom, a guilty man. He was a *curator*. He had written two letters, signed a false report, blackmailed and been blackmailed, all because he could not stand a story that ended without his hand on it. He had killed Danny Haddad in a panic and then spent three years arranging the aftermath like flowers, and what he wanted, what he had always wanted, was an audience.

"You think you understand what happened down there," Sorel said softly.

"No," Ansel said. "Tell me."

* * *

**Noor**

He told them.

He told it in the voice of a man giving a lecture, standing now, pacing the four steps the conference room allowed, and Noor stood against the wall with her nails biting her palms and let him, because every word was going onto the tape in Ansel's cuff, every word was the thing the sheriff's report had never contained.

He'd been drinking that night — just two, he insisted, just two — driving home along the slough road when Danny stepped out of the dark to flag him down. A dispute. Danny had been running errands for Marla Voss, little erasures, little payments, and he'd wanted more, or wanted out, or wanted both, and Sorel was the one who handled the quiet things. A scuffle. The bank was slick. Danny went into the water and Sorel stood there — he said this plainly, almost wonderingly — and *did not jump in*.

"He grabbed at me," Sorel said. "He grabbed my jacket and the watch caught on the fabric, and he went under, and I stood there and I listened to the water and I thought: this is the moment. This is the moment where I find out what I am."

Noor's vision went white at the edges. She heard herself say, from very far away, "And what were you?"

Sorel turned and looked at her, and for the first time his face lost its polish, and what was underneath was not a monster. It was something worse. It was a man genuinely asking himself the same question.

"I was the one who writes the ending," he said. "I signed the certificate. I closed the file. I wrote the letters because — do you know what it's like, Mr. Okonkwo, to be the only person who knows a story? To hold a town's whole truth in your chest for three years? I needed someone to *find* it. I needed a witness. Grange was supposed to be the witness, but Grange just — hoarded it. Sat on it like a dragon. So I found a new witness. You."

"You killed Mr. Whitlock's predecessor," Noor said. "The heart attack."

"The predecessor was a leech," Sorel said, with real disgust. "He found the discrepancy in the file and he wanted five hundred a month to forget it. I adjusted his medication. It was practically hospice work."

The recorder in Ansel's cuff had been running for eleven minutes. Noor knew because she'd timed it, because she timed everything now, because timing was the only prayer she had left.

* * *

**Ansel**

When it was over — when Sorel had run out of story and stood there in the middle of the room breathing like a man who'd just set down something heavy — Ansel did the thing he had decided on at three in the morning, alone, staring at his alphabetized shelves.

He did not call the sheriff.

He picked up the green folder, put the photograph back inside it, and said, "This reading is concluded. Dr. Sorel, you'll want to leave through the back. Deputy Reyes is watching the front."

Sorel stared at him. "You're letting me go?"

"I'm letting you *walk*," Ansel said. "Where you walk is not my decision."

He took the recorder from his cuff, stopped it, and held it out — not to the phone, not to the deputy idling outside — but to Noor Haddad.

"Your brother," he said. "Your family. You decide what happens next. The law can have what's left."

Noor took it in both hands, the way you take something that weighs more than it should, and for a moment the only sound in the room was the tape spool settling.

* * *

**Noor**

Her mother's house smelled like cardamom and the dust of three years of closed curtains. Noor played the recording on the kitchen table, phone flat between them, and watched her mother's face do the thing Noor had spent three years being afraid to imagine: nothing, and then everything.

When it ended, Samira Haddad sat for a long time with her hands around a cooling glass of tea. Then she said, in Arabic, the words Noor had waited three years to hear and had never once let herself rehearse:

"Then it wasn't his fault. He didn't go into the water on purpose. My son didn't choose the river."

"No," Noor said. "He didn't."

Her mother nodded slowly, and then she stood, and she washed her hands at the sink the way she did before every serious thing, and she dried them, and she said: "Call the sheriff. And then call the lawyer. And then we will go to the river, and we will say his name out loud where he can hear it."

* * *

**Ansel**

The sheriff's cars came at dusk, two of them, quiet and unhurried. Ansel watched from his office window as they took Emmett Sorel out of his own front door in handcuffs, the corduroy jacket over his arm, the Breitling glinting on his wrist in the porch light. Sorel did not struggle. He looked, once, directly across the street at Ansel's darkened window — Ansel was sure of it, though he could never prove it — and inclined his head slightly, one curator acknowledging another.

Ansel stayed at the office long after the cars were gone. He unlocked the file cabinet and pulled the Haddad file and did not reshelve it. He set it on his desk, open, under the lamp, pages fanned out crooked, and he sat with the disorder of it like a man sitting with a wound.

For twenty years he had believed the alphabet was a form of justice. That if you filed things correctly, in the right order, under the right names, the world would resolve. He knew better now. The file had been edited by a clerk, blackmailed over by a lawyer, closed by a coroner who wrote endings for a living — and every one of them had touched it before he ever had, and he had never noticed, because the folders were all straight and the labels were all true.

The letters had promised a full confession if he could name the writer before the statute ran out. He had. With four days to spare.

But there was one thing the letters had never explained, one detail Ansel had turned over every night for six weeks and never solved: the first letter had arrived *the day after* Elias Grange's funeral, before the will was even opened. Before anyone could have known Ansel was the custodian of anything.

Which meant the writer hadn't been watching the file at all.

He'd been watching *Ansel* — and had been, Ansel understood now, sitting very still in the back of the funeral, long before any of this began.
