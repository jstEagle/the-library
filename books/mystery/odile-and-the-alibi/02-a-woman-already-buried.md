## 2. A Woman Already Buried

The envelope sat in Isandro's locker for two days before they opened it again.

Not because they'd forgotten it. Isandro forgot nothing — that was the whole architecture of their life. They remembered which cabs ran hot on their meters, which drivers padded their fuel receipts, which of the night crew took their coffee black and which of them lied about taking it black. The envelope stayed shut because opening it a second time would mean admitting the first time had mattered, and Isandro had built forty-one years on the principle that nothing should matter enough to leave a mark.

But Tuesday night came around again, slow as syrup, and the depot at 1:40 a.m. had nothing in it but the hum of the vending machine and the blue glow of the dispatch console, and Isandro's hands were idle, and idle hands were the one thing they had never learned to tolerate.

They slit the envelope along its crease with a box cutter and laid everything out on the desk like evidence at a trial. Cash — still there, still fifties, still too much. The photograph of Odile Vasseur. And beneath it, the instruction sheet, typed on paper that had no watermark, no letterhead, no fingerprint of personality whatsoever:

*Confirm subject remains alive through December 21. Report by prepaid channel. Do not make contact.*

Do not make contact. As if the woman in the photo were a bear, or a fault line.

Isandro put on their reading glasses — the ones nobody at the depot knew about, because knowing would require looking — and studied the photograph properly this time. It was a surveillance shot, not a portrait. Taken from across a street, slightly high angle, the kind of frame you got from a parked car or an upper window. Odile Vasseur was mid-stride on a sidewalk, coat collar up, a canvas bag slung across her body. Her face was turned three-quarters toward the camera, caught in the flat gray light of what looked like late afternoon. She wasn't beautiful in any way a magazine would bother with. She was the kind of face the city produced by the thousand: watchful, unremarkable, arranged specifically so that no one would give it a second glance.

Isandro knew that arrangement intimately. They wore one themselves every shift.

And yet something in the set of her mouth — a tension, a held breath, the look of a person listening for footsteps behind her even in a still photograph — snagged on Isandro like a burr in wool. He knew that look. He'd seen it in his own bathroom mirror for three decades.

He turned the photo over. Nothing. No date stamp, no location. Just the white gloss and, faintly, the ghost of a thumbprint in the corner where someone had handled it before sealing it away.

"Okay," Isandro said aloud, to the empty office, because saying it aloud made it smaller. "Who are you?"

The terminal in front of them could pull license plates, run driver credentials, cross-reference fare disputes back seven years. It could not, officially, do anything else. But Isandro had spent eleven years learning the seams of the city's databases the way a seamstress learns the flaws in cheap fabric — where they gave, where they tore, where you could slip something through without anyone noticing the stitch. A records search on a private citizen was technically a violation of the commission's data-use policy. Isandro had never once cared about policy. Policy was for people who wanted to be noticed breaking things.

They typed the name into the municipal registry first, expecting nothing. Small names returned small results: parking tickets, business licenses, the occasional jury summons. Isandro expected a lease record, maybe. An old utility account. Something to anchor the woman to the ground.

What came back instead was a death certificate.

Isandro stared at it for a long moment, long enough that the screen dimmed and they had to touch the mouse to wake it. *Vasseur, Odile Marie.* Date of birth: March 3rd. Date of death: November 14th, two years ago. Place of death listed as St. Ambrose Hospital. Cause of death: blunt force trauma, undetermined circumstances. Informant's signature: *Tomas Reyes* — spouse. Filed, stamped, certified. The little seal in the corner of the digital record was as official as anything the city produced, which was to say it was bureaucratic and gray and absolutely certain of itself.

Odile Vasseur had been dead for two years.

Isandro took off their glasses, cleaned them on the hem of their shirt, and put them back on. The certificate did not change. Dead women did not walk past the depot every night at 2 a.m. with a canvas bag and a face built for not being seen. Dead women did not appear in surveillance photographs taken — Isandro checked the shadows, the light quality, the length of the season's shadows against the buildings — sometime within the last few months.

Unless, said a small cold voice in the back of Isandro's skull, the woman walking past the depot was not Odile Vasseur. Unless someone was wearing a dead woman's face, and the client who paid in fifties wanted to know whether the mask would hold until the solstice.

That thought should have been the end of it. That thought was, in fact, the only sane reading of the evidence, and Isandro had survived this long by being the sanest person in any room, quietly, without anyone noticing they were doing it. Log the anomaly. File the envelope. Return the cash to the drop box with a note declining the contract. Go home. Sleep badly, as always. Come back tomorrow.

Isandro's hand was actually moving toward the drawer where they kept the outgoing mail when the other part of their brain — the part that had never forgiven the world for making them invisible — whispered: *or she isn't dead, and everyone who says she is, is lying.*

*

The police file was harder.

Isandro started where all careful people start: with the public-facing portal, the one journalists and insurance adjusters used. Case number lookup required either a case number or a name plus date of incident. Isandro entered *Vasseur, Odile* and the date from the certificate, and the portal spun its little wheel and returned:

**RECORD SEALED. Access restricted under Municipal Code 14-9(b). Direct inquiries to the Office of the City Registrar.**

Fourteen-nine-b. Isandro knew that code the way most people knew their own phone number, because fourteen-nine-b was the code that got invoked whenever the taxi commission did something it didn't want printed in the morning paper. Sealed pending investigation. Sealed for privacy. Sealed at the request of counsel. The code was a trapdoor, and over the years Isandro had watched it swallow a bribery inquiry, a fleet-maintenance scandal, and one very memorable incident involving a councilman, a commission-owned sedan, and a motel on Delacroix Street. Fourteen-nine-b was not a law. Fourteen-nine-b was a broom.

A dead woman's file, sealed under the same broom that swept up political embarrassments. Isandro sat back in the creaking dispatch chair and let that settle.

The night deepened around the office. At 2:07 a.m., right on schedule, the exterior camera feed caught a figure crossing the far end of Depot Street — small, coat collar up, canvas bag. Isandro switched to the street-level cam and zoomed, and there she was, grainy and gray in the infrared wash: the woman from the photograph, walking the same route she walked every night, past the chain-link, past the fuel island, past the dead marquee of the old Rialto Theater, gone by 2:09. Two minutes of existence per night. Isandro had logged her passage eleven times now without ever intending to; she was simply part of the depot's nocturnal inventory, like the raccoon that raided the dumpster and the 2:15 freight train that made the windows hum.

Dead women did not keep schedules. Ghosts, in Isandro's experience, were considerably less punctual.

They pulled the hospital record next, or tried to. St. Ambrose's patient index was a patchwork of legacy systems, but Isandro had once spent an entire summer mapping its quirks for reasons that had seemed important at the time and now seemed merely characteristic. The search on *Vasseur* returned an admission record dated November 13th, two years ago — one day *before* the certificate's date of death. Admitted 11:40 p.m., trauma bay, status on arrival: critical. And then, appended to the record like a footnote someone hoped no one would read: **Record transferred to sealed custody per MC 14-9(b).**

Sealed custody. Not transferred to another facility. Not discharged. Sealed. Isandro had processed enough paperwork in eleven years of dispatch work to know what that phrasing meant: someone with authority had reached into the hospital's system and pulled the file out of the world. People did not get pulled out of the world. Files did. Which raised the question Isandro was beginning to suspect sat at the center of everything: which one was Odile Vasseur?

*

At 3:30, Ren wandered in from the break room with the particular shuffling gait of a man who had lost an argument with the vending machine.

"You're doing the thing," Ren said.

Isandro didn't look up. "What thing."

"The thing where you go quiet and the screen reflects in your glasses and everybody in the building knows better than to ask." Ren dropped into the chair by the door, the one with the broken spring that he'd claimed as his own since before Isandro's time. "Halloran asked if you were okay. I told him you were fine, you're always fine, you're the finest."

"I'm fine."

"Sure." Ren peeled open a granola bar he'd apparently extracted from the machine through sheer persistence. "Slow night. Third one this week. You notice that? Winter always kills the fares, but this is different. Like the whole city decided to stay home."

Isandro noticed everything; that was the problem. Yes, the fares had been thinning for weeks in a way that didn't match the season. Yes, Halloran had been down in the archive room more than usual, and yes, the archive room's lock had been changed last month without a memo. These observations lived side by side in Isandro's head like strangers at a bus stop, each minding its own business, none yet aware they were connected.

"Ren," Isandro said, keeping their voice at the level, bored register that made questions sound like small talk. "Two years back — November, roughly — anything strange come through dispatch? Big incident, hospital run, anything with cops attached?"

Ren chewed. Considered. "November's always weird. Solstice season, the crazies come out early." He shrugged. "There was a fatality call, I think. Woman hit near the river district. Cabs were all over it for a night, then nothing. Why?"

"Just filling in gaps in the log archive. Halloran wants the old weeks reconciled."

"Halloran wants a lot of things lately." Ren said it lightly, but his eyes flicked toward the archive room door and away again, quick as a card trick, and Isandro filed the flick away with all the others.

When Ren shuffled off to relieve Bo in the garage, Isandro went back to the terminal and pulled the incident logs for the second week of November, two years ago. There it was, buried between a drunk-and-disorderly at the Greyhound station and a multi-car fender bender on the viaduct: **23:52 — Pedestrian struck, River District, 400 blk. VASSEUR, O. Transported St. Ambrose, critical. PD on scene.** And then, appended twelve hours later, a single line in the flat administrative language Isandro had learned to read the way priests read Latin: **Case referred. No further dispatch action.**

No further dispatch action. A woman had been struck by a car — or by something — at midnight in the river district, and within half a day the entire apparatus of the city had closed around the event like water closing over a stone. No follow-up calls. No press inquiries routed through dispatch, which was unheard of; a pedestrian fatality usually generated a week of reporter calls. No insurance requests, no family inquiries, nothing. The stone sank, and the surface of the water went smooth, and everyone agreed to pretend the water had always been smooth.

Someone had done that. Smoothness like that took effort. Isandro knew, because they manufactured their own smoothness daily, and it was the hardest work they did.

*

By 4:45 a.m., the office had gone blue-gray with the first suggestion of dawn, and Isandro had assembled the facts on a legal pad in their small, backward-slanting handwriting, because some things needed to exist on paper, away from terminals that kept records of who searched what:

*1. Odile Vasseur died Nov 14, two years ago. Certificate filed. Informant: Tomas Reyes, spouse.*
*2. Cause: blunt force trauma. Hospitalized the night before. Both records sealed under 14-9(b) — the same code used to bury commission scandals.*
*3. No press. No follow-up. No family noise. A fatal pedestrian strike that vanished inside 24 hours.*
*4. A woman matching her photograph walks past this depot nightly at 2:00 a.m.*
*5. Someone paid $4,000 in cash to confirm she stays alive through Dec 21.*

Five facts. Isandro stared at them and felt the vertigo rise, slow and tidal, the way it always did when the world's filing system failed. Because here was the thing about Isandro Dravenmoor's life, the thing that made it livable: it was ordered. The city was chaos, but the depot was a grid, and the grid ran on Isandro's attention, and as long as Isandro paid attention, nothing surprised anyone. Drivers got home. Fares got picked up. The night moved in its channels. Isandro was the invisible hand on the tiller of a machine nobody thanked, and that invisibility was not a wound — Isandro had told themselves it was not a wound for thirty years — it was a craft. A discipline. You stayed unseen, and in exchange, nothing touched you.

And now a dead woman was walking past the window every night at 2 a.m., and the city's entire machinery of records and seals and certificates had conspired to insist she didn't exist, and somewhere out there a person with four thousand dollars and excellent operational security believed that before the winter solstice, someone was going to try to make the fiction true.

Which meant the fiction had been true once already. Or had been made true once already. Either way: someone had killed Odile Vasseur on paper, and paper, in Isandro's experience, was how this city did its real killing. Paper took your name, your license, your pension, your witness statement, your case file. Paper erased you while leaving your body conveniently ambulatory, free to be finished off later at the killer's leisure.

Isandro looked at the clock. 5:02. In fifty-eight minutes, the day crew would arrive, and Halloran would come out of whatever early meeting he'd begun holding with the commission liaison, and the depot would fill with voices, and the night's work would dissolve into the ordinary noise of the day.

Isandro folded the legal pad in thirds and slid it into the inner pocket of their jacket, against the ribs, where the envelope's cash had briefly lived. Then they pulled up the route logs one more time and did the thing they had been circling all night, the thing that violated the client's single explicit instruction, the thing that would have horrified the version of themselves from three days ago:

They began planning a route change. Effective tonight, cab 44 — Isandro's own preferred vehicle for unlogged errands — would take the River District loop at 1:55 a.m. There was a stretch of Depot Street, just past the Rialto's dead marquee, where a cab could idle with its hazards on for a few minutes without drawing any particular notice. Isandro had driven that stretch a thousand times and been seen by no one, ever.

Tomorrow night, they intended to be seen. Or rather — they intended, for the first time in their working life, to see.

Outside, the freight train moaned through the crossing, and the windows hummed, and at the far edge of the camera feed, right on schedule, a figure with a canvas bag stepped off the curb into the blue dark and walked the two minutes of her existence past the depot fence. Isandro watched her go, and felt the ground tilt beneath the orderly grid of their world, and heard themselves say, quietly, to the empty office:

"Two years dead. Punctual as a fare meter. Okay, Ms. Vasseur. Let's find out who buried you — and why they're planning to do it again."

The screen in front of them refreshed, and a new line appeared in the overnight queue, routine as breathing: a pickup request, prepaid, flagged priority. The pickup address was the decommissioned substation on Canal Street. The destination field contained a single word: *SOON.*

Isandro's hand hovered over the keyboard, and for the first time in eleven years, they were afraid of the radio.
