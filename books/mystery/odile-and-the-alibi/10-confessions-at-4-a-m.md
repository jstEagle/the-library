## 10. Confessions at 4 A.M.

The Starlight Diner sat on the corner of Meridian and Ninth like a lit match in the dark, its windows fogged from the inside, its neon sign missing the *S* so that it read TARLIGHT, which Isandro had always privately thought was more honest. Everything in this city was a tar light — a dim orange glow you drove toward without ever quite arriving.

They took the booth at the back, the one with the torn vinyl seat and the view of both doors. Isandro noticed that Odile had chosen it without appearing to choose it; she'd simply drifted toward it the way water finds the low ground. Old habit. The kind of habit you build when your body is always doing threat assessment your mouth never mentions.

The waitress — a heavyset woman with reading glasses on a chain and the unbothered air of someone who had seen every species of 4 a.m. human — poured two coffees without asking and left them alone. The diner was empty except for a trucker dozing over a plate of hash browns and a couple of teenagers sharing one milkshake with the solemnity of people splitting a secret.

Odile wrapped both hands around her cup. She was wearing a gray wool coat that made her look like weather itself, something that had blown in off the river. Up close, in honest light, she looked less like the woman in the photograph than Isandro expected. The photo had been sharp, official, a face caught mid-blink by some bureaucratic lens. This woman was softer-edged and harder-eyed all at once.

"You first," she said.

"First what?"

"Confession." A ghost of a smile. "You've been circling me for three weeks, dispatcher. You know my route better than I do. You know I buy the same coffee every night at the kiosk on Delancey and never drink it. You know I take the long way past the impound lot even though it adds eleven minutes to my walk home. You've earned one question answered honestly. So ask."

Isandro turned the cup slowly on its saucer. Outside, a cab hissed past through the wet street, its roof light a small yellow moon going somewhere else.

"Why does someone walk past the depot every night at 2 a.m. if they're trying not to exist?"

* * *

**Odile**

Because the dead get to have routines, she almost said. That's the one luxury.

She looked at Isandro — this strange, quiet person who had materialized out of the dispatch office like a rumor, who drove her route under the flimsiest pretext Odile had ever heard ("commission safety audit," delivered in a voice that clearly did not believe itself) — and felt something she hadn't felt in two years: the itch of wanting to be known.

It was a dangerous itch. She scratched it anyway.

"I didn't used to be invisible," Odile said. "I was very visible, actually. Odile Vasseur, wife of Tomas Reyes, hostess of dinner parties, wearer of good coats. My face was on the commission's charity gala program two years running. Do you know what it's like to be looked at all the time? Everyone thinks visibility is a gift. It's a debt. Every person who looks at you believes they're owed something."

"And Tomas collected."

"Tomas collected." She said it flatly, without drama, because the drama had been burned out of those words long ago, the way a wick burns down until only the shape remains. "The first year, it was just his voice getting louder when I said things he didn't like. The second year, it was his hands. The third year, I understood that he would eventually kill me, and that no one would stop him, because half the people who might have stopped him owed him favors. He'd been a commissioner's aide for a decade. He knew everyone. He'd done everyone a kindness they couldn't afford to repay."

The coffee steamed between her hands. Somewhere behind the counter, a radio murmured a weather report for a morning neither of them intended to see.

"So I did the thing nobody does," she went on. "I stopped running *from* him and started running *into* the system. Because the system has one beautiful flaw: it believes paperwork. If the paperwork says you died, then you died. Grief is very tidy. Nobody checks a grave twice."

Isandro's eyes came up sharply. "You arranged your own death certificate."

"A friend helped. Someone inside the commission — I still don't know how deep their help went, or what it cost them, or whether they're the reason someone's now telling me I'm about to die for real." Her laugh was dry as ash. "I walked into the river one night in November. Left my coat on the bank, my shoes on the pilings, my wedding ring in my coat pocket. And Odile Vasseur drowned, and the city held a small sad vigil, and Tomas cried beautifully at the funeral — he really is gifted — and a woman with no name got on a bus with a duffel bag and became whoever the next shift required her to be."

"What did it cost?"

The question landed harder than any accusation could have. Odile stared into her cup.

"Everything you'd expect. My mother thinks I'm dead. She leaves flowers. I've stood across the street from the cemetery twice and watched her do it." Her voice caught, and she let it catch, because hiding it would have been one more erasure. "But that's not the real cost. The real cost is smaller and worse. It's that I don't answer to my name anymore. When someone says 'hey, you,' I turn around, because 'you' is all I am. Two years of being nobody, and now nobody is the only self I have left. I'm free the way an empty house is free."

* * *

**Isandro**

Isandro had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being overlooked, and had told themselves it was a talent. Sitting across from a woman who had paid for her invisibility with her entire life, Isandro suddenly saw the difference between a skill and a wound.

"My turn," Isandro said, and found their voice lower than usual, scraped clean. "Nobody erased me. I erased me. Slowly. On purpose. Which might be worse, honestly, because at least you got a dramatic exit. I just... faded."

Odile said nothing. She had the listening quality of certain people — the ones who never interrupt because they know exactly how rare it is to be heard.

"I started dispatching nights because nights are empty. Empty is safe. On day shift, there are meetings, there are names, there's a supervisor who remembers your kid's birthday. On nights, you're a voice on a frequency. 'Unit twelve, fare at Ninth and Alder.' Nobody asks the frequency what it dreams about." Isandro turned the cup again. "My father was a loud man. Union man, bar fighter, the kind of guy whose silences were louder than other people's shouting. Growing up, I learned that the safest place in any room was the corner where nobody tracked you. I got so good at corners that eventually I lived in one. Twenty years of corners. I have coworkers who couldn't pick me out of a lineup. I have a landlord who calls me 'the tenant.' Last year I had appendicitis and the hospital called my emergency contact and it rang in an empty apartment, because the number belonged to my father, who died six years ago, and I never updated it, because updating it meant admitting there was no one else."

The confession hung in the air between them, smelling of coffee and vinyl.

"That's not invisibility," Odile said quietly. "That's a tomb you built yourself and furnished nicely."

"Yeah." Isandro huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "And here's the stupid part. I liked it. I liked being the person nobody needed. It meant nobody could hurt me. No one to disappoint, no one to lose. I told myself it was freedom." They shook their head. "Then an envelope shows up with cash and your picture and a request to prove a dead woman stays alive, and for the first time in years I wanted something. Wanted it enough to break my own rules. Do you know what that's like? To feel the walls of your tomb and realize you bricked yourself in?"

"Yes," Odile said simply. "That's why I walk past the depot. It's the one place in the city where somebody knows my face and doesn't want anything from it. Ren saves me the bad coffee. Bo waves. Halloran nods." Something flickered across her expression at Halloran's name — a shadow Isandro filed away but didn't press. "It's pathetic, probably. A dead woman collecting scraps of acknowledgment from a taxi depot."

"It's not pathetic."

"No?"

"No. It's the same reason I kept taking your route after the pretext fell apart. Which it did, by the way, about nine days ago. Your kiosk coffee is terrible and you should stop buying it."

Odile laughed — a real one, startled out of her, brief and bright as a struck match. In the window, their reflections leaned together over the table like conspirators, which, Isandro supposed, they were.

* * *

**Odile**

The trucker paid and left. The teenagers left. The waitress refilled them once, wordlessly, a sacrament performed without ceremony, and the diner grew quieter until the silence had texture, like felt.

"You haven't asked the obvious question," Odile said.

"Which is?"

"Whether I killed anyone. Whether the sealed file is sealed because there's a body in it that isn't mine." She met Isandro's eyes straight on, no flinch. "Ask it."

"Did you kill anyone?"

"No. But thank you for asking instead of deciding." She turned her cup a quarter-turn, aligning the handle with the table edge — Isandro noticed she did that, ordered small things when the large ones were chaos. "Here's what keeps me up at 4 a.m., though, since we're confessing. Whoever sent us both those warnings wants a murder to happen on the solstice. Either they're lying to scare me out of hiding — which means my hiding place is already blown — or they're telling the truth, which means someone needs a death on that date specifically. A scheduled murder. Like a fare you book in advance." Her jaw tightened. "And there's only one reason to schedule a death. Alibi. You schedule a murder for a night when you can prove you were somewhere else."

The word landed between them and neither moved.

"The alibi," Isandro said slowly. "Of the title."

"I don't know what title you're talking about, dispatcher, but yes. The alibi." Odile leaned forward. "So here's my proposal, and understand that I have spent two years trusting no one, so this costs me more than you can imagine. We can't watch each other's backs separately. A tail watching a ghost gets you two ghosts and zero witnesses. But two people who see each other — really see each other, on the record, in the light—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "Be my witness. If something happens to me on the solstice, be the one who says: she existed, she was here, she was afraid, and here is who profited. And I'll do the same for you. Whatever you saw, whatever you find in those logs, whatever name comes out of that dispatch office — I'll stand up and say you told me, and I believed you."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the sky was still fully dark, but the dark had gone thin at the edges, the way it does before dawn, before anything.

"You understand what you're asking," Isandro said. "A witness has a name. A witness sits in a courtroom where everyone looks at them. You'd be asking me to stop being invisible, and you'd be putting your own face back in the window."

"I know exactly what I'm asking." Odile extended her hand across the table, palm open — the gesture of someone offering a fare, or a truce, or a life. "Two nobodies, testifying for each other. It's either the worst idea we've ever had or the first honest thing either of us has done in years."

Isandro looked at the hand. Then they reached into their jacket, pulled out the prepaid envelope — creased now, soft at the corners from three weeks of handling — and laid it on the table between them.

"One condition," Isandro said. "Before I shake on it. Tomorrow I'm pulling the old dispatch logs from the week you disappeared. Halloran keeps them locked in the back office, and tomorrow he's off shift. If those logs say what I think they say — if someone inside the depot helped stage your death — then this stops being a ghost story and becomes evidence. And evidence makes enemies."

"Then we'd better be each other's alibis," Odile said, "before we become each other's epitaphs."

Isandro took her hand. Her grip was strong and cold and alive, and holding it felt like stepping out of a doorway Isandro had stood in for twenty years.

Neither of them saw the gray sedan roll past the diner's fogged window at 4:47 a.m., slow, its lights off, pausing just long enough for the driver to note the booth at the back — two heads bent together over a single envelope — before sliding on toward the impound lot and the long dark hours still left before the solstice.
