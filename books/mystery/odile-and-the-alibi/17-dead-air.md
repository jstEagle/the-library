## 17. Dead Air

The call came in at 3:47 a.m., and it was wrong in three ways before Isandro even touched the headset.

First, the channel. Emergencies from the fleet came through Channel One, always had, since the depot's radios were installed the year Isandro started — but this one lit up Channel Four, the dead line, the one nobody monitored because nobody was assigned to it. The little amber light blinked on the board like a firefly trapped behind glass.

Second, the voice. It belonged to Bo, or something wearing Bo's cadence — the mechanic's gravelly rasp, the habit of swallowing his vowels. But Bo was standing twelve feet away under the bay lights with his arms buried to the elbow in a transmission housing, and he looked up when Isandro's eyes went to him, and he shook his head slowly, a man watching his own ghost speak.

Third, the address. 4400 Kessler Yard. The impound lot.

Isandro knew all of this in the space of four seconds, and knew, too, that they should not go. That was the whole point of being invisible for eleven years: you learned to read a room through its silences, and this silence was screaming. A fake voice on a dead channel asking them, by name, to come alone to the place where scrapped cars went to be forgotten.

"Dispatch, do you copy?" the voice said again. "Driver down at the yard. Need a unit now."

Isandro's hand hovered over the transmit key. Under the bay lights, Bo wiped his hands on a rag and mouthed something — *who is that?* — and Ren, at the far desk, had gone very still over her crossword, pencil suspended, the way she'd been going still all week whenever the phones rang.

*Every errand traced back to a terminal in this office.* Isandro had found the pattern at two in the morning, hunched over reconstructed logs, and had felt the cold climb up their spine rung by rung like a ladder. Someone in this room had been listening to the whole city through the same equipment Isandro touched every night. Someone who could make a dead channel speak with a living man's voice.

Which meant the voice on the line right now might be sitting close enough to watch Isandro's face while it talked.

Isandro keyed the mic. "Copy, 4400 Kessler. Unit Twelve responding." Then, quietly, without looking up, they did the thing they'd practiced a hundred times as a joke and never once in earnest: they pressed the GPS panic tag on their belt clip twice, fast, the sequence Odile had insisted they both memorize over diner coffee three weeks ago. *If it ever goes bad,* she'd said, stirring her coffee with a fork because the spoons were dirty, *I want to know where your body is.* It wasn't a joke anymore. Somewhere across town, if she was watching her phone — and she was always watching her phone — a pin had just dropped on a map.

Isandro took the keys off the hook for Unit Twelve, an old Crown Victoria with a heater that rattled like dice in a cup, and walked out into the cold.

* * *

The city between the depot and the river was at its most honest hour — the hour when the bars had emptied and the bakeries hadn't woken, when the streets belonged to street sweepers and stray dogs and the particular loneliness of sodium lights. Isandro drove with the windows cracked despite the December air because the rattle of the heater made it hard to think, and thinking was the only weapon they were carrying tonight.

They thought about Halloran.

Supervisor Halloran, who kept a photo of his late wife taped inside his locker door. Who bought the night crew donuts on the first Friday of every month and remembered that Ren hated the glazed ones. Who had stood in front of the shredder last week with a garbage bag full of dispatch logs and told Isandro, with wet honest eyes, *I'm protecting these kids, not myself.* Isandro had wanted to believe him so badly that believing had felt like a physical act, like lifting something heavy.

But the logs didn't lie. Isandro had spent eleven years learning to read the city's paperwork the way other people read faces, and the logs said that every burner-phone errand Ren had been blackmailed into running — the plate checks, the impound releases, the "deliveries" to the commission annex — had been authorized, logged, and erased from a terminal whose access code belonged to exactly one person still employed at the depot.

And the sealed file. Marisol Quintero had said it herself, that night in her kitchen that smelled of cardamom and old cigarettes: *Sealed from above. Not the department. Above the department.* And who had come up through the commission's administrative wing before taking the supervisor's desk? Who still played golf twice a month with men who signed warrants?

Halloran. It had been Halloran for days, maybe weeks, and Isandro had let the donuts and the locker photo stand in the way of seeing it, because wanting a family meant flinching away from evidence against it.

The impound lot rose out of the dark ahead — a chain-link acre of dead cars under a single floodlight tower, fenced on three sides by the river's brown fog. The gate stood open. That was wrong too; the gate was supposed to be chained at midnight, and Isandro knew because they'd read the commission's own operating manual cover to cover during a slow shift in March, back when the strangest thing in their life was an envelope full of cash.

Unit Twelve crunched onto the gravel. Isandro parked where the light could see them — old instinct, though instinct seemed suddenly quaint — and sat for a moment with both hands on the wheel, listening to the engine tick.

On the passenger seat, their phone glowed once. A message from Odile, sent ninety seconds ago:

**Pin received. 6 min out. Do NOT get out of the car.**

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. Six minutes was either a lifetime or no time at all, depending entirely on what happened next.

They got out of the car.

* * *

"I told you to stay in the vehicle."

The voice came from between the rows of dead cars, and it was Halloran's voice, and it sounded tired in a way Isandro had never heard it — tired the way a man sounds when he has been carrying something heavy for years and has finally decided to set it down, consequences be damned.

Halloran stepped into the edge of the floodlight. He wasn't wearing his coat. In this cold, that detail frightened Isandro more than anything else — a man who had stopped caring about small comforts was a man past negotiation. He held something down at his side, and the light caught it once: matte black, compact, the kind of gun that existed to be practical rather than impressive.

"The call," Isandro said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "That was you. On Channel Four. You can spoof the fleet IDs."

"I've been spoofing fleet IDs since before you grew a beard, kid." Halloran moved a step closer, angling himself so the cars at his back stayed in shadow. Old habit. Even now, even here, the man positioned himself like someone who had spent decades making sure only half his face was ever in the light. "Sit down on that bumper. Hands where I can see them."

Isandro sat. The bumper of a totaled sedan was ice-cold through their coat. "Bo's fine," they said. Not a question. "You used his voice."

"Forty hours a week for six years, you learn how a man talks." Halloran's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You'd be amazed what this office taught me. Every fare in this city, every route, every driver who takes cash under the table and every commissioner who takes worse than cash. You people think dispatchers are furniture." He shook his head. "Furniture hears everything."

"Why the fake emergency?" Isandro asked. "Why not just—" They stopped. Because there was no polite way to say *why not just kill me at the depot*, and saying it aloud would make it true.

"Because I needed you somewhere with no cameras and no witnesses," Halloran said simply. "Same reason I needed everything else somewhere with no cameras and no witnesses. You want to know how a woman dies and doesn't die? You want to know how a case gets sealed and a witness retires to a lake house he couldn't afford? It's all the same trick, Isandro. You do it somewhere nobody looks." He gestured at the lot with the gun, a slow sweep that took in the rusted hulks, the fog, the dark water beyond the fence. "This city runs on places nobody looks. I just learned to work there. Same as you."

"You staged it." Isandro's breath hung white in the air. "Odile's death. Two years ago."

For a long moment Halloran said nothing. The floodlight buzzed overhead. Somewhere out on the river, a barge horn moaned, low and mournful, like the city clearing its throat.

"She came to me," he said finally. "Did she tell you that? Your ghost. She came to me first. Crying in my office at four in the morning, saying Tomas was going to kill her, saying she'd gone to the police and the police had gone to *him* — because his brother-in-law wore a badge, and everybody in that building owed somebody." His jaw worked. "She said, 'You move people around this city unseen. Move me.' And I thought about my wife's medical bills, and I thought about what a certain board member would pay to have a problem become a corpse on paper, and I said yes." He laughed, short and awful. "Two hundred thousand dollars to erase a human being. Cheaper than a funeral, if you count what the funeral costs the person being buried."

"So the certificate—"

"A body was needed. We found one. Unclaimed, county morgue, wrong age by ten years but the file photos were grainy and nobody wanted to look closely. Nobody ever wants to look closely." Halloran's eyes came up and fixed on Isandro, and in them was something worse than menace — it was appeal. He wanted, absurdly, horribly, to be understood. "I did it to save her life. You understand that, don't you? She's alive because of me. Whatever else happened — the money, the file, Quintero's partner and his little embezzlement trail we papered over — she got to live. I gave a drowning woman a new name and a quiet job where nobody looks at your face. Sound familiar?"

It did. It sounded like a mirror held up at a bad angle, and Isandro hated how much of their own reflection they could see in it.

"And now she's resurfaced," Isandro said quietly. "Now she's riding cabs and drinking diner coffee and existing where people can see her. And that's a loose end."

"Now she's a *liability*." The word cracked out of him. "The board member — you don't know what he's capable of, what he's already — there are people above me, kid, and when they say tie it off, you tie it off, or your whole crew starts having accidents. Ren first. She's already in deep enough to drown." He raised the gun, not aiming it, just holding it in the space between them like a fact. "I'm sorry. I mean that. Of everyone who ever sat at those desks, you're the one I'd have picked to leave out of it. But you reconstructed my logs, and you rode along on her route, and you made yourself visible, and visible things get cleaned up. That's the whole job. That's been the whole job for two years."

The floodlight flickered. In the stutter of darkness, Isandro's mind ran the arithmetic it had been running since the bumper: six minutes minus four was two minutes, and two minutes was a very small number, and Halloran was eight feet away with a gun and a confession he clearly intended to survive.

"Halloran," Isandro said, and used his first name, the one stitched inside his cap that nobody ever said aloud — *Desmond* — and saw him flinch as if struck. "You said you'd have picked me to leave out of it. So leave me out of it. Drive me back to the depot, shred whatever's left, and disappear. You know every unmonitored road in this city better than anyone alive. You could be a ghost by sunrise. Isn't that what you taught all of us? How to not be seen?"

Something moved behind Halloran's eyes — temptation, real and terrible, the longing of a tired man for an exit — and Isandro watched it happen and thought, *please, please take it,* not for their own sake but because some stubborn part of them still wanted the donut-buying, wife-photo-keeping man to be real.

"That door closed," Halloran said softly, "the night you called me Desmond."

And then headlights swept the fence line.

A car was coming up the access road — slow, unhurried, its high beams cutting the fog into pillars. Gray. Isandro knew the shape of it before the color registered, knew it the way you know a recurring nightmare: the gray sedan, the phantom plate, the car that had been scrapped on paper a year ago and yet kept driving through their nights like a debt that refused to die.

Halloran turned toward it, and his shoulders dropped half an inch, and Isandro understood with a lurching, freezing clarity that this was relief on his face. He hadn't come alone after all. Or rather — he had come alone, and his owners had sent a chaperone.

"Told you," Halloran murmured. "People above me."

The sedan rolled through the open gate and stopped twenty yards out, engine idling, headlights blazing straight down the row of dead cars so that everything between it and Isandro was washed in glare and shadow. The driver's door opened. A silhouette unfolded from it, tall, unhurried, buttoning a coat against the cold, and began to walk toward them through the fog with the easy gait of a man arriving at his own property.

Isandro's phone, forgotten in their pocket, buzzed once against their thigh.

Then twice.

Then it started ringing — and on the floodlit gravel, in the dead air of the impound lot, with a gun fifteen feet away and a stranger closing the distance, the ringtone sang out bright and clear into the night: Odile's tone, the one she'd assigned herself, a tinny loop of an old jazz standard, playing to the empty lots of 4400 Kessler like a dinner bell.

Halloran's head snapped toward the sound.

The tall figure in the headlights stopped walking.

And from the darkness beyond the fence line — from the direction of the river, the direction of the six minutes that had just run out — came the crunch of a second set of footsteps on gravel, moving fast.
