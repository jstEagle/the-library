## 20. Testimony

**Isandro**

The subpoena arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the slowest nights at the depot, the nights when the city seemed to hold its breath between one week and the next, and Isandro had always preferred them for exactly that reason — nothing happened on Tuesdays, and nothing happening was the safest weather there was.

The envelope was cream-colored, official, heavier than paper had any right to be. *In re: The People v. Halloran, Marcus E., et al.* And beneath that, in type that did not care how Isandro felt about it: *You are hereby commanded to appear and give testimony.*

Isandro sat with it in the dispatch office for a long time, the headset warm against one ear, the other ear full of the ordinary music of the night — Bo's wrench clattering in the bay, a fare dispute crackling through channel three, the radiator ticking like something counting down. Two months ago, an envelope like this one had contained cash and a photograph and a question: *will she live?* Now this envelope contained only a date, a room number, and Isandro's own name printed in bold where anyone could read it.

*Isandro Dravenmoor.* It looked strange in capital letters. Like a stranger wearing their coat.

"You going to open that, or are we having a funeral?" Ren stood in the doorway with two coffees, and there was something new in Ren's face these days — a steadiness that hadn't been there before the blackmail came out, before the burner phone went into an evidence bag, before Ren spent four hours in an interview room telling a detective everything. Guilt had burned off Ren like morning fog. What remained was simpler and harder to look away from.

"It's a subpoena," Isandro said. "I'm testifying."

Ren set the coffees down. For a moment neither of them touched them. "Public testimony? Open court, cameras, the whole circus?"

"The commission cover-up made the front page. There's going to be a hearing." Isandro turned the paper over, as if the back might offer an escape hatch. "State oversight. They want me to describe how the dispatch terminal was used. How Halloran ran his errands through our own system. How nobody noticed."

"Nobody noticed because he was good," Ren said quietly. "And because noticing is not really in the job description."

That was the truest thing anyone had said all week. Isandro had built an entire life on the principle that the person behind the headset was furniture — a voice, a schedule, a name nobody retained. Dispatchers were the city's subconscious. You called, you were driven, you forgot. It had been a hiding place once. It had been a home.

Now a state attorney wanted Isandro Dravenmoor to stand in a bright room and say things into a microphone while people wrote the name down.

"I keep thinking about the first envelope," Isandro said. "The prepaid fare. Whoever sent it knew I'd take the job. Knew I was the kind of person who'd rather do the strange right thing than the easy wrong one." A pause. "I never found out who it was."

"Quintero's partner," Ren said. "Vasquez. He set the whole thing moving so Halloran would overreach." Ren shrugged when Isandro looked up. "The detectives figured it out from his phone records. He needed someone outside the commission to start pulling threads. Someone with no ties he could burn."

So the impossible request had been bait, and Isandro had been the hook, and the fish — Halloran, Vasquez, the whole rotting structure of the taxi commission — had been landed. It should have made Isandro feel used. Instead, sitting there in the amber light of the depot with coffee going cold, Isandro felt something closer to gratitude, which was its own kind of vertigo.

"When is it?" Ren asked.

"Thursday. Ten a.m."

"In daylight," Ren said, and almost smiled. "You're going to hate that."

**Odile**

The clerk at the Bureau of Vital Records had a voice like wet cardboard and a stamp she wielded like a gavel. Odile stood at her counter with a folder of documents two inches thick — affidavits, fingerprints, the detective's letter, a notarized statement from Marisol Quintero that read like a confession of everyone else's sins — and waited to be told, once again, that resurrection was not among the services offered.

"Petition to void a certificate of death," the clerk repeated, reading the top page as though translating from a dead language. "These take months."

"I've been dead for two years," Odile said. "I'm prepared to wait. But I'd prefer not to."

Something flickered across the cardboard voice — not quite sympathy, but its distant cousin. The clerk flipped through the folder, slowed at Quintero's letter, slowed further at the newspaper clipping Odile had included almost out of spite: *TAXI COMMISSION SCANDAL DEEPENS; WOMAN DECLARED DEAD FOUND ALIVE, KEY WITNESS.*

"This is you?" the clerk asked, tapping the photo.

"That's me. Or it was. We're negotiating."

The stamp came down — not approval, but receipt, which was its own small miracle. *RECEIVED*, in violet ink, dated, real. Odile walked out of the bureau into a morning so aggressively ordinary it felt staged: pigeons, a fruit cart, a man arguing cheerfully into his phone about a dry-cleaning order. Two years ago she had walked out of a life through a door that closed behind her like water. Now there was a paper trail leading back toward it, one violet stamp at a time.

She should have felt triumphant. What she actually felt, riding the bus back toward the depot with the folder on her knees, was a grief so specific it startled her.

Because the woman in the clipping — the composed witness, the survivor with the tidy quote — was not the woman Odile had spent two years becoming. The ghost had been free. The ghost could stand at a bus stop at 3 a.m. and know that no one looking at her was looking for anything. The ghost had no debts, no history, no face that Tomas Reyes might one day see across a courtroom and remember loving. Getting her name back meant getting everything back: the fear, the file, the version of herself that had flinched at footsteps. She had petitioned the state to make her visible again, and visibility, she was learning, was not a gift. It was a bill.

At the depot, the night crew had decorated.

It was not yet the party — that was promised for after the hearing, after the certificate, after whatever came next — but someone (Bo, obviously; the man owned eleven rolls of tape and no shame) had strung a banner over the mechanic's bay that read WELCOME BACK FROM THE DEAD, ODDIE, and beneath it someone else (Ren; the handwriting gave it away) had added in smaller letters: *again.*

Odile stood under it a long moment.

"You're supposed to laugh," Bo said, emerging from under a cab with grease on his forearms and anxiety on his face. "It's a joke banner. Mostly."

"It's terrible," Odile said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she let it. "It's the most terrible thing anyone has ever made for me. I'm going to cry about it later, privately, like a professional."

Bo nodded solemnly, as if this were standard maintenance procedure, and handed her a rag to wipe her hands on even though her hands were clean. That was the thing about the night crew, Odile thought. They understood that some gifts were just permission slips — permission to be seen, to be fussed over, to exist loudly in a room full of people who had decided your existence was worth celebrating. She had spent two years perfecting the art of being nobody's business. These people kept making themselves hers.

"Isandro's testifying Thursday," Bo said, quieter now. "You knew that?"

"I'm testifying too. Different room, same circus." She folded the rag with unnecessary precision. "They want me to walk through the whole thing. The staging, the death certificate, what Halloran admitted at the impound lot. Under oath." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the old survival register, and deliberately unclenched it. "Under oath, in my own name. First time in two years anyone official will hear it."

"Scared?"

"Terrified." She smiled, and found, to her surprise, that the smile was real. "But it's a different terror than before. Before, I was afraid of being seen by the wrong people. Now I'm going to be seen by everyone, and the wrong people will be in handcuffs." She looked up at the banner, at the crooked hand-lettered *again*. "Turns out that's the whole trick, Bo. You can't be erased if you insist on the record."

**Isandro**

The hearing room had the acoustics of a church designed by accountants — high ceilings that swallowed sound and returned it flattened, fluorescent light with no mercy in it, rows of chairs filled with reporters, clerks, and the gray-faced functionaries of the taxi commission who had spent decades believing that paperwork was a form of armor.

Isandro wore the one good jacket and felt like a costume. At the witness table, hands folded on the oak, they swore an oath — *do you solemnly swear* — and heard their own voice say *I do* in a room where forty people were listening, and understood in that instant why they had spent ten years behind a headset. Anonymity was not shyness. It was a load-bearing wall. Remove it and you found out what the building was made of.

The attorney questioning them was a compact woman named Okafor with reading glasses she used like weapons. "Mr. Dravenmoor. Ms. Dravenmoor—" she glanced at the file, adjusted without comment, and Isandro felt something loosen slightly in their chest at the adjustment itself, at being handled correctly by a stranger. "Walk us through the terminal. In plain terms. How does a dispatcher's station become a surveillance tool?"

"Every cab reports in," Isandro said. Their voice sounded enormous in the room and very far away, both at once. "Location, fares, routes. The dispatcher sees all of it, and the drivers hear the dispatcher. If you control the headset, you control what the whole fleet knows and when. Halloran had a second login. Unauthorized. He used it to run the gray sedan's errands — moving it, parking it, keeping it near the impound lot where it couldn't be traced to him. Every time Ren got a call from the burner, the instructions matched what that terminal had already seen. He wasn't guessing where people would be. He was watching the map."

"And no one at the company detected this?"

"Our logs rotate every ninety days. His access was buried in routine traffic." Isandro paused. "No one detected it because no one was looking, and no one was looking because the commission taught us that looking was insubordination. When drivers reported the sedan, they were told it was a fleet vehicle. When I asked about it, I was told to drop it. The invisibility wasn't an accident of the system. It was the product."

A murmur moved through the benches. Somewhere a camera shutter clicked, and Isandro did not flinch, and was astonished at themselves for not flinching.

Then Okafor asked the question Isandro had rehearsed all week and still wasn't ready for.

"Why did you come forward? By your own account, you valued being unnoticed. Testifying is the opposite of unnoticed. Help the panel understand."

The room waited. Isandro looked at their hands on the oak, at the small tremor in the left one that no amount of rehearsal had cured, and told the truth because there was nothing left that was more efficient.

"For years I thought being unseen kept me safe. Then a stranger paid me to make sure a woman stayed alive, and I learned that being unseen doesn't keep you safe. It keeps you *available*. To people like Halloran. To systems like this one." They lifted their head. "He listened to this city through a headset like mine for six years, and none of us saw him, because we had all agreed — drivers, dispatchers, commissioners — that the people behind the radios weren't really there. I'm here to un-agree. Publicly. On the record. That's the whole of my testimony, and it took me thirty years to be able to say it."

Silence, then the scratch of stenography, then Okafor's small nod — the nod of a person filing something away that mattered.

Cross-examination was briefer and uglier. Halloran's attorney, a silver-haired man with the patience of a fisherman, tried to establish that Isandro had acted outside protocol, taken personal interest in a client, assigned themselves to a route without authorization. Isandro answered each question plainly, refusing the bait of apology. Yes. All true. And if those small transgressions had not occurred, Marcus Halloran would today be planning a murder for the winter solstice instead of awaiting sentencing for conspiring to commit one. The attorney sat down. The panel made notes. Outside the tall windows, the city went on honking, oblivious, alive.

It was only afterward, in the marble corridor with its echo of a hundred institutional footsteps, that Isandro saw Odile waiting by the fountain — dressed in dark colors, folder under her arm, face set in the expression of a soldier reporting for a duty she'd volunteered for and resented anyway.

"You looked steady in there," she said.

"I was performing steady. It's a skill. You learn it driving night shift — sound calm no matter what's screaming through the radio." Isandro studied her. "Your turn next week?"

"My turn tomorrow." She said it lightly, but her thumb was pressing a crease into the folder hard enough to whiten the skin. "They're going to ask me to describe the night I 'died.' Every detail. In front of the Reyes family lawyers, in front of press, in front of—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "In front of Tomas. He's listed as an interested party. He gets to sit there and watch me explain why I let him bury an empty box."

"Are you sorry?" Isandro asked, because it was the question underneath all the others, and they had learned — from her, actually, at 4 a.m. in a diner booth — that the honest question was a kindness even when it cut.

Odile was quiet long enough that the fountain's trickle became loud. "I'm sorry for the shape of it," she said finally. "Not for the fact. He mourned a woman who didn't die, and that's cruel, and I chose it, and I'd choose it again, because the alternative was a shallow grave with my actual name on the paperwork. But sorry-for-the-shape-of-it is still sorry." She exhaled. "Two years ago I testified to nothing. I signed nothing. I was a rumor. Tomorrow I raise my hand and swear to my own biography in a room full of strangers, and if my hands shake, they shake on the record." She looked up, and there it was — the fear, fully present, fully acknowledged, and standing next to it something harder that had grown around the fear like bone around a break. "You know what the worst part is? Part of me is *looking forward to it*. Who does that?"

"Somebody coming back to life," Isandro said. "It's supposed to hurt. I've read about it."

Odile laughed — a short, surprised sound that bounced off the marble and came back sounding like it belonged there.

**Odile**

The hearing room for her petition was smaller than Isandro's, meaner, with a judge who had the face of a man perpetually disappointed by lunch. But the mechanics were the same: raise your right hand, state your name.

"State your name for the record," the bailiff said.

And here it was — the hinge, the door, the moment she had rehearsed in shower stalls and stairwells and the mirror of a diner ladies' room until the words lost their teeth. Two years of silence balanced on five syllables.

"Odile Marie Vasseur."

Her voice did not shake. She had expected it to, had budgeted for the shaking, and its absence nearly undid her more than its presence would have. The stenographer typed it. The record held it. Somewhere in the machinery of the state, the name existed again in ink, attached to a living, breathing, testifying body.

The questions came for three hours. Where were you on the night of March fourteenth. Why did you not report the threats to police. Did you understand that staging your death involved fraud upon the county registrar. Did you understand that your husband identified a body. (*Identified clothing,* she corrected, and the correction went into the record like a splinter.) Did you understand that Mr. Reyes inherited your estate.

"Yes," she said to that one, steadily. "That was the point. A dead wife with assets was safer than a live wife with bruises. I've had time to consider the arithmetic. I don't recommend it, but I don't retract it either."

Through all of it, Tomas sat where the bailiff had placed him, third row, hands clasped, and he cried — quietly, beautifully, the way he did everything, grief worn like good tailoring. Once, when she described the night she walked out with a duffel bag and a forged death arranged by a man she'd never met, his composure broke and he said, "You could have told me," and the judge struck it, and Odile met his eyes and did not strike it from herself.

"I know," she said, off the record, into the space between them. "That's the shape of it. I'm sorry for the shape of it."

He looked at her for a long moment — the widower and the widow, both of them mourning the same marriage from opposite sides of its grave — and then he nodded, once, and looked down, and let her go.

The ruling would take weeks. The judge said so in the tone of a man closing a ledger. But as Odile gathered her folder, he added, unprompted, dry as dust: "For what it's worth, Ms. Vasseur — yours is the most straightforward fraud I've seen in twenty years on this bench. Everything else about it is complicated. The motive isn't." He shuffled his papers. "Stay alive. It simplifies things."

She walked out of the courthouse into late afternoon light, and Isandro was on the steps — of course they were, holding two coffees like talismans, having clearly abandoned a shift or traded for it or simply refused to be anywhere else — and Odile descended toward them feeling lighter than she had any legal right to feel, given that nothing was settled, nothing was stamped, nothing was safe.

"Well?" Isandro said.

"I said my name out loud." She took the coffee. "To a judge. With a court reporter."

"How'd it feel?"

"Like pulling a splinter out." She considered. "It bleeds a little. But you forget it's there until it's gone, and then you can't believe you carried it that long."

They stood on the courthouse steps while the city streamed past — cabs mostly, a river of them, yellow and anonymous and carrying hundreds of invisible lives behind their windshields — and Odile realized she was no longer afraid of the river. She had been a stone in its current for two years. Now she was a person on the steps, drinking bad coffee beside the only other person who knew what the current cost, and the certificate was still technically valid, and the sentencing was still weeks out, and Halloran's unnamed accomplices were still being peeled off the commission board one indictment at a time.

Her phone buzzed. A number she didn't recognize, and a text:

*Ms. Vasseur — Marisol Quintero gave me your contact. I'm the clerk from upstate. I've been thinking about what I didn't say in my statement. There's a name I left out. I think you should hear it before the sentencing hearing. Can I call tomorrow?*

Odile stared at it. Then she turned the screen toward Isandro, whose face did the thing it always did — went very still, very quiet, the dispatcher's face, the face of someone listening to a frequency no one else could hear yet.

"There's always another envelope," Isandro murmured.

"There's always another name," Odile said, and pocketed the phone, and together they watched the cabs flow past like a city exhaling.
