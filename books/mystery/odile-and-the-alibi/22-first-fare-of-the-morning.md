## 22. First Fare of the Morning

The morning light came through the dispatch office windows the way it never had before — honestly, without apology, falling in long gold bars across a desk that used to belong to no one and now belonged to Isandro Dravenmoor.

Isandro had taken the day shift on purpose. That was the part that still felt like wearing someone else's coat. For eleven years, the day shift had been the enemy territory of people with names and faces and lunch breaks at actual restaurants — Halloran's kingdom, once, back when Halloran was only a supervisor and not a headline. Now the desk was Isandro's, the headset was Isandro's, and when drivers came in off their routes they said *morning, Dravenmoor* like it was the most natural thing in the world, like the name had always hung there in the fluorescent hum of the depot.

It had been seven months since the impound lot. Seven months since the open channel, since Halloran's voice cracking across every cab in the fleet, since the gray sedan rolled to a stop behind two patrol cars that Odile Vasseur — dead woman, living woman, witness — had summoned with her own name spoken aloud into a 911 line for the first time in two years.

Seven months, and Isandro still sometimes reached for the dimmer switch on their own personality, the old habit of turning themselves down until they were background noise. But the switch wasn't there anymore. The trial had burned it out. You cannot testify for three days in a courtroom full of reporters and go back to being furniture; something in you reorganizes around being seen, the way a plant reorganizes around a window.

Isandro had learned to like the light. Mostly.

"Dravenmoor." Bo leaned in the doorway, grease to the elbows, holding a clipboard he never actually wrote on. "Unit twelve's got a rattle in the rear axle. I want it off the board till tomorrow."

"Take it off," Isandro said. "And Bo — the coffee machine's doing the thing again."

"The thing where it sounds like it's dying?"

"The thing where it *is* dying."

"I'll commune with it." Bo tapped his temple, shuffled out. Through the window Isandro could see the yard, cabs gleaming with morning frost-melt, and beyond the fence the corner where the impound lot used to be. The city had torn the fence down in the spring. There was talk of a farmers' market. The world kept insisting on continuing, which was its most disorienting quality.

Ren came in at nine, as Ren always did now, punctual as penance. They'd kept their job — the commission's new leadership had decided, with the particular mercy of institutions covering themselves, that a blackmailed dispatcher who helped lock down a fleeing suspect deserved a second chance. Ren wore the second chance carefully, like a borrowed suit. They nodded to Isandro, hung up their jacket, sat down at the night desk they'd traded for, and said, "Quiet one coming?"

"Too quiet," Isandro said. "I don't trust it."

"You've been saying that for seven months."

"And I've been right twice."

Ren smiled — a real one, small but real — and put on their headset. That was the thing about the found family: it had survived. It had a dent in it, a Halloran-shaped absence nobody talked about directly, but it held. Bo fixed things. Ren answered calls. Marisol Quintero came by on Thursdays with pastries and retired-detective gossip, complaining loudly about her former partner's sentencing hearing the way other people complained about weather. And Odile —

Odile came and went like weather herself, warm fronts and cold fronts, present and gone, except now everyone knew her name when she walked in.

* * *

At 11:40, the envelope arrived.

Isandro saw the courier first — a kid on a bicycle, no logo, no receipt required — and felt the old vertigo rise like water in a locked room. Because Isandro knew envelopes. Knew the weight of them, the way cash shifted inside, the specific silence of a request that doesn't want to be traced. The last one had arrived on a slow Tuesday shift, fourteen months ago, containing four hundred dollars, a photograph of a woman named Odile Vasseur, and an instruction that had rearranged two lives: *confirm she is alive through the solstice.*

This envelope was thinner. Cream-colored. No return address, of course. Of course.

Isandro set it on the desk and looked at it for a long moment while the radio murmured fare requests and Ren laughed at something a driver said about a parrot left in a back seat. Then Isandro opened it.

Inside: a single sheet, typed, and a smaller slip of paper folded around what felt like a key.

The typed sheet read:

*Ms. Dravenmoor —*

*(They'd gotten the honorific wrong. Or maybe not wrong. Maybe the sender simply didn't know, and hadn't cared to find out, and there was something almost restful in that — being misnamed by someone who meant no harm by it.)*

*I am told you are the person to ask when a thing is impossible. I have an impossible thing. Someone is going to disappear on the twenty-first of next month, and I would like very much for them not to. I can pay. I cannot explain everything yet. Please call the number below after dark.*

*— A friend of a friend of Ms. Vasseur*

Isandro read it three times. The third time, the smile arrived — unbidden, crooked, entirely unfamiliar to the person Isandro had been two years ago. Because this was absurd. This was impossible. This was exactly the kind of request that should have made Isandro's stomach drop through the floor of the world, and instead it landed somewhere near the heart and glowed there like a coal.

Someone out there had heard the story — the trial had been public, the commission scandal had been front-page for a season — and had thought: *the taxi dispatcher. The one who caught him with the fleet lock and the open channel. Ask them.* Word traveled. In this city, word traveled through cabs the way blood travels through a body, quietly and everywhere, and somehow Isandro Dravenmoor had become a node in that circulation. Not invisible. *Findable.* There were worse fates. Two years ago Isandro would have said there were no worse fates, and that was precisely how much had changed.

Isandro unfolded the small slip. Inside was not a key but a photograph — old, creased, the kind of photo that has lived in a wallet through bad years. A man, maybe sixty, standing in front of a diner with his arm around a younger person whose face had been worn nearly blank by light damage. On the back, in ballpoint: *Marco. Last seen March. He drives nights.*

A missing driver. Another ghost in a city that manufactured them industrially.

Isandro slid the photograph into their shirt pocket, over the heart, where important things went now, and picked up the phone.

* * *

Odile answered on the second ring, which meant she was home, which meant she was probably at her kitchen table with the case files spread out like tarot cards. She'd taken up the work officially in the spring — *Vasseur Investigations*, a license, a rented office above a laundromat with a window that faced east so she could watch mornings arrive, those rare commodities she'd missed for two years. Half her clients came from the night crew's gossip network. The other half came from strangers who'd read the trial transcripts and believed, correctly, that she knew more about disappearing than anyone alive.

"It's me," Isandro said, though the caller ID said it too, because they'd exchanged real numbers months ago, unburnered, unhidden, a small ceremony neither of them had remarked upon at the time and both of them remembered constantly.

"Let me guess," Odile said. "You're calling because it's noon and you miss my voice."

"That's true, but also—" Isandro held the envelope up even though she couldn't see it, an old gesture from the days when they communicated mostly through parked cabs and thermoses. "Another one came."

Silence on the line. Then, quieter: "Another one."

"Cash this time? No. No cash. Just a photograph and a promise to pay. A missing cab driver named Marco, last seen in March. And a signature that says 'a friend of a friend of Ms. Vasseur.'"

A sound came down the line that might have been a laugh or might have been a sigh; with Odile the two had always lived close together, sharing a border like nervous countries. "We're famous," she said. "We're a *referral.*"

"We're a problem for somebody," Isandro said. "Somebody's friend is going to disappear on the twenty-first, and whoever sent this knows enough to be scared and not enough to know why. Which means somewhere out there is a person who knows exactly why, and they think no one's watching."

"And they're wrong," Odile said.

"They're wrong," Isandro agreed.

There was a pause, the comfortable kind, the kind that had taken months to learn how to hold. Through the phone Isandro could hear the east-facing window doing its work — traffic below, a kettle starting somewhere, the ordinary music of a life being lived in daylight by a woman who had once scheduled her entire existence around not being perceived at 2 a.m.

"You know what I was thinking about this morning?" Odile said. "The diner. Four a.m., that terrible coffee, when we agreed to be each other's witnesses." She said it lightly, but Isandro heard the weight under it, the same weight Isandro carried everywhere now: *alibi* had stopped being a word about murder and become a word about company. To alibi someone was to say *this person was with me; this person exists; I will swear to it.* They had sworn. They were swearing still, daily, in the boring heroic grammar of showing up.

"I was thinking about it too," Isandro said.

"Were you thinking about how the coffee tasted like radiator water?"

"I was thinking about how it was the best cup I ever had."

Odile laughed outright this time, and Isandro grinned at the empty office, at the gold bars of light, at the nameplate on the desk — DRAVENMOOR, in block letters, ordered special, slightly too large, because if you were going to exist you might as well exist at a readable font size.

"So," Odile said. "The twenty-first. Missing driver. Untraceable client. A photograph of a man who drives nights and stopped coming home." Her voice had shifted registers — Isandro could hear her settling into it, the strategist taking the chair the frightened woman used to occupy. "You realize this is either nothing or everything."

"It's always both," Isandro said. "That's the job now."

"That's the job now," she agreed. "Pick me up at six. We'll start with Marco's last route. Somebody saw something — somebody always sees something, they just don't know they did. That's what we're for."

"What are we for?" Isandro asked, and meant it as a real question, a solstice-eve kind of question, the kind you ask when the light is finally returning and you want to hear the answer said aloud.

Odile didn't hesitate. "For finding the ones nobody's looking for," she said. "And for being found ourselves, when it comes to that. Six o'clock, Dravenmoor. Bring the thermos."

Isandro hung up, folded the cream-colored letter closed, and slipped it into the drawer — the drawer where the first envelope lived now, flattened under a paperweight, a relic of the person Isandro used to be. The new photograph stayed in the shirt pocket, over the heart, warming there.

Outside, the afternoon came on bright and unafraid. The radio crackled: unit nine requesting a fare to the airport, unit four with a lost umbrella, the city unspooling its endless small emergencies into Isandro's headset, and Isandro took them all, named and visible and known, a node in the circulation, a keeper of other people's comings and goings.

At six, Isandro would pick up Odile. Tonight they would chase a ghost named Marco through the city's blind spots — and if the trail bent toward another sealed file, another hidden hand, another solstice deadline, then so be it. They had done impossible things before, two professional nobodies with a thermos and a fleet of witnesses on wheels.

Isandro checked the clock, straightened the nameplate, and smiled at the door, waiting for evening the way other people waited for nothing at all.
