## 17. Pell's Silence

Edmund Pell had practiced law for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one lesson above all others: the truth is like a cat. It does not come when called. It sits where it pleases, watches you with an expression of mild contempt, and only comes to you when it has decided—entirely on its own terms—that you are worth the trouble.

He was thinking about cats, or rather avoiding thinking about anything at all, when Kestrel Amberdale walked into his office on a Tuesday morning in November, smelling faintly of salt and old wood smoke, and sat down across from him without being asked.

"I'd like to talk about the codicil," Kestrel said.

Pell's fountain pen, which had been hovering over a perfectly innocent letter to the water authority, stopped moving. He set it down with the deliberate care of a man who had once watched a colleague knock an inkwell into a client's lap and never live it down.

"I'm afraid I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Yes, you do," said Kestrel. "But I appreciate that we're going to do this properly. May I?" They nodded at the tea things on the sideboard—the dented brown pot, the two cups, the milk jug with the chipped spout that Pell had been meaning to replace since the Thatcher administration.

"Help yourself," said Pell, because he was, whatever else might be said of him, a civilized man. And because, if he was honest—and honesty was the thing he had been circling for weeks now like a dog around a bone it wasn't supposed to have—a part of him had been waiting for this visit since the funeral. Since before the funeral, if he were truthful, which was apparently the order of business today. Since the afternoon Odile Amberdale had sat in that very chair, thin as a heron and twice as sharp-eyed, and said: *Edmund, I want to leave everything to my child, and I want you to help me do it without telling anyone until I'm dead.*

*My child.* Not *my nephew* or *my niece*. She had said *child*, and she had looked at him over her reading glasses as though daring him to ask.

He hadn't asked. That was the first silence. There had been so many since.

Kestrel poured the tea—two cups, unasked, which Pell noted with a small internal alarm; they had already learned his habits, this concierge, this professional noticer of people—and settled back with their own cup balanced on one knee.

"You helped her write it," Kestrel said. "The codicil. The condition about finding the truth. And the alias—A. Winter. You set up the accounts."

Pell looked out the window. His office overlooked the high street, and below, Mrs. Tredwell was arguing with the fishmonger about the price of mackerel in a voice that carried beautifully through single glazing. He envied her, briefly and profoundly. Mrs. Tredwell had no secrets larger than mackerel.

"Before I say anything," Pell said, "I should explain something about my position. You'll understand it's delicate."

"I imagine it is."

"I represented Odile for forty years. I also represented her brother Gerald—that would be your grandfather, though I suspect nobody ever told you that either—and after him, Marcus and Vivian. One lawyer for a family is a convenience. It is also, I see now, a structural flaw." He laced his fingers together on the desk. "Everything I know, I know on behalf of everyone. Which means everything I know belongs, in some sense, to everyone. Including the things that would ruin them."

"And including the things that sent me to trial," Kestrel said quietly.

Pell winced. It was not a performance. "Yes," he admitted. "Including those."

* * *

Here is what Edmund Pell had done, in the order he had done it, and he laid it out for Kestrel now in the flat, careful voice of a man reading his own indictment:

Fourteen years ago, Odile had come to him with a name—A. Winter—and asked him to establish a trust. The trust would receive funds from an account Pell was not permitted to trace, and the trust would make payments: to the Gull's Rest, keeping its head above water year after failing year; and, in smaller sums, more regularly, to a young man in the city whose name Pell had not known at the time but whose birthday, he realized much later, appeared in the trust documents with a significance he had been too well-trained—or too cowardly—to question.

"She told me it was restitution," Pell said. "She didn't say for what. Lawyers learn not to ask. We tell ourselves it's privilege. Mostly it's relief."

"And the codicil?"

"Two years ago. She came in on a wet Tuesday—she always came on Tuesdays, she said the fish van blocked the road otherwise and she enjoyed watching me be late—and she said she wanted to change her will. Everything to her child. Conditional on the child finding the truth." He paused. "I asked her what truth. She said, 'The one in my ledgers.' I assumed she meant accounts. I was wrong, of course. With Odile, the accounts were never accounts."

Kestrel turned their cup slowly on its saucer. "You drew up a will leaving everything to someone you knew was her abandoned child, and you never told them?"

"I posted the letter myself," Pell said. "After the funeral. As instructed. I did not know—I want that understood—I did not know who you were beyond a name and an address. She gave me both and made me swear not to look further. I swore. It was easier than disobeying her. Disobeying Odile Amberdale was rather like disagreeing with the tide. One could do it, technically, but one would end up wet and wrong."

Something almost like a smile crossed Kestrel's face. It vanished quickly, but Pell had spent thirty years reading faces in witness boxes, and he catalogued it: grief, wearing humor's coat.

"There's more," Kestrel said. It was not a question.

"No," Pell agreed. "There's more." And here his voice did something he disliked—it thinned. "There is the matter of the draft."

* * *

The morning after Odile died, Pell had gone to the Gull's Rest to collect her papers. This was standard practice; he had done it after Gerald's death, after old Aunt Marguerite's death, after every death in that family for half a century, walking through the front door with his document case like a vulture with a briefcase, as his late wife had once described it, not unkindly.

Odile's desk in Room 9 had been tidy in the way of a person who expected to be interrupted. Papers squared. Pens capped. And in the center, weighted under the brass clock with the stopped hands, a handwritten draft.

"I read it," Pell said. "I shouldn't have. Privilege dies with the client, but decency doesn't, and I violated both in about four seconds. It was a confession. In her hand, unmistakably hers—I'd seen her handwriting on forty years of instructions, furious little notes in margins, *Edmund, this clause is nonsense, fix it.* The draft confessed to her own murder. Dated the day she died. Signed Odile Amberdale."

Kestrel had gone very still. "But she wasn't murdered."

"No," said Pell. "The autopsy says her heart simply stopped, hours before anyone found her. She died alone in Room 9 of natural causes, and then—" he spread his hands, "—then someone moved her, and staged a drowning, and somewhere in that tangle a confession appeared that she could not have written after her death. Except that she *had* written it. Before. Alive. Deliberately."

"Why would a woman write a false confession to her own murder?"

Pell took off his glasses and polished them on his tie, which was what he did instead of sighing. "Because she wanted it found. By you. Think about it as she would have. She was dying—she'd known for months, the doctor told me, though she'd sworn him to secrecy too, the woman collected secrets the way other people collect stamps. She wanted you here. She wanted the ledgers read. She knew you wouldn't come for a legacy—you'd have sold the place and been gone in a fortnight, no offense intended."

"None taken. It was the plan."

"But a murder? A mystery? A confession naming you, pulling the police down on your head?" He shook his head with something close to admiration. "She knew you couldn't walk away from a puzzle. She'd read your file. Of course she had—she'd been paying into your life for thirty years, she knew everything. So she wrote a confession designed to be impossible, a confession that could only unravel by unraveling *everything else first*. The ledgers. The alias. Her whole hidden life, laid open by necessity. It was—" he searched for the word, "—it was the most Odile thing imaginable. She couldn't apologize directly, so she constructed an apology you'd have to excavate."

The fire ticked in the grate. Down on the high street, Mrs. Tredwell appeared to have won the mackerel argument; the fishmonger was wrapping it with the defeated air of a man paying danegeld.

"The draft is gone," Kestrel said.

"Gone. When I returned the following week—to return it, actually, I'd taken it home overnight like an idiot, meaning to decide whether to give it to the police—it was no longer under the clock. Someone in that house found it first. And whoever found it didn't destroy it." Pell leaned forward. "They *used* it. Your confession letter—the one delivered to you, the copy mailed to Detective Harrow—is built on that draft. Same structure, same central lie, but the hand is different. Trained, but different. And there is exactly one person in that family, besides Odile herself, who ever learned to write figures and letters in the house style."

"Marcus," Kestrel said.

"Marcus studied bookkeeping under her for one miserable summer when he was nineteen. She told me he was hopeless at arithmetic and excellent at imitation. She said it the way one says a thing one finds amusing and slightly alarming." Pell put his glasses back on. "Marcus stood to lose everything under the codicil. The developer's offer has been sitting in his inbox for a year—he and Ms. Crane go back further than any of us suspected, and I suspect further than Crane's testimony will survive. A dead aunt's hotel, a stranger heir, a forged confession that makes the heir look guilty and the property look cursed—sell fast, sell cheap, bury the codicil before anyone reads it. It's not subtle. But greed rarely is. Greed is the least imaginative sin. That's what makes it reliable."

"You've suspected him since the draft disappeared. Why haven't you said so? To Harrow, to anyone?"

And here Pell arrived at the thing he had actually invited Kestrel here to say, the thing that had kept him up three nights running staring at his ceiling while his wife slept the untroubled sleep of the innocent beside him.

"Because I am complicit," he said. "Not in the forgery. In the architecture. Every secret in that family passed through my office, and I filed them all neatly and asked nothing, because asking nothing was comfortable, and because Odile paid well, and because—because it was easier. Forty years of easier. If I go to Harrow now with suspicions I cultivated in silence, the first question he'll ask is what else I kept silent, and the honest answer is: everything. I am not a witness, Mr. Amberdale. I am a piece of the machine. And a machine does not testify against itself; it can only be taken apart by someone willing to get grease on their hands."

Kestrel regarded him for a long moment. Then they said, with a gentleness Pell found far worse than anger: "You loved her, didn't you. In your way."

"I admired her," Pell corrected. "Loving Odile was above my pay grade." He cleared his throat. "But yes. In my way. She was the only member of that family who ever used money as though it were a language instead of a weapon. Even her sins were correspondence."

* * *

They talked for another hour. Pell surrendered the trust documents—all of them, copies and originals, the complete paper skeleton of A. Winter—because, he said, privilege died with the client and he was tired of being a mausoleum. He told Kestrel about the second Tuesday visit, six weeks before the death, when Odile had asked him to hold an envelope "until the boy asks the right question," and how he had asked what the right question was, and she had said, *You'll know it when you hear it,* and he hadn't heard it yet, and the envelope sat in his safe weighing approximately four hundred pounds.

At the door, Kestrel paused with one hand on the frame.

"Pell. The trial starts Monday. Harrow's going to call you."

"I know."

"What will you say?"

Pell looked at this person—this quiet, watchful person assembled from Odile's stubbornness and some kinder material besides—and felt the strange lightness of a man setting down a case he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its weight.

"I shall tell the truth," he said. "In order, and at tedious length. I'm rather good at tedious length." He managed something that was nearly a smile. "It occurs to me that I've spent my career keeping the Amberdales' silences. It's time somebody in this affair broke one on purpose."

Kestrel nodded once and went out into the gray morning, and Pell stood at the window watching them cross the high street—past Mrs. Tredwell, past the fishmonger, past the whole indifferent town—toward the courthouse where, in five days, everything he had hoarded would finally be spent.

Then he went to his safe, took out the heavy envelope, and read, for the hundredth time, the single line written on its face in Odile's fierce, slanting hand:

*For my child — when they ask who I was.*

Monday, he thought. God help them all, Monday.
