## 9. Cipher at the Waterline

The note arrived folded inside a library copy of *A History of Merrow's Bend, Vol. II*, delivered to my motel room by a boy on a bicycle who couldn't have been more than twelve and who pedaled off before I could ask who sent him. The book fell open to a page about the founding of the marina, and there, in the margin, in pencil, was the cipher.

I recognized it the way you recognize a voice through a wall. Substitution cipher, the same one from the Margate insurance fraud case three years ago — the one where a widow had encoded her husband's actual cause of death into her recipe cards because she was too frightened to say it aloud. I'd cracked it over a weekend and a bottle of cheap pinot grigio. This one took me four minutes and no wine at all.

*The arrangement ends where it began. Boathouse. Midnight. Come alone or the old man drowns for real this time.*

Charming. Whoever wrote it had a flair for the theatrical that would have embarrassed a community theater director. But under the melodrama was arithmetic: the killer knew about Fenwick. Knew what he was, or at least suspected enough to use him as bait. And they were right about the one thing that mattered — I would come.

What they didn't know — couldn't know — was that I'd already been to the boathouse. Two days ago, in daylight, with Daniel Vane-Byrne chattering nervously beside me about his father's sailing trophies while my mind did what it always does, what everyone always underestimated: it kept everything.

Every board. Every shadow. Every exit.

* * *

I told Vane first. He sat in the archive's back room among sixty years of other men's paperwork, looking smaller than the man who had built a town, and when I showed him the decoded note his hands went still in a way that frightened me more than the note itself.

"You can't go," he said.

"I'm not going alone. That's the point of telling you."

"They'll expect me to stop you. If I don't—" He stopped. His eyes went to the window, to the lake beyond it, flat and silver in the evening light, holding its secrets the way it had held them for sixty years. "Elodie. In 1962 I stood on that dock and watched them lower an empty coffin into the ground, and I thought: this is the last lie. One lie, well-tended, and everything after is true." He laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "Lies breed. Nobody warns you."

"Then let's end the bloodline of them tonight." I set the recorder on the table between us — a small black thing, unremarkable, bought at the pharmacy two days ago along with batteries and aspirin. "You stay here. You call Sheriff Dunmore at eleven-forty-five and tell him everything, starting with your name. If I'm not out of that boathouse by midnight-thirty, the tape doesn't matter anyway."

"And if you are?"

"Then we finish this properly. With paper."

He looked at me for a long moment — the look of a man memorizing something, which I understood, being fluent in it myself. "Your mind," he said quietly. "It's a terrible gift, isn't it. You can never put anything down."

"It's also why I'll walk out of there," I said. "They're counting on me being scared. Scared people forget things. I don't get to forget things."

* * *

The boathouse at midnight was exactly as I remembered it, which was the whole point.

I had catalogued it two days ago without meaning to — I never mean to; the filing happens whether I consent or not. Long structure, cedar-shingled, built on pilings over the shallows. Main doors facing the dock, warped at the bottom left corner so they stuck unless lifted. Inside: six boat slips, four occupied. A workbench along the north wall with a vice and a coil of rope. A loft above the east end, reached by a ladder, used for storage — I knew because Daniel had climbed it to retrieve a tarp and I'd counted nine steps up and noted the loose fourth rung. A side door on the west wall, padlocked from outside but the hasp screws rusted to lace. And beneath the floorboards of slip three, a crawl space where the lake came through — I'd heard water moving under there, noticed the boards stained darker, remembered it the way I remember everything, uselessly, until tonight.

Useless knowledge is just ammunition you haven't fired yet.

I parked half a mile down the shore road and walked in the dark, because whoever built the trap expected a car in the lot and a woman coming through the front doors. I came through the west wall instead, working the hasp loose with a flathead screwdriver I'd brought for exactly this purpose, each rusted screw giving way with a small scream I felt in my teeth.

Inside, the dark smelled of gasoline and lake rot and old wood. Moonlight came through gaps in the shingles in thin silver blades. Slip three's boat was gone — taken out, or moved — and the gap in the floorboards above the crawl space had been widened since my visit. Freshly pried. Waiting.

The stage was set. All it lacked was an audience.

I stood in the shadow of the workbench and waited, and my heart did its animal work against my ribs while my mind, traitorously calm, recited the layout back to me like a bedtime story. Six slips. Four boats. Nine rungs, fourth one loose. Vice on the north wall. Side door behind me, screws spent. Front doors, stuck, lift to open. Loft ladder east. Crawl space below slip three.

Footsteps on the dock. Slow, unhurried, the footsteps of someone who believes the outcome is settled.

The front doors groaned — lifted, then pushed — and a flashlight beam swept the interior, catching dust motes like stirred ash.

"Miss Kavanagh." Richard Harrow's voice. Of course. It had been Harrow money in the trust ledger, Harrow initials in the margin of the removed pages, Harrow boats at the marina. The warmest family, the matriarch's job offer and house and friendship — all of it practiced warmth, and practice makes permanent. "I confess I hoped you wouldn't decode it. You were supposed to be clever about insurance forms and incurious about everything else."

"I get that a lot," I said, and stepped into the moonlight, because the script required it.

He looked tired, Richard Harrow. Silver-haired, cardiganed, holding a flashlight in one hand and nothing in the other, which somehow was worse. Behind him, half in shadow, the shape of the widened hole in slip three's floor.

"My grandfather signed the first payment in 1963," he said, conversational, walking toward me along the dock planks inside the boathouse. "Forty thousand dollars a year, split four ways, to keep a dead man dead. Do you know what that does to a family? It becomes a religion. My father tithed to it. I was raised on it. And then eight years ago I discovered the trust could be *managed* — expenses, administration fees, discretionary disbursements. A dead man needs no accounting." He smiled, and it was the smile from the church picnic, from the welcome dinner, from every warm handshake. "Deputy Coyle figured out the skimming. Then he got nostalgic and started talking about confession before death. People should really learn to keep their sins to themselves."

"Like you're doing now?"

"Oh, I'd never say any of this." He gestured with the flashlight, almost playful. "That's rather the point of the arrangement. Accidents happen on this lake. They've happened before. A nosy investigator comes to town, pokes around, goes out on the water at night — the sheriff will call it tragic. The town will call it sad. And in a year, nobody will call it anything."

He moved then — faster than a man in a cardigan should move — and I learned the second reason he'd brought no visible weapon: the boat hook, leaning against slip five where I had seen it two days ago and filed it away and not, until this exact moment, understood why my mind had bothered.

He swung it at my legs. I had known he would — the geometry of the space demanded it, the workbench at my back, the open water of slip three ahead — and I was already moving, dropping and rolling left toward the ladder, because the loft was the only high ground and the fourth rung was loose and I needed him to follow me up.

People underestimate two things about total recall. First, that it isn't a party trick; it's a map of everywhere you've ever been, drawn in ink. Second, that the person being chased has already read the map.

He followed. Of course he followed. The trap required a body, and bodies don't walk into lakes on their own.

The third rung held. The fourth gave way under his weight with a crack like a starter pistol, and Richard Harrow pitched forward into the loft's darkness, and I was already past him, down the ladder on the good rungs, sprinting for slip three.

I dropped through the widened gap into the crawl space before my mind finished approving the plan — cold water to my knees, the lake breathing through the pilings, the underside of the boathouse ribbed above me like the belly of some enormous sleeping animal. Through the gap overhead I heard him thrash upright, hear the loft boards, hear him realize the loft had no other way down except past me.

Except it did. Because I had counted the boards. Because two days ago, bored, waiting for Daniel to find his tarp, I had noticed that the loft's east wall had a hatch — painted shut, probably forgotten for decades — opening onto the roof slope above the side door. I hadn't been sure it opened. I gambled a murderer's patience that it did.

It didn't matter whether it did. What mattered was the thirty seconds of confusion, and I spent them well: recorder out of my coat pocket, thumb on the button, and my voice pitched to carry up through the floorboards.

"Say the part again about Deputy Coyle. The sheriff's recording now, Mr. Harrow. Has been since you walked in."

Silence. Then the worst sound I heard all night: a man beginning to laugh, and the laugh curdling into something else.

"You're lying."

"The town thinks I have a photographic memory," I called up, wading toward the gap beneath the front doors, where moonlight spilled through the warped corner. "Nobody ever asks what else I remember. Your grandfather's signature. The dates. Coyle's phone records. And every word you just said, verbatim, twice — once in my head, once on tape." I reached up through the gap and lifted the stuck door, the way I'd watched Daniel do it, the way my memory had never once gotten wrong. "Come down and dispute it."

He didn't come down. He ran — out the front doors, onto the dock, into the dark — and I let him, because a running man leaves a trail, and Sheriff Dunmore's cruisers were already turning onto the shore road with their lights off, because Vane had made his call at eleven-forty-five and given them, in his precise archivist's voice, everything.

I climbed out through the front doors and stood on the dock, shaking now that it was permitted, the recorder clutched to my chest like something rescued from a fire. The lake lay flat and silver and finally, finally empty of secrets.

Behind me, at the tree line, a figure detached itself from the shadows — old, straight-backed, wrapped in a coat too thin for the season. Aldous Vane, founder of Merrow's Bend, dead these sixty years, alive and watching his town's longest lie bleed out on the dock planks.

"You heard?" I asked.

"All of it." His voice was steady, but his eyes weren't. "Every word."

"Then you know what happens next. The tape goes to the sheriff, the sheriff goes to the prosecutor, and the prosecutor is going to want the man at the center of it. On the record. Under oath. Under *name*."

Vane looked out at the water for a long time. When he turned back to me, he had the expression of a man who has finally finished paying a debt.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we go to town hall. And I'll show you where I keep the only document in this town I never filed."

"What document?"

"A birth certificate," said the ghost of Aldous Vane. "Mine."
