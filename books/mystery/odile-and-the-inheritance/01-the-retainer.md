## 1. The Retainer

The rain had been falling on Coldharbour since Tuesday, which was not unusual. What was unusual was the woman who walked into my office at four o'clock on a Thursday, dry as a ledger page, carrying an umbrella she clearly hadn't needed and a briefcase that looked older than I was.

I was elbow-deep in the Hollis file — old man Hollis, seventy-nine, found at the bottom of his own cellar stairs with a fresh bruise on his temple that the police report called "consistent with the fall." It is not consistent with a fall. A fall leaves one bruise where you hit. Mr. Hollis had two, and the second one was shaped like the corner of a cast-iron pan, which his daughter-in-law happened to own. But that's neither here nor there yet.

"Ms. Coldharbour?" the woman said.

"Coroner," I said, without looking up. "Wren Coldharbour. If you're here about the Henderson dog, I've already ruled it death by misadventure, and I stand by that even though Mrs. Henderson disagrees."

"I'm not here about a dog."

I looked up then, because her voice had the particular flatness of someone reciting from memory rather than speaking from feeling. She stood just inside the door as though she'd measured the room beforehand and knew exactly how far in to come. Pale — not sickly pale, but pale the way paper is pale, or moonlight. Dark hair pinned back with mathematical precision. Grey coat, grey gloves, grey eyes that took me in the way I take in a body: systematically, respectfully, missing nothing.

"My name is Odile," she said.

"Just Odile?"

"For now."

I set down the Hollis file, mostly because she made me want to look professional and partly because something in her tone suggested the conversation was about to require both hands. "Can I offer you coffee? The machine's temperamental, but it produces something technically warm."

"No, thank you." She sat in the chair across from my desk without being asked, which should have annoyed me and instead felt like watching someone do a thing correctly. "I'd like to retain you."

"I'm a coroner, not a lawyer. Retainers are more of a—"

"A year's fees," she said, and opened the briefcase.

I will be honest with you, whoever ends up reading this: I have been the coroner of Coldharbour for eleven years, and in that time I have been paid in cash, in casseroles, once in a live goat (the Lessups, don't ask), and once, memorably, in nothing at all. Nobody has ever slid a cashier's check across my desk for twelve months of my salary before I'd agreed to anything. My hand hovered over it the way you hover over a door handle in a house you're not sure you're allowed to enter.

"That's a lot of money for a town where half my cases are 'fell off the dock' and the other half are 'old age, complicated by stubbornness,'" I said.

"The fee is not negotiable upward," Odile said. "Only downward, if you fail."

"If I fail at what, exactly?"

She folded her gloved hands in her lap. For a moment she looked out the window at the rain, and I had the strange impression she was checking it against some internal forecast.

"I would like to hire you," she said, "to prove that no murder has occurred."

"Well," I said, relieved and vaguely disappointed at once. "That shouldn't be hard. Most days, no murders have occurred. It's practically my specialty."

"Not in general." Her eyes came back to mine. "One specific murder. Mine."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the street, Doreen Pruitt's shutters banged, because they have banged every windy day since 1998 and will bang until the heat death of the universe, because Doreen believes ladders are for people with something to hide.

"Someone's threatened you?" I asked. "Because threats go to the sheriff. Sheriff Dunmore has a whole system. There's a form."

"No one has threatened me. Not yet." She said *not yet* the way other people say *tomorrow*. "I am asking you to establish, formally and with evidence, that no murder has taken place — that when I die, if I die, it will be of natural causes, at a natural time, by natural means. And that the person everyone will suspect did not do it."

"And who does everyone suspect?"

She didn't blink. People blink when they say things like what she said next. She didn't blink at all.

"Your brother," she said. "Elias Coldharbour."

* * *

Let me tell you about Elias, since apparently the universe has decided we're going to spend some time together.

Elias is my older brother by three years, which in childhood meant everything and in adulthood means he still reaches for the last word first. He left Coldharbour at nineteen with a duffel bag and our father's disappointment, came back twice — once for Dad's funeral, once for nothing anyone could name — and left again both times. He calls at Christmas. Sometimes. He owes money; I know this the way you know things about siblings, through the grapevine of aunts and the occasional collections letter addressed to my address because he still uses it, lazy and hopeful, like a cat that moved out years ago but keeps showing up at dinner.

He is also, and I want this on the record, constitutionally incapable of murder. Not because he's gentle — he isn't, particularly — but because Elias has never finished anything in his life. A murder requires follow-through. Elias once abandoned a sandwich halfway through because he "lost interest in the narrative."

So when the pale woman across my desk named my brother as her predicted killer, my first feeling was not fear.

It was laughter. I swallowed it, because she had that kind of face, but I felt it all the way down, warm and absurd, like brandy in tea.

"You'll forgive me," I said, "if I observe that Elias couldn't premeditate his way out of a parking garage."

"He doesn't need to premeditate," Odile said. "That's rather the point. Premeditation can be prevented. Impulse cannot. That's why I'm hiring you now, while there is still time to prove nothing happens."

I turned the check over. It was real. Banks in this county issue exactly one style of cashier's check, and it looks like a wedding invitation printed by a committee.

"Walk me through this," I said. "You believe my brother will kill you. You haven't met him. Have you?"

"No."

"So a stranger walks into my office, pays me a year's wages, and tells me my estranged brother is going to murder her, and asks me to prevent it by proving it won't happen. Do you hear how that sounds?"

"Yes," she said. "It sounds like a woman who has done the arithmetic and doesn't like the answer."

I should have shown her the door. Any sensible person would have. Instead I heard myself ask the question that has, in retrospect, rearranged my entire life:

"Why me? Why not the sheriff, the state police, a private investigator in the city with a license and a gun?"

"Because you find things," she said simply. "Three accidents in this town were not accidents, and you corrected the record each time. Quietly. Without credit. The Prue girl's insulin. The ferryman's brakes. Old man Hollis—" she nodded at the file under my hand, and I confess my stomach dropped a full inch, "—and his cellar stairs. You notice what others agree not to notice. That is the only qualification that matters."

"How do you know about Hollis? I haven't filed the amendment yet."

Something crossed her face then — the first thing all afternoon that wasn't precise. It might have been regret. It might have been weather.

"I know a great many things about this town, Ms. Coldharbour," she said. "More than I would like. Fewer than I need."

* * *

Here is what I did after she left: I locked the check in the drawer with my father's watch, which is where I keep things I don't trust, and I made the coffee I'd offered her, and I drank it standing at the window watching the rain come down on Main Street like the town was being gently erased and redrawn.

And here is what I kept coming back to, turning it over like a stone found in a shoe:

She hadn't asked me to protect her. She hadn't asked for a bodyguard, an alarm, a safehouse. She'd asked for a *record*. Proof, in advance, that a murder did not occur. Who asks for that? You ask for protection if you're afraid of dying. You ask for documentation if you're afraid of what happens *after* — of being misread, misfiled, closed as an accident.

Closed, say, the way certain cases in this town get closed.

I thought about my father then, the way I try not to, the way you don't press on a bruise unless something makes you. Twelve years ago this spring. The lake. The ruling: accidental drowning, alcohol a contributing factor, case closed by unanimous agreement of everyone who mattered, including Elias, who signed whatever needed signing because signing things is how Elias says goodbye.

Dad didn't drink. That's the thing nobody ever wanted to sit with. Twenty-six years sober, chip in his pocket like a talisman, and the report gave him a blood alcohol that would have felled a horse. I was twenty-three and gutted and I let it go, because the whole town — Vivienne Ashcombe herself, God rest her eventually — told me it was kindness to let him rest.

Odile had paid me to prove a negative. But sitting there with my cooling coffee, I understood, with the slow cold certainty of water rising in a sealed room, that she had actually hired me for something else entirely.

She had hired me to open something that someone, somewhere, was counting on staying shut.

I pulled the drawer open. The check sat on top of my father's watch, gleaming, patient. Twelve months of fees, paid in advance, by a woman who knows how she's going to die.

Outside, the rain kept up its patient work, and down the street, Doreen Pruitt's shutters banged like something trying to get out.

Tomorrow, I decided, I'd walk the cemetery. Every grave by name. Starting with his.

It was, I would later understand, exactly what she wanted.
