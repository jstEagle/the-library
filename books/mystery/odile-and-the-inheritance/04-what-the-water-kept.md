## 4. What the Water Kept

The exhumation permit took eleven days to arrive, which gave the town eleven days to talk about it.

By day three, Marisol at the diner had stopped charging me for coffee — a kindness that felt more like a subscription to gossip. By day five, someone had left a casserole on my office steps with a note that read *Thinking of you during this difficult time*, which was either condolence or threat; in Coldharbour, the two were often indistinguishable. By day seven, I'd received four phone calls from people urging me to let my father rest, each of them using the word *rest* with the particular emphasis of a person who meant *quiet*.

I drank the coffee. I ate the casserole. I did not rest.

On the morning of the exhumation itself, the sky was the color of dishwater and Odile was already at the cemetery gate when I arrived, standing precisely where the gravel met the grass as though she had calculated the exact boundary of her welcome.

"You didn't have to come," I said.

"I paid for this," she said. "I intend to watch it."

The gravedigger was a man named Tomás Ferro who had dug half the graves in Coldharbour and attended most of them. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a fence post that had decided against falling over, and he regarded Odile with the frank appraisal of a man who buried secrets for a living and recognized one on sight.

"Coldharbour plot," he said to me, spitting thoughtfully into the grass. "Your father. Good man. Terrible luck."

"That's what we're here to find out," I said.

Tomás nodded slowly. "Terrible luck," he repeated, in a tone that suggested terrible luck was something he'd been asked to manufacture before.

*

Digging up a body is nothing like television. There is no dramatic crane, no gasp from the assembled crowd. There is a small yellow excavator, two county men who complained about the frost, and a tarp to keep the dirt off the grass because even now — especially now — Coldharbour cared about its lawn.

Odile watched all of it without blinking. She stood at the edge of the tarp in a gray coat, hands folded, like a woman attending a recital. Once, when the excavator bucket bit into soil that came up dark and reeking of twelve years underwater, she closed her eyes for exactly three seconds. I counted. Then she opened them again and went back to watching.

"You've done this before," I said quietly.

"No," she said. "But I've read about it. Extensively."

Of course she had.

My father's coffin came up at 9:40 in the morning, water-stained at the corners, lighter than it should have been. The county men set it on the portable table with a reverence they hadn't shown the lawn, and Dr. Ansel Prue — the same Dr. Prue who had signed the original autopsy, now older, softer, and visibly sweating in forty-degree weather — began to work.

I want to be honest here: I expected nothing. That was the strategy I'd chosen, the emotional equivalent of bracing for a wave by standing very still and pretending to be furniture. Twelve years. Water. A sealed case. What could possibly be left?

What was left was everything.

"Interesting," Prue murmured, and then, catching himself: "Routine. This is routine."

"Ansel." I kept my voice level. "You said 'interesting.'"

He did not look at me. He lifted the mandible with gloved fingers and tilted his head, and I saw it — or rather, I saw the absence of it, the place where bone should have healed smooth and instead carried a shadow, a fracture line long knitted but knitted wrong, the way a bone heals when nobody sets it.

"The jaw," I said.

"Old injury," Prue said quickly. "Pre-mortem. Weeks before death, possibly. Consistent with a fall."

"A fall," I repeated.

"People fall," said Prue, dabbing his forehead. "It's practically a hobby."

But he wasn't looking at the jaw anymore. He was looking at the hands, and so I looked too, and there — under twelve years of water and silence — were the fingernails, and beneath two of them, preserved in the cold chemistry of the lake bottom, thin crescents of something dark.

Tomás Ferro made a sound low in his throat. It might have been a prayer. It might have been the opposite.

"Diatoms," Prue said, switching registers entirely, suddenly brisk, suddenly professional, suddenly a man reading from a script he'd rehearsed. "I'll need lab confirmation, but the lung tissue shows diatom presence consistent with live drowning. He went into the water alive. As reported. As always reported."

"And the fingernail scrapings?"

"Could be anything. Silt. Organic material." He snapped the specimen case shut harder than necessary. "Wren. Listen to me. Your father drowned. That is what happened, that is what has always happened, and digging him up doesn't change what happened. It only changes how much everyone has to keep explaining it."

There it was. The sentence underneath the sentence.

"Keep explaining it," I said.

Prue's face did something complicated — guilt and fear fighting for the steering wheel — and then smoothed itself into the bland professional mask I'd trusted for twenty years of death certificates. "Lab results in six weeks," he said. "You'll receive copies."

He packed up fast after that. He did not meet Odile's eyes, though she stood directly in his path to the car, and he stepped around her the way you step around a puddle you suspect is deeper than it looks.

*

We stayed while the coffin went back down. It seemed rude not to. Tomás worked slowly, deliberately, taking longer than the job required, and when the last of the dirt was tamped flat and the temporary marker set straight, he straightened up, pressed both hands into the small of his back, and spoke without looking at either of us.

"Your daddy's funeral," he said. "Twelve years back. Biggest one I ever dug for. Whole town came. Flowers from the Ashcombe greenhouse — Vivienne sent them herself, white lilies, hundreds of dollars' worth. Paid for the plot, too. Paid for the vault." He paused. "Paid for the coffin."

I turned. "She paid for my father's funeral?"

"She paid for everybody's funeral." Tomás said it simply, the way you'd say *she owns the mill* or *it rains in spring*. "Forty years, near enough. Any family that couldn't cover it, the Ashcombe fund covered it. No questions asked." He picked up his shovel, considered it, put it down again. "No questions asked," he repeated, and this time the phrase landed differently, the way a familiar word lands differently when you finally see it spelled wrong.

"Mr. Ferro," Odile said, and her voice was soft as snowfall, "who arranged the payment? For this funeral specifically?"

Tomás looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face made up his mind about something.

"Not Vivienne herself. She never handled money — wouldn't touch it, called it vulgar. Always the son. Marcus Ashcombe. He'd come down to my office with an envelope, sign the ledger, done." He hesitated. "Except that week. That week Marcus was away — some conference, I heard. So it was the other one. The quiet fella. Lived up at the house, did the books, drove Miss Vivienne around."

"The caretaker," I said.

"Reeve," Tomás said. "Silas Reeve." He shouldered his shovel at last. "Nice enough man. Never said two words unless one of them was *yes*."

He walked off across the frosted grass, leaving us alone with a fresh-turned grave and the particular silence of a town holding its breath.

*

Odile and I sat in my car for a long time afterward, engine running for the heat, neither of us speaking. On the dashboard, the specimen receipt from the county lab curled slightly in the warmth. Diatoms consistent with live drowning. My father had gone into Blackwater Lake breathing. Everyone had always said so. Everyone had always been right, in the way that a forged signature is technically handwriting.

"He was held under," I said finally. It sounded obscene out loud, so I said it again, plainer. "Someone held my father under the water until he died, and then the whole town helped bury it."

"The jaw fracture predates the drowning," Odile said. She had her notebook open, writing in that tiny upright hand of hers, each letter a prisoner in its cell. "Weeks before, per Prue. Which means there was violence first, and drowning second, and a gap between them where somebody decided which story to tell." She clicked her pen. "And the fingernail evidence — if the lab confirms dermal tissue —"

"It'll confirm it," I said. "Prue knows. You saw him. He's not surprised, Odile. He's *scheduled*. Six weeks for results puts us past the statute. He's not hiding the truth. He's timing it."

For the first time since I'd met her, Odile looked genuinely unsettled — not afraid, exactly, but recalibrating, a compass needle discovering north had moved. "You think he falsified the original autopsy."

"I think Prue signed whatever he was asked to sign twelve years ago, and he's been signing it ever since, every year, in his own memory." I stared through the windshield at the cemetery, at the rows of stones marching up the hill like teeth. "Here's what I can't get past. The funeral. The flowers. The fund. Vivienne Ashcombe paid to bury my father, and she paid to bury every secret in this town along with him. Forty years of no questions asked. You don't build loyalty like that by accident. You build it the way you build a levee — on purpose, ahead of the flood."

"So the case wasn't closed," Odile said slowly.

"The case was *purchased*."

She wrote that down. Underlined it twice.

I should have felt triumphant. Somewhere under everything, I suppose I did — the coroner's cold little pilot light, the part of me that had corrected three accidents the police ignored, flickering brighter than it ever had. But mostly what I felt was the ground shifting, literally and otherwise: the sense that every grave I'd catalogued, every name in my ledger, every kind casserole and free coffee and gentle *let him rest* was load-bearing masonry in a structure I'd lived inside my whole life without once looking up.

My father hadn't just drowned. He'd been held down by someone, and then held down by everyone.

My phone buzzed against the console. Elias. Third call today, fourth voicemail. *Wren, please, I just got to town, I'm at the Ashcombe thing, why is everyone looking at me like — call me back.*

I looked at the screen for a long moment. The brother the stranger had named as my future killer, calling from the house on the hill where the money lived.

Then the phone buzzed again — a different number, local, unfamiliar. I answered it because answering things is my whole personality.

"Wren Coldharbour?" The voice was young, female, pitched low, like someone calling from inside a closet. "This is Nina. I work at Dr. Prue's office. I wasn't supposed to see this, and I'm probably going to lose my job, but—" a breath, ragged, "—he just shredded something. Right after he got back from your exhumation. And the shredder bin goes out tonight, and I pulled it out, and—"

Her voice cracked.

"Nina," I said gently. "What did you pull out of the bin?"

A pause long enough to hold a whole drowned man.

"It's a page from the original autopsy," she whispered. "Twelve years old. And the cause-of-death line isn't typed like the rest of it. It's been *replaced*. You can see the tape marks." Another breath. "Dr. Prue didn't write your father's autopsy report, Ms. Coldharbour. Somebody else did. And whoever it was signed it with Prue's name — except they signed it *before* Prue ever examined the body. The date's wrong. The date's three days early."

Outside, the frost was coming down off the cemetery hill in silver threads, and somewhere up at the Ashcombe house, a telephone was presumably ringing.

"Bring me the page," I said. "Tonight. Before the bin goes out."

"I'm scared," Nina said.

"Yes," I said. "Be scared. Come anyway."
