## 5. Odile's Ledger

My grandmother kept her accounts the way other women kept their sins: in a locked drawer, in a green ledger with a cracked spine, entered in a hand so beautiful it looked like an apology.

I found the ledger the night after her lawyer read us the first will — the old one, the one that left everything to the town and nothing to the family, as if we were a rounding error she'd decided to forgive herself for. I had gone to Ashcombe House to collect my mother's things, which amounted to three boxes and a coat that smelled of camphor and defeat. The house was empty of people but full of listening. It always had been. As a child I'd believed the walls repeated what they heard to Vivienne at dinner, and she would nod slowly, filing it away under *leverage* or *disappointment*.

The study drawer wasn't even locked. That was the insult of it. She'd stopped bothering to hide anything from me years before she disinherited me, on the reasonable theory that I had never once been a threat.

I sat on the floor of her study with my back against her desk chair — the one she'd ruled the town from for fifty years — and I read until the light went grey and then blue and then gone.

*Funeral, Halloway infant. Paid in full. Stone: "Beloved."*

*Dr. Ferris, retainer, annual. Note: discretion re: Coldharbour.*

*E.C., settlement, final. Do not contact again.*

*Coldharbour boy's schooling — see separate account.*

Page after page, decade after decade. Births paid for. Deaths paid for. Funerals dressed up in marble because Vivienne Ashcombe believed a beautiful gravestone was cheaper than a question. The whole town buried in ground she owned, mourned at services she funded, remembered in words she had effectively commissioned. We were not a community. We were a portfolio.

And there, near the end, in ink still sharp enough to sting:

*Retainer: W. Coldharbour, coroner. Purpose: prevention.*

She had written it eleven days before she hired me to hire him. My own fee, itemized. *O.A., expenses, see attached.* Attached was a single sheet in her hand, shakier now, listing the dates she expected things to happen. She had predicted the exhumation within four days of its actual occurrence. She had predicted Dr. Ferris would recant his original autopsy if pressed, and that he would deny recanting within the week.

She had predicted, in a line that made me put the ledger down and walk to the window and breathe at the garden like a normal person, that *the boy will be blamed, and it will be too late by the time anyone thinks to look at me.*

The boy. Elias Coldharbour. A man I had met exactly twice, both times at funerals, both times standing apart from the mourners the way a man stands when he isn't sure he's allowed to grieve.

Here is what I want to be understood, since no one in this town will ever understand it willingly: I did not hire Wren Coldharbour out of malice. I hired him out of arithmetic.

* * *

Let me explain the arithmetic, because Wren will ask — Wren asks everything the way a scalpel asks — and I would rather tell him myself than let him find it in some drawer.

Vivienne's revised will leaves everything to me. Not to her son Marcus, my father, who spent forty years being disappointing in expensive restaurants. Not to the historical society or the church roof fund or any of the other worthy mouths she'd fed for half a century. To me. On conditions.

Condition one: I must survive thirty days from the reading. Thirty days. She might as well have written *survive the hunting season* and pinned a target to my coat.

Condition two: the entire bequest is void if the death of Aldous Coldharbour — Wren's father, drowned twelve years ago in Miller's Pond, ruled accidental, case sealed — is ever officially ruled murder.

Read those two conditions together and you get a very simple equation. Someone wants me dead before day thirty, and someone wants that drowning to stay an accident forever. And if you can't kill the coroner's sister-in-law-to-be without the coroner noticing — well. You don't kill the coroner's sister. You arrange for the coroner's brother to do it, in a fit of grief and debt and old resentment, and then the case closes itself, and the statute runs out, and the truth goes into the ground with everything else Vivienne ever paid to bury.

I know how this sounds. I have rehearsed saying it aloud in my car, in hotel bathrooms, in the parking lot of the diner where I've eaten eleven consecutive breakfasts because the waitress has stopped asking questions and started bringing extra toast. It sounds like paranoia wearing a nice blazer. It sounds like a woman who read her grandmother's ledgers and caught the family disease: the conviction that everything is connected, because in this town, everything is.

But consider the alternative explanation. Consider that a dying woman of ninety-one, lucid in the way that only the truly guilty are lucid, spent her last months constructing a machine — a will, a retainer, a timeline — designed to force one stubborn coroner to dig up his own father. Consider that she could have confessed. She had priests on speed dial; the Catholic church is very understanding about deathbeds. Instead she built a puzzle whose only solution was exposure, and she left the solving to strangers, because she was a coward to the last, and cowards prefer to be caught by arithmetic rather than admit anything.

I loved her. That's the part nobody will believe. I loved her the way you love a storm — from indoors, admiring the lightning, counting the seconds until the thunder tells you how far away it is.

* * *

Wren came to my motel room on Thursday, which I had predicted for Wednesday, which means either I'm losing my touch or he's more careful than his file suggested. His file — yes, I had a file. Grandmother's money buys surprisingly good files. He parks two spaces away from doors. He reads menus bottom-up. He apologizes to waitresses for things that aren't their fault. He is the most predictable unpredictable man I have ever studied, and I include actuaries.

He stood in my doorway holding the exhumation report in a folder squared to the edge of his body, and I thought: here is a man who irons his shirts for corpses, because the dead deserve creases.

"You knew," he said. Not a question. He'd found the ledger reference in Vivienne's papers, or guessed, or simply done what he always does, which is follow the money until it confesses.

"Come in," I said. "Mind the ironing board. It's also the desk."

He stepped inside the way a man steps into water he's already decided is cold. He looked at the ledger open on the bed — I had left it open deliberately, page turned to the entry about his father, because I am not above stagecraft — and something crossed his face that I recognized from photographs of him at twelve, at his father's funeral, standing next to Elias. Grief, but tidy. Grief that had been told to wait its turn.

"How long have you known your grandmother killed my father?" he asked.

"I don't know that she did." I sat on the radiator, which clanked in protest. "I know she paid for the autopsy to be wrong. I know she paid Dr. Ferris every year since, which is a strange annuity for an innocent woman. I know she wrote down, in her own hand, that the boy would be blamed. Whether she held him under the water herself or merely arranged the world so that someone else would — I genuinely don't know. She didn't write that part down. Even her confessions have footnotes."

"And the retainer." His voice was very level, the way water is level right before it goes over the edge. "You approached me with her money. Her instructions. Her timeline."

"Yes."

"Why not come yourself? Why the theater? The stranger with the envelope?"

"Because you'd have said no." I said it plainly, because lying to Wren Coldharbour would be like lying to a scale. "A stranger buying your skepticism, fine. You'd take the money and disbelieve every word, and disbelief would keep you looking. But if Vivienne Ashcombe's granddaughter had walked into your office and said *my grandmother murdered your father*, you'd have spent nine days proving me a liar instead of proving her guilty. I needed you working against me. It's the only direction you work honestly."

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the motel ice machine delivered its hourly sermon.

"The thirty days," he said finally. "Day nineteen is Friday."

"I know. I've been counting since before you accepted the retainer. I count everything now. It's inherited."

"Odile." He said my name carefully, testing whether it was a weapon. "Are you trying to catch a killer, or are you trying to inherit?"

And there it was — the question I'd been waiting for, the one I'd rehearsed answers to in three motel rooms and one very long bath. The honest answer is that I don't know where the line is. I want the truth and I want the money and I want my grandmother, posthumously and permanently, to have been wrong about me. These desires are not separable. They grew together, like roots through a foundation, and you cannot pull one out without cracking something.

What I said was: "If I wanted the money, the fastest route was to let Elias hang and stay alive. I'm doing the opposite. Draw your own conclusion."

"That's what worries me," Wren said. "It's exactly what someone clever would say."

We looked at each other across the ironing board, ally and suspect, which in this town may be the same job title.

Then his phone buzzed, and he glanced at it, and all the blood left his face with the efficiency of a man who has seen many bodies and knows exactly what drained color looks like leaving a living one.

"What?" I asked.

"Elias," he said. "Someone broke into his room last night. Took nothing." A pause. "Left a copy of Dad's death certificate on the pillow. With the word *accident* circled."

The radiator clanked. Somewhere across town, I would have bet the whole unclaimed inheritance, a clock was ticking over somebody's fireplace, keeping perfect time.

"We're past prediction now," I said. "Whoever it is, they're improvising. Which means they're scared. Which means, Mr. Coldharbour—" I picked up the ledger and held it out to him, green cover, cracked spine, fifty years of beautiful apologies, "—we're winning. God help us."
