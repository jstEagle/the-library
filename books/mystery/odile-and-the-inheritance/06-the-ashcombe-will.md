## 6. The Ashcombe Will

The will arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day I did my filing, and there was something about the Ashcombe estate's paperwork that suggested it had been filed by someone who believed paper was a form of warfare.

The envelope was cream-colored, heavy as a paving stone, and hand-delivered by a courier who looked like he'd been told not to make eye contact with anyone in Coldharbour under any circumstances. Inside: a certified copy of Vivienne Ashcombe's revised testament, along with a note from the town clerk — *Per your request, Dr. Coldharbour. Please return when finished. And Wren? Be careful with this one.* The clerk, Marisol, had known me since I was nine years old and had once fainted into an open grave at a funeral. She didn't say things like "be careful" lightly.

I made coffee first. This is a habit of mine that Elias used to mock mercilessly — *you can't process a crisis without a beverage, Wren, what are you, a Victorian* — but I've found that the ritual of it buys the mind thirty seconds of honesty before the work begins. I stood at my kitchen window watching the fog peel off the harbor, drank half the cup, and then sat down at my table with the will and Odile's ledger side by side, like two witnesses who hadn't yet been introduced.

The revised will was dated eleven months ago. Eleven months. Nearly to the week before Odile walked into my office with her year's fees in a plain envelope.

I read it three times. The third time, I read it aloud, because some sentences only confess their cruelty when they're given a voice.

"To my granddaughter, Odile Almeida, I leave the whole of my estate" — and the whole of the estate was, conservatively, most of the visible horizon from Coldharbour Hill — "*provided she survives thirty days from the date of my death.*"

There it was. Clause four.

And then clause five, which I read twice more because my eyes kept trying to slide off it the way they slide off a word you've misspelled so long it looks correct:

"*Provided further that the aforementioned bequest shall be rendered null and void in its entirety should the death of Aldous Coldharbour, occurring October 14th twelve years past, be formally reclassified or adjudicated as homicide.*"

My father's name, sitting in a stranger's will like a landmine in a flowerbed.

I set down the page. I picked it up again. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed the particular aggrieved scream of a gull that has been personally wronged, and I thought: *well, get in line.*

Here is what the two clauses meant, laid end to end like dominoes:

If Odile died within thirty days of Vivienne's death, everything reverted to the residual estate — which, per clause nine, flowed to a trust administered by... I checked. Of course. The Ashcombe Family Foundation, whose trustees were the same five founding-family names that appeared on every charitable board, every funeral fund, every plaque in this town. And if my father's drowning was ever ruled a murder, Odile got nothing anyway.

So there were exactly two ways to disinherit Odile Almeida, and both of them required the same thing: my father stays an accident, and Odile stops breathing before the paperwork settles.

Someone needed her dead quickly. Someone needed the case closed permanently. And someone — this was the part that made my hands go cold around the coffee mug — someone needed a killer for both jobs, and had apparently looked around Coldharbour and settled on my brother.

Elias, who owed money to people who collected it with tools. Elias, who had left town the week after our father drowned and never explained why. Elias, who would inherit nothing, know nothing, and be perfectly positioned to look desperate enough to do anything.

*You don't hire a frame,* I thought. *You grow one. You water it for years.*

* * *

Marisol Fenn at the town clerk's office keeps a kettle, a tin of lemon biscuits, and an intelligence network that would embarrass several governments. I went to see her at four o'clock, when the office empties out and she becomes, briefly, a private citizen willing to gossip.

"I need the probate history on the Ashcombe estate," I said. "And whatever you can tell me about who pushed for the revision."

Marisol poured two cups without asking. "Vivienne revised it herself. Came in person, eleven months back." She slid a biscuit toward me, which was either hospitality or a bribe. "Sat right where you're sitting. Sharp as a tack that day, too — everyone says she's failing, and she is, but that afternoon she read every page before she signed. Caught an error in the legal description of the orchard parcel. Made the lawyer fix it."

"Who was the lawyer?"

"Hollis. From Ashford." Marisol lowered her voice even though we were alone, which is how you know the information is good. "But here's the thing, Wren. She didn't come alone."

"No?"

"That caretaker of hers. Marek. Drove her. Waited in the hall the whole time, standing by the window like a coat rack that dislikes you." She stirred her tea though there was nothing to stir. "And when Vivienne signed, Marek took the pen out of her hand himself. Wiped it with a handkerchief and put it back in his jacket pocket. Like evidence."

I let that sit. "Did that strike you as strange at the time?"

"Wren." Marisol gave me the flat look of a woman who has processed forty years of small-town deaths and found almost none of them surprising. "In this town, a man keeping a pen is the least strange thing I've seen this month."

I asked my real question carefully. "The original ruling on my father. Who certified the autopsy?"

Something flickered across her face — the specific discomfort of a person who has just realized they're holding the last rung of a ladder over deep water. "Dr. Pruett signed it. You know that."

"I know his signature is on it. I want to know if he examined the body."

Marisol set down her cup. For a long moment she looked out the window at the war memorial, where the founding families' names were carved in stone, and I watched her decide something.

"Aldous Coldharbour was pulled out of the water at six-forty in the evening," she said quietly. "I remember because I was closing up and heard the sirens. Dr. Pruett didn't arrive until after eight. And Wren —" she turned back to me, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing — "the body was already at the funeral home when he got there. Vivienne Ashcombe's funeral home. The one she paid for. The one she paid for everybody's funerals in, going back thirty years, and nobody ever asked why a woman with no children in town needed to own the only place we bury our dead."

"She owned the ground," I said. "And the preparation of what goes in it."

"And the paperwork that describes it." Marisol folded her hands. "I've filed every death certificate in this town since 1987, honey. You'd be amazed what a signature can bury."

* * *

That night I did something I'm not proud of, in the sense that I am extremely proud of it and simply aware it was illegal.

I drove to Dr. Pruett's house — a tidy gray cottage near the school, garden immaculate, porch light on a timer — and while he attended his Thursday bowling league (a fact I knew because everyone in Coldharbour knows everything about everyone, which cuts both ways), I let myself into his home office through the unlocked side door that he has never once locked in the eleven years I've lived here, because small towns are safe right up until they very much aren't.

His files were organized with a precision I could have kissed. Patient records by year. And there, in a folder marked only with my father's initials and a date, was the original autopsy worksheet.

The copy in the official file said: *no signs of struggle, consistent with accidental drowning, alcohol a contributing factor.* I'd read it a hundred times. It was clean, bland, final.

This worksheet was different. This was the working document — the one with the doctor's actual observations, scribbled fast, in handwriting that deteriorated down the page like a man losing an argument with himself.

*Contusion, mandibular angle, left. Antemortem. Perimortem?* A question mark, pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly.

Then, lower: *Diatom sample taken — sent?*

And beneath that, in different ink, added later, the sentence that stopped my breath:

*Revised per consultation. Final report to reflect accidental. No further samples retained.*

*Consultation.* With whom?

I photographed every page with shaking hands. On the last sheet, tucked behind the worksheet, was a carbon copy of a check stub — Pruett's own records, the meticulous bookkeeping of a careful man. Dated nineteen days after my father's funeral. Payor: the Ashcombe Family Foundation. Memo line: *consulting services, records review.* Amount: a number with enough zeros in it to buy a small boat, or a large silence.

Pruett hadn't falsified the autopsy for free. He'd falsified it the way everything in this town got done — through the Foundation, through the funnel of Vivienne Ashcombe's generosity, which turned out to have a spout on both ends. Money in for the town. Money out for the town's forgetting.

I stood in the dead doctor's — no, not dead, I corrected myself, the *lying* doctor's — tidy office, surrounded by his bowling trophy and his framed diploma and his photographs of grandchildren, and I understood the shape of the thing at last.

Vivienne Ashcombe had spent thirty years buying this town's funerals, and a town whose funerals you pay for is a town that doesn't ask you questions at the graveside. She'd bought my father's death certificate the same way she'd bought everything else. And now, dying, she'd rewritten her will so that the truth itself became the detonator — murder ruled, inheritance voided — which meant that everyone with a stake in that money had exactly one job left to do:

Keep Odile alive long enough to kill her legally, keep my father drowned forever, and put a brother's face on the whole arrangement.

Outside, headlights swept the driveway. Pruett, home early from league night — rained out, probably, or maybe the universe simply has a sense of comic timing.

I went out the side door, quiet as grief, and sat in my car at the end of his street with my heart hammering and the photographs glowing in my lap.

My phone buzzed. A text from a number I didn't have saved:

*You've read the will. Now you understand why I paid you in advance. — O.*

And beneath it, a second message, arriving thirty seconds later, that turned my blood to harbor water:

*Elias checked into the Harborview Inn tonight. He told the desk clerk he's staying through the reading. He also told her — and she tells everyone, so verify this yourself — that someone called him last week and offered him money to "handle a family matter." He said no.*

*The problem, Wren, is that whoever made the offer doesn't take no for an answer. And they've already chosen the date.*
