## 8. The House on Coldharbour Hill

The house at the top of Coldharbour Hill had always looked to me like a wedding cake left out in the rain — three tiers of white clapboard, icing of gingerbread trim, sagging a little more each decade but refusing, out of sheer stubbornness, to fall. I had delivered death certificates up that drive for twelve years. Vivienne Ashcombe signed them all. She had signed my father's too, back when I was twenty-three and too numb to notice that the town's great benefactor took such a personal interest in paperwork.

Now I climbed the hill one more time, with the exhumation report folded in my coat like a knife.

Marta, the caretaker's housekeeper — no, not caretaker, I reminded myself; Marta was *staff*, and the caretaker was a man named Gideon Roe whom I had never once seen smile — met me at the door. She was small and gray as a moth, and she looked at me the way people look at weather they've been promised.

"She's expecting you," Marta said. "She's been expecting you for two days. She said, 'When the coroner comes, put out the good tea.' I don't know how she knew."

"Everyone in this town knows everything," I said. "It's our chief export."

Vivienne Ashcombe received me in the sunroom, propped in a wingback chair under a blanket the color of oatmeal. Dying had pared her down to essentials: sharp eyes, sharper hands, and a voice like dry paper turning. She was smaller than the monument she'd built herself into over ninety years, and yet the room arranged itself around her anyway. The light came in and seemed to ask her permission.

"Wren Coldharbour," she said. "You have your father's frown. Sit down before you fall down. You look like a man carrying a piano upstairs."

"I have some questions about—"

"The good tea first." She nodded at the tray Marta had left. "Darjeeling. The other kind is for people I'm being polite to."

I sat. I poured. My hands were steadier than I expected them to be, which felt like a small betrayal of my own dread.

"You exhumed Thomas Coldharbour," she said, before my cup touched the saucer. Not a question. "Gideon tells me the ground was disturbed Tuesday last. He notices things. It's why I keep him."

"Gideon Roe keeps a great many things, from what I understand."

Something flickered across her face — amusement, or its fossil record. "Do you know what I own in this town, Wren? Everyone assumes it's the mill, or the bank shares, or the Ashcombe name. It isn't. I own the cemetery."

I set the cup down. "You own the *ground*."

"Sixty acres of it. Every grave, every plot, every body your town has buried since 1911. The Coldharbours are in it. The Harmons. The Delgados. Half the headstones were carved by stonemasons I paid, the plots deeded by lawyers I retained, the funerals catered by a hall whose mortgage I hold." She smiled without warmth, the way winter smiles. "People think grief is what binds a community together. Grief is expensive, Wren. Debt is what binds them. And I have held every family in this town by the graves of their dead for eighty years."

"That's why nobody would reopen my father's case."

"That's why nobody *wanted* to. There's a difference, and you of all people should appreciate it. I never once told anyone to keep quiet. I simply made silence the comfortable option." Her hands folded on the blanket like two birds settling. "You're here to accuse me of murder. Ask me, then. I haven't got all year, though admittedly I've said that every year since 1987."

I took out the report. "The bruising on his jaw predates the drowning by hours. Someone held him under. The diatoms say he went into the water alive. Dr. Pell wrote the autopsy to match an accident, and he was paid — there are entries in a ledger, Mrs. Ashcombe. Payments going back decades. Hush money. Funeral costs. A retainer, recently, paid to me by your granddaughter."

"Odile." She said the name the way you'd spit out a cherry pit. "That child has her grandfather's talent for arithmetic and none of his sense about when to stop counting."

"Did you kill my father?"

And here is the thing I will carry to my own grave, which — given her arrangements — may well be a plot she owns outright: she did not flinch. She did not deny. She looked at me with those ledger-keeper's eyes and said, "Your father came to this house on the fourteenth of October, twelve years ago, soaking wet and furious, and he told me things about my son that I have spent my whole life paying to keep buried. What happened after he left this house is between him, the river, and God."

"That's not a denial."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

"Why give me this?" I asked. "Why now? You could have let the statute run out in nine days and taken it all with you."

For the first time, the old woman looked tired — genuinely tired, the performance stripped off like paint. "Because I am dying, Wren, and I find I mind less about the truth than I minded about the *cost* of the truth. Those are different weights. When you're young you carry the cost. When you're dying you finally feel the weight." She reached beside her chair, slow as a drawbridge, and produced an envelope, thick and official, sealed with wax because of course it was. "The deed. Recorded, notarized, and a copy already filed with the county clerk this morning. If I waited for my death, it would go through probate, and probate goes through lawyers, and my lawyers answer to my family. This way it answers to you."

"Why me?"

"Because you correct accidents." She almost smiled. "Three of them, quietly, over the years. You thought nobody noticed. I noticed everything in this town, dear. It was my full-time occupation." She pushed the envelope across the table between us. "Take the cemetery. Bury whoever you like in it. Dig up whoever you must. Let the dead be evidence instead of collateral. Consider it my only apology — and note that I have not actually apologized."

I took the deed. It weighed nothing and everything.

"One more thing," she said as I stood. "Gideon will be upset about this. Gideon has managed my affairs for thirty years, and men who manage affairs begin to believe the affairs belong to them. Watch him. And watch Dr. Pell — a man who falsifies one autopsy will falsify another if the price changes." She waved a hand like a queen dismissing a court. "Go home, coroner. Mind the road. It's going to be a long night, and I say that with the confidence of a woman who has watched this town's long nights arrive on schedule for nine decades."

* * *

I should have gone straight home. Instead I drove to Odile's rented cottage on Larkspur Lane, deed on the passenger seat like a witness, and found her light on and her door — for the first time since I'd known her — unlocked.

"You left it open," I said, when she opened it.

"I left it *ajar*. There's a difference, and you of all people—" she began, then stopped, because we had both heard ourselves become each other lately. "Come in. I've been reading Grandmother's ledgers again. Did you know she paid the Harmon family's funeral costs twice? Same names, eleven years apart. I can't decide if that's tragedy or bookkeeping."

"It's both. That's usually what both is." I handed her the news of the deed, and watched her read it twice. "She gave me the cemetery, Odile. Every grave. She handed me the leash the whole town was wearing and just... gave it away."

Odile sat down slowly on the arm of her sofa. For a woman who had orchestrated a retainer, an exhumation, and a countdown clock with the precision of a Swiss train, she suddenly looked very young. "She's clearing the board," she said. "Grandmother never gives anything away. She repositions."

"Or she's telling the truth for the first time in her life."

"People like her don't tell truths. They release them strategically, like dam water." But her hands were trembling slightly, and I noticed, because noticing is my profession. "Wren. Why are you really here?"

"Because I didn't want you alone tonight. Because Vivienne warned me about Gideon Roe, and Gideon Roe has been parked outside your cottage twice this week — yes, I checked, it's a habit — and because in nine days either the statute closes or someone closes you." I paused. "Also because I make excellent coffee and terrible decisions, and tonight calls for both."

That earned me the closest thing to a laugh she'd produced in weeks. We drank the coffee. I checked her locks, her windows, the loose board on the porch that creaked like a confession. Around eleven I prepared to leave, hating it, and she stopped me at the door.

"Wren. Whatever happens tonight — thank you. Not for the investigation. For knocking."

I was halfway down Larkspur Lane when I heard the glass break behind me.

I was back at her door in six seconds flat, which I mention not as heroism but as arithmetic. The side window was shattered inward. A figure in dark clothes was already through it, moving toward Odile with something in one hand — not a gun, I registered with a coroner's cold clarity, something quieter — and Odile, bless her precise, calculating heart, had not frozen. She had thrown the ledgers. All of them. A blizzard of seventy years of numbered pages, and the intruder faltered for exactly one page's worth of time, which was enough for me to hit him at the knees with a cast-iron doorstop I still maintain I picked up on instinct.

He went down hard. I got a knee between his shoulder blades and my flashlight beam on his face, and it wasn't Gideon Roe. It was younger, stranger, a face I half-knew from somewhere wrong — and he laughed, actually laughed, breath wheezing under my weight.

"You're too late, coroner," he said. "This part was never about her."

Then Odile screamed — not at him, but past us both, out the broken window toward the street — and when I turned to follow her eyes, headlights were swinging away fast down Larkspur Lane, and my phone was buzzing in my pocket.

Dispatch. I answered with my free hand, pinning the stranger, watching Odile gather her scattered ledgers like a woman gathering her own spilled bones.

"Wren." Deputy Alvarez's voice, tight and strange. "We need you out on Route 9. It's Dr. Pell." A pause I didn't like. "Looks like he hung himself in his garage about an hour ago. Except—" another pause, the kind I've heard a hundred times and never once wanted to hear, "—except his shoes are on the wrong feet, Wren. Nobody hangs themselves and puts their shoes on the wrong feet first."

Under my knee, the stranger laughed again, soft as water closing over something.

Nine days to the statute. One witness dead. One attacker caught — or delivered. And somewhere on Route 9, a garage door waiting to be raised on the town's second murder in twelve years, committed by the same patient hands.

I looked down at the man beneath me and asked him the only question that mattered.

"Who taught you to wait like that?"

He smiled up into the flashlight, and said, "She did. Right up until the day she decided to stop needing me."
