## 10. Inheritance

The ruling came down on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were when my father used to take me fishing, back when he was alive and the town was smaller and I believed that paperwork, done properly, could hold back the dark.

Judge Alvarado read the amended finding aloud in a courtroom that smelled of lemon polish and old carpet: *the death of Thomas Coldharbour, previously ruled accidental drowning, is hereby amended to homicide by asphyxiation.* Her voice didn't shake. Mine would have. I kept my hands flat on the table in front of me and looked at the wood grain and thought, absurdly, *he would have hated the tie I'm wearing.*

Beside me, Odile sat very straight, her pale hands folded, her face arranged into the particular stillness of a person who has spent a long time preparing for a moment and finds, when it arrives, that no preparation is sufficient. When the gavel came down she exhaled — one small breath, like a pin being drawn from a wound.

Outside on the courthouse steps, the town had assembled the way towns do: half of them to gawk, half of them to mourn, and a healthy minority to decide, on the spot, which version of history they'd been telling themselves for twelve years they could live with now.

Vivienne Ashcombe had died in March, lucid to the end and unrepentant to the last breath, which I suppose is one way to go. The caretaker — her son, though the birth certificate said otherwise and the DNA said *otherwise louder* — was in the county jail awaiting trial, his confession sitting in evidence like a stone in a shoe. The doctor's "suicide" had been reopened too. The truth, once it started moving, turned out to be terribly difficult to stop.

"You're scowling," Odile said.

"I'm thinking."

"Your thinking face and your scowling face are the same face. I've had a year to study it."

"A year," I said. "It's been six weeks since the boathouse."

"Time is a ledger," Odile said, "and I keep it closely." She pulled her coat tighter against the October wind. "Come on. We have graves to dig."

* * *

Well — not dig, exactly. Fund.

The first letter arrived the week after the ruling, on cream paper with a letterhead Odile had designed herself: *The Coldharbour Review*, which she insisted was named after me and which I insisted, to no effect, was named after my father. It offered, in careful legalese, to underwrite the exhumation and reexamination of every death in Harrow County over the past forty years that had been signed off as accident, misadventure, or act of God.

There were nineteen of them. I knew, because I'd spent years keeping my own quiet list — the ones the police waved through, the ones where the paperwork was *too* tidy, the ones where Vivienne Ashcombe's funeral fund had quietly covered the costs. Nineteen names. I'd written them in the back of my father's old tide almanac, in pencil, because I'd never quite had the courage to do it in ink.

The morning after Odile's letter arrived, I went out to my truck, retrieved the almanac from the glovebox, and inked them. All nineteen. It took me four minutes and my hand only shook a little.

"Which one first?" Odile asked. She'd taken to showing up at my office with coffee and no announcement, like weather.

I'd thought about it for a long time, and the answer surprised me. "Marta Voss."

Odile tilted her head. "The schoolteacher. 1987?"

"Fell down her own stairs. Broke her neck. Except Marta Voss taught gymnastics for thirty years and had the balance of a cat, and her stairs were four steps with a banister on both sides." I turned the almanac toward her. "And her funeral was paid for by the Ashcombe Fund. First one ever, actually. Before my father."

Odile looked at the entry for a long moment. "She knew something," she said quietly. "About Grandmother."

"Maybe. Maybe she just knew about the money. Either way, she's waited thirty-seven years, and she's waited long enough." I capped the pen. "We start with her."

"You're going to be good at this," Odile said, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before — not calculation, for once. Something almost like hope.

"Don't say things like that. I'll get a big head and start charging for my scowls."

* * *

Elias left on a Thursday in early November, and he did it properly, which is to say he came to the cemetery first.

He found me by our father's grave, where I was doing the thing I do most mornings now — checking the stone, clearing the leaves, having a one-sided conversation that I have decided counts as closure even if no licensed therapist would sign off on it.

"Hey, Wrennie."

I didn't turn around. "You still can't call me that. You're forty-one."

"And you're still the only person whose opinion I'm scared of, so." He came and stood beside me, hands in the pockets of the good coat he'd bought with — I suspected — the last of his settlement money, and we looked at the stone together. *THOMAS E. COLDHARBOUR, 1958–2012. BELOVED. MISSED. TRUTH TOLD AT LAST.* The last part had been Odile's suggestion for the addition. Elias had wanted *AT PEACE.* We'd compromised by putting both, and then the stonecutter ran out of room, so it just says *TRUTH TOLD,* which Elias claims sounds like a threat and I claim sounds like an epitaph, and we have agreed to disagree for the rest of our natural lives.

"I'm taking the job in Duluth," Elias said. "Warehouse management. It's not glamorous."

"Neither is being alive. Take it."

He laughed, and then he didn't, and then he crouched down and put his palm flat on the cold granite the way our father used to put his palm on our heads when he checked us for fevers. "I keep thinking about that night," he said. "The night he drowned. I was seventeen. I saw Vivienne's car on the lake road and I never said anything, because why would I? She was *Vivienne Ashcombe.* Saying something would've been like saying the lake was on fire." His voice cracked in the middle. "I let it be an accident because an accident was easier."

"I know," I said. "Me too."

"You didn't see anything."

"No. But I read the autopsy at nineteen and I noticed the water didn't match, and I put it back in the drawer, because a drawer is easier than a war." I looked at him. "We were kids, Elias. They built a whole town on making it easy for us. That's what she was good at."

He stood up. His eyes were wet and he didn't bother hiding it, which was new, and which I decided to accept as part of his inheritance too. "You forgive me?"

"I forgave you somewhere around the boathouse, when you put yourself between that man and Odile without a weapon and without a plan." I shrugged. "It was a stupid thing to do. It was also the right thing. I've decided that counts."

He hugged me. It was brief and awkward and one of us got granite dust on the other's coat, and neither of us mentioned it.

"Write to me," he said, getting into his truck. "Actual letters. I want to hear about the exhumations. All of them. Even the gross parts."

"Especially the gross parts. You've changed, Elias."

He grinned, and for a second he was seventeen again, and then he was gone, taillights shrinking down the hill road, and I turned back to the grave and finished my conversation with our father.

*He's forgiven,* I told him. *I know you'd have wanted that. I also know you'd have wanted me to keep the receipts, and I have. Both things are true. You taught me that.*

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first real snow arriving the week before Thanksgiving, and I spent it the way I intended to spend all my winters now: underground, metaphorically speaking.

Marta Voss's exhumation was in December. Her neck, it turned out, had two fractures, and one of them was from the front. The second name on my list was a drowning — a fisherman, 1994, ruled "misadventure," and when we opened the records we found the Ashcombe Fund had paid his widow a settlement two weeks after the funeral, with a confidentiality clause so broad it covered, as far as I could tell, the widow's own name. By February, the county prosecutor had opened a cold case unit with Odile's money and my reluctant, unpaid, extremely scowly consultation.

The town took it the way towns take everything: in stages. First there was anger — at me, mostly, for digging, though a few enterprising citizens managed to be angry at Odile for paying for the digging, which I thought showed initiative. Then there was the strange, chastened quiet of people rereading their own memories. Mrs. Halloran, who ran the diner, started putting a free slice of pie in front of me every time I came in, which I understood to be an apology, an offering, and a bribe all at once, and I accepted it as all three.

"You know what's funny," Odile said one evening, in my office, surrounded by nineteen folders and the wreckage of two large pizzas. "Grandmother spent fifty years buying this town's silence. And it turns out the truth was cheaper. All it cost was one stubborn coroner and a year's retainer."

"You overpaid," I said. "I'd have done it for the pie."

"No, you wouldn't have. You'd have done it for free and starved, which is why I overpaid." She looked at the folders, and her voice went softer. "Do you ever think about what she left me? All of it. The house, the money, the ground under the cemetery. I own the hill now, Wren. I own every grave."

"I know. I read the deed."

"And it doesn't frighten you? That I could be her?"

I considered it honestly, because she deserved that. "It frightened me for about a week," I said. "Then I watched you spend four hundred thousand dollars digging up dead strangers to give their families answers nobody asked you to give. Vivienne spent her whole life making sure the ground stayed shut. You're spending yours opening it." I shrugged. "You inherited the money. You didn't inherit her."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me," in a voice that suggested it might also be the only thing.

"Don't get sentimental. It's the pizza talking."

* * *

On the first anniversary of the ruling — a Tuesday, naturally — I did the rounds.

I checked Marta Voss's new stone, which her grandniece had finally been able to afford to replace, and which now says *TEACHER, GYMNAST, MURDERED, REMEMBERED,* because her family had requested the stonecutter leave nothing out and the stonecutter, a practical man, had complied. I checked the fisherman's grave, and the others as we got to them, nineteen names becoming fewer every season, my pencil list slowly turning into case files and case files slowly turning into amended certificates, the whole quiet machinery of the truth grinding forward at the speed of bureaucracy, which is to say: slowly, but with terrible patience.

Then I climbed the hill to my father's grave, and I sat down in the snow beside it, because the ground was cold but I've never minded cold, and I told him everything. About Elias's letters from Duluth, which arrive monthly and are getting longer. About Odile, who had stopped by that morning to drop off coffee and a folder labeled *TWENTY* — a twentieth name, from before even Marta Voss, one I'd never found, and she had, in a ledger, in her grandmother's own handwriting. About the pie. About the way the town had begun, in its slow, stubborn, unfashionable way, to heal — not by forgetting, but by finally, finally remembering out loud.

The sun was going down over Coldharbour Hill, gilding the snow, gilding the stones, all those names in all that light.

People think an inheritance is what you're left in a will. Money, houses, ground. But I've stood at enough gravesides to know better. An inheritance is whatever you carry out of the dark and refuse to put down. My father left me a tide almanac with nineteen names in pencil. Odile left herself a ledger and the nerve to read it. Elias left with forgiveness, which weighed more than he expected and fit in the truck anyway.

And me — I stayed. Keeper of the graves, keeper of the list, keeper of the truth, which is heavy, and cold, and mine.

I brushed the snow off the stone, patted it twice — the way he used to pat our heads — and headed down the hill, where the porch light was on, and the coffee was still warm, and tomorrow there was work to do.
