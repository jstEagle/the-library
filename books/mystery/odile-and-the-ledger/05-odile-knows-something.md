## 5. Odile Knows Something

The dying teach you to read faces. That was the first thing the work gave me, before the steady hands and the ability to say *comfort* without flinching: a fluency in the small weather of other people's expressions. You learn that fear doesn't always look like fear. Sometimes it looks like tidiness. Sometimes it looks like a woman folding the same towel four times because her hands need a job while her mind runs somewhere dark.

So when Lior came into the break room at Bellwether Hospice on Tuesday morning with their tie knotted slightly wrong—half an inch too long, which Lior never did, not once in the six years we'd worked overlapping shifts—I knew before they said a word.

"You're staring," Lior said.

"I'm assessing." I handed them a coffee from the pot nobody trusted but everybody drank. "You look like you slept in your car."

"I slept in my office. There's a difference, technically."

"Uh-huh." I let them drink. In hospice you learn timing too—when to push, when to sit quietly beside someone until the truth gets bored of hiding. Lior had taught me half of that, back when I was nineteen and couldn't look at a body without my throat closing. The other half I'd learned on my own, in rooms where people were leaving and everyone pretended they weren't.

Lior sat down across from me, cradling the paper cup like it was warm, though it had gone cold twenty minutes ago. Their eyes had that particular shine—not tears, exactly, but the residue of something almost cried out or almost said.

"Can I ask you something strange?" they said.

"People ask me stranger. Last week Mr. Adeyemi asked if his late wife could haunt him gently, just on Sundays."

That got half a smile out of them. It faded fast. "When you were little. Before Bess. Do you remember much about—about before?"

The room went very quiet around the question. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere down the hall, Mrs. Calloway's oxygen concentrator breathed its slow mechanical tide.

"Why?" I asked, and I heard it come out too flat, too quick, the way a door sounds when it closes a beat early.

Lior noticed. Of course they noticed; they were raised by the same dead I was, more or less, in the same house full of the same careful silences. But they didn't press. They just turned the cup in their hands and said, "No reason. Forget it. Long week."

It wasn't no reason. I'd bet my license on it. And I would have been right to bet, because later that same day I saw what they'd been carrying around all morning, tucked in the inner pocket of their bag like contractions: two envelopes, cream-colored, postmarked in faded blue ink from a town called Meridian Flats—a town I knew hadn't run mail since the mill closed, since before I was old enough to know what a postmark even meant.

I saw them because Lior left their bag open on the counter while they went to sit with the Delgado family, and because I have spent ten years being the kind of person who notices things and says nothing. It is practically my profession now. Noticing and silence. Hospice pays for both.

I shouldn't have looked. I looked anyway.

* * *

Here is what I will tell you about guilt, since apparently this is the season for confessions: it does not feel the way books describe it. It isn't a weight on the chest or a stone in the stomach. For me it has always been a performance problem. Guilt is the exhausting daily work of seeming normal when every cell in your body wants to run—to pack a bag, to change your name, to get on a bus going anywhere the roads don't lead back here. I have been performing normalcy so long and so well that I genuinely don't know anymore which parts of me are real and which parts are set dressing. Maybe that's what everyone's life is. Maybe most people just get to find out gradually, over decades, instead of all at once at fourteen years old in the back of a station wagon with plastic over the seats.

The handwriting was the thing. That's what I need you to understand, if you're going to judge me—and you will, everyone does eventually, it's just a question of how long I get before the verdict comes down.

The handwriting on those envelopes was Marisol Vance's.

I stood at the counter with the envelopes three feet away and my whole body went cold and then hot and then cold again, the way it does when you're standing at a graveside in November. Because I knew that hand. I knew the way she crossed her sevens with a little horizontal slash, the way her capital M's looked like folded birds, the way she pressed hard enough on the page that you could read her letters from the back, like braille, like something trying to get through from the other side. I used to trace them with my finger when I was small enough that tracing was still allowed. She wrote me notes. Little ones, folded into quarters, tucked under my pillow when I stayed at her place while my mother worked doubles: *Eat something green today. You are braver than the dog thinks.* Things like that. Things a child keeps in a shoebox until keeping them becomes dangerous.

Marisol Vance died ten years ago. I stood at her funeral. Well—"stood near." Bess kept me in the kitchen of the funeral home that day, gave me a task, dishes or flowers or some such, anything to keep my face away from the open casket and away from the handful of mourners who came, and away from Lior, who was already coroner by then and who signed the certificate with that careful coroner's hand of theirs.

And now her handwriting was coming through the mail. Addressed to Lior. Postmarked from a ghost town.

I put the bag down exactly as I'd found it and walked to the supply closet and stood there among the gauze and the gloves and the boxes of morphine with my forehead against the shelf, breathing the way they teach you to breathe with panicking families—in through the nose, four counts, hold, out through the mouth—until my hands stopped shaking.

Then I went back to work. I smiled at the Delgados. I checked Mrs. Calloway's pulse and told her daughter she was comfortable, which was true, and that it wouldn't be long now, which was also true, and which is the only kind of lie I'm licensed to tell.

* * *

That night I burned a photograph in my sink.

I want to be honest about how deliberate it was, because there's a version of this story where I lose control, where grief or panic makes me do it, and that version lets me off the hook. This wasn't that. I took the photograph out of the false bottom of my jewelry box—the one Bess installed for me when I was fifteen, along with the instruction that some things should be able to disappear quickly—and I carried it to the kitchen, and I ran the tap, and I held a match to the corner of it, and I watched it go.

It was a photograph of Marisol. Not the one from the funeral program—that one was official, soft-focus, her smile arranged for posterity. This one was candid, taken in someone's yard, summer light, her head thrown back laughing at something outside the frame. Her hair was dark then, before whatever happened to her happened. She wore a yellow sundress and one of those thin gold chains women wore in the nineties, and behind her, blurred and half-cropped, was the edge of a porch I recognized. The Thornes' porch. Elias Thorne's porch, though I didn't know his name yet when the picture was taken, didn't know any of the names yet. At fourteen I knew only that the woman in the photographs was not to be mentioned, and that the not-mentioning had a weight to it, a gravity, like a planet you're not allowed to name but whose pull organizes your entire orbit.

Why burn it? Because Lior was asking questions. Because Lior had asked me *do you remember much about before*, and because Lior does not ask questions without a reason, and because the reason was sitting in their bag in cream-colored envelopes in a dead woman's hand. If they were pulling threads, I needed to know which threads led to me. And a photograph of Marisol Vance, laughing on the Thorne porch, in the possession of the girl Bess smuggled out of the county under a fabricated surname—that thread led directly, straight as a survey line, to my door.

The photograph curled and blackened. The tap water hissed against the embers. I watched Marisol's laugh dissolve edge-first, the yellow dress going brown, going black, going to flakes that swirled down the drain, and I felt—nothing clean. Relief, maybe, mixed with a self-disgust so sharp it tasted metallic, like sucking on a coin. Ten years ago I had stood silent while they buried a coffin. Tonight I stood silent at my own sink destroying evidence, and the skill was the same skill, and I had gotten better at it, and that was the part that made me sick. Not the burning. The ease.

Mrs. Calloway died that Thursday, peaceful, her daughter holding one hand and me holding the other, and I said the things I always say—*she's not suffering, she's surrounded by love*—and meant every word, and drove home, and lay awake until three a.m. listening to my own heartbeat and thinking: *this is what I do now. I am good at endings. I have made a whole career of helping people leave gracefully.*

Somewhere in the dark, the thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do: *Lior buried the wrong woman.*

I don't know how I knew it. I didn't know it the way you know a fact. I knew it the way you know weather is coming—in the ache of old injuries, in the pressure behind the eyes. Something about those envelopes had cracked something open in Lior, and Lior had spent their whole life believing the things they signed, and Marisol Vance's handwriting does not come back from the dead unless somebody lied to the coroner, and the only person positioned to lie to the coroner about a burial in Vesper Hollow was family. Was Bess. Was the woman who taught me which silences mattered.

Which meant the letters weren't just letters. They were a detonation with a long fuse, lit ten years ago, burning toward everything I loved.

* * *

Friday, Lior came by the house. Not the funeral home—my house, the little rental on Corvus Lane with the crooked porch step, which Bess pretends not to disapprove of and checks on anyway when I'm on shift. They brought a casserole, which is how our family says *something is wrong*, because none of us can say it out loud. Aunt Bess's recipe. Squash and cheese and a butter-cracker crust that could stop traffic.

"Eat it or freeze it, either way," Lior said, handing it over. They hovered on the porch. Lior never hovers. Lior is the person who walks into hospital rooms like they own the air in them, calm as deep water. Watching them hover on my porch step was like watching a lighthouse sway.

"Come in," I said. "Tell me what's actually happening, because you've been carrying those envelopes around like they're live rounds, and you asked me about *before*, and I've known you since I was fourteen, Lior Almeida, and you are a terrible liar."

"I'm an excellent liar."

"You're an excellent *quiet*. It's different." I stepped back to let them in. "Casserole goes in the fridge. Talk."

They talked around it for a while—the way you do, the way we all learned to do in that house. Filing permits. A records discrepancy. An irregularity in an old burial. Each sentence was a stone placed carefully so as not to disturb the water, and I nodded at each one and performed my listening face, the good one, the one that says *go on, I'm with you*, while inside me a voice screamed *stop digging, stop digging, you don't know what's under there.*

But here's the thing about Lior, the thing I forget at my peril: they are patient the way water is patient. They set down their fork and looked at me across my own kitchen table and said, gently, terribly:

"The handwriting on the confession is Marisol Vance's."

The refrigerator hummed. The clock over the stove ticked. I had prepared for this—I had spent three days preparing for this, rehearsing in the shower, in the car, over dying patients' bedsides—and when it finally came, my preparation evaporated and what came out of my mouth was:

"Who?"

One syllable. One stupid, reflexive syllable, and I watched it land. I watched Lior's face do the thing faces do in hospice when a family member asks *what do you mean the will* —the slow settling, the recalibration, love and suspicion arriving at the same table and sitting down together like old acquaintances.

"Marisol Vance," Lior said again, softer. "You knew her, Odile. You were fourteen when she died. Bess took you in that same year. I've never once asked you why, because it wasn't my business, because you were a scared kid and Bess said the past was the past." They turned their coffee mug a quarter turn on the table. "But I'm asking now. Not as the coroner. As your brother. What aren't you telling me?"

And God help me, I looked at them—looked at this person who had taught me to drive stick, who sat with me through my first code, who brings squash casserole to a rental house on Corvus Lane because that's how our broken little family says *I love you*—and I performed normalcy one more time. I felt myself do it from the outside, almost, the way you watch your own hands work during CPR. My shoulders dropped. My brow softened. I let my eyes go wet, which wasn't even acting, which was the cruelest part.

"Lior," I said, "I was a foster kid who lost her mother and landed in a funeral home. There's nothing under that but sad. I barely remember Marisol Vance. She was—" I searched, and found, and hated myself for how easily I found it— "she was nice to me. That's all I've got."

The silence after it lasted four seconds. I counted. Four seconds is nothing in a hospice room and a geological age at a kitchen table.

Then Lior nodded, and stood, and thanked me for the company, and said the casserole smelled great, and hugged me at the door with arms that felt like a question I refused to answer.

After their taillights faded down Corvus Lane, I washed the two plates we'd used, dried them, put them away. Then I went to the bathroom and threw up, quietly, thoroughly, like a professional.

Because here is what I hadn't counted on, what I should have: Lior had said *confession*. Singular. One letter. Which means the second letter—the promise, the one about *the rest of the ledger*—was still folded away somewhere in that bag, unmentioned, held back the way you hold back a card. And Lior Almeida holds cards only when they already know what the other player is hiding.

They weren't asking me what I knew.

They were letting me show them.

I stood at my sink—over the drain where Marisol Vance's laughter had gone down in flakes and ash—and understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the point where it can do any good, that the performance was over. That the letter had named a victim, or would soon. That the statute clock Alma Reyes liked to lecture about at the library was ticking somewhere under all of this. And that sooner or later—eleven weeks, or eleven days, or the next time Lior knocked on my door with their coroner's eyes and their terrible patience—someone was going to ask me what I saw when I was fourteen years old on the Thorne property, and this time I was going to have to decide whether the girl who stayed silent was someone I still wanted to be.

I took the jewelry box out of the closet and opened the false bottom.

It was empty except for one item I'd forgotten: a note, folded in quarters, creased soft as cloth from a decade of handling. *You are braver than the dog thinks,* it said, in folded-bird capitals, pressed hard enough to read from the back.

Underneath the fold, in smaller writing I had never noticed before—or had never allowed myself to notice—were four more words:

*If they ask, tell the truth.*

Someone pounded on my front door.
