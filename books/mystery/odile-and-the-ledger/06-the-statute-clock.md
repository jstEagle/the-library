## 6. The Statute Clock

Alma Reyes lived at the end of Corvid Lane in a house that had once been the county's first courthouse, back when Vesper Hollow still bothered to hold trials instead of simply deciding things over pie. Lior knew this because everyone in town knew it; Alma had bought the building at auction thirty years ago and never let anyone forget what it had been. The brass plaque by her door still read *JUSTICE OF THE PEACE*, though the office beneath it was now a sunroom full of orchids and a filing cabinet she jokingly called "the appeals court."

Lior sat across from her at a table scarred with coffee rings, the two envelopes laid flat between them like specimens under glass.

Alma read the first letter twice. She read it a third time with her reading glasses pushed up onto her forehead, which Lior had learned, over years of county meetings and funeral potlucks, meant she wasn't reading anymore—she was thinking. Her hands were brown and spotted and absolutely steady, the hands of a woman who had spent four decades signing papers that ended arguments and started others.

"Marisol Vance," she said finally. "I remember the funeral. Small. Bess did it right—she always does it right." She set the letter down and aligned its edge with the table's grain, a small compulsive courtesy to order. "You're certain about the postmark."

"Certain as I can be. San Simeon Station closed in 2011. The route was discontinued two years after that. I called the regional office this morning before you were awake." Lior kept their voice level, the coroner's voice, the one they used for families who needed facts more than comfort. "The clerk laughed at me. Then he checked, and stopped laughing. He said mail hasn't originated from that station in over a decade. Whatever stamped these letters did it years ago, or somewhere else entirely."

"And the second one?"

Lior slid it across. *Find R. Bring me my witness. The rest of the ledger is yours when the clock runs out—or before it does.*

Alma read it once. That was enough. When she looked up, her eyes had changed—not softer, not harder, but narrower, the way light narrows through a closing door.

"You came to me because I'm retired," she said. "You came to me because whatever I tell you today can't be subpoenaed, can't be repeated under oath, and can't get anyone in this town nervous enough to start making phone calls. Smart. Also insulting, but smart."

"I came to you because you're the only person in three counties who will tell me the truth without asking first whose feelings it might bruise."

That earned the ghost of a smile. Alma Reyes had been famous for exactly that, right up until the day she'd handed down a sentence against the Hollis Timber Company's third foreman and found her docket mysteriously cleared of every case that touched Hollis interests for the following year. She'd retired six months later, telling anyone who asked that she wanted time for her orchids. Nobody had believed her then either.

"All right," she said. "Let's talk about clocks."

* * *

She got up, went to the filing cabinet she called the appeals court, and pulled out a manila folder so thin it was nearly translucent with age. From it she took a single sheet of legal pad paper, yellowed at the edges, covered in her own cramped handwriting. She smoothed it on the table between them like a dealer laying down a card.

"General rule," she began, tapping the page. "In this state, murder has no statute of limitations. None. A confession to homicide is prosecutable the day it's written and the day the writer dies—though I'll grant you, prosecuting a dead woman presents certain logistical challenges." Her finger moved down the page. "So if all we had was letter number one—a confession to killing Elias Thorne in 1998—the law would say: fine, charge her. Posthumously. Try a corpse. Good luck getting a jury to care, and better luck getting a conviction that means anything to anyone."

"But that's not all we have."

"That is not all we have." Alma looked at Lior over the rims of her glasses, and for a moment Lior felt like a first-year clerk again, twenty-two years old and terrified, though they had never actually been a clerk and had never worked a day inside a courtroom. Some people carried that authority the way others carried scent. "Because your dead woman didn't just confess. She made a promise. And promises, in the eyes of the law, are contracts—and contracts have teeth."

"The ledger."

"The ledger. Or whatever 'the rest of the ledger' turns out to be. Listen carefully, because this is where it stops being a ghost story and starts being a machine." She turned the legal pad around so Lior could read her own notes from some long-ago case. "Fraud. Forgery. Falsification of records. Theft by deception. Obstruction. Those charges carry limitations periods—typically five to seven years depending on how they're charged. If Marisol Vance helped bury Elias Thorne in 1998 and helped erase him from every record in this county, those crimes expired sometime around 2005. Statutorily dead. Buried deeper than the man himself."

"So there's nothing."

"There'd be nothing," Alma agreed, "if the fraud had ended. But fraud has a beautiful property, from a prosecutor's standpoint: it doesn't necessarily die when the act ends. It dies when the *concealment* ends. If someone has continued—actively, recently—to maintain the lie, to profit from it, to enforce it, then each day of concealment can be argued as a fresh act. A continuing offense. And here—" she tapped the second letter again, "—here is the part that should keep you up tonight. Your correspondent isn't just promising evidence. She's naming a witness. And a witness changes everything."

"How?"

"Because of something we call tolling. When a witness to a crime comes forward and gives testimony or a sworn statement material to an ongoing scheme of concealment, the clock doesn't just resume—it can reset, in whole or in part, for the related charges. The fraud against the estate. Against whoever's estate held that land. Against the county itself, if records were falsified here." Alma leaned back. "A live witness transforms a dead confession into a living case. Suddenly you're not prosecuting a memory. You're prosecuting a conspiracy that, arguably, never stopped conspiring."

Lior sat with that. Outside Alma's window, the orchids glowed in their humidity, absurdly lush against the gray morning, and somewhere down Corvid Lane a dog was barking at nothing, the way dogs in Vesper Hollow always barked at nothing—as if the town itself were trained to alert at phantoms no one else could see.

"R," Lior said. "The witness. Who signs an affidavit in 2004 and then vanishes?"

"Someone the county system swallowed." Alma's voice flattened. "I've seen it a hundred times. A frightened person, often young, often with no family leverage, gets committed, transferred, relocated—papered into invisibility. Once someone's inside that machinery, they stop existing as far as most people are concerned. They become a bed number. A transfer record. A signature on a consent form they may or may not have understood." She paused. "Or they leave town quietly and stay gone, which is rarer than you'd think, because leaving requires resources and fear requires none."

"You think R is alive."

"I think a woman who planned her own death ten years ago, who staged a burial convincing enough to fool a competent coroner—" Alma's gaze flicked, briefly, deliberately, to Lior's face, "—does not write letters promising a witness who is dead. Dead witnesses make poor leverage. Whoever wrote that letter believes R can be found. Which means R left a trail, or R was left somewhere findable."

The flick lasted less than a second, but Lior felt it land like a splinter. *A burial convincing enough to fool a competent coroner.* Alma had signed the death certificate's witnessing line herself, ten years ago—one of three signatures required. She would know exactly how convincing it had been. She would know, too, whose signature anchored it.

"Alma." Lior heard their own voice go quiet. "If this goes where it seems to be going—if there's a body under something, if there are falsified certificates, if the concealment is ongoing—what's the deadline? Realistically. Not theoretically."

Alma didn't answer immediately. She rose, went to the window, and stood looking out at her garden with her hands clasped behind her back, a posture Lior recognized from a dozen county hearings: the judge consulting something higher than the bench.

"The Thorne parcel," she said, without turning. "It's being sold. You know that."

"Everyone knows that. The Hollis estate has had it listed since spring."

"Not listed. *Moving.* There's a buyer—a development group out of the state capital, the kind that buys land through three layers of LLCs so nobody has to look at what they're buying. Closing is scheduled." She turned around. "Eleven weeks from Monday. And here is why that matters more than any statute I've quoted you: once that land transfers, everything on it belongs to the new owner. Every inch of soil. Anything buried there becomes theirs to protect, pave, or destroy, and any claim we might press to search it gets tangled in a year of property litigation while the concrete trucks do their work. Evidence doesn't need a statute of limitations to die, Lior. It needs a bulldozer."

"Eleven weeks."

"Eleven weeks to find R. Eleven weeks to get a sworn statement that tolls the clock and gives a judge—I'm assuming not me, I'm retired, but *some* judge with a spine—grounds to freeze the sale pending investigation. After that, the land sells, the trail cools, and the only thing left of Elias Thorne is a confession written by a woman who's already dead." Alma came back to the table and stood over the letters, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Your correspondent chose her timing well. Or she chose it desperately. With women like that, it's usually both."

* * *

Lior stayed another hour. Alma walked them through the mechanics—the affidavit requirements, the difference between information sufficient for a warrant and information sufficient to survive Vivienne Hollis's attorneys, because they both knew, without saying the name aloud, whose money would meet them on the other side of any filing. Alma sketched the chain on the back of the legal pad: witness, statement, tolling, freeze, search, remains, identification, charges. It looked so simple drawn in blue ink, five boxes connected by arrows, the whole architecture of justice reduced to a flowchart a child could copy.

"It never looks like this," Alma said, catching Lior staring at it. "In real life, every arrow is a fight. But it holds. It's held for two hundred years, mostly. That has to be enough for you."

On the porch, in the cold clean air, Lior paused.

"One more question. Off the record, as you say."

"Those are the only kind you get from me."

"The signature on Marisol Vance's burial certificate. Mine. I don't remember signing it. I've gone through my files twice. Everything else from that week is in my hand—the permit, the transit form, the disposition log. But that certificate..." Lior looked out at the street, at the maples dropping their leaves on Corvid Lane like small red confessions. "Either I signed it and forgot, which frightens me, or someone signed it for me, which frightens me more. Is there a version of this where I'm already implicated?"

Alma was quiet for a long moment. An orchid petal had blown onto the porch rail; she picked it up and held it in her palm as if weighing it.

"There's a version of this," she said at last, "where someone who loves you decided you were safer not knowing. And there's a version where someone who owns you decided the same thing. In my experience, the two versions look identical from the outside, and the only way to tell them apart is to follow them to the end." She opened her hand and let the petal go. It skittered off the porch into the wind. "Follow it to the end, Lior. And do it fast. Eleven weeks sounds like a season. It's a heartbeat."

Driving home, Lior passed the funeral home's sign—*BESS'S PLACE, EST. 1962, EVERY GRAVE BY NAME*—and the words snagged on something, some burr of unease they couldn't yet name. Every grave by name. Except, perhaps, the ones that weren't.

That evening, Lior unlocked the archive room below the chapel, the one that smelled of dust and camphor and forty years of other people's endings, and pulled the burial ledgers off the shelf. If the clock was running, they intended to know exactly what it was counting down toward.

They opened the oldest volume to 1971, and the hair on their arms rose before their mind caught up to why: the binding was wrong. Too loose. Too many pages turning too easily, like a mouth missing teeth.

Someone had cut pages out.
