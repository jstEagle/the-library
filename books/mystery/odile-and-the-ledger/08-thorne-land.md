## 8. Thorne Land

The parcel map said forty-one acres, but the land had stopped agreeing with the paper decades ago. Lior stood at the rusted gate on the county road, a legal pad in one hand and the second letter in their coat pocket, and looked out at what the Hollis estate's listing called "prime developable acreage." It was a forest that had been arguing with itself since before Lior was born. Birch and black cherry crowded a lane that might once have been a driveway; bramble had eaten the fence line whole; somewhere in the green dark, water moved without any visible creek to explain it.

The gate itself was the only honest thing on the property. Wrought iron gone to lace, hanging from one hinge, and on it, half-swallowed by a honeysuckle vine, a small brass plate: THORNE. Someone had polished it recently. That was the first wrong note. Everything else here had been left to rot in the ordinary way things rot—slowly, indifferently—but the plate gleamed. Somebody came here. Somebody cared what this gate remembered.

Lior squeezed through the gap where the second hinge had surrendered and let the lane take them.

They had told Aunt Bess they were going into the county seat for filing supplies. It wasn't quite a lie. There were supplies in the truck bed—a box of permit folders they genuinely needed—and Bess had kissed their temple at the door and said, "Mind the potholes on Route 9," which meant she believed them, or wanted to, or was very good at not asking. Lior had been cataloguing, all week, the small ways the people they loved performed belief. It was an ugly skill to be developing. They kept doing it anyway.

The confession letter sat against their ribs like a stone warmed by a body. *Elias Thorne, surveyor, 1998.* A man who had never been reported missing because, as far as Vesper Hollow's records went, he had never existed loudly enough to be missed. Lior had spent two evenings in the annex basement pulling tax rolls, and the picture that emerged was of a man who owned this land outright, paid his taxes in cash, filed no liens, joined no church, attended no town meetings. In a town like Vesper Hollow, that was practically a disguise. You could vanish from a place you never let hold you.

And now the Hollis estate—the estate of Vivienne Hollis, whose charity had paid the funeral home's furnace bills for thirty years—was selling his land.

Lior walked the lane with the particular attention their profession had trained into them: not looking for anything, but refusing to look away from anything. Deer trails braided through the undergrowth. An old stone wall surfaced and sank along the eastern edge like the spine of something swimming. The trees here were older than the ones nearer town, and quieter. Even the birds seemed to conduct themselves at a lower volume, as if the parcel had a reputation.

A quarter mile in, the lane opened into what had clearly once been a dooryard. Lior knew it from the way the ground remembered: a flattening of the grade, a scatter of foundation stones furred with moss, the ghost rectangle of a garden plot still slightly darker than the soil around it, the way old blood is darker than new. A chimney had fallen into itself and become a heap of brick that some patient decade of ivy was slowly taking apart, brick by brick, and carrying away.

This had been a house. Elias Thorne had lived here, alone, keeping his own counsel on his own land, and then one year he hadn't, and nobody in town had asked after him, and the grass had come up over the dooryard like water closing over a dropped coin.

Lior stood in the middle of it and felt the vertigo again—the same one from the exhumation file, from their own signature on a certificate they didn't remember signing. The past wasn't buried here. The past was *load-bearing*. The whole town stood on it, and everyone went about their business upstairs pretending the floors were solid.

That was when they saw the stakes.

*

They were orange, fiberglass, the modern kind with reflective tape, and there were six of them, set in a rough rectangle maybe sixty feet by forty, back from the ruined house site toward a stand of oaks. Fresh surveyor's ribbon fluttered on each one, pink and white, barely faded. Lior crouched at the nearest stake and read the tag: HOLLIS ESTATE / PARCEL 7 / SURVEY DATE — MARCH THIS YEAR.

March. Four months ago. The sale was moving.

But it was the seventh thing—not a stake—that stopped Lior's breath.

Inside the rectangle, the ground had been poured. Concrete. A slab, flat and gray and brutally new, sitting in the middle of twenty-five years of unchecked growth like a dinner plate set down in a meadow. It was perhaps eight inches thick by the exposed edge, troweled smooth, with expansion joints cut in a clean grid. No forms left behind. No construction debris. No tire ruts older than last spring. Whoever built it had come in, done the work, and taken every trace of themselves away except the one thing they'd come to leave.

Lior walked its perimeter slowly, counting paces. Forty by fifty-eight. Big enough for a garage. Big enough for a small barn. Big enough—

Big enough for whatever was underneath it.

They knelt at the south edge where frost heave had already begun its slow argument with the pour, lifting a corner of the slab a quarter inch off the grade, and shone their phone's light into the gap. Dark soil, undisturbed-looking, damp. And threaded through the soil, pale as fish bones, fragments of lime.

Lior knew lime. Every coroner did. It was the old-fashioned answer to a question nobody wanted asked.

Their hands were steady. That was the strange part, the part they would remember later. Their hands did their work while the rest of them stood very far back behind glass, watching. They took photographs—of the slab, the stakes, the ribbon, the lime, the gate with its freshly polished name. They measured the slab with a tape from the truck. They wrote the time in their notebook in their careful coroner's hand, the hand that signed certificates, the hand that had signed Marisol Vance's burial certificate ten years ago without remembering it, and the act of writing steadied them further, because writing was testimony, and testimony was the one weapon the dead could put in a living person's grip.

*Somebody poured this recently,* Lior wrote. *Somebody is hiding something under concrete on land scheduled for sale.*

And then, underneath, because honesty was the discipline that held everything else up:

*Or somebody is hiding something that was already hidden, and is hiding it better.*

Because that was the thought that wouldn't leave them alone. If Vivienne Hollis—or whoever moved for her estate—had killed Elias Thorne in 1998, why wait twenty-five years to pour a slab? Bodies didn't get more dangerous with age; they got safer. No. This read differently. This read like someone who had learned, recently, that something was coming to light—the letters, maybe, though how would anyone know about the letters?—and had moved to seal it. To entomb it under a legal description and a deed transfer, so that by the time anyone thought to dig, the ground would belong to someone else, someone innocent, someone who would need a court order and a reason and a lawyer to open it.

You couldn't destroy evidence on land you didn't own. But you could make sure no one ever got permission to look for it.

Lior straightened, knees cracking, and turned a slow circle inside the stake rectangle. The oaks threw afternoon shade across the slab in long bars, like the slats of a cell door. Somewhere north, faintly, a vehicle passed on the county road and was gone.

They were still standing there, notebook open, when they heard the crunch.

*

It came from the direction of the fallen chimney—a footstep, deliberate, on dry brush. Not a deer. Deer don't weigh enough to snap chokecherry stems. Lior closed the notebook and turned, and their heart did something complicated and unwelcome, because standing at the treeline in canvas work clothes, holding a machete loosely at his side like a man who'd forgotten he was holding it, was Tomas Reyes.

Tomas. Who dug graves for the funeral home. Who had taught Lior, at fifteen, how to set a lowering device and how to read soil moisture by smell. Who had carried Lior on his shoulders at the Founders' Day fair when Lior was seven. Who was, as far as Lior knew, supposed to be at the Kowalczyk interment until four.

"Tomas," Lior said. Their voice came out level. They were almost proud of it.

"Lior." He didn't sound surprised, and that was worse than surprise would have been. His eyes went—took Lior a full second to track it—to the notebook in their hand, then to the slab, then back. "Long walk from the road."

"I could say the same."

"I wasn't walking from the road." He said it simply, without apology, the way he said most true things. He nodded at the dooryard. "I come out here sometimes. Used to be a spring house, back past the oaks. I liked the quiet." He shifted the machete to his other hand. "What brings you?"

"Sale survey," Lior said, and held up the parcel map, which was even true. "Hollis estate's listing Parcel 7."

"Lot of that going around." Tomas looked at the slab the way a mason looks at bad mortar. "Poured this spring. Came out one morning in April and there it was. Didn't hear a truck, didn't see a crew. Land like this, you'd hear them for miles." He paused. "Concrete doesn't pour itself."

"No," Lior agreed. "It doesn't."

The silence between them stretched, and in it Lior heard everything neither of them was saying, arranged like instruments waiting for a conductor. Tomas had been the funeral home's gravedigger for longer than Lior had been alive. Tomas dug every hole in Vesper Hollow, certified or otherwise—and Lior's mind went, with a cold sliding feeling, to Marisol Vance's coffin, to the John Doe with the surgical scars, to the question of who had lowered that box into the ground and whether he had known what it weighed.

"You knew Thorne?" Lior asked. "Elias Thorne."

Tomas was quiet long enough that a jay crossed the clearing and left. "Everybody knew of him. Didn't know him. Kept to himself." He rubbed his jaw with the back of the machete hand, an old habit, careless of the blade. "Odd fellow. Good with numbers. Folks said he kept accounts on everybody." A short laugh that had nothing in it. "Town like this, that's a dangerous hobby."

"He disappeared in '98."

"Did he." Flat. Not a question. Not agreement either. Tomas looked at the slab again, and something moved behind his face—something Lior had seen a hundred times across a hundred open graves and never once on Tomas: the look of a man deciding how much of the truth a living person can carry. "Land's got bad ground, parts of it. Spring-fed. You dig wrong here, you hit water." He met Lior's eyes. "Whatever gets buried wrong in bad ground has a way of coming up."

"Tomas—"

"I've got a hole to finish before dark." He was already turning, machete swinging loose, cutting a path back toward the lane with the ease of a man who had walked this land many more times than *sometimes* implied. At the treeline he stopped, without turning around. "Lior. Whatever you're doing with that notebook—" the machete tip indicated it without looking, "—be careful who sees you writing. Paper remembers. People forget. That's usually the safe arrangement." A beat. "Around here, it isn't."

Then he was gone, and the brush closed behind him, and Lior stood alone in the dooryard of a vanished man, listening to the water move invisibly somewhere under the trees.

*

They stayed another hour. They shouldn't have—every instinct of self-preservation said leave, say nothing, come back with witnesses and a warrant—but the light was going gold and long through the oaks, and the slab sat there like a sealed envelope, and Lior had spent their whole life being the person who opened envelopes.

They circled it twice more. On the second pass, at the northwest corner, they found it: a surveyor's pin, driven flush, but beside it, pressed into the wet concrete before it cured, a mark. Small. Deliberate. A single letter, drawn with a finger or a stylus, in a confident looping hand.

**M.**

Lior stared at it until the shadows ate it.

Marisol Vance, dead ten years, buried by Lior's own signature. Whose handwriting filled two letters on Lior's kitchen table. Whose sister—if the sanatorium thread was real—might be dying in Room 9 of a shuttered hospital an hour east. Who had promised *the rest of the ledger* if Lior could find her witness before the clock ran out.

Eleven weeks, Alma had said. Eleven weeks, and the land was already staked, already listed, already poured over.

Lior walked back down the lane in the failing light, past the gate with its bright brass name, and did not allow themselves to run. But at the truck, before starting the engine, they took out the second letter and read it once more by the dome light, and noticed—for the first time, in the postscript they had read a dozen times and somehow never *seen*—a line beneath the signature, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*Ask the man with the shovel what he buried in April.*

Lior's hands went cold on the wheel.

Tomas had said he found the slab in April. Had said it plainly, easily, the way a man says a thing he's decided to give you—while keeping back everything the thing was attached to.

The engine turned over. Headlights swept the gate, the honeysuckle, the polished brass word THORNE, and Lior drove back toward town with the letter burning in their pocket, understanding that the next conversation at their own supper table would decide whether the family they loved was sheltering them—or burying them.
