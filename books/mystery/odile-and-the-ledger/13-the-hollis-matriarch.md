## 13. The Hollis Matriarch

The invitation came the way weather comes to Vesper Hollow—suddenly, and with everyone already knowing about it before it arrived.

It was a cream-colored card, hand-lettered in a slanted copperplate that belonged to another century: *Mrs. Vivienne Hollis requests the pleasure of Lior Almeida's company for Sunday supper, five o'clock.* No reason given. None needed. In this town, a summons from the Hollis house was not an invitation so much as a fact of the calendar, like the frost date or the church bell.

Lior turned the card over twice, looking for something on the back that wasn't there. Then they set it on the kitchen table beside the two letters from Marisol Vance, which had lived there all week like a pair of unexploded shells.

"You're going," Aunt Bess said from the stove. It wasn't a question. She was stirring something she'd been stirring too long, her back a rigid line under her cardigan.

"It would be rude not to."

"It would be *safer* not to." Bess caught herself, softened it with a laugh that didn't reach her eyes. "Safer for your digestion, anyway. That woman serves venison like it's a test of character."

Lior almost smiled. Almost. "Bess. Why does Vivienne Hollis want me at her table?"

The spoon stopped. For a moment the only sound in the kitchen was the tick of the old wall clock that had been wrong since 1997 and right twice a day since. "Because you're the coroner," Bess said finally, "and because when a Hollis asks, people come." She resumed stirring. "Wear the good jacket."

* * *

Sunday arrived gray and mild, the kind of day that couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Lior drove out past the feed store and the shuttered cinema, past the last of the working farms, up the long gravel drive that wound through sugar maples toward the Hollis house—a white-columned Georgian that had watched over Vesper Hollow for a hundred and forty years and looked like it intended to watch for a hundred and forty more.

Lior had been here before, of course. Everyone had. Vivienne Hollis hosted the winter coat drive in her front hall, the historical society in her parlor, the memorial tea for every deceased notable in her garden room. Her charity—the Hollis Foundation, though everyone just said *Vivienne's money*—had kept the lights on at Almeida & Sons Funeral Home through three lean decades. Had paid for Tomas's equipment when the county wouldn't. Had funded Odile's nursing school with a scholarship nobody remembered applying for.

Every kindness, Lior thought now, pulling the car to a stop beneath the porte-cochère. Every single one. They were starting to look different in the rearview mirror.

A housekeeper let them in, and the door closed behind Lior with the soft, final sound of a bank vault.

* * *

Vivienne Hollis received them in the drawing room, standing by the tall window with a sherry glass held delicately between thumb and forefinger. She was eighty-one years old and carried it like sixty—spine straight, silver hair pinned in a chignon that had probably survived hurricanes, dressed in dove-gray wool with a single strand of pearls. Her face was a landscape of fine lines arranged around two sharp blue eyes that did not miss anything, and had not missed anything, Lior suspected, since roughly 1954.

"Lior Almeida." She extended her free hand. Her grip was dry, cool, and brief. "You look tired. Sit down before you fall down—that's what my mother used to say, and she was never wrong about anything except men."

"Thank you for having me, Mrs. Hollis."

"Vivienne. Anyone who calls me Mrs. Hollis is either selling something or suing me, and I don't intend to do business with you today." She gestured to the sofa opposite her chair and sat herself with the controlled descent of someone who had decided long ago that aging was a negotiation she intended to win. "Sherry?"

"Water, please."

"Of course you drink water. You spend your days among the dead; I imagine you keep yourself very clean." She rang a small bell without raising her voice, and the housekeeper appeared as if from the walls. "Water for our guest. And tell Marta we'll sit when the rolls come up."

While they waited, Vivienne talked—about the maples, about the roofers who were robbing her blind, about Deputy Corliss's grandnephew, whom she called "the boy" with a fondness that made Lior file the name away. It was masterful small talk, unhurried and warm, and Lior understood that it was also a kind of fencing. Vivienne was measuring them. Finding their weight, their balance, where they liked to stand.

The water came. The rolls came. They moved to the dining room, where the table could have seated twenty and was set for three—Vivienne, Lior, and, to Lior's mild surprise, no one else. A private audience, then.

The food was excellent and Lior barely tasted it. Venison, indeed, with juniper and a red currant glaze; potatoes from her own garden; wine the color of garnets that Lior declined and Vivienne did not press. She ate with genuine appetite and talked throughout, and somewhere between the soup and the meat, the talk turned, the way a hawk turns, without any visible effort at all.

"I hear you've been receiving letters," Vivienne said.

Lior set down the fork. Kept the hands steady. Ten years of delivering death notices to grieving families had taught them how to make a face into a locked door. "Small towns," they said. "Nothing stays quiet long."

"No, it doesn't." Vivienne dabbed her mouth with the napkin, folded it, laid it aside. All of it done with the economy of ritual. "And I hear these letters are causing you distress. Postmarked from Copper Bluff, isn't that right? Such a strange thing. There hasn't been a post office in Copper Bluff since the mine closed. I remember when there was—I attended the closing ceremony. Terrible little cake."

"You're well informed."

"I'm *attentive*," Vivienne corrected gently. "There's a difference. Information is what other people give you whether you want it or not. Attention is a discipline." She smiled, and the smile was genuinely lovely, and it made the back of Lior's neck prickle like a change in barometric pressure. "Now. Someone is harassing the town's coroner with letters from a dead woman—or a dead post office, which amounts to the same thing—and I find that I mind. I mind very much. This town runs on trust, Lior. On the sense that things are *kept*. Records, promises, graves. When someone shakes that, they shake everything, and everything includes my family."

"And what exactly would you like me to do about it?"

"Let me help you." Vivienne leaned forward slightly, pearls swinging once against her collarbone and going still. "I have resources. Lawyers, investigators, people who are very good at finding where a letter truly comes from. You're a coroner, dear, not a detective. You deal beautifully with what's *in front* of you—but whoever sent those letters is behind you, and around you, and possibly—" the pause was surgical, "—beside you. Let me take the burden. Give me the letters. I'll have copies made, experts consult them, and within a fortnight you'll know exactly who wants to upset you and why. You can go back to your work. Your work matters. It's some of the most important work in this town, and I've always said so."

There it was. Lior felt the shape of it settle over the table like a net dropped from above, so finely woven it might have been silk, might have been kindness. *Give me the letters.*

"The letters are evidence," Lior said carefully. "If they concern a crime—even an old one—they belong with the sheriff's office. Or the judge. Not in private hands."

Something moved behind Vivienne's eyes. Not anger. Something older and more patient than anger—the expression of a woman watching a chessboard and noting, without alarm, that the game would simply take longer than she'd hoped.

"Evidence," she repeated, tasting the word. "You think there's a crime in them."

"I don't know what's in them yet. That's rather the point."

"Mm." Vivienne picked up her wine glass, remembered Lior wasn't drinking, set it down again. For a moment she looked out the window at the darkening lawn, and her profile went somewhere far away—to 1998, perhaps, or wherever her attention actually lived. When she turned back, the warmth was fully restored, seamless as fresh paint. "You remind me of your mother, did anyone ever tell you? Elena had the same stubborn jaw. The same way of sitting very still when she didn't like a question." She smiled. "She was a wonderful woman. Complicated. This town never quite deserved her."

"My mother is not why I'm here."

"No," Vivienne agreed pleasantly. "But she's why you're *able* to be here. Everything you have, Lior—the house, the trade, the name on the door—it all came through hands, and hands pass things along, and sometimes what they pass along is heavier than the person carrying it knows." She rose, smoothing her skirt, and the dinner was somehow over although neither of them had stood. "Come. Walk the garden with me before it's fully dark. I want to show you the roses."

* * *

The garden was terraced stone and late-season roses gone leggy and wild at the edges, tended by someone skilled enough to keep them blooming into October. The air smelled of cold earth and sweetness. Somewhere below the slope, past a ha-ha and a line of fence Lior hadn't known marked the property edge, the land ran down toward the river—and toward, Lior realized with a small cold turn of the stomach, the old Thorne parcel. From here you could see the survey stakes if you knew where to look. Little orange flags in the dusk, patient as insects.

Vivienne walked slowly, one hand resting on Lior's arm as though they were an escort rather than a suspect, and told stories. About the founding families. About her grandmother, Adela Hollis, "who kept this town's conscience in a book, God rest her, though half of what she wrote down was gossip." About Elias Thorne—said lightly, in passing, the way one mentions weather from a decade ago. *"A surveyor who worked for us briefly. Clever man. Took liberties with accounts, I'm afraid. Left town suddenly. These things happen in small places; people drift."*

Drifted. Lior kept their breathing even. *He drifted into a hole on his own survey land, maybe. Under concrete.*

At the terrace's end Vivienne stopped and turned, and the last light caught her face and made it briefly, startlingly beautiful—the way certain ruins are beautiful.

"I'm going to say one plain thing, and then we'll go in and have coffee and speak of nothing but roses until you drive home safe," she said. "Whoever is sending you those letters wants to hurt this town. Not me—*this town*. Its peace. Its memory. And they will use you to do it, because you are honest, and honest people are the best tools there are; they never notice they're being held." Her hand tightened fractionally on Lior's arm. "So I am asking you—as a friend of your family, as the woman whose foundation has kept your funeral home's doors open through floods and recessions and your aunt's stubborn pride—come to me first. Whatever you find. Whoever claims to speak from the grave. Come to me *first*, and I will protect you from it. That is not a threat, dear. It is the only offer anyone in this valley has the power to make."

The silence after it was enormous. Down the slope, one of the orange survey flags lifted in a gust and lay flat again.

"Thank you for dinner, Vivienne," Lior said. "The venison was extraordinary."

Vivienne studied them for a long moment. Then she laughed—a real laugh, bright and unguarded, and somehow that was worse than everything else.

"Oh, you *are* Elena's child," she said. "Coffee. Come along before the damp gets into your lungs."

* * *

The drive home took eleven minutes and lasted approximately four years.

Lior replayed it all against the windshield: *give me the letters, come to me first, I will protect you from it.* A matriarch's charity offered like a hand and shaped like a cage. And threaded through all of it, casual as lint: Elias Thorne, who took liberties. Marisol Vance, unmentioned but present at every course, sitting invisibly in the third chair. The way Vivienne had said *your work matters* — the way a person praises a guard dog.

By the time the funeral home's porch light swung into view, Lior had filled three mental pages and reached one firm conclusion: Vivienne Hollis knew about the letters, knew their content or feared it, and had just spent two hours trying to buy them with venison and roses.

Which meant the letters were true. Which meant the clock was real.

The house was lit warm against the dusk. Through the kitchen window Lior could see Aunt Bess at the sink, Odile drying dishes beside her, Tomas's truck parked crooked at the curb—he was forever staying for supper and pretending it was an accident. Ordinary. Safe. The whole found family of it glowing like a lantern.

Then Lior noticed Tomas himself, standing at the edge of the yard by the tool shed, not coming inside. He had a flashlight in one hand and an odd, hunched posture, and when he saw the car he raised the light—not waving, signaling—and mouthed something across the dark grass.

Lior parked and crossed the yard fast. Up close, Tomas looked gray. He was holding the shed door open with his shoulder, and beyond him, in the smell of oil and earth, the long-handled grave shovel that had hung on its pegs for thirty years—

The pegs were empty.

"Somebody's been in here," Tomas said quietly. "And Lior—" he swallowed, aimed the flashlight beam at the dirt floor, at a print pressed deep and clean-edged in the clay, a boot tread newer than anything else in that shed, "—whoever it was, they dug something up first. Look at the hole."

At the shed's far corner, neat as a surgical incision, a square of earth had been opened and refilled.
