## 35. The Hearing

The county courthouse in Vesper Hollow had been built the same decade as the funeral home, and both buildings shared a certain funerary dignity—dark wood, high windows, the smell of old varnish and older paper. But where Bess's parlor was warm, the courtroom was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the radiator clanking under the window. It was the cold of a room designed to hold people at arm's length from their own lives. Lior felt it settle over them like a coat they hadn't asked to wear.

They sat in the second row of gallery benches, behind Odile's counsel—a lean, exhausted public defender named Priya Nair whom Alma Reyes had personally requested, over the objections of half the county bar. Beside Lior sat Tomas, who had put on a collared shirt for the first time in anyone's memory and kept tugging at the collar like it was a noose he'd agreed to temporarily. Bess sat on Lior's other side, her hands folded in her lap with the deliberate stillness of a woman who knew that if she let them move, they would shake.

The courtroom was full. That surprised Lior, though it shouldn't have. Word traveled in Vesper Hollow the way water travels through limestone—slowly, invisibly, surfacing where no one expected. Half the town had come to watch. The Hollis pew—the front row on the right, the one Vivienne's family had occupied since the courthouse opened—was occupied by lawyers instead. Six of them, gray-suited, arranged around Vivienne Hollis like crows around something already dead.

Vivienne herself wore lavender. She looked, Lior thought, like she was attending the christening of a grandchild rather than an emergency evidentiary hearing that could end with her indictment. She had nodded at Lior when they entered—*nodded*, the way she did across the sanctuary at St. Brigid's—and Lior had felt the nod land on their skin like frost.

At nine-oh-two exactly, Alma Reyes took the bench.

She was seventy-three and had retired from the circuit court six years ago, but the emergency petition had dragged her back under some obscure provision she'd clearly been waiting her whole career to use. She wore her old robe, which fit her more loosely now, and she surveyed the room the way she used to survey defendants: without hurry, without mercy, and without any interest whatsoever in being impressed.

"This hearing," she said, "concerns the admissibility of testimony and physical evidence relating to the death of Elias Thorne, last seen alive in Vesper Hollow in 1998, and the disposition of human remains recovered from a sealed cellar on the Thorne parcel. Before we begin—" she looked down at the papers before her, then up again, "—I want it noted for the record that I have received four phone calls this week from county officials expressing, quote, *concerns about the propriety of these proceedings*. Those calls are also noted. Let us proceed."

Lior heard the gallery shift behind them—a rustle like wind through dry corn. Alma had just told the entire town, in the politest possible language, that everyone knew whose hand was on the scale, and that the scale didn't care.

Priya Nair rose first. "Your Honor, the State calls Odile Vance."

* * *

Odile came through the side door in a navy dress Lior had never seen before. Her hair was pinned back. She carried nothing—not notes, not water, not the small comforts people bring into rooms that frighten them. She walked to the witness stand the way she walked down the hospice corridors at night, Lior thought: evenly, quietly, with the particular steadiness of someone who has decided that panic is a luxury scheduled for later.

Her hand shook only once, when she raised it to be sworn. Then it stopped.

"State your name for the record."

"Odile Marisol Vance."

"You were born Odile Thorne."

A pause. Lior watched her fingers curl once against the rail of the stand and release. "Yes. My mother changed my name after my father died. I've gone by Vance since I was fourteen."

"And how is it you came to live in Vesper Hollow?"

"I was brought here by Bess Almeida, after my mother's death—or what I was told was my mother's death—in the spring of 1998."

Lior felt Bess go very still beside them. Not flinching. *Bracing.* The way a house braces in a storm, all its weight settling downward.

Priya led her gently through the early ground: the arrival, the invented niece, the photographs never discussed, the years of silence constructed around her like scaffolding. Odile answered in a low, clear voice, each sentence complete, each fact offered without ornament. She did not look at the gallery. She did not look at Vivienne Hollis. She looked, mostly, at a point slightly above Priya's shoulder, which happened to be roughly where Lior was sitting, and Lior understood that she was looking at them without looking, drawing on them the way a climber draws on a rope.

Then Priya said, "Tell the court what you witnessed on October the ninth, 1998."

And the room changed temperature again.

"I was fourteen," Odile said. "My mother—I'll call her Marisol, though she was Rosa's sister, and there are things about that year I'm still untangling—my mother worked for the Hollis estate. Housekeeping, and some of the accounts. Mr. Thorne was a surveyor who'd been hired to map the boundaries of the old Thorne parcel for a sale. He came to our cottage twice. The third time, he came to the Hollis house, and my mother brought me along because school was out and there was no one to leave me with."

She paused. Alma said, "Take your time."

"He was arguing with Mrs. Hollis. In the study. About a book." Odile's voice did not waver, but it dropped half a register, the way voices do when they're carrying something heavy across a long distance. "I wasn't supposed to hear any of it. I was in the hallway by the kitchen, and the door was open because it was a warm day. He said the ledger proved the deed transfers were fraudulent. He said he'd been hired to survey boundaries, not bury history, and that he intended to take the ledger to the county recorder on Monday. And then—" her hands flattened against the rail, "—there was a sound. Like a door slamming. But it wasn't a door. I looked through the gap and Mrs. Hollis was standing over him and he was on the floor and there was blood coming from the back of his head where it had hit the corner of the desk, or been hit—I don't know which, I was fourteen and I closed my eyes—but he didn't get up. He didn't move again while I could see him."

"In the hallway," Priya said, "did anyone see you?"

"My mother found me. She put her hand over my mouth and took me out through the kitchen garden. We went home and she packed one bag and she held me all night, and the next morning she made arrangements. She told me we were leaving because of what I saw. Then she told me I was leaving alone, and that she would follow. She never followed. What I learned this month is that she couldn't follow—that staying behind, being dead on paper, was the only way to keep me hidden and keep the evidence safe. She hid the ledger and Mr. Thorne's journal in the root cellar under the Thorne parcel, and she spent twenty-five years trying to find a way to bring it up without getting me killed."

"How do you know that, if she never told you?"

"Because she wrote it down," Odile said. "In letters. Two of them arrived in Vesper Hollow in September, addressed to Lior Almeida. They're in evidence."

Lior watched the lead attorney for the Hollis defense—a silver-haired man named Whitcomb whose firm's name was engraved on the courthouse's donor plaque—make a small note on his legal pad. The note-taking of a man cataloguing ammunition.

Priya continued for another forty minutes. The root cellar. The remains. The journal, with its surveyor's precision, its dated entries, its final page recording the ledger's location in a cipher Elias had apparently believed no one would ever need to break. Odile answered everything. Twice she asked for a moment, and both times she took only five seconds, drank nothing, and resumed. Lior realized, with a strange vertigo, that they were watching Odile do what she did every night at the hospice: sit with something terrible, without looking away, without letting it spill.

When Priya sat down, Alma said, "Cross-examination," and Whitcomb unfolded himself from the crow's nest of attorneys like a man who had been waiting all morning to stretch.

* * *

He began pleasantly. That was the strategy, and everyone in the room knew it. Warmth first; the knife comes later, wrapped in sympathy.

"Ms. Vance. Thank you for your courage today. I know this can't be easy." He smiled at her the way Vivienne smiled at Lior across the sanctuary. "Let's talk about your memory. You were fourteen years old in 1998. That's twenty-seven years ago. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"And in those twenty-seven years, have you ever previously given a statement about what you claim to have seen? To police, to a coroner, to anyone?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I was a child, and then I was afraid, and then I was told the man who could protect me was dead, and the woman who could destroy me was untouchable. Fear doesn't file reports, Mr. Whitcomb. It hides."

A murmur ran through the gallery. Alma's gavel came down once, softly, more punctuation than rebuke.

Whitcomb absorbed it without visible damage. "Of course. Fear is very real. Now—you spent time, as a young adult, in institutional care. Isn't that right?"

There it was. Lior's hands closed on the edge of the bench.

Odile didn't blink. "I was hospitalized at nineteen for depression. Voluntarily. I signed myself in after a suicide attempt."

"A suicide attempt," Whitcomb repeated, turning toward the juryless gallery as though presenting an exhibit. "Following, I believe, the anniversary of your mother's death. A young woman in profound grief, hospitalized, medicated, diagnosed with—let me read from the record—*episodic dyscontrol and dissociative features*. Ms. Vance, isn't it true that dissociative disorders involve disturbances in memory?"

"Sometimes. Mine involved none. You're welcome to read the treating psychiatrist's letter; Dr. Okafor wrote one specifically for this hearing. She says my recall is precise, consistent across twenty-five years, and unchanged in every detail since I first described the events to my aunt at age sixteen."

"But you were *sixteen* when you first described them. Fourteen when they occurred. Two years of silence in between."

"Two years of a child trying not to know what she knew. Ask any trauma specialist what that does to a memory. It doesn't erase it. It engraves it."

Whitcomb moved closer to the stand. "You say you saw Mrs. Hollis standing over Mr. Thorne. Did you see her strike him?"

"I heard the strike. I saw the aftermath."

"So you did *not* see the blow."

"I did not see the blow."

"Could the sound you heard have been something else? A door, perhaps? A dropped object?"

"It could have been anything, if I were inventing it. I'm not. I'm telling you what I heard and what I saw, and I've never once changed either."

"And yet"—Whitcomb lifted a paper with theatrical regret—"your hospital records indicate an episode at age twenty-one in which you stated, to nursing staff, that you *remembered nothing* about 1998. Nothing at all. Do you recall that statement?"

For the first time, Odile hesitated. Lior leaned forward. In the front row, Vivienne Hollis turned her lavender shoulder slightly, as if adjusting her view of a garden.

"I recall," Odile said slowly, "that at twenty-one, a social worker visited me and asked questions about my childhood, and two days later my placement at the group home was terminated and I was moved three counties away. I learned very quickly that remembering out loud had consequences. So yes. There were years when I said I remembered nothing. I said it the way prisoners say what their captors want to hear. It was survival, not amnesia."

"That's a convenient interpretation."

"It's the true one. Convenience has never once entered into it. If I wanted convenience, Mr. Whitcomb, I'd have stayed quiet for another twenty-seven years."

Alma leaned forward over the bench. "Mr. Whitcomb. The witness's psychiatric history goes to weight, not admissibility, and the treating physician's letter is in evidence. Unless you have questions that go beyond fishing, move on."

Something flickered across Whitcomb's face— irritation quickly mastered. "Of course, Your Honor." He turned back, and now the pleasantness drained away entirely, and Lior understood they had reached the actual blade. "One final area. Your sworn affidavit, signed this month, states that Marisol Vance—your mother—is deceased. Yet the letters that initiated these proceedings were mailed by Marisol Vance. Can you explain to the court how a dead woman mails letters?"

"The letters were mailed by her sister, Rosa, following instructions Marisol left. Rosa was dying. She executed the plan after Marisol's death last spring. This is all documented in the sanatorium records and Rosa's testament, both in evidence."

"Both provided by parties with obvious motives. Tell me, Ms. Vance—if your mother spent twenty-five years trying to expose this crime, why wait until the statute of limitations was nearly expired? Why wait until the land sale forced her hand? Why not simply go to the police in, say, 2005? 2010? Any year in which her testimony might have mattered?"

"Because every year she tried, someone reminded her what happened to people who tried. Because Ruben Paz tried—he was an orderly at the county hospital who helped move Mr. Thorne's body, and he spent the rest of his life terrified, and when he died in 2014 his death was certified by no one and his coffin was filled with a stranger. My mother buried the evidence deep because shallow graves get found. She waited until the land itself was threatened—until the thing Elias died protecting was about to be sold and paved—because that was the one deadline even the Hollis money couldn't move." Odile's voice, which had been steady throughout, did something remarkable here: it got quieter, and in getting quieter it got stronger, the way a river strengthens as it narrows. "You're asking why she didn't come forward sooner. I'm asking you to look at what happened to every person who did."

The gallery was silent. Even the radiator seemed to have stopped clanking.

Whitcomb stood for a moment, legal pad forgotten at his side. Then he said, "No further questions," and sat down, and the silence broke like ice in spring.

* * *

Alma called a recess at noon. In the corridor outside, the crowd had thickened—shopkeepers, farmers, the women from St. Brigid's altar society, a cluster of teenagers who should have been in school. Someone had brought coffee in thermoses. Someone else had brought a folding table. Vesper Hollow, which had spent decades perfecting the art of not seeing, was seeing, and it had turned out for the occasion the way it turned out for funerals: somber, curious, and unable to stay home.

Tomas pressed a cup of terrible courthouse coffee into Lior's hands. "She's doing good in there," he said quietly. "Better than good."

"She's doing what none of us could," Lior said. "She's the only one who was actually there."

Bess appeared at Lior's elbow. Up close, Lior could see what the courtroom lighting had hidden: she had aged ten days in ten hours. "I keep thinking," Bess said, "about the day I brought her home. She was so small. She held onto my sleeve in the truck the whole way from the county line, and when we pulled into the drive she asked me one question. Just one. She asked, 'Will I be safe here?' And I told her yes." Bess's jaw tightened. "I told a fourteen-year-old girl yes, and then I built her whole life on a lie to make it true. Whatever happens in that room today, Lior—I need you to understand that I'd do every piece of it again."

"I know," Lior said. And found, to their surprise, that they meant it without bitterness—which frightened them more than anger would have.

Across the corridor, the Hollis legal phalanx had gathered near the water fountain, murmuring. Vivienne stood apart from them, alone by the window, looking out at the square with her hands clasped at her waist. As Lior watched, she turned her head, caught them looking, and smiled—the same unhurried, proprietary smile she'd worn at Sunday dinner, at church, at every charity gala where she'd handed Bess a check that kept the lights on. Then she mouthed something through the glass. Lior couldn't be sure, but it looked like two words.

*Watch him.*

Him. Lior turned, scanning the corridor. Deputies by the doors. Clerks. Townspeople. And near the stairwell, half-hidden behind a pillar, Deputy Corliss—young Corliss, who had broken down in the funeral home parlor three weeks ago, who had recorded Vivienne's blackmail offer on Odile's phone, who was listed on tomorrow's witness schedule as a witness *for the State*.

Corliss was looking at his phone. His face was gray. And as Lior watched, he read whatever was on the screen, pocketed the phone, straightened his uniform, and walked—not toward the courtroom, not toward the district attorney's table, but toward the exit, moving fast, like a man late for something he dreaded.

The bailiff called the recess's end. The crowd began to flow back through the doors.

"Lior." Odile had come out of the witness room, still in the navy dress, and she touched Lior's arm. "What is it? You've gone white."

"Corliss," Lior said. "He just left. He looked like a man walking to his own execution."

Odile followed their gaze to the empty stairwell. When she turned back, her face had gone carefully blank—the hospice face, the face she wore when the news was bad and the family was watching.

"Tomorrow," she said quietly, "he's supposed to testify about the recording. About what Vivienne offered me." Her hand found Lior's sleeve and gripped it, the way a much younger Odile had once gripped Bess's on the drive from the county line. "If he doesn't show up tomorrow, Lior—then whatever Vivienne has on him is worse than what she had on me. And we need to find him before she uses it."

Inside the courtroom, Alma Reyes struck the bench with her gavel and called the afternoon to order.
