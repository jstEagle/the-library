## 1. Postmarked Yesterday

The mail came late on Thursdays, which suited Tamsin Zhou fine, because Thursdays were the days she pretended the bookshop wasn't dying.

She stood behind the counter with a rubber band around her wrist and the day's post fanned out in front of her like a losing hand of cards: two utility notices, a publisher's invoice she'd already promised to pay twice, a flyer for a septic service, and something in a cream envelope with no return address. The rest went into the drawer beneath the register, where unpaid things went to be forgotten. The cream envelope she held up to the light, as if honesty might be visible through paper.

It was addressed to *The Keeper's Shelf* in careful, old-fashioned cursive — the kind of handwriting taught by nuns or grandmothers, all its letters standing at attention except where they didn't. Where they didn't was what caught her eye. The *y* in *Zhou* wobbled. The final stroke of the address trailed off like a sentence someone had lost the nerve to finish.

Tamsin had spent twenty-two years reading hands. Confessions, denials, alibis written at kitchen tables while their authors cried into their sleeves. She knew what a trembling line meant before she knew anything else about it.

She slit the envelope with the letter opener shaped like a lighthouse that her ex-husband had given her as a joke and kept using as a habit.

The paper inside was good stock, heavy, slightly damp at one corner as though it had been carried against a body before being posted. The ink was blue-black. The hand was the same as the envelope's, but here the tremor was worse, the letters shivering like reeds in wind, and yet the content beneath the shaking was precise as an autopsy report.

*Tamsin Zhou,* it began. No title, no *Detective*, no *Ms.* Just her name, laid down flat, like a card turned face-up.

*I am writing to confess to the death of Edith Marchand, who drowned off Gull Point on the fourteenth of September. The coroner called it an accident. It was not.*

Tamsin set the letter down. Her hands were steady; she made sure of that first, the way you check your own pulse before checking anyone else's. Outside the window, rain had started, the fine grey drizzle that came off the sea and made the whole town look like a photograph left out too long.

Edith Marchand. She remembered the name from the local paper, back in September — seventy-one years old, retired schoolteacher, found washed against the rocks below Gull Point two days after she was reported missing. The coroner's finding had been quick and clean: accidental drowning, likely disoriented, possibly a fall. Tamsin had read the article over coffee and thought, the way she still thought about every closed box, *at least that one got to stay shut.*

She picked the letter up again.

*I struck her,* the hand continued, the words pressing harder now, the pen nearly tearing through in places. *Not with intent to kill. I struck her because she saw something she should not have seen, and she would not agree to forget it. We argued at the top of the path above Gull Point. I pushed her. She fell further than I meant her to. The tide did the rest, but the tide is only ever an accomplice.*

*I am dying. I will not see another spring. What I cannot carry into the ground, I am leaving with you, because you are the only person in this town who has ever looked at a thing until it told the truth.*

*There was a witness. Mara Voss. She was there. She has been there all along. Ask her.*

*Mara Voss died seven years ago.* Tamsin said it aloud to the empty shop, and her voice sounded strange among the shelves, too loud, too awake. Because she knew that name too. Not from a newspaper. From a headstone.

St. Brendan's churchyard, the row nearest the seawall, where the salt ate the inscriptions within a decade. *MARA VOSS, 1961–2018. THE SEA KEEPS WHAT IT LOVES.* Tamsin had visited that grave once, early in her retirement, when she'd been walking the town the way a man walks the streets of a city he used to patrol — out of habit, out of grief, out of some stubborn refusal to stop looking even though no one was paying her to look anymore. She'd stood at that headstone with wet grass soaking her shoes and thought about the girl she hadn't saved, the one from her last year on the job, the case that had ended her career and most of her sleep. Different girl. Same water. Same town that swallowed its dead politely and asked no questions.

Mara Voss was in the ground. Tamsin had seen the mound of fresh earth herself.

And yet this letter, written by a hand that shook, described conversations with her — *she has been there all along* — present tense, living tense.

She checked the postmark. That was instinct, old instinct, the kind that lived in the fingers rather than the head. The cancellation stamp was smudged but legible: **GULL HARBOR, 15 OCT**.

Edith Marchand's funeral had been held on the fourteenth of October. Tamsin remembered because the funeral procession had passed the bookshop while she was sweeping the step, and she'd stood with her broom still until the last car turned the corner toward St. Brendan's.

Postmarked yesterday. Whoever wrote this had sealed a confession to murder, walked it to the post office, and mailed it the day after they'd buried the woman they killed — close enough to the grave to hear the dirt land.

Tamsin read the letter three more times. Then she locked it in the cash drawer, under the tray, beneath the unpaid invoices, and stood for a long moment with her palm flat on the counter, feeling the shop breathe around her. Forty thousand books. A roof that leaked over the poetry section. Six weeks, maybe eight, before she'd have to make the call she'd already drafted in her head — the call to the agent, the listing, the end of the only thing she'd built since the badge.

Later, she told herself. First, Gull Point.

* * *

The Marchand house sat on the coast road where the land gave up pretending to be farmland and surrendered to gorse and wind. It was a long, low stone house, the kind built to shrug off storms, with a view of the point from every window — the same view, Tamsin realized, that would have included the path above Gull Point, the whole fatal geography of it laid out like a painting.

The gate was new. The paint was newer.

That was the thing that stopped her, halfway up the gravel drive with the rain beading on her coat. The house had been repainted recently — white, thick, applied with care over every sill and lintel. But paint, especially fresh paint over old wood, tells on itself. It thins. And along the lower course of the north wall, beside the cellar door, where the white ran thinnest, Tamsin could see what the painter had been working so hard to cover.

A stain. Broad, dark, soaked deep into the stone itself, spreading in the shape of something that had been poured out or dragged across. Painted once, twice, maybe three times — she could see the layers, each one a shade less successful than the last, the ghost of the stain rising through them like a face through frosted glass.

Bloodstains don't survive two months of coastal weather unless they've been allowed to set first. Unless someone waited. Unless someone needed time to decide what to do about them.

Tamsin stood in the rain and let the old machinery turn over. Twenty-two years of it, rusted and buried, and now grinding awake joint by joint: *who paints a wall in November? Who repaints a cellar door twice? Who mails a confession the day after the funeral instead of going to the police while the body was still warm?*

Someone protecting something. Someone buying time. Someone dying, maybe — the letter had said so, and dying people lied differently than other people, more carefully, with more to lose.

She walked the perimeter slowly, hands in her pockets, not touching anything, just looking. A wheelbarrow by the shed, half-full of sand. A child's bicycle rusting against the fence, too small and too old for anyone currently living here. And in the downstairs window, behind curtains that twitched — just once, just enough — the shape of someone watching her back.

Tamsin didn't knock. Knocking was for people with warrants, and she was just a bookseller now, out walking in the rain.

But as she turned back down the drive, she glanced at the headland, and there — small against the grey sky, at the very edge of the property, where the path began its climb toward Gull Point — stood a figure in a dark coat, motionless, watching her with the particular stillness of someone deciding whether to run.

Even at that distance, even through the rain, Tamsin felt the recognition land in her chest like a dropped anchor.

She had stood at that woman's grave. She had watched them lower the coffin.

Mara Voss was alive, and she had just made the mistake of letting Tamsin Zhou see it.
