## 4. Four Names in the Register

The sacristy smelled of beeswax and old linen, of candle smoke layered over decades until the air itself seemed to have a memory. Wendell stood in the doorway for a long moment on Thursday morning, letting her eyes adjust, letting the room introduce itself before she presumed to enter it.

Father Anselm had kept his archive here — she'd learned that much from Mrs. Okafor, the parish secretary, who had mentioned it in passing with the particular pride of someone who has dusted the same shelves for twenty years and considers them an extension of her own household. *Everything's in the sacristy cabinets,* she'd said. *Father was old-fashioned. He didn't trust computers.* She'd said it as though distrust of computers were a virtue, and perhaps in this parish it was.

The morning light came through a narrow east window in bars, falling across a worktable where vestments lay folded under clean sheets like patients waiting to be seen. Along the far wall stood four tall cabinets, oak gone dark with age and handling, each door fitted with a small brass pull worn bright at the point where thumbs had pressed it, year after year, decade after decade. Wendell thought about that wear — the geography of habit made visible — and felt the familiar quickening in her chest that she had learned, over three quiet investigations, to treat as both compass and warning.

She was supposed to be practicing. The organ upstairs waited for her, its pipes breathing dust into the empty nave, and Mrs. Okafor expected her back by eleven to run through the hymns for Sunday. Wendell had left the loft twenty minutes ago with the stated intention of finding the restroom. No one had questioned it. No one ever questioned the organist; organists were furniture that played music, permitted to come and go along the walls of other people's grief.

*Never accuse, only illuminate.* Her own rule, spoken to herself so many times it had worn smooth as the brass pulls. But rules were easier in hotels, among strangers whose secrets belonged to no one she loved. Here the light fell differently. Here, when the widow Ruth Calloway had shaken her hand on Sunday, something in the woman's grip had lingered half a second too long, and Wendell had wondered — not for the first time since receiving the letter — whether the letter-writer had chosen her because she was good, or because she was near.

She crossed to the cabinets and opened them one by one, gently, the way one opens doors in a house where someone has recently died.

The first held vestment records and fabric swatches. The second, financial ledgers going back to the eighties, their columns ruled in faded green ink. The third was hymnals, broken-spined, some with slips of paper still marking pages no one would ever play again. The fourth —

The fourth held the registers.

They stood in a row like headstones: bound volumes in maroon cloth, one for every few years, their spines labeled in white paint. Baptisms, marriages, deaths. The civic record of a community that believed heaven kept better books than the county did. Wendell ran her finger along the spines until she found it.

*1991–1996.*

She lifted it down. It was heavier than she expected, or perhaps her hands were less steady than she pretended. She carried it to the worktable, into the barred light, and set it down, and for a moment she simply looked at it — the way she had once looked at a locked hotel room door in Zurich, at a wine glass in Lisbon, at all the small objects that had turned out to hold whole human catastrophes inside them.

Then she opened it.

* * *

The first hundred pages were ordinary and therefore beautiful, if you knew how to read them. Anselm's handwriting — and it was his, she recognized it now from the letter, that liturgical training showing itself in every carefully proportioned ascender, every letter formed with the patience of a man who believed that writing a name into a book was itself a kind of prayer — recorded the parish's life in entries of perfect regularity. Baptisms with their dates and parents' names. Marriages with their witnesses. Funerals with their causes, delicately rendered: *after a long illness borne with grace*, *suddenly, in his sleep*, *following an accident at the cannery*.

Wendell read the way water flows downhill, following the path of least resistance toward October 1994.

The letter had named the date. *What we buried on the twelfth of October will not stay buried, and neither should it.* Thirty years past. She turned pages. September gave way to October. Entries appeared in their orderly procession — a baptism on the fourth, a funeral on the ninth, a marriage banns notice — and then:

A gap.

Not an absence of entries; Anselm had been too disciplined for that. A gap in the *binding*. Wendell noticed it first as a wrongness in the way the pages turned, a thinness at the gutter between the entry dated October 9 and the next surviving entry, dated October 21. She went back. Turned forward again. Pressed the pages flat with both palms and looked closely at the stub where something had been cut away.

Someone had razored out a page.

Not torn — razored. The remaining edge was clean, surgical, done with care and probably done slowly, by lamplight, by someone who wanted the removal to be invisible to any casual reader. Only the deep stub remained, a sliver of paper still sewn into the binding, and on that sliver, caught at the very margin, a fragment of ink: the tail end of a letter, possibly an *r*, possibly nothing at all.

Wendell stood very still in the barred light.

Here was the shape of the thing, then. The letter had told the truth: something had happened on October 12, 1994, and Father Anselm Doyle had recorded it — whatever *it* was — in his own hand, in his own book, with whatever ceremony the Church required. And then, at some later date, someone had come into this room with a razor and removed the record.

Anselm himself? Possible. Men who keep secrets sometimes also keep the impulse to destroy them, and thirty years is a long time to live beside a page. Or the deacon — Voss, with his careful hands and his careful silences. Or any of the others the letter implied. Four names, the letter said. *Four of us signed our names to silence.* Witnesses to something unnamed.

But a razored page in a parish register was not the end of the trail. Wendell had spent fifteen years in hotel lobbies learning how institutions duplicate themselves — how every act of record-keeping breeds its carbon shadow, filed elsewhere, forgotten by everyone except the people who need it forgotten least. Dioceses were no different from hotel chains. Somewhere, in a chancery office, a copy of everything existed.

She memorized the surrounding entries — dates, names, the exact position of the gap — closed the register, and slid it back into its place among its fellows, spine aligned precisely as she'd found it. Then she washed her hands in the sacristy sink, though they were already clean, because the ritual steadied her, and because some part of her understood that she had just touched the hinge of a door thirty years old, and wanted to mark the moment somehow.

Upstairs, the organ waited. She played hymns for an hour with flawless attention and thought about nothing else at all, which was its own kind of lie, and a necessary one.

* * *

The diocesan office occupied a granite building downtown that had once been a bank and still carried the manner of one: marble floors, teller-window grilles repurposed as reception screens, a hush that suggested large sums changing hands invisibly. Wendell arrived Friday morning in her most respectable coat, carrying a folder of fabricated credentials and a story she had assembled overnight with the care of a jeweler setting stones.

Her name — the name she wore these days, the organist's name — was Wendell Marsh. She was, she explained to the archivist, a researcher preparing a history of sacred music in the diocese, and she needed to consult certain parish registers from the early nineties, specifically St. Brigid's, specifically 1994. The archivist, a man named Mr. Pell with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, received this without suspicion. Researchers came and went. Archives rewarded patience and punished urgency, and Wendell had dressed herself in patience: sensible shoes, unhurried voice, a request modest enough to seem beneath conspiracy.

"The original registers stay at the parishes," Pell said, leading her back through corridors lined with portrait photographs of bishops, each one wearing the same expression of benevolent exhaustion. "But we hold microfilm copies of everything up to 2001. Carbon duplicates, originally — Father Doyle was meticulous about it, actually. Sent us a copy of every page within the month. Some of the older pastors couldn't be bothered." He unlocked a door onto a room of gray steel cabinets and green-shaded lamps, a room that smelled of cold paper and toner, and Wendell felt the particular pleasure she always felt upon entering an archive: the sense of standing inside a building's honest memory, where nothing could be razored out without leaving a ghost.

"October of '94," she said. "St. Brigid's."

Pell found the reel, threaded a microfilm reader whose machine hummed had probably not changed since the Nixon administration, and left her alone with it.

The film advanced with a stuttering whir. Pages of St. Brigid's register scrolled past in negative — black fields, white script, Anselm's handwriting glowing like bones in dark earth. Wendell moved steadily through 1993, through the early months of 1994, watching the parish's life flow by in its orderly white lines. And then October.

She slowed the crank.

*Baptism, October 9.* White letters on black. *Funeral, October 11.* She cranked again, breath shallow, eyes fixed on the space where the razored page should have been —

And there it was. Whole. Untouched. Glowing.

The page began mid-entry, the tail of the October 11 funeral record, and then, below it, in Anselm's most careful hand — more careful than anywhere else in the register, she noticed, as though he had written it slowly, deliberately, pressing hard enough that even a carbon copy bore the weight of what he wrote:

*A private baptism, administered under extraordinary circumstances, the thirteenth day of October, in the year 1994.*

Wendell's hand stopped on the crank.

The thirteenth. Not the twelfth. The letter had said the twelfth — *what we buried on the twelfth of October* — but the baptism was recorded the day after. Whatever had happened on the twelfth, whatever had been buried, this ceremony had followed it by a single day, performed quietly, outside the ordinary rhythm of parish life, witnessed by —

She read the witness line twice. Three times. The words did not change, though the room seemed to tilt around them, the green lamp swinging in her vision like a lantern in rough water.

*Witnesses:* four names, signed in four different hands, each one distinct enough to be a face.

*Ruth Calloway.*

*Emil Voss.*

*Tomas Brandt.*

And the fourth name, written in a hand Wendell knew better than any handwriting on earth — a hand she had watched write grocery lists and birthday cards and the margins of paperback novels, a hand she had kissed goodbye at a hospital bedside eleven years ago —

*Maren Marchetti.*

* * *

The room did not go dark. That was the strange part. The lamps stayed green, the machine went on humming, the portraits of dead bishops went on gazing their exhausted benedictions from the corridor, and Wendell Marchetti stood in the middle of all this ordinariness while the floor of her life quietly gave way.

*Maren Marchetti.*

Her mother's name. In a parish register. As witness to a private baptism performed the day after the night the letter called a burial.

It was impossible. It was a coincidence. It was — she reached for the rational explanations the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood, and found them rotten in her grip. Maren Marchetti was not a common name. And her mother had never, in Wendell's entire childhood, once spoken of St. Brigid's. They had lived across the city, in a different world entirely — Maren working double shifts at the county hospital, Wendell raised on library books and leftover casseroles, on a mother's love that was fierce and absolute and guarded by silences Wendell had never dared map. Maren had been Catholic in the way many women of her generation were Catholic: baptized, lapsed, unwilling to discuss it. There had been no parish. There had been no priest at her funeral, only a civil service at the crematorium, and Wendell had assumed that was Maren's wish, and had honored it, and had never asked why.

*You already know this family.* The letter's second line. She had read it as misdirection, as the letter-writer's flourish. Now it stood in her memory like a sentence handed down by a court.

She became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking, and she folded them together on the desk and breathed the way she had taught herself to breathe in hotel service elevators and police interview rooms: four counts in, seven counts held, eight counts out. The shaking subsided. The room reassembled itself. The page glowed on.

She forced herself to read the rest of the entry with clinical attention, cataloguing details the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was the raft she had and she was going to cling to it.

The baptism recorded no father. The mother's name field held a single word, and Wendell read it and felt the vertigo open beneath her again, wider this time, bottomless:

*Mother: Maren Marchetti.*

A child. There had been a child. Baptized privately on October 13, 1994, the day after whatever was buried was buried, witnessed by four parishioners of St. Brigid's including a nurse from across the city who never spoke of her faith and died without last rites.

*The secret may be a person, not a crime scene.* The thought arrived fully formed, cold and luminous, and Wendell rejected it and accepted it in the same breath. A child born in secret, baptized in secret, hidden in a register that someone later mutilated — and a drifter, the letter implied, connected to all of it, connected to the burial, connected perhaps to —

To whom? To Maren?

Wendell looked at her own hands, folded on the desk, and saw them as if from a great distance: her mother's hands, exactly her mother's hands, the same long fingers, the same prominent knuckles. She had always taken a quiet pride in the resemblance. Now it was evidence. Now it was a question with teeth.

Who was her father?

Maren had answered that question exactly once in Wendell's life, when Wendell was nine, with a single sentence delivered while peeling potatoes, not looking up: *He wasn't a good man, and he isn't in our lives, and that's all you need.* Wendell had asked no more, because children learn quickly which doors in a house are load-bearing, and she had built her identity on top of that sealed door and lived there comfortably for forty years. A bad man. Gone. Fine.

But a bad man who might have been a drifter in a storm in October 1994. A bad man who might have been buried beneath a church by four witnesses, one of whom was the woman who tucked Wendell in at night.

No. She shut the thought down. It was too large, too soon, too shaped like the thing she feared. There were other explanations. There had to be. Maren had been a nurse; perhaps she'd been called to help with a difficult birth. Perhaps the child — whoever the child was — had grown up somewhere in this city, unaware, and the connection to Wendell was nothing but the accident of two women sharing one name.

Except the letter had been addressed to *the one who notices*. And the letter had said *you already know this family*. And the letter had been written in a hand trained in liturgical script, a hand that matched the careful entries in this register, a hand that belonged to a dead priest who had spent, if Ruth Calloway's evasions meant anything, thirty years arranging for the truth to surface after his death.

Arranging. For whom?

Pell knocked softly and put his head in. "Finding what you need?"

"Almost." Wendell's voice came out level. She was grateful to it. "Could I trouble you for copies? Just this page and the surrounding ones."

"Not a problem at all." He threaded the printer with the ease of long habit, and the machine began to hum, and Wendell stood behind him watching her mother's name emerge line by line in warm gray toner on white paper — Maren Marchetti, Maren Marchetti — materializing out of thirty years of darkness into the fluorescent light of a diocesan basement, where no one knew her, where no one knew what she had signed, where her daughter stood holding the evidence of a life her mother had successfully hidden from everyone, everyone, until a letter arrived addressed to the one who notices.

She thanked Pell. She signed the access log with the organist's name, the false name, and noted with a distant, almost hysterical clarity that she had now committed her second forgery in a week, and that it bothered her far less than it should have.

Outside, the afternoon had turned gold and cold. Wendell walked without direction for six blocks, past a bakery, past a school letting out its river of children, past a bus stop where an old man fed pigeons from a paper sack, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary around her while she carried the page in her folder against her ribs like a coal.

Four names. Three of them belonged to the living or the recently dead of St. Brigid's — the widow with the lingering handshake, the deacon with the careful hands, the dying man in the care home whom the letter's author had apparently also contacted, or would contact, or — no. She was getting ahead of herself. One thread at a time. That was the discipline. That was the craft.

But the fourth name was hers. Not hers — her mother's. Which, in the country where Wendell now found herself living, amounted to the same thing.

She stopped at a corner, waiting for a light, and caught her reflection in a shop window: a woman in a respectable coat, unremarkable, forgettable, the perfect instrument of infiltration. Except the instrument had developed a crack, and through the crack she could see something she had never agreed to see.

Whatever was buried beneath St. Brigid's, her mother had stood over the grave. Her mother had signed her name to silence. And Father Anselm Doyle — gentle, meticulous, doomed Anselm, who had fallen from his own bell tower and been ruled accidental — had known it, and had written it down, and had sent a letter to the one person alive who would refuse to let it stay buried.

Because he hadn't sent it to a stranger. Wendell understood that now, standing at the corner while the light changed and changed again and the crowd flowed around her. He had sent it to family.

The realization settled over her not like a revelation but like weather — gradual, total, inescapable. She was not investigating the parish anymore. The case had folded inward, quietly, the way a hand closes, and she was inside it.

Tomorrow she would go back to St. Brigid's. Tomorrow she would watch Emil Voss across the nave and wonder what those careful hands had held besides a censer. Tomorrow she would sit beside Ruth Calloway at choir practice and listen for the slip beneath the widow's practiced calm. Tomorrow she would find Tomas Brandt, the fourth name, the one she knew nothing about yet, and see whether he was the confessor the letter promised or another keeper of the seal.

Tonight she would go home to her small apartment above the Meridian's laundry, take down the box of her mother's things she had never finished sorting through, and begin looking at eleven-year-old grief with new eyes.

The light changed. Wendell crossed the street, and behind her, in the window of the diocesan archive, the green lamps burned on over the honest memory of the Church, where a razored page in a sacristy cabinet still kept its secret, and its carbon ghost had finally, after thirty years, been read by the daughter of the woman who signed it.
