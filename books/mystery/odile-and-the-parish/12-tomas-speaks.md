## 12. Tomas Speaks

The care home smelled of lavender and bleach, two scents at war with each other, neither winning. Nadia had noticed that on her first visit three weeks ago and noticed it again now, standing in the corridor outside Room 9 with her father's confession letter folded in her coat pocket like a stone she couldn't put down.

*Rosewood Court Assisted Living*, the sign by the entrance read. *A gentle place for life's final chapter.* The brochure in the lobby promised dignity. What it delivered was the smell — lavender over bleach, kindness laid like a sheet over something antiseptic and final — and a nurse at the desk who looked up with the particular weariness of someone who had learned not to promise anything.

"You're back," the nurse said. Not unkindly. "He had a good morning. Ate half his eggs."

Half his eggs. Nadia nodded as though this were news worth carrying, as though she hadn't spent the drive from her flat rehearsing what she would say to a man who might not hear any of it. Her father had been dying for eleven months now, the cancer moving through him with the patience of water through limestone, hollowing out rooms she'd thought were solid. The doctors spoke in ranges. Six weeks. Three months. Tomas Brandt had always been a man who defied estimates, mostly out of stubbornness, and so he lingered, and so Nadia visited, and so they sat together in the lavender-and-bleach hush while he drifted in and out of morphine dreams he never described.

She hadn't come today to sit with him, though. She'd come because of the letter.

It had arrived on a Tuesday — the same Tuesday, she would later realize, that a woman named Wendell Marchetti received hers — postmarked the day after Father Anselm Doyle's funeral. Nadia had never heard of Father Anselm Doyle. She had never set foot in St. Brigid's parish, had never lived within forty miles of it. Her father had left that part of the country before she was born and never once spoken of it, and when she'd asked him about the letter during one of his lucid afternoons, he had looked at her with an expression she still couldn't name — not guilt exactly, and not fear, but something older than either, the look of a man seeing a debt he'd hoped to die owing.

"Burn it," he'd said. Then, after a long silence: "No. Keep it. Keep it where I can see it." And he'd turned his face to the window, and the conversation was over.

She kept it. She read it perhaps fifty times in the week that followed, until she could recite it herself, until the words stopped being words and became a kind of weather inside her:

*I am dying, and before I die I must tell you what was buried beneath St. Brigid's on the twelfth of October, 1994. Your father knows. Ask him what he carried.*

That was all. No signature. Liturgical handwriting, the organist-woman had said when they finally met — and Nadia still didn't fully trust Wendell Marchetti, with her hotel concierge calm and her questions shaped like gifts, but she trusted the letter less, and trust between two women who suspect each other is at least a place to stand.

*Ask him what he carried.*

She pushed open the door to Room 9.

* * *

Her father was small in the bed. He had been a big man once — broad-shouldered, a stonemason's build, hands like slabs — and the illness had taken him down by degrees until what remained seemed almost childlike, folded under the blanket, his chest rising in shallow tides. The afternoon light came through the blinds in bars and lay across the bed like something measured out.

"Dad." She pulled the chair close and sat. "It's Nadia."

His eyes opened halfway. For a moment they were unfocused, swimming somewhere below the surface, and then they found her and sharpened, and she saw — with a jolt she tried not to show — that he was *up*. Fully up. Surfaced. It happened rarely now, these clearings in the fog, and when they came they were brief, and everyone in Rosewood Court knew to use them or lose them.

"Nadia-girl." His voice was gravel and breath. "You've got your mother's frown."

"I don't frown."

"You're frowning now." The ghost of a smile. Then it faded, and his eyes went to the shape of the letter in her coat pocket — she hadn't even realized she'd moved it into view, but he saw everything today, surfaced men did — and the smile was gone entirely. "You brought it."

"I need you to tell me what it means."

"No."

"Dad—"

"No." But there was no force in it. There was nothing in it but exhaustion, and beneath the exhaustion something that frightened her more: shame. She had seen her father angry, amused, stubborn, tender. She had never seen him ashamed. Shame sat on his face like frost.

"Father Anselm is dead," she said quietly. "Did you know that? He fell from the bell tower. They ruled it an accident. And then I get a letter saying—" she stopped, because she couldn't say it aloud yet, couldn't make it real by speaking it. "Someone wrote to me, Dad. Someone who knew you. Who knew you thirty years ago, in a place you never told me existed. You have to explain that to me. You owe me that much."

The light lay across the bed. Somewhere down the corridor a television murmured through a closed door, laughter without context. Her father stared at the ceiling, and Nadia watched him decide — watched it happen, actually watched the decision move through him like weather crossing a field — and when he finally spoke, his voice had changed. It was steadier. It was the voice of a man giving testimony, and she understood, with a coldness that started in her stomach and spread outward, that he had rehearsed this. That some version of this speech had been waiting in him for thirty years, the way a splinter waits in flesh.

"Close the door," he said.

She closed it.

"The twelfth of October, 1994," Tomas said. "There was a storm. You wouldn't believe the storm — the gutters on the church were running like rivers, and the wind came off the water and hit the tower and the whole building groaned like a ship. Four of us were at the parish that night. Me, Emil Voss, Ruth Calloway, and Maren Marchetti. Father Anselm sent us home early because of the storm, and none of us made it home, because of what we found in the undercroft."

He paused. His hands had come out from under the blanket and lay on top of it, and Nadia saw that they were trembling, the great mason's hands, fine as tuning forks.

"There was a man," he said. "A drifter. He'd been sleeping in the undercroft — the old crypt space under the chapel, where the boiler pipes run. Anselm knew about him, had been leaving food. Maren knew too." Something crossed his face at the name, something Nadia filed away and didn't understand yet. "And the storm flooded the low channel that night, faster than anyone could have guessed, and the man was asleep, and by the time we found him—" His throat worked. "By the time we found him he was gone. Cold. The water had taken him hours before."

"Oh God," Nadia said.

"We should have called it in. That's what we should have done. But the orphanage funding vote was that month — the diocese was deciding whether to keep St. Brigid's children's wing open, and there'd been talk, terrible talk, about liabilities, about how a death on parish property, even an accident, even a trespasser, would end it. Forty children, Nadia. Forty children sleeping in that wing every night." His eyes found hers, and they were wet and fierce. "You judge us. Go ahead. I've judged myself every day for thirty years. But you weren't standing in that rain with Anselm Doyle looking at you, asking what Christ would weigh against forty beds."

"What did you do?"

"We buried him." Flat. Final. "In consecrated ground, under the flagstones in the undercroft, with Anselm reading the last rites over a man whose name nobody knew. And we signed the register as witnesses — me, Emil, Ruth, Maren — because Anselm said the truth needed witnesses even if it could never be spoken. He said a lie held by four people is still lighter than a truth that kills forty children. And then we went home in the storm, and we never spoke of it again, and we raised our families and buried our dead and prayed every Sunday in a church with a secret under its floor." He laughed, and it was the worst sound Nadia had ever heard, dry and cracked, like a door opening on a room no one had entered in decades. "You want to know what I carried? I carried a shovel. That's what I carried. I carried a shovel, and I've been carrying it ever since."

Nadia realized she was crying only when the tears reached her mouth. She wiped them with the heel of her hand. "The letter says more than that. The letter says—"

"I know what the letter says." And here her father's voice dropped, dropped so low she had to lean toward him, into the bar of afternoon light, into the lavender and the bleach. "Because I wrote it."

The room went very quiet. Even the television down the corridor seemed to hold its breath.

"Not all of it," Tomas said. "Anselm wrote most of it — he'd been writing them for years, letters, dozens of them, sealed and dated, locked in a box at his solicitor's. He told me last spring, when his heart started failing. He said, *Tomas, when I'm gone, the truth goes out whether Emil likes it or not.* He'd arranged it all. Scheduled deliveries. Postmarks by proxy." His breath caught; Nadia reached for the water cup on the bedside table and held it to his lips, and he drank, and continued, and she understood that he was racing the morphine now, racing the tide coming back in, spending his clearing all at once because he might never surface again. "But my copy — the one that came to me — I added lines to it before I sent it on. Lines Anselm didn't write. Because Anselm was going to confess everything himself, publicly, gently, in his own time. And then he fell."

"Dad—"

"*Listen.*" His hand shot out and gripped her wrist, and the strength in it astonished her, the dying man's strength, borrowed from somewhere. "Anselm didn't fall. Do you hear me? I saw Emil carry the rope. Two days before, in the sacristy — the bell rope, coiled, wrapped in a tarp, and Emil carrying it like it was nothing, like it was laundry. And I thought — I told myself I was wrong, I told myself old men misremember, I told myself Emil Voss has served that parish for forty years and carries things for people all the time. I told myself the story I needed." Tears ran freely down his face now, and he didn't wipe them. "And then he died, and it wasn't an accident, and I knew — I *knew* — and I was too much of a coward to go to the police, so I did the only thing left. I added my lines to the letters. I sent them where Anselm's schedule said to send them. To you. To the Marchetti girl. Because if I was going to die with this in me, I was damned if Emil Voss was going to die clean."

Nadia's mind was moving fast now, fast and cold, laying pieces side by side. "My letter didn't have those lines. Mine only said to ask you what you carried."

"I know." His grip tightened. "Because there are more letters, Nadia-girl. More than two. Anselm's box at the solicitor's — it isn't empty. There's a schedule. Deliveries timed to dates, to anniversaries. And the last one—" his voice frayed, and he gathered it back like rope slipping through gloves, "the last one goes to whoever solves the first ones. Anselm designed it that way. He wanted the truth found, not just told. He wanted someone to *notice*."

The word landed strangely, heavily, and Nadia thought of Wendell Marchetti's steady gray eyes and her careful questions and the way she'd said, in the café three days ago, *I notice things. It's all I do.* She had thought it was modesty. It had been a key turning in a lock.

"Dad, listen to me. If Emil killed Anselm, and he knows about the letters—"

"He knows." Tomas said it simply, terribly. "Emil has always known about the box. He was there when Anselm told me. He smiled and shook my hand and said God's will be done." His eyes were closing now, the tide pulling him back, but he fought it, dragging words up from somewhere deep. "Nadia. Listen. The thirteenth of October. Check the dates. Everything Anselm scheduled — check the dates against October. He chose them on purpose. He wanted to finish it on the anniversary, the way he started it. Whatever comes next comes on the anniversary, and the anniversary is—"

His eyes closed. His hand slid from her wrist.

For a long moment Nadia sat motionless, watching her father breathe — the shallow tide rising and falling, rising and falling — and the room filled slowly with the ordinary sounds of the care home, the television's distant laughter, a cart rattling somewhere, the world proceeding as though nothing had happened, as though a man had not just handed his daughter a murder.

She took out the letter. Unfolded it on her knee with hands that were not quite steady. And there, in the margin, in her father's blunt carpenter's hand — writing she had somehow never registered in fifty readings, or had registered and refused to see — she found what he had added, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*He carried the rope. E.V. The thirteenth.*

October the thirteenth. She counted backward from the funeral, forward from 1994, her lips moving with the arithmetic. The anniversary was nine days away.

Nine days — or fewer, if Emil Voss had already begun cleaning the list.

Nadia folded the letter, stood, and kissed her father's forehead, and he stirred but did not wake. Then she walked out past the nurse's desk, past the lavender and the bleach, into the grey afternoon, and took out her phone, and dialed the number of the woman who noticed things.

"Wendell," she said, when it connected. "We need to talk tonight. My father talked. And there's something else — something about the dates. I think Anselm left one more letter, and I think I know when it's coming."

On the other end of the line, Wendell Marchetti was silent for a moment. When she answered, her voice was very quiet, and very certain.

"So do I. And Nadia — I think someone's already asking the solicitor about it."
