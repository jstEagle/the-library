## 15. Locked In

The nave held its breath after Voss left it.

Wendell stood a long moment among the empty pews, listening to the way silence behaves in a church — not the absence of sound but the presence of something waiting to be filled. Evening light came through the east windows in long amber blades, laying stripes across the stone floor like bars, and she thought, with the detached precision that had served her through three deaths before this one: *that is what a cage looks like from the inside.*

Voss had been calm. That was the thing she kept returning to as she gathered her coat and her music satchel, the prop of her disguise suddenly feeling like a costume she'd worn too long. Men who threaten you raise their voices; men who intend to harm you lower them. Voss had spoken about penance the way other men spoke about weather — inevitable, seasonal, nothing to be done. He had admitted to the cover-up of 1994 in the same breath he'd denied murder, and he had told her, without ever saying it plainly, that he knew about the letters. That he had been intercepting them.

*Intercepting them.* Which meant Anselm Doyle had spent his final months arranging his own posthumous testimony, and Deacon Emil Voss had spent those same months standing at the mailbox like a customs officer, deciding which truths were fit for entry into the world.

She walked up the center aisle toward the main doors, her footsteps swallowed by the carpet runner, and it was only when her hand closed on the cold iron pull that she understood the quality of the silence behind her.

The doors did not move.

She pulled again, harder, and heard the answer: chain. The flat metallic whisper of links drawn taut, wrapped through the door handles and padlocked somewhere out of sight on the exterior side. She pressed her face to the narrow window in the door and looked out into the blue dusk of the forecourt. Yes — a length of galvanized chain, looped twice, secured with a padlock the size of a fist. Not improvised. Deliberate. Someone had gone to the maintenance closet, taken the chain the parish used to secure the storage yard, and done this while she sat in the nave being told, gently, that she was no longer welcome.

No longer welcome. No longer safe. The two meanings lived inside each other like a seed inside its husk.

Wendell stepped back and let her eyes adjust, let the habit of thirty years of noticing take over the work that fear wanted to do. The side door by the baptistry — she'd seen it locked during her first week, and Ruth Calloway had the key, and Ruth was home by now with her tea and her television and her complicity. The sacristy door opened onto the rectory corridor, and the rectory's back entrance was bolted at vespers; she'd catalogued that detail without knowing why, the way she catalogued everything, hoarding small facts against a hunger she hadn't yet felt.

Which left the tower.

* * *

The stair to the bell tower was a spiral of worn limestone, narrow enough that her shoulders brushed both walls, lit by slit windows that gave her a stripe of light every half-turn, then darkness, then light again. Her hand found the rope handrail — old hemp, soft with age — and she climbed with the measured pace of someone refusing to run, because running is a confession to whoever might be listening.

But no one was listening. That was worse. The parish had emptied after vespers; she'd watched the last of the congregation file out through the doors she now knew were chained behind them. Whatever happened in this building tonight would happen without witnesses, and Voss had made sure of it with a length of chain and a padlock, and the elegance of it — the *liturgical* neatness of it — made her stomach tighten. He hadn't threatened her. He had simply closed the church, the way you close a church at night. If she was inside when it closed, that was between her soul and God.

At the first landing, the belfry opened around her — the great bronze bell hanging in its oak headstock, dark as a struck match, big enough that its shadow had a temperature. She could feel the cold coming off it. Above, the second flight continued toward the clock housing and the louvred openings where Anselm Doyle had spent so many evenings alone with the ropes and the view and, presumably, his conscience.

She climbed past the bell. The air grew colder, tasting of pigeon lime and old metal, and through the louvres she could see the town going amber and violet below, streetlights waking one by one, the whole world carrying on without any idea what was sealed up here above it.

The clock housing occupied the topmost chamber — a wooden cabinet taller than a man, housing the mechanism that drove the tower clock, all brass gears and lead weights and the patient tick of escapement. Wendell circled it once, running her fingers along the panels, looking for what? She didn't know yet. She only knew that Voss had chained the doors tonight, tonight of all nights, and that men who hide things in churches hide them high, where only the faithful climb.

The panel on the north face was wrong.

Not broken. Not forced. Simply wrong — the gap between it and its neighbor a hair wider than the others, the dust along its edge disturbed in a clean line rather than a smear. She pressed, and the panel swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently enough to still carry the smell of the oil, sweetish and industrial amid all that stone-and-incense antiquity.

Inside, coiled on the shelf beside the winding drum, was a rope.

* * *

She did not touch it at first. She stood in the clock chamber's dimness with her heart going hard and slow, the way it did when the puzzle finally showed her its teeth, and she looked at the rope the way you look at a snake you've found sleeping in your own garden.

It was a bell rope — or it had been. Bell ropes are beautiful things, splayed at the grip end into a woven tail, bound with wool in the colors of the parish. This one had been cut from its bell, maybe eight feet of it, and the cut end was frayed. But what stopped her breath was the stain.

Along the lower third of the rope ran a discoloration, darker than age, soaked deep into the fibers and dried to a brown-black that no cleaning had fully lifted. She'd seen stains like that once before, three years ago, in a case file she was never supposed to have read — the Meridian's night manager had a brother on the police force, and Wendell had spent one long insomniac night with a folder of photographs concerning a fall from a fourth-floor fire escape that everyone but her had accepted at face value. The photographs had included the tarp the body was carried down on. Canvas, army-surplus, stained along one edge.

Fibers. She remembered the word from the pathologist's note — *textile fibers consistent with canvas recovered from the deceased's clothing* — remembered thinking at the time how strange it was that a man falling past a fire escape should come away carrying threads of a tarp nobody could account for.

And now here was a rope, hidden in a clock, stained the way a body stains the thing that holds it, and in her memory the case file opened itself page by page because Wendell Marchetti forgot nothing she had ever read.

Anselm Doyle had not fallen past anything. He had been lowered — or dropped — with a rope, and the rope had worn against him the way ropes wear, and it had carried away fibers of the canvas beneath him, and afterward someone had cut it free, folded it, and hidden it in the one place in the parish no one opens except to wind the clock. And the person who wound the clock, who else but the deacon? The deacon's hands, she thought. Ruth had said it herself, weeks ago, in another life: *Emil keeps the clock. Emil keeps everything.*

Her fingers, when she finally reached out, were steady. That surprised her. She took a corner of the stained section between thumb and forefinger, pressed it to her nose — old blood has a smell even dry, an iron-sweet ghost of it — and then, because evidence means nothing loose in your pocket and everything documented, she took out her phone and photographed the rope where it lay, the housing, the oiled hinges, the panel, the room, the view through the louvres with the timestamp visible in the corner. She photographed the chain on the doors too, on her way down — no. The chain was downstairs. She would photograph it when she passed it. If she passed it.

Because that was the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the panel swung open: the rope proved murder, and the rope also proved that Voss — or whoever hid it — believed the tower was safe. Safe to store the instrument of a killing ten feet from where the killing was staged as a fall. Safe because the tower was locked. Safe because *he* controlled the lock.

Except the lock had been forced from the inside. She'd found that herself, days ago, scratched paint and a bent hasp, evidence that someone had been up here since the funeral — Anselm himself, she'd assumed, some last arrangement, some final letter planted among the pigeons. But if Voss had intercepted the letters, then Voss knew about the arrangements, and if Voss knew Anselm had been up here, then Voss would have come up here too, searching, and found—

Found what? Or found nothing, and chained the doors anyway, because the message mattered more than the search?

A sound stopped her hand mid-note.

Below. Not in the tower — in the church proper. The heavy groan of the sacristy door, the one that needed a shoulder. Then footsteps on stone, unhurried, spaced like a man walking a familiar aisle. Then silence, the particular silence of someone standing still and listening.

Wendell killed her phone screen and stood in the dark of the clock chamber with the dead man's rope two feet from her elbow, and listened the way prey listens — not with curiosity anymore, not with the delicious analytical hum that had carried her through three mysteries and most of her life, but with the whole animal attention of a body that understands it is inside the trap and the trapper has come back early.

The footsteps resumed. They crossed the nave. They paused — she could picture it, the pause at the baptismal font, the pause at the third pew where Ruth always sat — and then they turned toward the tower stair, and the first tread on the bottom step creaked the way old stone steps creak, a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

There was nowhere to go but up, and up ended at the louvres forty feet above the slate roof.

She took the rope. It seemed right, somehow, obscene and right — she wrapped the stained length in her scarf, tucked it inside her coat against her ribs like a stolen relic, and moved. Not up the open center of the stair, where her silhouette would print itself against the louvre-light with every turn, but hugging the outer wall, in the shadowed curve, one hand on the cold stone, weight on the outsides of her feet the way she'd learned decades ago moving through the Meridian's corridors at 3 a.m., when the difference between a guest's rest and a guest's death had sometimes depended on exactly this kind of quiet.

The footsteps climbed below her, patient as a metronome. Voss wasn't hurrying. He knew the doors were chained. He knew the side doors were locked and the rectory was bolted. Wherever she was, she was in a box he had built, and boxes keep.

The second landing. The belfry again, the great bell looming in the dark like a drowned thing, and Wendell had a sudden image — vivid, unwelcome — of what a bell sounds like from four inches away when it swings, and of what a body sounds like. She put the image away. Later. Everything later.

Above the belfry, the final flight rose to the clock chamber and the louvred openings, and beyond the louvres there was the outer ledge — she'd noticed it her first week, a maintenance walkway no wider than a shoe, ringing the tower beneath the open arches where the bells showed themselves to the town. Maintenance men used it once a year. Pigeons used it daily. It was not an escape route; it was a place where people died in newspaper stories.

But the alternative was the stairwell, and the stairwell had a deacon in it.

She reached the clock chamber as the footsteps reached the belfry below, and pressed herself flat against the housing's north face, in the wedge of black behind the open panel, and made herself breathe the way you breathe when breathing is the loudest thing you own. Through the gap under the housing she could see the top steps, lit faintly by the town-glow through the louvres, and she watched the shadows there the way sailors watch the horizon.

The footsteps stopped at the belfry landing.

Silence. Long enough that her lungs began to burn with the discipline of quiet. Long enough that she could hear the clock mechanism ticking beside her ear, the escapement counting out seconds that belonged to someone else now, and beneath it, faintly, the sound of a man praying.

She couldn't make out the words. She didn't need to. She knew the cadence — the rise and fall of Latin learned in boyhood, worn smooth as the stair stones. Voss stood in the dark of his belfry reciting prayers over the space where Anselm had died, and the piety of it was the most frightening thing Wendell had encountered in all her years of noticing, more frightening than rage or greed, because rage and greed know they are sins, and this man prayed as though the rope in her coat were a rosary.

Then the footsteps started upward again.

* * *

Later — much later, when her hands had stopped shaking and the town had gone fully dark below her — Wendell would try to reconstruct how she got onto the ledge, and she never quite could. Memory kept the moments separately, unstrung: the panel closing behind her with her palm muffling the latch; the last three steps taken in the time between one tick of the escapement and the next; the cold slap of night air through the louvre slats; the ledge under her shoes, gritty with pigeon lime and sixty years of weather; the tower wall against her back, rough as a confession.

She stood on a shelf of stone six inches wide, forty feet above the churchyard, with a murdered priest's rope against her heart and a murderer's footsteps ten feet away on the other side of the wall, and she discovered what hunted animals know and comfortable women don't: that clarity is a form of terror, and terror is a form of clarity. Every fact she had gathered in six weeks of gentle questions rearranged itself at once into a single sharp line pointing at her own throat. The letters. The register. Tomas Brandt dying in his care home with a testimony in his mouth. Ruth Calloway begging her to stop before *Emil finishes his penance*. And now the chain on the doors, the prayer in the dark, the rope hidden in the clock — Voss was not cleaning up a crime. He was completing a set. Anselm. The witness from room 411. Tomas soon, surely, morphine being such a merciful accelerant. And now the organist who asked too many questions, whose body would be found — if it was found — at the foot of the tower where the priest fell, and the parish would grieve the poor substitute organist, accident-prone St. Brigid's, and Deacon Voss would preach beautifully at her funeral.

The footsteps crossed the clock chamber. She heard the panel open — the oiled hinge, soft as a sigh — and then a silence she could feel through the stone, a man looking at an empty shelf.

She did not wait to hear what he said to God about it.

The ledge ran leftward toward the south face, where the louvres stood open widest and the drop to the vestry roof was shortest. She went along it sideways, arms out, fingertips trailing the wet stone, the town spinning gently below whenever she glanced down — she made herself not glance down — and at the corner of the tower she found what she had gambled everything on finding: the old iron bracket where the steeplejacks anchored their harnesses, rusted but solid, and below it the shallow pitch of the vestry roof, and below that the yard behind the parish hall, and beyond the yard, the lane, and beyond the lane, the living world.

She went over the edge the way you leave a burning building — fast, committed, past the point of politeness. Slate under her shoes, then gravel, then grass, then the lane's cracked asphalt cold through her soles, and she walked, she did not run, she walked with the rope pressed against her ribs and her breath sawing despite her, out of the churchyard's iron gate, past the lych-gate, into the streets where people had their suppers on, and she did not stop until the parish was three blocks behind her and the hotel's lights rose ahead like a harbor.

In the lobby, Marcus looked up from the desk and said, "Rough gig?" — the organist, back late, nothing remarkable — and Wendell smiled the smile she kept for guests and liars and said something pleasant, and rode the elevator up with her hand flat against her sternum, holding the rope in place, holding herself together.

In her room she unwrapped it from the scarf and laid it on the bed, and under the lamp the stain looked almost black, almost holy, a relic of the opposite kind. She photographed it again, properly this time, scale beside it, and then she sat down and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to be afraid — fully, shakingly afraid — because fear was data too, and the data said: *he knows you know, and he is faster than you, and there is a letter out there somewhere that he hasn't found.*

One minute. Then she picked up the phone.

Nadia Brandt answered on the second ring, and before Wendell could speak, the young woman's voice came through low and urgent, tumbling: "I was going to call you — the solicitor called me tonight, he's released the last letter early, he wouldn't say why, only that someone else had been asking questions — Wendell, it names a date. October thirteenth. Tomorrow."

Wendell looked at the rope on the bed, and out the window toward the distant tower, black against a sky just beginning to bruise with dawn.

"Tomorrow," she said. "I know. I've already been there."
