## 2. Postmarked Yesterday

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing at the time.

Tuesdays were the slowest mail days in Marrow Bay — the regional sorter up in Kestrel Junction ran light after the weekend backlog cleared, and what trickled down to the harbor district was usually catalogs for stores that had closed years ago and bills addressed to people who'd moved inland. Freya Galloway collected her mail the way she did most things: late, efficiently, without expectation. She'd stopped at the bank of dented brass boxes in the lobby of the Cordova Building on her way home from shift, still in her dispatch jacket with its coffee stain shaped like a seagull she'd never been able to wash out, and there it was between a hydro bill and a flyer for a church rummage that had already happened.

A plain white envelope. Her name typed across the front — actually typed, the kind of slightly uneven impression you got from an old machine, the *y* in Galloway riding a hair higher than the other letters. No return address. A Marrow Bay postmark, smudged but legible.

She almost didn't open it in the lobby. Later, she would think about that a lot — the version of her who stood under the flickering fluorescent tube with a dead moth in the fixture, sliding her thumb under the flap because she was too tired to wait for the stairs. The version of her whose life split cleanly into before this envelope and after it, and who wouldn't know it for another few minutes yet.

Inside: one sheet of paper, folded once. Typewritten too. And no salutation, no signature. Just text, starting flush against the left margin like a police report or a prayer.

*You don't know me. That's the point.*

*I killed Daniel Odile. The paper will tell you he drowned, and the paper isn't lying, exactly. He did drown. But he drowned because I put him where the water could reach him, and I have been carrying that the way you carry a stone in your boot — fine while you stand still, unbearable the moment you try to walk.*

*He took a cab that night. Your cab. Unit nine, the Crown Victoria with the bad door seal on the passenger side, the one that whistles above forty miles an hour. He was picked up at the harbor steps at 11:47 p.m. Fare 4471. Check your log. You'll find it, or you'll find where it used to be.*

*You're the only one who keeps the true record of this town. Everyone else's books can be edited. Yours can't be, not really — not if someone reads them carefully enough.*

*I'm sorry it has to be you. But it couldn't be anyone who knew him. It had to be a stranger. Strangers do the arithmetic honestly.*

*There will be more.*

Freya read it twice standing in the lobby, then a third time walking up the two flights to her apartment, which was either very stupid or very human depending on how you looked at it. By the third read the words had stopped being words and started being weight. She let herself in, set the hydro bill on the counter out of pure habit, and put the letter flat on the kitchen table under the overhead light, the way you'd pin a specimen.

Then she sat down and made herself think.

Her first instinct — the one she trusted least and followed most — was dispatcher's instinct. Read the voice. Fifteen years of night shifts had trained her ear to strip language down to its load-bearing parts: what a caller said versus what they meant, the pause before a lie, the specific texture of panic versus performance. She'd talked overdosing teenagers through their worst hour and talked drunk husbands out of their wives' front doors, and she'd learned that everyone told you the truth if you listened to the shape of it instead of the content.

So she listened to the shape of this.

It wasn't raving. That was the first thing. No grandiosity, no taunting, none of the theatrical cruelty you'd expect from someone playing games with a stranger's mailbox. The voice on the page was tired. Almost gentle. *I'm sorry it has to be you.* Whoever wrote this believed every word, or had practiced believing it long enough that the seams didn't show.

And it was precise. Not the precision of imagination — imagination rounds its numbers, gets vague about clocks. This was the precision of memory. *11:47 p.m.* *Unit nine.* *Fare 4471.* *The bad door seal on the passenger side.* That last detail stopped her cold both times she read it, because it was true — unit nine's passenger door had been whistling since October, and she'd been after Halvorsen at the garage to fix the seal for three weeks before he finally got to it. Nobody outside the dispatch office knew that. Nobody outside dispatch even thought about it. It was the kind of detail that lived nowhere except in the head of the woman who listened to drivers complain about it every night at shift change.

Which meant whoever wrote this letter either worked with her, drove for her, or had spent a great deal of time listening to things they weren't supposed to hear.

She got up, checked the deadbolt out of reflex, and sat back down.

Daniel Odile. She turned the name over. She knew it the way you know a name from a town this size — peripherally, the way you know weather. He was the artist, the one who did those big moody harbor paintings that hung in the Maritime Museum and the waiting room at the dental clinic and, once, briefly and controversially, in the lobby of the cannery offices until management decided a painting of dockworkers with their heads bowed was bad for morale. She'd seen his face in the paper. Thin, dark-haired, a beard kept badly, eyes that looked at cameras the way some people look at doors they want to leave through.

Dead now. Drowned. Three weeks ago, maybe four — the timeline blurred the way small-town deaths blur, arriving as rumor first and fact later. She remembered the talk at the diner off Route 1: poor Odile, went in off the pier, must've been drinking, artists usually were. Case closed before the gulls finished arguing over the harbor. There'd been a funeral at St. Brendan's that half the town attended and nobody cried at, because Marrow Bay mourned the way it did everything else — quietly, suspiciously, and with an eye toward whether the deceased had ever treated anyone right at the fish counter.

The postmark. She picked the envelope back up and held it under the light, tilting it, reading the date stamp again to make sure her tired eyes weren't lying.

The postmark was dated the day after the funeral.

Not the day of. Not months after, when guilt ferments into confession. The day after. Which meant whoever wrote it had sat through the service — or known exactly when it was — and gone home and typed this instead of sleeping. Had walked it to the post office the next morning, or dropped it in the box by the ferry terminal, and let the machinery of ordinary mail carry it to her.

To her. To Freya Galloway, who had never met Daniel Odile, never spoken to him, never so much as shared a bus stop with him. As far as she knew, their paths had never crossed in thirty-eight years of living in the same eleven square miles of coastline. She racked her memory and came up with nothing. No gallery opening, no diner encounter, no wrong-number call. Nothing.

*Strangers do the arithmetic honestly.*

She read the line again and felt the first real chill of the evening move through her, the kind that doesn't come from temperature. Because the sentence wasn't just an explanation. It was a compliment, of a terrible kind. Someone had looked at the whole town of Marrow Bay — the harbormaster, the councilmen, the cops, the priests, the sisters and brothers and drinking buddies of the dead man — and decided that the one person who could be trusted with a murder confession was the woman who answered the phone at night and whom nobody, as far as she knew, had ever thanked.

*You're the only one who keeps the true record of this town.*

Her records. Her logs. Every fare, every pickup, every route, timestamped and initialed, fifteen years of the town's nocturnal movement preserved in carbon triplicate in the filing cabinet behind her chair. She'd always thought of it as the most boring archive on the coast. Somebody else apparently thought it was the only honest one.

She looked at the clock. 9:40 in the morning. She'd been up since six after a shift that ended at four, and sleep was sitting in the corner of the room making reasonable arguments she had no intention of hearing.

Unit nine. Fare 4471. 11:47 p.m.

There was only one way to know if the letter was telling the truth about any of it, and that way was downstairs in the dispatch office, in the cabinet, in her own handwriting.

* * *

The Bay Cab Company office occupied the ground floor of the Cordova Building, a storefront with a bulletproof-glass service window left over from some earlier, more optimistic decade's idea of crime. Inside it smelled the way it always smelled: burnt coffee, carbon paper, and the faint electrical warmth of the radio base station, which hummed even when powered down, like something dreaming. Freya had spent more waking hours of her life in this room than in her own apartment, and she knew its sounds the way sailors know rigging. This morning, alone in it, the silence sounded different. Watchful.

She unlocked the filing cabinet with her key — hers alone; the night dispatcher kept her own logs, that was policy, that was pride — and pulled the book for the relevant month. She had to work backward from the funeral date to find the death date, and the death date to find the night in question, and by the time she had the ledger open on the desk her hands were doing something she recognized distantly as trembling.

She flattened them against the page. Dispatcher's hands. Steady hands. Hands that had written down the last known movements of a hundred bad nights.

The entries marched down the page in her own neat print. 23:12, pickup at the Rusty Anchor, drop at Seaview Apartments, fare 4463, driver Okafor. 23:31, hospital run, fare 4466. 23:44, standby. Then—

Then nothing.

There should have been an entry at 11:47. The letter had named the minute with the confidence of someone reading off a document, and Freya's log was continuous — she prided herself on continuity, on the unbroken chain of fares, because a gap in a log was a question you'd have to answer someday. But between the 23:44 standby notation and the 00:15 pickup at the all-night diner, the page showed a clean stretch of ruled lines and empty space.

Except.

She leaned closer. Brought the desk lamp down low, angled the page against the light the way she'd seen Voss from the state police do once with a suspect document at some seminar she'd slept through half of. And there it was, faint as a watermark: ghosting beneath the blank lines, the pressure marks of writing that had been erased. Not crossed out — erased, carefully, the surface of the page disturbed in the shape of numbers. She could make out fragments. What might be a *9*. What was unmistakably, in the far column, the ghost of *4471*, the digits pressed deep enough by whatever pen had written them to survive their own destruction.

Someone had logged the fare. And then someone had removed it.

Freya sat back in the rolling chair, and the chair creaked in the quiet office, and outside the window a gull screamed at something on the roof across the street, and none of it registered. Her whole awareness had narrowed to the page in front of her, to the small violence done to it.

This was her book. Her territory. Fifteen years of nights, and in all that time she had never once doubted the integrity of these pages — not when drivers begged her to fudge a pickup time, not when the owner wanted overtime questioned, not when anybody asked for anything. The log was the one thing in Marrow Bay that didn't bend. And somebody had bent it. Somebody with access — a key, or a window of hours when the office stood empty, or simply the rank to stand behind her shoulder and say *let me see that* and take it from her hands.

The letter had said: *check your log, you'll find it, or you'll find where it used to be.*

It had known. Before she did, it had known that the entry was gone, and it had known she would find the wound where the entry used to live. Which meant the writer understood her records better than she'd ever understood them herself — understood that erasure leaves a scar, that carbon paper remembers, that the truth of a town survives in pressure marks if you hold the page up to the light.

Freya took out her phone and photographed the page. Then she photographed it again from a different angle, lamp lower, shadows longer, so the ghost-writing showed. Then, because fifteen years of institutional caution lived in her spine, she photographed the photograph's subject a third time with the office's ancient Polaroid from the supply drawer, so there existed a copy that lived on film, in a shoebox, unconnected to anything with a password.

Then she sat very still and let the dread finish arriving.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't avoid, the honest arithmetic of a stranger: if the letter lied, it lied with impossible precision, naming a fare number that existed in erased form in her own book. And if the letter told the truth — if Daniel Odile had gotten into unit nine at 11:47 on the last night of his life, and that ride had been written down and then unwritten by someone with keys to this room — then a man had died, and the record of his last movement had been murdered right along with him, quietly, bureaucratically, with an eraser.

And the second thing, the thing that made her look at the office door and check that it was locked:

Whoever sent that letter knew she would come here. Knew her routines, her pride, her habits of verification. Knew that Freya Galloway, given a claim and a filing cabinet, would always choose the cabinet.

*There will be more,* the letter had said.

She folded it back into its envelope, put it inside a manila folder, and put the folder in the bottom drawer of her desk, under the spare ribbons and the emergency chocolate, and locked the drawer. Then she pulled up the local paper's website on her phone with cold, careful fingers, and searched the archives for a name.

**ODILE DROWNING RULED ACCIDENTAL**, the headline said. **CASE CLOSED IN NINE DAYS.**

Nine days. Freya stared at the number, and at the article beneath it, and began, slowly, to read.
