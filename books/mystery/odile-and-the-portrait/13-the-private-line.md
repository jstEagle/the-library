## 13. The Private Line

The private line had a sound of its own.

Every dispatcher learns the voices on her board — the drunk, the desperate, the bored teenager testing whether the number still works — but the private line was different. It bypassed the queue, bypassed the logging software, rang straight to the old handset bolted under the desk with a cord frayed back to some administration that no longer existed. Officially it was for harbor authority calls, medical transports, the police. Unofficially it was for anyone who knew it existed, which in Marrow Bay meant maybe forty people, and half of them were dead.

It didn't ring like the other lines. The other lines chirped, bright and impatient. The private line had a low, flat bell, like something knocking from underwater.

Freya had been sitting with that fact for two days now, turning it over the way she'd turn a fare receipt with a smudged total. The second letter hadn't named a logged fare. It had named a call — 11:52 p.m., October the ninth, pickup at the fish market, destination withheld. A call she'd taken. A call she'd answered and then watched vanish, because the private line wrote nothing down. No timestamp in the system. No fare number. Just her own voice saying *Marrow Bay Cab, go ahead* into a receiver connected to nothing that remembered.

She'd checked her personal logbook — the paper one she kept out of habit, because systems crashed and Marrow Bay's system crashed often. There it was, in her own slanted hand: *11:52 PM — priv line — fish mkt — male, quiet — sent unit?* And then a blank where the unit number should have been, because she couldn't remember sending anyone, because by the time the call ended she'd already been pulled onto a rollover on Kestrel Road and the night had swallowed it whole.

That was the thing about being the town's memory. You remembered everything, and none of it well enough.

* * *

The fish market at midmorning was a different country from the fish market at midnight. By day it was gulls and hose water and men in rubber aprons shouting prices across ice beds; by night it was a canyon of shuttered stalls and sodium lights that turned the wet concrete the color of old bruises. Freya walked it now with her hands in her coat pockets, past the ice machines groaning through their cycles, looking for a payphone in a town that had spent a decade tearing them out.

There were three left in Marrow Bay. She'd driven past all of them before choosing this one, and she chose it for a stupid, perfect reason: it was the only one whose coin box still worked, according to Sal Ivers, who ran the bait shop next door and complained about everything with the devotion other men gave to religion. The phone hung on the wall between the market office and the loading dock, its housing sun-bleached to the pink of an old scar, its cord long enough to reach a plastic crate someone had dragged over years ago so callers could sit.

Someone had sat there. Recently or not, she couldn't say. But the crate was there, and the phone was there, and 11:52 p.m. on October the ninth, a man had stood in this exact spot and asked her — quietly, she remembered that much, quieter than any fare she'd ever booked — for a ride he never took.

Or did take. That was the question the letter wouldn't stop asking.

"You're staring at that thing like it owes you money."

Sal Ivers stood in his doorway wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a bollard, with the red-rimmed eyes of a man who'd spent fifty years arguing with weather.

"Does this phone work?" Freya asked.

"It works." He came out, squinting at it as if seeing it for the first time in months. "Nobody uses it. Everybody's got their little phones now. Except the drunks, and they just smash 'em." He nodded at a hairline crack in the handset. "Somebody did that in August. Or July. Time's soup down here."

Freya weighed how to ask what she needed to ask. In her experience people didn't remember nights; they remembered interruptions. "Sal, do you work late sometimes? Past midnight?"

"Market closes at ten. I lock up around eleven, eleven-thirty, depending on how bad the ice machine's acting." His eyes narrowed, not suspicious exactly, but wary in the way of a man who'd learned that questions from women with notebooks were rarely casual. "Why?"

"There was a man here one night in October. Late. Using this phone, or waiting near it. Maybe the ninth."

"I don't keep a calendar of who stands by the phone."

"No," Freya said. "But you'd remember somebody who didn't belong."

Sal chewed on that. Down the row, a forklift beeped its reverse warning, and a gull screamed theft at another gull. Then something shifted behind his eyes — the small recalculation of a man deciding that whatever this was, it wasn't going away.

"The ninth," he said slowly. "That'd be the night the Odile thing happened. The painter." He said it the way everyone in Marrow Bay said it now — *the Odile thing* — a death compressed into an event, a man compressed into a headline. "Cops all over the harbor road after. So yeah, I was up late. Couldn't sleep, came back down for cigarettes around midnight, and there was a fella at this phone."

Freya's pulse went careful and slow, the way it did when a voice on the line started telling her something true. "What did he look like?"

"Tall. Good coat — you know, one of those wool ones, dark, the kind that costs more than my truck. Not a fisherman. Not a drunk. Wrong shoes for this neighborhood." Sal rubbed his jaw. "He wasn't using the phone when I seen him. He was standing there holding the handset against his shoulder, like this—" Sal pressed an invisible receiver to his collarbone, "—like he was listening to somebody talk. And then he put it back real gentle, like it was made of glass, and he just stood there a minute looking at the water."

"Did he make a call at all?"

"He was off it by the time I got close. But the light was still on the display, if you know what I mean. Like he'd just hung up." Sal shrugged. "I said evening to him. He said evening back. Polite fella. Voice like a radio announcer."

*A voice like a radio announcer.* Freya felt the words land somewhere below her ribs. She thought of every caller she'd ever calmed, every frightened man she'd talked through a bad hour, and how she prided herself on hearing what people sounded like underneath what they said. A radio announcer's voice. Smooth. Practiced. A voice built to be believed.

"Which way did he go?"

"Toward the docks first. Then I lost him. There's a lot of ways to disappear down here after midnight." Sal looked at her properly for the first time. "You're the cab lady, aren't you? Night dispatcher. My wife knows your sister-in-law or something. You're the voice on the machine."

"I'm the voice," Freya said.

"You find out who smashed your phone, you let me know." He retreated toward his shop, then stopped. "That fella in the good coat. He waited maybe twenty minutes after hanging up. Just standing there. Like he was waiting for a cab that never came."

*Like he was waiting for a cab.* Freya thanked him and walked back to the payphone and stood where the man had stood, and looked at the water the way he had looked at it, and understood, with the cold clean clarity of a route finally resolving on a map, what she was looking at.

The killer hadn't just used her dispatch line. He'd used it the way you use a tool you've tested. He'd called the private line — the one line that left no record — from the one payphone that still functioned, at an hour when the only witness would be a man too tired to care. He'd spoken to her, or listened to her, and then he'd waited to see if she'd send a car.

And she hadn't. Whatever he'd said — however he'd framed the request — she hadn't sent a unit, and either that failure was the point, or the point had come later, in a car she'd dispatched without knowing what it carried.

Either way, he'd rehearsed her. He'd rung her like a bell and timed how long the echo lasted.

* * *

Back at the depot, the day shift was shuffling out and the boards were quiet, that dead-hour lull between the lunch fares and the school runs. Freya spread her materials across the dispatch desk the way other people spread tarot cards: the letter, photocopied twice and hidden twice more since Renner's visit; her paper logbook open to October; a printout of the official case summary Maren had gotten hold of through a records request that had taken four weeks and cost eighty dollars.

11:52 p.m., private line, fish market. She'd logged it herself and then failed to follow through — no unit assigned, no callback noted. Why? She closed her eyes and went back into the memory the way she went back into recordings, hunting the texture of the voice.

Quiet. That was what she'd written. Male, quiet. And now, dredging, she found more: he hadn't given a name. He'd given a location and a time — *the flats, low water, I'll need to be picked up before the road goes under* — and then he'd paused, and in the pause she'd heard the rollover coming in on Kestrel Road, sirens already in the audio feed, and she'd said *sir, I have to put you on—* and the line had gone dead. Not hung up angrily. Dead the way lines go dead when the caller has simply decided the conversation is over.

She'd never called him back. There was no number to call back. The private line didn't take incoming numbers; it was hardwired like something out of a war movie. He'd known that. He'd known that the only trace of him would be her memory, and memories could be doubted, dismissed, overwritten — especially the memory of a woman nobody in this town had ever needed to believe.

Her system. Her one sacred thing. He'd reached inside it and used the part that doesn't write anything down.

Freya pulled the tide tables for October and laid them beside the logbook. Low water at the flats that night: 12:40 a.m. The call at 11:52. Forty-eight minutes between the request and the road going under. Enough time for a cab to make the run out — twelve minutes if the driver didn't dawdle — and enough time for something else to happen at the edge of the water, in the dark, with the mud sucking at everything.

Daniel Odile had been alive when he left the harbor. She knew that now; the logs proved it, whatever Renner's file claimed. Which meant the last leg of Daniel's night was a ride that existed only in the place the killer had chosen precisely because it left no record.

Except it did leave a record. It left her.

She was writing the timeline in her notebook — *11:52 call, 12:00-ish hang-up, 12:40 low water* — when the private line rang.

The low, flat bell. The knock from underwater.

Freya looked at it. Her hand hovered. Every instinct she had as a dispatcher said answer; every instinct she had as a woman knee-deep in a murder said the same thing, and both instincts were screaming that this was impossible, because the private line was a harbor line, a police line, a line for people who knew it existed — and the man in the good coat had known it existed, and Detective Ivo Renner had access to every line in this building.

She picked up.

"Marrow Bay Cab."

Silence. Not empty silence — occupied silence, the kind with breathing in it, the kind where someone is listening to hear what you sound like. She'd held hundreds of these silences open on purpose, letting callers fill them with the truth they couldn't start saying. She knew how to wait. She waited.

Then, very softly, a click. Dial tone.

She sat there with the receiver against her ear, heart tapping out a rhythm she refused to acknowledge, and told herself: coincidence. Harbor authority testing the line. A wrong number. The town full of ghosts that every dispatcher eventually hears.

But her hand was steady when she wrote it down, because writing things down was the whole of her religion: *2:47 p.m., private line, no speech, approx. 20 seconds, hung up.*

He was checking the line. Or checking her. Or — and this was the thought that made her set down the pen — letting her know he could.

* * *

She almost called Voss. She had the sergeant's card taped inside her desk drawer, next to the spare fuses, and she got as far as holding it before she stopped. Voss was honest — Freya believed that with the cautious faith she extended to perhaps three people in Marrow Bay — but honest wasn't the same as safe. Anything that touched the department's phone system touched Renner, and Renner smiled the way a tide smiles, wide and flat and indifferent to what it covers.

Instead she called Maren.

"Maren Odile," the salvager answered, wind roaring behind her, somewhere out on her boat by the sound of it.

"It's Freya. The dispatcher." They were past formalities and not yet at friendship, that narrow strait between two women who'd decided to trust each other provisionally, like pilots exchanging signals. "I found where the second call came from. The unlogged one. Fish market payphone."

"And?"

"And there's a witness. Man in a good coat, tall, radio-announcer voice, hung up and waited twenty minutes watching the water. And then today—" she hesitated, hating how it would sound, "—today the private line rang at the depot. Nobody spoke. Twenty seconds, then gone."

The wind filled the pause. When Maren spoke again her voice had dropped into the register she probably used for reading depth gauges. "He's telling you he can reach you whenever he wants."

"That's one reading."

"It's the right one." A gull shrieked somewhere close to Maren's end. "Freya, listen to me. My brother called a taxi the night he died. We know that now. Somebody answered him. Somebody heard him alive at eleven-something and did nothing, or worse than nothing." The wind gusted; Maren waited it out like a sailor. "If that was you — if Daniel's last call to the outside world went through your board — then he didn't die talking to strangers. He died talking to the one person in this town who picks up."

Freya looked at the private line, squat and black under the desk, its frayed cord coiled like something sleeping. She thought about the man in the good coat pressing the handset gently to his shoulder, listening to her voice the way she listened to everyone else's. Two listeners, that night. Only one of them had sent help.

Or only one of them had sent a cab.

"I'm going to pull the maintenance records on that payphone," she said. "Coin collections, repair tickets. If he used it more than once, there'll be a pattern."

"And if there's a pattern?"

"Then he's been rehearsing longer than we think." She clicked her pen. "Maren — the man Sal described. Good coat, tall, smooth voice. I keep trying to make him into a stranger."

"But?"

"But I've heard that voice before. On my board. Calm, polite, the kind of voice that makes you trust the route." She stared at the wall where the roster hung, at the small printed card listing emergency contacts, and one name on it in particular. "I just can't yet prove where."

On the other end of the line, Maren was quiet a moment. "Then prove it," she said. "You're the one who keeps the record. Keep it."

Freya hung up and sat in the humming quiet of the depot, the afternoon light going amber on the windows, and beneath the desk the private line waited like a held breath. Somewhere in this town was a man who had dialed her voice to test whether she'd answer — and she was beginning to suspect, with a dread that settled into her bones like cold ballast, that the first time he'd called, the voice on the other end asking for a ride to the flats had belonged to Daniel Odile himself.

And that the man in the good coat had been standing at that payphone not making a call at all.

Listening to one.
