## 23. Renner Takes the Bait

The scanner on Renner's kitchen counter had been his wife's idea, back when there was a wife, back when the counter held a fruit bowl instead of a charging cradle and a stack of unopened mail. He'd kept the habit after she left — the scanner, not the fruit — because a man in his position needed to know what the town was saying before the town knew it was saying anything. Marrow Bay talked on the radio the way it talked in the Anchor Room: in fragments, in codes, in the long pauses where the truth got stuck.

At 9:47 on the morning of the spring tide, the truth got stuck, and then it came loose all at once.

"Dispatch to all units, be advised." The voice was female, calm, professionally flat. Freya Galloway's voice. He'd know it anywhere now; he'd been listening to it for weeks the way a man listens to a smoke alarm he can't find. "We have a civilian request logged, reference a recovered item at the tide flats, west channel. Party states she's holding property belonging to the Odile estate and will transfer custody to the named party at low water today, approximately eleven-forty. Meet location is the old oyster shed, flats access road. Requesting one unit, plainclothes, no lights. Caller identifies herself as—" a pause, the sound of keys, the small bureaucratic theater of it, "—A. Kowalczyk. Unit 12, retired."

Renner's coffee stopped halfway to his mouth.

He set the mug down with the exaggerated care of a man who has just heard his own name spoken in a room he thought was empty. The scanner hissed. Somewhere a unit acknowledged with a lazy click of the shoulder mic and a "10-4, dispatch, we're on the pier, can't take it." Nobody wanted the flats. Nobody ever wanted the flats. The call sat there in the air, unclaimed, and Renner listened to it die the way you'd listen to a match burn down toward your fingers.

A. Kowalczyk. Ada.

He hadn't heard her name spoken aloud in fourteen months. He'd thought about it, of course — thought about it the way you think about a tooth that aches only when you bite down. She'd left Marrow Bay in a hurry that nobody had explained, sold her cab to a cousin, taken a warehouse job in Corliss County two towns up the coast. He'd checked. Of course he'd checked. He'd driven up there once in the fall, sat in the parking lot of the Corliss Freight & Storage for forty minutes watching the loading dock, and decided she was a woman who had been paid to forget and had taken the money and the forgetting both. That was the deal. That was always the deal. People took the money and the forgetting came free, because remembering was the more expensive item on the menu and nobody ever ordered it twice.

But now she was calling dispatch. Now she was saying *recovered item* and *Odile estate* and *transfer of custody* in a voice that had been broadcast to every scanner in three counties.

He replayed it in his head, once, twice, the way he'd learned to do in interrogations — not listening to what was said but to how it was said. And that was where the first cold thread of doubt began to work its way in, because the call was too clean. Too complete. A scared woman didn't call dispatch with a parcel of information that neat. A scared woman called and hung up, or called and cried, or called and asked for a welfare check on a neighbor's dog. This was a press release. This was a woman who wanted witnesses.

Or a woman who wanted one witness in particular.

He turned off the scanner. The kitchen went quiet in that heavy, underwater way kitchens have when the appliance that's been humming all along stops. He stood at the counter with his hands flat on the laminate and made himself think, because thinking was the one thing he'd always been better at than the people who caught people, and the day he stopped trusting that was the day he'd already lost.

* * *

Here was what he knew, laid out on the counter like evidence he'd never book:

One. The Galloway woman had the letters. She'd reported the first one — he'd made sure it went into evidence, into his evidence, into the drawer in his office where things went to become nothing — and she'd reported it with the careful, flat politeness of a civilian who suspected the police the way you suspect a landlord. He'd read her right. He'd read everyone right for thirty years. But in the weeks since, something had shifted in her voice on the open channel. She'd started saying *copy* when she meant *noted*. She'd started logging calls that didn't need logging, timestamping things that had no business being timestamped, building — and he recognized the architecture because he'd spent a career dismantling it — a paper trail.

Two. The letters. Four of them now, or four that he knew of, because the fourth was his own composition and he knew its itinerary the way you know a letter you've written yourself: it had gone into her mailbox Tuesday, and as of Thursday morning it had not gone to the police, because he'd have seen the report. Which meant she was holding it. Which meant she was reading it. Which meant she was either believing it or testing it, and he'd built the fourth letter specifically to be believed — Ada's name in it, Ada's cab in it, Ada's guilt laid out with the loving specificity of a man framing a portrait. He'd retired the old dispatch codes for it, the ones from before the system upgrade, because Ada would have used the old codes, Ada was an old-codes woman, and the detail would sing with authenticity.

Except.

Except that he had never actually done her job. He'd ridden in the front seat of a hundred cabs and sat behind a dispatch desk exactly never, and there was a species of knowledge that lived only in the doing, the way you can't learn a tide table from a book. He'd thought about that once, briefly, composing the fourth letter at three in the morning with the burner phone propped against the lamp, and he'd decided the risk was small. Small risks were his profession. He managed them the way other men managed their blood pressure.

Three. The tide. Today was the lowest water of the season — minus two point one feet at 11:42, a number he'd had memorized since March, because the flats gave things up at low water and he'd spent fourteen months being very, very sure the flats had given up everything. The sketchbook was the thing that wouldn't stay buried in his head. Daniel Odile had held it on his knees in the back of the cab, Renner remembered that, remembered the boy's hands — artist's hands, ink-stained, restless — turning the pages while the harbor lights slid past the windows. He'd told himself the book went down with him, into the channel, into the silt, into the category of things the sea keeps. He'd told himself that for fourteen months. But the sea had been in a giving mood lately. He could feel it the way sailors feel weather.

Four. Ada Kowalczyk was either calling to sell him out or calling to be heard selling him out, and the difference between those two things was the difference between a problem and a catastrophe.

He poured the coffee out. He didn't want it anymore. He wanted to be moving.

* * *

The drive to the station took six minutes and he used all of them. Not the department — he couldn't be seen near this, not today, not with the flats call sitting in the log like a lit fuse — but the records annex, the windowless brick afterthought behind the station where the closed files lived in banker's boxes and nobody went unless they were paid to or curious to, and Renner was both. He still had a key. Keys outlasted assignments; that was the quiet joke of every institution he'd ever worked inside.

The Odile box was where he'd left it, third shelf, misfiled under *O'Dell* the way he'd misfiled it himself nine days after the drowning, back when the case was hot and the syndicate's money was warm in the pocket of his good coat. He carried it to the annex table and went through it with the speed of a man rereading his own handwriting.

The file was thinner than it should have been. He knew that the way you know a wallet is short — not by counting, but by the weight. He'd removed the autopsy addendum in the winter, burned it in a coffee can behind his brother-in-law's boat shed, and the ash of it had gone into the bay with the outgoing tide, which he'd found satisfying at the time in a way that embarrassed him now. But the dispatch records — the copies, the ones the department had pulled from the taxi company's system — were still here, and he flipped to the night of the drowning and read the fare log the way a gambler reads his losing tickets.

Fare 4471. Harbor district to the flats road, 11:03 p.m. Logged, then overwritten — he'd done the overwriting himself, from the records terminal, a five-minute job with his access code and a plausible error message. The cab that never officially existed. The boy's last ride, erased by the man who'd driven him to the edge of the water and put him in it.

And below it, in the system's indifferent memory, the ghost of the private-line call. The one that had come to Freya Galloway's desk at 10:41 that night, unlogged, untraceable except that nothing was ever untraceable to the woman who owned the switchboard. He'd made that call himself, from the fish market payphone, in his good coat, while Daniel Odile waited at the harbor rail believing the cab was coming for him. He'd used her dispatch system like a tool, like a lever, like an accomplice, and the thought of it had given him a pleasure at the time that he now recognized — with the flat, clinical honesty he reserved for three a.m. — as the pleasure of a man who had stopped distinguishing between the crime and the cleverness of the crime.

That was the thing the letters had been for. He understood that about himself now, or told himself he did, which amounted to the same architecture. The letters weren't guilt. Guilt was for men with somewhere to put it. The letters were control — a confession schedule, a narrative, a story with him written out of it and a drowning man's taxi written in. If the story was going to exist, and it was, because the Galloway woman would not stop pulling threads, then it was going to be *his* story. Every letter was a hand on the tiller. The fourth letter had put Ada at the wheel.

And now Ada was calling dispatch.

He closed the box. He stood in the annex's fluorescent hum and let the last comfortable assumption of fourteen months die quietly in his chest: the assumption that Ada had taken the money and stayed bought. Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't dodge — she hadn't called him. Fourteen months of silence, and when she finally surfaced, she hadn't called the number he'd given her, the burner, the one that still worked, he was almost certain it still worked. She'd called *dispatch*. Openly. On the record. With a name attached.

That wasn't a woman coming to renegotiate. That was a woman who had already renegotiated with someone else.

* * *

He went home the long way, past the harbor, past the fish market where the payphone still hung on its rusted bracket like an exhibit in a museum of himself. The tide was already pulling back, the flats emerging in their long brown exposure, the oyster shed at the end of the access road sitting on its stilts with its shadow stretched thin and long across the wet sand, pointing at him like something with an opinion.

He parked where he could see the shed and did what he did best, which was nothing, visibly, for forty minutes.

The access road stayed empty. A heron worked the channel margin with the patience of a creditor. The water withdrew another inch, then another, and the flats gave up their ordinary archaeology — bottle glass, a crab pot door, the white vertebrae-look of old pilings — and gave up nothing else. No car. No Ada. No Galloway woman in her hatchback with its dispatch radio antenna whipping in the wind.

Which was, he admitted to the windshield, exactly what it would look like if it were a trap.

He made himself walk the other side of it anyway, because the alternative was the thing he could not survive: doing nothing, and being wrong. If Ada was really there, really holding something — a page of the sketchbook, a photograph, a memory with a date on it — and he let it walk into a police station, then the fourteen months of careful erasure were over, and everything after would be done *to* him instead of *by* him. He had built his whole life on the principle that the man who acts owns the room. He was not going to surrender the room to a cab driver and a dispatcher.

And if it was a trap — well. He smiled at the windshield, at the heron, at the whole flat gray theater of the tide. If it was a trap, it was a trap built by a woman who answered phones for a living, and he was a detective with thirty years of knowing how rooms got built. He'd walk in knowing it was a trap, and he'd walk out having turned it inside out, and then he'd know exactly how much she had, and knowing was nine-tenths of winning. The other tenth was the good coat and the smile and the badge in the back pocket, and he had all three.

There was one more thing. He took out the burner phone and looked at it for a long moment, and then he did the thing he'd been not-doing for fourteen months: he called Ada's number in Corliss County.

It rang four times. Five. He was composing himself for the voicemail when she picked up, and her voice came down the line small and strange, a voice with a hand over it.

"Who is this?"

"Ada." He put the warmth in, the old warmth, the payroll warmth. "It's been a while. I heard you might be coming back to town."

A silence so long he checked the screen to see if the call had died.

"I don't know what you heard," she said.

"I heard you found something. Out at the flats." He kept his voice level, almost kind. "I'd hate for you to make a trip for nothing. The tide's a liar, Ada. It shows you things that aren't yours."

Another silence. And then — and this was the moment, the one he would replay later, the one he'd fail to read correctly because he wanted so badly to read it correctly — she said, quietly: "Eleven-forty. The old oyster shed. If you have something to say, say it there."

And she hung up.

He sat with the phone against his ear, listening to the dead line, and felt the strange double exposure of the moment: triumph and warning laid over each other in the same image, like a double-printed photograph. She'd confirmed the meet. She'd confirmed it *to him*, the man she was supposedly selling out. Either she was too scared to hang up on the payroll that had kept her employed, or she wanted him there — wanted him there so badly she'd risked the whole performance to make sure he came.

He chose the first reading. He chose it the way a man chooses a door in a burning building, which is to say quickly, and toward the light, and without checking what's behind it.

* * *

He dressed for it the way he'd dress for a funeral he was attending as a stranger: dark coat, no badge, the unremarkable sedan instead of the department car. He loaded the recording pen into the inside pocket — if there was going to be a conversation at the flats, it was going to be *his* conversation, on *his* tape, a confession from Ada with the framing already built around it, and if it came to that, the fourth letter and the pen would make a tidy married pair. He checked the tide table one last time. Minus 2.1 feet, 11:42. The flats would be at their widest, their most exposed, their most honest. Everything the sea had been holding would be in plain sight, and everything a man said out there would carry across the wet sand like it was spoken in a church.

He left the scanner on as he walked out. He always left the scanner on. It was the last thing his wife had given him that still worked.

"Dispatch to all units," said the voice at 11:12, calm, flat, professionally perfect, as he pulled out of his driveway. "Be advised, plainclothes meet in progress, flats access road, oyster shed, reference Odile matter. Recording in progress. All units copy traffic only."

*Recording in progress.*

Renner's hands went still on the wheel for the length of a heartbeat, and then he laughed — one short breath of it, surprised out of him — because it was so neat, so perfectly neat, a broadcast announcement on an open channel, the kind of thing no dispatcher alive would say unless she wanted it heard. Unless she wanted *him* to hear it. Unless she was telling him, in the only language they shared: *come and see. Bring your badge. The whole town's listening.*

And that was the moment the trap closed — not at the flats, not at 11:42, not in the shadow of the oyster shed, but here, on Harbor Road, in the unremarkable sedan, in the space between one calm sentence and the next. Because a man who has spent thirty years reading rooms will read any room he's given, and she had given him one, and he drove into it at eleven-twenty with the tide going out ahead of him like an usher, believing all the way down the access road that he was the one arriving to listen.

The oyster shed stood on its stilts against the enormous flat light. The water was still withdrawing. Somewhere under all that shining mud, a sketchbook waited with its pages full of him.
