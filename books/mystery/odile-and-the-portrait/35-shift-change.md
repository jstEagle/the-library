## 35. Shift Change

The offer came on a Tuesday, which Freya would later decide was the first wrong thing about it. Good news in Marrow Bay arrived on Fridays, when people had time to feel it. Bad news came at night. Tuesday was a nothing day, a filler day, and Voss delivered the offer in the dispatch office at four in the afternoon, standing under the fluorescent hum with her hat in her hands like a woman who'd rehearsed this and still wasn't sure of her lines.

"Ilsa." Freya didn't get up. The headset stayed around her neck, warm from her skin. "You can't smoke in here."

"I'm not smoking."

"You're holding it like you want to."

Voss looked down at the unlit cigarette, seemed surprised to find it there, and set it on the corner of the console next to the tide table magnet — the one Maren had given her, laminated, the year's lowest water circled in red ink that had faded to pink. "The department's creating a position," she said. "Civilian analyst. Case-intake review. It's attached to my unit."

"Attached how?"

"Desk. Two doors from mine. You'd read files the way you read calls." Voss paused. "You'd have found Renner in nine days instead of eleven months, if someone had been listening to the intake the way you listen to a radio. That's the pitch. That's the whole pitch."

The radio crackled — Unit 9, a welfare check on Elderbrook Road, an old man who hadn't answered his phone in two days, and Freya's hand went to the transmit key out of pure habit while she kept her eyes on Voss. She gave the unit the gate code off the top of her head, told them about the dog, a brown one, half-blind, that wouldn't bite but would scream like it was dying, and the deputy laughed once, tight, and signed off.

When she looked back up, Voss was watching her with the expression of someone who had just made her own argument for her.

"That," Voss said. "That thing you just did. The gate code. The dog. You did it while deciding whether to take a job."

"The job doesn't require deciding. The radio does."

* * *

She thought about it through the rest of the shift, which is to say she didn't think about it at all, because the shift didn't allow thinking. A drunk fare called himself a lost king and needed delivering to a wife who no longer lived at the address he gave; Freya talked him into going to his brother's instead, three miles east, and heard him weep with gratitude when the porch light came on. A woman called twice in forty minutes to report a car that might have been following her and then called a third time to say it was her husband's car and could they please forget the first two calls, and Freya said of course, and logged all three, because forgetting was not something the log did.

That was the thing about the work nobody understood who hadn't done it. The radio didn't care what you felt. It cared what you said, when you said it, and whether you were still there at 3 a.m. when the voice on the other end started to come apart. Everything else — the badges, the titles, the two-door offices — was furniture in a room where the real work happened without any of it.

At 2:40 a.m., the private line rang.

It still rang, sometimes. Less than it used to. Since the trial, since the papers, since the mural went up on the wall across from her console — Daniel's roof light rendered in tile and grout by Maren's hands, glowing amber even under fluorescents — the private line had become something between a confession booth and a memorial. People called it now knowing whose voice used to answer it. They called to say things they couldn't say to anyone wearing a uniform.

Tonight it was a young man who wanted to know if it was true that a dispatcher had caught a detective. He said it carefully, like testing ice.

"It's true," Freya said.

"How?"

"She listened. Same as I'm doing right now."

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "I don't need anything. I just wanted to know somebody picks up."

"Somebody always picks up," Freya said, and meant it as protocol, not comfort, and heard him understand it that way too, before he hung up.

* * *

Maren came by at dawn, the way she sometimes did, smelling of diesel and cold water, carrying two coffees in a cardboard tray and setting one on the console without asking, because asking would have required conversation and Maren saved her words for things that mattered.

"You look like you slept in the chair," Maren said.

"Close enough."

"Voss was here yesterday." Maren lowered herself onto the spare stool, the one Ada used to sit in, back when Ada still came around, before the county line and the immunity deal and the slow work of learning to sleep through a full night. "Word travels. Harbor's smaller than the police think."

"She offered me a desk."

"A desk." Maren turned the word over like a piece of salvage she was pricing. "Two doors from hers."

"Something like that."

"And?"

Freya looked at the mural. In the early light through the blinds, the amber tiles caught fire and threw a faint glow across the ceiling, the way a cab's roof light must have thrown it across the inside of a car on a wet night, seen from below, from the passenger seat, from the last seat Daniel Odile ever occupied. She had spent eleven months learning what that light meant. She was not sure she wanted a job that required her to explain it in reports.

"And I haven't said yes," Freya said.

Maren drank her coffee. Outside, the harbor was waking up — gulls, a winch, the low complaint of a boat that needed its bottom scraped. "You know what my brother would say?"

"He'd say paint it."

"He'd say paint it," Maren agreed. "But he'd also say — and he wrote this in a letter once, one of the ones Voss mailed me — he'd say that institutions are just rooms where somebody decided whose voice counts. And the whole trick is figuring out if you're the room or the voice." She set the cup down. "I dive wrecks, Freya. I know what happens to things that leave the water. They fall apart faster. Some things are only themselves where they live."

Freya said nothing. The radio murmured its dead-channel hush, that particular silence a dispatcher learns to hear as a kind of company.

* * *

She gave Voss her answer on Thursday, in person, because a phone call would have been cowardice dressed as efficiency.

They walked out to the lot behind the station, where the light was honest and there was nowhere to sit and make it comfortable. Voss listened all the way through without interrupting, which was the most professional thing Freya had ever seen her do.

"I'll consult," Freya said. "When you've got intake that smells wrong — codes retired, times overwritten, fares that never got logged — send it to me. I'll read it the way I read a shift. But I do it from the console. Not a desk."

"You're turning down a career."

"I'm keeping one." Freya nodded toward the building, toward the room inside it where the private line lived, where the letters lay in the drawer under the tide table, filed not as trophies but as proof of concept. "Eleven months ago a man called this town in panic and one person picked up. That's the entire difference between a closed case and a closed coffin. I'm not going two doors away from that. I'm going to stay exactly where the picking up happens."

Voss was quiet. Then she took out the cigarette, looked at it, and put it away unlit, which Freya chose to take as respect.

"For what it's worth," Voss said, "the analyst position description says 'must be reachable at all hours.' I wrote it myself. I figured whoever takes it should be someone already answering at all hours." She held out her hand. "Consultant, then. Per call. The paperwork will be obscene."

"The paperwork always is," Freya said, and shook it.

* * *

Her shift ended at six. She stayed until 6:20, because the day dispatcher, a kid named Tomas with good instincts and bad handwriting, was still learning the gate codes, and because leaving a console mid-handoff felt like leaving a sentence unfinished.

Walking out, she passed the mural. Morning sun hit it full now, and the roof light blazed — amber, steady, the light that means *someone is coming for you*, the light Daniel had drawn from the inside looking out, or maybe from the outside looking in; Maren said even she didn't know which, and Freya had decided the not-knowing was the point.

Her car sat in the lot. Her home was eight minutes away. Her bed was in it, and the bed knew her name.

The radio in her pocket — the personal one, the one Voss had issued with the consultant paperwork, the compromise made hardware — crackled once.

Not the department channel. The private line frequency, forwarded, the way it always was now, to wherever she was.

A voice. Young. Scared in the specific register she had spent fifteen years learning to hear — scared the way people are scared when the danger hasn't arrived yet but has been scheduled.

"Is this — is this the taxi place? I don't know who else to—"

"Dispatcher," Freya said, already turning back toward the door, already reaching for the log. "I've got you. Start at the beginning."

Behind her, through the glass, the roof light burned on.
