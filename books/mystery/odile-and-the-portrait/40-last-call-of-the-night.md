## 40. Last Call of the Night

The night had been the kind Marrow Bay gave you in late autumn — long, wet, and mostly empty. By four a.m. the dispatch board had gone quiet except for the hum of the radio and the tick of the wall clock that ran two minutes fast and always would, because no one who worked nights trusted a clock that agreed with the day shift.

Freya Galloway sat with her feet hooked on the rung of her chair, a mug of coffee gone cold beside the tide table she still kept taped to the desk's edge, though there was no case anymore, no reason, just habit — or maybe not habit. Maybe memory. The paper was soft at the corners now, the ink faded where her thumb had worried it through a winter of phone calls. She'd replaced it twice. Same table, same tape. Some things you kept because they were true.

Unit 9 was dropping a fare at the cannery road. Unit 3 had gone home sick an hour ago. The harbor lights burned their usual orange through the rain-streaked window, and beyond them the flats lay dark under a rising tide, holding whatever they held, giving back nothing tonight, which was fine. The sea had finished its testimony a year ago. Freya had walked out to it on the anniversary, at low water, and found nothing but the clean ribbed sand and a single gull standing like a man waiting for a cab that wasn't coming. She'd been glad. That was the strange part she never told anyone — how glad she'd been, to find nothing.

The letters lived in the bottom drawer, three inches of them now, tied with the rubber band from the morning paper. Not trophies. Protocol. Voss had asked her once, gently, if she wanted to hand them over to evidence, and Freya had said no, and Voss had nodded like she understood, because Voss did understand — some records belong to the person who kept them alive.

At 5:12 the board lit up.

She saw the number before she answered, the way she always did, reading the screen the way other people read faces. Payphone. Fish market. The old one, the rusted shell of it that the town kept threatening to remove and never did, because removing things in Marrow Bay required a committee and committees required daylight.

Her hand paused over the button for half a second. A year ago that half-second would have been dread. Now it was something else — attention, total and quiet, the way a lighthouse doesn't panic when it sees a boat.

She picked up.

"Dispatch, this is Freya."

Silence first. Then breathing — quick, shallow, the breathing of someone young, or someone scared enough to sound young. Rain on the receiver, wind, the hollow boom of a payphone's plastic hood catching gusts off the water.

"Hello?" she said again, softer. "I'm here."

"I—" The voice cracked, started over. "Is this — is this the taxi place?"

"It's the taxi dispatcher. My name's Freya. Who am I talking to?"

A pause so long she checked the line was still live. Then: "Danny."

Something moved through her chest, slow and cold and then warm, all in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Not *the* Danny. Not Daniel Odile, who had been dead for more than a year, whose sketchbook hung framed behind glass at the library now, whose mural glowed on the dispatch office wall every time she switched on the hall light — the cab's roof light seen from inside, amber and endless, Maren's brushstrokes turning grief into a lamp. This was another Danny. They were always another Danny. The town produced Dannys the way the sea produced fog.

"Okay, Danny," she said. "Where are you?"

"The fish market. I think — I don't know where I am exactly. The address thing is broken." A breath. "I called the wrong place before. I called nine-one-one and they put me on hold. They put me on *hold*, lady."

"They do that sometimes." She kept her voice level, unhurried, the voice she'd spent fifteen years building out of night shifts and drunk fares and heartbreak calls — the voice that said *nothing happening here is too big for me*. "But you got me now, and I'm not going anywhere. Tell me what's going on."

"I took my dad's car." The words came out fast now, tumbling, the dam broken. "I'm sixteen. I don't have a license. I was gonna drive to Kestrel Point, my mom's there, she left in March, and I was gonna — I don't know what I was gonna do. Ask her to come home, I guess. Stupid."

"Not stupid," Freya said. "Wanting your mom isn't stupid."

"And then the car died." A wet laugh, awful. "Right here. Out of gas, probably. And it's raining, and my phone's dead, and the payphone only takes quarters and I had three, and I used one calling nine-one-one and they *held* me—"

"Danny. Look at me. Well — listen to me. You're not in trouble. Not with me. First thing: are you hurt? Are you cold? Are you safe right this second?"

"I'm okay. I'm just — I keep thinking about the water." His voice dropped. "It's right there. The flats. I could hear it. It sounds like it's breathing."

Freya closed her eyes for one second. One second only. Then she opened them and reached, without looking, without needing to look, for the pad by her elbow, and pulled the board toward her, and keyed the mic with her free hand.

"Unit 9, Dispatch."

"Go ahead, Dispatch." Ruiz. Good, steady Ruiz, who had driven nights since before the Odile case and stayed after, because he said somebody had to keep the frequency honest.

"I need you at the fish market payphone, north end. Priority one, non-emergency. Sixteen-year-old male, stranded, vehicle out of fuel. Bring a blanket from the kit and treat him better than you treat me."

A pause, and then Ruiz's voice came back warmer than regulation allowed. "Copy that, Dispatch. Five minutes out."

"You hear that, Danny? There's a man named Ruiz coming to get you. Big guy, drives cab twelve — well, unit 9 now, but he'll always be twelve to me. He's got a blanket and he'll probably have a story about his daughter you won't be able to shut up about. Five minutes."

"Five minutes," Danny repeated, like a man being handed coordinates.

"Now here's what we're gonna do while we wait," Freya said. "You're gonna stay on the line with me. Can you do that?"

"Yeah."

"Good. Because I've got a rule on this frequency: nobody rides alone at night. Not drivers, not fares, not kids in stolen cars at the fish market. If you're on my line, you're not alone. That's the whole job, really. Everything else is paperwork."

He laughed — small, startled, real. "That's a weird job."

"It's the best weird job in the world. Now talk to me. What's your mom's name?"

"Colleen."

"Colleen. And does Colleen know you were coming?"

"No. God. She'd kill me."

"Well, we'll work on our pitch before she gets the chance. Start from March. Why'd she leave?"

And he talked. He talked the way people talk at five in the morning when the night has stripped everything else away — about the fight he couldn't remember the shape of, only the temperature of; about the house that went quiet in a way houses shouldn't; about school, which had become a rumor he attended occasionally; about the water, again, the flats, how at night it sounded like something inhaling. Freya listened the way she listened, which was not waiting for her turn but actually listening, marking the places where his voice steadied and the places where it frayed, steering him gently away from the frays the way you'd steer a cab around ice.

Once, mid-sentence, he stopped. "Hey. Are you the one from the newspaper? The dispatcher? With the detective and the artist guy?"

The question landed soft as sleet. "I'm the one," she said.

"My uncle knew him. Daniel. Said he used to draw the boats down at the pier and give the drawings away to kids." A pause. "Did they ever figure out why the letters went to you? I read about it. Everybody read about it."

Freya looked at the mural through the office doorway — the roof light glowing on the far wall, amber against the dark, seen from inside a car that no longer existed, painted by a sister who had learned to hold a brush the way her brother held a pencil.

"Because once," Freya said, "he called a taxi line in the middle of the night, scared, and I picked up. That was all. I didn't know it mattered. But it turned out being the person who picks up matters more than almost anything."

"That's it? That's the whole reason?"

"That's the whole reason. Which should tell you something, Danny. Somebody answered for him, and it was too late, and I've thought about that every day since. So now when the phone rings, I answer like it's already too late for somebody. You know what I mean?"

He was quiet a moment. "Yeah," he said, and he did, she could hear it, hear the sixteen-year-old understanding settle into him like ballast. "Yeah, I think I do."

Headlights swept the rain outside the fish market — she could hear them before she saw them on the monitor feed, Ruiz's wipers going, the cab easing to a stop by the payphone hood. Through the line she heard the door, the boots on wet concrete.

"That's Ruiz," she said. "Danny. You did good tonight. You called. Of all the things you could've done with a dead car and three quarters, you called. Remember that. Whatever happens with your mom, with any of it — calling works. Someone always picks up."

"Someone always picks up," he repeated.

"Not everyone," Freya said honestly. "Not everywhere. But on this frequency, yeah. Always. Now go get in the warm car."

The line clicked as the hood opened, and she heard Ruiz's voice, low and kind, and Danny saying, "You're the blanket guy," and Ruiz laughing, saying, "I'm the blanket guy," and then the door shutting, muffling the world, and then nothing but the dial tone humming like the sea at rest.

Freya set the handset down gently, the way you set down something sleeping.

* * *

By six the sky over Marrow Bay had turned the color of dishwater going pale, and the day dispatcher, Theo, came in shaking rain off his coat and complaining about the ferry schedule like the ferry schedule had personally wronged him, which, knowing the ferry company, it probably had.

"Anything overnight?" he asked.

"One pickup at the fish market. Kid, stranded. Ruiz handled it."

Theo grunted, logged it, and moved on to his coffee ritual, which involved more ceremony than most weddings.

Freya stayed a few minutes past shift change, the way she did. She untied the rubber band on the drawer and looked at the letters — the confessor's careful block print, the retired codes, the tender apologetic third letter, the forged fourth that had undone him because he'd never learned the difference between a fare and a life. Renner was eleven months into a nine-year sentence at Fairhaven, pleading to obstruction and manslaughter while the syndicate scattered its murder across three silent men who would each serve less time than Daniel Odile had been dead. It wasn't justice. Freya had stopped expecting justice somewhere around the second letter. It was arithmetic — partial, bitter, real — and it was more than the sea usually gives back.

Voss had told her once, over bad coffee at the Harbor Light, that the department was different now. Freya had said she believed the department wanted to be different, and Voss had laughed and said that was the most honest answer anyone had given her in twenty years, and they'd left it there, because both of them knew institutions don't change, they just get watched harder. Freya watched. That was her role now, unofficial, unbudgeted: the listener. When Voss's unit needed a voice analyzed, a timeline rebuilt, a record cross-checked against the tide tables, they called the taxi line and asked for Freya, and Freya picked up.

She locked the drawer. She shrugged into her coat.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The flats were emerging from the ebb, wide and silver and patient, and somewhere out on Kestrel Point a boy named Danny was probably asleep in a warm kitchen while a woman named Colleen decided, hopefully, to come home. The gulls were starting their arguments. The cannery whistle wouldn't blow for another hour.

Freya stood on the dispatch office steps a moment, looking at the water, and let herself feel the thing she felt at the end of every shift now — not triumph, not closure, nothing so finished as that. Just the quiet certainty of a woman who knows the phones will ring again tonight, and tomorrow, and every night after, and that on the other end of each ring will be someone at the edge of something, needing one voice in the dark to say *I'm here.*

Someone always picks up.

That was the whole of it. That was the whole of the justice we get.

She turned up her collar and walked home through the waking town, and behind her, in the empty office, the radio hummed on its standby frequency, patient as the tide, ready for the next call, and the next, and the next — and when it came, as it always came, the voice that answered would be hers, or Theo's, or Ruiz's, or some kid not yet born who'd learn the board the way she had, one frightened caller at a time.

The light on the wall stayed on.

It always would.
