## 2. An Entry Past the End

The evidence for the trial of Raúl Serrano fit into a single gray archival box, and Ines Quintana hated it with a passion she usually reserved for mislabeled folios.

She had said as much to the box itself that morning, standing in the archive's back room with her sleeves pushed up and her reading glasses perched on her nose like a small disapproving bird. "You are insufficient," she told it. The box did not argue. Boxes never argued; that was why she preferred them to people.

Three years of a disappearance, one man's liberty, and the reputation of the Cathedral of Santa Rufina—and it all came down to forty-one items. A photograph of the crypt door. A chain-of-custody form signed in handwriting so loopy it might have been a seismograph reading. A pair of gardening gloves with soil on them, because of course there was soil on them; Raúl Serrano had been the groundskeeper for nineteen years, and soil was his native language. And at the bottom, wrapped in acid-free tissue like something precious instead of something terrible: Marisol Vega's diary.

Ines lifted it out with both hands, the way she'd been taught to handle incunabula, and felt the familiar ache settle behind her ribs. It was a cheap notebook, really. Spiral-bound, its cover a faded turquoise printed with a pattern of lemons that Marisol had bought from the stationery shop on Calle del Agua because, she'd claimed, it made her look organized even when she wasn't. Ines knew this because she had been with her when she bought it. That was the kind of fact Ines collected without meaning to—the way other people collected stamps or grudges. She remembered the exact afternoon: late September, three years ago, Marisol holding two notebooks against her chest and asking which color suited her better, and Ines saying the turquoise, because it matched nothing else Marisol owned and therefore suited her perfectly.

Marisol had laughed. Then she had vanished eleven days later, and the notebook had turned up inside the locked crypt beneath the cathedral, and everything after that had been paperwork.

"Right," Ines said aloud, because the silence in the archive was the kind you could talk into without feeling foolish. "Let's be thorough."

Thoroughness was her religion, more reliably than the one practiced upstairs. She set out her tools on the green baize mat: cotton gloves, a magnifying loupe, a ruler, a pencil—never pen near documents, a rule she'd broken exactly once in her career and still thought about during sermons. She opened the diary to the first page and began, as she had done twice before, to read her dead friend's handwriting.

It was good handwriting, loose and slanted, with letters that leaned forward as if hurrying somewhere. Marisol had written the way she spoke: too fast, too much, delighted by her own tangents. The early entries were full of the town. Doña Pilar's dog had eaten an entire rosary and passed the beads over four days, which Marisol declared a miracle of digestion rather than faith. The new priest at San Benito couldn't sing. The oranges were early. Ines appeared in the margins of these pages the way she appeared in life—quietly, usefully. *Ines says I should write dates at the top. Ines is right, obviously, don't tell her.*

Ines had not told her. She had underlined the entry in her heart instead.

* * *

By midmorning the light through the archive's high window had gone from pale gold to honest white, and Ines had reached the last dated entry: October 3rd. Three days before Marisol disappeared. The entry was short, which was unlike her, and strange, which was also unlike her—or perhaps entirely like her, if you knew what to look for, and Ines had spent three years not knowing what to look for, which was its own kind of knowledge.

*I asked someone today and they looked at me like I'd grown a second head. Maybe I have. Maybe it's the sensible one.*

That was all. No name. No context. Ines had read those two sentences perhaps four hundred times. She had tried to imagine who *someone* was, what question had been asked, whose second head had sprouted. She had catalogued every adult in Almadura against the possibility. It was a useless exercise, like alphabetizing smoke, but it was hers.

After October 3rd, blank pages. Forty-one of them, then the cardboard back cover with its peeling lemon print. Ines had counted the blank pages herself, twice, once with the ruler and once with her fingertip, because grief makes you precise about the wrong things.

Today she counted again anyway. Procedure. She fanned the pages gently, letting them breathe past her gloved thumb—

And stopped.

There was writing where there should have been none.

Not far in. Perhaps eight pages past the final entry, close enough to the end that a careless person would assume the book simply ran out of words. But Ines Quintana had never been careless about pages in her life, and the ink sat there on the page like a bird that had landed on a statue.

Her first thought, absurdly, was that she had missed it before. That some previous version of herself—grief-stricken, sloppy, human—had fanned past it. She tested this theory and found it flimsy. She had photographed every page for the case file. She pulled the file now, hands steady in the way she forced them to be steady, and checked the photograph of that leaf: blank. Clean, unmarked, turquoise-ruled emptiness. Dated from the police examination, fourteen months ago.

So the writing was new.

Ines sat very still. The archive hummed around her in its usual way—the tick of the radiator, the distant shuffling of Father Anselmo's old sacristan Tomás moving chairs in the nave above, a pigeon conducting negotiations with the gutter. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that suddenly seemed to belong to a different world than the one she was sitting in.

She lowered the loupe over the page.

The handwriting was unmistakably Marisol's.

That sentence, she knew, would sound like sentiment to anyone who hadn't spent twenty years authenticating documents. But it wasn't sentiment. Handwriting was a fingerprint made of habit. The particular open loop of Marisol's *d*s, the way her crossbars on *t* drifted leftward like someone walking into wind, the little hook she put on the end of her *y*s that their schoolteacher Sister Remedios had spent a year trying to erase and had failed gloriously. Ines had authenticated enough of Marisol's notes—grocery lists, birthday cards, the furious letter to the water company—to know that hand the way she knew her own reflection.

This was it. Every habit present. Every loop and drift and hooked *y*.

And the date at the top, in Marisol's forward-leaning numerals, read **October 17th**.

Two weeks after she vanished.

* * *

Ines did the sensible thing, which was to stand up, walk to the window, and grip the sill until her knuckles reported their objections.

Below, the cloister garden went on being beautiful in its indifferent autumn way, all rust and gold, the fountain doing its eternal small business. Somewhere out there Raúl Serrano sat in a cell awaiting trial for a murder everyone believed he'd committed, and here was a woman who had been dead for three years apparently keeping appointments with a spiral-bound notebook.

Vertigo was the only word for it, and Ines distrusted the word because it sounded dramatic, and she had built her whole life on the principle that drama was what happened to people who failed to keep proper records. But there it was: vertigo. The sensation that the floor of the world had a filing error. Three years ago the past had closed its cover, and she had shelved it, and labeled it, and now it had opened itself and was looking back at her.

She returned to the desk. Sat. Read the entry properly this time, slowly, aloud in a whisper, because Marisol had always said you didn't understand a text until you heard it in your own voice.

*October 17th.*

*Still here. Still can't say where. Don't look for me yet—I mean it, Ines, don't. When it's safe I'll come back and explain everything and you can scold me properly, you'll enjoy that. Tell Tomás the candles idea was lovely. Tell Father Anselmo I'm praying for him, even though we both know I'm terrible at it.*

*P.S. You alphabetized my complaints again. I found the folder. I'm not angry. I'm never angry with you. But some things shouldn't be filed, querida. Some things should be shouted.*

The loupe trembled slightly in Ines's hand.

There was so much wrong with it, and so much right, and the wrongness and rightness were tangled together like yarn from a dropped basket. The voice was Marisol's—warm, teasing, that affectionate exasperation she wore like perfume. The details were true ones: Tomás and his endless ideas about candles; Father Anselmo's fondness for being prayed at; the running joke about Ines's filing, which had been a joke between them since they were fifteen and Ines had organized their shared school locker by subject and then, when Marisol protested, by color.

But. *Don't look for me yet.* Written two weeks after the entire town had torn itself apart looking for her. Written—as far as Ines knew—inside a sealed crypt that no one had entered. And that last line, *some things should be shouted*, landing on the page like a hand placed gently on Ines's shoulder by someone standing behind her.

She looked over her shoulder. Nothing, of course. The archive, the baize mat, the gray box, the radiator ticking its idiot metronome. Just her own shadow thrown long across the floor by the high window, looking, she thought, remarkably like a woman who had made a serious error somewhere and hadn't located it yet.

* * *

She spent the next hour doing what she always did when the world misbehaved: she documented it.

Photographs, front and raking light, to show the ink's depth in the paper grooves. Measurements of the entry's position relative to the spine. Notes on the ink itself—it was blue-black, and under the loupe it sat crisp and unfaded on the page, not bleeding, not ghosted through from any neighboring leaf. Fresh ink. Weeks old at most, she guessed, though she was no chemist and wrote down honestly: *appears recent; requires expert analysis.*

Then she sat with the harder questions, the ones that didn't fit on an evidence form.

Possibility the first: forgery. Someone imitating Marisol's hand. But forgers, Ines knew, captured the general shape of a hand and lost its soul—the hesitations, the speed, the private shortcuts. This had the soul. Whoever wrote it had either studied Marisol's writing obsessively for years, or—

Possibility the second, which she refused to finish thinking, because finishing it meant entertaining the impossible, and the impossible did not get shelf space in her archive.

Possibility the third, the one that arrived quietly while she was re-examining the *P.S.* and stayed like a guest who wouldn't leave: that the entry was genuine—genuinely Marisol's hand, genuinely recent—and that the explanation was neither resurrection nor haunting but something worse and more ordinary. That someone alive, someone with access, was writing in a dead girl's diary. Wearing her voice like a borrowed coat. And had addressed the result, specifically, deliberately, to Ines.

Because look at it again. *Don't look for me yet—I mean it, Ines.* Her name, in the body of the text. The real diary entries had used her name maybe six times in ninety pages; Marisol mostly called her *querida* or *the archivist* when she was being funny. But this entry named her twice, framed her, aimed itself at her like a letter that had taken three years to arrive.

And the last line. *Some things should be shouted.*

Ines looked across the room at the long wall of gray boxes, the town's secrets in their careful alphabetical order, each one filed and stamped and silenced by her own hand. *QUINTANA, INES — ARCHIVE STEWARDSHIP, 1998–PRESENT.* She had spent her whole professional life believing that keeping things safe and keeping things quiet were the same act.

*Some things shouldn't be filed,* the page said, in her dead friend's living handwriting. *Some things should be shouted.*

"I hear you," Ines whispered to the notebook, and then, because whispering felt insufficient and shouting felt insane, she said it again at full volume to the empty archive, startling the pigeon off the gutter outside. "I hear you."

The radiator ticked. Above her, faintly, Tomás was still moving chairs, humming something tuneless and content.

She closed the diary with exaggerated gentleness, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal you're not entirely sure is asleep. Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, wrote the date and time at the top—because procedure was her prayer, and right now she needed to pray—and began:

*Item 42. Discovered 11:40 a.m., this day. One (1) handwritten entry, undeniably in the hand of M. Vega, dated October 17th — fourteen days AFTER the date of her disappearance. Provenance unknown. Author unknown. Meaning unknown.*

She stopped. Looked at the three *unknowns* lined up at the bottom of the page like a small choir of trouble.

There was, she realized, a fourth thing she didn't know, and it was the largest one, and it concerned the crypt itself. Because the diary had been found inside a room kept locked for three years—a room with five keys in all the world, each accounted for, each sleeping in a pocket or a drawer or an evidence locker. If the entry was fresh, then fresh hands had reached that notebook. Which meant the lock was not the seal she had always trusted it to be. Which meant the sanctuary had a door in it she had never seen.

Ines put down the pencil, removed her glasses, and rubbed the bridge of her nose.

"Well," she said to the gray box, to the archive, to the whole orderly edifice of her life. "That won't do at all."

Tomorrow she would go down to the crypt and check the seal. Tonight she would not sleep, but that was tomorrow's problem wearing tonight's clothes.

Outside the high window, the bells of Santa Rufina rang the hour, and for the first time in twenty years of service, Ines Quintana flinched at the sound.
