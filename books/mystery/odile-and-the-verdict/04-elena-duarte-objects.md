## 4. Elena Duarte Objects

Elena Duarte had a rule about grief: it was allowed in her courtroom the way weather was allowed—acknowledged, endured, and never permitted to make arguments.

She was thinking about this rule on a Tuesday morning in late October, standing at her office window with a coffee that had gone cold out of neglect, watching Ines Quintana cross the plaza toward the courthouse with a document folder clutched to her chest like a shield. The archivist walked the way she did everything, Elena had noticed: precisely, upright, as though the pavement might be alphabetized if she only applied herself.

"Here we go," said Rosa, Elena's clerk, from behind the mountain of case files she was pretending to organize. "The cathedral lady. Third time this week she's called."

"She didn't call today?"

"She called twice before eight. I told her you were in session." Rosa lowered her voice to the register reserved for gossip of civic importance. "Marisol Vega's brother is her cousin's neighbor, you know. Small town."

Everything in Almadura was small town. Elena had learned that within a week of arriving from Seville, when the baker refused her money because she'd once ruled in favor of his nephew's noise complaint and he considered them practically family now. She'd been prosecuting in Almadura for fourteen months, long enough to know that everyone knew everything about everyone, except—apparently—the one thing that mattered.

The trial of Raúl Serrano for the disappearance of Marisol Vega was eleven days away. It would be the biggest thing to happen to this courthouse since the roof leaked in 2011, and Elena intended to win it cleanly, thoroughly, and without any archivists wandering in with folder-shaped feelings.

"Send her in," Elena said, because she was a professional, and because Rosa would talk about it either way.

* * *

Ines Quintana sat down across the desk without being asked, which Elena noted as either confidence or exhaustion, and opened the folder with the care of someone handling a relic.

"Prosecutor Duarte. Thank you for seeing me."

"You've been persistent."

"I've been accurate," Ines said. "There's a difference. Persistence is wanting something. Accuracy is having it." She laid three photographs on the desk, aligned to the edge with a fingertip. "I found a new entry in Marisol Vega's diary. Dated October third. Two weeks after she disappeared."

Elena picked up the photographs. She kept her face neutral, which took some doing, because her first instinct—the unprofessional one, the one she fed coffee and kept in the basement—was a cold slide of interest down her spine.

The diary itself sat in the evidence locker two floors below, sealed, logged, photographed exhaustively during the original investigation. Elena had read every page of it twice. A young woman's handwriting, looping and impatient, full of complaints about the bakery queue and tender observations about the cathedral swallows and, in the last weeks, an odd tightness Elena couldn't name but had underlined in her own working copy anyway.

The entry in these photographs was in that same hand. *The bells are wrong this week,* it read. *Someone keeps moving my things. I think they want me quiet.*

"Handwriting can be forged," Elena said.

"Yes."

"Ink can be aged. Paper can be sourced from old stock. Grief," Elena said, gently, because she wasn't a monster, "can be very creative."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. She had gray threading through dark hair and eyes that seemed to be filing Elena even while Elena talked, cataloguing her into whatever system the woman used instead of sleep.

"Do you think I forged it?" Ines asked. Not offended. Curious, almost, like a scientist encountering a stupid hypothesis.

"I think you loved your friend. I think three years of not knowing does things to careful people. And I think—" Elena set the photographs down, "—that if this entry were genuine, it wouldn't have appeared now, conveniently, before a trial. Evidence doesn't materialize. It gets made."

"Or it gets missed." Ines leaned forward. "Who examined the diary originally?"

"A police technician."

"With what training in paper forensics?"

Elena paused. "He photographed it."

"He photographed it," Ines agreed, "in bad light, with the binding pressed flat, which cracked the spine—there are hairline losses along the gutter, you can see them in your own evidence photos—and he catalogued forty-one entries. There are forty-two pages with writing. He counted the leaves, not the entries. Page thirty-four verso was stuck to thirty-five by humidity. It came free last week when I was preparing the exhibit copies. The entry underneath was dated after she vanished."

Elena said nothing for a moment. This was the trouble with Almadura: nobody here was supposed to be good at anything. The town ran on goodwill and inherited jobs. An archivist who actually understood forensic documentation was like a church mouse who could do taxes.

"Even granting all that," Elena said, "an entry appearing inside a sealed evidence item isn't a miracle. It's a custody problem. Which means either your friend's diary has been accessible to someone for three years—which is its own catastrophe—or someone added ink to a hidden page recently and is playing games with us."

"That's what I've come to say, more or less."

"It is?"

"I don't need you to believe the entry is hers," Ines said. "I need you to explain how it got there. Because I've been asking myself all week, and every answer frightens me."

There it was again, that cold slide. Elena reached for her coffee, remembered it was cold, drank it anyway.

* * *

After Ines left—refusing coffee with a small apologetic smile, as though caffeine might disorder her—Elena pulled the chain-of-custody log for Exhibit Fourteen and spread it across her desk.

The diary's documented life was short and tidy. Recovered from the crypt beneath the Cathedral of Santa Rufina, seventeen months after Marisol Vega's disappearance, during a routine structural inspection. Logged by Officer Bécares. Transferred to the judicial archive. Requested by Elena's office in March. Returned. Requested again in September for trial preparation. Returned.

Five entries. Five signatures. Elena ran her finger down them and felt the itch start between her shoulder blades, the itch that had won her more cases than her memory had, the itch that said *look again.*

Between the second transfer and the third—the eighteen months sitting in the judicial archive—there was a gap. Not a missing signature. A missing *date*. The archival intake form recorded the transfer as received, stamped, initialed by the archive clerk. But the date field held only a smudge where a stamp should have been, and beside it, in pencil, faint as breath: a correction. Someone had written a date, erased it, written another.

Elena got her loupe from the drawer—it had been a gift from her father, who'd wanted her to be a jeweler and settled for a prosecutor, and she suspected he'd have said the professions weren't so different—and bent over the form.

The first date, half-erased: **14 March**. The corrected date, in different pressure, possibly different pencil: **2 June**.

Two and a half months. The diary had sat in the archive for two and a half months longer than the paperwork claimed, or the paperwork had been backdated, or someone had simply needed the diary to be somewhere else for a while and then put it back and tidied the record.

Tidied. That was the word that bothered her. Elena had grown up with a mother who tidied—hid the chipped cups before guests arrived, turned the sad chair toward the wall—and she recognized the impulse in the pencil mark. Not destruction. *Housekeeping.* Someone hadn't stolen the diary. Someone had borrowed it and made the borrowing polite.

She sat back and looked out the window at the cathedral, pale against the morning, its bell tower wearing pigeons like a bad hat.

"Rosa," she called. "Who was the archive clerk in March of the year after the Vega disappearance?"

Rosa appeared in the doorway wiping her hands. "That'd be old Señor Peralta. Retired now, bless him, plays dominoes at the Café Central and tells anyone who'll listen that digitization is the death of civilization."

"And who had access besides him?"

Rosa thought. Elena watched her think; it moved across her face like weather. "Well. The clerk, the judges, the prosecutors—you, eventually. And the cathedral sent over a volunteer sometimes, to help with the ecclesiastical records they keep deposited with us. Very trustworthy lot, church people." Rosa paused. "Why? Has something gone missing?"

"No," Elena said slowly. "Something may have gone *added*."

* * *

That evening she stayed late, which was not unusual, and ordered food she didn't eat, which was also not unusual, and built two columns on a legal pad, which was extremely unusual, because Elena Duarte believed in one column, the correct column, and here she was making a list titled *Reasons the Entry Is Fake* and another titled *Reasons It Isn't.*

The first column filled quickly. Handwriting forgery was trivially achievable by anyone who'd known Marisol Vega. The timing was theatrical. The content—*someone keeps moving my things, I think they want me quiet*—read less like a frightened woman and more like a message composed for an audience. And the audience, apparently, was her.

The second column filled more slowly, and in a smaller hand, as though the words were embarrassed to be there.

One: the hidden page showed genuine age-creases consistent with long compression, not recent insertion. She was no expert, but she'd spent enough evenings with her loupe and her father's old coin collection to know what paper did when it was folded for years versus weeks, and this paper had the tired look of the former.

Two: whoever wrote the entry had known details of the original investigation that had never been made public—including, if Ines Quintana's reading was right, the exact phrasing of a witness statement about the church bells that existed only in a sealed file.

Three: the custody gap. Fakes didn't require backdated paperwork. Fakes required only nerve. But a real diary, borrowed and returned and quietly re-dated—that required access, patience, and a reason to hide the borrowing. Those were the fingerprints of an insider, and insiders were the one species of suspect her case against Raúl Serrano could not afford.

Because that was the thing she hadn't written in either column, the thing she'd been circling for a week like a moth around a lamp she disapproved of: her entire theory of the prosecution rested on the crypt being closed. Raúl Serrano had a key to the crypt—groundskeeper's ring, never recovered—but so, technically, did four other people, and the defense had already hinted they'd raise it. Her answer, prepared and rehearsed, was that the crypt seal was intact, the lock undamaged, and Serrano's key the only plausible instrument. A tidy answer. A *tidied* answer.

And now a page had appeared inside the sealed evidence, dated after the disappearance, and every tidy answer she owned suddenly sounded like a chipped cup turned toward the wall.

Elena put down the pen and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes.

The problem—she admitted it to the empty office, because the empty office kept her secrets better than anyone in this town—was not really evidentiary. The problem was Ines Quintana. The problem was that when the archivist had said *every answer frightens me*, Elena had wanted, immediately and unprofessionally, to sit closer. The problem was that Ines had a way of aligning photographs to the edge of a desk that made Elena's chest feel briefly reorganized, and that Elena had caught herself, twice now, thinking about the gray in the woman's hair at moments when she should have been thinking about reasonable doubt.

Love, in her experience, was just attention with nowhere left to put itself. And attention was the one tool of her trade she could not afford to misplace.

She looked at the legal pad. Then, deliberately, she tore off both columns, folded the sheet in quarters, and slid it into her jacket pocket—not thrown away, never thrown away, Elena threw nothing away, a fact about herself she was choosing not to examine tonight—and went home through the cold plaza.

At the corner by the bakery, she stopped. Across the street, outside the Casa del Libro, which had closed an hour ago, a figure stood in the dark doorway. Broad shoulders, familiar cardigan, the patient posture of a man waiting for someone. Tomás, the sacristan. She'd seen him at the cathedral a dozen times, always helpful, always gentle, the kind of man who carried old ladies' baskets up the steps.

He wasn't looking at her. He was looking up at the dark windows of the building above the bookshop—Ines Quintana's apartment, Rosa had mentioned once, in the way Rosa mentioned everything—and he stood there in the cold with his hands folded, not leaving, not knocking, just keeping watch over the dark like it was a duty assigned to him.

Elena crossed the square and went home. She locked her door, which she usually forgot to do. Then she took out the folded legal pad, unfolded it, and added a line to the smaller column, in her smallest hand:

*Four: why does the sacristan stand outside her window?*

Eleven days until trial. Somewhere in the dark of the town, a diary was telling the truth or telling lies, and Elena Duarte, who objected for a living, found—for the first time in her career—that she no longer knew which side of the room she was arguing from.
