## 5. Marginalia

The archive at three in the morning is a different country.

I had learned this years ago, back when I first took the post and still believed that insomnia was a temporary condition, something a person caught like a cold and shook off with enough chamomile and discipline. Now I understood it as citizenship. At three in the morning the cathedral above me stopped being a building and became a heartbeat — stone warming and cooling, timbers settling their old arguments, the bell tower creaking like an old man turning over in bed. And down here in the undercroft, among the shelves, I was the only witness to all of it.

I had brought the diary home to my worktable. Not home-home — I would never have taken it past the archive door; some superstition of mine held that documents grew disoriented outside their proper climate, the way orchids wilt in a draft. But my worktable sat in the corner of the reading room, beneath the one lamp I trusted, and so the diary came to the table, and I sat before it the way one sits before a difficult relative: respectfully, warily, with both hands visible.

The new entry — the impossible entry, dated two weeks after Marisol Vega walked out of this world — had been sitting inside me for two days like a swallowed stone. I had done what I always did with stones: catalogued it, shelved it, pretended the shelf was load-bearing. But at three in the morning the pretending wore thin, and I found myself doing something I hadn't permitted myself since her funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, since the empty coffin the town had insisted on because Almadura could not abide an unresolved ending.

I turned to the beginning.

Marisol's diary was not, strictly speaking, a diary. It was a 1998 appointment book from the farmacia — Doña Pilar had given them out free with purchases over ten euros, bright green covers with a cartoon stork on the front, which Marisol had found hilarious and covered with stickers until the stork looked like it was fleeing a crime scene. She'd kept it for six years. The early pages were innocent as bread: dentist appointments, arguments with her mother about the motorbike, a running tally of how many times Father Anselmo said *in conclusion* during sermons (the record was eleven).

And in the margins — God, in the margins.

We had invented our shorthand the summer we were fourteen, during the worst of the heat, sprawled on the cool tiles of her mother's kitchen with lemonade going warm between us. It wasn't sophisticated. It was a schoolgirl's cipher, half Pig Latin, half the alphabet we'd stolen from a detective novel Marisol loved, with our own additions scrawled in the backs of our notebooks. We used it to pass notes about boys we were too embarrassed to name, about teachers, about Marisol's stepfather's moods. We called it *the whispering*, because written in it, even your own handwriting sounded like someone leaning close to your ear.

I hadn't read the whispering in fifteen years. My fingers remembered it anyway.

That is the thing no one warns you about grief — or maybe they do warn you, and you file the warning away like everything else. The body keeps its own archive. My hand moved across those margin notes and decoded them without consulting me, the way you can still ride a bicycle, the way you can still hear your mother calling you in from the street twenty years after she stopped calling.

*V. tmb va al granero los jueves.* Vera also goes to the barn on Thursdays.

*No le digas a tu madre lo del perro.* Don't tell your mother about the dog.

Small things. Sixteen-year-old things. I smiled at the dog — I had forgotten the dog, a three-legged stray Marisol had smuggled into the hayloft and named Obispo, Bishop, out of what she insisted was affection and everyone else recognized as blasphemy — and then the smile caught in my chest like a fishhook, because she had been dead three years and I was smiling at her jokes and there was no one left alive who would get them but me.

That was the intimacy of it. That was the wound of it. Reading her margins was like hearing her voice through a wall — muffled, but unmistakably hers, and absolutely unable to hear me knocking.

* * *

By four I had stopped smiling.

The margins changed the year she started working at the parish office. The shorthand stayed the same, but the sentences grew older, the way children's clothes stop fitting overnight. She wrote smaller. She wrote faster — I could tell because the loops flattened, the way hers did when she was transcribing rather than composing, when she was trying to get facts down before she lost her nerve.

*El libro de cuentas de los niños no cuadra. No ha cuadrado NUNCA.*

The children's ledger doesn't balance. It has never balanced.

I set down my pencil. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above me the cathedral ticked through its small nocturnal business, indifferent.

*Los recibos dicen una cosa y el banco dice otra,* she had written in the margin of a page dated March, three years ago. *Los recibos mienten mejor que el banco.*

The receipts say one thing and the bank says another. The receipts lie better than the bank does.

There was more. Page after page, in the corners where the appointments should have been — *bought stamps for the parish office, 9:15* — the whispering crept along underneath like water under ice. She had copied figures. She had compared years. She had a phrase she returned to the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth:

*Firmas. Siempre firmas. Nadie firma sin saber.*

Signatures. Always signatures. Nobody signs without knowing.

I am an archivist. People imagine my work is dust and patience, and it is, but underneath the dust it is this: an understanding that paper remembers what people prefer to forget. Every document is a confession waiting for a reader. Marisol had understood it too — she'd teased me about it often enough. *You and your stamps, Ines. You make everything official just by touching it.* She'd meant it as a joke. Sitting there at three-forty in the morning, I felt the joke turn over slowly and show me its underside.

Because there was a page — near the end, in the last third of the book, where the entries thinned and the margins thickened — where the whispering stopped being observation and became something else. Something addressed outward. Addressed, I realized with a slow cold spreading under my ribs, to whoever might someday read it.

To me. It had been addressed to me all along, and I had never read it.

*Si no vuelvo,* she had written, and then stopped. The sentence hung there, half-formed, the ink trailing off into nothing — not crossed out, not finished. Abandoned mid-thought, the way you abandon a sentence when someone walks into the room.

*If I don't come back—*

Below it, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages, in full ordinary handwriting, not the whispering at all, as if she needed it legible to any eye in the world:

*MIRA LO QUE DIMOS.*

Look at what we gave.

* * *

I made tea I didn't drink. This is a ritual of mine, tea as a handrail — the boiling of it, the waiting, the holding of a hot cup gives the hands something honest to do while the mind does dishonest work. Elena Duarte would say it was avoidance dressed up as self-care. Elena Duarte says a great many precise things that land like darts thrown by someone who claims not to be playing.

I thought about her anyway, standing at the little sink in the archive's back room, watching the untouched tea go cold. Two days ago I had shown her the impossible entry and she had looked at me with that flat professional kindness that is worse than disbelief, and said, *Ines. Grief does strange things to careful people.* As if I had forged my dead friend's handwriting out of longing. As if longing were capable of such forgery — and I suppose I couldn't entirely blame her, because wouldn't it be easier? Wouldn't it be a mercy, almost, if the madness were mine?

But the margins weren't madness. The margins were arithmetic.

I went back to the table and did what I do. I made a concordance. Every marginal note in the whispering, transcribed, dated, cross-referenced against the printed entries beside which it sat. It took two hours. It was the most painful two hours of my working life, and I once spent a summer restoring water-damaged baptismal records from the flood of '89, including the page for my own cousin's twins.

The pattern assembled itself with terrible patience.

For two years, Marisol had been noticing. Small discrepancies first — a donation recorded twice, a repair invoice for work no one remembered being done. Then larger ones. The orphanage accounts appeared again and again: *los niños, los niños, los niños.* She never wrote the word *theft*. I searched. She danced around it — *la diferencia* (the difference), *lo que falta* (what's missing), once, memorably, *el agujero* (the hole), underlined three times. But she never once wrote *robo*. Stolen.

She was protecting someone even in cipher. Or she didn't believe it yet herself. Both, probably. Belief arrives late to the kindest people; they keep setting extra places at the table for doubt long after doubt has stopped coming to dinner.

And threaded through all of it, recurring every few weeks like a refrain, the phrase from the last page — *lo que dimos, lo que dimos, lo que dimos.* What we gave. What we gave. What we gave.

Not *what they took.* What *we* gave.

I sat with that distinction for a long time, the way you sit with a word in a foreign language, turning it toward the light. If the orphanage money had simply been stolen, the natural cry would be *look what they took.* But Marisol wrote *we*. Gave. As if the money had gone somewhere it was sent. As if the town itself — the donors, the parish, the faithful lines of grandmothers slipping coins into the box for the children — had given, and given, and given, and the giving had been diverted somewhere quiet and respectable and signed.

*Nadie firma sin saber.* Nobody signs without knowing.

My eyes went, without my permission, to the row of ledgers on the shelf behind my table. Parish financial records, 1971 to present. My own accession stamps marched along their spines in neat blue ranks. I had processed every one of them. I had stamped them, numbered them, entered them in the finding aid in my tidy hand, and shelved them in the climate-controlled dark where paper goes to remember.

Three years ago, Marisol had asked me for something. I had said no. I had reasons — good ones, professional ones, the kind that look airtight when you write them down and look like cowardice when you say them aloud at three in the morning. I had filed her request away the way I filed everything, and a week later she was gone, and the town got its verdict without a trial: runaway girl, sad story, candle at the shrine, move along.

Raúl Serrano was going to stand in a dock in three weeks for what happened to her. A man who fixed boilers and fed the cats behind the sacristy and once spent an entire afternoon helping me carry boxes because I looked tired. The prosecution's case rested on a diary found in a locked crypt and a town's appetite for a simple answer.

And here was Marisol, whispering up at me from the margins of her own murder's exhibit A: *if I don't come back, look at what we gave.*

She had known she might not come back. She had written it down and hidden it in plain sight, in a code only two people in the world could read, trusting — with a faith that made my throat close — that the other one of us would eventually come looking.

It had taken me three years.

I put my face in my hands. The lamp hummed. The cathedral ticked. And then, because grief is a luxury with a time limit and dread is a discipline, I lifted my head, pulled the financial ledgers off the shelf — 1988 through the present, the years the margins pointed at like a compass needle — and carried them to the table.

The children's ledger didn't balance. It had never balanced. Marisol had told me so in her own secret alphabet, and now I was going to check her homework, three years late, with my own stamp in my hand.

I opened the first volume to the orphanage donations, and the first thing I saw, at the foot of the very first page, was a familiar signature authorizing the annual transfer of funds to the diocesan account — and beside it, in the corner, small and blue and damning, the accession stamp that proved the page had passed through my hands.

I had touched this. I had catalogued this. I had alphabetized the evidence of my friend's death and shelved it where no one would ever think to look.

Outside, far above, the first bell of dawn began to gather its breath.
