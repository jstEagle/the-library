## 14. What We Gave

The phrase had been sitting in front of me for three days, patient as a cat.

*If I don't come back, look at what we gave.*

I had transcribed it onto an index card in my own careful hand, because Marisol's shorthand was beautiful but my memory was not, and I had pinned it above the reading desk in the archive where I could glare at it while pretending to catalog the diocesan correspondence of 1974. Father Anselmo's predecessor had written four hundred letters that year and every one of them was about the roof. Even the archives of the dead are mostly about roofs.

*What we gave.* The *we* bothered me more than the *gave*. Marisol had written it in the margin beside a passage about the orphanage—Casa de los Ángeles, its ledgers stamped with my own notarial seal, God forgive me—and she had used the first person plural as naturally as breathing. As if I were included. As if the sentence were addressed to me specifically, which of course it was, because who else would know the shorthand? We had invented it ourselves, the summer we were fourteen, huddled in the back pew during interminable vespers, encoding notes about boys and teachers and Mrs. Ferrán's unfortunate habit of tucking her skirt into her underwear.

We had thought ourselves so clever. Two girls with a cipher made of saints' names and inverted vowels, certain no adult could ever crack it. Certain, too, that we would be friends forever, which is the kind of certainty fourteen-year-olds have and archivists should know better than to trust.

*Look at what we gave.*

I had run through every possibility like a woman counting beads. What we gave: alms? Confessions? The money itself—the donations, the collections, the little brass box at the orphanage door? None of it resolved into anything actionable. A phrase you can't act on is just grief wearing a costume.

It was Elena, indirectly, who broke it open. She had come by the archive on Tuesday evening—not officially, she was careful about that now, arriving after five with her coat collar up like a woman conducting an affair rather than an investigation—and she had stood at the reading desk turning my index card over in her fingers.

"You're reading it wrong," she said.

"I've read it eleven ways."

"You're reading it as a sentence. It's not a sentence. It's an instruction." She tapped the card. "In Spanish, when someone says *lo que dimos*—what we gave—they don't mean the giving. They mean the thing given. The object." Her dark eyes came up to mine. "Marisol wasn't being poetic. She was telling you where something is."

"A place," I said slowly.

"Or a container." And then she smiled, that dry half-smile she deployed like a scalpel, and said, "You're the archivist. Where do people put things they want found later, but only by one specific person?"

I didn't answer her out loud. But my mind had already gone down into the town, street by street, past the pharmacy and the bakery and the shuttered cinema, and stopped at the Banco Provincial on the Plaza del Sol, which had occupied the same corner since before either of us was born, and whose basement vault had been renting sealed deposit boxes to the cautious citizens of Almadura for three generations.

"Boxes," I said.

"Boxes," Elena agreed.

---

The bank opened at nine. I was there at eight-forty, standing under the arcade across the plaza with my coat buttoned to the throat, watching the manager—a round, mild man named Señor Otero who had once been in Marisol's brother's class at school—unlock the door and arrange his newspapers with the ceremony of a priest preparing an altar.

I made myself wait until nine-ten. There is such a thing as looking too eager, and I had learned, these past weeks, that eagerness in Almadura travels faster than the morning bus.

Inside, the bank smelled of paper and floor wax, the way banks did everywhere and always, as if the smell itself were collateral. Señor Otero brightened when he saw me. "Señora Quintana! An honor. Are the cathedral accounts in order? Not that I doubt—" he flapped a hand, "—of course they're in order, you keep them."

"They're fine, thank God. Actually, this is a personal matter." I lowered my voice, though the only other soul in the lobby was a sleeping beggar and a rubber plant. "A private matter. I need to ask about a deposit box. My family held one. My mother's name—Quintana."

Something flickered behind his glasses. Not suspicion exactly. More like a man checking whether the ice will hold.

"Your mother's box was released some years ago," he said carefully. "After the estate settled."

"Not hers. Another one." I took a breath. "Marisol Vega."

The flicker became something else entirely. He glanced toward the back office, then at me, and for a moment I saw him weigh two loyalties—discretion, which banks sell, against curiosity, which banks cannot help.

"Come into my office," he said.

His office was small and hot and decorated with a framed photograph of himself shaking hands with a regional banking official, both men grinning as if they'd just gotten away with something. He closed the door, sat, laced his fingers over his stomach, and looked at me with genuine, unguarded sympathy.

"Marisol Vega," he repeated. "Yes. Box 31. She rented it—I want to say six years ago? Seven. Paid annually, always in cash, always on time. Very punctual young woman." He paused. "You were friends, weren't you? Everyone knew. After she... after."

"After she disappeared," I said. "Yes. I'm asking because of the trial. Because of things connected to the trial." I kept my voice level. "Has anyone accessed the box since?"

And here Señor Otero did something I did not expect. He took off his glasses, polished them on his tie, put them back on, and said, "That depends on whether you're asking me as a private citizen or as someone the police might subpoena."

"Elena Duarte is already aware I'm here."

He nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something. "Then I'll tell you what I'd tell her. Yes. The box was accessed. Twice after her disappearance. Once—once I'll show you in the log, because it was done properly, with the key and the signature card. The second time..." He rubbed his jaw. "The second time is why I'm glad you mentioned the prosecutor."

My heart had begun a slow, heavy percussion against my ribs. "When was the second time?"

"Two years ago this spring. Almost to the week." He opened a drawer, removed a ledger bound in green cloth, and turned it toward me without quite letting go of it, a man offering bread while keeping hold of the loaf. "Box 31 was emptied. Contents removed, rental terminated, key returned. Signed for by an authorized party."

"Who?"

He turned the ledger fully around and rotated it to face me, and pointed with one blunt finger at the signature line.

The name written there was *Diócesis de Almadura*. Below it, in smaller characters, the countersignature: *P. A. Herrera, Obispo*, or rather the stamp of it, the episcopal seal pressed in violet ink, crisp and official and utterly damning. And beneath that, in the space marked *Presentado por*—presented by—a third line, filled in with a hand I recognized even before I read it, because I had seen it a thousand times on the spine cards of my own archive, on donation receipts, on the parish registers I dusted every Thursday.

*Tomás Iglesias, sacristán.*

The room went very quiet. Even the beggar outside seemed to stop snoring.

"He presented church credentials," Otero said quietly. "A letter of authorization on diocesan letterhead, signed by the bishop's office, stating that the contents of the box belonged to the parish and were being reclaimed for the estate of the deceased. It was all perfectly in order. Perfectly." He said the word twice, and the second time it curdled. "Except that I knew Marisol. Slightly. Enough to know she wasn't the type to leave her affairs to the Church. And except that the letter's reference number—" he tapped the ledger again, "—corresponds to a filing series I happen to know doesn't exist, because my wife works in the bishop's chancery office and I asked her, very casually, over dinner, whether the diocese maintained a records series beginning with the letter R."

"And it doesn't."

"It begins with C. Correspondencia. Always has." He spread his hands. "But what was I to do? Refuse the Holy Mother Church her property? Report it? To whom?" His smile was bitter and small. "To the police, who had spent three years failing to find out what happened to the girl whose box it was? I am a small man, señora. I manage a small bank. I wrote down what I saw, and I kept the copy of the authorization letter—it's procedure, we retain copies—and I told myself that someday someone would come asking. Someone like you."

"Do you still have the letter?"

He opened the drawer again, deeper this time, and produced a photostat, yellowed at the edges, folded once. I unfolded it with hands that were not entirely steady.

It was beautifully done. That was the first thing I noticed—the craftsmanship of it, the correct letterhead, the correct violet ink, the bishop's seal reproduced with loving fidelity. Whoever forged it had access to the real seal, or a very good impression of it. The text declared that the Diocese of Almadura, acting in its capacity as administrator of charitable bequests, claimed the contents of deposit box 31 as belonging to the funds of Casa de los Ángeles, and authorized its sacristan, Tomás Iglesias, to receive them.

Dated the eleventh of April. Two years ago. Eleven days after Marisol's disappearance had been formally reclassified from missing persons to cold case.

Eleven days. As if whoever emptied the box had waited—waited through the search, through the vigils, through the candles in the plaza—until the town's attention finally slid away, and then moved, quiet as a sexton, to sweep up the last of her.

"Señora Quintana." Otero's voice pulled me back. "May I ask what was in the box?"

"I don't know," I said. And then, because lying to this kind, frightened man felt like a sin I couldn't afford: "Documents. I think documents. Copies of financial records."

He absorbed this. I watched him absorb it—watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes, the donations his bank processed for the orphanage every Christmas, the cheerful posters of smiling children taped in his tellers' windows—and watched him arrive, with visible discomfort, at the same sum I had arrived at days ago in the archive.

"The children's ledger," he murmured. "Is that what they're calling it?"

"You've heard that phrase?"

"My wife has heard it. From the chancery. Apparently there was a panic about it, years ago, before your friend disappeared. Files pulled, seals ordered." He met my eyes. "Señora, I'm going to give you a certified copy of this letter, and I'm going to note in my log that you viewed it, and if Prosecutor Duarte wants my testimony, she'll have it, under oath, with pleasure. But I'd get it out of this building today. Because two years ago I assumed the man who took those papers was following orders." He straightened the photostat on his desk with two fingers, aligning its edges precisely, a fellow creature of systems. "Lately I've been wondering whether the orders followed him."

---

I walked out of the bank into a morning so bright and ordinary it felt like an insult. The plaza was full of its usual theater: old men arguing chess moves on the benches, Doña Pura arranging oranges into pyramids of impossible perfection, two schoolboys racing paper boats down the gutter from the fountain. Life, proceeding. Three years of it, proceeding right over the top of a girl who had tried to tell us all exactly what was rotting beneath our feet.

I sat on a bench near the fountain and let myself feel it, briefly, completely: the hope snatched away. Because for one hour—one golden hour in a bank vault smelling of wax—I had believed the answers were sitting in a steel drawer with Marisol's name on them, waiting for me like a letter held at the post office. *Look at what we gave.* Open the box, find the ledgers, carry them to Elena, save Raúl Serrano, end it. Clean as an index.

Instead: an empty box. A forged letter. A stolen inheritance, carried out of the vault in Tomás Iglesias's capable, courteous hands, two years ago, while I was alphabetizing correspondence about roofs.

But underneath the loss, something else was settling into place, cold and clear as groundwater. Because the forgery told me things the empty box could not.

Tomás had needed a letter of authorization. Which meant the bank required one—which meant that in April, two years ago, Marisol's box was not freely his to empty. He had needed cover. And the cover he chose was the bishop's seal.

Either he had stolen the use of that seal—and a forgery this good, this *correct*, suggested otherwise—or someone above him had supplied it. Father Anselmo, dying and silent in the rectory. Bishop Herrera, gentle as evening, offering me Seville with one hand while his chancery's ink dried on a false claim with the other. Or both, each protecting the Church in the manner they knew best: Anselmo with silence, Herrera with stationery.

And one more thing. The box had been emptied *after* Marisol disappeared—but the rental had been paid annually, punctually, in cash. Who pays rent on a secret for six years? Someone planning to retrieve it someday. Someone who expected to come back.

Unless the payments hadn't been Marisol's at all.

I sat with that thought while the paper boats dissolved in the gutter and the chess game collapsed into recrimination. If the payments continued after her death—if someone had kept box 31 alive, year after year—that meant the contents mattered enough to maintain, and the maintenance meant regular visits, regular cash, regular presence in this bank. I turned back toward the doors.

"Señor Otero," I said, when he appeared, wiping his hands on his trousers, "one more question, and then I'll trouble you no further. The annual payments. The cash. Can you tell me—who made them? Each year?"

He didn't even need the ledger. He had clearly gone over this ground alone, many nights, the way I went over mine.

"The first four years, a young woman. Dark hair, always a scarf. I assumed it was her—the renter herself." His voice dropped. "The last two payments were made by a man. Middle-aged, polite, workman's hands. He said he was paying on behalf of his aunt, who had moved away." Otero's mouth twisted. "He signed the receipt. Same name both times."

"Tomás Iglesias."

"Tomás Iglesias," he agreed. "Who told me his aunt had moved to Valencia." He looked at me steadily over his glasses. "There is no aunt, is there, señora."

"No," I said. "There never was."

I thanked him, took the certified copy, and folded it into the inner pocket of my coat, against my heart, where I keep the things I can't afford to lose. Then I walked out of the Plaza del Sol with my spine straighter than it had been in three years.

Because here is what the empty box gave me, though it took everything else away: a shape. For three years Marisol's disappearance had been a fog, and fogs forgive everyone. But fog burns off. What remained, standing in the sunlight, was simple and terrible and *provable*: a forged document bearing the bishop's seal; a sacristan with workman's hands who lied about an aunt; a dead girl's papers carried off eleven days after her case went cold. No longer a mystery. A chain. And chains, unlike mysteries, can be pulled.

Elena would need this before nightfall. Elena, who had risked her career petitioning a judge who wouldn't listen; Elena, who kissed me goodbye on Tuesday with her coat collar up, as if caution could protect either of us from what we were becoming to each other. I would bring her the letter, and the ledger record, and Otero's name, and together we would decide how to use a forgery against the men who forged it.

I was halfway across the plaza, already composing the conversation, when I saw him.

Tomás stood outside the bakery in his gray work jacket, holding a paper bag of rolls, chatting with Doña Pura, laughing at something—laughing, his head tipped back, a good man's laugh, a caretaker's laugh. He caught sight of me across the square and raised the bag in greeting, warm as ever, easy as ever.

"Señora Quintana! Beautiful morning. Can I interest you in—" 

"Not today, Tomás. Thank you."

He watched me go. I felt it between my shoulder blades, that mild, attentive gaze, the gaze of a man who notices everything and forgets nothing, and I understood suddenly, with perfect clarity, why Marisol had paid for her box in cash and worn a scarf and told no one—not even me—where she kept the truth.

She had known that in Almadura, the most dangerous place for a secret was anywhere a kind man could find it.

I turned the corner toward the archive, and my hand was steady on the letter over my heart, and my mind was already moving forward, past the fear, past the grief, to the one door left unopened: the rectory, and the dying man inside it, who knew exactly whose seal had signed away the last of Marisol Vega.

Father Anselmo owed me an answer. Tomorrow, God willing, I would collect it.
