## 18. Why He Stayed

They took him to the small room behind the parish office rather than the police station—Elena's idea, though she framed it as a courtesy to the Church. "He'll talk faster where the walls are familiar," she said, and I understood what she was really saying: she wanted me in the room, because Tomás would not perform his grief for a stranger.

The room smelled of candle wax and old varnish. A table, three chairs, one window looking out on the cloister garden where the orange trees were just beginning to bud. It was the room where, for thirty years, Tomás had counted the collection plates on Sunday evenings, and I think he knew it, because he sat down in his usual chair as if nothing at all had happened, folding his hands like a man waiting for tea.

His wrists were red where Elena had cuffed them. He kept turning them over, examining the marks with mild curiosity, as though they belonged to someone else.

"You should put something on those," I said. It was the only sentence I could find. My voice came out thin, like paper left too long in the sun.

"There's a first-aid kit in the sacristy," Tomás said. "Top shelf, behind the altar wine." He almost smiled. "You know how Father Anselmo is about that shelf. He thinks no one knows it's there."

I did know. I had catalogued the first-aid kit's existence in my inventory four years ago, entry 2211, *kit, medical, location: concealed, purpose: unclear*. Even then I had found it funny. Now the joke sat in my chest like a stone.

Elena stood by the window with her arms crossed. She had not spoken since we came down from the crypt. She had watched Tomás burn the forged page with an expression I had never seen on her—not triumph, not satisfaction, but something closer to sorrow, the way you look at a house fire and think of the photographs inside.

"Tomás," she said now, quietly. "Before the recording starts. Before any of it becomes official. Tell Ines why."

"Why," he repeated. He looked at me then, and his eyes were wet but steady, and I realized with a lurch that he had been rehearsing this—not the confession itself, but the *telling of it*—for three years. Perhaps every night. Perhaps into the dark of the crypt, in Marisol's voice, because there had been no one else to tell.

"Because she asked me once," he said, nodding at me. "Years ago. She said, 'Tomás, everyone in this town has a reason. I collect them.' Well. Here is mine."

* * *

He began, as I suppose he had always begun, with the beginning.

"I came to Santa Rufina when I was nineteen. Sweeping floors, polishing brass, carrying what needed carrying. Father Anselmo was already old then, or seemed so to me—young men always think forty is ancient—and he taught me everything. How to fold the linens. How to read the ledger. How to be useful." He paused. "Useful. That was the whole of my ambition, Ines. To be useful to something larger than myself. You understand that, don't you? You've spent your life being useful to paper."

I said nothing. My hands were cold.

"Marisol came later. The orphanage girl who never quite left. She'd come by on Thursdays to help sort the donation envelopes, and she talked the whole time—about her students, about the sea, about some book she was reading. She talked the way rain falls. Everywhere at once, and you couldn't help getting soaked." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I was fifty-one years old and I had never been in love. I didn't even recognize it. I thought I was simply glad it was Thursday."

Elena shifted by the window. I didn't look at her. I couldn't look at anything except Tomás's hands, which had gone still on the table, as if even they understood the gravity of what was coming.

"She found the discrepancies in the spring. You know this part—she told you herself, didn't she? Came to you with her suspicions." He glanced at me, apologetic, and the apology cut deeper than accusation would have. "She'd been keeping copies. Little scraps, mostly. Numbers copied onto the backs of hymn schedules. She showed me once, laughing—laughing, Ines, because she thought it would be simple. 'We'll show the bishop, he'll fix it, and the children keep their school.' That was all she wanted. Not scandal. Not justice, even. Just the money back where it belonged."

"And you told Father Anselmo," I said. My voice sounded far away.

"I told Father Anselmo." He nodded slowly. "Because that was my whole life, you see. Useful. The church was the largest thing I belonged to, and when a woman I loved began pulling threads from its hem, I did what I had always done. I carried it to the man whose job it was to hold the weight." He exhaled. "And Father Anselmo—God forgive him, God forgive us both—decided the weight was better buried."

"The sealed records," Elena said. "Chapter fifteen of this town's long silence."

"He ordered the archive closed. He ordered her warned off. And I—" Tomás's voice caught, and for a moment he was not a murderer or a keeper of terrible secrets but simply an old man in a small room, frightened. "I offered to speak to her. Man to—well. Friend to friend. I thought I could make her see that some things are bigger than being right. That the orphanage might close. That the donors would scatter. That the town—the *town*, Ines—would tear itself apart over numbers from thirty years ago."

"And she wouldn't see it," I whispered.

"She saw it perfectly. That was the trouble with Marisol. She saw everything clearly and forgave most of it, and the things she couldn't forgive she set out to fix, immediately, in daylight, without fear." He pressed his palms flat against the table. "We met at the crypt stairs on the last night. She'd come to take more copies—I don't know how she got in; she had her ways—and I followed her down. I begged her. I stood between her and the door, and I begged her, and she said the worst thing anyone has ever said to me. She said, kindly, *Tomás, you're a good man who works for people who aren't, and someday you'll have to choose.*"

The room was very quiet. Outside, a gardener's shears snipped rhythmically at the hedges, absurdly peaceful.

"We struggled," he said. "That's the truth, whatever they charge me with. We struggled, and the stair was narrow, and she fell. She fell, and I knelt there, and I knew before I touched her that she was gone, and do you know what my first thought was?" He laughed—a horrible, cracked sound. "My first thought was that Thursday would be ruined. That she wouldn't be sorting envelopes. That the world had made an administrative error."

I felt Elena move away from the window, coming to stand near me, though she didn't touch me. Her presence at my shoulder was its own kind of hand.

"So you staged it," I said. "The bus. The disappearance."

"A ticket bought in cash, weeks earlier, when I still hoped she might actually leave. A bag I packed from her things. I drove her car to the station lot and left it. I walked back through the olive groves in the dark like a boy sneaking home." He shook his head. "It went exactly as lies do—smoothly, and then not at all. People grieved. Raúl Serrano was arrested for a crime he had nothing to do with, and I told myself the courts would sort it out, and they didn't, and I told myself God would sort it out, and He didn't seem inclined."

"And the diary," I said. My throat ached around the words. "The entries. After."

Tomás was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—softer, stranger, and infinitely more disturbing for its tenderness.

"They were going to lock the crypt for renovation that winter. Did you know? Seal it up for two years. And I thought: her diary is in there. Her *voice*. Everything she ever wrote, shut behind stone." He looked at his hands. "I couldn't bear it. So I took the key—one of five, as you discovered—and I went down, and I read her. All of it. Every page. And somewhere in the reading I stopped being able to tell where she ended and I began. Grief does that, Ines. It erases the border."

"So you wrote."

"So I wrote." He said it plainly, without shame, which was somehow worse than shame. "At first just finishing sentences she'd left open. Then answering questions she'd asked in the margins. Then—new days. New thoughts. I knew her shorthand, you understand. I'd watched her write it across three winters of envelope-sorting. I knew how she crossed her sevens, how she dotted her i's slightly left of center. I practiced for months before I let the ink touch her pages." He lifted his chin, and there was something almost defiant in it. "Everyone says I desecrated her. The priest, the police, now you. But I ask you—what is a voice, if not something that continues? She wanted the thefts known. I wrote them known. She wanted the children protected. I protected them, in her name, in her hand. I became her will, Ines. Is that monstrous?"

"Yes," I said.

The word came out of me before I could soften it, and I did not try to soften it, because softness had been the disease of our town for thirty years.

"Yes, Tomás. It's monstrous. You didn't become her will. You became your own guilt wearing her handwriting. She was a living woman who argued and laughed and changed her mind, and you turned her into a puppet that only ever agreed with you. That's not devotion. That's the loneliest kind of possession—possessing someone who can't object."

He stared at me. And then, horribly, he began to cry—not the dignified tears of the contrite, but the ragged, ugly weeping of a man hearing a truth he had outrun for three years. "I know," he gasped. "I know, I know. Every night I knew. That's why I kept writing. Because as long as the diary continued, I didn't have to arrive at the end of it. The end where she's dead and it's my fault and Raúl rots in a cell for my sin." He wiped his face with his cuffed hands, an awkward, childlike gesture. "I couldn't let her die. Do you understand? I loved her, and I killed her, and I could not let her die, so I made her live badly, falsely, in stolen ink, rather than let her be finished."

* * *

Elena turned on the recorder then. She read him his rights in a clear, unhurried voice, and he answered each question with the same terrible calm, confirming everything—the fall, the staging, the entries, the burning of Marisol's real final pages, which he had taken from the crypt the night he confronted her and had carried against his heart ever since, "because they were hers, truly hers, and someone ought to keep them safe."

"Safe," Elena repeated flatly, producing the evidence bag. Inside were the pages recovered from his coat, Marisol's true last words, written in a hand that faltered near the end—not with fear, I noticed, but with urgency, as if she'd known her time at the page was short.

Tomás signed where Elena indicated. Then, as the guards from the civil guard post arrived to walk him out, he stopped at the door and turned back to me.

"Ines. One more thing, off the record, if the prosecutor permits."

Elena hesitated, then nodded.

"The bishop's offer. The post in Seville." His eyes were red-rimmed but steady. "Take it or don't, but know this: he offered it to me once too. Twenty years ago, after the second audit was buried. A quiet parish in the north, a modest stipend, a fresh start far from the ledgers." He smiled faintly. "I stayed. Staying felt like loyalty. It was just a slower way of drowning. Whatever you decide about your own silence, decide it while you're still above water."

Then they led him down the corridor, past the framed portraits of pastors dead and disgraced, and the door closed, and the room smelled only of candle wax again, as if no one had ever confessed anything there at all.

* * *

Elena and I walked out through the cloister without speaking. The orange trees were budding; somewhere a bell rang the half hour, indifferent and beautiful. At the gate she finally stopped.

"The recovered pages," she said. "Marisol names the accounts. Dates, amounts, signatures—including Herrera's predecessor, including two sitting diocesan officials." She looked at me, and beneath the prosecutor's composure I saw the other thing, the tender dangerous thing, flickering like a lamp. "Tomorrow morning I file to dismiss the charges against Raúl Serrano. And by afternoon, half this town is going to want your head, my head, and possibly each other's."

"Then it's a good thing," I said, "that we're used to unpopular company."

Almost, she smiled. But her eyes went past me, up to the cathedral facade, where Bishop Herrera stood at a high window, watching us with an expression I had seen only once before—in the archive photograph of a man watching his own house burn.

"Elena," I said quietly. "He knows we have the pages."

"Yes," she said. "And tomorrow, so does everyone else."
