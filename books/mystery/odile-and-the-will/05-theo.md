## 5. Theo

The bell over the door of Vane & Daughters Books had a crack running through it, so when Theo Marchetti pushed it open on the last Tuesday of October, it didn't ring so much as it sighed—a thin, wounded sound, like something trying to remember how it used to sound before it broke.

He had walked past this shop maybe four hundred times in five years. He knew its rhythms the way he knew the rhythms of his mother's breathing through the wall of their small house on Pelican Street: the light in the window at seven, off at nine-thirty; the silhouette of the woman inside, moving between the shelves like a hand moving through water. Yara Vane. The detective who hadn't saved his sister. The detective his mother had forbidden him to ever, ever speak to.

*She's done enough damage,* his mother used to say, and her voice would go flat and hard, like a lid closing on a pot. *She closed the file. She signed the paper. She let them put that poor man away and walked out of the bureau like it was a job she'd finished. Don't you go bringing her our grief, Theo. She'll only lose it again.*

So for five years he had walked past. And for five years, in the drawer of his mother's sewing table, wrapped in a dish towel that smelled of lavender and dust, the diary had waited.

Now his mother was in the ground at St. Brigid's, under a granite stone that read *Rosa Marchetti, Beloved Mother*, and the drawer was empty, and the diary was inside his jacket, pressed against his ribs like a second heart, and the bell over the door was sighing him in.

The shop smelled the way he'd always imagined a detective's memory would smell: old paper, cedar, coffee gone cold in the pot, and underneath it something faintly chemical—archive tape, maybe, or the glue of a thousand repairs. Rain had followed him in from the harbor road, and it ticked against the front windows while the lamps inside made small amber pools between the stacks. There were very few customers. There were, as far as Theo could tell, no customers at all. There was only the woman.

Yara Vane stood at the counter with a book in her hands—not reading it, he realized, but *repairing* it, a thin brush loaded with paste moving along a cracked spine with a steadiness that made him think of surgeons, or of people who had learned, somewhere in their lives, that if their hands stopped moving they might start shaking. She was smaller than he remembered from the trial. Her hair was more gray than black now, pulled back in a knot that had given up on being tidy. She wore a cardigan the color of wet sand, and she did not look up.

"We close at six," she said. Her voice was quiet, unhurried, the voice of someone who had spent five years talking mostly to herself. "But I can hold a book if you want to come back tomorrow."

"I'm not here for a book." His voice came out wrong—too high, scraped. He swallowed and tried again. "I'm here because of one."

The brush stopped moving.

She looked up then, and Theo felt the full weight of her attention land on him, and it was nothing like he'd expected. He had imagined, all these years, that she would look at him with guilt, or pity, or that professional blankness cops wear like a coat. Instead she looked at him with recognition—the immediate, unerring recognition of a woman who had spent her life cataloging faces and had never once lost one. He watched her arrive at his name before he said it.

"You're Theo," she said. "Marchetti."

"Yes."

"Odile's brother."

The name in the shop was like a stone dropped into still water. Theo felt the ripples move through the shelves, through the amber light, through his own chest. "Yes," he said again, and it came out as barely a sound.

She set down the brush. She set down the book. She came around the counter slowly, and up close he could see the fine lines around her eyes, the small scar through one eyebrow he'd never noticed at the trial, the way her hands—ink-stained, precise—hung at her sides as if they were waiting for instructions they'd been denied a long time.

"Your mother," Yara said. "Rosa. She passed in August."

"September." His throat worked. "You knew her?"

"I knew *of* her. She used to come to the library on Thursdays. Marge mentioned—" Yara stopped herself, and something moved behind her eyes, something filed away. "I'm sorry for your loss."

"She forbade me to come here." The words were out of him now, tumbling, five years of held breath finally exhaling. "She forbade me to talk to you, to call you, to even think about bringing you anything. She said you'd closed the file and closed the door and there was nothing left in either. She said—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, hated being twenty-six years old and cracking in a stranger's bookshop, but there was nowhere left for the words to go. They had been building pressure in him since the funeral. Since before the funeral. Since the night he'd found the diary and his mother's face had gone white as the dish towel she wrapped it in. "She said the case was over and digging it up would only bury us deeper."

Yara said nothing. She had gone very still, in the way that he would later learn was her version of leaning in.

"And now she's gone," Theo said, "and I'm the only one left, and the sheriff is going to put Roy Calloway on trial again in—" He checked, uselessly, as though the date might have changed since he'd last checked it an hour ago. "In eleven weeks. And I found something, Ms. Vane. Two years ago. Three. I don't even know anymore. Time doesn't—" He pressed his knuckles against his eyes. "I found my sister's diary. On the riverbank, where they said she went into the water. And it has entries in it that were written *after* she disappeared. After. Three weeks after. And I've been sitting on it for years because my mother told me to, and now she's dead, and Roy Calloway is going to be convicted again for something I don't think he did, and I can't—" He pulled the bundle from inside his jacket. The dish towel. The lavender and dust. "I can't sit on it anymore. It's sitting on *me*."

He held it out.

For a long moment Yara Vane didn't take it. She looked at the towel the way a doctor looks at an X-ray—reading the shape beneath the wrapping before the wrapping came off. Rain ticked at the glass. Somewhere in the back of the shop a clock Theo couldn't see was keeping a small, stubborn time of its own.

"Sit down," she said finally.

"I can stand."

"Sit down anyway." She nodded toward a reading chair near the window, its cushions worn into a shallow bowl by years of other people's waiting. "What you're about to hand me is going to take a while, and I don't hand things back to people who faint in my shop. It's bad for the carpet."

It was almost a joke. It wasn't quite a joke. Theo sat.

She took the bundle from him then, and her hands were steady, and that steadiness steadied him in some way he couldn't explain—watching her unwrap the dish towel with the same unhurried care she'd given the book's cracked spine, folding the towel back corner by corner, until the diary lay in her palms.

It was small. Blue. Water-stained along one edge, the cover buckled where the river had gotten to it before the riverbank had given it up. Odile's name was written inside the front cover in his sister's looping, left-slanted hand—*Odile Marchetti, this belongs to me, and if you're reading it, put it back*—and beneath that, in his mother's tighter script, a single date: the day she'd found it.

Yara opened it.

Theo watched her read. He had expected her to skim, to flip, to do the cop thing, the fast thing. Instead she read the way she'd repaired the book—slowly, thoroughly, her thumb pausing at the corner of each page before turning it, as though the pages might bruise. The rain kept ticking. The clock kept its small time. And Theo sat in the shallow bowl of the chair with his hands between his knees and felt, page by page, the weight of five years begin to shift—not lifting, not yet, but *moving*, the way a great load shifts on a shoulder before it can be set down.

"Three weeks after," Yara said at last. Her voice had changed. It was quieter and harder at once, like metal cooled in water. "This entry is dated three weeks after she disappeared."

"I know."

"Your mother saw this."

"She saw this and she wrapped it in a towel and she put it in the sewing table and she never spoke of it again for the rest of her life." Theo's laugh came out broken. "I used to think she was protecting me. Then I thought she was protecting herself. Now I think—" He stopped. Looked at his hands. "Now I think she was afraid. Not of the diary. Of what it would mean that she'd kept it. That she'd known, all this time, and said nothing while Roy Calloway rotted in a cell."

Yara closed the diary gently, but her hand stayed on it, flat, the way you'd keep a hand on something alive.

"Do you understand what you've brought me, Theo?" she asked.

"I think so."

"I don't think you do." She looked at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen in her at the trial—something that had been asleep a long time and was now, unmistakably, awake. "This isn't a book. This is a wound with a cover on it. The moment I open it past this page, everything your mother tried to keep closed—everything *I* tried to keep closed—it opens. The sheriff, the Aldous family, the whole rotten arrangement this town runs on. It won't go back in the towel. You understand that?"

The rain had thickened against the windows, running in long silver threads. Theo thought of his mother's grave, the granite, the grass not yet grown in over the turned earth. He thought of Roy Calloway, whom he'd seen once through the glass of a courtroom, a big ruined man with his hands folded like a man at his own funeral.

"I've spent five years doing what my mother told me," Theo said. "And it got us here. Roy's still in a cell. Odile's still gone. My mother died with a secret in her sewing table and I don't even know if it kept her safe or just kept her small." He stood up. His legs shook, but they held. "I don't want to be kept anymore, Ms. Vane. I want to know what happened to my sister. And I want Roy Calloway to walk out of that courthouse a free man before they can bury him a second time. I'm asking you to do the thing everyone says you're best at. I'm asking you to open the file."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the harbor light came on through the rain, a white eye blinking in the gray. When she spoke, her voice was almost too low to hear over the weather.

"The last case I opened," she said, "cost me everything I had. I walked out of the bureau and I told myself I was done, and I built this shop, and I filed this town's secrets on these shelves like a librarian of other people's sins, and I told myself that was enough. That was the bargain. I keep the records. I don't open them." Her thumb moved across the diary's buckled cover, once, like a pulse being taken. "You're asking me to break the bargain."

"Yes," Theo said. "I am."

Another silence. Then Yara Vane did something he did not expect. She smiled—not the polite shopkeeper's smile he'd braced for, but something smaller and truer and more tired, the smile of a woman who has just felt, somewhere deep in the machinery of her, a gear engage that had been rusted for five years.

"You know what your sister wrote in here?" she said. "On the last page. Before the entries stop."

Theo's breath caught. "No. I never—I couldn't read past the first few. It felt like—"

"Like reading her mail."

"Like reading her ghost."

Yara nodded slowly. She looked down at the diary in her hands, and when she spoke again, she was almost speaking to herself.

"She wrote: *'If someone finds this, they should know I didn't go quietly. I went carefully. There's a difference.'*" Yara looked up. "She went carefully, Theo. That's not what a girl writes three weeks before she drowns. That's what a girl writes when she's planning to survive."

The words landed in Theo's chest like a fist and a hand at the same time—pain and something dangerously close to hope, tangled so tightly together he couldn't tell which was which. His eyes burned. The shop blurred into amber smears.

"Then help me find her," he whispered. "Not the file. Not the case. *Her.*"

Yara Vane closed her hand around the diary and held it against her sternum for a moment, the way a soldier holds a flag, or a letter, or anything that has to be carried through fire.

"First," she said, "we find out who she was afraid of. Because a girl who goes carefully is a girl who's being *hunted*, and I want to know the hunter before the hunter knows we're looking." She was already moving toward the back of the shop, toward the shelves that weren't shelves, toward the archive Theo had heard rumors of his whole life. "And Theo—"

He looked up.

"From tonight on, you don't walk past this shop anymore. You come in the front door. You're in this now, and the people who did this to your sister—they've had five years to get comfortable. Comfortable people make mistakes." She paused at the threshold of the back room, the diary held tight against her, and the lamplight caught the scar through her eyebrow, and for a moment she looked less like a bookseller than like something that had been waiting five years in the dark for exactly this bell to ring. "We start tomorrow. First light. Bring your mother's sewing table key."

"The key? Why?"

Yara's eyes went to the diary, then to him, and there was something new in them—something sharp and bright and awake.

"Because your mother didn't just hide the diary, Theo. A woman that afraid hides *everything*. And I want to know what else was in that drawer."

* * *

That night Theo walked home through the rain with the empty dish towel folded in his jacket where the diary had been, and the lavender smell rising off it like a question. At the house on Pelican Street he did not turn on the lights. He went straight to the sewing table in his mother's room, knelt on the cold floorboards, and slid the little brass key into the lock his mother had told him, all his life, was for buttons.

The drawer opened.

Inside, on top of a neat stack of mending, lay a second bundle—smaller than the diary, tied with kitchen string, and beneath the string, in his mother's hand, a label that stopped Theo's heart where he knelt:

*Letters. Do not open. Do not open. Do not open.*

Three times. His mother had written it three times.

And on the floor of the drawer, half-hidden under the sewing box, glinting dully in the light from the streetlamp outside—a bus station token. The kind they stopped selling five years ago.
