## 14. The Bookshop Vigil

Yara Vane woke on the cot behind the register to the sound of a broom.

For a moment she didn't know where she was—the cot was narrow and the blanket smelled of dust and old paper, and her body had learned, over five years, to interpret every unexpected sound as a threat. Then the sound resolved itself: the rhythmic hiss of bristles on hardwood, patient, unhurried, coming from the front of the shop. Someone was sweeping her floor.

Her hand found the lamp switch. The back room swam into being—crates of unsorted paperbacks, the filing cabinets she'd never let anyone see, the cot she'd started sleeping on three nights ago, after the ransacking, because the apartment upstairs felt too far from the door.

The ransacking. Right.

She came around the shelves with her heart going hard and stopped.

There were four teenagers in her bookshop.

They were not looting. That was the first thing her detective's mind cataloged, the way it cataloged everything whether she asked it to or not: not looting. Two boys and two girls, maybe sixteen, seventeen, in hoodies and work gloves, and one of them—she recognized him vaguely, the Delacroix boy who came in on Thursdays to read the marine magazines without buying anything—was on his knees with a dustpan, gathering glass into neat little piles while the others worked the brooms in careful arcs, avoiding the shelves, avoiding the books that had been knocked to the floor, setting some of them upright as they went. One girl had a roll of masking tape and was marking the spines of damaged books with small X's, an inventory system, a triage system, and Yara thought: *someone taught them that*, and then thought: *no, someone taught them to care about books, the system they invented themselves.*

The Delacroix boy looked up and went pale. "Ma'am. Ms. Vane. We didn't—we knocked. Nobody answered."

"I was asleep," Yara said. Her voice came out gravel-rough, unused. "It's—" She looked at the window, where the light was doing something gray and early and salt-colored. "It's not seven."

"We know," the girl with the tape said. She had a serious face, freckles, hair pulled back with a pencil through the knot. "We came early so you'd see it was done before you had to see us doing it. But you were asleep, so." She shrugged, as if that explained everything, and maybe it did.

Yara stood there in her cardigan with her hair uncombed and felt something crack in her chest that was not unpleasant, exactly, but was not anything she had a shelf for.

"Who told you to do this?" she asked, because there had to be a hand behind it, an order, a chain of command. There was always a chain of command. That was the first thing they taught you and the last thing you unlearned.

"Nobody," the Delacroix boy said. "I mean—everybody. It was all over the diner. That somebody broke your windows and tore up your shop. My mom said, *that woman files everything for this town, the least we can do is file her floor.*" He said it without embarrassment, as though it were simply a fact of the world, and Yara had to turn away and pretend to examine a shelf so that none of them would see her face do whatever it was about to do.

*That woman files everything for this town.*

She had thought of the archive as a secret. All those years of index cards and clippings and cross-referenced gossip, filed in the dark of the back room, she had believed she was a spy in her own life, a ghost with a card catalog. It had not occurred to her—not once, not in five years—that the town knew. That they had always known. That they had let her keep her secrets because they understood, in the wordless way small towns understand things, that a woman who files everything is a woman holding something up.

The bell over the door chimed.

Marge came in carrying a thermos the size of a fire extinguisher and a paper bag that was leaking the smell of cinnamon into the room like a rumor. She was seventy-one years old and moved like a woman who had spent forty years shelving books on ladders, which she had, and she took in the scene—Yara in her cardigan, the teenagers, the brooms, the taped spines—and nodded once, as if confirming a delivery.

"Coffee," she announced. "And the Hendersons sent kolaches, which I am claiming credit for because I told them you'd want something sweet and they can't resist me." She set the thermos on the counter, which was still scarred from where someone had gone through the drawers two nights ago, and looked at Yara over her glasses. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome. Drink the coffee." Marge turned to the teenagers and became, in the space of a single breath, the woman who had run the Marrow Bay library since before any of them were born, a woman whose disapproval was a weather system. "Delacroix. You missed a spot by the poetry section. Poetry is fragile. Everything about it is fragile."

"Yes, Ms. Okafor."

Yara drank the coffee. It was terrible—burnt, over-sweet, the way diner coffee is when it's been on the burner too long, except this hadn't been on a burner, this had been carried across town in a thermos at six in the morning, and it was the best coffee she had ever tasted, and she hated that, hated the way her throat tightened around it, hated the way her eyes stung, and she drank it anyway, standing behind her broken counter in her broken shop, letting a seventy-year-old librarian bully her into being taken care of.

* * *

By nine, the shop had changed.

Not restored—the glass was still gone from the front window, and someone had tacked a sheet of clear plastic over the frame as a temporary mercy against the wind, so the light came in strange and rippled, underwater light, the whole shop lit like the inside of an aquarium. But it was *ordered*. The books were upright. The glass was gone. The filing cabinets in the back—Yara had checked, three times, with her heart in her teeth—were untouched, because the ransackers had never found them, and because the teenagers, without being told, had kept their sweeping out of the back room, some instinct telling them that was the part of the house where the family kept its grief.

The diner owner came at nine-thirty. Sal Marchese, sixty, forearms like dock rope, who had run the Blue Heron Diner for thirty years and had never once, in Yara's five years in Marrow Bay, said more to her than *the usual?* even though she had never once had a usual. He set two foil trays on the counter without asking where, and said, "Clam chowder and the rice casserole. The casserole's my wife's. Don't ask what's in it, she won't tell me either." Then he stood there a moment, working his jaw, and said, "Whoever did this—my money's on it wasn't a burglar. Burglars take the register. They didn't take your register."

"No," Yara said. "They didn't."

"They were looking for something." He said it flatly, the way men in fishing towns say things about weather and tides and other forces they can't control. "You find what they were looking for, you tell them Sal Marchese says hello." And he left before she could answer, which she understood was a kindness, because it meant she didn't have to.

The morning filled up after that the way a tide fills a channel—quietly, and then all at once. The woman from the hardware store came with a roll of plastic sheeting and a staple gun and fixed the window properly, refusing payment with a look that ended the conversation before it started. The pastor's wife brought flowers, which Yara didn't have a vase for, so Marge produced one from somewhere, because Marge produced everything from somewhere. A man Yara knew only as the oyster guy—big, sunburned, always at the end of the counter at the diner—dropped off a bundle of firewood "for the stove, if you got one, and if you don't, you should." Two of the teenagers came back after school without being asked and started alphabetizing the history section, which had been a disaster for years, and Yara watched a boy who couldn't have been more than fifteen squint at a spine and murmur "*B* before *Br*, right?" and felt her whole understanding of her own life tilt on its axis.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she kept circling like a bird over water.

She had spent five years believing she was hiding. She had closed the bureau door behind her, closed the case file, closed the shop every night at six and her heart every night at six-oh-one, and she had believed—she had *needed* to believe—that she had made herself invisible. A woman behind a counter. A name on a lease. The town had been scenery to her, a set she moved through on her way to nothing. She had cataloged it—every grudge, every debt, every affair and inheritance and quiet bankruptcy—because cataloging was how she loved things, or how she had learned to do the next best thing to loving them: she *knew* them, exhaustively, from a distance, the way you know a tide chart without ever going in the water.

And the whole time, the town had been watching her back.

Not surveilling—she would have caught that, some part of her would have. *Seeing* her. The way a shore sees a lighthouse. They had known who she was, what she had been, what she'd lost—of course they had; Marrow Bay forgot nothing, it was a town built on remembering things its founding families wanted buried—and they had let her be, and waited, the way you wait for a wounded animal to decide it lives with you now. And the moment the windows broke, they had come. Not with questions. Not with pity, which she could not have survived. With brooms. With coffee. With a rice casserole of undisclosed composition.

"You're crying," Marge said, not looking up from the shelf she was dusting.

"I am not."

"You are. It's fine. The chowder's salty." Marge set down her cloth and looked at her, and her eyes behind the glasses were older and kinder than her voice would ever admit to being. "Yara. Sit down."

"I'm fine."

"Sit down anyway. It's not a request, it's a shelving decision. You go where I put you."

Yara sat. The armchair by the window was old and green and had been there when she bought the shop, and she had never once sat in it—customers sat in it, waiting people, people with somewhere to be that wasn't here—and sitting in it now felt like wearing someone else's coat. Through the plastic sheeting, the street rippled. A car went by and became something dreamlike, something underwater.

"They were going to burn it," Yara said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Whoever ransacked the place. The archive. They took every box, every card file, five years of—" She stopped. Started again. "I filed this town, Marge. Every secret. Every debt. I told myself it was for the town's sake. That someday it would matter, that the record would matter. But I think—I think I just needed somewhere to put everything I couldn't feel."

Marge was quiet for a while. Outside, the Delacroix boy was scraping the last of the tape residue off the window frame with a razor blade, careful as a surgeon.

"You know what I did when my husband died?" Marge said at last. "I cataloged his tools. Every wrench, every screwdriver, every damn thing in that garage, labeled and boxed. Took me four months. People said, *Marge, you're not grieving, you're organizing.* And I said, *yes.*" She shrugged. "It's the same thing. Nobody organizes like that unless they're grieving. And nobody grieves like that unless they loved something they couldn't keep."

Yara looked at her.

"The archive wasn't a hiding place," Marge went on, turning back to her dusting as though she were discussing dewey decimals. "It was a promise. You were keeping the town's memory safe because nobody else would. And now the town's keeping *you* safe, and I don't want to hear one word about how you don't deserve it, because I've heard you alphabetize under your breath and I know exactly how stubborn you are."

The bell chimed. The pastor's wife was back, or the hardware woman, or someone else from the long list of people whose names Yara knew from index cards and whose faces she was only now, shamefully, only now, learning to attach to lives. The shop filled with the smell of chowder and woodsmoke and old paper, and the plastic window rippled, and somewhere a teenager laughed at something, and Yara Vane sat in the green armchair she had never sat in and understood, with the slow vertigo of a woman stepping off a ledge she'd been standing on for five years, that she had never been hiding at all.

She had been *kept*.

* * *

It was full dark when they finally left.

Marge had left an hour earlier, over Yara's protests, with instructions about locking the door and an ominous promise to return in the morning. The teenagers had gone home in twos, waving through the plastic. The chowder was gone. The casserole sat in Yara's cold little kitchenette, waiting. She had locked the door at last and stood in the middle of the shop in the lamplight, in the underwater quiet, and let herself feel the shape of the day.

Then she saw the envelope.

It was on the counter, half under the thermos Marge had left behind—deliberately, Yara would later decide, because Marge did nothing accidentally. A plain white envelope, no name on it. Yara opened it with the care she brought to everything now, everything, since two nights ago when her shop had been torn apart by hands looking for a dead girl's diary.

Inside was a photograph.

A tourist snapshot, the kind taken from the deck of the Marrow Bay ferry on a bright day, water glittering, gulls frozen mid-wheel. On the back, in ballpoint, in a hand she didn't know: *Saw this in my aunt's photo album when she visited. Thought of the girl in the papers. Thought you'd want it. —R.*

Yara held the photo under the lamp.

It was a crowded shot—tourists in windbreakers, a man asleep against the rail, children feeding gulls. And there, at the edge of the frame, half-turned toward the water, a woman in a gray coat with her hair pulled back, her face in profile, sharp and young and unmistakably, impossibly—

Yara's hands began to shake.

She knew that profile. She had studied it for months, five years ago, in photographs that stopped at seventeen years old. This woman was older. This woman had lived. This woman was standing on a ferry that ran out of Marrow Bay twice a day, in a photograph that someone said was two years old.

*Odile.*

The lamp hummed. The plastic window breathed in the wind. Outside, the town lay dark and quiet, holding its secrets, and Yara Vane stood in her bookshop—her *kept*, her *found*, her no-longer-empty bookshop—with a photograph in her hands and the dead case opening in her chest like a door she had nailed shut finally giving way.

Two years ago, Odile Marchetti had ridden the ferry.

Which meant she hadn't just survived. She had come *back*. And someone in this town—someone who still remembered the girl in the papers, someone kind enough to leave an envelope under a thermos—had seen her too.

Yara turned the photograph over again, reading and re-reading the note. *Thought you'd want it.* Not the sheriff. Not the papers. *You.*

She reached for her coat.
