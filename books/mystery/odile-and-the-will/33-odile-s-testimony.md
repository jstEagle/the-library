## 33. Odile's Testimony

The boathouse smelled of tar and old water, and Odile Marchetti had been holding her breath in it, one way or another, for five years.

She sat on an overturned crate with the shotgun across her knees—not aimed, not lowered, just *there*, the way a held breath is there—and watched the two women who had come down the dock at dusk. The detective was the one from the books. Odile had known it before the woman said her own name, known it the way you know weather coming: something in the set of her shoulders, something in the way her eyes went first to the exits, then to the girl, then back to the exits. Yara Vane looked at a room the way Odile's mother used to look at a pot about to boil over. Like she could hold it together by attention alone.

The other one was a deputy. Odile had seen her badge and nearly raised the gun. Five years of *trust no one with a badge* written in her own hand, hidden in a marsh, and here was a badge standing in the doorway with rain dripping off its owner's hat.

But the deputy had put her weapon on the floorboards, palms out, and said, "Cora Lin. I kept the diary when I was ordered to burn it." And that was the password, wasn't it. That was the only password that mattered anymore.

So now Odile sat with the shotgun across her knees and the story rising in her throat like water coming up through floorboards, and she understood that this was the moment she had been running toward and running from for five years, and that she could not do both anymore.

"You should sit," she said. "It's a long story. I've only ever told it to the dark."

* * *

She started, as she always started it in her head, with the smell.

"People think you remember the big things," she said. "You don't. You remember the smell. That night it was cut grass and chlorine and cigar smoke. The Aldous estate, the party for Edmund's seventy-fifth birthday. I was seventeen and I was there because my mother cleaned for them and I sometimes filled in when the regular girl was sick, and the regular girl was sick." She laughed, a small dry sound with no humor in it, like a stick breaking. "That's the whole reason. A girl with a cold. If she hadn't had a cold, none of this would have happened, and Roy Calloway would have gone on fixing fences his whole life and nobody would ever have learned his name."

Yara Vane didn't write anything down. Odile noticed that. The detective just sat, hands loose on her knees, listening with her whole body, and something in Odile unclenched a little at that. Everyone else who had ever listened to her had been writing something down—statements, reports, checks.

"I went down to the boathouse to get more glasses. That was my excuse, anyway. Mostly I went down there to breathe. That house was full of people who looked through you like you were a window, and the boathouse was the one place on the property where the windows were just windows. It was nearly midnight. The party was still going up at the house. And I heard voices."

She made herself say it plainly. She had spent five years saying it plainly in the dark, over and over, so that the plainness would hold the shape of it and the shape wouldn't melt.

"Mr. Voss—you know who he is, the lawyer, silver hair, voice like a locked drawer—and Julian Aldous. The son. Edmund's only son." Her hands had gone still on the shotgun. "Edmund was in his wheelchair on the dock. He'd been wheeled down there to look at the water—he liked the water at night, the housekeeper told me, it was the only thing he still liked. And they were standing over him. And it looked wrong. It looked wrong the way a picture hung crooked looks wrong, you know? Nothing dramatic. No struggle. Voss was holding Edmund's wrist like he was taking a pulse, and Julian was looking at his watch, and Edmund wasn't moving, and there was a glass tipped over on the boards, and the smell of cigar smoke was very strong, too strong, like it had been blown onto him on purpose."

She stopped. Breathed. The rain had softened to a hiss on the roof, and out on the black water a heron called once, that raw wooden sound, and she let it fill the silence the way it had filled so many silences before.

"Then Voss took something out of his coat. A little bottle. I couldn't see it clearly, but I saw what he did with it—poured it into the glass, set the glass down by the chair, arranged Edmund's hand near it. Like setting a table. And Julian said—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "Julian said, 'He signed it Tuesday. It's done. Make it look like his heart.' And Voss said, 'The heart is a generous organ. It agrees to anything.'"

The words came out of her and hung in the boathouse air, and for a moment Odile felt five years lighter and five years heavier at once. Saying it in the dark had never cost anything. Saying it to faces was different. Faces received it. Faces made it real.

"I ran," she said. "I didn't decide to run. My legs decided. I went out the back of the boathouse through the reeds and up the creek path, and I heard someone shout behind me—not an alarm, just a shout—and I ran the way I used to run from the house when I was little and the dogs were loose. I knew those woods the way you know your own teeth. I made it to the road and I hitched a ride with a logging truck as far as the junction, and I sat in the cab shaking, and the driver asked if I was all right and I said yes, and that was the last lie I told for free."

* * *

"They looked for me," she said. "I learned later how hard they looked. Not the sheriff—well, the sheriff too, but the sheriff was the show. Voss had a man, a private one. Big, quiet, wears a canvas jacket even in summer. I saw him twice in the first month, once at the bus station in Corvallis and once outside a diner in Newport, and after the second time I understood: he wasn't looking for a missing girl. He was looking for a witness. There's a difference, and the difference is what happens after you're found."

Yara's jaw tightened. Odile saw it, a small motion like a door closing, and thought: *she knows him. She's met him.* Good. Then she wouldn't have to make the man real.

"I got lucky. Or I got found, which is its own kind of luck, the bad kind. Mrs. Halloran found me."

The name came out of her mouth and she had to sit with it a moment. Grief for a frightened old man was a strange shape to carry; grief for a frightened old woman was stranger.

"Mrs. Halloran had kept the Aldous house for thirty years. Cook, housekeeper, the one who knew where every body was buried, metaphorically. She'd seen me come out of the reeds that night—she'd gone down to the dock after the party to tidy, and she saw me running, and she saw Voss and Julian standing over the chair, and she put it together faster than I did, because she'd spent thirty years learning what that family looked like when they were arranging something. She found me two weeks later. I was sleeping in the laundromat in Brookings, in the warmth of the dryers. She sat down next to me and said, 'You're the Marchetti girl. You saw what happened on the dock.' And I said, 'I didn't see anything,' and she said, 'Child, I've been lying to that family for thirty years. Don't insult us both.'"

Odile's eyes stung. She let them. There was no one left to be strong for except the future, and the future was sitting right here, listening.

"She hid me. That's the part nobody will ever believe, so I want to say it exactly right: she hid me *for the family*. Not against them. She thought—she believed, all the way down—that she was protecting me from Voss's man, and protecting the Aldous name from a scandal, and that in time, when Edmund's death had settled into history, it would be safe for me to come back and say what I saw. She moved me three times. The cannery first—she'd kept a key since the strike in '79, when the company locked the workers out and she smuggled food in through the seawall. Then a room over her sister's garage in Reedsport. Then back, because she was getting old and scared and she wanted me where she could see me."

"And then she got more scared," Yara said quietly. Not a question.

"And then she got more scared." Odile looked down at the shotgun. "Voss's man came to the house. I don't know what was said. I only know that after, Mrs. Halloran changed. She started talking about moving me inland, somewhere with no water, no roads. She started sleeping with a chair under her doorknob. And one night in the spring—eight months ago now, almost to the day—she said, 'If anything happens to me, you go to the marsh. The herons. You know.' And two days later she was dead. Heart attack, they said. She was seventy-nine and she'd had a bad heart for years, and maybe it was true. But she'd also had that bad heart for thirty years, and she'd out-argued death the whole time, and then suddenly, right when I needed her to keep arguing—"

She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the dark went red.

"I'm sorry. I've never said that part out loud either."

"Take your time," Yara said. "I've got nowhere to be that matters more."

And that—*that*—was when Odile started to believe. Not because of the diary, not because of the deputy's badge on the floorboards. Because of that sentence, said without performance, by a woman who had clearly spent her whole life with nowhere to be that mattered more, and had finally found the place.

* * *

"After she died, I ran for real. I took the money we'd saved—my mother had been sending it through the ferry office, did Theo tell you? No? Then that's his story to tell, and it's a hard one, and I'm sorry for it. I took the money and the name E. Marsh, because I have a mean sense of humor and the marsh had saved me twice, and I went to ground in the cabin up the coast. Eight months. Eight months of watching the road through a gap in the curtain, of buying everything with money orders, of writing in the diary in code because the habit was the only thing Mrs. Halloran left me that still worked."

She looked up. "You decoded the dates. The book titles."

Yara nodded. "The Shipping News for the day she died. I'm sorry."

"The Shipping News." Odile laughed, and it came out wet and cracked and real. "I chose it because it was the saddest book on my mother's shelf. She'd have hated it and she'd have understood it. That was the whole art of the code—pick the title that hurts."

She set the shotgun down on the crate beside her, finally, fully, and laced her fingers together so they'd have something to do besides shake.

"So that's the story. That's all of it. A girl with a cold, a party, a dock, a glass arranged by a dead man's hand. A lawyer with a generous heart. A son who checked his watch. An old woman who hid me and died of it, maybe. And eight months of being the man in the canvas jacket's shadow, until I heard the will might finally be read—really read, not the clean copy, the one with the amendment—and I came back, because the amendment is the only thing that proves I matter to them, and the only thing that proves they were afraid of me."

"The amendment," Yara said. "'The girl from the boathouse.'"

"You found it." Odile searched the detective's face. "Then you know why I came back. It's not the money. It's not even justice, not really—justice is a word for things that happen to other people, in my experience. I came back because that piece of paper is the first thing in five years that says I was *there*. That I saw. That I counted. They wrote me into a will and then they buried the will, and a buried will is just another kind of missing girl."

The rain had stopped. Through the boathouse doors the water lay flat and pewter-colored, and the first thin light of what would be a gray morning was coming up over the headland. Odile realized she had talked all night. She realized, also, that for the first time in five years, the silence on the other side of the story didn't feel like a held breath. It felt like an exhale.

"Why me?" she asked suddenly. "I need to know. I picked you—don't look surprised, I picked you, the diary didn't fall into Theo's hands by accident, I left it where I knew he'd look, after Mama died, because I needed it to reach someone and I couldn't carry it myself anymore. But I picked you because of the books. Because of what people say about you. I need to know if I was right."

Yara Vane was quiet a long moment. Out on the water, the herons were waking, those gray prehistoric shapes unfolding against the reeds.

"You were right," the detective said, "because I spent five years filing this town's secrets instead of facing my own, and I know exactly what it costs to hide. And because when I read your diary, I didn't read a case file. I read a girl. Nobody else in this town has managed that in five years, and most of them never tried."

Odile nodded, and looked down, and found she was crying, and found that it was fine, that it was just weather, that it would pass.

* * *

It was the deputy who broke the moment, because deputies are practical people and the morning was practical.

"We have a problem," Cora Lin said. "The trial. Roy's defense rests today. Tomorrow, maybe. After that, the jury goes out, and if they come back with a verdict before Odile testifies—"

"Then Roy Calloway goes to prison for a second time for a crime that never happened," Yara said, "and no amendment to any will undoes it."

"Voss will have the courthouse watched," Cora went on. "He'll have the roads watched. He knows the marsh gave you something, he's had a tail on you for days—my tail, technically, which is now our tail, which is a sentence I never thought I'd say. And the fixer—"

"The man in the canvas jacket," Odile said, and her voice went flat and cold, the voice of the girl in the curtain gap. "He's close. He's been close since the cannery. I watched him watch you there, from the tree line. I've been one step behind him for eight months and I'm tired of being the shadow."

Yara stood. Odile watched her do it—the unhurried way she rose, the way her eyes swept the boathouse doors, the water, the tree line, cataloguing, always cataloguing—and thought: *yes. Her. The diary chose right.*

"Then we stop being shadows," Yara said. "The trial is in session at nine. Voss expects you to run, Odile. Every move he's made for five years assumes you'll run. So we're not going to run." She looked at Odile, and then at Cora, and something passed between the three of them that had no name yet but would, later, have many. "We're going to walk you into that courthouse through the front door, in broad daylight, while he's watching the back."

Odile picked up the shotgun, broke it open, and emptied the shells into her coat pocket. Not because she was done being afraid. Because some doors you walk through armed, and some doors you walk through witnessed, and she had spent five years learning the difference.

"The front door," she said. "My mother always said I had a talent for entrances."

Out in the marsh, the herons lifted all at once, a gray thunder of wings, going up into the light.
