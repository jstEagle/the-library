## 35. The Girl in the Well of the Court

They dressed me in a dead woman's coat.

That was the joke I made in my head, standing in the courthouse bathroom with the mirror fogged and my hands shaking under the faucet water, because the coat was Yara Vane's—borrowed, not dead—and because if I didn't make the joke I was going to be sick on the tile floor of the Marrow Bay County Courthouse for the second time that morning.

The coat was gray wool, too big in the shoulders, smelling faintly of paper and cedar and something like rain that had been carried indoors. Yara had buttoned it around me in the back seat of Cora Lin's car and said, "Nobody looks at a face when there's a coat they recognize." Which was a detective thing to say, and also a mothering thing to say, and I think she knew both.

Five years. Five years of practicing this moment in the dark of rented rooms, in the marsh reeds at dawn, in the mirror of a ferry terminal bathroom in a village where nobody knew my name. In every rehearsal I walked in alone. In every rehearsal my voice held.

In none of them did Theo hold my hand so hard in the stairwell that his knuckles went white, or did Marge—a woman I only knew as the librarian who once let me hide from a rainstorm between the shelves when I was nine—press a peppermint into my palm and say, "Old trick. Keeps you from crying. Don't tell anyone I told you."

* * *

The courtroom smelled the way courtrooms are supposed to smell: old wood polish, cold coffee, the ghost of a hundred nervous sweats soaked into the benches. But underneath it, Marrow Bay. Salt. The sea got into everything here, even justice.

Cora went first, in her uniform, her badge catching the light like a small defiant sun. Then Marge, then Theo's shoulder against mine, then me.

I heard it before I understood it—the sound a room makes when it stops. Not silence. Silence is empty. This was full: forty breaths caught at once, a pen rolling off a desk and no one reaching for it, the prosecutor's mouth still open around a word he'd never finish. It was the sound of a town seeing a ghost, and I was the ghost, and God help me, after five years of being one, it felt almost like coming home.

Roy Calloway saw me first.

He was sitting at the defense table in a suit that fit him wrong—too clean, too new, bought by people who felt sorry for him—and when his eyes found mine they did something I wasn't ready for. They didn't widen. They didn't fill with vindication or tears. They just *softened*, the way you look at a kid you once drove to a bus station in the rain because she asked, and he said yes, and he never told anyone, and it cost him everything.

His lips moved. No sound came out. But I read it. Everyone who ever spent time around Roy Calloway learned to read his lips, because Roy was a man who'd learned early that speaking could get you hit.

*Hey, Odile-girl.*

The judge had to say my name three times before I could answer to it.

"Miss Marchetti. Do you swear—"

"I swear," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle like river ice in March, and I put my hand on the Bible, and my hand was steady even though nothing else was. "I swear."

* * *

The well of the court is what they call the space between the bench and the gallery, the open floor where witnesses stand and lawyers prowl. I'd looked the word up years ago, in a library dictionary, back when I was still a girl who looked things up instead of running from them. *Well.* Like a place you draw water from. Like a hole you can fall into.

Yara Vane sat in the third row with her gray coat folded over her arm—she'd taken it back the moment we were inside, some sleight of hand, some detective's trick of misdirection—and she watched me the way I imagined she watched everything: like a woman filing things away, but kindly. Like the filing itself was a form of care.

The defense lawyer—his name was Okafor, and his hands shook until Roy put one of his own enormous scarred ones over them, and then they didn't—asked me to state my name for the record.

"Odile Wren Marchetti." My whole name, spoken aloud in a room full of people, for the first time in five years. It came out of me like something I'd swallowed and kept swallowed so long it had grown roots. "I'm Roy Calloway's alibi. I'm the reason he's been in prison. And I'm here to take it back."

The prosecutor objected to something. The judge overruled it. I barely heard. I was looking at the jury, twelve faces arranged in the careful blankness of people trying not to stare, and I thought: *you have one job, all of you. Just listen.*

So I told them.

* * *

I told them about the night of October ninth, five years ago, when I cut through the Aldous property the way I'd done a hundred times—the gap in the hedge behind the boathouse, the path along the water, the shortcut home that saved me twenty minutes and cost me everything else.

I told them the lights were on in the main house at an hour when Edmund Aldous was always asleep by ten. I told them I heard voices through the open study window—raised, then low, then that terrible quiet that means something has already happened and everyone is deciding what to do about it.

"I saw two men carrying Mr. Aldous," I said. "Not helping him. Carrying him. The way you carry something heavy that can't walk on its own." My voice did the crack again. I let it. "One of them was Mr. Voss. The family lawyer. The other one—" and here I looked directly at the man sitting behind the prosecution table, the pale young man in the good suit whose face had gone the color of candle wax, "—was Julian Aldous. His grandson. He'd inherit everything if the old man died without changing his will. And the old man had changed it. I know because I heard them arguing about it through that window, weeks before. Julian saying, *he's losing his mind, he doesn't know what he's signing.* And Mr. Voss saying, *then we make sure he signs nothing else.*"

The courtroom made its stopped-breath sound again.

"Why didn't you come forward?" Okafor asked gently. "Why run?"

Because, your honor. Because I was seventeen and I'd just watched two powerful men commit murder and I knew—I *knew*, the way you know weather coming—that no one would believe me. Because Sheriff Pruitt had already decided Roy Calloway was guilty before Roy was even questioned; I'd heard him say so at the diner, laughing, saying the handyman had priors and juries loved a man with priors. Because when I ran to the bus station that night, shaking so hard I couldn't count my own money, it was Roy Calloway who found me huddled behind the ticket machine, and Roy who didn't ask questions, and Roy who drove me himself in his rattling truck and paid for my ticket out of an envelope of cash someone had pressed into his hand that same week—cash he thought was charity, cash I now understood was payment, because someone in that house had seen me at that window and wanted me gone quietly, and Roy was the convenient vehicle for my vanishing.

"They used him," I said, and my voice finally broke all the way open, and I let it break in front of everyone, because five years of holding a thing together teaches you that breaking is sometimes the strongest thing a body can do. "They used him as the lock on the door they closed behind me. He took my punishment. He sat in a cell for five years for a crime that never happened while the men who actually killed Edmund Aldous walked around this town being *respected*."

Julian Aldous's lawyer was on his feet. Objection. Speculation. The witness was traumatized, unreliable—

"Sit down, counselor," Judge Halloway said, in a voice like a door closing. She was an old woman with reading glasses on a chain and a reputation for patience, and there was no patience anywhere in her face now. "The witness will continue."

* * *

I told them about the hiding. About the retainer—the estate groundskeeper, a soft-spoken man named Ellery who had worked for the Aldouses for thirty years and who found me two days later, half-frozen in the culvert past the marsh, and who didn't turn me in. Who fed me. Who moved me to the cannery, then to the boathouse, then grew afraid—so afraid—because Voss's fixer was asking questions around the county, a man with a boat-shoe walk and a smile like a stapler, asking had anyone seen a girl, a Marchetti girl, and Ellery started sleeping with a shotgun across his doorway and finally gave me money and a burner phone and said, *run farther than I can reach, child, and don't ever write to me.*

I told them about E. Marsh. About the ferry office and the money orders and my mother—my mother, who knew, who had always known, who mailed me cash every month through a clerk who never asked questions, because an Aldous man had come to our house a week after I vanished and stood in our kitchen with his hat in his hands and said words that were half threat and half prayer: *the girl is alive and safe, Mrs. Marchetti, and she stays alive exactly as long as she stays gone.*

My mother had bargained with the devil and called it keeping me safe, and she had died carrying it, and I said that part too, into the hush, because Theo was in the front row with his jaw set like a boy refusing to cry and he deserved to hear his mother named brave out loud, in daylight, on the record.

Then Okafor handed me the papers.

"The People's exhibit," he said, though they weren't the People's—they were ours, Yara's and mine and Cora's, assembled in a motel room at two in the morning with the curtains taped shut. "A certified copy of an amendment to the last will and testament of Edmund Aldous, executed six weeks prior to his death, witnessed and notarized."

I read it aloud because Okafor asked me to, and because I wanted it in my own mouth, in the air, unburied.

"*Item fourteen,*" I read. "*To the girl from the boathouse, whom I shall not name here lest naming endanger her, but whom I know by sight and by kindness received: the sum of two hundred thousand dollars, and the use of the east cottage in perpetuity, should she ever wish to come home.*"

Two hundred thousand dollars. A cottage. *Come home.*

Edmund Aldous had known. The old man in the study window's other side—he'd known a girl watched him feed the herons off the boathouse dock some mornings, knew she took nothing and touched nothing and once, memorably, helped him up when he slipped on the wet planks. And whatever else Edmund Aldous had been—hard, cold, a man who built a fortune on fish guts and other people's backs—he had known he was dying, and he had known his heirs, and he had left a door open in his will for a stranger's granddaughter because she'd once been kind to him on a dock.

And they had buried it. Buried the amendment, buried the girl, buried the handyman. Three burials, one grave.

"Your Honor," Okafor said, "the defense moves to enter the amendment into evidence, and further moves that the court direct the county clerk to produce the original from the probate file."

Judge Halloway took off her glasses. Put them back on. Looked at the amendment for a long time, and then at Julian Aldous, and then at the empty chair where Sheriff Dale Pruitt should have been sitting and wasn't.

"So ordered," she said. "The amendment is admitted. Clerk, pull the probate file. And counsel—" her gaze swept the room like a searchlight, "—this court will reconvene tomorrow morning, and I strongly suggest that anyone with knowledge relevant to the execution of Edmund Aldous's will consider very carefully where they would rather be: in this courtroom, or on the wrong side of a subpoena."

* * *

Afterward, in the side room, I put my forehead against the cool painted cinderblock and breathed.

Theo found me there. He didn't say anything at first, just stood next to me the way he used to when we were kids waiting out thunderstorms on the porch, shoulder to shoulder, letting the noise happen overhead.

"You sounded like Mom," he finally said.

"That's the nicest and cruelest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Yeah." He laughed, wet-eyed. "She'd have been in the front row. She'd have brought snacks. She'd have glared at Julian Aldous so hard he'd have combusted."

"She'd have been proud of you," I said. "You're the one who carried the diary all these years. You're the one who finally brought it to her." I nodded toward the door, beyond which I could feel Yara Vane moving through the hallway like a current—people parting around her, deputies avoiding her eyes, one young deputy in uniform standing apart from the others with her hat in her hands and the look of a woman about to do something irreversible.

Theo followed my glance. "Is that the one who kept the diary? When they ordered it burned?"

"That's the one."

"She looks scared."

"She is," I said. "That's how you know she's going to do the right thing. Scared and doing it anyway—that's the whole recipe."

Through the frosted glass of the side-room door, I watched shapes move. Yara's shape, unmistakable now that I knew her—the particular economy of it, the way she stood like a filed document, everything in order. And approaching her, hurrying, a broader shape in a sheriff's uniform, moving with the loose-armed urgency of a man who has just realized the building is on fire and he is standing in it.

Pruitt. Coming to find Yara Vane, of all people, on the worst day of his life.

Theo and I looked at each other.

"Do we—" he started.

"No," I said. "That part isn't ours. That part's hers." I picked up the dead-woman's coat—Yara's coat, the borrowed armor, the gray wool that smelled of paper and rain—and slung it over my arm, and for the first time in five years, the future didn't feel like a country I was sneaking into.

It felt like a door, and it was opening, and somewhere on the other side of it Sheriff Dale Pruitt was about to start talking, and everything he said was going to burn all the way down.
