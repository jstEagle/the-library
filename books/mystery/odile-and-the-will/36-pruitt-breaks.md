## 36. Pruitt Breaks

The courthouse smelled of old varnish and newer fear, and Dale Pruitt had learned over thirty years to tell the difference between them by the way they sat in the back of his throat. Varnish was sweet, almost like maple syrup on a Sunday morning when he'd been a boy running the boardwalk at Marrow Bay before the tourists woke. Fear was metallic. It tasted like a coin held too long in the mouth, like blood you hadn't yet spilled but had already promised.

He stood in his office with the door closed and did not open the folder on his desk.

Cora Lin's report sat there like something dead that refused to stop breathing. Forty-one pages. He'd counted them twice last night, standing at his kitchen counter at two in the morning with a glass of bourbon he never poured from, just holding it, because pouring it would have been an admission that his hands needed steadying. Forty-one pages, typed, dated, signed, with an evidence log attached and a sworn statement that Deputy Cora Lin had been ordered by Sheriff Dale Pruitt to destroy a diary belonging to Odile Marchetti—and had not.

She'd kept it. Five months she'd kept it, folded into her life like a splinter working toward the heart, and then she'd walked it across town to Yara Vane's bookshop like a woman delivering a verdict of her own.

Pruitt put his palm flat on the folder. The paper was cool. Everything in this office was cool except him; he'd been running hot for five years, a furnace fed on a fuel he didn't have a name for until this week. Now he knew what it was called. It was called knowing.

Through the wall, muffled by plaster and forty years of repainting, he could hear the courtroom. The retrial of Roy Calloway had entered its second day, and the prosecution—the state's man, brought up from the county seat, a sharp-faced prosecutor named Delgado who'd never met Roy Calloway and never would need to—was walking the jury through photographs of a riverbank. Pruitt couldn't see the photographs but he knew them. He'd looked at them so many times in the first investigation that they'd worn grooves in him, the way water wears stone: slowly, patiently, without malice. A mudflat. A scatter of belongings. A girl's shoe, small and pale as a shell.

Except the girl wasn't in the river. The girl had walked into his courtroom yesterday afternoon wearing a gray coat and the face of every ghost he'd ever failed, and the whole room had made a sound he'd never heard a courtroom make—not a gasp exactly, more like the building itself inhaling—and Dale Pruitt had understood, standing at the back beside the water cooler, that his life had a floor and he had just fallen through it.

* * *

He'd known, in the way that men who lie know things, since the beginning. Not everything. Not the shape of it. But the grain of it, the way you can tell rotten wood by its weight.

Five years ago, Marcus Voss had come to his office on a Tuesday with rain still beaded on the shoulders of his coat, and he had set a checkbook down on Pruitt's desk—actually set it down, like a man laying a pistol on a bar—and said, "The family would prefer this stay simple."

"The family," Pruitt had said, meaning *which* family, though he'd already known. There was only one family in Marrow Bay that got spoken of with a capital F, the way you'd say the Church or the Sea. The Aldouses had built the cannery that built the town, and when the cannery died they'd built other things, quieter things—land trusts, a marina, half the commercial leases on Front Street, and the unspoken understanding that Marrow Bay ran on their gravity the way the tide ran on the moon.

"Roy Calloway was seen with the girl that night," Voss had said. "That's convenient. Use it."

And Pruitt—who had been sheriff for eleven years by then, who had a pension coming, who had a wife whose medical bills came monthly like weather, who had wanted since he was a boy standing on the boardwalk to matter to somebody—he had used it.

That was the sentence he kept circling in the dark hours. Not *I suppressed evidence*, which sounded procedural, administrative, survivable. But *I used it.* He had taken a frightened handyman with no family and no money and no one to ask questions, and he had used him, the way you use a sandbag when the levee leaks. And the levee had held. That was the worst part. It had held for five years. He'd been re-elected twice on the strength of having closed the case, and people shook his hand at the diner, and Mrs. Aldous—Eleanor, silver-haired, kind-eyed, terrifying—had sent flowers when his wife died, and he had stood at the graveside thinking: *this is what mattering costs,* and he had paid it, and he had told himself the price was fair.

His wife had known. That was another thing the dark hours gave him. Ruthie had looked at him once, near the end, when the morphine made her honest the way drink makes other men honest, and she'd said, "Dale, whatever it is you carry, put it down before I go, so I don't have to wonder where you'll drop it after." And he had kissed her forehead and lied to her with his whole face, and she had closed her eyes like a woman filing something away for later, and there had been no later.

* * *

At nine-forty, Cora Lin knocked on his door. Two knocks, crisp, the way she knocked when she was on duty and meant it. She'd been on duty, he realized, for every hour of this. That was the thing about Cora he'd misread from the day she transferred in—young, earnest, a little too straight-backed, he'd pegged her as ambition in a uniform, someone to mold or manage. He'd never considered that she might be the cop he'd stopped being somewhere around year twelve, and that everything he taught her by example was teaching her exactly what not to become.

"Sheriff." She stepped in and closed the door behind her. She was in uniform, report finished, testimony given—she'd taken the stand yesterday, after the girl, and read the relevant paragraphs aloud in a voice that never wavered, and Pruitt had watched the jury watch her, and he had seen belief move through them like current through wire. "Judge Halloway wants your statement by four o'clock. He's giving you the day to consult counsel." A pause. Her jaw worked. "I'm telling you that as a courtesy. Off the record."

"Counsel," Pruitt said. The word felt foreign in his mouth, like a tool from someone else's trade. "Marcus Voss offered me counsel yesterday. Did you know that? Called me at home, ten o'clock at night. Said the firm would represent me personally, pro bono, no conflict of interest whatsoever." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry rattle. "No conflict of interest. Cora, I've spent five years being the conflict of interest."

"I know." She said it simply. No satisfaction in it. He'd expected—what? Gloating? He supposed he'd earned gloating. Instead she pulled out the chair across from his desk and sat down in it, uninvited, the way you sit with a dying man. "Sheriff, I'm going to ask you something, and I need you to answer it like the man who trained me, not the man who wrote that order. Julian Aldous. How much did he know?"

The name landed in the room and stayed there.

Julian Aldous. Edmund's grandson, the heir, thirty-four years old, handsome in the way of men who have never been told no by anything that mattered. Pruitt had seen him at the funeral—Edmund Aldous's funeral, five years ago, three weeks after the girl vanished, the town packed into St. Brendan's while the casket went by and everyone agreed what a peaceful passing it had been, in his sleep, at the estate, with his doctor's letter attesting to the heart condition. Everyone agreed. Agreement was cheap in Marrow Bay. It was practically the municipal currency.

"He knew everything," Pruitt said.

It was the first time he'd said it out loud, and saying it changed the acoustics of his own skull. Thirty years of law enforcement, and he had learned that confessions were physical things—they had weight, temperature, a sound like ice cracking on a pond in early winter, that first report that says the season has turned and nothing will freeze solid again.

"Tell me," Cora said. "All of it. Not for the record—for you. Then we'll do the record."

* * *

So he told her, and the telling took two hours, and the office grew dim around them because neither of them turned on the lamp and neither of them moved, and the fog came up off the bay and pressed against the windows the way it always did in November, softening the town until it looked like a photograph of itself.

He told her about the night of the disappearance—how he'd gotten the call from the estate, not from the girl's family, from the estate, from a housekeeper with a tremor in her voice saying Mr. Aldous wanted the sheriff personally, not a deputy. How he'd driven out through the eucalyptus windbreak and found the household in a state he could only call *arranged*, everyone positioned correctly, everyone grieving in the correct register, and Julian Aldous meeting him on the gravel drive in boat shoes and a cash sweater, calm as a man discussing drainage.

How Julian had said: "There was a girl on the property that night. An employee's daughter. She saw something she shouldn't have. My grandfather was ill, Sheriff, very ill, and what she saw was us easing his suffering, and she panicked and ran, and I'd take it as a personal kindness if the search concentrated south, toward the highway. She'll turn up. Girls like that turn up."

*Girls like that.* Pruitt heard himself repeat it now, in the dim office, and hated the sound of it in his own voice, because he had repeated it then too, to his deputies, to the search teams, steering them south with a map and a lie, while the girl went north, toward the marsh, toward the herons, toward a boathouse on Aldous land where somebody—Voss, he suspected, or one of Voss's quiet men—had stashed her like a document in a safe.

He told Cora about the diary. About how, eight days after the disappearance, a fisherman had found a canvas bag snagged in the pilings under the old trestle, and inside it a journal with *O.M.* embossed on the cover, and how Pruitt had read three pages in his truck with his hands shaking and understood that the girl had written down what she saw, in her own private cipher, dates disguised as book titles, observations dressed as book reviews—and how he had driven not to the evidence locker but to the estate, and how Voss had met him on the same gravel drive and taken the diary out of his hands with a gentleness that was worse than violence.

"You've been very helpful, Sheriff," Voss had said. "The family remembers its friends."

And then the arrest of Roy Calloway, fast and clean, built on a sighting and a grudge and a jury pool that had grown up believing the Calloways were trash, and the conviction, and the sentence, and the flowers from Eleanor Aldous, and the second election, and the third, and Ruthie dying with her question unanswered, and all of it stacking up year over year like silt, until the river of his career ran brown and deep and nothing clear could live in it.

"And the girl?" Cora asked quietly, when he finished. "Did you know she was alive? All this time?"

"No." His voice broke on it, and he let it. "God help me, no. I thought—I told myself the boy had done it and hidden the body well, and I slept at night because I believed my own lie. That's the trick of it, Cora. You don't decide to become a coward. You just keep deciding to sleep." He looked at his hands, flat on the desk, an old man's hands suddenly. "Yesterday, when she walked in—I knew her face before I knew why. I'd studied it for a year. Missing posters, school photos, that little crooked part in her hair. And there she stood, alive, and every lie I ever told myself about sleeping well walked out of me at once."

* * *

At noon, Delgado rested the prosecution's case, and the courtroom emptied for lunch, and Pruitt walked the two blocks to the diner because sitting still had become unbearable. Marta at the counter wouldn't meet his eye. The booth by the window, where he'd eaten breakfast three times a week for fifteen years, sat empty, and nobody invited him into it, and he understood that the town had already tried him, quietly, in the court of the lunch rush, and the verdict had come back faster than any jury's would.

He ordered coffee he didn't drink and watched the fog eat the harbor, and he thought about Roy Calloway in his cell—five years, five years, a man who fixed screen doors and outboard motors and had once, Pruitt remembered now, remembered with a pain like a hook set behind his sternum, had once repaired Pruitt's own porch steps for free because Ruthie had trouble with the risers. *Free,* the man had said. *For the missus.*

Yara Vane found him there. Of course she did. She slid into the booth across from him without asking, gray-streaked hair escaping its clip, a woman who looked like she'd been assembled out of paper and stubbornness, and she set something on the table between them: a photograph. The boathouse. Police tape in the foreground, and beyond it, blurred, a figure being escorted toward a car—Voss's fixer, in cuffs, Cora's hand on his elbow.

"They're processing the scene," she said. "Your crime scene unit. Your warrant, signed by your judge, on your county's evidence." She let that sit. "Whatever happens next, Sheriff, some of it is going to happen in your name. You should decide whether you want it to."

"What do you want from me?" he asked. It came out smaller than he intended.

"The truth. On the record. Before four o'clock." She leaned forward. "I've spent five years watching this town hide things in plain sight, Sheriff. I know how it's done. I also know how it comes undone—it comes undone when one man decides the weight is heavier than the hiding. Today's the day the weight wins or the hiding does. Not tomorrow. Today."

She left the photograph. She left a twenty for the coffee. She was gone before his hands stopped shaking enough to pick either up.

* * *

At three-fifty, Dale Pruitt walked into Judge Halloway's chambers with Cora Lin at his side and his resignation letter in one pocket and his statement in his hand, typed himself, single-spaced, six pages, every word of it true, which made it the most unusual document he had produced in five years.

Halloway read it in silence. The judge was near retirement, a man with a face like a dried riverbed, and Pruitt watched his eyes move down the page and watched the color change in them, the slow flood of a man realizing the map he'd trusted was drawn wrong.

When he finished, Halloway took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose for a long moment.

"Sheriff," he said finally, "do you understand what you've put your name to?"

"Yes, Your Honor."

"You understand that Mr. Voss and Mr. Aldous will be arrested, that your own conduct will be referred to the attorney general, that anything you've touched in five years is now subject to review?"

"Yes, sir." Pruitt's throat was dry as the judge's face. "I'd ask one thing. Not for me. For Calloway. Whatever happens to me—let that jury hear it today. Let them acquit him today. He's waited long enough on my lies. I don't want him waiting one more night on my honesty."

Halloway looked at him for a moment longer, and something in the old judge's expression shifted—not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but recognition, the look one tired man gives another across a shared failure.

"Take the stand yourself, then," Halloway said. "Under oath. This afternoon. If you're going to confess, confess where it counts—in front of the jury that's about to deliberate. Let them weigh your words along with the rest."

* * *

The courtroom at four-fifteen was full in a way Marrow Bay's courtroom had never been full, standing room, people pressed against the back wall, the fog-gray light coming through the tall windows onto the jury box where twelve citizens sat very still. Roy Calloway sat at the defense table between his terrified lawyer and Theo Marchetti, and when the bailiff called the court to order, Roy looked up and found Pruitt across the room, and the two men held each other's eyes for one long second, and Roy Calloway—God help him, Pruitt thought, God help every one of us—nodded. Just once. Like a man granting permission.

Pruitt raised his right hand. The words of the oath came out of him steady, and that surprised him, because underneath them everything was shaking, everything was falling, thirty years of varnish peeling off at once to show the raw wood beneath—and yet his voice held, and he understood, standing there in the well of the court with the whole town watching, that this was the first time in five years his voice had held because it was carrying weight instead of hiding it.

"State your name for the record."

"Dale Pruitt. Sheriff of Marrow Bay County." He gripped the rail. Somewhere in the gallery, he heard Yara Vane exhale. "And I'm here to tell this court that everything the prosecution has said for two days is a lie—and that I'm the one who built it."

The room made that sound again, the building-inhaling sound, and outside the tall windows the fog was lifting off the harbor for the first time in a week, and Dale Pruitt opened his mouth to begin.
