## 37. The Verdict

They let me wash my face before the verdict came down.

A deputy I didn't know—young, freckled, hands that shook a little when she unlocked the holding cell—stood me at the sink in the corner of the room and turned the tap on for me, like I was a child or a man with no hands left. Maybe both. Five years of county soap had taught me that the water here came out rust-warm and smelled of iron, like the pipes had been drinking blood for decades. But this morning the water ran cold and clean, and the deputy—Cora, her name was, Cora Lin, the one everyone said had gone strange and quiet since the ambush at the marsh—handed me a bar of real soap, the kind with oatmeal in it, the kind you'd buy at the pharmacy on Delacroix Street for a dollar ninety-five.

"Where'd this come from?" I asked.

"Yara Vane," she said. "She sent a bag. There's a shirt in there too. Blue. She guessed your collar size."

I stood there holding the soap like it might detonate. Five years in this county, and the only people who'd sent me anything were my sister in Shreveport, twice, and once a church group whose card said *God sees the truth but waits*, which I'd taken as a threat at the time.

"You guessed my collar size," I said.

Cora almost smiled. It didn't survive the trip to her mouth, but it got as far as her eyes. "She said handyman, so she measured off a jacket you wore to your first trial. She still had the case photos."

The case photos. Somewhere in that woman's bookshop there was a file with my face in it, my life flattened into eight-by-ten glossies, and she'd gone digging through it not to bury me but to find the measurement of my neck. I turned to the little mirror above the sink and looked at what five years had made of me. Gray had gotten into my beard the way silt gets into a harbor—gradually, completely, until you couldn't remember the water being any other color. My eyes were the same, though. That was the one thing they hadn't managed to take. I checked them the way you check a tool you're about to need.

"You want to know something, Deputy?" I said. "I stopped picturing this morning about two years ago. The acquittal. I stopped letting myself see it, because every time I did, I'd wake up and the ceiling would be the same ceiling, and it was like drowning twice."

Cora was quiet a moment. Then she said, "Ms. Vane said you'd say something like that. She said, *tell him to picture it now. All of it. The doors opening.*"

I washed my face with the oatmeal soap. I put on the blue shirt. It fit like she'd sewn it on me herself, and I stood in the holding cell with the door unlocked and I pictured it: the doors opening.

* * *

The courtroom had filled up while they held me in the back room, and when the bailiff walked me down the aisle I made the mistake of looking, the way a drowning man looks at the surface even when he's told not to.

The gallery was full. Not full the way it had been at my first trial—full then the way a spectacle is full, faces leaned forward hungry for the monster, mothers holding their daughters' hands a little tighter when I passed. Full now the way a church is full. Marge from the library sat in the third row with her reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair and a hardcover in her lap she wasn't reading, just holding, the way you'd hold a rail. The diner man—Rudy, big forearms, the one who'd left food on Yara's doorstep through all the ugliness—had put on a jacket with a tie for the first time in the history of Marrow Bay, and the tie was crooked, and he kept tugging at it like it was choking him. Teenagers I knew by their orders at the counter, not their names, sat in the back with their phones put away. Phones put away, in that back row. I noticed that. I noticed everything this morning. Five years of noticing compressed into one hour.

Theo Marchetti sat beside Yara Vane.

That was the one that got me. The brother of the girl they said I took, sitting shoulder to shoulder with the only person who'd ever believed me, and when I came down the aisle Theo stood up. He stood up in a courtroom where I was still, technically, the man on trial for his sister's murder, and he stood there until I'd passed, and he didn't sit until I sat, and I had to look at my hands on the defense table because if I looked at him I was going to come apart in front of the jury and they'd have their monster after all, weeping and unglued.

My lawyer—Petrosian, the court-appointed one, the one who was scared of the Aldous name right up until the Aldous name got dragged out of its grave and shot—squeezed my shoulder once. "Whatever happens," he murmured, "sit still. You've gotten good at sitting still. Use it."

* * *

The jury was out an hour and fifty-one minutes. I know because I counted. I counted the way I'd counted every minute of five years—ceiling tiles, water drips, the number of steps from my cell to the yard (forty-one, forty-one every day, the one honest arithmetic left in my life)—and when the knock finally came at the jury room door, my whole body did a thing I couldn't control, a shudder like a horse shaking off flies.

The foreman was a woman I didn't know. Middle-aged, reading glasses on a chain, the kind of face that had probably sat on a hundred juries and hated every minute. She unfolded the paper, and Judge Okafor said, "Madam Foreman, has the jury reached a verdict?"

"We have, Your Honor. On the charge of murder in the first degree, in the case of the State versus Roy Calloway—" and here she stopped, and looked down at the paper, and I watched her jaw do something, tighten, like she was biting down on the words to keep them steady— "we find the defendant not guilty."

The room made a sound I had never heard a room make. It wasn't cheering. It was something older than cheering—a low, collective exhale, like a whole building letting go of a breath it had held for five years, and underneath it a woman crying, and I found out later it was Marge, and underneath *that* the creak of Rudy's folding chair as he half-rose and caught himself.

"Not guilty," the foreman said again, because the judge asked her to confirm it, and the second time it sounded like a bell.

I sat still. Petrosian had told me to sit still and by God I sat still, while the judge thanked the jury and the bailiff moved and the gavel came down, and it was only when Judge Okafor said the words—*the defendant is discharged*—that I understood my legs had forgotten how to work. Five years of standing when they told me to stand and sitting when they told me to sit, and now nobody was telling me anything, and the doors at the back of the courtroom were just doors, and I could not make my body believe them.

Yara Vane did. She came down the aisle—this small, sharp-faced woman who'd spent five years hiding in a bookshop the way I'd spent five years hiding in a cell, two people hiding in the same small town without ever once hiding in the same place—and she stood at the rail in front of me and said, in a voice pitched for me alone:

"The doors, Roy. Picture it now."

I stood up. I walked through the doors.

* * *

What happened on the courthouse steps was the part I'll keep until I die.

Because the law wasn't finished with Marrow Bay that day—it was only getting started. We were barely out onto the stone steps, into the cold bright noon, the gulls screaming over the harbor like they'd been waiting too, when the black cars came up Front Street. Not sheriff's cars. State police, two of them, and behind them an unmarked sedan with a woman in a gray coat driving, and they came up the steps two at a time and into the courthouse, past us, past everybody, and a deputy I'd never seen before stood at the door and said, in a voice that carried down the whole staircase:

"Aldous. And Voss. State warrant."

Voss came out between two troopers maybe four minutes later. I want to tell you I felt triumph. I want to tell you there was some hot clean joy in seeing that gray, careful man with his careful hands cuffed in front of him on the steps of the courthouse where he'd built his whole kingdom of paper. What I actually felt was colder and stranger: I felt *young*. I felt twenty-five years old, standing on those steps, watching the man who'd bought my silence before I ever knew I had anything to sell, and I understood that the thing they were arresting him for—the staged death, the buried will, the girl in the boathouse—was only the newest layer of it. Underneath was the retainer who'd hidden Odile and grown afraid. Underneath that was a handyman who drove a scared kid to the bus station one night for money he never should have taken, and a family that decided, in its infinite paper patience, that a guilty handyman was cheaper than a curious town.

Voss looked at me as they walked him past. He looked at me the way you look at weather. And then his eyes went to the blue shirt, and something moved behind his face—recognition, maybe, that Yara had sent it, that the shirt had been chosen for me by the one person in Marrow Bay he'd failed to buy, frighten, or file away—and he looked away first.

The heir came out after, the young Aldous, and he did not look at anyone. He wept into his collar, quietly, the way rich men weep, like it was a service they'd arranged.

The crowd on the steps didn't cheer for that either. They did something better. They went quiet, all of them, the whole town that had come to watch, and they watched in silence, and the silence was the loudest thing I ever heard. It said: *we saw. We see. It ends here in the daylight where you buried it in the dark.*

* * *

Rudy from the diner found me at the bottom of the steps. He was still tugging at the tie.

"Roy Calloway," he said, and stuck out his hand.

Five years. Five years of *Calloway* spat like a pit, *that Calloway*, *him*, the name said in the lowered voice people use for cancer and drownings. And now this enormous man in a crooked tie stood in front of God and the state police and said my whole name, both parts, the way you say a name you're glad to have a use for again.

"Rudy," I said, and shook his hand, and his grip was warm and huge and careful, the way big men's grips are when they know what they're holding.

"Best gumbo on the coast," he said, gruff, embarrassed, looking past me at the harbor. "You come in tonight, it's on the house. You come in ever, it's on the house. That's—that's all I got to say." His voice cracked on the last of it and he turned away fast, and I watched him go, and I understood that the gumbo was not the point. The gumbo was the door. The gumbo was *you can come in now*.

Marge came next. She pressed the hardcover from her lap into my hands—*The Count of Monte Cristo*, of all things, the library's old rebound copy, the spine cracked white with use. "It's due back in three weeks," she said, stern, wet-eyed, "and I expect you to renew it at least twice, because that's how long it takes to read properly, and you've got time now. You've got nothing but time."

Theo Marchetti shook my hand and couldn't say anything at all, and didn't need to. His sister was alive. His sister had come home. Whatever the town had taken from him in my name, it was giving pieces of it back, and he held my hand a second longer than a handshake needs and let go.

And then the crowd did the thing I will not be able to describe to my sister on the phone tonight, the thing I'll fail at, because it didn't have words. They didn't part for me. A crowd parts for a man it's afraid of. This crowd *closed*, gently, the way water closes over a stone, and I was inside it, and hands found my shoulders—Rudy's people, church people, kids, the freckled deputy Cora standing at the edge with her hat in her hands like a woman at a funeral finally allowed to grieve—and somebody said, "There he is, there's Roy," and somebody else said, "Mr. Calloway, sir," and a teenage girl I vaguely knew from the diner asked me, shy as anything, if it was true I'd built the shelves in the old hardware store, because her mom said nobody could build shelves like me, and I said it was true, and she said, "Cool," and walked off, and I stood there on the courthouse steps of Marrow Bay with the gulls screaming and the sun coming through the cold like a blessing, and I was nobody's monster. I was the man who built the shelves.

* * *

They let me walk home. Nobody escorted me; there was nobody left to escort me anywhere. I walked the long way, down Delacroix, past the pharmacy where the oatmeal soap cost a dollar ninety-five, past the hardware store with my shelves still standing in the window, straighter than anything else on that street.

At the corner of Harbor Road I stopped, because the bookshop was there, and the lights were on, and through the glass I could see Yara Vane behind the counter, and Cora beside her, and Marge arriving with a box of files, and a tall girl in the doorway with sea-wind in her hair—Odile, the ghost herself, alive, holding a broom like she was learning what her hands were for—and they were laughing at something. Laughing. In the window of that sad little shop, in the town that had failed all of us in its own particular ways, they were laughing.

I stood in the cold a long time, holding a library book against my chest.

Then the door opened. Yara Vane looked out at me, and she didn't smile—she wasn't a woman who spent smiles—but she stepped back and held the door wide, and the warmth of the place came out into the street like a hand.

"Roy," she said. "We were just talking about shelves."
