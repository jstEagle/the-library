## 2. The Registry That Remembers

The Grand Verity kept its dead the way it kept everything else: in the basement, alphabetized, under fluorescent light that hummed like a wasp in a jar.

Jonah Montrose had spent twenty-two years learning the hotel's memory. The registry archive occupied a windowless room off sublevel two, past the wine cellar and the room where the Christmas decorations went to die, and it smelled of dust and old glue and the faint mineral tang of the building's bones — limestone sweating through concrete, the way old hotels do when they are thinking. The ledgers lived on steel shelving in green cloth-bound volumes, one per year, their spines stamped in gold leaf gone dull as tarnished coins.

They had come down at six in the morning, before the day shift arrived, before Marta's replacement — a pleasant, forgettable young man named Dev who checked his phone too often — could ask questions Jonah wasn't ready to answer. They carried the confession letter in their breast pocket, as they had every day for a week now, folded along its original creases until the paper had gone soft as cloth.

*The proof is in plain sight,* Vane had written. *You of all people will see it. You were raised to.*

Jonah pulled the 1994 ledger from its shelf and felt the weight of it settle into their hands — three pounds of paper and secrets, heavier than any object had a right to be.

* * *

They started with the obvious and worked toward the impossible, which was how Jonah did everything.

First they read the year the way anyone would read it: arrivals, departures, the small notations of a hotel's daily life. *Room 212, Mrs. Abernathy, extended stay, requests extra blankets.* *Room 118, wedding party, do not disturb before noon.* The handwriting changed hands through the seasons — different clerks, different pressures, the same patient record-keeping that had been the Verity's spine since 1911.

Then Jonah began again, and this time they read it the way Vane might have wanted them to.

They copied every registration into a notebook. Date, name, room number. It took four hours. Their tea went cold twice; they didn't notice either time. And when the transcription was done, they sat back on the little stool by the shelf and looked at what they'd made, and the humming fluorescent light seemed suddenly very loud.

Room numbers repeated. Not randomly — Jonah had spent two decades reading patterns in human behavior, in the lies guests told at the front desk, in the way a man's eyes moved when he said he was alone. Randomness had a texture, and this wasn't it.

Room 407 appeared eleven times across the year. Eleven registrations, eleven different names, and here was the thing that made the hair rise along Jonah's forearms: each registration fell exactly nine days after a departure from the same room. A guest checks out of 407 on the third of March; nine days later, a new guest registers into 407 under a name that shares no letters, no rhythm, no country of origin with the last. Check out, nine days, check in. Again. Again. Eleven times, metronome-steady, through a spring and summer and into the autumn of 1994.

And the dates themselves — Jonah wrote them in a column and stared until the column confessed. The eleventh of March. The second of June. The nineteenth of September. Every ninth-day arrival fell on a date whose digits summed to a multiple of three. Every single one. Thirty years ago, some clerk — or some owner — had been using the registry itself as a calendar, a signal, a ledger within the ledger.

*In plain sight.* Of course. Where else would Aldous Vane hide anything? The man had built a hotel on the principle that no one looks at what is always visible. Guests walked past the front desk ten thousand times and never once read it.

Jonah turned to the final entry in the pattern. October 4th, 1994. Room 407. And the name written there in a hand Jonah knew better than their own —

They closed the book.

For a long moment they simply sat in the humming dark, listening to the building breathe above them. Somewhere overhead, a vacuum cleaner droned across the mezzanine carpet. A pipe knocked twice in the wall, the Verity's old arthritic complaint. The hotel was awake, and it was watching them read its diary.

The name on the October 4th line was *M. Okafor.*

Marta Okafor. The woman who had taught Jonah to tell time by the freight elevator's rhythm. Who had slipped them warm milk and cardamom during night shifts when they were nine years old and couldn't sleep in the staff quarters. Who had signed, in her careful schoolteacher's cursive, every birthday card Jonah had ever received, because there had never been anyone else to sign them.

It proved nothing, Jonah told themselves. Marta had audited the night books for forty years; her handwriting was in every volume in this room the way watermarks are in paper. A name in a registry meant a guest registered, nothing more.

But the letter had been addressed to Jonah. And Vane had known — had *counted on* — that Jonah would follow the thread all the way down to the people who raised them.

That was the cruelest engineering Jonah had ever encountered: a cipher designed so that solving it would hurt.

* * *

They found Marta at dusk, in the auditor's office behind the front desk, a closet of a room she had refused for thirty years to let anyone modernize. The desk lamp threw amber light across her silver braids. She wore her reading glasses on a beaded chain Jonah remembered stringing for her when they were eleven, the beads clicking softly whenever she bent over the night books.

"Marta." Jonah set the 1994 ledger on her desk, open to October. "I need your eyes."

She looked up over the glasses, and her face did what it always did when Jonah appeared — warmed first, question second. "You've got your case face on. I haven't seen that face since the Kessler business."

"This isn't a case. Not yet."

"Everything is a case with you. You came out of the womb taking statements." She reached for the ledger, and Jonah watched her hands — seventy-eight years old, spotted, steady as a surgeon's — smooth the page flat.

"Tell me about the ninth-day registrations," Jonah said. "Room 407. Nineteen ninety-four."

The beads clicked. Marta read. And Jonah, who had made a career of watching faces, watched hers go through something they had no word for. The warmth drained not quickly but thoroughly, like color leaving a photograph left in the sun. Her thumb stopped on the page. Pressed there. Held still against the paper as if the ink might move.

"Where did you get this?" Her voice was level. Too level. Jonah knew that voice; they'd used it themselves a hundred times, on guests who were lying about things that mattered.

"It came to me. In a manner of speaking." They kept their own voice gentle. "Marta. Whose name is this?"

She didn't answer. She was looking at the entry the way one looks at a scar — knowing already how it got there, unable to stop looking.

"You registered a guest into 407 on October fourth," Jonah said quietly. "Eleven times that year, someone did. Nine days after every checkout. The dates are coded — multiples of three. This isn't hospitality, Marta. This is a message system running through my hotel's registry, and your handwriting is on the last line of it."

"I know whose handwriting it is." She said it very softly. She took off her glasses and set them down with exaggerated care, the way she did when she needed her hands to be busy so her voice could be steady. "I wrote that name because I was told to write it. I was twenty-nine years old, Jonah. I was new, and I was scared, and Mr. Vane stood right where you're standing and told me what to put in the book."

"Told you? Vane dictated registrations?"

"He dictated that one." Her eyes came up, and they were wet, but her gaze didn't waver, and Jonah understood suddenly that whatever was coming had been waiting thirty years to be said aloud. "There was a guest in 407 that autumn. He checked out on the twenty-fifth of September, and he never—" She stopped. Started again. "He never actually checked out. Not really. But the book says he did, because I made the book say it."

The lamp hummed. Down the corridor, the freight elevator began its slow climb, counting floors the way it always had, and Jonah thought absurdly of being seven years old, falling asleep to that sound, believing the whole building was singing to them.

"The police came asking about him," Marta said. "In October. And Vane handed them the registry, open, and there was my entry — checkout confirmed, key returned, no forwarding address. Clean as a whistle. And they believed it, because why wouldn't they? The book doesn't lie." Her mouth twisted. "I learned that month that a book lies exactly as well as the person holding the pen."

"What was the guest's name?"

Marta looked down at the ledger. Her finger moved to the line above her own — the September checkout, the false exit she'd recorded — and Jonah leaned in to read the faded ink.

The name there was one Jonah had never heard spoken in thirty years inside these walls. And yet the moment they saw it, they understood the shape of everything to come, because beneath the name, in the margin, in pencil so faint it was nearly a ghost, someone had written a single word:

*witness.*

"Marta," Jonah said. "Who erased this man?"

But the old woman had gone pale as the ledger paper itself, and when she finally spoke, her voice was barely above the hum of the lamp.

"Not who. *Whom.* Because whoever it was, child — they're still in this building. Some of them are still on the payroll." She closed the book with both hands, gently, the way you close a coffin. "And I think your letter-writer knew that. I think that's why it came to *you*."
