## 1. The Will That Shouldn't Exist

The file arrived at 8:47 on a Tuesday morning, which was thirteen minutes before Casper Nystrom considered the workday officially open, and she resented it for that reason before she even knew what it contained.

It came in a manila folder with no return address, dropped through the mail slot rather than handed to Dev at the front desk, which meant it had been posted late the previous afternoon or early this one. The postage was correct to the cent. Someone had weighed it. That detail would matter later — everything mattered later, that was the trouble with this case, every trivial thing turned out to be load-bearing — but at 8:47 in the morning it was just a manila folder sitting on top of her inbox like something washed up on a beach.

Casper hung her coat, set her bag in its drawer, aligned her three pens parallel to the edge of the blotter, and only then picked up the folder.

The cover sheet was a probate intake form, filled out in block capitals by someone who understood forms. *Estate of Lena M. Voss. Deceased.* Below that, in the space marked DATE OF DEATH: *Presumed — see declaration of death in fact, filed [date], five years prior.* The bracketed date had been left blank, as if the preparer couldn't be bothered, or as if the date itself were negotiable.

"Dev," she called.

Her assistant appeared in the doorway holding a coffee he hadn't offered her one of, which was his way of keeping score about something. He was twenty-six and wore cardigans unironically and had once told her, during his interview, that he liked estate law because "everything is already over." She'd hired him on the spot.

"Voss," she said. "Lena Voss. Pull the file."

"We have a Voss file?"

"That's what I'm asking you."

He came around the desk, coffee in hand, and typed into the shared index while she watched. The firm's filing system was hers — built over eleven years, alphabetical within category, cross-referenced twice, backed up weekly. It had never failed her. It did not fail her now, exactly. What it did was worse.

"Here," Dev said slowly. "Voss, Lena M. Estate opened... five years ago. Declared dead in absentia. Judge Halloway. Closed — no assets located, no will presented. Case number's right here." He looked up. "So why are we getting mail about it?"

"That," said Casper, "is an excellent question," and she took the folder into her office and closed the door, because some questions you want privacy for.

* * *

She read the declaration first. It was standard: petition by next of kin — a brother, Anders Voss — supported by affidavits from the sheriff's department regarding the recovery of a vehicle from the Harrow Reservoir, and from two private citizens regarding the decedent's state of mind preceding her disappearance. The vehicle had been found eleven days after Lena Voss was last seen. The reservoir had been dragged twice. No body. The court had waited the statutory period, then done what courts do with absence: converted it into certainty, stamped it, filed it under V.

Five years ago. Casper remembered it dimly — she'd been living in the city then, still at Merrick & Hale, still telling herself she'd never come back to Harrow Falls. Her father had died the following spring. The timing blurred together now, grief and geography smudged into one gray season. She didn't remember the name Lena Voss from the news. She wasn't sure whether that meant anything or not. Small towns lose people quietly; the losing is the town's whole art form.

Then she opened the second document in the folder, and the morning changed shape.

It was a will. A last will and testament of Lena Marit Voss, executed — according to its own attestation clause — four months ago. Two witnesses, signatures legible: Hal Brandt and Ruth Calloway. And beneath the witness signatures, the notary acknowledgment, stamped and embossed, the raised seal catching the light when she tilted the page:

**G. ELLERY, NOTARY PUBLIC, HARROW FALLS COUNTY. COMMISSION EXPIRES [date].**

Casper sat very still.

Gordon Ellery. She knew the stamp because she'd seen it on perhaps four hundred documents over eleven years — deeds, powers of attorney, the occasional affidavit, all of them passing across Ellery's desk at the bank until he retired, and then, last winter, she'd seen it on exactly one more document: the certificate of death itself, or rather she'd seen the obituary, because Gordon Ellery had died in January. Heart, in his sleep, sixty-eight years old. She had sent a card. She always sent cards; it was the kind of thing her father had drilled into her, the civic liturgy of a small town, though her father had stopped practicing most of the rest of it long before he died.

A notarized document bearing the seal of a dead man was not impossible. Seals got stolen; stamps got lost in drawers and found by hands that shouldn't find them. But it was rare, and it was serious, and above all it was *not clerical*. Clerical errors were transpositions, missed dates, a name spelled wrong. Clerical errors did not emboss.

She put on her glasses, which she did not need for reading and needed entirely for thinking, and went through the will line by line.

It was short. Suspiciously short — no, she corrected herself, *efficiently* short. Lena Voss had apparently owned little: the house on Corliss Road, sold years ago in the settlement of her own declared estate (which raised a question Casper flagged in the margin with a small, precise tick); a savings account; a car that was at the bottom of a reservoir. The residue of the estate — such as it was — was left to "my brother, Anders Voss, if he asks for it honestly, and otherwise to the Harrow Falls public library, earmarked for the local history collection."

Casper read that sentence three times.

*If he asks for it honestly.*

You could not condition a gift on honesty. Honesty was not an event; it had no date certain; no executor could certify it without turning probate into theology. Any competent drafter would strike it. Which meant either the drafter was incompetent — possible, but the rest of the instrument was clean, almost surgically clean — or the drafter wanted that sentence in there. Wanted it read. Wanted someone, someday, to sit where Casper was sitting and stop on that line like a nail in a tire.

There was a codicil too. One paragraph, appended, same execution date:

*I am aware of what my death certificate says. I am aware of what it does not say. If this will reaches probate, then whatever I intended has failed, and whoever is reading this should look at the dates before they look at anything else.*

Look at the dates.

Casper looked at the dates. Execution date: four months ago. Notarization date: the same day. Commission expiration printed on the stamp itself: valid — had been valid, on paper, for another year beyond that, except that commissions do not survive the commissioner, and Gordon Ellery had been in the ground since January, and the ground was the one place his stamp could not legally follow him.

Four months ago. Ellery had been dead for nearly two months by then.

Unless the date was wrong. Unless the will had actually been executed earlier and backdated — but why would anyone backdate a will *toward* a notary's death? Backdating bought you nothing there. Forward-dating made sense if you wanted the will to seem fresh, current, recently executed by a woman very much alive—

Casper set down her pen.

That was the thought she'd been circling without landing on, the way you circle a pothole in the dark. A will is a document of death; it speaks at death. This will spoke in the present tense. *I am aware.* Present tense, four months ago, from a woman the state had certified dead for four and a half years by then.

Either someone was forging documents in the name of a dead woman — which happened, occasionally, usually involving insurance or spite — or Lena Voss had walked into a room four months ago, signed her name in front of two witnesses, and watched a dead man's stamp come down on the page.

And one of those two witnesses was Hal Brandt, whom Casper knew slightly, the way you know everyone slightly in a town of nine thousand — retired deputy, fished the reservoir, fixed other people's outboard motors at his own dock because he couldn't stand watching them do it wrong. And the other was Ruth Calloway, whom everyone knew, because Ruth Calloway was the mayor.

Casper wrote both names on a legal pad, drew a line between them, and looked at the line for a long time.

* * *

At ten o'clock she did what she always did with an anomaly: she checked it against the system. Not the firm's index — against herself. Eleven years of files, five of them here in Harrow Falls, each one a small sealed room of somebody's ending. She pulled the last five years of closed estates and read the closing sheets the way other people read tea leaves, looking for the pattern she half-hoped wasn't there.

She found three things, and she hated how neatly they arranged themselves.

An estate two years ago — the Sorensen farm — where a deed of transfer bore a signature dated six days after the signatory's hospitalization record showed him non compos mentis. She'd caught it, then. She'd flagged it, the title company had re-papered, everyone had agreed it was an error, a helpful son signing ahead of himself. Filed under error.

An estate last year — old Mrs. Tannhauser — where the inventory listed a safe-deposit box at the First Harrow Bank whose rental records showed it had been accessed, once, eleven months after her death. By whom? The bank couldn't say. Dual-key boxes, the second key lost. Everyone had agreed it was a records mix-up. Filed under error.

And an estate eighteen months ago — the Pell family, one of the founding names, one of the four names on the bronze plaque outside the library — where the personal representative had distributed assets before the creditor-claim period closed, and nobody, including the court, had seemed to mind. She'd noted it. She'd let it go. Filed under error.

Three errors in five years. In her practice, which ran clean, that was already two too many. But it was the overlap that bothered her, the thing she couldn't quite see yet, the way you can't see the shape of a stain when your nose is against it: the Sorensen deed had been witnessed by someone named Ellery — not Gordon, a nephew, but still. The Tannhauser box had been at the bank where Gordon Ellery had notarized for thirty years. And the Pells — the Pells were Calloways by marriage, two generations back, which in Harrow Falls meant they were Calloways, full stop.

Casper wrote the connections down and then, after a moment, drew a small bracket around the whole page, the way her father used to bracket things in his own files — a habit she'd absorbed without ever deciding to, the way you absorb a parent's handwriting into your own.

She did not know yet that her father had kept files. That would come later, in a storage unit that smelled of cold concrete, behind a lock she'd have to break. At 10:40 on that Tuesday morning, Edwin Nystrom was simply her dead father, a county attorney for thirty-one years, a man who had grown quiet somewhere in the middle of his life and never explained why, and the bracket on her legal pad was just a bracket.

* * *

Dev knocked at eleven. He had the old Voss file open in his arms and an expression Casper had learned to read as *I found something and I'm deciding whether to say it.*

"Say it," she said.

"The original estate. Five years ago." He laid it on her desk. "Opened on the brother's petition, closed eight months later, no will, no assets worth administering. Standard declaration-in-absentia package. Except—" He tapped a page near the back. "The notice of hearing. Published in the *Harrow Courier*, run three weeks as required. Look at the third run."

She looked. The legal notice, dense and gray, the town's oldest genre. Third insertion, dated as required. Nothing wrong with it visually.

"I'm not seeing it," she said.

"Neither did I, the first four times." Dev had a way of being pleased by his own thoroughness that would have been insufferable in anyone less useful. "The typeface on the third run is different. Half a point off, maybe. Somebody reset that one insertion. And the address given for the estate's attorney of record—" he slid a photocopy across, "—is a suite number at the First Harrow Bank building that has been vacant since before the estate opened."

Casper studied it. A ghost office. A notice published into a room where no one sat, in type that didn't match, in a newspaper that would print whatever the legal department submitted because legal notices were revenue, not journalism.

"So the hearing notice may never have properly reached anyone," she said.

"Or reached everyone and pointed nowhere. Either way, nobody objected, because nobody was looking." Dev hesitated. "Boss, can I ask what this is? Because a five-year-old estate doesn't generate new mail unless—"

"Unless the dead person isn't finished," Casper said.

She heard it leave her mouth and wished she'd phrased it better. Dev blinked at her. Outside the window, Harrow Falls went about its Tuesday — the bakery van, the flag outside the courthouse hanging straight down in windless air, a woman crossing the square with a casserole dish covered in foil, some small kindness en route to some small table. A town that had gotten very good, over a very long time, at not looking.

"I want the original will authenticated," Casper said, pulling a fresh sheet. "Handwriting expert, ink dating, the works. Quietly. And I want to know who handled the mail this morning — did anyone else touch that envelope?"

"It came through the slot. I haven't touched it since I brought it up."

"Wear gloves if you do. And Dev—" he was already at the door; she made herself say the next part evenly, "—this stays between us for now. All of it. If anyone asks what we're working on, it's a routine reopening, records cleanup. Understood?"

He gave her a look — the look, the one that said *you're doing the thing where you decide alone and call it prudence* — and said, "Understood," in a tone that filed her answer under error.

When he'd gone, Casper sat with the will again, reading the codicil one final time. *Look at the dates before they look at anything else.*

She had looked at the dates. Execution: four months ago. Notarization: four months ago, by a man two months dead. Declaration of death: five years ago. Disappearance: five years and eleven days ago, give or take, the car in the reservoir, the divers coming up empty-handed twice, the town closing over the absence like water.

And one date more, the one the codicil hadn't mentioned because it didn't have to — the one she'd have to confirm in the morning, from the statutes, with a calculator, like the careful woman she was. The limitation period on whatever had happened out at that mill, or that road, or wherever it had happened, thirty years ago in the town's deep past. Thirty years was a wall. Walls had doors in them, and doors had dates.

Casper uncapped her pen, wrote *Lena M. Voss* at the top of a fresh file — a new file, because the old one was closed, and whatever this was, it was not the old case — and then paused, pen hovering, aware of the small absurdity of it.

Somewhere out there, if the document in front of her was what it appeared to be, a woman the state had buried on paper was walking around with a pulse and a grudge and a lawyer's name — Casper's name, presumably, since the will had been mailed to *her*, addressed to *her*, delivered to *her* door like a summons.

She wrote the date at the top of the file. Then, beneath it, because the codicil had asked and because she was constitutionally incapable of leaving a direct request unanswered, she wrote a single word:

*Received.*

Outside, the church bell rang noon, flat and familiar, marking the hour the way it had marked every hour of her childhood — faithfully, unquestioningly, for a town that had never once asked it what it was counting down to.
