## 3. Entries After the End

The diary arrived on a Tuesday, in a manila envelope that had been through three hands and two buildings before it reached mine.

Dev set it on my desk the way he'd set down something dead — carefully, at arm's length, as if it might still be warm. "Clerk's office sent it over. Judge Wrenn ordered it released into evidence yesterday. It was logged into the original disappearance file back in —" he checked his note — "five years ago. Never examined."

"Never examined," I repeated.

"Filed and forgotten. Apparently it came in with her personal effects from the car." He hesitated. "There's a chain-of-custody form stapled to it. One signature. The deputy who logged it."

"Hal Brandt?"

Dev nodded once and left me alone with it.

I want to be precise about what I did next, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. I did not open it immediately. I washed my hands — a habit, not a ritual, though later I would come to think of it as both. I put on cotton gloves from the supply drawer, the ones we use for fragile original documents, and I cleared my desk of everything except a legal pad and a pen. Then I sat for perhaps a full minute looking at the object itself.

It was a small book, cloth-bound, navy blue gone gray at the corners, the kind sold in drugstores for four dollars. A rubber band held it shut, perished now, flaking. On the cover, in silver marker faded to a ghost: *L.V.*

I broke the band. It crumbled.

The first thing I checked — before reading a single word — was the dates.

This is what twenty years of probate work does to a person. Before grief, before story, before meaning, I check dates. A will dated after death is an error until proven otherwise; a signature witnessed by a corpse is a clerical mistake until someone makes it a crime. Dates are the skeleton key of my profession. Everything else is flesh, and flesh lies.

The entries began in March of the year Lena Voss disappeared, which was unremarkable. What was remarkable — what made me set down my pen so it wouldn't leave a mark on anything — was that they continued.

They continued past the date her car was pulled from the reservoir. Past the date the coroner signed the presumptive death certificate based on blood evidence and a wallet found in the glovebox. Past the memorial service, which Anders Voss had paid to have catered, according to the file. They continued for eleven weeks after the day the world had agreed Lena Voss stopped existing.

* * *

I read the whole book twice that afternoon, and then a third time the next morning, because the first two readings I did as a lawyer and the third I did as something else, something I didn't have a name for yet.

Here is what the lawyer saw:

The handwriting was consistent throughout. I am not a forensic document examiner, but I had handled enough of Lena Voss's paper — she'd executed two prior wills with our firm, updated her trust, signed a dozen affidavits over the years she was alive and practicing veterinary medicine in Harrow Creek — to know her hand the way you know a voice on the phone. The forward slant. The way she crossed her t's low, like she was pressing them down into the line. The distinctive open-bowled *e* that looked slightly startled. The final third of the book matched the first two-thirds. If this was forgery, it was forgery by someone who had studied her writing for years, or by someone who could write for hours without fatigue or drift.

The ink changed. That was the other thing the lawyer saw. The early entries were ballpoint, blue. Somewhere around the fourth week after the disappearance, the pen became felt-tip, black, then later a gel pen in a color the manufacturer calls midnight but everyone else calls purple. People change pens. Displaced people change pens more often. But the changes were gradual, natural, spaced across pages the way real time spaces them — not the abrupt wholesale shift you see when a document is manufactured in one sitting.

And the content. The lawyer noted that the content contained details no forger could plausibly have invented in advance: the exact price of a room at a motel outside the county seat, the name of a waitress, a reference to a news story about a bridge closure that I later verified ran on precisely the date the entry mentioned. The diary knew things that only the road ahead could have taught it.

Here is what the something-else saw:

*March 14.* — *Dad called again tonight. He does this thing where he talks to me like I'm twelve. Like the mill is still ours, like any of it is ours. I told him I'm not coming to the dinner. He said Ruth will be there. I said I don't care if the governor is there. He hung up on me. Thirty years old and hung up on like a teenager.*

*March 22.* — *Saw Dr. Okafor about the headaches. She wants an MRI. I told her I can't afford it right now. She gave me samples. Everyone in this town gives you samples instead of help.*

*April 2.* — *Something happened at the mill site tonight. I'm not writing it down. Not here.*

That entry was three lines long and the pressure of the pen had torn the paper in two places. I sat with it for a long time. *Not here,* she wrote. Which means: somewhere else. Which means there may be another record, or none, or one that exists only in the body of a woman the state insists is dead.

Then the entries after the end.

*May 9.* — *New town. I'm not going to say where. The room has a window unit that sounds like a dying animal and I love it. Nobody here knows my face. I keep waiting to feel guilty and instead I feel like I can breathe for the first time since April. Is that terrible? Don't answer.*

*May 17.* — *Bought a wig today. Blonde. I look like someone's aunt from Florida. Anders would laugh himself sick. God, Anders. I miss him and I hate him and mostly I miss him. He wouldn't look at me the last week. Like I was already a photograph.*

*June 3.* — *The blood thing worked. I read about it online — how they declared me. Presumed. There's something obscene about being presumed. I'm right here. I'm eating gas station sushi and being presumed dead.*

*June 21.* — *A man asked too many questions at the laundromat. Friendly. Too friendly. Left before I could decide if I imagined it. Moving again this weekend.*

*July 8.* — *Third town this summer. I've gotten good at this. That's the part that frightens me — not that I had to run, but how quickly running started to feel like competence.*

*July 30.* — *Dreamed about Dad. In the dream he was apologizing and I woke up furious because even asleep I know he never will.*

*August 19.* — *Thinking about coming back. Not yet. But soon. When it's safe. When they've all gotten comfortable thinking I'm bones at the bottom of the reservoir. Let them get comfortable. Comfortable people talk.*

*September 4.* — *Heard from H. H says everything's quiet. H says wait. H always says wait. I've been waiting my whole life inside this family. What's five more years.*

H. I underlined it on my legal pad, then drew a box around it, then stared at it until the letters stopped being letters. Hal? Hal Brandt, the deputy who logged this diary into evidence and never looked inside it? Or someone else entirely — a name I didn't have yet?

*September 26.* — *Decision made. I'm going home. Not to stay. To watch. You can't fight a war from a motel room. I'll be close. I'll be patient. And when the statute runs out on their little arrangement, I'll walk into the courthouse and resurrect myself just to watch Margaret Ellery's face.*

She knew. Five years ago, she already knew about the statute — the obstruction clock, the thirty-year fuse lit in 1994 that would burn out this fall. She hadn't run to escape the town. She had run to position herself. Every mile of gas station sushi and wig-buying and dying air conditioners had been a deployment.

And then the last entry. The one Dev found before I did, because he'd flipped to the back first, the way impatient people do, and come to my door white-faced.

*October 12.* — *Something strange today. A woman came into the diner where I eat breakfast. Young. Suit. Briefcase like a shield. She asked the waitress for directions to the county records office and got them wrong twice. I liked her immediately, which is dangerous, so I left cash on the table and went out the back.*

*Her name was on the briefcase. NYSTROM.*

*The Nystrom girl has come to town.*

* * *

I closed the book.

For a while I did nothing. The office hummed its ordinary hum — Dev's keyboard, the copier cycling down, the radiator's autumn knock. Outside, Harrow Creek went about its business, which is to say it pretended very hard that it had none.

Here is the fact I could not file, could not alphabetize, could not reduce to a chain-of-custody form: I remembered that morning. October 12th, five years ago. My first week in practice here, after Dad's funeral, after I'd decided — in the way you decide things in the fog of grief, permanently and badly — to take over his firm in the town he'd never let me visit as a child. I remembered the diner. I remembered asking for directions and getting them wrong, because I hadn't yet learned that in Harrow Creek nobody gives you straight directions to anywhere that matters.

And I remembered — this came back to me with the slow horror of ice spreading across a windshield — a blonde woman in the corner booth. Reading a paperback. Who had looked up when I came in, held my eyes for exactly one second too long, and looked back down.

I had thought: *she's judging my suit.* I had thought nothing else about her for five years.

She had been watching me arrive. She had written it down that night, in a book that would sit in an evidence locker while the state certified her death, and she had called me *the Nystrom girl* — which meant she knew whose daughter I was. Which meant she knew more about my father than I did, probably. Which meant that when she chose, five years later, to mail a freshly notarized will naming our firm as executor, she had not chosen us despite knowing who I was.

She had chosen us *because* of it.

I picked up the phone to call Dev, then put it down. I picked up my pen, then put that down too. Finally I did the only thing that made sense, which was nothing, which was sitting in the accumulating dark of my office holding a dead woman's diary and understanding, with total clarity, that I had been a character in this story since before I knew it existed — cast, costumed, and directed to this desk, this envelope, this page — by a woman the county had buried on paper five years ago.

The last line of the last entry, beneath everything else, smaller, written in the purple gel pen with a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*If you're reading this, Casper, then it worked.*

My full name. First and last. In a diary entered into evidence five years ago, addressed to a woman who did not move to town until the week it was written.

I turned off my desk lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the radiator knock like something asking to be let in.

In the morning, I was going to find out where Lena Voss's brother had been standing when his sister died.
