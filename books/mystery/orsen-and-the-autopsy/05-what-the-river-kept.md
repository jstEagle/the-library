## 5. What the River Kept

The reservoir sat eleven miles east of town, past the shuttered cannery and a stretch of road where the pines grew close enough to touch both shoulders of the asphalt at once. Casper had driven it once before, five years ago, in the week after Lena Voss's car was pulled from the water — not because she'd been assigned to anything, but because Dev had asked her to pick up certified copies of the recovery report from the county sheriff's substation, and the substation was on that road. She remembered thinking, as she passed the turnoff to the boat launch, that it was an ugly place to die. Flat water. Gray gravel. A single aluminum sign warning of sudden depth changes.

She was thinking it again now.

Her car crunched to a stop in the empty lot. October had stripped most of the color out of the hills, and the reservoir lay under a sky the color of old pewter, so still it looked solid, like a slab of slate set into the land. At the far end, near the concrete boat ramp, a pickup truck sat with its tailgate down and an old man beside it on a folding stool, a fishing rod propped against a cooler, doing absolutely nothing that resembled fishing.

Hal Brandt. She recognized him from the file photographs — younger by twenty years, in uniform, standing waist-deep in the shallows during the recovery operation, one hand raised toward the camera as if to say *stop looking*. He'd been the senior deputy on scene when they pulled the Saab out of the water. He'd signed the dive report. And then he'd retired six months later, at fifty-two, which the file did not explain.

Casper got out. Gravel shifted under her heels. The air smelled of cold water and two-stroke exhaust from some distant engine, though she couldn't see a boat anywhere.

"Mr. Brandt," she called, when she was close enough not to shout but far enough to be polite about it. "Casper Nystrom. I'm handling the Voss estate."

He didn't startle. That was the first thing she noticed. Old men alone at reservoirs startled easily; this one turned his head with the slow economy of someone who had been expecting her, or at least expecting *someone*, for a long time.

"Nystrom," he said. He said it the way you'd test a coin between your teeth. "You're Erik's girl."

"I'm handling the Voss estate," she said again, because repeating herself was a technique, and because confirming or denying the other thing felt like handing him something he hadn't earned.

"Erik Nystrom's daughter." He said it anyway. "Huh." He gestured at the second folding stool, propped against the cooler, already open, already waiting. "Sit if you want. Fish aren't biting. They never bite. I just like the chair."

She didn't sit. She stood at the edge of his little camp — the rod, the cooler, the thermos with a dent in its side — and looked out at the water. "I wanted to ask you about the recovery. Five years ago. The Voss vehicle."

"The Voss vehicle." He nodded slowly, working the phrase around. "That's what we called it too. Never 'Lena's car.' Never 'the body.' The Voss vehicle. You know why?"

"Procedure."

"Procedure." He laughed, a dry sound with no floor to it. "Sure. Procedure." He reached down, opened the cooler, and took out not beer but a paper bag, folded over twice at the top. He held it in his lap like a man holding a small animal. "You've read my report."

"I've read the report filed under your name."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has been waiting years for someone to phrase it correctly.

"That's right," he said quietly. "Filed under my name. Not written by me."

* *

The wind came up off the water in a long slow push, flattening the surface into gray ripples, and Hal Brandt talked.

He talked the way old law enforcement talks — sideways, chronological only when convenient, punctuated by silences that were meant to be filled. Casper let them stay unfilled. She had learned early, watching her father interview clients across the kitchen table with a legal pad and terrible coffee, that the person who speaks first after a silence loses the room.

"Dive team went down at 6:40 in the morning," Brandt said. "Water's maybe forty feet off the drop-off there, where the old quarry wall goes under. Car was nose-down on the ledge at thirty-one feet. Doors closed. Windows up. Seatbelt — driver's side — buckled." He held up one finger. "Now. You're a lawyer. Tell me what's wrong with that picture."

"A lot depends on the vehicle," Casper said. "Some cars float briefly. Some sink fast. If she went in asleep, or—"

"She didn't go in asleep." He said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd correct someone's arithmetic. "Road doesn't run within eighty feet of the water at any point above the drop-off. There's a guardrail the whole stretch, and it wasn't breached. No tire marks on the shoulder. No paint transfer on the rail. For that car to be where we found it, somebody drove it down the boat ramp, put it in gear, and let it roll off the ledge. In the dark. With the doors shut."

"Then how—"

"How did Lena Voss get in the water?" He looked at her directly for the first time. His eyes were pale blue, nearly colorless, the eyes of very old men and very young babies. "That's not the question I can answer. Here's the question I can: why does the official report say the vehicle was recovered ninety yards north of where we actually found it? Why does it list the depth as nineteen feet instead of thirty-one? Why does it describe the seatbelt as *unbuckled*?" He tapped the paper bag in his lap. "Because the man who typed it up wasn't me, and the man who told him what to type wasn't the sheriff."

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the water, a loon made its one sad sound.

"You signed it," Casper said.

"I signed it." He said it without flinching, and she understood that this confession had been rehearsed too — not the way Anders Voss's alibi had been rehearsed, smooth and defensive, but rehearsed the way a man rehearses falling on a sword. "I was three years from my pension. My wife was sick — real sick, the kind insurance pretends to cover. A man came to my house. Not from the sheriff's office. From *outside*. Nice suit. Knew things about my finances that I don't know about my finances. He didn't threaten me. He didn't have to. He explained how much easier everything would be if the report matched what everybody already believed, and he left an envelope, and I—" His jaw worked. "I signed it. I read every line of a report I knew was wrong, and I signed my name under it, and then I retired, and then my wife died anyway, and I have spent five years sitting in this chair looking at that water."

"Why tell me?" Casper asked. "Why now?"

"Because you're here." He said it simply. "Five years, nobody's come asking. Then in one month: a will that shouldn't exist, and Erik Nystrom's daughter walking down the boat ramp in good shoes." He glanced at her heels, sunk slightly into the gravel, and almost smiled. "Things are moving. When things move, men like me get to stop sitting still."

"What was in the envelope?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously. It establishes—"

"Miss Nystrom." He said her name gently, the way you'd steady a child reaching for a hot stove. "It matters to a court. To me, it matters that I took money to bury the truth about a woman who might've been alive the whole time. That's the part I carry. The envelope's just the receipt." He unfolded the top of the paper bag and drew out a thin stack of pages, soft-edged with age and handling. "This is what matters to you."

* *

It was the dive report. Her original. Dated the day of the recovery, typed on a different machine than the filed copy — the font was a half-size larger, the margins uneven — and initialed H.B. in the corner of each page in fading ballpoint.

Casper read it standing up, the pages trembling slightly in the wind until she pinned them flat against her thigh.

*Vehicle located at 31 ft., adjacent to submerged quarry wall, approx. 90 yds south of public ramp.* *Driver-side restraint engaged.* *Both doors closed; no signs of forced egress.* *Recovery recommended pending coroner.*

And at the bottom, in handwriting she didn't recognize — cramped, hurried, pressing hard enough to emboss the page beneath — a note she suspected had never been intended to survive:

*No way this car comes off the road here. Rail intact full length. Someone brought it down the ramp. Flagging for Det. review — E.N.*

E.N.

The letters seemed to lift off the page. The wind picked up and she let it move the papers now, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"My father flagged it," she said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, that it came out level.

"He did." Brandt watched her with those colorless eyes. "Your dad was the detective assigned before it got handed off. He wrote that note the morning of the recovery. Two days later he was pulled off the case — reassigned, they said. And the report that finally got filed wasn't mine anymore. It was cleaned up. Tidied. Nineteen feet, unbuckled belt, accident, drowning, closed." He shook his head slowly. "Erik fought it for a while. Quietly. He was a quiet fighter, your father. Then around Christmas that year he stopped fighting, and nobody ever told me why, and I never asked, because men who ask questions on that case end up retired early. Or worse."

Casper stared at the initials. E.N. Her father's hands had touched this page. Her father had stood on this same gravel, in the same gray light, and written *no way this car comes off the road here* — and then he had gone home, and gone silent, and stayed silent for twenty years until his heart quit on a Tuesday afternoon, and not once, not one time in all her childhood and all her grief, had he ever said a word about a woman named Lena Voss.

She thought of the will with the dead notary's stamp. She thought of Anders Voss's too-smooth alibi. She thought of her father's study, the locked lower drawer she had never opened because opening it would have meant admitting she wanted to.

"Mr. Brandt," she said carefully. "Who gave the order to rewrite the report?"

"I don't know the name." He said it fast — too fast, and she heard the shape of a lie worn smooth by repetition. "I know the suit. I know the voice on the phone. I know the sheriff wouldn't look at me for a year afterward. But the name, no. Names are dangerous. I kept the paper and lost the name. That was the trade."

"And you kept this copy hidden for five years. Why not give it to the police? To the coroner? To anyone?"

"Give it to who?" He spread his hands, taking in the whole gray reach of the reservoir, the empty lot, the dead cannery smokestack on the horizon. "To the department that buried it? This town is a small pond, Miss Nystrom, and the same four families have owned the pond since before either of us was born. You don't hand evidence to the aquarium." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "But a lawyer from outside the families — well. Even a Nystrom is better than nobody. Especially a Nystrom. Because a Nystrom asking questions makes people nervous in a way nobody else can."

"Why is that?"

"Because your family was inside it," he said, "and then it wasn't, and nobody ever found out which side of the glass you're on."

* *

She copied the report on the hood of her car, photographing each page twice with her phone while Brandt watched, and then she returned the original to him.

"Keep it," he said. "I've had my five years of hiding things. I'm tired of being a safe-deposit box." He pressed it back into her hands and closed her fingers over it, and his grip was stronger than she expected, dry and hard as tree bark. "You take it. You're the one the river's calling."

"That's a strange thing to say."

"It's a strange case." He settled back onto his stool, picked up his rod, and cast a line into water he clearly had no intention of fishing. "One more thing, and then I'll let you go."

She waited.

"How is your father?" he asked. "These days."

The question landed strangely, softly, like a stone dropped into deep water. Casper stood very still.

"My father died two years ago," she said.

Brandt's reel stopped mid-crank. For a moment the only sound was the wind and the lap of water against the ramp concrete. He turned and looked at her fully, and whatever he saw in her face made his own go carefully blank — the practiced blankness of a man who has spent a career controlling what it shows.

"Did he," he said.

"Yes. Heart attack. March, two years ago. There was an obituary."

"There was," Brandt agreed slowly. "I remember reading it." He turned back to the water. "Funny. Could've sworn I saw him last spring. Down at the marina, early, before anybody was around. Man built just like Erik, walked just like him. I called out and he got in his truck and left." He reeled in a few inches of empty line, unhurried. "Probably wasn't him. Dead men keep to themselves, generally."

"Generally," Casper said.

"Mm." He studied his bobber, which floated motionless in the gray water, telling him nothing, promising nothing. "Well. You drive careful going back. Road's got deer on it this time of day." A pause. "And Miss Nystrom — that report. Don't file it yet. Just hold it. Hold it and watch who gets nervous. In this town, the nervous ones always tell you more than the paper does."

She walked back to her car with the photocopied pages zipped inside her coat, against her ribs, and the original in her bag, and it wasn't until she had the heater running and the reservoir shrinking in her mirror that she let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the boat ramp:

*Could've sworn I saw him last spring.*

Her father had been dead two years. She had planned the funeral. She had chosen the casket, signed the forms, sorted his files, sealed his study.

And a retired deputy who had just handed her proof that the Voss investigation was buried — proof her own father had tried to raise and been silenced for — had just told her, with a straight face, that he'd seen him alive last spring.

Either Hal Brandt was senile, or cruel, or testing her.

Or the town of her childhood was keeping secrets even bigger than the one in the water.

Her phone buzzed against the console. Dev. She answered on the second ring, eyes on the road, the pines flickering past.

"Casper." Dev's voice was tight in a way she'd never heard it. "I need you to come back to the office. Now. There's a box on the front step."

"What kind of box?"

"Coroner's evidence box," Dev said. "Sealed. Labeled *Voss, L. — personal effects, 2019*." A breath. "Except the seal's dated last Tuesday. And Casper — whoever left it also left a note. It says, *'Stop reading the diary. Ask Brandt what happened to the first deputy who helped her.'*"
