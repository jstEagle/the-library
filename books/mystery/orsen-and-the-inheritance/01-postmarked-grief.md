## 1. Postmarked Grief

The condolence cards had been arriving for six days, and by the seventh they had achieved a kind of architecture. Juniper Villalobos had stacked them on the corner table of the bookshop — the one by the window where Delia used to sit every Thursday with a pot of tea she never finished and a paperback she always finished — and the stack had grown into a leaning tower of sympathy that Juniper kept meaning to straighten and kept not straightening, because straightening it felt too much like admitting Delia wasn't coming Thursday.

They were not, by nature, a person who avoided things. Juniper Villalobos had spent thirty-one years as a detective walking toward the things everyone else walked away from. But retirement had taught them a new and humbling skill: the art of leaving the tower of cards alone.

"You're going to have to read them eventually," said Priya from behind the register, not looking up from the invoice she was pretending to study. Priya had worked the front counter for three years and had developed the unnerving habit of narrating Juniper's avoidance strategies in real time, like a nature documentary.

"I'm reading them," Juniper said. "I'm reading them in order. Alphabetically. By sender."

"The cards don't have senders on the outside."

"Then I'm reading them in the order the mail carrier delivers them, which is a system, and systems take time."

"Mm," said Priya, in the tone of a person filing something away for later use in an argument.

Juniper went back to shelving the new arrivals, which was the other thing they did instead of grief. There was a whole choreography to it — mystery in the front, because Delia had loved mysteries and had once said, with the air of a woman confiding a great scandal, that she only read them to see if she could beat the detective, and she usually could. Poetry in the back, because Delia had loved poetry too but claimed it embarrassed her, the way some people are embarrassed by singing.

The bell over the door rang at four o'clock, and Juniper's chest did the thing it had been doing all week — the hopeful lurch, the immediate deflation. It was only Mrs. Okafor from the florist, coming to collect the vase Delia's funeral flowers had come in, and to say, for the fourth time, that Delia had been the finest woman on the lake road and that she'd once caught her husband overcharging for chrysanthemums and had said nothing but had never bought chrysanthemums there again, which Mrs. Okafor considered the highest form of moral courage.

Juniper agreed that it was. They agreed with everyone, all week. Delia had been the best of them, the funniest, the stubbornest, the one who'd shown up at Juniper's door twenty-two years ago with a casserole and no explanation and stayed, in some essential way, ever since. Everyone had a Delia story. Juniper had forty of them and had told six at the funeral and had kept the rest like stones in a pocket.

It was nearly closing when Priya went home, and the shop went quiet in that particular way — the radiator ticking, the last light going amber on the spines of the poetry section Delia pretended not to love. Juniper made tea in the back room. Two cups. They caught themselves doing it, stood there holding the second cup, and then drank both, because waste offended them more than grief did.

That was when they finally sat down at the corner table, in Delia's chair, and pulled the tower of cards toward them.

Most of it was what condolence cards are. Beautiful handwriting saying very little, which is not a criticism — there is very little to say, and the people who say it beautifully are performing a genuine kindness. A card from the Halloran family, Marisol's looping script: *She loved you like a sister. We're here for you, always.* A card from the retired officers' association that managed to make death sound like a scheduling conflict. A card from someone Juniper didn't know, which turned out to be a misdelivered sympathy for a "Bernard," and Juniper made a note to walk it two streets over, because somewhere a Bernard was un-mourned.

Then the last card. At the bottom of the stack. Plain white envelope, no printed border, no calligraphy. The handwriting on it was Delia's — that upright, unhurried hand, the hand of a woman who wrote her grocery lists in full sentences — and the postmark was dated the day *after* her funeral.

Juniper sat very still for a moment. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the street a dog was making its opinions about a squirrel known.

They opened it.

*Juniper,*

*If you're reading this, then I managed the one trick left to me, which was dying before I had to say any of this out loud. You always said I'd find a way to have the last word. I'm having several. Pour something and settle in.*

*Twenty-two years ago, I was at Miller's Point on the night Orsen Halloran drowned. I've said for two decades that I wasn't, and I've been ashamed of the lie for every one of those days, and I'm tired, Juniper. Tired in the bones. So here it is: I saw what happened at the lake, and it was not an accident. Whatever the coroner wrote, whatever the town decided to believe so it could sleep at night — it was not an accident.*

*You're the only one I trust with this, and I'm sorry, because I know exactly what it's going to cost you. You'll want to leave it alone. You'll try. You'll get about four days into trying and then you'll be down at the lake at dawn with your notebook, and I'll be laughing at you from wherever it is one laughs from.*

*Finish it. I couldn't. There are reasons I couldn't that you'll find, and when you find them you may hate me for a while, and that's all right. I'll be dead, so it won't ruin my week.*

*The rest is in the blue box, the one you already have. I made sure of it years ago. You clever thing, you never even looked.*

*Love you like a sister,*
*Delia*

Juniper read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way they used to read witness statements, hunting the seams.

The blue box. They knew the blue box. It had come into their possession eleven years ago, when Delia had moved out of the lake house and into the apartment over the pharmacy, and had deposited on Juniper's counter a battered tin painted the blue of a summer sky and said, "Keep this. Don't open it. If I die, it's yours, and you can open it then, and not a minute before." And Juniper — who had spent a career opening other people's boxes — had kept this one shut for eleven years out of respect, or superstition, or the simple animal wisdom that some things, once seen, cannot be unseen.

It was upstairs. It had been upstairs through the whole week of funeral and flowers and cards, sitting on the top shelf of the closet next to the winter boots, patient as a held breath.

Juniper did not go upstairs. Not yet. They sat in Delia's chair with the letter in their hands and let the grief and the dread braid themselves together, because that was what they were doing, they realized — the sadness of losing her and the horror of what she'd left behind were no longer two separate things. She had known. All those Thursdays, all those half-finished pots of tea, all those mysteries she'd solved before the detective on the page did — she had known, and she had sat across from Juniper and said nothing, and now she had arranged, from beyond the grave, with the postmark of a woman who'd planned her exit down to the mail schedule, for Juniper to know too.

*It was not an accident.*

Orsen Halloran. Juniper closed their eyes and he was there: a big warm man with paint on his hands and a laugh that arrived before he did, who'd run the boat rental at Miller's Point and fixed the town's bicycles for free and taught half the children on the lake road to swim. Who'd had a daughter, Marisol, twelve years old that summer, who'd grown up into the kind of woman who sent condolence cards with *always* underlined twice. Who'd gone into the water on a June evening twenty-two years ago and not come out, and the town had grieved for a month and then, with the terrible efficiency of towns, had filed him under *accident* and moved on.

And Delia — Delia, who had appeared at Juniper's door that same summer with a casserole and red eyes and no explanation, who had said only, "You're a good person, Juniper Villalobos, and I'm glad you're here" — Delia had been at the lake that night.

Juniper got up, washed both teacups, dried them, put them away. Ritual. Order. The old discipline of hands that needed to be doing something while the mind did the real work.

Then they climbed the stairs to the apartment, took down the winter boots, and lifted the blue box off the shelf.

It was lighter than they remembered. Or heavier. It was impossible to tell; their hands had gone strange. They set it on the kitchen table under the light, and for a long moment they just looked at it, at the faded paint, at the strip of masking tape across the lid on which Delia had written, years ago, in that upright unhurried hand: *FOR JUNIPER. WHEN I'M DONE DODGING.*

"Damn it, Delia," Juniper said quietly, to an empty room, to a woman who was finally, permanently, out of reach and yet somehow still arranging their life from beyond it. "You couldn't have just left me the casserole dish?"

The tape came off in one clean strip. The lid came up.

Inside, on top, was a photograph — Orsen Halloran on the dock at Miller's Point, squinting into the sun, one arm around a teenager Juniper didn't recognize, a thin, frightened-looking kid in an oversized flannel shirt who was turned slightly away from the camera, as if even in a photograph they didn't want to be fully in it.

Beneath the photograph, a ledger with a cracked spine. And beneath the ledger, a second envelope, this one sealed, with three words on the front that stopped Juniper's breath:

*Not the whole.*

* * *

Juniper stood in the kitchen for a long time with the unopened envelope in their hand, and outside the window the lake road ran dark toward Miller's Point, and somewhere in the town below, other people who had been at the lake that night were going about their evenings, not knowing — or knowing, and having spent twenty-two years not knowing that Juniper now knew.

They put the envelope down. They picked it up. They put it down again, and made tea — one cup, this time — and drank it standing up, staring at the box.

Then they went to the closet, found the old notebook they'd sworn they'd never need again, and wrote, on the first clean page, in a hand far less steady than Delia's:

*Orsen Halloran. Drowned June 14. Not an accident. — D.*

The case, they understood, had never actually closed. It had only been waiting for her to finish her sentence.
