## 1. Postmark

There are three kinds of letters that arrive at St. Aldhelm's archive, and I have a system for all of them.

The first kind is administrative: diocesan circulars, insurance renewals, the quarterly reminder that our fire extinguishers require certification. These go in the blue tray, to be dealt with on Tuesdays. The second kind is sentimental—parishioners writing to ask whether we still hold their grandmother's baptismal record, or whether the 1974 flood damaged the marriage registers. These go in the green tray, and I answer them within the week, because grief deserves promptness even when it arrives on cheap stationery.

The third kind is addressed to the dead.

These are rare. Perhaps four or five a year. Letters meant for people buried in our crypt, written by the living who cannot quite accept that correspondence requires two living parties. *Dear Mum, the garden's gone wild without you.* *Tom, you never told me about the money in the shed, so I'm telling you now.* They arrive with no return address and no expectation of reply, and my procedure—the one I designed myself, in the first year of my employment, when designing procedures was the only intimacy I allowed myself—is to log them in the register of unclaimed post, date them, and shelve them in Box 44 with the other documents that belong to no living person.

I am very good at this. I want to be clear about that before anything else happens in this account. Freya Dunmore is very good at her work, which is fortunate, because her work is very nearly all there is of her.

* * *

Old Tom Wicker was buried on a Thursday in October, under a sky the colour of dishwater, with half the town in attendance and the other half wishing they'd thought to close their shops. He had been, by every account I overheard at the memorial tea (which I attended, standing at the edge of the vestry with a cup of tea I had no intention of drinking), a pillar. A benefactor. The man whose name was on the restoration fund plaque by the west door, third from the top, in letters larger than the vicar's.

"He did so much for this parish," said Mrs. Halloway, who runs the flower rota and has run it, as far as I can determine, since the Norman Conquest.

"Seventy years a parishioner," said Mr. Petch, who says this about everyone, including people who moved here in 2019.

The grief was genuine. That's what struck me, watching them. Not performed, not dutiful—genuine, uniform, like weather. Everyone loved Tom Wicker, and they loved him in exactly the same way, with exactly the same words, and if you pressed your ear against the mourning you would have heard nothing underneath it. No static. No interference. A signal too clean to be trusted, though I didn't think that yet. On Thursday I only thought: *what a well-attended funeral,* and went back to the archive before the sherry came out.

Three days later, on Sunday, the letter arrived.

* * *

I found it Monday morning in the post tray, because the cathedral does not believe its archivist should be disturbed on the Sabbath, which suits me fine; the Sabbath is when I do the real sorting. It lay on top of the blue tray's administrative stratum, face up, and I knew immediately it belonged to neither the blue nor the green category, because the envelope was good stock—heavy cream paper, hand-addressed—and because the address read:

**To the Dead of the Crypt of St. Aldhelm's,**

and nothing else. No name. No street. As though the postal service maintained a route through the floor of the church, down past the heating pipes, into the dark where our most distinguished parishioners sleep in their ranked coffins.

The postmark stopped me.

Postmarks are the sort of detail I notice the way other people notice faces. This one read: **ST. ALDHELM'S SORTING OFFICE — FRIDAY 14 OCT.**

Friday. The day after the burial.

Someone had written a letter to Tom Wicker—or to the dead generally—and posted it the day after his funeral, from our own town, and it had taken the Royal Mail three days to travel perhaps four hundred metres, which is the sort of inefficiency I would normally note with mild professional irritation and move past.

Instead I stood in the archive with the letter in both hands, and I noticed something else, something that has stayed with me since with the persistence of a stain: the handwriting trembled. Not the tremor of age—I've catalogued enough octogenarian correspondence to know the difference—but the tremor of someone writing fast, or frightened, pressing hard enough on the final strokes to nearly tear the paper.

I opened it.

I want to be precise about what happened in that moment, because everything after follows from it. My procedure states that mail addressed to the crypt's dead is logged but not opened—it is not ours to read; it belongs to the addressee, however impossible the delivery. In eleven years I have never once broken that rule. I am the woman who does not break rules; it is the load-bearing wall of my entire personality.

I broke it.

*To whoever keeps the records,* the letter began, in that trembling hand, *because the dead cannot answer and the living will not.*

*I killed him. You all know it, even those of you who say otherwise, even those of you who wept loudest on Thursday. I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking that someone write down what happened, properly, in ink, where it cannot be amended. Because everything else in this town gets amended. Everything else gets a tidy entry and a closed drawer.*

*Check the ledger. Check the year 2018. Ask who Ruth Calloway was, and then ask why nobody asks.*

*I was there. I saw. And by the time you read this, I will be no one at all.*

There was no signature. There was a blot at the end, as though the pen had been held to the paper a moment too long, bleeding out whatever name had been considered and abandoned.

* * *

Here is what I should have done, and I can recite the protocol as fluently as a catechism: report unusual correspondence to the vicar, who reports it to the churchwardens, who report it to the diocesan office, where it enters a system and disappears with the efficiency of a stone entering a pond. The letter would be photocopied, filed, discussed at a meeting scheduled for a month hence, and returned to me in an archival folder marked PENDING, and by then whatever it referred to would have been tidied away by people far better at tidying than any confession could survive.

That is what I should have done. Instead I sat down at my desk, in my chair that squeaks on the second rotation, beneath the window where the light comes in slanted and gold from ten until noon, and I read the letter four more times.

*Everything else in this town gets amended.*

You need to understand something about me, since apparently I've decided to tell you things. I came to St. Aldhelm's eleven years ago, and in eleven years I have built an archive so orderly that visiting researchers have called it, without irony, a place of pilgrimage. Every document dated, cross-referenced, shelved. Every anomaly resolved or annotated. I am not a religious woman, but I believe in the catalogue the way the devout believe in providence: nothing is lost, everything is recorded, order will outlast us all.

And I chose this. That's the part I keep circling. Invisibility isn't something that happens to a person like weather; it's a discipline, practised daily. I wear grey. I speak softly. I attend funerals and stand at edges. When Mrs. Halloway tried to recruit me for the flower rota in 2016, I developed a stammer that has never troubled me before or since. There is safety in being furniture. Furniture is never suspected, never questioned, never asked to testify about anything.

But furniture does not open other people's mail. Furniture does not sit holding a confession with hands that won't stay steady, reading *check the year 2018* over and over while the radiator ticks and the light moves across the floor and the ordinary day goes on happening outside the window, indifferent.

*Ask why nobody asks.*

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. The thing I'd felt at the funeral without naming it. The grief had been genuine, yes—but it had also been agreed upon. Uniform. A town that mourns in one voice is a town that has rehearsed.

* * *

At half past four I did the last thing I will ever be able to describe as innocent. I took a key from the ring in my desk—the small brass one, third from the left, for the drawers of the old plan chest—and I unlocked the bottom-left drawer. The unfiled drawer. Every archive has one, whatever we claim publicly; mine holds the items that resist classification, that wait for a context that hasn't arrived yet. It is the one drawer in my kingdom governed by chaos, and I guard it jealously.

I put the letter inside. Face down. Beneath a folio of undated photographs of the bell tower.

Then I locked it, and hung the key back on its hook, and wrote in the register of unclaimed post, in my best hand: *No items received this week.*

A lie. In ink. In the ledger itself.

I sat for a long moment after that, listening to the building settle around me—the creaks and drips and distant organ-practice of a church going about its evening—and I understood that something had shifted in me, some pin drawn from some hinge, and that I could not put it back. The letter had been addressed to the dead, but it had been delivered to me. To the keeper of records. *Write it down properly, where it cannot be amended.*

Tomorrow, I told myself, I will decide what to do. Tomorrow I will report it, or file it, or burn it, but tomorrow I will be sensible again.

But tomorrow morning found me at the crypt door at seven o'clock, before anyone else arrived, with the ring of keys in my pocket and my heart going like a trapped bird, because I had spent the night doing arithmetic instead of sleeping, and the sum kept coming out wrong.

Ruth Calloway. Deceased 2018, according to a name scratched into the crypt ledger in handwriting I did not recognise.

And no death certificate anywhere in the county records.

I know, because I checked. Twice.

The crypt door was locked, as it had been locked since the Wicker interment, sealed with the vicar's own wax over the hasp. But I am the archivist, and the archivist has keys for everything, and as I knelt there breaking the seal with a thumbnail—my first act of vandalism in eleven years of immaculate service—one thought rang in me louder than the others:

For once, the archive was asking something of me.
