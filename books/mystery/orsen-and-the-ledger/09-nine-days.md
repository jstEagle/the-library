## 9. Nine Days

I have spent seven years learning how to be unremarkable, and I will tell you the secret of it: it is not hiding. Anyone can hide. Hiding is a hobby for amateurs and teenagers. What I did was harder. I made myself *useful*. I learned which aches the town carried in its knees, which widows needed their blood pressure flattered downward, which children required a lollipop and a stern word about sweets in equal measure. A doctor who is indispensable is never examined. Nobody looks closely at the hands that are always busy.

But Freya Dunmore looked at my hands.

She had come in with a paper cut gone septic—of course she had; the woman filed documents like other people juggle knives—and I cleaned it and dressed it and asked her, because I could not help myself, what she was cataloguing these days. And she said, "Deaths," in that flat archival voice of hers, and watched me the way you watch a lock you suspect has been picked before.

I told myself she knew nothing. I told myself this for six days while I refilled prescriptions in handwriting I had not used since 2018, because sentiment is a disease and I caught it last spring when grief got into my pen. Six days of telling myself stories. Then, on the seventh day, Idris Vane came to my surgery at nine o'clock at night, smelling of whisky and apology, and sat down without being asked, which Vane never does, because Vane's whole life is a study in asking permission.

"They know," he said. "Some of them. Aldous rang me tonight. He wanted to know if I'd spoken to anyone about the crypt."

"Have you?"

"I spoke to *her*." He rubbed his face. "Nine days, Elias. That's what's left on the statute. I gave her nine days because I'm a coward with a calendar. And now they're going to move before the calendar does."

"Move how? It's a crypt. It's been sealed for years."

Vane looked at me with the particular pity of a man watching someone walk toward a cliff edge he himself has already fallen off. "Aldous has been after the vestry committee for two years to have the east wing underpinned. Subsidence. Cracks. He's got an engineer's report somewhere, I'd wager, dated conveniently recently. They'll pour the footings, seal the entrance, 'for safety.'" He paused. "And the engineer will need to inspect the interior first. Alone. In an old building with bad air and older stone."

I am a doctor. I know exactly how many ways there are for a man to die in a building that everyone agrees was dangerous. I have signed death certificates for three of them.

"When?" I asked.

"Thursday night. They're calling it an emergency survey."

It was Monday.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about living under a borrowed name: you stop being able to tell which fears are yours. For seven years I have felt fear the way you feel weather—ambient, general, belonging to no one in particular. But sitting in my surgery that night, listening to Vane's car grind away down the lane, I felt something specific and cold: they were not going to kill *Dr. Elias Marsh*, beloved local physician. They were going to kill a trespasser found in a condemned crypt, some unidentified man, poor soul, must have broken in, tragic, the building was known to be unsafe. And then Dr. Marsh would grieve, professionally, at the funeral of a stranger, and no one would ever count the fingers or notice that the dead man's hands were a doctor's hands.

They were going to bury Ruth Calloway's brother in the same ground where they'd buried the lie about her.

I packed that night. Not much—a bag, the photographs I'd kept duplicates of, my father's watch, which I had worn as Elias Marsh for seven years and which had belonged to Thomas Calloway before Wicker put him in the ground. Sentiment again. The disease progresses.

Then I sat by the phone and did the arithmetic of trust, which is the loneliest arithmetic there is. Vane had given me the warning, but Vane had also, apparently, given Aldous something—loose talk, a hesitation, something a man trained to keep secrets lets slip only when he's decided, deep down, that secrets are heavier than he can carry alone. I could run. Running was the thing I was best at; I had a decade of practice and a second identity half-built in a drawer for exactly this contingency.

But running meant the strongbox evidence stayed buried, meant the statute closed on Thursday with the truth inside the crypt like a body in a coffin, meant my father stayed murdered and my sister stayed dead and Tom Wicker stayed mourned by the entire town that helped kill him.

And it meant leaving Freya Dunmore holding a ledger full of falsifications with her name nowhere near it—which was, I realized with a shame that arrived late and hit hard, exactly the position I had occupied for seven years. Invisible. Useful. Unexamined.

I picked up the phone.

* * *

She answered on the second ring, at nearly midnight, and did not say hello. She said, "I wondered how long you'd take," and I confess that for one absurd moment, crouched in my dark surgery with a packed bag, I almost laughed.

"You knew?"

"I suspected. Now I know. Tell me."

I told her. All of it—the council, the survey, the engineer, the nine days collapsing into three. She listened the way she does everything: completely, silently, and then with one sentence that rearranged the furniture of my panic.

"The crypt entrance is on the parish plan as two exits," she said. "The main door and the ventilation shaft behind the north buttress. It's bricked up, but it's in the 1962 renovation drawings. I've filed them." A pause. "Obviously. I file everything."

"Freya—"

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me Thursday, if we're both still breathing. Where do you keep the strongbox evidence?"

"It's not here. It's—" I closed my eyes. "It's in the crypt. I put it back. Behind Wicker's coffin, in the niche where Ruth hid it originally. I couldn't risk it in the cottage again, and I couldn't risk it at the surgery, so I put it where no one would ever look, because no one goes in there except—"

"—except now they're going to seal it with you inside it, and the evidence inside you." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Freya Dunmore; nothing about her ever rose. She simply became more present, the way pressure becomes depth. "Then we go in Wednesday night. Before their Thursday survey. We take the strongbox out through the shaft, and you leave through it too, and when they open the crypt Thursday morning to stage their accident, they'll find it empty."

"And then what? They'll know. They'll come for us both."

"Yes," she said, and I heard something in her voice I had never heard there before—not fear, not resolve, but something underneath both, like bedrock. "They will. Which is why tomorrow I'm going to walk into the middle of the market square in daylight and hand copies of everything to three people who hate each other equally. The newspaper man. The solicitor. And old Mrs. Penhale, who has been waiting seven years for someone to give her permission to be furious. If anything happens to either of us, it all comes out anyway. We make ourselves too visible to disappear."

I sat with that. Seven years of invisibility, and this woman proposed to end it in a single afternoon by the simple expedient of refusing, absolutely, to be quiet.

"Why?" I asked. "You could file it away. You could do what everyone else did. You told me once you built your whole life around not being noticed. Why burn it down for my father?"

The line was quiet long enough that I checked it hadn't died.

"Because I've spent ten years filing other people's truths," she finally said, "and every year I filed a little more of myself along with them. Someone has to be counted, Dr. Marsh. It might as well be me."

* * *

Wednesday came down grey and windless, the kind of evening that makes old buildings feel like they're listening. We met at the lychgate at eleven. She had a torch, a crowbar, and a canvas satchel, and she was wearing, of all things, her good coat, as though breaking into a crypt were a formal occasion. Perhaps for her it was. An archive is a kind of church; she was simply changing parishes.

The ventilation shaft was where the 1962 drawings promised, behind the north buttress, bricked over with soft old mortar that had spent sixty years dreaming of exactly this. Freya worked the crowbar with a methodical violence that I found genuinely moving. Bricks came away in breaths of cold air that smelled of stone and time and, faintly, of incense that had soaked into the walls decades ago and never left.

"You first," she said. "You know the way."

I went down into the dark I had entered once before, seven years ago, on the worst night of my life, when I had crawled out of here with my father's blood on me and Ruth's name in my mouth and no idea that I would spend the rest of my youth being kind to the people who erased us. The torch beam found the coffins, the ledger stand, the niche. The strongbox was there. Of course it was. Things hidden by careful people stay hidden until careless people go looking.

We hauled it up the shaft between us, Freya climbing ahead with the box roped to her waist, and I want the record—I want *some* record—to show that when she reached the top and turned back for me, torchlight catching her face, she was smiling. Small. Fierce. The smile of a woman discovering that she has been enormous all along and simply filed herself small.

Then the floodlights came on.

Every light in the churchyard, all at once, white and total, and voices—several, unhurried, the voices of men who believe the building is theirs. Through the broken shaft opening I saw Aldous Pemberton standing by the lychgate with his arms folded, and beside him the engineer with his clipboard, and beyond them, at the edge of the light, a police car with its engine running.

"Well," Aldous called pleasantly into the dark. "There's our intruder. Both of them, by the sound of it. Best call it in, constable—dangerous building, two trespassers. Anything could happen to them down there."

Freya looked down the shaft at me. Above her, the night was full of men. Below me, the crypt held seven years of my life and my father's bones.

"Freya," I whispered. "Give them the box. Run."

She adjusted her grip on the crowbar, stepped directly into the floodlight where every one of them could see her plain as noon, and raised her voice to carry across the entire churchyard.

"Gentlemen," she said, "you're just in time. I have evidence, witnesses, and nine days left on a statute—and I intend to use every hour of them. So someone had better arrest me *now*, or step aside."

Nobody moved. Somewhere in the dark behind Aldous, a camera flash went off—Mrs. Penhale, bless her furious heart, had brought her grandson and his telephone.

And that, I thought, gripping the strongbox rope as the shouting began, is how the invisible woman lit the fuse.
