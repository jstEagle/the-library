## 4. Heirs and Apparitions

There is an art to watching people without being watched back, and Paloma Sandoval had spent forty years perfecting it. The trick, he had learned long ago behind the desk of the Hotel Meridian, was not to hide but to be unremarkable—to arrange yourself in a doorway or a pew or the corner of a café so thoroughly that the eye slides off you like rain off oiled canvas. A man carrying nothing attracts no attention. A man carrying a tray attracts less.

So on Thursday morning, Paloma carried a tray.

The tray held two coffees and a plate of almond biscuits he had purchased from the bakery on Calle del Agua for no reason other than that it gave him somewhere to stand. He stood now beneath the arcade across from the Palace of Justice, where Judge Emilio Varga arrived each morning at eight-forty to dispense the parish's idea of justice, which by all accounts ran about ten minutes late and always favored whoever had arrived first.

Emilio came up the steps the way water comes to a boil—gradually, inevitably, surrounded by a rising froth of clerks and petitioners. He was fifty-three and wore his late father's build like a borrowed coat: broad through the shoulders, going soft at the middle, silver at the temples in a way that suggested either distinguished maturity or an expensive barber, depending on your generosity. He shook hands. He laughed at something a bailiff said. He touched a young woman's elbow as she thanked him for something, a gesture of such practiced warmth that Paloma found himself wondering how many thousands of elbows that hand had touched, and whether any of them had ever been touched *back*.

Then a boy—sixteen, maybe seventeen, lanky, in a school blazer with the crest of San Ildefonso Academy—came running up the steps calling "Judge Varga! Judge Varga!" and Emilio turned with his smile already assembled.

And then the boy said something. Paloma was too far away to hear it. But he saw what happened to the judge's face.

It lasted perhaps a second and a half. The smile stayed exactly where it was—the lips, the teeth, the crinkled corners—all of it perfectly preserved, like a face pressed between the pages of a book. But everything behind it drained out, the way color drains from a photograph left too long in a window. For one second and a half, Emilio Varga looked at that boy the way a man looks at a door he thought he'd locked.

Then the color came back, the smile resumed its duties, and the judge clapped the boy on the shoulder and sent him inside ahead of him, laughing at some joke Paloma would never hear.

Paloma ate an almond biscuit and considered this.

Grief, properly worn, becomes part of the body. It settles into the shoulders and the voice and the particular way a person says a name. Ten years after a mother's death, you expect the grief to have fossilized into something smooth and portable—a stone kept in a pocket, taken out on anniversaries. What you do not expect is for the stone to still be sharp. What you do not expect is for a stranger's innocent words to open a wound that should have scarred over years ago.

Unless, of course, the wound had never been allowed to close. Unless someone had been picking at it, all these years, in private.

Paloma brushed the crumbs from his jacket, set the empty tray on the bakery's return shelf with a nod to the girl behind the counter—who did not nod back, because she had not once registered his existence, which was exactly as he liked it—and went to find the daughter.

* * *

The Varga Foundation occupied a converted granary near the river, all exposed beams and donated furniture, the kind of place where charity went to be photographed. A banner over the door announced RESTORATION FUND: SAN ILDEFONSO PARISH CHURCH — IN LOVING MEMORY OF OUR FOUNDER, INES VARGA, with a photograph of the deceased beneath it. Ines in the photograph was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, formidable, wearing the expression of a woman who has signed many checks and forgiven few debts. Her portrait hung in the nave of the parish church too, Paloma knew; he'd seen it yesterday, candlelit and serene, a saint with excellent cheekbones.

He entered the foundation under the oldest disguise in the world: a man with a question.

"I'm sorry to trouble you," he told the receptionist, a young woman with the harried air of someone who answered the same question eleven times a day. "I'm researching my family's history, and I believe my grandmother may have received assistance from Señora Varga's charitable work. Years ago. I wondered if there might be records."

This was even true, more or less. His grandmother had certainly been poor enough.

"Records go back to the founding," the receptionist said, "but Renata handles anything older than five years herself. She's very—" She paused, searching for the word, and settled on "—thorough."

Renata Varga was thorough. Paloma saw it within four minutes of being shown into her office, ostensibly to wait while she finished a call. She was fifty, dressed in the uniform of serious women everywhere—dark trousers, white shirt, no jewelry except a watch—and her office was arranged not for comfort but for function. Files squared to the edges of shelves. Pens in a row. On the wall behind her desk hung her mother's portrait again, smaller than the church version, positioned so that anyone sitting across the desk would have to look at it. Or so that *she* would have to look at it, Paloma amended, depending on which way you believed the furniture faced.

She finished her call, apologized for the wait with genuine grace, and listened to his fabricated genealogy with an attentiveness that would have flattered a cardinal.

"The name doesn't come up," she said at last, apologetic, turning pages in a ledger older than Paloma's career. "But I'll have Marta check the pre-digital files. My mother believed every person who asked for help deserved to be remembered by name." She smiled. "She used to say a name was the only thing the poor owned outright."

"A wise woman," Paloma said.

"She was." And here Renata paused—one beat, no more—with her hand flat on the ledger page, and something moved behind her eyes that was not love and not quite its opposite. Something more like a woman checking a lock she checks every night, knowing it will hold, hating that she needs to check. "People say that. They said it at the funeral. They say it every year at the dinner." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "They'll say it again next month. Tenth anniversary. You should come—it's open to the parish. She'd have hated it, actually. All that fuss. She preferred giving quietly."

"Did she?" said Paloma. "Prefer quiet?"

"Oh, endlessly." Renata laughed, and it was a lovely laugh, warm and easy, and Paloma noted that it arrived precisely half a second too late, like thunder after lightning that hadn't happened. "She once made me return a donation because the donor wanted a plaque. 'Charity that signs its name,' she told me, 'is just advertising with a conscience.'" 

"And yet," Paloma said mildly, gesturing at the portrait, the banner outside, the restoration fund in loving memory, "here we are."

The silence that followed lasted three seconds, which in a conversation is roughly the length of a confession. Renata's smile remained fixed, but Paloma watched her thumb press against the edge of the ledger hard enough to whiten the nail, and he watched her glance flick—not to the portrait, but to the bottom drawer of her desk, the locked one, the one with the brass keyhole worn brighter than the others from use.

"You're right," she said lightly. "We've made rather a monument of her. She'd have charged us for it." She stood, extended her hand, promised to have Marta look into the grandmother, and walked him to the door with the flawless courtesy of a woman who wanted very much for a certain concierge to leave her building.

At the threshold, Paloma turned back. "One small thing. Your mother's last week—I read she stayed nearby, in town, before the accident at the reservoir. Did she visit you here?"

Something crossed Renata's face then that Paloma would spend the rest of the day taking apart. Not guilt. Not fear. Recognition—the brief, appalled recognition of a card player who realizes, mid-hand, that the person across the table knows exactly which cards she holds.

"No," Renata said. "She didn't visit me that week."

Which was interesting, Paloma thought, walking out into the sun, because he had asked about a visit, and she had answered about *that week*, and only guilty people answer questions that haven't been asked.

* * *

Finding Tomás Varga required no craft whatsoever. Finding Tomás Varga required only the ability to follow the sound of laughter to the Café Rialto at half past noon, where the youngest heir held court at the corner table with the devotion of a man who has been holding the same court at the same table for twenty years.

Tomás was forty-six and looked fifty-five in a way that was almost stylish about it—rumpled linen, silver stubble, the reddened nose and bright wet eyes of the functioning drunk, which is to say a man whose functioning is itself the drink's most impressive achievement. He was telling a story to an audience of three old men and a dog, and by the gestures involved it appeared to concern a bishop, a boat, and possibly a goat.

"—so the bishop says to me, Tomás, that animal is *consecrated*, and I said, Your Grace, then we shall drown it with full honors—"

The table roared. The dog thumped its tail. Tomás laughed hardest of all, head back, throat working, and Paloma, standing at the bar with a vermouth he had ordered for camouflage, watched the laugh end the way a wave ends—suddenly, all at once, leaving flat water behind. For a moment Tomás just sat there, glass halfway to his mouth, looking at nothing, at the middle distance where the dead keep their appointments. Then he drank, and the light came back on, and he was charming again.

Paloma let twenty minutes pass. He let Tomás tell the story twice more, with variations—the bishop became a monsignor, the goat acquired a bell—and he let the audience thin until only the dog remained, professionally loyal. Then he did the simplest thing in his repertoire. He sat down across the table.

Tomás blinked at him with the frank, unfocused appraisal of his condition. "Do I know you?"

"No," Paloma said. "But I know your mother's name."

What happened next, Paloma would later describe to himself as watching a building settle. Nothing fell. Nothing cracked. But every load-bearing element shifted at once, invisibly, and the whole structure came to rest in a slightly different shape. Tomás's hand stopped on its way to the glass. His eyes cleared—not sobered, nothing so dramatic, but focused, the way a camera focuses, suddenly and mechanically.

"Inés," Tomás said. Just the name. He said it the way you'd touch a bruise to check if it still hurt.

It still hurt.

"They tell me she slipped," Paloma said quietly. "At the reservoir. Ten years ago."

"They tell everyone that." Tomás turned the glass a quarter-turn on the table, aligning it with the wood grain, a small compulsive ceremony. "They've told it so many times it's practically scripture now. *She slipped.* Two syllables. Cheaper than a gravestone." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, though whether from the drink or the name would take a better diagnostician than Paloma to determine. "Who are you? Really."

"A hotel man," Paloma said. "That's true, at least."

"A hotel man." Tomás smiled, and it was the smile from the story about the bishop—warm, crooked, self-mocking—but underneath it something moved like a fish under ice. "You know what's funny about hotels? Everything happens in them. Births, deaths, affairs, crimes. Whole lives, checked in and checked out. And the staff see all of it and forget none of it, and the guests never once wonder what the staff know." He raised his glass an inch, a toast to the profession. "My mother stayed in a hotel, three days before she died. Did you know that? Nobody knows that. I know it because I followed her. Because I was drunk, and jealous, and I thought she was meeting a lover." The quarter-turn of the glass again. "She wasn't meeting a lover."

"What was she doing?"

"Arguing." Tomás's voice dropped, and the charm dropped with it, and what was underneath was thirty years younger and badly frightened. "In the courtyard of that hotel. With someone I couldn't see—behind the archway, I only heard the voice. And she said—" He stopped. He passed a hand over his face. "She said, *'You can't bury it twice.'*"

The vermouth sat untouched in front of Paloma. Somewhere behind the bar a radio murmured a love song to nobody.

"And then?" Paloma asked.

"And then I did what I always do." Tomás's mouth twisted. "I went and got drunker, so I could forget it properly. And three days later they pulled her out of the reservoir, and the family said *she slipped*, and I—" He looked directly at Paloma then, and for one naked moment the drunkard vanished entirely and Paloma saw the boy who had followed his mother to a hotel courtyard and hidden behind his own cowardice ever since. "I let them say it. That's my inheritance. Not the money. The letting-them-say-it."

He drained the glass, signaled the waiter for another with the ease of long practice, and when the light came back on behind his eyes it was the charm again, the performance, the corner-table throne.

"But you didn't come all this way for my mother," Tomás said brightly. "Nobody comes for my mother anymore. She's a portrait now. Portraits don't need visitors." He leaned in, conspiratorial, wine-breathed, grinning. "So. Hotel man. Who are you really, and why does everyone in this parish suddenly want to know what you know?"

Paloma stood, laid coins on the table for the next round, and offered the truth in its smallest possible package.

"I'm the concierge at the Meridian," he said. "Your mother's hotel."

Tomás stared at him. The glass stopped halfway to his mouth. And Paloma watched the sentence land—watched it land not as surprise but as *confirmation*, watched Tomás's face do the arithmetic of ten years in the space of a breath, and understood, with the cold clean click of a lock turning, that the youngest Varga had been waiting a decade for someone from that hotel to walk into the Rialto and sit down at his table.

"Three days before she died," Tomás said softly, to no one, to the dog, to the ghost in the courtyard. "Room twelve. She signed the register herself." He looked up, and his grin was gone. "But not with her own name."

Paloma walked home through the afternoon heat with the phrase turning over in his mind like a coin he couldn't stop flipping. *Not with her own name.*

Back at the Meridian, the lobby was quiet, the guests accounted for, the afternoon sun laying long gold bars across the marble floor. Paloma took the guest ledgers down from their shelf—forty years of them, his whole life filed in date order—and carried the volume for October, ten years past, to the desk.

If Ines Varga had signed that register, her handwriting was in it. And whatever name she had used, whoever had checked her in, whatever she had been trying to bury twice—

It would be there, in ink, in room twelve, waiting for him all this time.

Paloma opened the book.
