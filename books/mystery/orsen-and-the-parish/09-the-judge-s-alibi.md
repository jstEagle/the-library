## 9. The Judge's Alibi

The invitation arrived the way invitations to trouble always arrive at the Hotel Meridian: on cream card stock, hand-delivered, and smelling faintly of money.

*Mr. P. Sandoval requests the pleasure of your company at lunch, Thursday, one o'clock, Casa Varga.* It was signed *Emilio Varga*, in a hand so controlled it looked typeset. There was no reason given. There was no RSVP requested. It was not, strictly speaking, an invitation at all. It was a summons wearing a bow tie.

Paloma read it twice at the front desk, then set it down beside the bell and regarded it the way one regards a spider that has decided to share one's bathtub—politely, but with a full accounting of exits.

"Bad news?" asked Sofía, the afternoon girl, who had a genius for appearing exactly when a man wished to be alone with his thoughts.

"It's an invitation to lunch."

"Oh, that's worse," she said, and went back to her crossword.

He should have declined. He knew this the way he knew which guests would skip out on their bills and which would cry at checkout over nothing at all—by instinct, by ten thousand small observations filed away in the drawer of his mind that never quite closed. A judge does not invite a hotel concierge to lunch. A judge summons a problem to lunch, and problems that attend such lunches tend to come home lighter by exactly the weight of their curiosity.

But there was another consideration, and it sat in Paloma's chest like a swallowed stone: Emilio Varga knew his name. Not "the concierge." Not "that quiet fellow at the Meridian." His name, spelled correctly, on cream card stock. The last week had been steadily dismantling the wall between Paloma Sandoval and the world, brick by brick, and here was a man holding another brick, smiling, asking if Paloma would like to help finish the job.

One did not leave such questions unanswered.

* * *

Casa Varga stood on the high side of town where the cypress trees grew old enough to be embarrassed about their height—a long, pale house with shutters the color of dried blood and a driveway that curved as though even the gravel had been taught manners. A housekeeper met him at the door and led him through rooms that smelled of lemon polish and old grief, past the portrait of Ines Varga that hung above the mantel like a saint who had been promised sainthood and was still waiting for the paperwork.

She'd had that look, Paloma thought. The look of a woman being remembered harder than she had lived.

Emilio received him on the terrace, beneath a pergola heavy with grapes nobody seemed to eat. The judge was sixty or nearly, silver at the temples, dressed in linen the color of Sunday morning. He rose, extended a hand that was dry and cool and precisely calibrated in its pressure—not too firm, not too soft, the handshake of a man who had spent thirty years making people feel they were being measured fairly even as they were being measured.

"Señor Sandoval. Thank you for coming."

"Your invitation was very persuasive, Your Honor."

"Emilio, please. We're not in chambers." The smile arrived and departed with bureaucratic efficiency. "Though I confess I've read your file."

"I don't have a file, Your Honor."

"Everyone has a file. Most of them are simply blank, which is itself information." Emilio gestured to the table, where lunch waited under white cloth: cold soup, bread, a chicken that had died with dignity, wine the color of garnets. "Sit. Eat. You'll find I'm a better host than my reputation suggests, and my reputation is excellent, because I pay attention to it."

Paloma sat. He ate, because declining food in that house would have been noted somewhere, in some file. The soup was almond and garlic, served cold, and it was superb, and he resented every spoonful.

They spoke of nothing for a while—the weather, the restoration of the parish roof, the scandalous price of olive oil. Emilio conducted this small talk the way a surgeon conducts anesthesia: smoothly, continuously, keeping the patient comfortable while deciding where to cut.

It came between the chicken and the cheese.

"You've been asking questions," Emilio said, pouring wine he did not drink himself. "About my mother."

"I work at a hotel, Your Honor. Asking questions is most of the job. *Where are the good restaurants? Is the water safe? Why is the elevator haunted?*"

"The elevator isn't haunted."

"No, sir. But you'd be amazed what people will believe if you say it gently enough."

Something flickered behind the judge's eyes—amusement, perhaps, or the professional appreciation of a craftsman watching another craftsman's hands. He turned his glass slowly on the cloth, leaving a ring like a small dark moon.

"Three cases," Emilio said. "Villanueva, nine years ago. The pharmacist who fell down his own stairs twice. Doña Amparo's nephew and the business with the well. And the fire at the granary, which the fire marshal called electrical until someone left him a newspaper clipping with certain paragraphs underlined in pencil." He looked up. "In each instance, the police received anonymous assistance. Notes. Clues. Little gifts, wrapped in block capitals. In each instance, an arrest followed, and in each instance the arrested party was guilty, which is more than can be said for most arrests in this province." He raised his glass an inch. "I found those notes fascinating. The grammar was careful. The spelling was perfect. Whoever wrote them understood evidence better than half my colleagues on the bench. And whoever wrote them wanted no credit whatsoever—which, in my experience, is the rarest appetite of all."

Paloma kept his face doing what it had done for twenty years at the front desk: pleasant, attentive, faintly bored. Inside, something cold was walking up his spine one vertebra at a time.

"People write letters, Your Honor. It's a dying art."

"Mm." Emilio smiled. "And people sign them, usually. Or they don't, and we call it crank mail. But these—" he tapped the table once, softly, "—these were *helpful*. There's a difference. Cranks want attention. Your correspondent wanted justice, tidily achieved, by other people's hands, at no cost to himself. That's not madness. That's *policy*. And policies belong to institutions, or to men who behave like institutions." He let that sit in the sun a moment. "I've often wondered who it was. I wondered for years. And then last month a guest at the Hotel Meridian mentions, in passing, that the concierge has been asking odd questions about my mother's death—and do you know, Señor Sandoval, the resemblance struck me before the coincidence did. The way you watch people. The way you remember things they'd prefer forgotten. I've spent my career studying witnesses. You have the eyes of a man taking depositions at all times, even when handing someone a towel."

The wind moved through the pergola. Somewhere below the terrace, a gardener's shears snipped rhythmically, like a slow heart.

"And what conclusion has the court reached?" Paloma asked quietly.

"That you are either the most remarkable concierge in Spain," said Emilio, "or you are a man who has spent his life hiding inside the most remarkable concierge in Spain. And I confess I don't much care which. What I care about is this." He set down his glass, and the charm remained—it never left; it was load-bearing—but underneath it now was something older and heavier, the way bedrock sits under topsoil. "My mother drowned ten years ago. It was ruled an accident. The parish grieved. The family grieved. We built her a chapel roof and named a scholarship after her, and every year we raise a glass, and every year it hurts a little less, which is what time is *for*. If you reopen that wound—if you go pulling threads through this town, stirring up the Verger, the ledgers, God knows what—you will not be punishing anyone who deserves it. You will be punishing everyone. My sister. My brother. Three hundred people who have made their peace. Grief is like a foundation, Señor Sandoval. You don't dig under it just to see what's there. Sometimes what's there is holding everything up."

"Including the truth?"

"*Especially* the truth." Emilio said it without flinching, without apology, and Paloma felt a strange, unwilling respect. Most liars blushed. This man had made peace with his own architecture. "You think I'm threatening you. I'm not. Threats are for men who lack imagination. I am *informing* you. There is a difference, and the difference is this: a threat wants something from you. Information merely describes the terrain. So let me describe it." He folded his hands. "You live alone. You have no family anyone can find, no property beyond a room above a garage, and a savings account that a careful man might call modest and an honest man might call sad. Your entire existence—your safety, your anonymity, your lovely invisible life—is housed in that hotel. And hotels, Señor Sandoval, are governed by licenses. Health inspections. Fire codes. Tax audits. All manner of weather. A word from this office to the right municipal clerk, and the Meridian could find itself having a very difficult year. Nothing criminal. Nothing traceable. Just weather."

There it was. Velvet, yes. Velvet all the way down. But a fist is a fist even in a glove, and Paloma could feel the shape of it.

He discovered, sitting there with cold soup in his stomach and the gardener's shears keeping time below, that he was afraid. Properly afraid—the animal kind, the kind that makes the hands want to hold something steady. For twenty years he had solved murders the way other men did crosswords: privately, safely, leaving the danger on doorsteps for braver men in uniforms to pick up. He had told himself it was humility. Sitting on that terrace, he admitted it had also been cowardice, and that the two had shared a coat for so long he'd stopped noticing whose arms were in the sleeves.

And beneath the fear, unwelcome as a weed through pavement, something else was growing.

Defiance. Small, stubborn, familiar. It was the same feeling he'd had at nineteen, standing in a police station doorway with a clue in his pocket, deciding—for reasons he still couldn't fully explain—that the truth deserved better than to die politely in a case file.

"May I say something, Your Honor?" Paloma asked.

"You may."

"You're right about me. Mostly. I did write those letters. I do take depositions with my eyes. And you're right that I love my invisibility—I'd fight you for it, honestly, and I'd probably lose, because you have weather and I have a room above a garage." He paused. "But you've made one error, and judges hate making errors, so I'll offer it gently. You think my questions are about your mother's death. They're not. They started with two letters sent to *me*—one confessing to her murder, one promising another death at your anniversary dinner. Someone is using your mother's memory as bait, Your Honor, and someone means to kill again in front of your entire parish. You can spend your weather trying to silence the concierge. Or you can ask yourself why a killer would want the concierge looking in the first place." He stood, and nodded to the untouched cheese. "Thank you for the lunch. The soup was extraordinary."

For the first time, Emilio Varga's composure shifted—not much, a millimeter, a hairline crack in poured concrete—but Paloma saw it, because seeing was the one thing he had never agreed to stop doing.

"Sandoval." The judge's voice followed him toward the door, mild as milk. "Whatever you think you know—remember that alibis exist for a reason. Mine, for instance, is ironclad. Ten years old and ironclad. Ask anyone."

"That's the interesting thing about alibis, Your Honor," Paloma said, without turning around. "Only guilty men keep them polished."

* * *

The walk back down into town took twenty minutes, downhill all the way, though Paloma's legs felt as if he'd climbed something. The parish bells rang four o'clock as he passed the fountain, and the water caught the light and threw it around carelessly, the way only water and children are permitted to do.

At the Hotel Meridian, the lobby was empty except for Marguerite, the head housekeeper, restocking towels on the shelf behind the desk with her usual unhurried precision. She glanced up.

"You're back early. How was lunch with the judge?"

Paloma stopped. "I didn't tell anyone where I was going."

"No," Marguerite agreed pleasantly, tucking a towel corner into line. "You didn't. But the judge's driver collects his cigars from the tobacconist on the corner, and the tobacconist's wife does our linens on Tuesdays, and I happened to be collecting them." She smiled the small, unremarkable smile of a woman discussing laundry. "Small towns, Mr. Sandoval. Everything travels. You'd know that better than most."

She gathered her basket and went up the stairs, and Paloma stood at his desk for a long moment, listening to the building breathe.

Then the telephone rang. Room 14—Tomás Varga's room, booked under a name that wasn't his, paid in cash, three nights running. Paloma lifted the receiver.

The voice on the other end was slurred, ragged, and terrified, and it said only this:

"Concierge. Come up. Before I lose my nerve again. I wrote one of those letters—and I need to tell you which one."
