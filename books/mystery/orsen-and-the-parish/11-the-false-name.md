## 11. The False Name

The name had been sitting in Paloma's mind for three days, the way a stone sits in a shoe—small, ignorable, and impossible to forget once noticed.

*Marisol Etchegaray.*

He had written it out on the back of a laundry slip in his careful concierge's hand, the hand that could render a guest's name legible from across a lobby or illegible to anyone but himself, depending on what the occasion required. He looked at it now across the little desk drawer he used as an office, beneath the flickering bulb that management had promised twice to replace.

The ledger entry was right there in his own memory as much as on the page. Room 214, three nights, paid cash, registered under *M. Etchegaray*. A woman of about sixty, "distinguished, dark coat, asked for extra hangers." He remembered her because she had tipped in old bills, crisp ones, the kind people keep when they don't trust banks—and because she had asked him, on her last morning, whether the reservoir road flooded often in autumn.

He had told her it did. He had thought nothing of it then. He thought a great deal of it now.

The question was not who M. Etchegaray had been at the Meridian. The question was who Marisol Etchegaray had been everywhere else.

* * *

The municipal archive occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, a building that had once decided which goods were legal and now decided which facts were, which Paloma found a distinction without much difference. The archivist, Señor Iriarte, was a man shaped like a question mark who had worked there for forty years and regarded every visitor as a personal affront to the documents.

"Etchegaray," Paloma said, setting a box of pastries on the counter, because Iriarte's affront could be negotiated with.

Iriarte looked at the pastries. He looked at Paloma. He looked, with visible suffering, at his ledger. "Civil records are in the east room. Births, marriages, deaths. If you're looking for land disputes, those are Tuesdays."

"I'm looking for a death."

"Aren't we all," said Iriarte, and took two pastries.

The east room smelled of dust and glue and the particular melancholy of paper that no one had loved in a long time. Paloma pulled the death registers for the relevant decades and began to walk backward through other people's endings, which was, he reflected, more or less his profession.

He found her in the register for 1961.

*Marisol Etchegaray, aged nineteen, cause of death: drowning, location: the Varga reservoir.*

Paloma sat down on the stool quite slowly, the way a man does when the floor has quietly changed its mind about holding him.

Nineteen years old. Drowned. In the same water, in the same season, that would take Ines Varga some fifty years later. And Ines Varga—the sainted benefactor, the parish's marble widow, the woman whose portrait hung in the nave of San Ildefonso with gilt around it like a halo that had gotten ambitious—had checked into his hotel under this dead girl's name three days before she herself went into that same reservoir.

He read the entry again. Then he read the column beside it, the one recording the informant for the death certificate, and felt the cold arrive in his hands before it arrived anywhere else.

*Informant: E. Varga.*

E. Varga. Emilio's father? Or Emilio himself, then a young man learning early how signatures work? Either way: a Varga had stood at this counter, half a century ago, and reported the drowning of a nineteen-year-old girl at the family reservoir, and the parish had shrugged and moved on, because in those days a servant girl drowning at the rich family's water was not a mystery. It was a Tuesday.

Paloma copied the entry in full, his handwriting steady, which surprised him. Inside, nothing was steady. The founding story of San Ildefonso—the Vargas as its benefactors, its protectors, its beloved first family—had just developed a crack running straight through the middle of it, and through the crack he could hear water.

* * *

He took the bus out to the parish records office next, because if Marisol Etchegaray had died at nineteen, she had likely also been baptized at a few days old, and baptisms came with parents, and parents came with descendants, and descendants were the sort of thing that did not simply evaporate.

The parish secretary, a cheerful woman named Amparo who treated every request as an opportunity for conversation, found the baptismal register while telling him about her hip. "Etchegaray, Etchegaray... here. Marisol, daughter of Joaquín Etchegaray and his wife Carmen. Farmers, up past the mill road. Poor as church mice, God love them." She tapped the page. "They had another girl, older. Lucia. And after Marisol died, well—" Amparo crossed herself with the efficiency of long practice. "The mother never recovered. They moved away. Sold everything. There was talk the Vargas settled money on them, quiet-like. Blood money, some said, though nobody said it loud."

"And the sister?" Paloma asked. "Lucia?"

Amparo flipped forward through the register, then backward, frowning. "Married, I'd guess. Here—Lucia Etchegaray, married 1958, to a..." She squinted. "To a Delgado. Left the parish after the sister drowned, same as the parents. But children—" she ran her finger down the births, "—yes. Two. A boy and a girl. The girl born 1960."

Born 1960. Which made her, today, a woman in her sixties. A woman with grandchildren, possibly. A woman whose grandmother's name had been worn like a coat by the richest lady in the parish, right up until the richest lady went into the water wearing—what color had Tomás said the coat was, in the letter only the killer could have known?

Dark. The coat had been dark. The same description, entered in Paloma's own ledger, fifty years apart, like a sentence repeating itself.

"Thank you, Amparo," Paloma said, and meant it, and gave her a pastry he had been saving for Iriarte, who would survive the betrayal.

* * *

He walked back through the parish square slowly, letting the town look at him without seeing him, which was its habit and his craft. The fountain, the café awnings, the noticeboard with the anniversary dinner announcement going soft and curling at the corners—ten years since we lost our beloved Ines, all welcome, dress code: Sunday best.

*If Ines's identity was false,* he thought, *what else is?*

It was not a comfortable question, because questions like that do not stay politely contained. It got into everything. If the matriarch had been walking around under a dead girl's name—if she had needed, late in life, to become someone else even for three nights in a hotel—then the whole edifice of the parish's gratitude was built on ground that had never been surveyed. The restoration of the church roof: Varga money. The school wing: Varga money. The fountain itself, with its fat stone cherub: Varga money, all of it laundered through generations of civic affection, and now perhaps all of it rooted in something that happened at a reservoir in 1961, when a nineteen-year-old girl went into the water and a powerful family signed the paperwork and everyone agreed, in the way towns agree, to remember nothing.

And Ines—Ines who had grown old inside that agreement, Ines who had spent fifty years being the saint of it—had gone, at the end, to a hotel desk and borrowed the victim's name. Why? Guilt? Fear? Or something worse: preparation. A woman rehearsing her own disappearance, testing the name she might need, the way you test a fire escape.

Three days later she was dead, and the name had done its work, and only a concierge who remembered everything and was remembered by no one stood between the parish and its pretty story forever.

Except someone else knew. Someone had known enough to write him letters. Someone had chosen *him*—the man who checked her in, the last ordinary person to speak with Ines Varga—and that thought, which had been circling him for days like a gull over a fishing boat, finally landed.

Whoever wrote those letters knew about Room 214. Knew about the false registration. Which meant they knew the hotel's books—or knew the person who kept them.

Paloma stopped walking. In the middle of the square, among the pigeons and the afternoon light, he stood very still, because there was exactly one person who had worked the Meridian longer than he had, who knew its ledgers the way priests know their psalms, and who had been asking him lately, gently, persistently, what he was reading so hard in the old registers.

Marguerite. Head housekeeper for twenty-two years. Keeper of keys, linens, and—as of this moment, in Paloma's rearranging mind—every secret the Meridian had ever swallowed.

He told himself there were a hundred explanations. He told himself that suspicion was a solvent and dissolved everything it touched, including the innocent. He told himself these things while his feet carried him, unbidden, back toward the hotel, faster than walking, nearly running, a concierge abandoning decorum in broad daylight.

At the staff entrance he paused, hand on the door, and made himself breathe. Whatever waited inside, he would meet it as himself—not the invisible man, not the shadow at the desk, but Paloma Sandoval, who had a name and, as of this afternoon, a use for it.

He pushed the door open. The corridor smelled of floor wax and lilies—the anniversary flowers, already arriving—and at the far end, standing at the open door of the records room where the old ledgers lived, her back to him, perfectly still, was Marguerite.
