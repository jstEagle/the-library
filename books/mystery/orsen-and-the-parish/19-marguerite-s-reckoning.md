## 19. Marguerite's Reckoning

The trouble with knowing everything about a hotel, Paloma had long ago decided, was that the hotel eventually knew it back.

He felt that now, standing in the linen room on the fourth floor at half past eleven at night, holding a lamp that flickered like a nervous witness. Marguerite had left him a note — of course she had; notes were becoming their love language, if love language meant mutual suspicion folded into thirds — tucked under his door in handwriting he now recognized as the second hand from the letters. *Fourth floor. Bring your questions. I'll bring my answers.*

It was not an invitation. It was a summons. And Paloma Sandoval, who had spent forty years perfecting the art of being exactly where he wasn't needed, found himself walking toward the one place he was most needed, and hating how natural it felt.

The linen room smelled of starch and lavender and, faintly, of bleach doing its honest work against the sins of guests. Marguerite stood among the shelves of folded sheets like a woman who had been waiting there her whole life — which, he supposed, she had.

"You came," she said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you would. Knowing is different." She smiled, and it was the smile he'd seen ten thousand times across the lobby: warm, competent, entirely opaque. "You want tea? I brought the good kind. The kind I don't put out for guests."

"That depends," Paloma said, "on whether this is an interrogation or a recruitment."

Marguerite poured two cups anyway. "Can't it be both?"

* * *

From the outside, Marguerite thought, he looked exactly as he always did: a small man in a small vest, holding a small cup of tea as though it were evidence. But she had watched him for years through the wrong end of the telescope — the concierge nobody noticed, noticing everybody — and she knew what lived behind those mild eyes. She'd known since the third letter. He hadn't gone to the police. He hadn't gone to Emilio Varga, whose velvet threats could have bought his silence for the price of a lunch. He had gone to the ledgers. Her ledgers, as it turned out. The girl's name in the old register, the dead girl's name, followed all the way down to its roots.

"You read the will," she said. "The sexton's copy."

"I read the will."

"Then you know Ines Varga died with my grandmother's name in her mouth and my family's money in her accounts." Marguerite set her cup down with a click that sounded, to her own ears, like a gavel. "Sixty years ago, a girl named Aurelia Mendieta worked herself to death in a house that bore her name on no paper anywhere. Ines took that name when it suited her — took it the way you take a coat from a cloakroom, without asking, without intending to return it. And then she built a parish on top of it. A saint, they call her. They hung her portrait in the nave."

"They did."

"And you've been looking at that portrait for two weeks wondering what I see when I look at it."

Paloma considered his tea. "I've been wondering what you see," he admitted, "since before I knew there was anything to see. You dust around it every Tuesday. You never look up."

"Because I already know what's up there." Her voice stayed level, which cost her something; she let him hear the cost. "A lie wearing a shawl."

* * *

"So you killed her," Paloma said. Not a question. Questions gave people room to maneuver, and after nineteen days of this case he had developed a deep professional aversion to giving anyone room.

"In the reservoir, yes." Marguerite said it the way she reported a broken elevator: factually, with an apology for the inconvenience. "She slipped first. I'll always be able to say that honestly. She slipped, and I held her under, and the difference between those two things is the whole of my soul, and I've made my peace with the arithmetic."

"Ten years ago."

"Ten years ago. And the sexton last week, because he sold silence to Renata Varga for a decade and then, when a stranger started asking questions, tried to sell me too. I don't buy what's already been sold." She tilted her head. "You're not horrified. I expected more horror. There's usually more horror."

"I'm rationing it," Paloma said. "I find if I spend it all at once I have none left for the important parts."

* * *

There it was again, Marguerite thought — that dry little deflection, the humor of a man who kept his feelings in a locked drawer the way she kept her grandmother's photograph. She had planned this conversation for years, rehearsed it in the steam of a hundred ironing boards, and in every rehearsal he flinched. In none of them did he make jokes about rationing.

"The letters," he said. "Both of them. Yours."

"The confession, yes. The threat, yes. Different hands because I write differently when I'm being different people — a skill I recommend; it keeps the mind supple." She counted them off on her fingers, housekeeper's fingers, scarred by decades of other people's silverware. "The confession went to you because I needed someone clever to start pulling threads, and the police here couldn't find thread with both hands and a map. The threat went to you because fear makes men fast, and I needed you fast. And the anniversary dinner—"

"—is the stage," Paloma finished. "You're going to poison the toast."

"I'm going to poison *a* toast," Marguerite corrected gently. "Details matter. You taught me that, actually, though you don't know it. Ten years I've watched you leave clues for the police — the umbrella stand business, the thing with the ferry tickets — anonymous little masterpieces. You never wanted credit. You wanted *correction*. The world slightly righted, and yourself nowhere in the picture." She leaned forward. "I'm offering you the biggest correction of your life, Paloma. The Vargas' fortune exposed. The forged will read aloud. Emilio's bribes, Renata's blackmail payments, the whole beautiful rotting edifice brought into the light where it can finally stink properly. All you have to do is let one glass of wine do its work, and afterward, the murders stay between us. Two professionals. A clean division of labor: you take the corruption, I keep the bodies."

* * *

Paloma looked at her for a long moment, and what he felt was not temptation, exactly. It was worse. It was *recognition*.

Because he understood the offer. That was the humiliating part. He understood it the way a man understands a mirror — every line of it drawn by a hand like his own. Patience weaponized. Invisibility weaponized. The conviction that the world is a badly kept ledger and someone must balance it, quietly, without thanks. If he had been born angrier, he thought, he might be standing on her side of the linen shelf.

And that was precisely why he couldn't say yes.

"No," he said.

Marguerite didn't move. "No?"

"It's a generous offer. Genuinely. You've thought of everything — you always do; I've admired your inventory rotations for years." He set down the cup. "But you've made one accounting error."

"Enlighten me."

"You think justice is a subtraction. Remove the guilty, and the sum comes out clean." He shook his head. "Justice is a *rebalancing*. Everything on the table. Your grandmother's name restored — yes, absolutely, that must happen, and I will fight for it. But Ines's murder confessed in daylight, not dissolved in a wineglass. Renata's blackmail answered for, not buried under a corpse. Even Emilio. Especially Emilio." He paused. "If the toast dies tonight, the parish gets a new saint to worship and a new secret to keep, and San Ildefonso goes on rehearsing lies until they feel like memory. I've seen that play. It has a very long run."

"And if you expose everything?" Marguerite's voice had cooled several degrees. "The estate goes to court, the lawyers eat for a decade, and Aurelia Mendieta's descendants get a fraction and an apology printed on the inside page. That's your rebalancing?"

"It's the truth," Paloma said. "Truth is the only currency that doesn't lose value when you spend it."

"What a lovely motto for a man who's spent his whole life counterfeiting himself." She stood, and the linen room seemed to shrink around her anger, starched and white and terrible. "I offered you partnership. Do you understand what I offered? I could have simply added you to the list."

"Yes," Paloma said quietly. "That's why I brought this."

He reached into his vest pocket and produced, of all things, a pen. Marguerite stared at it.

"A pen," she repeated.

"Look closer."

She did — and went very still. The barrel was scratched, the cap chewed, the clip bent from years of pocket service. It was the pen from the front desk. *His* pen. The one that had signed in every guest ledger, every delivery slip, every small transaction of the Hotel Meridian for twenty years.

"You switched the pens," she said slowly. "At the desk. When I checked the wine shipment in yesterday."

"I switch the pens every day," Paloma said. "Different ink, different hand, depending on what needs signing. You taught me that too, Marguerite — though I learned it thirty years before you did." He turned the pen in his fingers. "Yesterday's signature on the wine manifest isn't mine. It's yours. And the bottle you selected for the toast has been in my possession since four o'clock, along with a very polite note to the diocesan inspector, sealed and dated, explaining exactly where to find it if anything happens to me between now and Saturday."

* * *

Marguerite sat back down. Not because her legs failed her — she told herself firmly that her legs had not failed her — but because sitting gave her time, and time was the only luxury she had ever allowed herself.

"You anticipated me," she said.

"I anticipated *the plan*. The plan was excellent. The flaw was assuming I'm a piece on the board rather than the person who polishes the board." He almost smiled. "You should know that compliment cost me something. I've been saving it."

For a moment — one strange, suspended moment — Marguerite laughed. It escaped her before she could catch it, short and startled, the laugh of a woman surprised by her own amusement, and she hated how good it felt. Ten years of solitary precision, and the only man who truly saw her work was the one dismantling it.

"You're going to stop me tomorrow night," she said. "In front of everyone."

"I'm going to stop the toast," Paloma said. "What happens after that is up to the truth, and the truth has better lawyers than either of us."

"And me?"

Paloma rose, straightened his vest, and became once more the unremarkable concierge, the man made of furniture and lamplight. But at the door he paused, and when he spoke his voice was gentle in a way that frightened her more than any threat could have.

"You asked what I see when I look at Ines's portrait. Here's what I see when I look at you, Marguerite. I see a woman who waited ten years to become an instrument of her grandmother's grief — and instruments break, and are discarded, and the grief remains. Aurelia Mendieta deserved better than revenge. She deserved her *name*. Tomorrow night, she'll get it. What you get—" he opened the door onto the dark corridor, "—depends on what you do when the glass is raised. There will be a moment. Everyone gets one. Choose well."

* * *

Alone in the linen room, Marguerite stood among the folded sheets for a long time.

The backup vial was still sewn into the hem of her uniform — the second dose, the contingency, the habit of a decade. She pressed her thumb against the seam and felt the small glass heart of it, patient as she had always been patient.

Tomorrow the parish would gather. Tomorrow Emilio Varga would rise with the heirloom wine and rehearse his grief for the cameras of respectability. Tomorrow, unless she chose otherwise, Paloma Sandoval would stand up in front of three hundred people and unmake himself forever — the invisible man, choosing to be seen, betting everything that visibility was stronger than vengeance.

She could still beat him to it. One gesture at the table. One swallow in the wrong glass. She had done harder things with less warning.

But she kept hearing the door click shut behind him, and kept hearing, underneath the click, the sound of a man who had looked at the worst thing she had ever done and offered her, of all things, a *moment*.

Marguerite took out her grandmother's photograph — Aurelia, young, squinting into a sun that owed her nothing — and held it under the lamp.

"Tomorrow," she told the girl in the picture, "you get your name back. I promise you that much. As for the rest—"

She blew out the lamp.

Downstairs, in the lobby, the anniversary wine waited in its crate, and Paloma Sandoval stood at the front desk signing the morning's deliveries with a brand-new pen, practicing, over and over, the one signature he had avoided his entire life: his own.
