## 1. Paid in Advance

The fluorescent light over Freya Vasquez's desk had been dying for three weeks, flickering in a rhythm she'd come to think of as sarcastic. She was stamping the last of the day's paperwork — an elderly man from Millbrook, natural causes, no surprises — when she heard footsteps on the stairs outside her office. Slow ones. The kind that belonged to someone who'd stood at the bottom of those stairs for a while before deciding to climb them.

Hollow Creek's morgue occupied the basement of the municipal building, which everyone agreed was grim and which Freya privately appreciated. Dead things below, living things above, and a clear line between them. She liked lines. Lines were what kept a town like this one honest.

Or so she'd believed for seven years.

"Closed," she called out, without looking up. "Come back at eight tomorrow."

The door opened anyway.

He was tall and narrow, the way furniture gets narrow when it's been left in a damp room too long. Forty-something, maybe, though his skin had the bloodless quality of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. He wore a good coat — wool, expensive — but he wore it wrong, buttoned crooked, like he'd dressed in the dark or with his hands shaking. His eyes went around the room quickly: the steel table, the filing cabinets, the wall of drawers where Hollow Creek kept its dead until somebody claimed them.

Then they came back to her, and she saw what he'd been carrying up those stairs. Not fear exactly. Fear was loud. This was quieter and worse — the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"You're the coroner," he said. "Vasquez."

"I'm the coroner." She set down her stamp. "And we're closed."

"My name is Elias Crane." He said it like he was testing whether the name still fit. "I need to hire you."

"The county handles death investigations through my office during business hours. If you've lost someone—"

"I haven't lost anyone." He took a step closer and stopped himself, as if he'd caught the movement on film and found it incriminating. "That's rather the point. I need you to prove I haven't lost *me*."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. In eleven years as coroner — six of them here, in a town so small the job was half pathology and half listening to people cry about their dogs — she had heard every variety of grief and most varieties of madness. This was neither. This was something she didn't have a drawer for yet.

"Sit down, Mr. Crane."

He sat. Perched, really, on the edge of the plastic chair across from her desk, knees together, hands wrapped around a leather portfolio he held like a life vest.

"Start from the beginning," she said. "Plainly. I deal in facts."

"Yes." He swallowed. "Yes. Facts." A breath. "Three days ago, the town archive was having some water damage repaired. A contractor opened a section of wainscoting on the second floor — the old reading room — and found a cavity behind it. Sealed since sometime in the last century, by the looks of the mortar. Inside was a painting."

"A painting."

"An oil portrait. Quite old. Quite good, actually." His mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "It depicts a man half-submerged in dark water. Only his head and one hand above the surface. And the face—"

He stopped. Freya waited. The fluorescent light buzzed its sarcastic rhythm overhead.

"The face is mine," Crane said. "Not similar. Not suggestive. It's mine. The same scar over the left eyebrow I've had since I was nine years old. The same — I don't know how a painter would know this — the same way my hair falls when it's wet. It isn't a portrait of me as I am. It's a portrait of me *drowning*."

The room felt colder than usual. Freya told herself it was the basement.

"So you're telling me someone painted your death before it happened."

"I'm telling you someone painted my death *in detail*, sealed it behind a wall in a building older than my grandfather, and that I have spent three days trying to find another explanation and cannot." His voice cracked on the last word and he pressed his lips together, furious at himself, then continued more quietly. "I came to Hollow Creek four months ago. New accountant for the Hollis firm. Nobody knew me before that. There is no reason on earth for my face to be in that archive."

"People's faces end up in odd places, Mr. Crane. Resemblance is a cruel trick of genetics. And painters lie — it's most of what they do."

"It's not resemblance." He said it flatly, without heat, and somehow that was worse. "I've stood in front of it for hours. Ms. Vasquez, I know my own face."

She leaned back in her chair. Outside, above them, she could hear the muffled thump of the night custodian starting his rounds. Eighteen minutes past closing. She should send him away. Every instinct trained into her by eleven years of clean lines said: send him away, refer him to Dr. Prasad, keep the file tidy.

But there was another thing in her, older and less orderly, and it stirred now the way it always did when someone mentioned the archive, the lake, the past.

"And what exactly," she said, "do you want me to do about it?"

"Prove it hasn't happened yet." He opened the portfolio and set a check on her desk, sliding it across with two fingers, careful not to let go until she touched it. "You're the coroner. If I die — if I drown — it will be your signature on the report. So I want to hire you, in advance, to establish that I am alive. Documented, witnessed, dated. A record that says Elias Crane walked into this office on the fourteenth of October, whole and breathing, and that any subsequent death was not fated, not foretold, not arranged by whatever put that painting in that wall."

"That's not—" She picked up the check despite herself. "This is not how death certification works."

"Read the number."

She read the number. It was triple her annual consulting fee, offered for what amounted to a signed statement and a photograph. Her rent was three months behind. Her truck needed a transmission. There was a version of her life where she said no on principle, and she knew exactly which shelf of herself that principle lived on, and she knew it was dusty.

"This is insane," she said.

"Almost certainly."

"You understand I can't certify a non-death. There's no form for it. Believe me, I've seen all the forms."

"What I understand," Crane said, rising, gathering his coat around him, "is that you haven't thrown the check back at me. Which means part of you believes me, or wants to. Either way — the offer stands. Triple rate, retainer paid in full, and one condition." He paused at the door, and in the bad light his face looked briefly like the painting must have looked: pale, lit from nowhere, already halfway under. "Don't go looking at the portrait itself. Please. Some things are better filed than seen."

* * *

After he left, Freya sat alone in the basement for a long time.

The check lay on her desk where he'd placed it. She didn't move it. She made tea she didn't drink and let it go cold beside the check, and the fluorescent light kept up its dying commentary, and she thought about her brother.

Seven years. Seven years since Mateo's truck was found parked at the boat launch on Lake Verlaine, keys in the ignition, his jacket folded on the passenger seat like a man who expected to come back for it. Seven years since the search dragged the shallows for nine days and found nothing but a silted-over canoe paddle. Seven years since the coroner before her — old Whitcomb, three months from retirement, hands already shaking — signed a report declaring Matthew Vasquez deceased by drowning, body unrecovered, case closed.

She'd read that report forty times. She'd read it again after she got this job, because she'd told herself it was closure, professional curiosity, moving on. She'd noticed the small things even then: the report dated two days before the drag operation ended. The witness statements that all sounded like the same witness wearing different coats. The way Whitcomb's signature sat slightly higher on the line than his handwriting usually climbed, as if someone else had guided his pen.

She'd noticed. And she'd filed it. Because the alternative was believing that the town she'd moved to for the cheap rent and the quiet had eaten her brother and closed its mouth, and she could not live in a place where she believed that. So she'd built the orderly life instead. Drawers and labels and lines between the living and the dead. It worked, mostly. That was the thing nobody told you about grief — you didn't get over it, you just got very good at shelving it, and the shelves held, right up until a pale stranger sat down in your office and started talking about paintings of drownings.

A portrait of a drowning, sealed in a wall. A man certain it was his own death. And underneath the absurdity of it, ticking like a second clock, one question she couldn't put down:

*What if it isn't his face?*

She hadn't said that aloud to anyone in seven years, but she said it now, to the empty room, in a voice she barely recognized.

Because there had been a painter in Hollow Creek once — she remembered it dimly, from before, from the summer Mateo died. An itinerant artist who'd done commissions for the founding families, the Ashcombes especially. And there had been talk, afterward, of work that never got delivered. Work that got stored somewhere. Work that got *filed*.

Freya looked at the check again. Then she opened her bottom drawer, took out a pen, and wrote herself a note on the back of a blank intake form, the way she did when a case wouldn't let her sleep:

*Archive. Second floor. Old reading room. Wainscot.*

She stared at the words. Crane had asked her not to look. He'd paid her triple rate specifically so she wouldn't have to.

Which was, she realized, exactly why she was going to go first thing in the morning — before he could stop her, before Ruthie at the front desk could lose the key, before anyone in this town that never quite finished its sentences could get to it first.

Because Elias Crane had hired her to prove a murder hadn't happened yet.

And seven years ago, nobody had hired anybody to prove anything at all.

Freya turned off the dying light, pocketed the check, and climbed the stairs toward the living world — carrying, for the first time in years, the feeling that something down in the dark had just changed its mind about staying buried.
