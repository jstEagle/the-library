## 10. Filed Under Living

The indictment came down on a Tuesday in October, which felt right. Hollow Creek had always been a town of Tuesdays — nothing dramatic ever happened on a Friday, and the things that mattered arrived without ceremony, in manila envelopes, stamped by clerks who had no idea what they were carrying.

Freya read the charges standing at her office window while the rain came down soft and gray on the municipal parking lot. Conspiracy to commit murder. Obstruction. Fraud, embezzlement, racketeering — the federal prosecutors had unpacked the Ashcombe trust like a suitcase full of dirty laundry, and every item matched something Ruthie's duplicate archive had predicted months ago. Aldous Ashcombe surrendered at his lawyer's office at nine a.m., wearing a good coat. His son Julian was arrested at the marina trying to fuel a boat he'd owned for eleven years but had never once been seen to use.

The *Gazette* ran it above the fold: FOUNDING FAMILY FALLEN. Below that, smaller: *Coroner's testimony cited as pivotal.*

Her mother would have framed that. Freya cut it out instead and put it in a drawer, because some habits were older than grief.

* * *

The portrait came back to her in November.

It had spent the trial in an evidence locker in the county seat, tagged and photographed and argued over by lawyers who called it "the exhibit" as if it hadn't once hung in a walled alcove like a heart someone had tried to bury alive. When the case closed, the clerk mailed it back with a form letter. Freya signed for it at the front desk of the morgue office, which still made her laugh when she thought about it — signing for her brother's face like a package of toner.

She'd had it restored. Not hidden, not resealed behind fresh plaster the way Ruthie had begged her to do that first afternoon, when the paint was still tacky with seven years of silence. A conservator in Ashford worked on it for six weeks. When Freya went to collect it, the woman turned it around on the easel and said, "You'll want to know I didn't change much. Just the water."

"Just the water," Freya repeated.

"It's how these things work." The conservator tapped the lower third of the canvas, where dark lake had once swallowed the painted boy to his chest. "Whoever painted over this section did it later — different hand, different decade, you can see it under the raking light. Someone wanted him drowning. So I asked myself what the original wanted, and the original wanted light." She'd lifted the black water away layer by layer, and underneath found shallows, sunlit, the boy standing hip-deep and unafraid, one hand raised against the glare as if waving at someone on shore.

Mateo, alive, painted over with light instead of water. It sounded like a sentence from one of those novels Ruthie read, but there it was, hanging now on the wall beside Freya's desk where the diploma used to go. She'd moved the diploma to the bathroom. It seemed more honest there.

Visitors noticed it. They always noticed it. Deputy Parrish stood in front of it for a full minute during his first visit after the trial and finally said, "That's the kid from the lake, isn't it. The one from the file."

"That's my brother," Freya said. "He comes by on Thursdays."

* * *

Thursdays had become a thing without anyone deciding it should be. Mateo came by the cemetery first — old habit, Sam said, and Sam would know, having spent seven years leaving the gate unlatched and looking the other way with the professionalism of a man who had turned discretion into a skilled trade. Then Mateo walked down to the office, usually with coffee, sometimes with nothing but himself, and sat in the chair across from her desk like a man serving out a sentence he'd volunteered for.

They were learning each other again, and it was slower work than either of them admitted. Grief had built rooms in Freya that joy didn't know how to furnish. There were evenings she looked at him across a table at Sam's kitchen and felt the vertigo come back — the same vertigo from the archive, the day she pried open the wainscot and found the past refusing to stay filed. He was here. He was here. Her mind kept checking the fact like a lock, testing the handle.

"You could stop doing that," he said once, catching her staring.

"Doing what?"

"Making sure I'm real."

"I'm a coroner, Mateo. Verification is the whole job."

He laughed, and the sound went through her like weather. Seven years she'd imagined his voice — in dreams, in the bad first year, in the better years when she'd trained herself not to imagine it at all — and none of her versions had gotten the laugh right. His actual laugh had a hitch in it, a small broken gear near the top. She intended to hear it so many times that the hitch became the most familiar thing in the world.

He was working at the hardware store now, under a name that wasn't his, though Elias Crane — former accountant, current whistleblower, current houseguest of Sam's until the apartment above the pharmacy opened up — kept insisting he could teach Mateo bookkeeping, "for when it's safe." Mateo always said maybe. Freya understood the maybe. Safety was a language he'd have to learn to trust again, word by word, the way she was learning to believe in Thursdays.

* * *

In December, she started the new filing system.

It began as a practical matter. The trial had generated paper the way the lake generated fog, and the old cabinets — the ones she'd inherited from her predecessor, the ones whose drawers had held doctored reports and impossible signatures — were full past capacity. She could have ordered new cabinets. Instead, on a slow Saturday with snow ticking against the windows, she emptied all four drawers onto the floor and started over.

She sorted by hand. Every case, every report, every photograph and coroner's finding going back thirty years, laid out in drifts across the morgue floor like the town's whole memory spread open for inspection. She found the falsified drowning report with her predecessor's signature and the second signature beneath it, the one that had started everything, and she did not throw it away. Evidence of a crime stayed evidence forever; that was the point of her. But she moved it into a box marked CLOSED — REVIEWED, and wrote on the lid, in her own handwriting, the date the truth came out. Let the record show both things: the lie, and the day the lie died.

Ruthie came by with sandwiches around noon and helped sort, because Ruthie could no more stay away from an archiving project than the lake could stay away from the shore. Together they rebuilt the system: by name, then by year, then by outcome. It was Ruthie who suggested the last drawer.

"You'll want somewhere to put the people who aren't cases," she said. "The ones who are just... ongoing."

Freya looked at the empty fourth drawer. She got a label maker from the supply closet — the cheap one, the one that printed letters slightly crooked — and thought about what to call it. ACTIVE. OPEN. PENDING. All true words, all wrong words. They belonged to the language of waiting, and she was done waiting.

She typed LIVING and stuck the label on straight.

Into the drawer went the photograph of the two of them as children, the one Mateo had dropped in the cemetery grass seven years after losing the right to carry it. Into the drawer went the newspaper clipping. The restored portrait's original inventory tag, which the conservator had sent along like a birth certificate. A receipt from the hardware store, dated the week Mateo started work — proof of employment, proof of presence, proof of a Tuesday he'd shown up and been paid for. Sam's recipe for pickled beets, which Sam had written out for her at Thanksgiving and which had no earthly business in a filing cabinet except that Sam was family now too, and family went in the drawer marked LIVING.

Ruthie watched her close it and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, pretending it was the dust.

* * *

On the last Thursday of the year, Mateo brought flowers.

Not for the unmarked plot — though they stopped there first, the two of them, standing in the cold over the grave that had held only a story. He'd bought a proper stone in the fall, and it read MATEO VASQUEZ, 1989–2016, BELOVED BROTHER, and beneath that, smaller, in letters Freya had chosen: *He got out of the water.* People in town assumed it was a memorial. Only four living souls knew it was a victory marker.

Then he carried the rest of the bouquet — marigolds, out of season, absurd, from the grocery store — down to her office and set them in a jar on her desk, next to the portrait, where the painted boy stood hip-deep in sunlight with his hand raised against the glare.

"For the drawer?" he asked.

"The drawer doesn't take flowers."

"What does it take?"

Freya considered. Outside, the snow had stopped and the light was doing that thing Hollow Creek did in late December, turning everything briefly gold, even the parking lot, even the county road sign, even the town itself — this small, compromised, stubborn place that had lied for seven years and then, given the chance, had chosen to tell the truth through the mouths of a gravedigger, an archivist, a frightened accountant, and a coroner who took money she shouldn't have from a man who wasn't lying about anything except his name.

"It takes time," she said. "It takes showing up. You're getting good at it."

He smiled, and the hitch in his laugh caught, and she checked — just once, quickly, the way she always would — that he was real.

He was. Filed accordingly.

She was reaching for her coat when the phone rang. She almost let it go. But it was the desk line, the after-hours line, and after-hours calls in Hollow Creek had a way of mattering.

"Dr. Vasquez?" The night dispatcher sounded young and uncertain. "We've got a body out at the reservoir. Well — we've got a person. Alive. Says he walked out of the woods, says he hasn't eaten in three days, says—" a pause, papers shuffling, "—says you'd know who he is. Says to tell you the water kept its promise."

Freya looked at her brother. Her brother looked back.

She picked up a pen, opened the drawer marked LIVING, and reached for a blank form.
