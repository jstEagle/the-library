## 3. What the Town Swears By

By morning, Marrowfen had decided what to think, and it had decided without me.

I went out at nine with a notebook in my coat pocket — the gray coat, the one they'd pulled off a dead man in the crypt, though the police had returned it to me with an apology that felt more like a warning. *We'll be in touch, Mr. Vane. Best not to leave town.* As if I were the suspect. As if the body hadn't been wearing my clothes precisely so that someone, someday, would look at me this way.

The baker on Cordwainer Street was first. Mrs. Tallow, flour to the elbows, a woman who had sold me rye bread every Saturday for six years.

"Terrible business," she said, before I'd finished my second sentence. "But the Verdict settled all that."

"Settled what, exactly?"

She wiped her hands on her apron with the slow deliberation of someone buying time. "Whatever needed settling. The cathedral saw to it. Canon Wren was dealt with according to law, and Marrowfen moved on. We're a town that moves on." She said it smoothly, completely, like a line learned for a school recital. Then she smiled and asked if I wanted the usual.

I said yes. I don't know why. Habit, maybe. Or the strange cowardice of a man standing in a bakery realizing everyone else has been given a script he never received.

* * *

I tried eleven people that day. I kept count because counting was something I could still trust.

The fishmonger: "The Verdict settled all that."

The woman who repairs the cathedral's vestments: "Canon Wren was dealt with according to law."

A young constable outside the market hall, barely old enough to shave: "Marrowfen is a town that moves on."

Eleven people. Three sentences. Not similar phrasing, not the loose echo of shared opinion — identical. Word for word, pause for pause, as if they'd all been handed the same card and told their peace depended on reciting it. When I pressed past the script — *what was Wren convicted of? what year? who sat in judgment?* — the same thing happened every time. A flicker behind the eyes, like a lamp guttering. Then the smile again, warm and vacant, and some variation of: "You were there, Mr. Vane. You'd know better than any of us."

That last part was true, apparently. That was the horror of it. According to the fishmonger, according to the constable, according to a retired schoolmistress who patted my hand as though comforting a child, I had been central to it all. A witness. Perhaps *the* witness. And I could no sooner remember my own testimony than I could remember being born.

By four o'clock I was standing on the bridge over the Fen with my notebook open, reading back what I'd written, and the words swam. Eleven interviews, three sentences, zero answers. Below me the water carried leaves toward the sea, indifferent, and I thought: this is what it must be like to be mad. To be the only person in a town who hears the wrong words coming out of everyone's mouth.

There was one name left. Tam Hobb, the old verger, who had swept the cathedral stones for forty years and been pensioned off two winters ago. He lived above the boat shed on the fen's edge, and unlike the others, he had nothing left to protect. That, I reasoned, might make him honest. It might also make him dangerous. In Marrowfen those had begun to look like the same quality.

* * *

Tam Hobb answered his door already talking, as if my knock were merely the latest verse in a conversation he'd been holding with himself for years.

"They sent you, did they?" He was small and bent as a question mark, eyes pale as pond ice. "No. No, they wouldn't send you. They'd send anyone but you." He looked me over. "You're the watchmaker. The one whose coat came back wrong."

"Came back wrong?"

"Worn by a dead man." He stepped aside to let me in. "Come in before someone sees you on my step. They count steps now, they do."

The room smelled of lamp oil and dried lavender. On the table, a chess set mid-game against itself. He poured tea without asking and sat across from me, and for a long moment just studied my face like a man checking a clock for tampering.

"I asked around today," I said. "About the Verdict."

"And?"

"Everyone says the same three things. Exactly the same."

Hobb nodded slowly, unsurprised, and this frightened me more than anything the baker had said. "Rehearsed," he said. "Not rehearsed like actors. Rehearsed like prayers. You say a thing enough times, in enough voices, and it stops being a lie and starts being a creed." He sipped his tea. "Whoever taught them those lines knew what he was doing. Knew that a town doesn't need to be threatened. It needs to be *given* its lines."

"You know who taught them?"

The old man's teacup trembled, just slightly, against its saucer. "I know what I know from sweeping floors. Floors hear everything. People talk over the man with the broom like he's furniture." He set the cup down. "You want to ask about Canon Wren. Everyone does, eventually, and then they stop asking, and then they sleep well. That's how it goes here."

"He died in prison. That's what Elodie Ravensworth found in the records."

"Died in prison," Hobb agreed. "Innocent."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and I waited for the splash of qualification, the *so they say*, the *allegedly*. It didn't come.

"You can't know that," I said.

"I changed his linens for eight years, boy. Prisoners get visits; he got none. Prisoners get letters; he got silence. An innocent man waits for the world to correct itself. A guilty man works at being forgiven. Aldous Wren waited until his heart stopped waiting for him." His voice didn't rise at all, which made it worse. "And do you know the worst of it? Half the town suspected. Suspected for years. But suspicion is heavy, and peace is light, and people carry what's easy."

"Why are you telling me this? Everyone else—"

"Everyone else has something to lose." He leaned forward. "I've got a pension and a bad hip. But you—" he pointed a knuckled finger at my chest, "—you're the one they built it all on. Whatever the Verdict stands on, it stands on your word. And now a dead man turns up in your coat, in a crypt that's supposed to be sealed, and somebody out there is telling you a story. Question is whether it's a confession or a frame."

"A frame of what?"

"That's the wrong question." He smiled, and it was a terrible, kind smile. "The right one is: what did you see, Orsen Vane? Because they went to great trouble to make sure you'd say whatever they needed. And men who edit a witness once—" he tapped the table twice, "—edit him twice."

I walked back along the fen path in the last of the light, and the water was the color of pewter, and I caught myself doing something I hadn't done since childhood: narrating my own steps aloud, just to prove the day had happened. *Bridge. Gate. Lane. Door.*

My door stood open six inches.

* * *

They had been tidy about it. That was the first thing I understood, standing in my own hallway with my heart going like a runaway escapement. Nothing smashed. Drawers opened and closed properly, cushions replaced with their seams aligned, even the dust disturbed only where hands had been. A burglary conducted by an archivist, or a surgeon.

They had gone through everything. My tools were untouched — the fine jeweler's screwdrivers, the loupe, the trays of gears laid out in their foam slots exactly as I'd left them. My ledgers of repair work, untouched. Money untouched. Even the small tin where I keep emergency cash, sitting on the shelf like bait, unopened.

Only one thing missing.

The photographs.

I keep them in a shoebox on top of the wardrobe — photographs of my parents, of the shop's opening day, of a picnic at the fen I have no memory of attending but appear in, laughing, arm slung around a man whose face I've never been able to place. Photographs are supposed to be the anchors of a life. Mine had become evidence, and someone had come to collect it.

I sat down on the floor of my flat amid the neat, respectful absence of my own history and laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it at all. First the memories go. Now the pictures. Whoever was working on me was thorough — erasing the record and then erasing the proof the record existed.

On the wardrobe, where the box had been, lay a single sheet of paper, folded once. My handwriting. I recognized the slope of it, the way I cross my sevens, though I had no memory of writing it.

It read: *Stop asking the town. Ask the archive. E.R. knows which season is missing.*

E.R. Elodie Ravensworth.

I turned the note over. Blank. I held it under the lamp, looking for a watermark, a fingerprint, anything. Just paper, just my hand, just words I couldn't remember setting down.

Outside, along the lane, a church bell rang the hour — nine strokes, unhurried, the sound the whole town set its clocks by, the sound I had repaired the mechanism for two springs ago and never thought twice about.

Nine o'clock. Tomorrow I would find Elodie. Tomorrow I would ask her about a missing season.

Tonight I locked my door, sat facing it with the lamp lit, and understood, finally and completely, that I was not investigating a murder.

I was being *managed*. And somewhere in Marrowfen, someone was keeping notes on how well their edits were holding.
