## 9. The Open Crypt

The note was waiting on my workbench when I came up from the shop at dusk, weighted under the brass gear I'd been cleaning that morning — a gear I had no memory of cleaning.

*The crypt. Midnight. Come alone and you'll leave with everything you lost.*

It was written in my own handwriting.

I want to be precise about what that did to me, because precision is all I have left. It wasn't fear, not first. It was the watchmaker's flinch — the moment you open a case you've repaired a hundred times and find a part inside that doesn't belong to any movement you know. My hand had made these letters. The loop of the *y*, the way I cross my *t*s low and hurried, the little hook on the *f*. No one forges that. No one bothers. And yet I could not remember writing it, which meant one of two things: either someone had learned to counterfeit the last uncounterfeitable thing I owned, or I had written it in one of the gaps — one of the stolen hours where I was apparently still walking around Marrowfen, still buying bread, still shaking hands with neighbors, a man-shaped hole wearing my coat.

Both possibilities were unbearable. I chose the one with a destination.

I told myself I'd bring Elodie. I even wrote her a note of my own, folded it into my pocket. Then I stood outside her door above the archive for ten minutes in the cold and couldn't make myself knock. Because if Hale — and by then I was sure it was Hale; who else builds a town out of silence? — if Hale saw her there, she'd become a loose thread too. And I'd seen what happened to loose threads. They ended up in the crypt wearing my clothes.

So I went alone. Which was, I understood as I walked, exactly what the note wanted. A trap doesn't need to be clever when the quarry has been trained for three years to distrust his own judgment.

* * *

Marrowfen Cathedral at midnight is a different building than it is at noon. By day it's the town's best argument for itself — stone and glass and the smug permanence of a place that has outlived every scandal it ever hosted. At night the windows go black, and the nave becomes a throat, and you understand that cathedrals were built to hold two things: God, and whatever the town needed buried.

The crypt door stood open.

That was the first wrongness. For three years that lock had been new — Elodie had found the locksmith's ledger, had traced the replacement order back through a chain of signatures to Corven Hale's own office. The whole mystery had begun with that lock, a sealed door in a town that swore nothing was hidden. Now it hung ajar like a mouth mid-sentence, and from below came a smell of candle wax and old stone and something faintly chemical, like the sharp tang of a darkroom.

I took the stairs down with my hand on the wall. The steps were worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet, and mine fit them the way they're supposed to fit, the way your feet fit a path you've walked before — and I hated that they fit, because how many times had I come down here in the missing hours?

He was waiting among the tombs.

Corven Hale sat on a folding chair — a folding chair, absurdly domestic, set between two sarcophagi like a man attending his own garden party — beside a small table bearing a lamp, a decanter, two glasses, and a leather satchel that I recognized with a lurch as the kind court clerks carry. He wore his magistrate's gray, though he hadn't sat a bench since the Verdict. Some men retire from their offices. Hale had simply stopped needing an audience.

"Mr. Vane," he said warmly. "Punctual. You have no idea what a relief that is. The last man I waited for down here was late, and it made him careless."

"You killed him."

"I did." No hesitation, no flinch. He said it the way a craftsman admits authorship of a good piece of joinery. "Tobias Finch. Clerk of the court for nineteen years. Do you know what he was, Mr. Vane? He was the finest archivist this county ever produced — better than your Miss Ravensworth, though don't tell her, she's doing beautifully with material she can't imagine the half of." He poured two glasses of wine and did not offer me one. "Finch kept the real records. Every session. Every suggestion. Every transcript of what we made you say."

"*Made* me—"

"Oh, sit down. Standing makes men dramatic." He gestured at a second chair I hadn't seen, positioned facing him like a witness box. I didn't sit. He shrugged and continued as if I had. "You want the shape of it. Very well. Three years ago Canon Wren discovered what I'd been doing — quietly, for years — settling disputes before they reached a courtroom. Therapeutic suggestion, we called it in the papers. Witnesses who remembered things more usefully. Juries that arrived pre-convinced. A town at peace, Mr. Vane. Marrowfen hasn't had a riot, a feud, or even a decent lawsuit in a decade. I gave this place calm the way other men give charity. Wren found out and threatened to preach it from the pulpit on Sunday. So Sunday required a different sermon."

"And I testified against him."

"You were the only witness who mattered. You'd seen me leaving the sacristy that night — you were repairing the tower clock, weren't you, always some clock — and you saw me, and you would have said so. So I sat with you for four evenings in my study, very comfortable, very kind, and I read you a story about what you'd really seen. A story with a canon in it instead of a magistrate. And your mind, poor loyal instrument, wrote it down as fact and filed away the truth somewhere even you couldn't reach." He smiled. "You were magnificent on the stand. Detail after detail. Details only the killer could know — everyone said so at the time. They were right. Only the killer could have taught them to you."

The vertigo came again, the one from the tailor's shop, but deeper now, all the way down. I gripped a tomb's cold edge. "Wren died in prison."

"He died innocent," Hale corrected gently. "There's a difference, and it's the difference I've come tonight to discuss."

* * *

"Here is where we find ourselves," Hale said, and he spoke with the serene pride of a man showing off a cathedral he'd personally raised. "Finch kept copies. All these years, insurance. And three weeks ago he got greedy — or sentimental, with Finch it was hard to tell the difference — and started asking me for money, and then for more than money. Restoration of certain names. Public acknowledgment. He wanted to be remembered as a hero, and heroes need villains, so he began sending letters. To your archivist. To you. In handwriting he'd practiced until it could pass for yours — he'd handled your testimony documents for years, he knew your letters better than you did." He tapped the note in my pocket, or rather indicated it, because I hadn't taken it out. "The coat was his idea too. Commissioned from your tailor, copied stitch for stitch. He thought if you saw yourself dead in the crypt, the memories would crack open on their own. He wasn't wrong, actually. He was just early."

"He came to warn us."

"He came to blackmail us. Let's keep our dead honest." Hale rose, unhurried, and lifted the leather satchel from the table. "But yes. His letters are what woke you. And now here is my offer, and I want you to hear it as the generous thing it is."

He opened the satchel. Inside lay a small lacquered case, and in the case, nested in velvet, a hypodermic and a vial of something amber and patient.

"The compound has improved since your sessions. Four evenings then; one evening now. You'll wake tomorrow with no memory of Finch, of the crypt, of Wren, of Miss Ravensworth's archive — none of it. The gaps will close over smooth as poured wax. You'll keep your trade, your flat, your name. You'll be a man with a quiet past, which is the only kind of past this town respects." He held the case out toward me across the tomb. "Or you can refuse, and I will do what I did to Finch, and tomorrow this crypt will hold two secrets instead of one, and I will write you a story in which you simply moved away. I'm very good at stories, Mr. Vane. Ask anyone. Ask yourself."

I looked at the needle. I looked at his face, which held no cruelty at all — that was the terrible thing, there was no cruelty in it, only the mild satisfaction of a man whose system had worked for years and merely required maintenance.

And I thought about the four evenings in his study. About the voice reading aloud in candlelight, my own voice answering, agreeing, learning. About Canon Aldous Wren dying in a cell for a murder that happened in the space between one sentence and the next, while I slept in a chair and let a stranger edit my soul.

"No," I said.

Hale sighed, genuinely regretful, and closed the case with a soft click that sounded louder than the crypt deserved.

"I hoped you'd say that," he said. "It's cleaner this way. Consent complicates the paperwork."

He stepped toward me, and behind him, at the top of the stairwell, the crypt door — which I had left ajar, which I had *sworn* I'd left ajar — began, very slowly, to swing shut.

From beyond it came footsteps. More than one pair. And a woman's voice, level as a ledger line, saying words I will carry in whatever memory remains to me for the rest of my life:

"This session is concluded, Magistrate. Read into the record."
