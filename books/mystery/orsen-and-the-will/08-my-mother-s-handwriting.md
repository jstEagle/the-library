## 8. My Mother's Handwriting

The document examiner came up from the county on a Tuesday that smelled of rain that never fell. Her name was Dr. Ilse Brandt, and she arrived with a portable spectroscope, a magnifying lamp, and the unhurried manner of a woman who had spent thirty years reading the lies people left in ink.

Gideon met her in the coroner's office, which had once been her mother's office, and which still held her mother's filing cabinets, her mother's brass desk lamp with the green shade, her mother's habit of leaving a cup of cold coffee forgotten on the windowsill. Gideon had kept the habit without meaning to. Some mornings she found the cup there and could not remember making it, and she chose, most days, not to examine that too closely.

"Spread it out," Dr. Brandt said, and Gideon laid the will on the long steel table beside the samples she had gathered: a grocery list from 1991 in Vera Amberdale's hand, retrieved from a box Gideon had never been able to throw away; a letter of condolence her mother had written to the Pruitt family in 1998, when Dale's wife passed; the margins of three coroner's logs, annotated in that same slanted, deliberate script, each letter formed with a small, stubborn pressure at the downstroke, like a person pressing her opinion into the paper.

For two hours the office was silent except for the hum of the lamp and the small sounds of Dr. Brandt's work—the whisper of the loupe, the click of the spectroscope, once, a low sound of interest that made Gideon's hands go cold in her lap.

She had known, of course. She had known since the first night, when she'd sat in her kitchen with the will under the green lamp and felt the recognition move through her before her mind could catch it—that particular way the *f* crossed into the *o*, the *y* that ended in a hook rather than a loop, the *G* of her own name written like a key turning. She had grown up watching that hand write birthday cards and prescription slips and, once, a note left on the kitchen table that said *Gone to the river, back by dark, don't wait on supper.* She had grown up inside that handwriting the way you grow up inside a house.

But knowing a thing in the marrow and having it certified are different animals. One is a suspicion you can starve. The other is a fact you must feed for the rest of your life.

Dr. Brandt straightened at last and removed her glasses.

"It's her," Gideon said. Not a question. She had promised herself she would say it first, so that it would be her fact and not a verdict delivered to her.

"It's Vera Amberdale." Dr. Brandt set down the will with something like gentleness. "No question. The letterforms, the pen pressure, the idiosyncratic ligatures—identical across all your samples. But I'll tell you something more interesting, if you want it."

Gideon wanted it the way you want a tooth pulled. "Tell me."

"Paper and ink age." Dr. Brandt tapped the will's first page. "Iron gall ink oxidizes. It darkens over the first months, then stabilizes, then—decades on—it begins to brown at the edges of each stroke, and the paper itself yellows, gets brittle, takes on that smell. This ink is fully stabilized. No browning. The paper is bright, supple, no foxing, no acid migration." She looked up. "This wasn't written thirty years ago. But it wasn't written three weeks ago either, not with this ink. My best estimate—the ink is eight to twelve years old. The document was written a long time ago and *kept*. Stored flat, dark, dry. Someone has been sitting on this for a decade, waiting for a date."

"Waiting for a date," Gideon repeated.

"The will is dated three weeks before the exhumation. Precisely. Whoever wrote it knew the reservoir schedule, or made sure the schedule happened." Dr. Brandt began packing her instruments with the tidy economy of someone closing a door. "Your mother has been dead two years, Ms. Amberdale. But this document was executed in her hand, in her ink, years before she died. Whoever delivered it didn't forge it. They *kept their promise* to her."

* * *

After Dr. Brandt left, Gideon sat alone in the office for a long time with the rain still refusing to fall and the light going the color of old pewter. She did not cry. She was a coroner; she knew the stages of a body's surrender to the truth of it, and grief, in her experience, behaved the same way. There was the first cold hour when the fact lay on the table and you could not look at it directly. There was the hour of inventory—what did it touch, what did it spare, what had it been hiding. And then, if you were any good at your work, there was the hour of reading the body honestly, and letting it tell you what happened.

She read her mother.

The grocery list. *Butter, thread, batteries, the good bread.* The condolence letter, so careful with the Pruitts' grief, so gentle—*Dale was always the steadiest of men; lean on him*—and Gideon thought, with a nausea that arrived like a tide, *she was writing to a conspirator. She was steadying him.* The margins of the logs, where Vera Amberdale had recorded causes of death for thirty years, each entry a small act of public truth, and somewhere in the middle of all that truth, one entry that was a lie, one night in 1994 that her mother had papered over with her own steady hand.

*Forged the death certificate.* The words from her own investigation sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

And then the will. Written eight, ten, twelve years ago—Gideon tried to fix it, tried to remember what her mother had been in those years. Retired by then, or nearly. Walking the river path every morning. Coming to Sunday dinners with a casserole and questions that Gideon had answered absently, half-listening, a grown woman bored by her mother's small talk. *Do you ever think about the Voss grave? What do you suppose is in it? If you had to dig up one thing in this town, what would you choose?*

She had thought her mother was getting strange. She had thought it was age.

It had been a rehearsal. Her mother had been building the machine that would run after her death, checking the gears, asking her daughter the questions that would one day matter, and Gideon had answered them over pot roast without ever looking up.

The will appointed her. That was the part that sat coldest and burned hottest at once. Her mother had not simply left a document; she had left a *summons*, and she had addressed it to her daughter, and she had set a clock on it—forty days, or the county gets the records, or the town drowns twice, once in water and once in truth. Her mother had known Gideon would not be able to walk away. Her mother had *built* her, thirty years of Sunday dinners and coroner's-office Saturdays and *always read the whole chart, Gideon, the body never lies but people do*—she had built a coroner the way you build a key, precisely shaped to open one lock.

*The matter of 1994.*

Her mother had known. Had known for years—had known well enough to write a will about it, date it to the day, seal it in an envelope with her daughter's name, and hand it to someone she trusted to deliver it at the right hour. And that someone had then, apparently, begun killing the people who might have told the story their own way first.

Unless.

Gideon stood, went to the window. The street below was doing its Tuesday business, the hardware store rolling out its sidewalk display, two old men arguing gently outside the diner. Harrow's End, which she had autopsied for a decade and never once cut open.

Her mother had loved this town. That was the unbearable part. Not the town itself—Gideon could have understood ambition, or fear, even the ordinary cowardice that makes good people sign bad papers. But Vera Amberdale had loved Harrow's End with the fierce, unsentimental love of a woman who had chosen it, and she had spent her whole life keeping its books honest except for one page, and then she had spent her retirement building an instrument to un-keep that page after she was gone. Betrayal and love, poured into the same inkwell, indistinguishable under the lamp.

*She wanted you to find it,* Gideon thought. *She wanted it found by you, specifically, or not at all.*

Which meant the will was not a will. It was a letter. The longest, most patient, most terrible letter a mother had ever written her daughter—forty days long, written in the blood of whoever got in its way, and Gideon could not tell, standing there in the failing light, whether the woman who wrote it had been trying to save her or to use her, and whether there was, in the end, any difference.

* * *

She went home and pulled the box down from the closet shelf—the one she had not opened since the funeral. Inside: photographs, a sewing kit, a recipe card for plum cake in that same slanted hand, and beneath everything, wrapped in a dish towel, her mother's fountain pen. Black lacquer, silver clip worn smooth. Gideon uncapped it. The nib was dry, but when she held it to the lamp she saw, caught in the tines, a faint dark residue. Iron gall.

Her mother had written the will at this kitchen table. Probably in the evening, probably with the green lamp on, probably pausing to think with the pen resting against her lip the way she did. And then she had sealed the envelope, and written *Gideon* on the front in the hand her daughter knew better than her own voice, and given it to—

To whom? Who did Vera Amberdale trust with a thing like that? Who had she loved well enough, long enough, to hand them a bomb with her daughter's name on the fuse?

Gideon stood in her kitchen with the dry pen in her hand and the answer assembling itself out of everything she had learned and everything she had refused to learn, and she thought of Maren Halloway—her mother's closest friend, keeper of the town's every record, the one person in Harrow's End who had deflected every question about 1994 with a smile warm as a banked fire.

The phone rang.

She almost didn't answer. But she was her mother's daughter, built to the shape of one lock, and she picked it up.

"Gideon." Maren's voice, soft and unhurried, with the river running underneath it. "I've been going through some of your mother's things at the archive. I think there's something here you should see. Can you come tomorrow?"

Gideon looked at the pen. At the window, where the rain had finally begun, thin and cold against the glass.

"Tomorrow," she said, and heard her own voice steady, her mother's steadiness, the steadiness that had signed one lie and spent thirty years building the machine that would unsign it. "First light, Maren. And Maren—" she paused, the question leaving her before she could weigh it, "—did she ever write you letters?"

The silence on the line lasted three heartbeats. When Maren spoke again, her voice was as gentle as ever, and that gentleness was suddenly the most frightening thing Gideon had ever heard.

"Come and see, dear," she said. "Bring nothing. And Gideon—don't tell anyone where you're going."
