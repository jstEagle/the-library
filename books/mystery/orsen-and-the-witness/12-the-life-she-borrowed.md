## 12. The Life She Borrowed

The numbers had a direction.

Beatrix saw it at two in the morning, on the third pot of coffee and the fourth re-arrangement of Maren's ledger across her kitchen table, when she stopped reading the coded columns as amounts and started reading them as coordinates. Every discrepancy — every sum that didn't balance against the grocery receipts, the coal deliveries, the church envelope — carried a date. And every date, plotted against the tide tables she'd photocopied from the maritime museum, fell within a day of a low-water crossing at the old ferry slip.

But that wasn't the thing that made her set down her pen and press both palms flat against the table until the wood bit cold into her skin.

The thing was the drift.

For the first six years after 1974, the coded payments were small and local — sums routed through Ruth's father's ferry accounts, through the hardware store's petty cash, through a fishing co-op that had dissolved before Beatrix was born. Then, in 1980, they changed character. They got smaller. They got steadier. And the dates stopped matching the tides.

They started matching the first Tuesday of every month.

A salary. Or something shaped like one.

* * *

She worked it out the way her grandmother must have built it: backward from habit. Whoever received the money after 1980 wasn't being smuggled anymore. She was being paid — or paying herself — through some channel that required regularity. A pension. A wage. An account under a name that wasn't Elsie Vann.

Maren's coded figures weren't random. Once Beatrix assumed they were amounts in shillings converted to decimal currency in 1971 — a trick, a lock, a way of making the columns look like pre-decimal bookkeeping to anyone who glanced — the arithmetic opened like a door. Subtract the decoy column from the real one, divide by twelve, and you got monthly figures. Modest ones. Thirty pounds a month in 1980. Forty-five by 1990. Sixty by the time the entries thinned and finally stopped in 2011, the year Maren's handwriting began to fail.

And beside each figure, small as breath, a letter code. Not initials — Beatrix had tried initials for two days and gotten nowhere. Initials would have been too easy to trace, too obvious to a husband who might one day go looking. No: place names. She was almost sure of it now. The codes shifted over the decades the way postcodes did, and there was a pattern to the shifts — a slow migration up the coast.

Harrowgate. Then Harrow Point. Then, beginning in 1983, a code that resolved, when she cross-checked it against an old Ordnance Survey map spread under the fruit bowl, to a village called Saltmere.

Three hours north. Far enough that nobody from here would ever have a reason to go. Close enough that Maren could visit twice a year and call it a holiday.

Beatrix sat back in the chair until it creaked. The kitchen window had gone from black to the deep blue that isn't quite morning, and somewhere down the street a gull was arguing with the dawn about terms.

"Saltmere," she said aloud, just to hear it exist.

Then she reached for her coat.

* * *

Saltmere announced itself the way coastal villages do when they've stopped trying to impress anyone: a crooked sign, a chapel with salt-scoured stones, a single street that ran downhill toward a harbor so small it seemed more suggestion than port. Fishing boats that hadn't fished in years. A shop that sold postcards of itself. Gulls wheeling over everything with the entitled air of landlords.

Beatrix parked at the top of the hill and walked down, telling herself she was sightseeing. Telling herself she'd driven three hours on a hunch encoded in a dead woman's grocery ledger because she wanted sea air.

The lie lasted until she passed the village hall and saw the notice board.

It was the kind of board where history accumulates in layers — jumble sales pinned over regattas pinned over lost-cat notices going back years. And there, half-hidden behind a laminated sheet about coastal erosion, was a photograph from some anniversary or other. A line of villagers outside the hall, arms crossed against the wind, squinting into whatever summer that was.

Second from the left stood a tall woman with white hair pinned back, wearing a cardigan the color of the sea on an honest day, and smiling the way people smile when they've decided to.

Beatrix knew the face. She had spent ten years of her life knowing that face — the school photograph of Elsie Vann, aged twenty-six, that had run beside every article about the drowning; the grainy wedding portrait Gerald Vann had released to the papers; the composite sketches, the age-progressions, the artist renderings her own producer had commissioned for episode seven. She had looked at that face so long she could draw it drunk, blindfolded, backwards.

Older by fifty years. Softer around the jaw, the way faces get when they're no longer afraid. But the same. Unmistakably, impossibly the same.

The caption beneath read: *Miss Marsh with the leavers' class, Saltmere Primary, 1998.*

Miss Marsh.

Beatrix stood in front of the notice board for a long time. A woman with a pram nodded at her; she nodded back without moving her eyes. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and enormous, and her hands, when she finally lifted them to take a photo with her phone, were not steady.

*Edith,* said the voice in her head — Ruth's voice, though Ruth had never said the name. *Her name will be Edith.* Because Edith had been the name on the christening record Maren's ledger referenced once, in a margin note Beatrix had spent a week decoding: *E.M., b. 1901, St. Aidan's* — Elsie's mother. Elsie hadn't invented herself. She'd borrowed her mother's name and worn it like a coat.

Edith Marsh. Miss Marsh. Teacher.

"Elsie," Beatrix whispered, and a gull screamed overhead as if in objection, and she laughed — a strange, cracked sound that made the pram woman glance back — and then she made herself walk away, casually, the way an innocent person walks, while every nerve in her body sang.

* * *

She did it properly. That was the discipline ten years of true crime had ground into her: verify, verify, verify, and never fall in love with the answer before the evidence signs off.

The county records office for the northern district was a converted granary with a radiator that clanked like a ghost in chains. Beatrix gave the archivist a story she'd rehearsed on the drive — researching a retired teacher for a piece on rural education — and the archivist, grateful for any interruption to his cataloguing, pulled the file without suspicion.

Edith Marsh. Appointed to Saltmere Primary in September 1980. Qualifications listed as "equivalency assessment, County Board" — which meant no verifiable training history, which meant someone had smoothed the road. References on file: one M. Iversen, described as "family friend," address in Beatrix's own hometown.

Maren. Of course Maren. Maren, who had sat across kitchen tables and written grocery sums in two hands, had also written a reference letter for a woman who officially did not exist.

Beatrix copied the file number and photographed every page with hands she pretended were steady. Retirement date: 1996. A note in the file, dated 2003: *Long service recognition — E. Marsh, 23 years' service.* Twenty-three years. A whole career. A whole life, assembled out of nothing but a ferry crossing, a false name, and one neighbor's ferocious competence.

There was a photograph stapled to the appointment card. Younger than the notice-board picture, this one — early forties, hair still dark at the temples, standing stiffly in front of a classroom door. Looking directly into the camera with an expression Beatrix recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person performing ordinariness while something enormous hides behind their ribs.

"Popular teacher?" Beatrix asked the archivist, lightly.

"Oh, the best they ever had." He said it with the reflexive warmth of a man repeating community consensus. "Miss Marsh. Everyone round here went through her class. Strict, mind, but fair. Never married. Kept to herself a bit, but that's not a crime in a village this size." He chuckled. "Half the coast remembers her."

Half the coast remembered her. Fifty years of remembering, and not one of them had ever known her at all.

* * *

The question that remained was the only question that mattered, and Beatrix circled it all evening while she ate fish and chips in her car above the harbor and watched the light die over the water.

Was she still alive?

The math said maybe. Ninety-six, if the birthdate held — and the birthdate was the one true thing Elsie had kept, because even a stolen life needs an anchor. Ninety-six. Alive, if luck and stubbornness had held. Dead, if they hadn't, and if so, there would be a grave somewhere with the wrong name on it, and Beatrix would be three hours too late by fifty years.

There was one way to find out, and it was a phone call, and she made herself wait until morning because the version of her that existed at midnight made reckless calls.

In the morning she found the number online: Bayview Residential Care, four miles up the coast road from Saltmere, a low modern building in what the website promised were "extensive gardens with sea views." She sat in the car park of the Saltmere shop, phone in hand, engine off, and rehearsed.

*I'm calling about a resident, Edith Marsh. I'm a family friend from down the coast. I've lost touch and I'd love to send her a card.*

It was even mostly true.

The receptionist was cheerful, mid-fifties, the voice of someone who answered forty calls a day. "Let me just check our register — one moment, love."

Paper rustled. A keyboard clicked. Beatrix stared through the windshield at the harbor and discovered she had stopped breathing.

"Sorry, can I ask who's calling?"

"Beatrix Iversen. Family friend." The name felt dangerous in her mouth and safe at the same time — Iversen meant nothing here, surely, fifty miles and fifty years away from the Vann case. Surely.

More clicking. Then a pause that went on a beat too long.

"Miss Marsh is one of our longest-standing residents," the receptionist said, and Beatrix's heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a door. "Been with us since 2014. Lovely lady. Very private, keeps her own counsel, but sharp as anything." A warm little laugh. "Still does the crossword in pen."

Alive. *Alive.* Ninety-six years old and doing crosswords in pen, four miles from the village where she'd taught three generations of children how to read, and Beatrix sat in a rented car with her forehead against the steering wheel and let out a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and entirely involuntary.

"I— sorry," she managed. "That's wonderful news. Could I— is she able to have visitors? I'd hate to arrive unannounced."

"Well—" The receptionist's tone shifted, apologetic, careful. "I should say, we do ask family to arrange visits in advance, especially for our residents who prefer routine. And Miss Marsh particularly — she doesn't care for surprises. Hasn't for years, staff say." Paper turned again. "I can put you down for next week, if you like? Give her notice?"

Next week. Beatrix closed her eyes. Next week was an eternity and also, suddenly, fine — fine, because Elsie Vann had waited fifty years, and the least Beatrix owed her was the courtesy of a scheduled knock instead of a door kicked open by the past.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Thursday. Thursday afternoon, if she'll have me."

"Thursday the fourteenth, two o'clock. Can I take a contact number?"

Beatrix gave it. Thanked her. Hung up.

Then she sat very still, letting it settle over her like weather: *Elsie Vann is alive.* The case she had built her entire adult life around — the drowning, the mystery, the ten episodes, the confession letter sitting in a fireproof box in her apartment — all of it had been pointing, the whole time, at a woman doing crosswords in pen in a garden with a sea view. Not a victim. Not a body in the bay. A survivor, ninety-six years old, who had written to Beatrix because she was running out of time and had chosen, out of everyone alive, her.

Hope rose in her chest, huge and terrifying, and she let it — for exactly as long as it took to notice the flaw in the conversation, the small wrong note she'd filed away without examining, the way she'd been trained to.

*Hasn't cared for surprises, hasn't for years.*

Fifty years of hiding teaches a person to dread surprises. But there was something else in the receptionist's pause — that beat too long before she confirmed the residency, the faint formality, as if she were checking something besides the register. As if the name *Iversen* had snagged on something.

Or as if someone else had called recently. Someone asking after Edith Marsh. Someone whose questions had made the staff careful.

Beatrix told herself she was imagining it. She drove back down into the village, bought a thermos of coffee and a postcard of the harbor — *Thinking of you* was all she dared write, no signature, addressed to Miss Marsh at Bayview, mailed from the box by the chapel so it would arrive before Thursday and give Elsie time to decide whether to see her — and pointed the car south toward home.

She was twenty minutes out of Saltmere, the coast unspooling gray and glittering beside her, when her phone rang through the car speakers. Unknown number. Local exchange — her hometown's exchange.

She answered on speaker, one eye on the road.

"Ms. Iversen?" A man's voice. Smooth, unhurried, pleasant in the way that costs money. "Don't hang up — I know how you feel about my calls. This isn't about the ledger. It's about a woman named Edith Marsh. I understand you've been making inquiries."

The road bent. Beatrix took the bend slowly, her pulse loud in her ears.

"And I understand," Hollis Vann said, "that she's still alive. So I thought we should talk — you and I — before Thursday."
