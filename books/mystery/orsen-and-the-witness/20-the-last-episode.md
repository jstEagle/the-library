## 20. The Last Episode

The studio smelled of burnt coffee and old carpet the way it always had, but when Beatrix unlocked the door at six in the morning, three days after the coast guard boat pulled away from Gullwatch, it smelled like something else too — like a room she was about to leave.

She'd told Marcus on the phone at eleven the night before. He'd been quiet for so long she checked whether the call had dropped.

"You're sitting on the biggest true-crime reveal of the decade," he finally said, "and you're going to air a fairy tale."

"I'm going to air the truth," Beatrix said. "Just not her name."

"That's not how this works, Bea. That's not how any of this works."

"It's how *she* works," Beatrix said. "It's how Maren worked. It's how Ruth's father worked, poling that boat across black water with a woman who couldn't stop shaking. Fifty years of people keeping a promise they never made out loud. I'm not going to be the one who breaks it for a ratings bump."

Marcus sighed, and in the sigh she heard the whole architecture of their decade together — his pragmatism, her stubbornness, the strange marriage of the two that had kept 'The Witness' alive through two network cancellations and one very ugly lawsuit. "You're producing it yourself, then. I'm not putting my name on an episode that ends nowhere."

"Fine."

"Fine." A pause. "Send me the file when it's done. I'll listen. Off the clock."

That was as close to a blessing as she was going to get, and she took it.

* * *

The cottage at Gullwatch had given up its secrets slowly, the way old houses do — grudgingly, in pieces, each one costing something. The storm had passed. Hollis Vann sat in county lockup awaiting charges that read like a grocery list — unlawful confinement, breaking and entering, assault — and would, Beatrix suspected, read like a life sentence to a man who had spent sixty years believing himself owed a fortune that had never existed.

Elsie Vann — Edith Marsh, still, on every document that mattered — had refused the hospital. She'd let them look at her bruised wrist, let the young paramedic wrap it with hands that trembled more than hers did, and then she'd asked to be driven back to the cottage, because "the light is better there in the mornings, and I have lived eighty years without anyone telling me where to sit."

So Beatrix drove her back. And on the second morning, while fog burned off the water and the restored radio in the lighthouse played something faint and orchestral, Elsie had looked at the recorder Beatrix carried everywhere like a third hand and said:

"Turn that off, dear. What I say next isn't for broadcast."

Beatrix turned it off.

"What you do next," Elsie said, "is your business. But I'll tell you what I'd want, if anyone asked me, which no one has in fifty years." Her voice was dry and even, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed nothing because she'd been living the rehearsal since 1974. "I spent my whole life being someone's secret. First Gerald's prisoner, then Maren's project, then Edith Marsh, beloved schoolteacher, thirty years of children who never knew my real name was written in a ledger under a hymn number. I am tired of being a plot. If you tell the story — and I know you will, you have your grandmother's jaw and her inability to leave a sum unbalanced — then tell it sideways. Tell it about the town. Let me stay a shape in the fog. That's the only name I've got left that fits."

Beatrix had sat with that for a long time, watching the light move across water that had once, officially, held a drowned woman.

"And if someone figures it out?" she asked. "Someone always figures it out eventually. I did."

"Then they'll find a ninety-six-year-old woman in a house full of evidence that says Edith Marsh died in 2019 and was cremated in private, which is what the paperwork will say by Friday, because Ruth's nephew is a notary and Ruth does not forgive slowly." Elsie smiled then, and it was the smile from the confession letter — wry, unhurried, a little theatrical. "I've been dying on paper for decades, Bea. I'm very good at it."

* * *

She wrote the episode in four days, which was fast even for her, and she wrote it standing up at the kitchen counter of her apartment with the repaired lock, because sitting down made her think like a journalist and this could not be journalism.

The problem wasn't the facts. The problem was that every fact pointed home like a compass needle. A household ledger with double columns. A ferryman who carried more than passengers. A keeper's cottage bricked shut in 1974 and stocked ever after. Any competent listener — and her listeners were nothing if not competent; they'd solved cold cases from audio alone — could triangulate the geography in an afternoon. Names changed wouldn't survive contact with tide tables.

So she didn't change the names. She removed the map entirely.

The town became "a coastal community she would not name." The bay became "the water." The ferry became "a boat." The ledger became "an account book kept by a woman who was very good with numbers." Gullwatch became "a light station, decommissioned, that some of you may think you recognize — don't bother." She stripped the coordinates out of the prose the way you'd strip a bone: carefully, completely, leaving nothing for the dogs.

What remained was the story itself, and the story, she realized on the second day, had never actually been about a disappearance. It had been about a decision. A terrified twenty-six-year-old woman in 1974, standing in shallow water at dusk, leaving her shoes on the rocks where they'd be found, and walking away from her own death into a life that fit her like borrowed clothes. And behind her, unfolding across decades like a long slow conspiracy of decency: a ferryman who asked no questions, a neighbor who opened her account book and never closed it again, a daughter who inherited the silence along with the house.

A town that hid a murder, the original series had called it, ten episodes of mounting dread. A town that hid a rescue, Ruth had corrected her, and the correction had rearranged everything Beatrix thought she knew about the case, about her grandmother, about the whole grim genre she'd built a career out of.

She recorded the intro eleven times.

* * *

Take twelve worked.

"My name is Beatrix Iversen, and this is the last episode of 'The Witness.'

"For ten years, this show has been about one case — the disappearance of a young wife in 1974, presumed drowned, presumed murdered, presumed buried somewhere in the mud and memory of a town that never talked. I built my life around that presumption. I built ten hours of audio around it. I built a career on the promise that someday I'd stand in front of a microphone and say her name and say *found*, and whatever came after the word found would be justice.

"I'm not going to do that today. And I need you to stay with me for the next forty minutes while I explain why, because I think what happened in that town matters more than what happened to me, or to my ratings, or to the version of this story you've been waiting a decade to hear.

"This is a story about a woman who ran. Not from the law — from a man. And it's a story about the people who helped her run, and kept helping, for fifty years, without ever once saying out loud what they were doing. There's a word for that kind of arrangement. Most people call it a conspiracy. I've come to believe the better word is a covenant.

"The names in this episode are changed, and I want to be honest with you about why: not to protect the guilty. The guilty man in this story has been dead since 1989, and hell, wherever it keeps him, can sort out its own paperwork. The names are changed to protect the living — specifically, one living person, who after half a century of being other people's secret has earned the right to remain a shape in the fog. If you spend the next forty minutes trying to solve it anyway — and I know some of you will, God love you, it's why you're here — I'll ask you to consider what you'd actually be solving. Because I've solved it. I've held every piece. And I'm telling you: the answer is not a body. The answer is a life. A whole one. Ninety-six years and counting.

"Some cases close. This one, publicly, never will. Here's why that's the right ending — and here's the story."

* * *

She laid it out in movements, the way Maren balanced a column: what was known, what was hidden, what was paid.

The drowning that wasn't — shoes on the rocks, a coat on the beach, a husband whose grief lasted exactly as long as it took to start spending the insurance money. The boat crossing at dusk, a ferryman's lantern hooded, a young woman who didn't speak for the entire passage except once, near the far shore, when she said *tell the woman with the ledger I'll pay it back*. The account book with its two truths running side by side for five decades — groceries and coal in one column, and in the other, keyed to dates and disguised as errors, a monthly sum flowing north to keep a stranger alive. The cottage by the light station, bricked shut against prying eyes and stocked twice a year by a woman who told her neighbors she was storing preserves. The schoolteacher three hours up the coast whom generations of children loved and none of them ever really knew.

She used Ruth's voice — recorded with permission, pitched and processed past recognition, the words intact. *"We didn't hide a murder. We hid a rescue. My father used to say there were two kinds of carrying — the kind you get paid for, and the kind you answer for. He carried her for free, and he answered for it every day of his life, and he never once said he was sorry."*

She used Maren's ledger itself, photographed page by page, the pencil sums read aloud in her own voice until the numbers stopped being numbers and became what they'd always been: a heartbeat, measured monthly, for fifty years. *Fourteen dollars and sixty cents, March 1975. Twenty-two dollars, September 1981. Forty dollars flat from 1994 on, because Maren had decided inflation was no excuse for inconsistency.*

And she used the confession letter — fragments only, the handwriting described rather than shown, the postscript left out entirely. *If you are reading this, Bea, I ran out of time.* Some doors stayed shut even in a story about open ones.

Near the end, she let herself into the recording, because the episode demanded a witness and she was, after all, the witness.

"People are going to ask me if I regret it. Ten years, ten episodes, thousands of hours, and I end it with the case unsolved — publicly, permanently unsolved. Here's what I'll say to that. For a decade I believed I was chasing a killer. What I was actually doing, I understand now, was standing guard over a door somebody had bricked up to keep a woman safe, and calling myself an investigator for wanting to break it down. The best thing this show ever taught me wasn't how to find people. It was how to recognize, finally, the difference between a mystery and a mercy. This was both. Only one of those deserved to end.

"To the woman in this story, wherever the fog keeps her: the ledger is balanced. Paid in full. You don't owe anybody anything — not the money, not the explanation, not the name. Take it from someone who audited every line.

"'The Witness' is over. Thank you for listening. Go be merciful to somebody who needs it done quietly."

She saved the file. Sat for a moment in the booth with the red light off and her own breathing loud in the headphones. Then she exported it, uploaded it, and scheduled it for release at midnight — no promo, no teaser, no advance copy to press. Marcus would hear it when everyone else did.

Her finger hovered over the publish key longer than it needed to. Somewhere in the pause she understood she wasn't hesitating over the episode. She was hesitating over the decade — the woman she'd been when she started, the one who'd needed the answer more than she'd needed anything, who'd built a fortress of microphones around a nine-year-old's memory of her grandmother going quiet whenever Elsie Vann's name came up. Publishing this was also burying her. She pressed the key anyway, the way you close a coffin, gently, and all the way.

* * *

By morning it had done what her episodes always did — detonated quietly and spread like weather. By noon the forums were alight. By evening the theories had metastasized into the hundreds: the town was in Maine, in Wales, in Washington State; the light station was this one, that one, the famous one with the ghost tour; the schoolteacher was identifiable from a 1987 yearbook photo that was, in fact, a different woman entirely. Someone located a bricked-up cellar in Cornwall and posted drone footage. A Reddit thread titled "THE LEDGER = TIDE TABLES???" ran to four thousand comments and got every coastline wrong.

Beatrix read none of it past the first hour. She'd expected the frenzy and found, strangely, that it didn't touch her — that the story had left her hands the way a launched boat leaves a slip, and the water it went into belonged to strangers now. What touched her was smaller. An email from a listener in Ohio, subject line *my mother left in 1976 and we never knew why*: *Thank you for not solving it. Some of us are still hoping our missing women just walked.* She read that one three times and cried a little, standing at the kitchen counter, and then she put her phone face-down and went to pack the car.

Because there was one more thing the episode hadn't said, one thread left deliberately loose, and it was waiting for her three hours north — no. Not north. Waiting, rather, in a house on Larch Street with a sagging porch and Maren's roses gone feral over the trellis, where Ruth had spent the week supervising a deep clean with the ferocity of a field marshal and the tenderness of a woman furnishing a nursery.

"She can't stay at the cottage," Ruth had said on the phone, in the tone that ended discussions. "Winter's coming, she's ninety-six, and that road floods before the rest of the county even gets damp. Maren's house has the good light and the downstairs bedroom and it's been empty eight years, which is eight years too many for any house that size. So it's settled."

"Ruth, it's *my* house—"

"Which is why I'm telling you instead of asking you. She moves in Saturday. You'll want to be there. Bring the ledger — she wants to see it one more time before you seal it away, and frankly, girl, so do I."

Beatrix had opened her mouth to list the reasons this was insane — the logistics, the liability, the sheer improbability of a fugitive from 1974 taking up residence in her dead grandmother's guest room — and had heard herself close it again without producing a single one. Because there weren't any. There was only the fact of it, arriving with the same quiet inevitability as everything else in this story: the woman her grandmother had kept alive for fifty years was coming to live in her grandmother's house, and Beatrix was going to be there when she did.

She loaded the car at dawn — the ledger wrapped in a wool scarf, a box of Maren's things Ruth had asked for, fresh flowers because it seemed suddenly important. The fog was burning off the harbor as she pulled onto the coast road, and the last episode was already out in the world, unresolvable, untouchable, hers and not hers, finished.

Ahead of her, Larch Street waited, and a house with its windows open for the first time in eight years, letting the sea air move through rooms that had held their breath long enough.

On the porch, visible from the end of the street, someone small and upright was already standing — watching for her car, one hand raised against the glare, waving.
