## 6. Loyalty on Credit

The note appeared under my door at 6:47 a.m., which meant it had been slid there sometime after my last round of the corridor at six and before the early kitchen staff came up at seven. I knew this because I knew everything that moved through the Hotel Marlowe between midnight and dawn. For seven years, the building had been an extension of my own nervous system.

The note was written in blue ink on hotel stationery, in a hand I would have recognized anywhere, even upside down, even underwater.

*My office. Before the shift. — E.*

I stood there in my coat, keys still in hand, and felt something cold slide down the back of my neck. Edwin Marsh did not summon people to his office before the shift. In thirty-one years of managing the Marlowe, he had built his authority out of small kindnesses and softer silences—the extra day off nobody asked for, the raise granted without a request, the quiet word in the right ear. He didn't command. He *arranged*. That was why the staff loved him, and why I had loved him longer than anyone alive except perhaps my mother, who was dead.

I put the diary in its hiding place—inside the hollow leg of the card catalog in the back office, behind a false panel I'd installed myself two winters ago—and went down.

* * *

Edwin's office sat behind the front desk like a heart behind ribs: windowless, warm, smelling of coffee and lemon polish and, faintly, of pipe tobacco he'd given up in 1998 but never quite surrendered. He was standing at the window when I came in—not the window, since there wasn't one, but the framed photograph of the hotel taken in 1974, when the awning was green and he was a bellhop with hair. He didn't turn around.

"Close the door, Vesper."

I closed it. "You know," I said, keeping my voice light, "most managers just say good morning."

"Sit down."

I sat. The chair across from his desk was the one guests sat in when they complained about their bills. It was designed, I had always thought, to make you feel slightly guilty about something.

Edwin turned. He looked older than he had yesterday, which was impossible, but there it was—the gray in his beard suddenly dominant, the lines around his eyes carved deeper, as if someone had spent the night working on him with a chisel. He poured two coffees from the carafe on his sideboard with hands that were almost steady, and set one in front of me.

"You've been asking questions," he said.

"I ask questions all day. It's the job."

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't do that thing where you're cleverer than the room. Not today."

I wrapped both palms around the cup. It was too hot. I held it anyway. "What thing am I doing, Edwin?"

He sat down heavily behind the desk, and for a long moment he just looked at me, and I understood that whatever he was about to say, he had rehearsed it in front of that photograph of himself at twenty-two, and it hadn't gotten easier with rehearsal.

"The Calloways called me last night," he said. "Marcus Calloway. Eleven-forty p.m."

The heat of the cup traveled up into my wrists. "And?"

"And he wanted to know if a member of my staff had been making inquiries about Oscar Renner. About the Renner estate. About—" Edwin's jaw worked. "About sealed rooms and old occupancy records."

I kept my face still. Seven years at a front desk teaches you a face that stays still while your pulse climbs stairs. But Edwin had known me since I was nineteen and interviewing for a night-audit position with a résumé so thin it could have been a napkin. He watched the stillness settle over me and recognized it for what it was.

"So it's true," he said quietly.

"It's true that I'm curious. Curiosity isn't a crime in Bellhaven. Yet."

"Vesper." He said my name the way you set down something fragile. "I'm going to tell you a story, and then you're going to make a choice, and I need you to hear the whole story first. Can you do that? Can you just listen?"

I nodded, because my voice had gone somewhere else.

"Three years ago, when Oscar Renner disappeared, the police came through here twice. You remember. Detective Halloway and that young one, what's-his-name. They asked everyone the same questions, and everyone gave the same answers, and the case went cold because Bellhaven wanted it to go cold." He leaned forward. "But before they ever arrived, Marcus Calloway came here. Personally. Sat in that chair you're sitting in. And he told me that a guest of ours had died—an accident, a fall, a tragedy—and that the family would consider it a professional courtesy if the hotel's records reflected nothing unusual. No disturbances. No visitors. No anomalies."

"What records?" I asked. "There was nothing unusual in the records. I checked them myself a hundred times over the years. Room 217 checked out clean."

"That's because I made sure of it," Edwin said. "Every month. For three years."

The coffee had gone cold in my hands. I set it down very carefully, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw.

"I don't understand," I said, though I understood perfectly.

"Yes, you do." He opened the bottom drawer of his desk and took out an envelope, thick, unmarked, and laid it on the blotter between us. He didn't open it. He didn't need to. "A retainer, he called it. For discretion. For the continued smooth operation of the establishment. It arrives quarterly. It has arrived quarterly for eleven years, Vesper. Long before Renner. It paid for the roof in 2019. It paid for Marta's surgery when the insurance ran out. It paid for your mother's funeral when her policy wouldn't cover the whole of it."

The floor tilted—or didn't tilt, but I gripped the armrest anyway, because some part of me needed to hold onto something solid.

"My mother's funeral," I repeated.

"Four thousand dollars, unmarked, no questions. You thought it came from the staff fund. It did. The staff fund is him." Edwin's voice cracked on the last word, and I saw that he had carried this alone for eleven years, and that carrying it had bent him slowly, invisibly, the way water bends a doorframe. "I told myself it was hush money for nothing. For gossip. For the ordinary sins of rich men who want privacy. And then Renner happened, and I told myself the same thing again, harder, because the alternative was to admit what I actually knew."

"Which is what?" My voice came out flat. "What do you actually know, Edwin?"

He looked at me for a long time. Outside, faintly, I could hear the lobby waking up—Marta's vacuum, the freight elevator's groan, the world continuing its ordinary morning while mine came apart behind a closed door.

"I know that Oscar Renner was afraid of the Calloways," he said. "I know he came to me two weeks before he vanished and asked whether the hotel's phone system could be monitored, and I lied to him and said no. I know that the night he disappeared, a car with county plates sat across the street from eleven until three, and I know who was driving it, because I recognized the man, because he'd stayed here before, on the family's account. And I know that when the police declared him dead, Marcus Calloway sent flowers to the desk with a card that said *Thank you for your hospitality.*"

The words hung in the air between us like smoke.

"Why are you telling me this now?" I whispered.

"Because last night, Marcus Calloway didn't ask me whether you were asking questions. He told me to make you stop. And he used a word he has never used with me before." Edwin's hands were flat on the desk now, pressing down against it as if the desk might lift off. "He said *consequences.* Not for you. For the hotel. For the staff. For Marta, whose son just started at the high school. For Dev, who's got a record and can't afford another one. For all of them, Vesper. Eleven years of taking his money means eleven years of him knowing exactly how much we can't afford to lose."

I stared at him. This man who had taught me the difference between a double-entry ledger and a guest folio. Who had walked me home the night my mother died and stood on the porch not saying anything, because he understood that silence was sometimes the only honest currency. Who had been, for seven years, the single fixed point in a life I had chosen to spend watching other people's.

"You should have told me," I said.

"When?" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "When you were nineteen? When your mother was dying? When Renner vanished and you started pulling threads? Every year I waited made it worse. That's how it works. That's the whole architecture of it. You don't decide once to be corrupt—you decide every morning, a little bit, until the deciding doesn't even feel like a decision anymore. Loyalty on credit, Vesper. That's what he gave me. And now the bill's come due, and he's sent you to collect it."

"Is that what this is?" I asked. "Collecting? Because it sounds more like a warning."

"It's both." He leaned forward, and his eyes were wet, and he was still, after everything, the man who had walked me home. "Here's the warning part. They know you have something. I don't know what—I don't want to know—but Marcus used the word *ledger,* Vesper. Offhand, casual, the way you mention weather. Nobody mentions ledgers offhand unless ledgers are on their mind." He pushed the envelope across the desk toward me. "And here's the other part. Take this. Take the quarter's payment and the next one and the next one. Keep your head down for six months. Let me handle Marcus. I'll tell him you were chasing a ghost story for a novel or grief or boredom, and that it's finished. I can make him believe it. I've had practice."

"And if I don't?"

Edwin closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was older than thirty-one years of managing a hotel could account for.

"Then I can't protect you," he said. "And worse—I'll have to choose. Between you and them. Between the truth and everyone under this roof who depends on his money staying quiet." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "I'm telling you, as honestly as I have ever said anything: I don't know which way I'll break. So please, Vesper. Please don't make me find out."

I stood up. My legs worked, which surprised me. The envelope sat on the desk between us, fat and silent, four months of complicity dressed in plain paper.

"Oscar Renner wrote in his diary for six weeks after the town buried him," I said. "Did you know that?"

Edwin went very still. "No," he said. "I didn't know that."

"He was afraid of the Calloways, and he asked you about the phones, and you lied to him. And three days later he was gone." I picked up the envelope. I held it a moment—it was heavier than paper should be—and then I set it back down on the blotter, squared its corners to the edge of the desk, the way I'd square a folio, the way I'd straighten a pen cup, seven years of small orders, and stepped back.

"I'm not going to stop, Edwin."

"Vesper—"

"You took his money for my mother's coffin. I understand why. I even forgive it. But she's dead, and Oscar Renner is either dead or running, and either way somebody buried the truth under our roof and called it hospitality." I reached for the door. "I'm sorry. I know what I'm asking you to lose."

I opened the door. The lobby noise rushed in—Marta's vacuum, the elevator, the ordinary morning. Behind me, Edwin said my name once more, softly, the way you say a word you're trying to keep from breaking.

I didn't turn around. I walked to the front desk, tied on my apron, and opened the day's register with steady hands.

At 9:15, the mail came. Among the bills and brochures was a postcard, no message, no signature—only a photograph of the Hotel Marlowe taken from across the street, at night, and on the back, in block capitals, a single line:

*ROOM 314.*
