## 6. Edmund's Grief, Rehearsed

Edmund Voss lived in the best house in Hollowmere, which is to say the only house in Hollowmere with a gravel drive long enough to pretend it wasn't a driveway. I parked my car at the bottom of it because I have a small stubborn streak about being made to walk uphill toward people who want to be looked up at. The fog had thinned to a grey gauze that morning, and through it the house looked like something a child had drawn of a rich man's home: too many gables, all of them empty.

I'd called ahead. That was deliberate. Grieving men caught off guard give you truth; grieving men given an hour to prepare give you performance. I wanted to see what he did with his hour.

He gave me tea.

That was the first wrong note. Not because offering tea is suspicious — half this town would offer you tea at their own funeral — but because of *how* he offered it. The tray was already laid when Mrs. Pell — no relation, or so everyone insists — let me into the front hall. A silver pot, two cups, lemon slices on a small dish like a garnish on a sentence he'd been rehearsing. Men who are genuinely wrecked by grief hand you a mug and forget whether they own coasters. Men who are performing grief set a stage.

"Miss Eastwick." He rose from a wingback chair by the window as though he'd been sitting there waiting for exactly me, which he had. "Thank God someone's finally asking questions."

"Constable Rhee asked questions eleven months ago," I said.

"And look how far that got us." He gestured me into the facing chair. It was angled precisely toward his, close enough for intimacy, far enough for propriety. Someone had thought about this room. Possibly Edmund. Possibly longer ago than yesterday.

Up close, Edmund Voss was a handsome man aging out of it gracefully — silver coming in at the temples like frost claiming a lawn, jaw still holding its line. He wore black, of course. Not the black of a suit worn daily until it shines at the cuffs, but new black. Purchased black. Mourning bought off a rack sometime after the anniversary of his sister's disappearance, when it occurred to him that grief should be visible.

"You've kept her room?" I asked, nodding at nothing in particular.

"Untouched since the night she went out." His voice caught, professionally, on *went out*. "Her coat's still on the hook. Her boots by the door. Cressida wanted to pack things away and I wouldn't allow it."

It was a lovely detail. It was also, I happened to know from the file, almost certainly false — Ada Voss had left that house from the marsh road side, per the one witness statement that hadn't been paraphrased into mush, and she'd been wearing the coat. The coat was never found. But Edmund said *her coat's still on the hook* with the confidence of a man quoting scripture, and I understood, with a small cold click somewhere behind my sternum, that he wasn't lying to me about the coat.

He was lying to himself about the coat. Or — no. Worse. He was reciting.

* * *

We did the tea ritual. He poured; I declined sugar; he told me the lemons were from his own conservatory, which in Hollowmere is a flex roughly equivalent to owning a private jet elsewhere. Then he folded his hands and looked at me with wet, patient eyes and said, "Ask me anything. I have nothing left to hide."

Everyone who has something to hide says some version of that. The trick is what comes next.

So I asked him ordinary things first. Where he was the night Ada disappeared. Who saw him. What she'd been like in the weeks before. The texture of the family. He answered smoothly, fluently, a man reading from notes he'd memorized so thoroughly they'd stopped feeling like notes.

"I was at the Angler's Rest until half ten — Walter Tregaskis will confirm it, and Denny Croome, they were both there — then I drove straight home. Alone, yes, I know how that sounds, but the roads were flooded past the mill and I stopped twice to move branches. You'll find the log entries if the constabulary still keeps anything."

There it was. The second click.

*You'll find the log entries.* Not: *the police checked the roads.* Not: *an officer told me once.* Edmund Voss knew that road logs existed, knew they were the kind of thing an investigator might pull, knew the specific bureaucratic furniture of a missing-persons file. And here was the thing that made my tea go tasteless in my mouth: the road logs for that night weren't in the file I'd read. I'd noticed their absence — a gap between the witness statements and the search records, pages that had been removed or never filed. Rhee couldn't find them either. They'd been pulled, culled, tidied away by whoever had been housekeeping the Voss file before I ever opened it.

So how did Edmund know they existed?

Either he'd read the file in its complete state — before the cleaning — or he'd seen the cleaned version and remembered what used to be there. Either way, my brother-in-mourning across the tea tray had handled the official record of his sister's disappearance more intimately than most investigators ever do.

I didn't say any of this. I smiled and asked about the family instead, and he talked, and I catalogued.

* * *

Here is what I have learned in twenty years of opening people up, some of them metaphorically: the body doesn't lie well, but the *arrangement* of a life lies constantly. Grief has a physics. Real grief is messy in specific ways — it forgets dates, misplaces objects, repeats itself without shame. Performed grief is tidy. It has arcs. It saves its best material for visitors.

Edmund's grief had arcs.

He started with Ada as a child — the pond, the scraped knees, the way she'd name every animal on the marsh as if introducing them to company. Lovely. Believable. Then he moved, with the smoothness of a tide chart, to the estrangement years — *we didn't speak for nearly four years, Miss Eastwick, families are complicated* — which conveniently established distance, motive-neutralizing distance, before arriving at the reconciliation: *she came back to us last spring, and I thank God every day we had those months.* Then the loss itself, delivered with a catch in the throat timed like a rest in music.

Three acts. Setup, complication, tragedy. I have read worse novels. I have autopsied better liars.

But it was the details that undid him, the way a single loose thread undoes a hem. Because the details were *good*. Too good. He described the last time he saw her — not the night she vanished, but a lunch three days prior — and mentioned that she'd been upset about "the survey papers," that she'd said, and here he closed his eyes briefly, *"Someone's been moving lines on maps, Ed, and it isn't God doing it."*

I sat very still.

That quote does not appear anywhere in the file. I know because I've read the file the way other people read love letters — backwards, forwards, looking for what's between the lines. There is no witness statement recording Ada saying anything of the kind. If Edmund had heard it, it lived only in his memory, unrecorded, unverifiable.

Except.

The phrase *moving lines on maps* appears in the file. Once. In a paraphrased summary of an interview with Uncle Hal — the interview that reads like someone summarized a summary, all the blood boiled out of it. Buried in that grey paragraph: *H.V. states decedent expressed concern re: alterations to boundary documentation.* Alterations to boundary documentation. Moving lines on maps.

Two men, independently reporting the same sister using the same odd phrase? Possible. People repeat themselves; families develop idioms. But Hal's version was buried in a paragraph no casual reader would pause on, and Edmund's version was polished into a deathbed-quality quotation, complete with the invocation of God and the dramatic eye-close. One of them had gotten the phrase from the other. And my money — my professional, unromantic money — was on both of them having gotten it from the same place: the paper trail. From the file, in whatever form it took before it was laundered.

He was decorating his testimony with evidence. That's not what guilty people usually do. Guilty people avoid the file like a burn. This was something else — this was a man who had studied the record so completely that he could no longer tell where his memory ended and the paperwork began. Or a man who assumed I'd read the file too, and was speaking to me in its language, the way you'd speak louder to a foreigner.

Which raised the question I carried out of that house like a stone in my shoe: why assume the coroner had read it? Why perform *for me*, specifically? Unless the person who tidied the file knew I'd be the one pulling it. Unless someone had counted on my habits the way I count on the dead's.

* * *

I stayed another forty minutes. I asked about Cressida — *my sister carries the family's arithmetic, always has, God bless her* — and about Uncle Hal — *Hal's grief comes out sideways, drink mostly, don't hold his rambling against him* — pre-emptively poisoning those wells, I noted, handing me reasons to discount the other witnesses before I'd met them. Efficient. Almost generous of him.

And then, as Mrs. Pell showed me out — and yes, I clocked the surname, and yes, I intend to ask Ambrose Pell about the connection someday soon, because Hollowmere is small enough that every coincidence is really just a relationship wearing a disguise — Edmund touched my elbow at the door.

"One more thing," he said softly. "You're going to walk the churchyard, aren't you? Everyone says you do that. Read the stones."

The fog had come back while we drank tea, pressing against the windows like something listening.

"It's a habit," I said.

"My father's plot is third row from the east wall. My mother beside him. Ada's grandmother — the first Voss to fight over that land — is under the yew." He smiled, and it was a warm smile, a neighborly smile, and it chilled me more than anything else he'd said all afternoon. "I only mention it because people get lost out there in the fog. The rows aren't marked well. You'd think a town like ours would take better care of its dead."

*I only mention it because people get lost.*

I drove home with the wipers going and the stone still in my shoe, turning it over. Because here is what I couldn't shake, what I set down on my kitchen table that evening next to my cold dinner and examined under the lamp like a specimen:

Edmund Voss hadn't answered my questions. He had *anticipated* them. The alibi with named witnesses. The road logs. The pre-poisoned wells of Cressida and Hal. The churchyard directions delivered like a guided tour. Somewhere, someone had taken my methods — the ones I'd used quietly for twenty years, walking graves, reading files against memories, letting suspects talk themselves into architecture — and built a defense against them in advance.

You can't do that from outside. You can't anticipate a coroner's habits unless you've watched a coroner work. Unless you've stood in a room like mine and seen what I see and thought, *yes, and when she comes for us, here is what she'll ask, and here is what she'll check, and here is the tour we'll give her.*

I am not a paranoid woman. Paranoia is a luxury for people whose dead stay buried. But as I washed my teacup — I'd brought my own; call it superstition, call it habit — I had the distinct, physical sensation of being read. Of my own file existing somewhere, annotated, thumbed soft at the corners. Of someone knowing that I catalogue graves, that I park at the bottom of long drives, that I bring my own cup.

Ada Voss's diary had ended three weeks after she vanished. I had assumed the impossible date meant the writer was taunting the police.

Sitting alone in my kitchen, I revised the assumption. The diary wasn't addressed to the police.

It was addressed to *me*.

And the next morning, when I walked the churchyard as Edmund had so helpfully suggested — because I am contrary by nature and suspicious by profession, and because I wanted to see whether the Voss plots were where he said — I found the ground freshly turned on an empty row, a plot with no stone, no name, and paperwork, when I dug for it later at the registry, purchased two months before Ada Voss ever disappeared.

Bought in her name.
