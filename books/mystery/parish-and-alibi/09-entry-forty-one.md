## 9. Entry Forty-One

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day Hollowmere's postmistress delivered bad news with the efficiency of someone who had long since stopped apologizing for it. A brown paper parcel, no return address, postmarked from the county forensic lab in Ashcombe — which meant Rhee had sent it, which meant the results were in, which meant I could no longer pretend the diary was simply a strange object and not a live grenade.

I made coffee first. This is a habit I recommend to anyone whose work regularly involves the dead: never open bad news on an empty stomach. The body needs something to do with its hands while the mind is busy screaming.

Then I sat down at my desk, in my office that smelled of formalin and old paper and the particular institutional staleness of a building that had been a coroner's office since before either of us was born, and I opened the parcel.

* * *

Let me explain what I'd sent them, because the explanation matters to what came back.

The diary itself I'd photographed exhaustively — every page, every entry, front and back, under raking light and normal light and ultraviolet, the way you'd photograph a body before and after autopsy. The physical diary stayed in evidence storage at the station, wrapped in acid-free paper like the relic it was apparently going to become. But I'd pulled samples. Fibers from the paper. Scrapings of ink from a dozen entries, spread across the timeline — the early entries, written while Ada Voss was alive and walking around Hollowmere buying bread and arguing with her brother, and the later ones, the impossible ones, written after the day she vanished into the marsh and never came out.

Eleven months she'd been gone. The final entry was dated three weeks after her disappearance. Entry forty-one, if you were counting, and I was counting, because counting was the only thing keeping me from throwing the thing into the sea.

The question I'd asked Ashcombe was simple, or it should have been. Were the later entries written at the same time as the earlier ones? Ink ages. Paper ages. Even the oils from a writer's fingers migrate through paper in patterns that can be read like tree rings, if you know how to look and have the machinery to look with. Ashcombe had the machinery. Ashcombe had Dr. Ferro, who had once testified in a case I'd worked and who had the personality of a filing cabinet and the precision of one too, which I mean as a compliment.

The report was eleven pages long. I read it twice before I let myself understand it.

* * *

Here is what Dr. Ferro found, laid out in language so dry it could be used as kindling:

The paper was consistent throughout. Same manufacturer, same batch, same watermark — a stationery stock sold by the bookshop on Marsh Lane, the one that smelled of vanilla and cat. No substitution. Whoever wrote the diary used the same notebook from first entry to last.

The ink was consistent throughout. Same brand of fountain pen ink, iron gall with a blue dye additive, purchased — Ferro had traced the batch code — from the same stationer, in a lot that had sat on the shelf for the better part of a year. Again: no substitution. One pen, one ink, one continuous act of writing.

But here was the part I read three times, the part that made me set the report down and look out the window at the fog pressing against the glass like something with a face.

The oxidation profiles of the ink samples were consistent across the entire timeline. The entry dated the fourteenth of March — written while Ada Voss was, by every witness statement, alive — and the entry dated the seventh of April — written three weeks after she vanished — had aged identically. Same degree of oxidation. Same fading curve. Same everything.

Which meant one of two things, and Ferro, being a careful scientist and not a novelist, declined to choose between them. Either the entire diary had been written in one continuous stretch — meaning the dates were fiction, a performance, a diary wearing a calendar as a costume — or the later entries had been written with ink from the same bottle, on the same paper, by a hand so disciplined it could reproduce not just Ada Voss's handwriting but her pen pressure, her letter slants, her idiosyncratic crossed sevens and her habit of letting the tails of her *y*s trail off like something drowning.

Or — and this was the possibility Ferro didn't write down but which sat between his lines like a third party at a table for two — or the diary had been written by Ada Voss herself, over a longer period than her disappearance allowed, and the dates were wrong in the other direction. Written before. Antedated. A diary that knew the future because someone had planned the future and written it down in advance.

I put my head in my hands.

* * *

There's a particular kind of vertigo that comes not from heights but from evidence. Most people never experience it, and good for them. It happens when the thing you've been standing on — the solid, testable, physical fact — turns out to be standing on nothing. You spend your career believing that matter doesn't lie. Bodies don't lie; they testify. Ink doesn't lie; it records. The whole architecture of what I do rests on the assumption that the physical world is a worse liar than any witness, because the physical world has no motive.

And then you get a report from a filing cabinet in Ashcombe telling you that the ink doesn't care about your calendar. The ink says the diary is one continuous object. The dates say Ada Voss was writing from beyond the grave. The handwriting says — well.

I'd sent photographs of the handwriting samples to a document examiner Ferro recommended, a woman named Okafor who worked out of a converted garage in Ashcombe and charged like a Harley Street surgeon. Her report came in the same parcel, tucked behind Ferro's like a knife behind a cushion.

She had compared the diary against three authenticated samples of Ada Voss's writing: a letter to the land registry from four years prior, a grocery ledger the family solicitor had produced, and a birthday card — of all things — sent to Cressida Voss two years before the disappearance. Okafor had spent, according to her invoice, thirty-one hours on the comparison. Her conclusion ran to a single paragraph, and I have read a lot of conclusions in my life, and I have never read one that frightened me the way this one did.

*The writer of the disputed entries demonstrates mastery of the subject's hand at a level consistent with either prolonged, deliberate imitation practice — I would estimate a minimum of several hundred hours of disciplined rehearsal — or authorship by the subject herself. I cannot distinguish between these possibilities. The imitation, if it is an imitation, is the finest I have encountered in twenty-two years of practice. It reproduces not merely letterforms but rhythm, hesitation patterns, and the micro-tremor consistent with the subject's documented fatigue. Whoever wrote these entries either was Ada Voss, or knows Ada Voss's hand better than Ada Voss knew it herself.*

I read that last line aloud to my empty office, because some things need to hear themselves said.

"Knows her hand better than she knew it herself."

The office, being an office, said nothing back. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the corridor, Rhee was arguing with the photocopier, a fight he lost daily and with dignity.

* * *

Here's what I couldn't stop turning over, the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath and find that the stone goes all the way down.

Handwriting can be forged. This is not news. Forgers have been forging since there was writing to forge, and the good ones are very good. But a forgery is a copy, and a copy is made from something. You work from an exemplar — a letter, a signature, a ledger — and you reproduce what you can see. The letterforms, yes. The slant, the spacing, the pressure. But the invisible things — the rhythm of hesitation, the way a tired hand drifts at the end of a long page, the little stutters and accelerations that come from a person thinking while they write — those you cannot copy, because you cannot see them. Okafor called it the difference between a photograph of a river and a river.

The disputed entries had the river.

The entry dated three weeks after the disappearance — entry forty-one — described, in Ada's voice, a walk along the sea wall at dusk, the smell of the marsh at low tide, a conversation with someone she didn't name. The prose meandered. The hand tired halfway through and recovered. There was a place where the ink feathered slightly, the way it does when a pen pauses on damp paper, and Okafor had flagged it: a hesitation of perhaps two seconds, mid-sentence, before the thought resumed. A real hesitation. A human hesitation. The kind of thing that happens when you're writing and you hear a noise outside and you stop, and listen, and then keep writing.

Whoever wrote entry forty-one had either lived it or had studied Ada Voss's writing so long and so completely that they could perform her hesitations on demand.

Neither possibility made me feel better. One meant she was alive after the date the world had agreed she died, which meant everything we thought we knew was wrong. The other meant there was a person walking around Hollowmere who had spent hundreds of hours learning to be Ada Voss, which was a sentence I could not finish without my hands going cold.

* * *

Rhee came by at four, carrying two cups of tea he hadn't been asked to make, which was becoming his signature move. He set one down next to the report and read over my shoulder for a while without touching me, which he'd learned was a rule.

"So," he said finally. "The evidence is lying."

"The evidence isn't lying," I said. "The evidence is telling the truth. That's the problem. The truth it's telling doesn't fit inside the story."

"Which story?"

"Any of them." I turned Ferro's pages toward him. "Look. If the whole diary was written at once, after Ada disappeared, then the killer — or someone — sat down with a year of dates and wrote a dead woman's life in order. That takes the notebook, the ink, the patience, and the hand. If the later entries were written when they're dated, then Ada was alive in the marsh for weeks and nobody found her, and the diary is a message. And if the entries were written before—" I stopped.

"Before," Rhee said.

"Before she disappeared. Dated forward. A diary written in advance, like a script." I rubbed my eyes. "A person who plans a murder writes the aftermath first, Constable. Makes the record they want found. Ada's diary wasn't a diary. It was a stage set. And we've been walking around inside it touching the furniture."

Rhee was quiet for a long moment. He was young enough that his silences still had edges; older coppers' silences go smooth and round from use. "The lab can't tell us which?"

"The lab can tell us the ink is the same age throughout. That's all the ink knows. Ink doesn't keep a calendar. It just dries." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Do you understand what that means? The one witness in this case who can't be intimidated, can't be bought, can't forget — the ink — is telling us that time in this diary is a suggestion."

"Then we're back to the hand," Rhee said. "Somebody wrote it. Somebody with Ada's hand."

"Or Ada's hand wrote it, and somebody arranged when we'd be allowed to find it." I looked at the report again, at Okafor's paragraph, at the phrase *micro-tremor consistent with the subject's documented fatigue*, and felt the vertigo tilt the room a few degrees. "Rhee. How does a forger learn a dead woman's tiredness?"

He didn't have an answer. Neither did I. That was becoming the theme.

* * *

I spent the evening with the diary photographs spread across my desk like a tarot deck, reading entries I'd already read a dozen times, hunting for the seam. There had to be a seam. A forgery this good still had one — a letter formed from muscle memory instead of habit, a word spelled the way Ada wouldn't have, a comma where she'd have put nothing. Okafor hadn't found it, but Okafor hadn't known Ada. She'd known three samples. I had a year of the woman's voice in my head, her grievances, her jokes, her slow-burning fury at her family, and fury is the hardest thing to fake, because real fury has a temperature that rises and falls and fake fury just stays lit.

I read entry forty-one again. The sea wall at dusk. The smell of the marsh. The unnamed person.

*He says the town will thank him someday,* Ada had written, in her tired, trailing hand. *I told him the town doesn't get to thank anyone for what he's done. He smiled at that. He smiles like he's already been forgiven.*

I'd read it before as a dying woman's record of a threatening man. Tonight, with Ferro's report sitting next to it like a verdict, I read it differently. Tonight it read like something written by someone who already knew how it ended. *He smiles like he's already been forgiven* — that's not fear. Fear doesn't have that kind of narrative confidence. That's an epitaph. That's a line you write when you know the smile belongs to the man who will outlive you, and you are leaving a message for whoever comes after, and you are choosing your words the way you'd choose them for a headstone.

You don't choose words for a headstone while you're still running.

You choose them afterward. Or in advance.

I sat with that until the fog outside went from grey to black and the radiator gave up for the night, and then I did the thing I'd been avoiding since the parcel arrived. I took out the copy of the original Voss file — the thin one, the tidied one, the one with pages missing like a mouth with teeth pulled — and I looked at the list of names on the first statement. The responding officers. The investigating constable. The signatures at the bottom of a document that someone had groomed the way you'd groom a witness.

And there it was, the name I'd been stepping around for two weeks the way you step around a loose grave slab. Ambrose Pell. Retired. Beloved. The man who'd closed the file, and the man who'd been first to offer me his help.

I didn't have anything against him. That was the trouble. He'd been kind to me. He'd said my father's name like it was a gift.

But the ink didn't care about kind, and neither did I, anymore. The ink said the diary was one continuous lie, and lies that continuous take rehearsal, and rehearsal takes time and privacy and access, and there was exactly one person in Hollowmere who'd had all three, back when Ada Voss was still alive to be studied.

I put on my coat. There was a grave in the churchyard I'd been avoiding for eleven years, and it was time — past time — to go and read what it had to say.

My father's stone was waiting. And I was suddenly, terribly sure that whatever was written under Tom Eastwick's name, the truth of it had been tidied too.
