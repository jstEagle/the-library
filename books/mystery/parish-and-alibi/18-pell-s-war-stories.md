## 18. Pell's War Stories

Ambrose Pell lived in a cottage on Chapel Lane with a garden so tidy it looked apologetic. Rosemary along the path, clipped within an inch of its life. A bench by the door that had never once been sat on. The whole house had the air of a man who had spent forty years making sure nothing in his vicinity was ever out of place — including, I would come to understand, the truth.

He answered the door before I knocked.

"Miss Eastwick." He said it the way my father used to say *Mirabel* — like a name he'd been keeping warm. "I hoped you'd come. I've got the kettle on."

I hadn't told him I was coming. I'd told no one. I noted this the way I note everything now: calmly, in the ledger at the back of my mind, next to an entry that was getting longer and more troubling by the day.

"Ambrose," I said. "You've got second sight as well as a kettle?"

"Forty years in Hollowmere, you learn to read the weather." He stepped aside to let me in. "And you've been reading mine for two weeks. Seemed only polite to invite the storm in for tea."

* * *

His sitting room smelled of beeswax and old paper. Photographs on the mantel: Pell young and square-jawed in constable's uniform; Pell middle-aged shaking hands with someone important-looking; Pell retired, holding a trout, grinning like a man who had never once lost sleep. On the wall above the fire hung a framed commendation — the Voss Marsh drowning case, 1979, *for diligence beyond duty*.

I looked at that frame longer than I meant to.

"That old thing," Pell said, following my gaze. He carried in a tray — proper tea, proper cups, a plate of shortbread cut in fingers. "You'd think after twenty years they'd let a man take it down. But Mabel liked it there." His wife, dead six years. He said her name the way people set flowers on a shelf. "Sit, sit. You'll want the chair by the window. Everyone does."

I sat where everyone sits. It occurred to me, distantly, that this was how he worked — not by pushing, but by arranging the room so that pushing became unnecessary.

"I'll be honest with you, Miss Eastwick," he began, pouring. "When Rhee told me they'd pulled Ada Voss's diary out of the marsh, I didn't sleep that night. Not from fear." He handed me the cup. "From memory. That marsh has been giving up our secrets my whole career. You'd think we'd have learned to stop burying things in it."

"You were first officer on the original file," I said. "1979. The drowning."

"The drowning." He nodded slowly, tasting the phrase. "Yes. Though we never did agree what drowned, exactly. A man, officially. Officially." He smiled, and there was something underneath the smile like a stone under moss. "You've read the file. Or what's left of it."

"What's left of it is thin."

"Thin." He considered the word, rolling it like a sweet. "That's kind. I'd have said gutted. But then, files get hungry over the decades. Pages wander off. Whole statements go missing. You know how it is — everybody wants a souvenir of a scandal." He watched me over his cup. "You found that already, though. Of course you did. Your father used to say you'd find a lie in a haystack."

The tea went cold in my mouth for half a second. I made myself swallow.

"My father talked about me?"

"All the time. Tom Eastwick was the proudest man in three parishes and too stubborn to say so to your face." Pell's voice went soft and warm, and I hated how much warmth there was to hate. "He'd come round here of an evening, sometimes. After your mother passed. We'd sit where you're sitting and he'd talk about the survey office, the land disputes, and — mostly — about his girl who could look at a body and tell him the man's whole biography." He shook his head fondly. "I keep expecting to see him walk through that door. Twenty years, and I still half expect it."

Twenty years. My father had been dead nineteen. I filed the arithmetic away without letting my face move. Grief makes people generous with numbers; it also makes them sloppy. I couldn't decide which this was.

"Ambrose," I said, "why did you ask me here?"

"To help." He said it simply, without flinching, which is either the mark of an honest man or a very well-rehearsed one. "Ada Voss was a child when I knew her family. I carried that file for eleven months wondering if I'd missed something, and now she's turned up in pieces in my marsh, and some bright young constable is going to make the same mistakes we all made in '79 unless somebody who remembers says something." He leaned forward. "So. Ask me anything. I'm an old man with nothing left but memory and shortbread."

* * *

What followed was two hours of the most useful conversation I'd had since the diary surfaced, and I want to be precise about that, because precision matters more than comfort. Pell remembered everything. Dates, tides, names of witnesses long dead. The shape of the marsh in winter. Which constable had drunk on duty and which had covered for him. He talked about the Voss case the way old soldiers talk about wars — with detail so granular it could only be love or rehearsal, and no way to tell them apart.

"The trouble with the Vosses was never the money," he said. "It was the certainty. Every one of them certain they were owed. Old Josiah leaves the marsh split four ways and a will written in language nobody's untangled since, and suddenly brothers are hiring solicitors against sisters. Edmund's father — my generation, that lot — they fought it in court for nine years. Cost more than the land was worth, back when it wasn't worth anything." He refilled my cup unasked. "Then the highway plans came, and overnight the swamp was gold, and gold makes people honest about their appetites."

"And Ada?"

"Ada was the only one of them who never wanted the gold." Something moved across his face — quick, unreadable, gone. "She wanted the marsh itself. Funny girl. Used to walk it alone at low tide, taking notes on the birds. Her uncle Hal called it madness. I called it the sanest thing any Voss ever did." He paused. "You've spoken to Edmund."

It wasn't a question, but I treated it as one. "Yesterday."

"And?" He waited with genuine attentiveness, elbows on knees, an old man eager for news.

"He grieves," I said carefully. "Though his grief has very good posture."

Pell laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him. "Oh, that's Tom's phrasing exactly. *Good posture.* He said that about a witness once, a man whose alibi was ironed and folded like Sunday trousers." He wiped his eyes. "God, I miss him. Listen to me. You came about a murder and I'm reminiscing."

"You mentioned Edmund first."

Did he? I watched him register that I'd caught it. To his credit — and I mean that as observation, not mercy — he didn't pretend otherwise.

"Well," he said slowly, "yes. I suppose I did. Because if you want my professional opinion, free of charge and worth every penny—" he tapped the arm of his chair— "it's Edmund. It was always going to be Edmund. Firstborn, deepest in debt, most to lose if Ada's share of the marsh went anywhere but to him. In '79 it was his father in that position, and his father's brother before him. The Voss men inherit two things, Miss Eastwick: entitlement and panic. Ada disappeared eleven days after she served papers contesting the estate division. Eleven days. If I were still wearing the uniform, that's where I'd be digging."

It was a good theory. It was, in fact, almost exactly the theory I'd been building, assembled from facts I'd gathered myself, presented back to me in a warm room with excellent tea. And that was precisely what troubled me — not that he was wrong, but that he was so *helpfully* right, the way a stage magician is right about which card you chose.

Because here is what Ambrose Pell did not mention, in two hours of exhaustive memory: he did not mention that the estate papers Ada served were dated four days *after* the last entry anyone could find in her real hand. He did not mention Cressida's name at all. He did not mention Uncle Hal except once, dismissively, as a drunk. And he did not mention — not once, not in passing, not even to wave it away — the grave plot bought in Ada's name two months before she vanished.

A retired constable who ran the original investigation, a man who remembered the tide tables of 1979, had somehow failed to hear about the single strangest fact in the current inquiry. Either Hollowmere's gossip had finally failed, or Ambrose Pell was choosing, with great care, what not to know.

I thanked him for the tea.

* * *

At the door, he caught my hand briefly in both of his. His grip was dry and strong, a working grip, the kind that had pulled drowning men out of the marsh by main force.

"You're like him, you know," he said quietly. "Tom. You get a thought between your teeth and the world has to break it out. Just—" and here his voice dropped into something so gentle it took me a moment to hear it as a sentence at all— "be careful where you dig, lass. That marsh doesn't give things up evenly. It gives up what it wants to give up. And it keeps what it keeps." He smiled. "Your father learned that late."

I walked down the rosemary path with his kindness wrapped around my shoulders like a coat that fit too well. Because that was the horror of it, standing there in the lane with the gulls crying over the rooftops: nothing he'd said was wrong. Edmund *was* suspicious. The inheritance *was* rotten. Every word had been true, and every word had been aimed, and I could not for the life of me prove the difference between a man sharing forty years of wisdom and a man laying crumbs along a path he'd already chosen for me.

Gratitude, I thought, walking home. He'd handed me gratitude like a leash, and I'd put it on myself, and thanked him for the weight of it.

Back at the office, I spread my notes across the desk and forced myself to do the thing grief and charm had conspired to prevent: I wrote down everything Pell had *not* said. The gaps. The omissions. The tidy garden of a conversation with one bed dug out of it.

It was near midnight when I finished the list, and it was the last item that stopped my pen cold. Because going back through my recollection of his photographs, his mantel, his framed commendation — I realized I'd seen a document on the wall behind his shoulder, half-hidden by the commendation frame. A survey map, yellowed, signed by two witnesses at the bottom.

One signature had read *T. Eastwick*.

The other read *A. Pell*.
