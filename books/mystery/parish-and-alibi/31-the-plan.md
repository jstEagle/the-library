## 31. The Plan

Suspension, it turns out, is mostly waiting. Waiting with your badge in a drawer and your name on a clipboard somewhere in the state capital, and the fog pressing against the kitchen window like a creditor who won't leave the porch.

I had spent nine days at my father's old table with his papers spread around me like a card game I was losing, and on the ninth day I stopped waiting.

"Rhee," I said into the phone, "I've been thinking."

"That's usually how the trouble starts," he said. He'd taken to answering my calls on the first ring, which was either loyalty or a symptom. "I'm off shift at six."

"Come for dinner. Bring nothing. Especially don't bring anything official."

A pause. "You're going to say something I'll need plausible deniability for, aren't you."

"I'm going to say several somethings. Bring an appetite for regret."

* * *

He arrived at half past six with a bag of chips and the guilty face of a man who has brought exactly what he was told not to bring. I let him in, put the kettle on because that's what Hollowmere people do when the conversation is going to be bad — we are a town that processes grief through hot water — and laid my father's folder on the table between us.

Rhee looked at it the way you'd look at a sleeping dog of unknown temperament.

"That's the survey," he said. "The real one. Tom Eastwick's copy."

"Signed, dated, witnessed. With the river where the river actually was, not where Ambrose Pell needed it to be." I sat down across from him. "And a note in my father's hand. *If anything happens to me, ask Ambrose why the river moved.* Twenty years he sat on this. Twenty years, and then Ada Voss found the same truth and died in the marsh for it, and Hal Voss swung from a beam, and Cressida went off the coast road with her brakes already gone."

Rhee didn't touch the folder. He'd learned that from me, I think. In my office, the dead stay where they're laid until you're ready.

"So we take it to the state investigators," he said. "Tomorrow. First ferry. I'll drive."

"And it disappears," I said. "Or it gets ruled a forgery. Or the courier has an accident on the coast road — and Rhee, we have *seen* how accidents happen on the coast road. He anticipated my methods once already. He got into my office. He got into the archive with credentials that shouldn't exist. He has had twenty years to build a life out of knowing where every body is buried, and I mean that with unusual literalness."

"So we hide it."

"We hide it and he wins by default. The file dies in a shoebox, Edmund rots alone for a murder he didn't commit, and Ambrose Pell keeps walking to the bakery every morning being beloved." I turned the folder over. My father's handwriting on the tab, faded brown ink: *Voss — route survey — DO NOT LOSE.* He'd underlined it twice. My father, who underlined nothing. "No. He's spent this whole case telling me what he wants. The diary. The grave plot. The note quoting my own autopsy notes. Every step of it, he's been *performing* for an audience of one, and I finally understand why."

"Why?"

"Because being caught quietly isn't the same as being caught at all. He doesn't want to be arrested by a stranger with a warrant. He wants a reckoning. He wants the town to watch. He wants it *on his terms* — and I have been so busy being frightened of that that I forgot it's also the only weakness he has."

Rhee set down his chip, which for him was a gesture of extreme seriousness. "Mirabel. What are we doing?"

* * *

I told him. He listened all the way through, which I'll credit him for, and then he said, "No," which I'd also expected.

"It's insane."

"It's the only stage he can't refuse."

"You'd be announcing to a man who has killed four people — that we know of — exactly where you'll be standing and exactly what you'll be carrying."

"Yes."

"With no backup visible."

"Some backup. Just not visible."

"And if he doesn't come? If he just burns the town down around you from a distance instead?"

I poured the tea. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd expected them to shake and had privately budgeted for it. "Then I'll have learned something too. But he'll come. Rhee, I've read that man's whole life in the shape of this case. He came to my house and reminisced about my father like a favorite uncle. He told me accidents run in families. He's not trying to scare me off. He's trying to *recruit* me — into silence, into doubt, into the long respectable sleep he's been living in for twenty years. And when I wouldn't sleep, he decided I was worth the trouble of a proper ending. He wants a scene. The marsh is his church. He'll want to do it there, where Ada went, where it's all been heading."

"And the handover?"

"Announced publicly. Notice in the *Hollowmere Ledger*, word at the bakery, the whole small-town telegraph. Thursday, ten o'clock, the marsh boathouse. The original Voss route survey handed to state investigators, in the open, where the town can see its history change hands." I looked at him. "We hand over a decoy. The real survey goes to the state by a route nobody knows — not even you, not until it's done. And I stand at that boathouse as the most visible, most vulnerable, most *inviting* target on the coast."

"You're bait."

"I'm *bait with a purpose*. There's a difference. Bait is a worm on a hook. This is a door left open on purpose."

"That is not the comforting distinction you think it is."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

He argued for another hour. He was good at it — better than he'd been a year ago, when he was still a young constable who said "ma'am" and meant it as a wall. Now he argued like a man who'd read my files, which was worse.

"Backup where?" he demanded. "Half the station thinks you're a disgrace. The other half thinks you're a disgrace who's probably right, which is not the same as help."

"Deputy Marsh from the county. I called her this afternoon. She owes me from the Ostrand case, and she doesn't love Pell, and she doesn't love the state office that sat on her promotion. She'll have two people in the treeline by the boat ramp and a third on the water. Nobody in uniform. Nobody from Hollowmere."

"And the decoy?"

"An envelope. Sealed, official-looking, heavy. Inside, a copy of the falsified survey with a note. Something in my father's words. *Ask Ambrose why the river moved.*" I said it and felt the cold go through me, the way it always did, the way it had the night I found the note — my father's hand reaching across twenty years to close around my wrist. "If Pell opens it, he'll know it's a message. If he doesn't open it, the state will. Either way it does its work."

"And you're sure he'll come himself. Not send someone. Not just watch from a distance and let the moment pass."

I thought about the note on my desk, quoting my own autopsy notes verbatim — *ligature furrow inconsistent with simple hanging* — in handwriting I hadn't recognized because it was careful, performed, a hand writing a role. I thought about the grave plot bought in Ada's name two months before she vanished. About a man who had buried a woman with tenderness and then spent a year writing her diary for her, keeping her alive on paper, because erasing her hadn't been enough. He'd wanted authorship.

"He'll come," I said. "He's been coming the whole time. Every clue, every tidy file, every kind cup of tea — he's been walking toward this since the day I opened Ada's diary. I'm not setting a trap, Rhee. I'm finally agreeing to meet him where he's been waiting."

Rhee was quiet for a long time. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the fog had thickened until the harbor light was just a bruise in the window, and somewhere down the lane a dog was barking at nothing, the way Hollowmere dogs do, having learned from their owners.

"If this goes wrong," he said finally, "I lose my job."

"I know."

"And you're already suspended, so you've got nothing left to lose, which makes you the dangerous one."

"I prefer 'committed.'"

"I prefer 'terrified,'" he said. "Because that's what you are, and pretending otherwise is how people die in this town. Tom Eastwick was competent. Hal Voss was careful. Cressida didn't even know she was in danger. Competence and care haven't saved anyone yet."

I looked at my hands again. Steady. "I am terrified," I said. "I've been terrified since the diary. But there's a kind of fear that makes you small and a kind that makes you precise, and I've decided to be precise. Thursday. Ten o'clock. Are you in?"

He looked at the folder, at my father's underlined warning, at me. I watched him do the arithmetic that every honest cop in a rotten town eventually does — the one where you count what the job costs against what it would cost to walk away, and find the columns won't balance either way.

"Thursday," he said. "But I'm standing where I can see you the whole time. Non-negotiable."

"Wouldn't have it otherwise."

* * *

He left at nine. I stood in the doorway and watched his taillights shrink into the fog, and then I went back to the table and sat with my father's survey a while longer, because tomorrow I would have to give it up — hand it, in secret, to the one person I trusted to carry it out of Hollowmere unseen, and after that it would belong to the world instead of to me and to a dead man's underlining.

I thought about what Rhee had said. *Competence and care haven't saved anyone yet.* He was right, and it was the kindest thing anyone had said to me in weeks, because it meant the thing that had failed wasn't me.

I took out a clean sheet of paper and drafted the notice for the *Ledger*, short and flat, the way announcements are written in small towns so they read as ordinary:

*The Eastwick inquiry into the Voss matter will conclude Thursday at 10 a.m. with the transfer of original documents to state investigators at the marsh boathouse. The public is welcome.*

*The public is welcome.* Four words that were either an invitation to witness justice or a dinner bell. I read the line three times, and each time I heard Ambrose Pell's voice under it, warm and avuncular: *You're Tom Eastwick's girl. You have his eye for what's underneath.* He'd be pleased. That was the part that turned my stomach — that the trap and the invitation were the same sentence, and only one of us would know which.

I sealed the envelope for the *Ledger* and set it by the door for morning. Then I sat in the dark kitchen with my father's folder one last time, my hand flat on the cover, and made the promise out loud to the empty room, because the dead in this town had gotten used to hearing from me and I saw no reason to break the habit.

"Thursday," I said. "Bring him to me."

The fog pressed at the glass. Somewhere out in the marsh, the tide was turning, and I fell asleep at the table with the light off, dreaming of a boathouse and a man in a good coat walking toward me through the reeds, unsurprised, almost relieved — and in the dream, just before I woke, he smiled and said my name.
