## 32. Bait

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before you do something stupid on purpose. It isn't peace. It's the silence of a held breath, of a town holding its collective tongue, of a woman standing in a boathouse at dusk with an envelope that contains nothing but a folded page of yesterday's *Hollowmere Gazette* and her own signature on the flap, because I wanted him to know it was mine.

I had signed it, God help me. Ambrose Pell had spent forty years reading other people's hands — confessions, statements, the occasional forged will — and I wanted him to read mine and know that Mirabel Eastwick had personally prepared this for him. It was either a masterstroke or the most elaborate suicide note in coastal history. The jury, consisting of me, was still out.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been born at my kitchen table three nights earlier, among my father's papers and two cups of tea gone cold enough to develop opinions.

"We announce it," Rhee said. He had his constable's notebook out, which he didn't need — he was writing nothing down — but the ritual steadied him. "Publicly. Noticeably. You tell the state investigators you've found the original survey, Tom Eastwick's copy, and that you'll hand it over at the marsh boathouse. Thursday. Six o'clock."

"Because announcing evidence transfers in the newspaper is standard procedure," I said.

"It is if you want a certain retired constable to read about it over his breakfast." Rhee turned his teacup a quarter-turn, the way he did when he was nervous and pretending to be comfortable. "He won't be able to help himself, Mirabel. He's spent twenty years making sure that paper stays buried. He's not going to sit in his cottage while it walks into a state evidence locker."

"And if he simply burns my house down with me in it instead?"

Rhee looked at me the way young men look at older women who joke about death — with the specific horror of someone who has recently learned death takes requests. "We'll have coverage. Unmarked car on the coast road. Two officers in the treeline by the boat ramp. Me, close enough to hear you sneeze."

"You can't hear me sneeze from the treeline."

"I have excellent hearing."

"You once told me you failed the hearing test at the academy."

"That was a clerical error," he said, with dignity, and drank his cold tea like a man drinking to something.

The truth — the truth we did not say aloud, though it sat between us like a third guest — was that Pell would smell any trap we built too carefully. He had run investigations for thirty years; he knew what police protection looked like, knew the silhouette of a man trying to look casual behind a tree. If we wanted him to come, truly come, the stage had to look almost empty. Which meant me. Visible. Alone. Bait with a pulse.

"He'll talk first," I said. "That's the thing you have to understand about Ambrose. He never killed anyone he hadn't talked to first. Talked to, reasoned with, offered a cup of tea and a version of events. The violence is always the last courtesy." I heard my own voice go flat and clinical, coroner's cadence, describing a body rather than a man who had once taught me how to skip stones. "So when he arrives, he'll want to talk. And every minute he talks is a minute your people close the distance."

"And if he skips the talking?"

"Then I'll know everything I believed about him was wrong," I said, "and I'll be dead, so I won't have to live with it."

Rhee did not laugh. I hadn't expected him to. But he reached across the table and took the envelope — the decoy, already sealed — and held it up to the light as if honesty might show through newsprint. "You're sure about the signature?"

"Absolutely sure," I said. "It's the only part of this entire enterprise I'm sure about."

* * *

Thursday came in on fog, as most things do in Hollowmere.

The announcement had run in the Gazette's Wednesday edition, page four, tucked between the harvest festival notice and an advertisement for tractor parts: *In the matter of the Voss inheritance inquiry, original survey documentation will be transferred to state investigators Thursday evening, 6 p.m., marsh boathouse, Hollowmere Estuary Road.* No names. We didn't need names. There were perhaps four hundred people in Hollowmere who understood what that sentence meant, and exactly one of them had spent twenty years keeping a river's secret.

The day behaved with terrible normalcy. I performed a post-mortem in the morning — a heart, mercifully ordinary, a man of sixty-one whose arteries had simply filed their resignation — and I catalogued the instruments afterward with the same care I gave everything, because if these were to be my last hours of routine, I wanted them done properly. Mrs. Tallow brought me a sausage roll at noon and asked whether I'd be watching the festival fireworks Saturday, and I said probably, and lied the way you lie to kind people, smoothly and without pleasure.

At half five I locked the office, stood a moment in the street with the key in my fist, and let myself feel it: the specific weight of a town that did not yet know it was about to lose its favorite story. Gulls wheeled over the chandlery. Someone's radio played something tinny and cheerful. The fog was coming off the water in slow grey ropes, and the marsh beyond the sea wall lay flat and patient as a held breath.

I walked to the boathouse. On foot, deliberately, no car to hide behind, my figure printed against the last of the light for anyone watching from the ridge road. Let him see me. Let him count my steps the way I had counted his.

* * *

The boathouse is a long low shed of blackened timber at the end of the boat ramp, built when Hollowmere still believed in its own future. Inside: three skiffs under dust sheets, oars racked on the wall, the smell of tar and old rope and stagnant water. One window, salt-fogged, facing west across the marsh. One door, facing the ramp. Two ways in, one way out, and I had chosen this room myself, which meant that whatever happened here wore my fingerprints alongside everyone else's.

I set the envelope on the workbench in the middle of the room. Centered. Lit by the single bulb I'd left burning overhead, because a man coming to commit his final crime deserves to see what he's committing it for.

Then I did the thing I am apparently constitutionally incapable of avoiding: I waited, and while waiting, I took inventory.

Not of the room. Of myself. Pulse: elevated but steady, maybe ninety. Hands: cold, which was fine, cold hands are honest hands. Mouth: dry. Fear: present, accounted for, seated quietly in the back row like a well-behaved mourner. I have spent my life among the dead, and the dead have taught me one transferable skill above all others — how to stand very still next to something terrible and keep breathing.

Six o'clock came. The fog thickened. Somewhere out on the flats a curlew cried its two-note complaint, and the tide, going out, sucked softly at the pilings beneath the floorboards like the building itself breathing through its teeth.

I counted heartbeats. It's a habit from the table, from leaning over strangers' chests with my ear to the stethoscope, learning the rhythm of a life by its repetitions. At rest, mine ran about sixty-two. I counted them in hundreds. By the third hundred the curlew had gone quiet. By the fifth, the light through the salted window had gone from grey to pewter to the color of old scars.

Nothing moved on the marsh.

Which was, of course, wrong. Something was always moving on the marsh — herons, rats, the wind worrying the reeds into nervous applause. But the marsh had gone still in the specific way it goes still when a large animal is standing somewhere deciding. I had grown up on the edge of that stillness. My father had taught me to freeze when the flats went quiet, because quiet meant something with better ears than yours had arrived.

Ambrose, I thought. You're already here. You've been here longer than I have.

I kept my eyes on the window and my hands loose at my sides and I thought about Ada Voss, eleven months dead in the peat, buried tenderly in a grave bought in her own name by the man who put her there. I thought about Hal Voss swinging from a beam with his confession still sour on his breath. About Cressida's car folding around the same bend that had taken my father, brakes singing the same song. About Tom Eastwick, who had written *ask Ambrose why the river moved* and then died before he could ask it himself, or perhaps after asking, which amounted to the same thing.

Every one of them had waited somewhere, in the last minutes, counting their own heartbeats. It seemed only fair that I should get to do it too, and survive the counting.

A floorboard spoke.

Not outside. Inside. Back corner, past the second skiff, where the shadows pooled thickest — and I felt the cold hand close on the back of my neck, because I had checked that corner. I had checked every corner when I came in, coroner's habit, and found it empty. Which meant he had been outside the walls, in the fog, and had come through the water door on the seaward side, silent as the tide he'd learned to read before I was born.

"Ambrose," I said, without turning. My voice came out level, which surprised us both, I suspect. "You're late."

"No," said the warm gravel voice behind me, unhurried, almost fond. "I'm early. You're just punctual. Your father was the same — couldn't abide being made to wait, even when waiting was the safe thing to do." A pause, and the soft sound of a man settling himself, taking his time. "Turn around, Mirabel. Let's not do this shouting at each other across a room. We're neighbors."

I turned.

He stood by the far skiff with his coat collar up and his hands in his pockets, silver-haired, ruddy-cheeked, the very portrait of a retired man out for an evening stroll who had wandered indoors by mistake. In his right pocket, the fabric hung with a weight no pocket should carry. He saw me see it, and smiled the smile that had charmed three generations of Hollowmere juries, witnesses, and widows.

"Now then," Ambrose Pell said pleasantly, nodding toward the envelope on the bench. "Before you say anything you'll regret — tell me what's in the envelope, and I'll tell you why none of it needed to happen. Not Ada. Not Hal. Not your father." His smile deepened, gentle as dusk. "And then, if you're very good, I'll tell you what really happened to the river."

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like a crowd leaning in — and somewhere in it, faint as a heartbeat, I heard a branch give under a boot that wasn't his.
