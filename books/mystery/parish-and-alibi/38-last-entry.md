## 38. Last Entry

The diary sat on my kitchen table for six days before I could bring myself to touch it.

Not the forged one — that abomination had gone into evidence, bagged and labeled and destined for a courtroom where it would be held up as Exhibit A in the prosecution of a dead man. No. This was Ada's true diary, the one Rhee had pulled from her lockbox in the marsh, water-stained at the corners, smelling faintly of peat and iron. The one she'd actually written in, right up until the night Ambrose Pell chased her into the fog.

I'd read it twice already. Once as an investigator, hunting for anything we'd missed. Once as a woman, which was worse.

The problem was the ending. Or rather, the lack of one.

Ada Voss had kept a meticulous account of her life for eleven years — birthdays and grievances, tides and arguments, the slow poison of a family circling a fortune like gulls over a fish carcass. Forty-one entries in the final volume alone. And then nothing. Because Pell had cut the narrative off mid-sentence, the way he'd cut everything else. He'd even tried to finish it for her, that ghastly imitation diary with its careful errors, its entries dated after her death. He wanted to author her ending. He wanted to be the last voice in her book.

He didn't get to be.

* * *

It was Rhee who put the idea in my head, though I doubt he knew it at the time. We were in my office on a gray Tuesday, sorting through the state investigators' document requests — they wanted everything, in triplicate, with the enthusiasm of men who had never met a filing cabinet they couldn't love — when he paused over the evidence log.

"The real diary," he said. "What happens to it after trial?"

"It goes back to the estate. Edmund's solicitor, presumably."

"Edmund." He said the name the way you'd spit out a cherry pit. "The man who helped bury his sister under a headstone bought in her own name."

"He was lied to. Skillfully, by a professional liar." I said it because it was true, not because I liked saying it. "Pell told him it was an accident. Told him they'd both survive it. And Edmund, who had spent his whole life surviving things by doing what stronger men told him to do, believed him."

Rhee turned the log page. "Still. Her diary ending up in his hands feels wrong."

"Everything about this case feels wrong. That's rather the theme."

But the thought lodged, the way thoughts do, working at me like damp into plaster. That night I took the diary home — signed out properly, Rhee countersigning without being asked, which is its own small testament to how far we'd come — and set it on my kitchen table between the teapot and my father's box of papers, and looked at it for a long time.

Forty-one entries. A life interrupted. A story with its last pages torn out by a murderer's hand.

There are rules about this sort of thing. Evidence protocols, chain of custody, all the machinery we use to keep sentiment out of justice. I knew them. I'd spent twenty years among the dead learning that sentiment is a luxury the living can't always afford.

And then I thought about Ada, who had written *Cressida cried at dinner and I almost told her, but the ledger isn't proof yet and if I'm wrong I'll have made an enemy of the only one of them who might stand with me.* Who had written about the marsh herons with more tenderness than she ever wrote about her family. Who had been afraid, and brave anyway, and dead for it, and then posthumously impersonated by the man who killed her.

Some accounts need closing by someone who was there. She wasn't there anymore. But I had been — through every page of her, through the marsh at low tide, through the grave with her name on it that she'd never chosen. If anyone had standing to write the last line, it wasn't Ambrose Pell, and it wasn't Edmund, and it wasn't the state of whatever-this-is in triplicate.

I made tea. I sharpened a pencil, because ink felt presumptuous. And I opened the diary to the first blank page after Entry Forty-One.

* * *

Here is what I wrote. I copy it here because I want it to exist in two places, in case one of them burns:

---

*Entry Forty-Two.*

*I don't know who will read this, or if anyone will. Mirabel Eastwick found your notebook in the marsh, Ada, in the place where you hid it, which means you were cleverer than all of us for longer than any of us deserved. You bought yourself time with a lockbox and a half-burned ledger, and you used that time to build a case that eventually hanged a constable. Not bad for a woman everyone in Hollowmere called the quiet one.*

*You should know how it ended, since you didn't get to end it yourself.*

*Ambrose Pell killed you. I believe you knew that in your last minutes, running through the fog with your lungs full of salt air and terror. What you may not have known is why: not for the money, though the money was the excuse everyone gave. For the survey. For the lie he told twenty years ago that drowned a man named Corliss and made Ambrose Pell the hero of Hollowmere. Your father's death, Tom Eastwick's death, Hal Voss's death — all of it was Pell tidying up around that original sin, the way a housekeeper sweeps dust into corners. You found the corner. So he swept you too.*

*He has confessed. He is dead now, taken by the same marsh that took you, and I stood on the bank and watched it happen and did not look away, because I think you deserved one witness who stayed.*

*Your brother Edmund will answer for what he did — carrying you, burying you, keeping the secret that rotted him from the inside out. I feel sorry for him, which I suspect you would find either funny or infuriating. Possibly both. You wrote about him with more patience than he earned.*

*Cressida ran, and nearly died running, and lives now in a flat in Ashcombe that smells of cigarettes and fear. She sends money to a fund for the churchyard upkeep. I don't know if that's penance or performance. With Cressida it was always hard to tell.*

*The highway is coming anyway. The land will be worth what it was always going to be worth. It seems obscene to say it, so I'll say it plainly: the fortune killed you and it won't even notice. Money doesn't mourn. People do, badly, briefly, and then they buy new curtains.*

*You are buried now beside the graves you walked past every Sunday. Father Anselm said the words. Half the town came, including people who spent eleven months calling you a runaway and a disgrace. They cried. I catalogued their faces the way I catalogue everything, and I will tell you honestly: some of the grief was real. Grief usually is, even when the love underneath it was counterfeit.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you rest.*

*My father's name is in these pages. Tom Eastwick, witness to the survey, the man whose signature helped legitimize the lie. I spent most of this investigation terrified of what I'd find about him. Here is what I found: he suspected. He kept his own copy of the real survey. He wrote a note — "If anything happens to me, ask Ambrose why the river moved" — and he died before he could ask the question out loud. He wasn't guilty, Ada. He was afraid, and slow, and then he was dead, and those three things together look so much like complicity from the outside. I know, because I looked at them from the outside for a long time.*

*You weren't guilty either. You were a woman who found something true in a town built on a comfortable lie, and you refused to put it down. That's the whole of it. That's enough.*

*This account is closed. Not finished — you don't get to be finished; none of us do — but closed, the way a ledger closes: totals entered, balances struck, the truth written down where the next person can find it.*

*Rest. The herons are still in the marsh. I checked.*

*M.E.*

---

* * *

I sat back when I was done and discovered my tea had gone cold and my face was wet, which annoyed me on principle. I am not a woman who cries over paperwork. I am a woman who cries over nothing at odd hours and calls it allergies, like a civilized person.

The pencil had pressed grooves into the page. Good. Pencil fades; grooves persist. Even the medium argued for permanence.

I read it through once more, hunting for errors the way I hunt for everything, and found only one thing I'd change: I'd written *hanged a constable,* and technically the marsh had done the hanging, or the drowning, or whatever the marsh does — we never did recover enough of him for the inquest to be satisfying. But I let it stand. Precision matters less than truth sometimes, and the truth was that Ambrose Pell had been caught, and caught is caught, however the catching kills you.

Then came the practical question, and here I'll admit to a moment of genuine cowardice. Because the correct procedure was to return the diary to evidence, disclose the addition, and let a prosecutor decide whether a coroner's handwritten coda constituted tampering. And the correct procedure would very likely have resulted in the page being removed, or the diary sealed until after trial, or some clerk deciding that Entry Forty-Two was sentimental contamination and shredding it into the recycling bin of history.

I have broken procedure before. I broke it when I staged my own death in a boathouse with a decoy envelope and waited alone in the fog for a killer to come collect me, which Rhee still hasn't entirely forgiven, mostly because it worked.

So I did what I do. I photographed every page — the whole diary, including mine — and sent copies to three places: Rhee, the state investigator in charge, and a solicitor in Ashcombe who owes me a favor and stores things reliably. Then I returned the original to evidence with a signed disclosure attached, daring anyone to call it improper. Let them argue. The argument itself would preserve the page. Nobody reads a document harder than when they're deciding whether to destroy it.

Rhee called that evening.

"You added an entry." His voice had the particular flatness he deploys when he's decided to be calm about something and wants credit for it.

"I closed the account. There's a difference."

"There's a difference," he agreed, "between following your heart and following procedure, and you have never once in my acquaintance followed procedure."

"I followed it to the boathouse. Look how well that ended."

"A man died in a bog, Mirabel."

"And before that, four people died in silence, and the town slept soundly, and everyone agreed what a fine job everyone was doing." I heard myself and softened. "I'm sorry. I'm tired. It's been a long year."

A pause on the line. Rain against my kitchen window, the particular patient rain of Hollowmere in autumn, the kind that seems to be taking notes.

"Can I ask you something?" Rhee said. "And you don't have to answer tonight."

"That's usually a sign the question is terrible."

"How did you keep going?" He said it simply, no drama in it, which somehow made it worse. "After the suspension. After they tore your case apart and called your evidence planted and half the town spat at your boots. I watched you sit at that kitchen table with your father's papers every night, and I kept thinking, she should quit, anyone sane would quit, and you just — kept going. I've been wanting to ask for months. It seemed like the wrong moment. Then it seemed like it would always be the wrong moment."

I looked at the diary on my table. At my father's box beside it, lid slightly askew, the note inside that had started the avalanche: *ask Ambrose why the river moved.* At the window, where the fog was coming in off the marsh the way it always did, indifferent and ancient, taking the town into itself for the night.

"Honestly?" I said. "Because quitting was the one thing left that I hadn't tried."

And that was true, as far as it went. But it wasn't all of it, and Rhee had earned more than the short version, and the short version was already turning over in my chest looking for its better halves — my father, and Ada, and the strange arithmetic of grief that says you carry the dead forward or you put them down, and I have never once in my life managed to put anyone down.

"Come round tomorrow," I said. "Bring the good biscuits, not the ones you pretend are biscuits. I'll tell you the long version."

"It's a long version?"

"Rhee. I've got forty-one of her entries and thirty-eight chapters of my own." I closed the diary gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room. "Of course it's long."
