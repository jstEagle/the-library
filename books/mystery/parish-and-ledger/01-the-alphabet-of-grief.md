## 1. The Alphabet of Grief

The condolence cards had been arriving for eleven days, and Quentin Underhill had alphabetized every one of them.

It was not, they would have told you if anyone had thought to ask, an act of coldness. It was an act of care. Grief, in Quentin's experience — and their experience was considerable, having spent twelve years as archivist of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral, a building that was essentially grief with a roof on it — grief did not respond well to chaos. Grief wanted a system. Grief wanted to know that someone, somewhere, had noticed the difference between *Ashworth* and *Ashe*, between the two separate families named *Pike* who had apparently not spoken since 1987, and had honored that silence by filing them apart.

So: A through Z, in four long trays borrowed from the map drawers, each card slotted into its place like a small paper congregation. The Ashworths. The Bickfords. The Cattermoles, whose card smelled faintly of sherry and had been written by three different hands, suggesting either a family effort or a very long evening.

Quentin worked at the long oak table in the archive's reading room, where the light came down through leaded windows in soft grey coins and landed on everything equally, without favoritism. That was one of the things Quentin loved about this room. The windows did not care whether you were a bishop or a boy come in to look up his grandfather's burial record. Light fell. Records waited. Everything could be found, if you were patient enough to look.

Canon Edgar Mowbray would have appreciated the filing system. He had said so once, years ago, standing in the doorway of the archive with his coat still buttoned against the weather, watching Quentin sort a decade of parish magazines into chronological order.

"You know what you are, Underhill?" he'd said. "You're the only person in this cathedral who believes tomorrow will happen."

Quentin had taken it as a compliment. They were fairly sure it had been one. With Edgar Mowbray you could never be entirely certain; he distributed kindness the way some men distribute loose change — generously, but always with a little rattle, as though expecting something back.

He had died nine days ago, in his sleep, in the canonry, aged seventy-four. Peacefully, everyone agreed. *Peacefully* was the word that appeared in the obituary in the *Aldhelm Courier*, in the notice pinned to the cathedral door, in the mouths of every member of the congregation who pressed Quentin's hand at the churchyard and murmured it like a password. *At least he went peacefully.*

The funeral had been well attended. The cathedral had been full in that particular way it filled for the deaths of men who had outlived most of their enemies — pews packed with the old families, the Wexfords and the Harrowers and the Cattermoles, the mayor in his chain, the headmaster of St. Aldhelm's School with his jaw set like a man enduring a headache. Bishop Crane had preached the eulogy himself, which was unusual, and had done it beautifully, which was more unusual still. *A servant of this house for fifty years,* he had said, his voice rolling up under the vaulting like weather. *Edgar Mowbray kept our records, our accounts, and our conscience.*

Quentin, sitting in the third row with the other staff, had thought at the time that it was an odd trinity. Records, accounts, conscience. But then, Edgar had been all three, in his way. He had been sub-cantor and keeper of the muniments and, unofficially, the person the town came to when it needed something remembered properly. People brought him their disputes, their old deeds, their grievances going back generations. He settled them with a cup of tea and a citation from a minute book. He was the closest thing Aldhelm had to a court of appeal.

And now he was filed away himself, in the churchyard extension, third row from the yew, between a Mrs. Dorothea Pike (of the speaking Pikes) and a flat stone belonging to nobody in particular that the masons had never gotten around to engraving.

Quentin had organized the funeral flowers too. Alphabetically by sender. It had seemed the only possible tribute.

* * *

By half past four the trays were full, and Quentin sat back and looked at them and felt the specific tiredness that comes after a task is finished and there is nothing left between you and yourself.

The archive was quiet in the way it always was at that hour. Mrs. Tallow, the head verger, had been through twice already on her rounds — once to say nothing in particular about the heating, once to stand in the doorway for a moment looking at the trays of cards with an expression Quentin couldn't read before saying, "Very tidy," and going. Mrs. Tallow approved of tidiness the way the sea approves of boats: constantly, and with an undercurrent of menace.

"Very tidy," Quentin echoed now, to the empty room, and heard how thin it sounded.

That was the thing about the days after a funeral. The cathedral emptied out the way a theatre does. For a week everyone had a role — mourners, ushers, bearers, the choir singing the requiem they sang perhaps twice a decade — and then the roles ended and the building went back to being a building, and you went back to being whoever you had been before, except that one of the people who made your life make sense was no longer in it.

Quentin's life, examined honestly, was not crowded. There was the archive, open six days a week. There was the flat over the vestry, with its single window facing the cloister garth. There was Thursday supper with Mrs. Tallow, who cooked as though the war might come back any day and provisions must be laid in accordingly. There was a sister in Bristol who rang at Christmas and Easter and whenever she saw something in a shop window that reminded her of their mother, which was her way of saying she missed them both. And there had been Edgar, dropping in on Tuesdays and Fridays with questions about the 1930s restoration papers, or complaints about the new hymn numbers, or sometimes no pretext at all — just Edgar, settling into the chair opposite the table with a sigh like a ship coming to anchor, saying, "Well, Underhill. What have we got?"

*We.* As though the archive belonged to both of them. As though Quentin's twelve years here had been an apprenticeship under a master who never quite admitted he was teaching.

There would be no more Fridays. Quentin understood this intellectually. The rest of them — the hands that kept reaching for the teapot on its shelf, the ear that kept listening for the cloister door — had not yet been informed.

They were reaching for the teapot when the letter came.

Not by hand. Not through the cathedral post, which arrived at ten with the verger's round and was opened at the long table in plain sight, because an archivist who opened mail privately was an archivist with something to hide. This letter came the ordinary way, pushed through the outer door of the vestry stair sometime after five, when the porter had gone and the great west doors were locked and the only sounds in the whole vast stone organism of the place were the tick of the cooling radiators and, distantly, Mrs. Tallow locking up the north transept, her keys chiming against each other like small bells.

Quentin found it on the mat at twenty past five, when they went down to put the kettle on. A white envelope, lying face-down on the worn stone. No name on the front. Just:

**Q. UNDERHILL, THE ARCHIVE, ST. ALDHELM'S**

Typed. Not handwritten — typed, on what looked like an actual typewriter, the letters slightly unevenly struck, the *Q* a fraction high. Nobody in Aldhelm owned a typewriter anymore except the solicitors' firm of Harrower & Pike, and they used franked envelopes with their name embossed in the corner, because solicitors believed in announcing themselves the way heraldic beasts believed in roaring.

Quentin stood on the stairs holding it for a moment. Then, because curiosity is a discipline like any other and should be practiced daily, they carried it back up to the reading room, set it on the table under the lamp, and opened it with the bone folder they used for cutting the string on record bundles. Cleanly. Preserving the envelope.

Inside was a single sheet, folded once. Typed, same machine, same slightly drunken *Q*. No signature. No date.

Three lines.

> **He did not die in his sleep.**
>
> **Check the 1998 ledger.**
>
> **Burn this.**

* * *

For a long moment Quentin did not move. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, Mrs. Tallow's keys fell silent, which meant she had reached the vestry stair and was, presumably, about to call up her usual farewell — "Mind the gas, Mr. Underhill" — a sentence she had delivered every weekday evening for twelve years despite the fact that Quentin had never once located the gas in question.

"Mr. Underhill?" Her voice floated up the stairwell.

"Yes, Mrs. Tallow."

"Mind the gas."

"I will."

Her footsteps descended. The outer door boomed shut. And Quentin was alone with the letter and the four trays of alphabetized grief and the enormous, humming silence of a building that had just stopped being the place it had been thirty seconds ago.

They read it again. Then a third time, aloud, quietly, because saying a thing aloud sometimes revealed whether it was nonsense.

"He did not die in his sleep." Nonsense, surely. Edgar had been old and tired and had complained for months of his heart — *this old engine's misfiring, Underhill,* he'd said in October, thumping his chest cheerfully. He had gone to bed on the ninth of December and not woken. Dr. Loxley had signed the certificate without hesitation. *Cardiac failure, natural causes.* There had been no inquest because there had been nothing to hold one about. Everyone had said so. Everyone had agreed.

Everyone.

"Check the 1998 ledger."

This was the line that caught, the way a fishhook catches — not painfully at first, but with a slow, deepening insistence. Because Quentin knew exactly what the 1998 ledger was. There was only one ledger anyone at St. Aldhelm's called *the* ledger of any given year: the cathedral's own account book, the great bound volume in which the treasurer recorded every payment in and every payment out, every candle invoice and roofer's bill and honorarium, in Edgar's beautiful, upright, incorruptible hand. Edgar had kept the ledgers himself for forty years. He had taught Quentin the system: red ink for income, black for expenditure, and never, ever a rounding-off, because, he said, God could cope with odd pence but auditors could not.

The 1998 ledger lived in the strong room off the east aisle, shelf fourteen, between its siblings, in strict chronological order, because even money deserved to be filed.

Quentin knew it the way they knew their own handwriting. Which meant they also knew — with a small cold click, somewhere behind the sternum — that they had not looked at it in some time. Months. Since the summer audit, certainly. And that in all the years of knowing it, they had never once thought of it as anything but what it appeared to be: a dull, honest record of a dull, honest year.

*Check the 1998 ledger.* As though it were a door. As though behind the columns of figures there were a room.

And the third line. *Burn this.*

Quentin looked at the letter, and then at the envelope, and turned the envelope over and looked at the postmark, because an archivist looks at postmarks the way a doctor looks at eyes.

**ALDHELM — 11 DEC.**

The eleventh of December. The day after the burial.

Whoever had written this had posted it the day after they had stood at the graveside and watched Edgar Mowbray go into the ground. Had walked, presumably, from the churchyard or from one of the two postboxes in town — the one outside the Courier office, the one by the quarry road — and dropped it into the slot, and let it take its slow two-day journey through the sorting office and back to the cathedral, arriving tonight, while the condolence cards were still being filed.

Which meant the writer had known, on the eleventh of December, something that no one else in Aldhelm appeared to know.

Or had decided, on the eleventh of December, that it was time for someone else to know it.

Quentin sat down slowly in Edgar's chair — it was Edgar's chair really, though officially it belonged to the archive and to whoever kept it — and laid the letter flat on the table, and smoothed it once with both hands, the way they smoothed creases out of old maps.

The sensible thing — and Quentin was, by profession and temperament and the accumulated evidence of forty-one years, a sensible person — was to put the letter in a folder, label the folder, and take it to the bishop in the morning. *Someone has sent me this foolishness, my lord, and I thought you should see it.* Bishop Crane would frown kindly, and say something sonorous about cranks and grief, and the letter would go into a drawer, and Friday would arrive without Edgar, and life would resume its orderly shelving.

That was the sensible thing.

But Quentin's eye had already gone, traitorously, to the dark doorway of the strong room. Shelf fourteen. The 1998 ledger, standing between 1997 and 1999, spine unbroken by anything so vulgar as suspicion.

It was nearly six o'clock. The cathedral was locked. The town was closing its shutters. Outside the leaded windows the December dusk was coming down over the close like a lid.

"One look," Quentin said to the empty room, and was immediately ashamed of how much they sounded like Edgar.

They stood, took the ring of keys from the hook by the door — the heavy iron ring that had been passed from keeper to keeper for a hundred and sixty years, that Edgar had handed over to them with both hands and a strange formality, like a sword — and went to find out what a dead man's account book had to say for itself.

The strong room door swung open onto cold air and the smell of old paper and, beneath it, something else. Something Quentin would spend many nights afterward trying to name, and would finally name, much later, as the smell of a room that has recently been visited.

Shelf fourteen was where it had always been.

The 1998 ledger was not.
