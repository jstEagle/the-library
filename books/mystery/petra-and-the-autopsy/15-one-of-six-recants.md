## 15. One of Six Recants

The pain woke him at four in the morning, the way it always did now, arriving like a tenant who no longer bothered to knock. Tom Bricker lay in his bed in the house on Corliss Street and let it wash through him, wave after wave, his hands curled into the sheets like a man holding onto a raft. The morphine was on the nightstand. He didn't take it. He had begun to think of the pain as a kind of penance, though he knew that was vanity—real penance would have been thirty years ago, when it might have cost him something.

He got up because lying down had become its own kind of dying. He shuffled to the kitchen in his robe, put the kettle on out of habit, and stood looking at the window over the sink, where the dark reflected the room back at him: an old man in a thin robe, translucent as a ghost already, waiting for the light.

On the table sat two things. A pack of cigarettes he wasn't allowed anymore. And a yellow legal pad with three lines written on it in a hand that shook like a leaf in wind:

*It was not an accident.*

*Marcus made us all into witnesses.*

*Ada was there.*

He had written those lines eleven days ago, after Yara Amberdale's second visit, and he hadn't been able to write a fourth. Every night since, he came down and looked at them and told himself tomorrow. Tomorrow he would call her. Tomorrow he would finish it.

Tomorrow was running out. Dr. Feldman had used the word *weeks* in the tone doctors use for *days*, and his daughter Sarah was coming Sunday from Spokane, and he could not—he would not—let her find the legal pad after he was gone. Let her find nothing. Let the paper burn with the rest of him.

Or let him finally be a sheriff, one time, at the end, when it couldn't matter to anyone but the dead.

The kettle screamed. He took it off without making tea.

* * *

Yara Amberdale came at nine, unannounced, which he respected. She'd called twice; he'd let it ring. But she was the kind of woman who understood that some doors only open if you stand outside them long enough, and so she stood on his porch in the gray morning with a folder under her arm and no smile on her face, and when he opened the door before she knocked, she didn't look surprised.

"Sheriff Bricker."

"Coroner." He stepped back. "Coffee's terrible. I don't have any."

"I noticed you didn't offer."

"Come in anyway."

She sat across from him at the kitchen table—the same table where he'd signed off on forty years of Petra Hollow's tidy paperwork—and set the folder down but didn't open it. Her eyes went to the legal pad. He watched her read it upside down, watched her decide not to mention it yet. Smart woman. Patient. That patience scared him more than Delia Voss's charm ever had, because Delia wanted something from you and you knew what it was, and this woman just wanted the truth, and the truth was a thing he had spent his whole life learning to live without.

"You're dying," she said. Not cruelly. Just establishing the terrain.

"Pancreatic. The kind you don't argue with." He lowered himself into his chair, grateful for it. "You've been to see Eli Merrow."

"Yes."

"And he told you he wrote a letter."

"He did."

"And you want to know why I'm not surprised." Bricker looked at his hands. They had been strong once; he'd carried drowning men out of the quarry with them, or he would have, if anyone had ever asked him to. "I'll tell you what I told myself for thirty years, Ms. Amberdale. I told myself I didn't kill anybody. I told myself I arrived too late, that by the time I got to the water she was gone, that the ones who pushed her were children, drunk and stupid, and that Marcus Voss was a grown man with a future and a father on the county commission, and that if I wrote down what really happened, six families would burn and I'd burn with them and Cass Merrow would still be dead."

He stopped. The words had come easier than he expected, like water behind a bad dam.

"That's the version I slept on," he said. "Most nights."

"And other nights?"

"Other nights I remembered that I was the law. That's the whole of it. I was the law, and I chose the town over the girl, and then I spent thirty years being paid in gratitude to keep choosing it." He laughed, a dry rattle. "You know what they gave me? A plaque. *For thirty years of distinguished service.* They hung it in the station. Rosa Okafor dusts around it every week and doesn't know what it's for."

"The hush money," Yara said quietly. "In Harrow's ledger. Your signature is on the authorizations."

"Every year. First of March. Like taxes." He met her eyes. "I drove the check to Ruth Merrow myself, the first year. She wouldn't take it. Did you know that? She threw it at me in her own doorway. Said, *You bring me money instead of my daughter's body.* So the next year we sent it through the church charity fund, and the year after that she was too sick to refuse, and by the time she died we'd bought ourselves a clean conscience for the price of eight hundred dollars a year, split six ways." His voice cracked on the number. Eight hundred dollars. He'd spent more than that on his truck.

Yara opened the folder at last. "Tom. The car. The damage on the rear quarter panel—it matches a vehicle in the school lot that night. We know whose car followed hers to the quarry. What I need from you is the order of events. Who did what. Because right now everyone alive is pointing at everyone dead."

Bricker closed his eyes. Behind them, the quarry at night, 1994, the smell of gasoline and lake weed and teenage sweat, the moon like a hole punched in the sky.

* * *

They had been drinking at the point above the deep water, the six of them—Harrow with his father's whiskey, Delia with her laugh that carried across the water, Marcus Voss already the kind of handsome that made adults forgive things, and the others: Ada Renner, quiet Ada, who'd come because Marcus asked; young Tom Bricker himself, twenty-six, deputized eight months, invited because Marcus said *you're one of us now, Tommy*; and the sixth, the one nobody talked about, the one who'd left town the following spring and never come back.

Cass Merrow had come alone, walking, in her mother's old Datsun. She'd parked at the top of the access road. Bricker saw the headlights sweep the trees. Everyone did.

"She came to confront Marcus," he said now, to Yara, in the kitchen that smelled of sickness and coffee he hadn't made. "There'd been a party at the Harrows' the weekend before. Something happened there—I never learned exactly what, and God help me, I stopped asking. Cass threatened to tell. About Marcus. About—" His hand trembled toward the legal pad and stopped. "About what he was. What he did to girls when he thought no one would believe them."

"Cass was pregnant," Yara said. It wasn't a question. "We found it in the autopsy photographs Harrow suppressed."

Bricker nodded slowly, as if the nod cost him something physical. "She said it in front of all of us. Standing at the edge of the water with the whole night listening. She said, *Everyone's going to know what you are, Marcus.* And he laughed. That's what I can't get out of my head—not the pushing. The laughing. He laughed and said, *Who's going to believe you?*"

"And then?"

"And then it happened fast." Bricker's voice had gone flat, the flatness of a man reading from a transcript he'd memorized against his will. "There was shoving—I never saw whose hands landed first, I swear that on anything you want, there were six of us and it was dark and it was seconds. She went off the edge. The drop is twelve feet there. You know the spot. There's a rail now." He swallowed. "She hit the water and she didn't come up, and for a moment nobody moved, and then Marcus said, calm as a man ordering lunch, *Nobody goes in. Nobody calls anybody. Everybody go home.* And Ada—"

He stopped. The kettle sat cold on the stove. Outside, a crow argued with the morning.

"Ada Renner was the one who said it first," he finished. "*What if she tells someone she was here?* Not *is she alive.* Not *can we save her.* Whether Cass would talk. That's when I understood what I was standing in the middle of. And I ran. I walked back to my truck and I drove home and I told myself I'd figure it out in the morning, and in the morning Marcus was in my kitchen with coffee made and a story ready, and by noon Harrow had ruled it accidental, and by Friday the quarry was full of rain and the whole town had agreed, the way towns do, to remember nothing."

Yara was writing. He watched her write and felt the strange lightness of a man setting down weight he'd forgotten he carried.

"Marcus died in 2011," she said. "Heart attack, on the golf course. The town named the library annex after him."

"I sent flowers." Bricker smiled, and it was an ugly thing. "I've been sending flowers to that family for thirty years."

"But you said—" She turned the legal pad gently toward herself, read his three lines aloud. "*Marcus made us all into witnesses. Ada was there.*" She looked up. "Tom. Is that all Ada was? Someone who was there?"

And here it was. The fourth line. The one he hadn't been able to write.

Bricker got up, slowly, joints announcing themselves, and went to the cabinet above the refrigerator where Sarah thought he kept the good glasses. He brought down a shoebox, soft with age, and set it on the table between them.

"Two weeks after the funeral," he said, "Ruth Merrow came to the station. She said Cass had called her that night from the pay phone at the Gas-N-Go, ten o'clock, said she was going to the quarry to settle something and she'd be home by midnight. Ruth wanted it in the record. I told her the record was closed." He opened the box. Inside was a cassette tape, unlabeled, and a single sheet of paper in his younger, steadier hand. "But I took her statement. Off the record. Because some part of me—even then, even coward Tom Bricker—knew I'd need it someday, or she would."

"Why didn't you ever use it?"

"Because using it meant admitting I'd buried it." He tapped the box. "That's the arithmetic of a coward, Ms. Amberdale. Every year you wait makes the confession bigger than the crime. Until the confession is the crime." He slid the box toward her. "Statement's dated. Notarized by Harrow's secretary, God rot her efficient heart. Ruth's voice is on the tape. It's enough to reopen everything."

Yara took the box with both hands, careful, the way she'd handle evidence, the way she'd handled the letter. "Thank you," she said, and he waved it off because thanks were another debt he couldn't carry.

"There's one more thing." He sat back down. This was the part that had kept him awake past the pain, past the morphine, past everything. "You're building a case against the living. Delia. Harrow's estate. Whoever mailed the letters. But listen to me, and listen carefully, because I only figured this out last month, going through my old files while I still had the strength to lift them."

He leaned forward. Up close, Yara could see what the cancer had written on him, the jaundice at his collar, the map of veins at his temple.

"The second letter. The one that threatened Eli. Quoted things only the six could know." He waited for her nod. "I didn't write it. Harrow's dead. Marcus is dead. Delia's too smart to put threats on paper. Which leaves the sixth." He held her gaze. "The one who left town in '95 and came back seven years ago and took a job where she could watch every file, every permit, every record that touched that night. The one who was standing at the edge saying *what if she talks* before Cass even stopped breathing."

"Ada Renner is police chief," Yara said slowly.

"Ada Renner finished what Marcus started," Bricker said. "Marcus built the lie. Ada has been *maintaining* it—for thirty years, alone, while the rest of us aged out or died off. She suspended your deputy. She's watching your archive. And she knows I've been sick, and she knows sick men talk." He smiled again, that ugly smile. "Which means, Coroner, that by telling you this, I've probably signed whatever's left of my death warrant. So do me one courtesy."

"Name it."

"Don't let them give me a plaque."

* * *

After Yara left, with the shoebox zipped inside her bag like a heartbeat she was carrying somewhere, Bricker stood at the kitchen window a long time. The light had come up properly now, gilding the roofs of the town he had served and betrayed in equal measure, smoke rising straight from chimneys, a dog trotting down Corliss Street with the confidence of a citizen.

He thought about calling Sarah. He thought about the morphine. He thought about Cass Merrow, seventeen forever, waiting at the bottom of everything.

Then he picked up the legal pad, and beneath the three trembling lines, in a hand that shook worse than ever and pressed harder than ever, he wrote the fourth:

*I should have gone into the water.*

He set down the pen. Somewhere across town, he had no doubt, a police chief was reading a duty roster, deciding who to trust, who to watch, who to bury. Let her read it. The dam had broken; the water was moving; and Tom Bricker, who had spent a lifetime standing on the shore, had finally, at the very end, waded in.

At two that afternoon, a county coroner's request to exhume grave 114-B in the Voss plot landed on Police Chief Ada Renner's desk.

She read it twice. Then she reached for her coat.
