## 6. Cipher One: The Tide Tables

The tide tables hung in the boathouse the way a saint's portrait hangs in a chapel—faded, sun-struck, and treated with a reverence nobody could quite explain.

Bram found them at seven the next morning, after a night of sleeping badly in the east bedroom with its water-stained ceiling and its smell of brine and old varnish. He'd woken twice to the sound of the house settling, and once—though he refused to swear to it—to footsteps on the stairs below, unhurried and light, like someone who had every right to be there. By the time he'd made it down with the lamp, the foyer was empty and the front door was still bolted from the inside.

He hadn't mentioned it to Teo. Teo had enough opinions about him already.

The boathouse stood at the end of a collapsed stone path behind the garden, its doors swollen shut with seven years of salt air. It took him twenty minutes and a length of pipe to force them, and when they finally gave, the smell that rolled out was the smell of the diary itself—damp paper, rope, the sea kept at bay by inches. Morning light came through the gaps in the planking in long pale blades, and in one of those blades hung the tide tables: a framed chart of the year Marisol Petra disappeared, annotated in her narrow, slanted hand.

Beneath the frame, a brass plate. Beneath the plate, a keyhole.

He'd brought the diary. He'd started carrying it everywhere two days ago, against his own better judgment, the way a man carries a wound. Now he set it on the workbench, opened it to the entry he'd read a dozen times since the boathouse had appeared in its margins—*the tables keep the hours I couldn't*—and stood there in the salt light feeling, for the first time since Vane & Associates had handed him a folder that shouldn't exist, like he was actually doing the work he'd been hired to do.

*You'll want the year I left,* the entry read. *Not the year they think I left. The tables know the difference.*

He looked up at the frame. The chart was for the current year—this year, the year of the will, the year of everything. Someone had replaced it. Someone had stood in this rotting boathouse, recently, and hung a fresh tide table in the place of the old one.

The annotations were in Marisol's hand. He was certain of it now, in a way he hadn't been certain of anything since the reading of the will. He'd spent an evening with the diary and an evening with the estate's surviving correspondence, and the hand was the same—the same hard downstrokes, the same way the letter *r* ended in a small defiant hook, like the pen had been yanked away mid-thought. Seven years of writing in a hand that belonged to a dead woman.

But the dates on the annotations were wrong. That was the first thing he saw, and the thing that made his skin go cold in the warm boathouse air.

The tide tables ran January to December. Marisol had annotated them in a spiral—starting not at January but at October, the month of her disappearance, and winding forward through the year, marking certain tides with small circled numbers. One through nine, then the numbers stopped, and after nine there was only a single word at the December page, written small and pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the sheet beneath:

*ENOUGH.*

Bram took out his phone, photographed the chart from four angles, and then did the thing he'd been avoiding since Salt Hollow had first refused to give him a straight answer about anything: he sat down on an upturned lobster crate and started to do arithmetic.

* * *

The cipher, when it finally broke, broke the way all good ciphers break—not with a flash of insight but with a slow, humiliating slide, the lock turning because he'd been pushing on it wrong for an hour and then, suddenly, right.

The circled numbers were tides. Not tide heights—tide *times*. He'd been reading the annotations as data when they were coordinates. Each circled number corresponded to a high tide on the annotated date, and the number itself was the hour of that tide, measured not from midnight but from the moment of the previous low water. A tide clock. Marisol had been a cartographer; she'd thought in offsets and datums the way Bram thought in clauses and codicils.

Nine circled tides. Nine hours, offset from nine low waters. He wrote them down the way he'd learned to write down witness statements—verbatim, in order, without interpretation:

7. 4. 11. 2. 9. 6. 1. 8. 3.

He stared at the sequence for a long time. Then he did what the diary had been telling him to do since the first entry, the entry addressed to him by name in a dead woman's hand: *Count backward, Bram. Everything I've hidden, I hid facing the way I came.*

Backward. 3. 8. 1. 6. 9. 2. 11. 4. 7.

He mapped them to letters—1 through 12, the twelve hours of the tide clock—and got C, H, A, F, I, B, K, D, G. Nonsense. He mapped them forward instead: C was the third letter, so third from the start; but the diary said backward, so he tried third from the *end* of the alphabet—X. Eighth from the end: S. First from the end: Z.

X, S, Z. Worse nonsense.

He put the pen down and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. Outside, the tide was coming in; he could hear it working at the pilings beneath him, patient as a lawyer. The boathouse floor was maybe four feet above the waterline at high tide, and the water was already darkening the lowest strata of the stone path behind him. He had perhaps two hours of dry footing left, and he was spending them failing at a child's substitution puzzle.

*The tables keep the hours I couldn't.*

Hours. Not letters. He'd been so busy turning hours into letters that he'd never asked what the hours themselves were trying to say. He looked at the sequence again—7, 4, 11, 2, 9, 6, 1, 8, 3—and then at the chart, at the dates each circled number sat beside, and did the thing he should have done first.

He checked the dates against the diary.

Every annotated date had an entry. He'd read them all, twice, in the first feverish night at the estate, and had catalogued them as grief—Marisol writing to him across the void of her own declared death, day after day, about the house, the town, the sea, the slow rot of everything she'd built. But now, pulling the diary onto his knees and turning to the entry for the first annotated date—October 14th—he read it differently.

*The cellar floods at the seventh hour after low water. You'll want to be gone by then, or you'll want to be found, and those are not the same wish.*

The seventh hour. The first circled number was 7.

He turned to October 19th. *The chapel key is where the fourth hour keeps its secrets.* Fourth. The second number was 4.

November 2nd: *Eleven steps down from the chart room, the wall is a door.* Eleven.

Bram's hands had started to shake, and he let them. The nine circled numbers weren't a word. They were a *route*. Nine instructions, scattered through nine diary entries, each one pointing to a place in the house or on the grounds, each one keyed to a tide, because the places themselves were keyed to the water—the cellar that flooded, the wall that opened, whatever waited at the chapel and the chart room and the eight other stations of a treasure hunt a missing woman had built for him out of tide tables and grief.

She hadn't hidden a message. She'd hidden a *map*. And she'd written it in the only ink she could trust—the sea's own schedule, the one thing in Salt Hollow nobody had thought to burn.

* * *

He was still sitting on the crate, the diary open across his knees, when the boathouse doors groaned.

Not the wind. The doors had swollen shut—he'd needed a pipe and twenty minutes and most of a shoulder to open them, and nothing the wind did was going to close them again. This was a hand on the wood. A slow push, testing.

Bram closed the diary. He didn't stand; standing put you in the light blades, and the light blades put you on display. He stayed on the crate, in the shadow of the workbench, and watched the gap between the doors widen by an inch, and then stop.

"You left the pipe in the door," Teo's voice said, from the other side. "Amateur."

Bram let out a breath he'd been holding since the doors had first moved. "How did you find me?"

"Followed the noise of you swearing at a puzzle." The doors scraped wider and Teo came through sideways, sixteen years old, hollow-cheeked, wearing a raincoat two sizes too big and an expression of permanent grievance that Bram had learned, over four days, to read as a kind of affection. Teo's eyes went straight past him to the workbench. To the diary. To the tide chart above it.

"You're not supposed to be out here," Bram said.

"Nobody's supposed to be anywhere. That's the whole problem with this town." Teo crossed to the chart and looked at it for a long moment, and something moved across the kid's face—something Bram couldn't name and didn't like. "You figured out the numbers."

It wasn't a question. Bram stood slowly. "You knew about this."

"Everybody who ever cleaned this boathouse knew about the chart. Old man Odile's brother used to say Miss Petra talked to it. Like it was a person." Teo shrugged, the raincoat shrugging with him. "Nobody ever figured the numbers. People figured they were just her being strange. She was allowed to be strange. She was rich."

"And you didn't think to mention a tide chart covered in her handwriting to the estate lawyer handling her impossible will."

Teo looked at him then, and for a second the grievance dropped and something older and wearier looked out—something that had watched adults fail this town for a long time. "You didn't ask."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and by noon Bram had the route.

Nine stations. He wrote them on the back of a property inventory sheet in his neat lawyer's hand, and the list looked, he thought, like the estate plan of a woman who had understood exactly what she was doing: the cellar door that flooded at the seventh hour; the chapel on the headland path, keyed to the fourth; the chart room wall, eleven steps down; and six more, each stranger, each more specific—the boathouse itself, the well behind the kitchen garden, a seam in the sea wall below the north terrace that only showed at a certain tide. A circuit of the estate, mapped in hours and water, ending at the December page with that single embossed word.

*ENOUGH.*

"Enough what?" Teo asked, reading upside down.

"I don't know." Bram folded the sheet into his breast pocket, over his heart, which felt melodramatic and also correct. "Enough hiding. Enough evidence. Enough of me writing to a lawyer who might be too much of a coward to come." He looked at the chart one last time. "Or enough of someone else's patience."

"Whose else would there be?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Seven years ago, a detective named Voss had stood in the foyer of this house—Bram had seen the visit logged in the estate's correspondence, three days after the disappearance, a single line, no notes—and declared the case closed on a missing woman with no body, no note, and no reason to run. Seven years later, a will arrived at Bram's firm with postage that didn't match its postmark and a diary that kept writing after its author died. Somewhere between those two facts, someone had replaced the tide chart in this rotting boathouse with a fresh one, annotated in a dead woman's hand, and had left it hanging in the light for him to find.

Either Marisol Petra was alive, and leading him by the nose through her own estate like a lawyer-shaped bloodhound.

Or someone wanted him to believe she was—and had just spent considerable effort and a very convincing forgery making sure he'd start digging in exactly the places they chose.

"Teo," Bram said, "when you followed me here. Did anyone follow you?"

The kid's face did the thing it did when it was deciding whether to tell the truth. It took longer this time.

"Grey sedan," Teo said finally. "Been parked up by the chapel gate since yesterday. Nobody in it that I've seen. But it moves, sometimes. And it always comes back."

Bram thought about the footsteps on the stairs in the night. The bolted door. The house that kept its people, and the people who watched the house.

"From now on," he said, "you don't come out here alone. You don't go anywhere alone. And if that sedan is anywhere near you, you walk the other way and you don't look at it."

"You sound like Odile."

"Odile sounds smarter than me every day. Where is she, by the way? I knocked at the cottage this morning."

Something closed in Teo's face then, quick as a trap. "She's not home."

"When will she be?"

"I don't know." Too fast. Too flat. Teo picked up the pipe from the doorway, turned it over once, and set it down with exaggerated care, the way people handle things when they're not looking at you. "She goes places. She's old. Old people do that."

Bram had spent fifteen years reading witnesses, and everything in him that still did that work sat up straight. Odile Reyes had been the last person to see Marisol Petra before the disappearance—or so she'd told him, the second night, over a bottle of plum brandy and a fire that kept needing tending. Odile had keys to the estate that predated the will. Odile had known about the diary's existence before Bram had shown it to anyone. And Odile's cottage, when he'd knocked this morning, had had a light on inside and no one answering, and the smell of something recently burned.

He looked at Teo, who was sixteen and loyal and a very bad liar, and decided not to press. Not yet. The route came first. The route, and whatever waited at the first station—the cellar, the seventh hour, the water.

Outside, the tide had begun to turn. He could hear it in the pilings, the long slow exhale of the sea giving the stones back.

"Come on," he said, and pocketed the diary. "We've got about six hours until low water, and I want to see what's behind the cellar door before the seventh hour floods it again."

Teo fell into step beside him on the ruined path, and neither of them looked back at the boathouse—so neither of them saw, until they reached the garden and Bram glanced up at the headland out of pure habit, that the grey sedan by the chapel gate had started its engine.

It didn't drive toward town.

It drove down, slowly, toward the estate.
