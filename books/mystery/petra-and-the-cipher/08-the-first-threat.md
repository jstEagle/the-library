## 8. The First Threat

Bram woke to the sound of the sea doing something wrong.

He'd been in Salt Hollow nine days now, long enough to learn its rhythms — the gulls at dawn like a fistful of thrown keys, the foghorn out at the point sounding every ninety seconds as if the coast itself were counting, the tide going out each afternoon with a long wet exhale that seemed to pull the whole town a little closer to the water. He'd catalogued the sounds the way he catalogued everything, in the margins of his legal pad: *normal, normal, normal.* A man learns a place the way he learns a witness. First the surface. Then the tells.

This sound had no tell. It was a scraping, rhythmic, coming from the front of the cottage Odile had rented him — a fisherman's lean-to two streets up from the harbor, all salt-rotted clapboard and a porch that sagged like a tired shoulder. Scraping. Pause. Scraping.

Not the sea. Not the wind. Something being dragged against the door.

Bram lay still in the narrow bed, listening the way he'd learned to listen in twenty years of estate law, which is to say: listening for what a sound *costs* the person making it. This sound was patient. Deliberate. Whoever was on the other side of his door was not trying to be quiet. They were trying to be heard.

He reached for his watch on the nightstand. 3:47 a.m. The foghorn sounded. He counted. Ninety seconds. It sounded again.

The scraping stopped.

Then, through the door, a voice — low, unhurried, pitched to carry no farther than the room he was lying in.

"You should go home, Mr. Vane."

Bram's mouth had gone dry, but his mind — his mind did what it always did, which was to reach for the file. He thought: *he knows my name.* He thought: *he knows I'm not from here.* He thought: *he waited until the foghorn, so I'd have to strain to hear him, so I'd lean closer to the door.* Every detail was a message. The threat wasn't in the words. The threat was in the craft of them.

"I'm sorry," Bram said, keeping his voice level, "who is this?"

Silence. Then, closer — the man had stepped up to the door, and Bram could hear the wet grit of boots on the porch boards, could almost smell the night through the gap at the threshold, cold and iodine and something else, something like pipe smoke.

"You've been asking about the tide tables." A pause. "You've been asking Odile Reyes about the tide tables. She's an old woman with a bad heart and a good memory, and you've made her a problem. I'd like you to unmake her a problem by getting on the morning bus, which leaves at six-forty, and not coming back."

The foghorn sounded. Bram counted it without meaning to. Ninety seconds.

"Who told you I was asking about tide tables?" he said.

"You did," the voice said, and there was something almost gentle in it now, the way a man is gentle with a dog he's about to have put down. "You asked the harbormaster. You asked the woman at the chandlery. You asked the boy who sweeps the pier, and the boy told his mother, and his mother told the wrong person. That's how it works in a town like this, Mr. Vane. Everything you say goes into the water, and the water brings it to me."

Bram sat up slowly, his bare feet finding the cold floor. His heart was going hard, but his hands were steady — they were always steady, a trait that had unnerved more than one opposing counsel. He looked around the dark room for something to write with. His pen was on the table by the window, four steps away. He didn't dare move for it. Instead he began repeating the words to himself, silently, the way he'd once memorized the terms of a will read aloud to him over a bad phone line: *morning bus, six-forty, Odile Reyes, bad heart, good memory, the water brings it to me.*

"You haven't told me who you are," Bram said.

"No," the voice agreed. "I haven't."

"And you haven't told me what happens if I don't take the six-forty bus."

Another silence, longer this time. When the voice came back it had dropped even lower, and Bram had to press his ear almost against the cold wood of the door to hold it.

"Seven years ago a woman walked into this town's memory and erased herself. That was hard work, Mr. Vane. It took a long time and it cost people things they loved. And now you've come along with a piece of paper and a head full of questions, and you want to un-erase her, because it's your job to close files, and this one won't close." The boots shifted. "But here's what your piece of paper doesn't say. Some files aren't open because nobody bothered to close them. Some files are open because closing them would be a confession."

The foghorn. Bram counted. Ninety seconds.

"Is that a threat?" Bram asked.

"It's a tide chart," the voice said. "It comes in, it goes out. You're standing on the sand, counselor. I'd move."

Then the boots went down the porch steps, unhurried, and Bram heard them on the gravel path, and then he heard nothing at all except the foghorn, and the sea, and after a while — faint, from the direction of the harbor — a car engine starting and pulling away.

He sat on the edge of the bed until the sky went grey, writing it all down in the dark, from memory, in the tight backhand he used for things he didn't want anyone else to read.

* * *

By seven he was at Odile's door on Rope Walk, and she took one look at his face and let him in without a word, which told him she already knew.

She was drinking chicory coffee in a kitchen that smelled of cedar and old paper, a small fierce woman of seventy-odd with hands that trembled only when she wanted them to. She'd been Marisol Petra's schoolteacher once, and then her friend, and then — for seven years — one of only three people in Salt Hollow who still said her name out loud. The night before, over this same table, she had told Bram about the tide tables: how Marisol had marked them in the estate diary in a cipher of her own devising, and how the marked dates weren't dates at all but coordinates, and how the first coordinate, when Bram had worked it out at two in the morning with a ruler and the harbor chart Odile had lent him, pointed to the old chapel of charts on the north bluff — the one the town had let fall into the sea's keeping.

Now Odile set a second cup down in front of him and said, "He came to you."

"You knew he would."

"I knew he'd come to one of us." She sat across from him, folding her hands. "I hoped it would be me. I'm cheaper to frighten."

"Who is he?"

"You already know."

"I want to hear you say it."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Outside her window, the harbor was waking up — the chandlery opening its shutters, the boy with the broom starting his slow pass down the pier. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary.

"Detective Emory Voss," she said. "Retired. Thirty-one years with the county. The man who found Marisol's coat on the rocks at Cutter Point and her shoes in the sea cave below it, and wrote in his report that the evidence was *consistent with a fall from the bluff at high water*, and closed the file in eleven days." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "The man who told this town that grief was simple and then left before anyone could look at the arithmetic."

"What arithmetic?"

"Marisol Petra swam every morning of her life, Mr. Vane. Every morning, in all weathers, off that same point. She knew that water the way you know the alphabet. And Emory Voss wrote a report in which a champion swimmer fell off a cliff in the dark at the exact spot she swam past every day of her life, and nobody in this town asked him a single question about it, because he was the detective, and the detective had spoken, and it was easier to bury a woman who was already buried." She picked up her coffee. Her hands were steady now. "So yes. I say his name. I've had seven years to practice."

Bram turned his legal pad toward her, the page dense with his notes from the night. "He said I'd made you a problem. He said you had a bad heart."

"I do have a bad heart." She said it the way she might say *I have brown eyes.* "Mitral valve. I'm on a waiting list for a surgery I may not live long enough to have. He knows that because everyone knows that, because in a town like this everything you say goes into the water." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "He wasn't threatening me, counselor. He was threatening *you*. He was telling you that the people you touch have fragile things inside them, and that he knows where all the fragile things are kept."

Bram looked down at his notes. *The water brings it to me.* "He said some files are open because closing them would be a confession."

Odile set her cup down very carefully, as if the table were made of glass.

"Yes," she said. "He would say that. He's said it before."

"To whom?"

"To me. Seven years ago. I went to him with Marisol's last letter — the real one, not the diary, a letter she posted to me the week before she vanished, saying that if anything happened to her it would be because of what she'd found in the estate's papers. I brought it to Voss and he read it, and then he gave it back to me, and he said —" Odile closed her eyes, retrieving it exactly, a teacher to the end — "he said, *'Mrs. Reyes, some files are open because closing them would be a confession. You should think about what you want this town to remember.'*" She opened her eyes. "And then the letter disappeared. I've looked for it for seven years. It disappeared out of my own house."

The kitchen was very quiet. Out on the water, a boat engine coughed to life.

"He took it," Bram said.

"He took it, or he had it taken, or he told someone to take it, and that someone has spent seven years not asking themselves what they did." Odile leaned forward. "Listen to me. Emory Voss is not a man who does things. He's a man who arranges for things to be done, and then stands at a distance with his hands in his coat pockets, and when the thing is done he is the one who writes it down, and what he writes down is the truth, because he wrote it. He closed Marisol's file in eleven days because eleven days was exactly as long as it took for the tide to destroy everything he needed destroyed. He is not clever, counselor. He is *patient*. And patience is much worse."

Bram thought of the voice through the door. Unhurried. Pitched to be heard and no more. A man who had stood on a stranger's porch at four in the morning and never once raised his voice, because he had never once needed to.

"He gave me until the six-forty bus," Bram said.

"And will you take it?"

Bram looked out the window at the harbor. At the pier where the boy was sweeping. At the bluff to the north, where the old chapel sat in the fog with its coordinates waiting in a dead woman's cipher. He thought about the will that shouldn't exist, and the diary that kept writing after its author died, and the forty-one entries he hadn't read yet, and the sentence he'd found in entry nine, underlined twice in Marisol's hand: *If you are reading this, Bram, then someone is still afraid of me, and I need you to find out why.*

"No," he said.

Odile nodded slowly, as if he'd passed something. "Then you should know what happens next. He gave you a deadline because deadlines are how he works — he makes the world small enough that a man can fit inside his fear. And when you don't take the bus, he won't come back to your door. That was the polite visit. The next one won't knock." She reached across the table and turned his legal pad around, back to the first page, and tapped one long finger against the heading he'd written there on his first morning in Salt Hollow: *ESTATE OF M. PETRA — DEC'd.*

"You're going to want to change that," she said.

"Change what?"

"The *dec'd*." Her finger stayed on it. "You're an estate lawyer. Everything you do, every document you file, every question you ask — it all rests on that one little abbreviation. *Deceased.* As long as this town believes Marisol Petra is dead, Emory Voss is just a retired man with a pension and a garden, and you're a stranger chasing ghosts, and everything you find can be dismissed the way he dismissed her — as a sad story the sea told. But the moment someone proves she's alive—" She stopped. Her hand had begun, at last, to tremble. "The moment she's alive, the file reopens. The eleven days get looked at. The report gets looked at. The letter that disappeared from my house gets looked at. He doesn't fear you, Mr. Vane. He fears *her*. You're only dangerous to him as long as you might find her."

Bram stared at the page. *DEC'd.* Four letters he had written a hundred times in his career without a thought, the whole architecture of his authority resting on them like a house on pilings.

"And if he thinks I might find her," Bram said slowly, "then every day I stay—"

"—is a day he has to decide how far he'll go," Odile finished. "Yes. And here is the thing you must hold on to, when it gets dark, and it will get dark: he has already decided. Seven years ago. Whatever he did at Cutter Point, he decided it before Marisol ever went missing, or he decided it in the eleven days after, but he *decided*, and a man like that doesn't decide twice. He only decides how to make the next thing look like the last thing." She stood, and took both cups to the sink, and stood with her back to him, washing them, her voice coming over the sound of the water. "The diary. You said it kept writing after she vanished. Entries addressed to you."

"Forty-one of them so far. Dates through next spring."

"And the handwriting?"

Bram had brought a photograph of the last page on his phone; he turned it toward her now. Odile dried her hands and took the phone and held it close to her eyes, and for a long time she said nothing, and Bram watched her face do something complicated — hope and grief fighting for the same ground.

"That's her hand," she said finally. "That's Marisol's hand. The little hook she puts on the *d*. The way she crosses her *t*s low, like she's in a hurry even on paper." She gave the phone back. "Either she's alive, Mr. Vane—"

"Or someone is writing in her hand."

"Or someone is writing in her hand." Odile looked at him. "And ask yourself which of those two possibilities frightens Emory Voss more. Because a dead woman's diary is a curiosity. But a living woman's diary is a witness."

The clock on her wall ticked. Somewhere down the street, the six-forty bus ground through its gears at the corner of Rope Walk and Harbor Road, paused at its stop, and pulled away toward the county line without him.

Odile heard it too. She turned from the sink, and there was something in her face Bram would remember for a long time afterward — not relief exactly, and not fear, but the particular expression of a woman who has spent seven years keeping a candle lit in a window and has just seen someone else cup their hands around the flame.

"Then you're in it now," she said. "So we do this properly. Today you go to the chapel on the bluff, the first coordinate, before he decides you're more than a nuisance. And you go early, and you go the back way, through the dunes, and you tell no one — no one — where you're going." She was already moving, pulling an oilskin coat from a hook by the door, a flashlight, a tide chart folded into eighths. "Because there's one more thing you need to know about Emory Voss, counselor, and I should have said it first."

Bram stood. "What's that?"

"Eleven days after Marisol disappeared," Odile said, "before the file was closed, before the report was written — the chapel on the bluff burned down. Officially it was a lightning strike. But it was June, Mr. Vane, and there wasn't a cloud in the sky for a week." She handed him the flashlight. "Whatever Marisol hid in that chapel, Emory Voss already went looking for it once. And whatever's still there — whatever he missed —"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and the north bluff stood clear against the morning for the first time in days, and Bram Vane, who had come to Salt Hollow to read a will, found himself wishing — not for the last time — that he had taken the six-forty bus.

Almost wishing.

He pocketed the flashlight and went out into the light.
