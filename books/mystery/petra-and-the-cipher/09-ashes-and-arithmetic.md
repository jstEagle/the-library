## 9. Ashes and Arithmetic

The note stayed in my pocket all night, folded into a square small enough to forget. I didn't forget it. I lay in the guest room of the Petra estate listening to the house tick and settle, and every time a car passed on the coast road I put my hand over my jacket like a man guarding a wound.

*Stop reading. The tide keeps what it takes.*

By morning the fear had curdled into something more useful. Anger, maybe. Or the particular stubbornness that had kept me in estate law for nineteen years—the profession of people who insist that the dead be counted properly. Someone had put that note in my coat pocket while I slept in their house. Which meant the house had a hole in it. Which meant I needed to stop being afraid of the wrong things.

I made coffee in the estate kitchen, which still had plates in the cupboard and a kettle on the stove, as if Marisol Petra had stepped out to buy milk seven years ago and the house had simply decided to wait. Then I sat at the long table with a legal pad and did what I do best.

I did arithmetic.

* * *

The fire was the place to start. Every version of Marisol Petra's death began and ended with the fire—the boathouse blaze on the night of March 3rd, seven years ago, the one that burned hot enough to reduce a grown woman to fragments the coroner could certify. I'd read the file Voss had sent with the estate papers. I'd read it twice. Now I read it a third time with the note in my pocket and a different question in my head.

Not *did she die*. The diary had settled that for me, or unsettled it. The question was: *what did the fire actually burn?*

Here's what the file said. A fire, reported at 11:40 p.m. by a neighbor two properties down. The Salt Hollow volunteer brigade arrived in eleven minutes—fast, for them. By then the boathouse was fully involved. Accelerant was suspected but never confirmed; the lab report in the file was a single page, and the page was a summary of a summary, the kind of document that exists to end a question rather than answer it.

Here's what the file didn't say.

It didn't say where the accelerant samples went. There was a chain-of-custody form with a case number and no signatures. It didn't say why the dental records comparison—the one that identified the remains as Marisol Petra—was performed by a dentist in Ashford, ninety miles inland, rather than the county forensic odontologist. And it didn't say why the identification was signed off four days after the fire by Detective Inspector Aldous Voss, when the man's own case notes listed the remains as "pending confirmation" as late as the morning of the signing.

I wrote all of it down in my flat, careful lawyer's hand. Then I sat back and looked at the list and felt the shape of it, the way you feel the shape of a room in the dark. This wasn't a sloppy investigation. Sloppy investigations have gaps. This had *seams*—places where the record had been stitched closed by someone who knew exactly which threads to pull.

The fire had burned something. I was increasingly sure it hadn't burned Marisol Petra.

* * *

Odile found me at the table around ten, still in her coat, a canvas bag of groceries on her hip. She'd been coming by every few days since the will was read—bringing food, checking the boiler, pretending she wasn't checking on me. She was seventy-one and moved like the house itself, slow and load-bearing.

"You look like a man doing taxes," she said.

"Worse. I'm doing arithmetic." I turned the pad toward her. "The fire."

Her face did something small and controlled. She set the groceries down one item at a time, taking longer than the task required. "I told you what I know about that night. I was in Ashford. My sister's hip."

"You were. And your sister's hip is the most alibied joint in England, because you've told me three times." I kept my voice gentle. "I'm not asking about you, Odile. I'm asking about the boathouse. Whose boathouse was it, legally?"

"Marisol's. Everything down to the waterline was Marisol's."

"Everything down to the waterline." I tapped the phrase. "And below it?"

She frowned. "The foreshore. Crown land, mostly. There's an old lease—her grandfather had some arrangement for the private landing. Why?"

"Because of this." I pulled the inventory addendum from the estate file, the one page I'd flagged and couldn't stop flagging. "When the fire was investigated, the brigade logged a vehicle at the scene. A car, parked on the private landing below the boathouse. The report describes it as belonging to the deceased. But Marisol Petra's car was found the next morning in the long-term lot at the Ashford bus station, unlocked, keys in the visor, a parking ticket on the windshield dated the afternoon *before* the fire."

Odile had gone very still.

"So either Marisol drove two cars to the same coastline on the night she died," I said, "or someone else's car was on that landing, and the report lied about it. And if the report lied, it lied on purpose, and the only person with the authority to amend a report after the fact is the investigating officer."

"Voss," she said. Just the name, flat as a stone dropped in a well.

"Voss."

She sat down across from me, slowly, the way you sit when your knees have opinions. For a long moment she looked out the window at the garden, at the drowned map room's skylight glinting under its skin of green water.

"There was a second car," she said finally. "I saw it. Not that night—I wasn't here that night, and I'll thank you to stop circling that. The week before. A gray saloon, parked on the landing at dusk. I was walking the shore path. I thought it was a buyer. Marisol had been talking about selling the boathouse, or so she said." Odile's jaw tightened. "She said a lot of things that last month. She was jumpy. She'd started locking the studio. She asked me once, out of nowhere, whether I thought a person could disappear on purpose and be *believed*."

My pen had stopped moving. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her people believe what's easiest." Odile's eyes came back to me, and they were wet and furious at the same time. "And then I went to Ashford for a week and the easiest thing in the world happened. She burned. They buried a box. The town got to stop thinking about her. And I—" Her voice caught. "I believed it too, Bram. For seven years I lit a candle for a woman who was probably standing on a hill somewhere watching me do it."

* * *

I spent the afternoon at the county records office in Salt Hollow, a converted chapel with a damp smell and a clerk named Pell who remembered me from the will reading and seemed disappointed I hadn't brought more drama with me.

I pulled three things.

First, the property records for the boathouse parcel. The private landing lease, the one Odile had mentioned, was held under a name I didn't expect: not Marisol Petra, not her grandfather's trust, but *R. Calloway*. I knew the name from the diary—entry nineteen, a single line, *R.C. remembers the winter I taught him to read charts*. A boy she'd mentored, presumably. A boy who was now a man somewhere, holding a lease on the landing beneath the boathouse that burned.

Second, the fire brigade's original dispatch log. Not Voss's case file—the brigade's own contemporaneous record, which no one had thought to sanitize. It listed two engines and, in the margin, in a dispatcher's cramped hand: *2nd veh on landing, unreg., plate obscured, driver not observed.* The case file's version of that same notation read: *deceased's vehicle, confirmed.* Someone had rewritten a margin note. Someone had decided, after the fact, that the unregistered car with the obscured plate belonged to a dead woman who was, at that moment, parked ninety miles away in a bus station lot.

Third—and this was the one that made my hands go cold—I pulled the burial permit. Marisol Petra's remains had been interred in the Salt Hollow churchyard, Plot 114, on March 11th, seven years ago. Interred. Not cremated. The town had buried a box of certified fragments, and that box was still in the ground, forty feet from the chapel where I was standing, under a headstone that said *BELOVED CARTOGRAPHER* in letters the weather had begun to erase.

You can exhume a grave with a court order. You can get a court order with probable cause. And probable cause, I thought, walking back along the sea wall with the wind shoving at my shoulders, might be sitting in my jacket pocket in the form of a note written by a dead woman's living hand.

* * *

The diary was waiting for me when I got back, and I want to be honest about the fact that this had stopped feeling like coincidence days ago. I'd taken to leaving it in the estate safe. The safe was open. The diary was on the table, at the place where I'd been sitting that morning, open to a page I had never read, because the numbered entries ended at eighty and this one had no number at all.

Just a date. Today's date.

And beneath it, in that handwriting I now knew better than my own—*my* handwriting, the coroner had said, and God, the joke of that—three lines:

*Bram. You've been to the records office. Pell will mention your visit to someone by tonight, because Pell mentions everything to someone, and someone pays him to. So I'll say this quickly: the box in Plot 114 is not empty. That was always the risk, and the risk was mine to take. What's in it will make sense of the fire. But you cannot open it alone, and you cannot open it with a court order, because a court order has Voss's name on the service list, and the day Voss learns you've asked for Plot 114 is the day the arithmetic stops being yours.*

*Count what the fire couldn't burn. Then count what it did.*

*—M*

I read it four times. The safe was open. I had locked the diary in the safe myself, spun the dial, put the combination in my wallet. I stood in that long cold kitchen and let the fear come, properly this time, all the way up from the floor.

Then I did what I do best.

I sat down and I counted.

* * *

What the fire couldn't burn: teeth. Bone fragments, yes—the coroner's report listed fragments, which is why the dental comparison mattered at all. But a fire hot enough to leave only fragments doesn't leave a *full set* of identifiable dental structures, and the Ashford dentist's report described "sufficient restorative work for positive match." Fillings. Gold and amalgam. Gold doesn't vaporize at ordinary fire temperatures, but it *deforms*, and the report described the restorations as pristine. Pristine fillings in fragmentary remains is not evidence of death. It's evidence of a dental mold—of someone who had access to Marisol Petra's dental records, and to a laboratory, and to a week's lead time.

The fire happened March 3rd. Odile had told me, in our first interview, that Marisol had been "acting strange for weeks." The gray car on the landing, the week before. The locked studio. The question about whether a person could disappear and be believed.

She hadn't been planning to die. She'd been planning a *death*—someone else's death, a constructed death, a body built out of dental molds and bone fragments and a rewritten margin note. And she'd needed a fire hot enough to make fragments, which meant accelerant, which meant the lab samples had to vanish, which meant the chain-of-custody form with the case number and no signatures.

Count what the fire did: it burned a boathouse full of maps. I pulled the estate inventory again, the section I'd skimmed. The boathouse contents, per the insurance claim: charts, survey equipment, two drafting tables, a printing press for her limited-edition coastal maps, and—"items of personal correspondence, destroyed." No valuation given. No description. *Items of personal correspondence, destroyed*, in a claim signed by Aldous Voss as investigating officer, because the insurance company required police confirmation of loss.

Marisol had spent her life drawing coastlines. She knew better than anyone that the most important feature on any map is the waterline—the place where the land stops being knowable. She had burned her correspondence, her records, her own paper trail, in a fire that also burned a constructed body. And she had left behind the one document that couldn't be burned because it was still being written.

I looked at the diary. At the unnumbered entry in today's date.

"You're watching me," I said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the house, to wherever she was. "You've been watching the whole time."

The house ticked. The skylight over the drowned map room caught the last of the light.

* * *

It was dark when the knock came—not a knock, exactly, a scraping, a key or a knife or a stick drawn down the front door's paneling, once. I was at the table with the pad in front of me, forty pages of arithmetic, and I sat very still and listened.

Then, through the door, unhurried, almost friendly, a voice I recognized from a single handshake at the will reading and a phone call that had gotten too quiet at its end.

"Mr. Vane." Aldous Voss. "I hear you've been to the records office. Pell's a chatterbox. Old habit of his." A pause, and I could hear him smiling. "Funny thing about arithmetic, counselor. It only works if all the numbers are real. Come have a pint tomorrow. I'll give you the real ones."

Footsteps on the gravel, fading toward the road.

I looked down at my legal pad, at the last line I'd written before the knock, the line I'd underlined twice without quite knowing why until this moment:

*The box in Plot 114 is not empty.*

Tomorrow I would have a pint with the man who built the fire. And tonight, before I slept—or didn't—I was going to find out how a diary kept locked in a safe kept writing to me anyway, because there was one number in all my arithmetic I hadn't counted yet, and it was standing in this house with me now.

The lights in the east wing, I realized, were on.

I hadn't turned them on.
