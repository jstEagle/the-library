## 14. Two Handwritings

Bram didn't sleep. He told himself it was the storm rattling the shutters of the guest room Odile had given him at the back of her shop, but that was a lie he'd been telling since Salt Hollow first appeared on his windshield. The truth was simpler and worse: Entry Fifty-Two had described the drowned map room in detail no dead woman should have known, and somewhere between midnight and three he had stopped trying to sleep and started reading the diary again from the beginning.

Not reading this time. *Comparing.*

He had the lamp angled low, the diary flat on the desk beneath it, and beside it the stack of documents from the estate file—Marisol Petra's will, her property deeds, the letters she'd written to the county surveyor's office in the years before she vanished. He'd brought them as reference material, the way a lawyer brings exhibits to a deposition. He hadn't expected to use them like this.

He set a letter dated March 3rd, seven years ago, next to an entry from the same week. Both were signed with the same looping M that ended in a small flourish, like a wave curling over.

They were not the same handwriting.

The difference was subtle enough that Bram might have missed it entirely if exhaustion hadn't stripped away every layer of casual attention he owned. But there it was, once you saw it: the real Marisol wrote her lowercase *e* with a closed loop, tight as a knot. The diary's *e* floated open, unfinished, as if the writer kept lifting the pen too early. And the *t*-crossings—the letters in the estate file were crossed with quick, decisive slashes. In the diary they curved, almost apologetic.

Two hands. Two different people writing as one woman.

Bram sat back. The chair creaked. Rain worked at the window in long silver threads.

He turned pages slowly now, entry by entry, laying the estate documents alongside them like a man matching fingerprints. Entries one through nine: the open-*e* hand. Entry ten, written the week after Marisol's last confirmed sighting in town: still the open-*e*. Entry eleven—

He stopped.

Entry eleven was different. The loops were closed. The crossings were slashed. It was *her*, the hand from the file, the hand on the deed to the Petra estate, the hand that had signed a will naming Bram Vane executor of an estate belonging to a woman legally dead.

And then entry twelve reverted. Thirteen reverted. Fourteen through twenty all carried the floating, apologetic *e* of the impostor.

Bram counted. By four in the morning he had the whole shape of it laid out on Odile's desk in a column of sticky notes: entries 1–10, forged. Entry 11, genuine. Entries 12–40, forged. Entry 41—the one that had named him, the one that had started all of this—genuine. Entries 42 through 51, forged again. Entry 52, the drowned room, genuine.

It wasn't a diary. It was a relay. Someone had been writing most of it, and Marisol Petra—if it was Marisol Petra—had slipped her own hand into the book at intervals, like a swimmer surfacing for air.

Or like a prisoner passing notes through a crack in a wall.

* * *

Odile found him at dawn, standing at her counter with the diary open and forty-odd colored flags bristling from its pages like a paper reef. She looked at the desk, then at him, then at the coffee he'd made without asking permission, and something in her face shifted from suspicion to a kind of grim satisfaction.

"You found it," she said.

"You knew."

"I suspected." She poured herself a cup, black, and leaned against the counter. "I sold Marisol her pens. Did I ever tell you that? Fountain pens, the good ones, ordered special from the mainland. She came in every few months. I know her hand the way you know a customer's face." She tapped the diary. "That's not always her."

"Why didn't you say something?"

"Say it to who?" Odile's voice stayed level, but her eyes went hard. "To Detective Voss? The man who closed the case before her car finished cooling? To the town that took down her portrait from the library because it made people uncomfortable?" She sipped her coffee. "I said it to Teo. Teo said I was seeing ghosts. Then Teo read entry forty-one and stopped sleeping too."

Bram looked down at the flagged pages. "Eleven, forty-one, fifty-two. Three genuine entries out of eighty-some. Why so few?"

"Because writing is dangerous," Odile said simply. "Every time she puts pen to page, someone could catch her. So she writes only when she has to. When there's something worth dying for." She reached across and turned the diary to entry fifty-two, the drowned room. "This one cost her something. You can see it in the pressure. She was pressing hard enough to tear the page."

Bram had noticed that too. The strokes in fifty-two were gouged deep, the ink pooled in the valleys of the paper. A woman writing fast, or angry, or terrified—or all three.

"There's another thing," he said. "The forged entries aren't random. Whoever wrote them was keeping the diary alive. Keeping up the pretense that Marisol was still... documenting." He hesitated. "Which means whoever forged them wanted the diary to exist. Wanted it found. Wanted it to end up in an estate file with my name attached."

"Or," Odile said, "wanted it to look like exactly what Voss would expect—a dead woman's sentimental nonsense, nothing actionable." She set down her cup. "You're assuming the forger and the killer are the same person. Maybe they are. Maybe the forger is someone trying to help her and doing a bad job of it. Or maybe—" she met his eyes, "—maybe the forger is the reason she has to hide at all."

* * *

Teo arrived mid-morning, soaked through despite the umbrella, carrying a canvas document tube under one arm like a rifle. He was twenty-six, sharp-faced, with the restless energy of someone who'd grown up in a town that had already decided what he was. He'd been the Petras' neighbor's kid; now he was the closest thing the estate had to a caretaker, and the closest thing Bram had to an ally.

"You've seen it," Teo said, dumping the tube on the desk. Not a question. Odile had called him.

"The two hands. Yes."

"Then look at this." Teo unscrewed the cap and slid out a roll of tracing paper covered in pencil. He flattened it against the desk and weighted the corners with coffee cups. It was a transcription—every genuine entry copied out in careful block capitals, with the forged entries summarized in smaller print between them. Teo had done in two weeks what Bram had done in one night, and gone further: he'd annotated.

"I charted the gaps," Teo said. "Time between genuine entries. Eleven to forty-one is years—that's the disappearance itself, right, she couldn't write. Forty-one to fifty-two is about fourteen months. If the pattern holds, if she's still surfacing on some kind of schedule..." He pulled a pencil from behind his ear and tapped the tracing paper. "We're due. Entry sixty-something should be coming soon. Maybe already written, waiting somewhere we haven't looked."

"Or the schedule means nothing," Bram said. "Maybe she writes when she can."

"Sure. But look at the *content* pattern instead." Teo flipped the tracing sheet over. On the back, in the same block capitals, he'd drawn a rough map of the Petra estate grounds—the house, the chapel, the boathouse, the cliffs—and marked each genuine entry with a number. "Entry eleven mentions the chapel. Entry forty-one gives the cipher key hidden in the tide tables. Entry fifty-two describes the flooded map room. Every time she writes in her own hand, she points somewhere on the property. The forged entries never do. They talk about weather and memories and missing the sea. Fluff. Padding. Whoever wrote them was filling space, not leaving clues."

Bram studied the map. The marks formed a loose line across the property, from the chapel on the high ground down toward the waterline where the old map room sat half-drowned behind its rusted door.

"She's leading someone through the estate," he said slowly. "Step by step. Chapel, tide tables, map room."

"To what?" Odile asked.

"That's the question, isn't it." Bram straightened. "But here's what bothers me. If Marisol is hiding, if she's alive and scared, why leave a trail at all? A trail can be followed by anyone. Including the person who wants her dead."

Teo and Odile exchanged a glance.

"Unless," Teo said, "the trail isn't for just anyone. Unless it's ciphered so only the right reader can follow it."

"And the right reader is—"

"You," Odile said. "She named you in entry forty-one. By name. Before you'd ever heard of this town." She folded her arms. "I've been turning that over all night, Mr. Vane. Either Marisol Petra is psychic, or she knew you were coming. Knew your name, knew your profession, knew you'd be the one holding the file." Her eyes narrowed. "How does a woman who vanished seven years ago know which lawyer gets assigned her estate?"

Bram had no answer. He thought of the will's precise language, the odd clause appointing him specifically, the firm's puzzled senior partner saying *she requested you by name, Bram, do you know her?* He'd said no. He'd believed it.

Now he wasn't sure what he believed, except that the diary on the desk contained two hands, and one of them had been writing to him for years.

* * *

They spent the afternoon building the case for the forgery—not for court, though Bram's instincts kept drafting exhibit lists in the margins of his mind, but for themselves. For clarity. Teo photographed every page under raking light, the lamp held low so the shadows filled the pen strokes and made the differences unmistakable. Odile fetched her stock books, seven years of sales records, and found the purchase history: Marisol bought blue-black ink in bulk, twice a year. The forged entries were written in a slightly warmer black. Different ink. Different supplier. Possibly different region.

"Different *person*," Odile said, closing the ledger. "Stop hedging, lawyer."

"I'm not hedging. I'm being precise, because precision is going to matter later." Bram stood at the window, watching rain slide off the eaves of the shop across the street. "Here's what precision tells me. The forger knows Marisol's hand well enough to imitate it convincingly—well enough to fool a court, a detective, and me for eighty pages. That means access. Years of access to her writing. Samples. Practice."

"Voss had the case file," Teo said. "Seven years of her letters, her maps, her statements. Everything a forger would need."

"Voss also closed the case," Bram said. "Closed it fast, declared her dead, pushed the estate through probate. And now the diary—one of the few pieces of evidence suggesting she's alive—is sitting in that same estate file, half of it forged, under the control of the man who benefits most from the whole story staying buried."

"You think Voss wrote the fake entries," Odile said.

"I think it fits. But it doesn't fit perfectly, and that's what's keeping me up." Bram turned from the window. "If Voss forged the diary to bury the case, why forge entries that keep the mystery alive? Why write anything at all? Burn the diary, case stays closed, done. Instead someone spent months maintaining it. That's not destruction. That's *curation*. Someone wanted this diary to reach me looking authentic—but not too revealing. A controlled burn that never quite burns."

"Or," Teo said quietly, "the forger isn't working for Voss. The forger is working around him. Someone inside whatever happened to Marisol, someone who couldn't tell the truth outright but couldn't let the diary die either. Someone walking a line." He looked at the tracing paper, at the little numbered map of the estate. "Someone who's been leaving us breadcrumbs while pretending to sweep the path."

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the street a shutter banged, and all three of them flinched—three people in a dry room, jumping at weather, because the last week had taught them that in Salt Hollow even the harmless things arrived sounding like warnings.

"We need entry sixty-three," Teo said finally. "Or whatever comes next. We need her voice again, the real one, telling us the next step."

"And how do we get that?"

Teo smiled, and there was something fierce in it. "We go back to the estate. All the genuine entries point somewhere on the property. We've done the tide tables. We've found the map room door. But we haven't done the chapel—not properly. Entry eleven, the first real entry, the oldest clue. Everyone skips the beginning, Mr. Vane. Detectives skip the beginning. That's how cases stay closed."

Bram gathered the diary, wrapped it in oilcloth, and tucked it inside his coat against his chest like a second heartbeat. As he did, his thumb caught on the back cover—on the slight thickness he'd felt before and dismissed as binding glue.

Tonight, with two handwritings confirmed and his mind sharpened by sleeplessness, he didn't dismiss it.

He got Odile's letter opener and worked it along the seam of the spine. The cloth gave. Beneath it, glued flat against the inside cover, was a folded sheet of paper, thin as onion skin, hidden where only someone dismantling the book would ever find it.

He unfolded it under the lamp. One line, in the closed-loop hand, the true hand, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*If you are reading this, you have learned to tell my hand from theirs. Good. Now learn to tell their lies from mine — because the detective who closed my case wrote half this diary, and he is coming to collect it.*

Bram looked up. Odile had gone white. Teo's hand was frozen halfway to the tracing paper.

Outside, headlights swept slow across the front window of the shop, paused, and moved on.
