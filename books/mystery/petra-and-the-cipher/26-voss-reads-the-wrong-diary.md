## 26. Voss Reads the Wrong Diary

The forgery was good. Bram knew because he'd made it himself, and because Odile had read it twice and pronounced it "cruelly convincing," which from Odile was a eulogy.

They had built it over four days in the back room of the Salt Hollow archive, working by lamplight with the shutters drawn. Bram had transcribed forty entries from the real diary—harmless ones, early ones, the Marisol of tide charts and grocery lists and small domestic grievances—and then he had written eleven entries that did not exist. In those eleven entries, the cipher pointed not to the flooded map room beneath the estate but to the old chapel of charts on the north bluff, a ruin so collapsed that even the gulls had abandoned it. He aged the paper with tea and salt air. He blotted the ink the way Marisol blotted hers, impatiently, at an angle. He even reproduced the small habit she had of letting a final letter trail off when she was writing fast, as if her hand were already moving to the next thought.

Teo had contributed one crucial detail. "She never crossed her sevens," he said, watching Bram work. "People who forge her would cross them. It's the first thing a handwriting examiner looks for—the things a forger adds, not the things they leave out."

Bram had uncrossed every seven in the manuscript.

Now the false diary sat in the archive's document case, and Bram sat across from retired Detective Hal Voss in the Salt Hollow diner, watching the man eat a grilled cheese sandwich with the unhurried competence of someone who had never once been surprised by a meal.

"You wanted to see me," Bram said.

"I wanted to *talk* to you." Voss wiped his mouth with a paper napkin, folded it into a precise square. "There's a difference. Talking is free. Seeing costs something, eventually."

Outside the diner window, the town moved through its morning the way it always did—slowly, sideways, as if approaching its own reflection. A fishing boat chugged out past the breakwater. Two teenagers argued about a bicycle. Salt Hollow had spent seven years perfecting the art of not looking at the Petra estate on the hill, and it had gotten very good at it.

"You've been busy," Voss said. "The archive. The chapel. Asking Odile Ferrand about tide tables from 1998. Asking the harbormaster about the night Marisol's boat was found. Asking Teo—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes, something patient and cold, "—about a lot of things."

"I'm an estate lawyer. Diligence is the job."

"Diligence." Voss smiled. He had a good smile. It was the kind of smile that had closed a hundred cases and, Bram now suspected, a few other things besides. "You know what I did for thirty-one years? I was diligent. I was so diligent the county gave me a plaque. You want to know the secret of diligence, Mr. Vane?"

"Enlighten me."

"Knowing when to stop." He picked up his coffee. "The Petra case was closed because it was *over*. Woman walks into the sea, sea keeps her, case closed. It happens. It's not a mystery. It's just water." He drank. "But you've got a document that says otherwise, and documents make people ambitious. Ambitious people dig. And in Salt Hollow, when you dig, you mostly find mud. Sometimes you find other things."

"Such as?"

Voss set the cup down with a small ceramic click. "Such as I'm going to do you a courtesy. Because you seem like a reasonable man, and reasonable men are rare in this business. The diary you've been carrying around—the one in the document case, the one you've been showing to Odile and to the boy—when you're done with it, I'd like to see it. Before anyone else. Before any court. Before you file so much as a footnote."

Bram kept his face still. Inside, something cold and bright turned over.

*He knows about the case. He's been watching. He wants the diary.*

*He wants to destroy it.*

"And why would I do that?" Bram said.

"Because it's the law," Voss said pleasantly, "that documents pertaining to a declared death pass through the coroner's office before probate, and I still consult for the coroner's office, and I would hate to see your little probate tied up in procedural appeals for the next three years while the estate rots and the town forgets why it mattered. Because I'm offering you cooperation instead of obstruction, and cooperation is faster. And because—" he leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, "—because Marisol Petra is dead, Mr. Vane. Whatever wrote that diary, whatever game she's playing with you from beyond whatever grave she has, she is *dead*. And the kindest thing you can do for a dead woman is let her rest. I've watched men ruin themselves over her. I watched her brother ruin himself. I watched her father drink the estate down to the wallpaper. Don't be the next one."

"You knew her brother."

"Everyone knew her brother." Something flickered—fast, quickly buried. "Tomas Petra. Drowned too, as it happens. This coastline collects Petras." He stood, dropped bills on the table with the same precision he'd folded his napkin. "I'll expect the diary by Friday. Bring it to my office. And Mr. Vane—" he paused at the end of the booth, and the morning light caught the gray at his temples, the creases around his eyes, all the ordinary furniture of a decent old man's face, "—I closed that case because it was finished. Don't make me open it again. When I open things, I have to close them my way."

He left. The diner bell chimed behind him, a small bright sound, absurdly cheerful.

Bram sat with his untouched coffee and let his hands shake, now that no one could see them. Then he took out his phone and texted Odile a single word:

*Friday.*

* * *

They met at the archive at dusk, all three of them—Bram, Odile, Teo—around the document case like conspirators around a bomb.

"He took the bait," Odile said. It wasn't a question. She was sharpening a pencil with a small knife, an old habit, the shavings falling in a thin spiral onto a spread of tide charts. "He asked for the diary itself. Not a copy. Not a look. The object. A man who wants to *read* asks to read. A man who wants to *end* asks for the thing."

"He gave me a deadline," Bram said. "Friday. And a speech about letting dead women rest. And—" he hesitated. "He mentioned Tomas Petra. Marisol's brother. He said the coastline collects Petras. There was something in his face when he said it."

Odile's pencil stopped.

"Tomas," she said slowly, "went out the night before Marisol did. Did you know that?"

Bram looked up. "The diary doesn't mention it."

"The diary wouldn't. She was protecting him, even in ink." Odile set the pencil down. "Tomas Petra went to see Hal Voss the night before the disappearance. Everyone knew it, and no one said it, because saying it would have meant the detective had to explain why a man who was never charged with anything went to a private meeting with the detective at ten o'clock at night, and then walked home along the cliff road, and then—" she spread her hands, "—went swimming, apparently. In October. In his good coat. The coroner called it misadventure. Voss called it a tragedy. The town called it nothing at all, because by then the Petras were already the family you didn't discuss."

Teo had gone very still in the corner, the boy's usual watchfulness sharpened into something harder. "You think Voss killed him."

"I think," Odile said, "that Hal Voss has spent thirty-one years being thanked for closing cases, and that at some point the thanking became the job, and the closing became the pleasure. And I think Marisol figured it out. And I think that's why she's been dead for seven years."

Bram looked down at the false diary in its case. Eleven forged entries pointing to a collapsed chapel on the north bluff. A ruin with one approach road, visible from a distance, no exits, no water, no cover.

"Friday," he said. "He expects me to bring it to his office. Which means he'll be at his office. Which means—" he looked at Teo, "—he won't be at the estate. And when he reads it, he'll read a diary that sends him to the chapel. Alone. On his own timeline. Thinking he's destroying the last piece of evidence."

"And we'll be where?" Teo asked.

Bram reached into his coat and drew out the real diary, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his body. "Under the estate. In the map room. Finishing the last cipher." He set it on the table between them. "Entry Eighty. It's the one she wrote in a hand I've only seen once before—and I need to know why, and I need to know what it opens, and we have until Friday to find out."

Odile was already pulling the tide tables toward her, pencil moving. "Friday's an ebb tide," she said. "Low water at 4:40 in the afternoon. The flooded room drains for almost two hours." She looked up, and her eyes had the flat bright quality of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "If you're going into that room, Mr. Vane, you go at low water. And you go knowing that when the tide turns, it turns fast, and it doesn't care what you've figured out."

"Then we figure it out before it turns," Bram said.

Teo laughed—a short, startled sound, the first Bram had heard from him. "You sound like her," the boy said. "In the diary. That's exactly the kind of thing she writes."

Bram didn't answer. But he thought, as he gathered the oilcloth and the tide tables and the small cold weight of everything they didn't yet know, that it was the closest thing to a compliment Salt Hollow had given him.

* * *

He worked alone that night in the estate's east wing, the real diary open under a hurricane lamp, the last cipher spread across the floor in photocopies and thread. Entry Eighty resisted him the way a locked door resists a shoulder—stupidly, solidly, until you found the hinge.

The hinge turned out to be the waterline.

Every cipher Marisol had built used the tide tables as its key, but the first two had used the *heights*—numbers, arithmetic, the measurable sea. Entry Eighty used the *times*. Not how high the water rose, but when. And the times, converted through her grid, spelled out not a location but a sequence:

*Ebb. Wait. Flood. Wait. Ebb.*

Three tides. Three visits. The room could only be entered at low water—but whatever she had hidden there, whatever she wanted found, required the room to be seen at *three* different states. Drained. Still. Rising.

Bram sat back on his heels in the cold room, the lamp guttering, and understood two things at once.

The first was that Marisol had not hidden a document in the map room. She had hidden a *process*—something that only made sense watched over time, something that changed with the water. A mechanism. A message written in the room itself.

The second was that the diary's final entries, the ones in the unfamiliar hand, weren't written by Marisol at all.

They were written by someone she trusted enough to finish her book.

He was still staring at that realization when he heard the sound from downstairs: the front door of the Petra estate, opening without a key, closing without haste.

Footsteps on the marble. Unhurried. Competent.

Bram closed the diary, killed the lamp, and stood in the dark of the east wing, listening to Hal Voss climb the stairs toward him—three days early, and reading the wrong diary be damned, apparently not willing to wait for Friday after all.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

"Mr. Vane," Voss said through the wood, pleasantly. "I couldn't sleep. Thought I'd come see the house."
