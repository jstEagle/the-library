## 11. Agnes Breaks Silence

The convent had taught Sister Agnes Marchetti three disciplines: silence, obedience, and the ordering of small things. For thirty-one years she had practiced all three with the diligence of a woman trying to bury something alive. She folded linens into squares sharp enough to cut. She alphabetized the sacristy candles by height and hue. She kept her hands busy so her mind would have no room to wander back to a cliffside house full of glass, and a sister who had stopped writing to her in the autumn of 1979.

Now Odile was dead, and the glass house was full of strangers, and Agnes's hands would not stay still.

She sat in the morning room where Wren Ishikawa had asked—gently, the way one asks a witness rather than a suspect—to speak with her alone. The others were somewhere on the grounds. She could hear Juno's sneakers squeaking on the terrace flagstones, Marco's low voice arguing with someone on his phone about a debt he clearly could not pay. The house breathed around them, that peculiar respiration of old buildings with too much glass, expanding in the sun, contracting as the fog came off the water.

Agnes folded her hands in her lap. Unfolded them. Folded them again.

"You've been avoiding me for four days," Wren said. Not unkindly. The lawyer had a way of speaking that reminded Agnes of the confessor's chair—no judgment waiting at the end of it, only the truth, whatever shape it took. "You flinch every time someone mentions the windows. You haven't looked at the quarry pane. I don't think it's grief."

"No," Agnes said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "It isn't grief. Grief is what I'm entitled to. This is something older."

Wren waited. That was the discipline Agnes recognized and feared most in the woman: the patience of someone who had learned that silence pulls confession out of people like thread from a spool.

Outside, a gull cried once and gave up. The light through the morning room's east window fell across the floor in long colored blades—amber, then green, then a blue so deep it looked wet—and Agnes watched those blades move and understood that she had been rehearsing this moment for half her life without knowing it. Every silent retreat. Every unanswered letter from Odile, returned unopened until the handwriting changed and the letters stopped coming at all. Every night she had knelt and prayed not for forgiveness but for forgetfulness, and been denied both.

"I was nineteen," she said.

* * *

In 1979, the house had belonged to their mother, and then briefly to no one, and then to Odile, who bought out Agnes's share with money Agnes never questioned because she was nineteen and wanted nothing more than to be gone—from the coast, from the fog, from a sister whose silences had begun to feel like walls built around something buried.

"I thought she was cold," Agnes said. "I thought she'd chosen the glass over me. Over people entirely. She was already famous by then—the gallery in the city, the commission for the cathedral. And I was angry, because when Mother died she didn't cry at the funeral, and I decided that made her a monster." Agnes's mouth twisted. "It's remarkable, the stories we tell ourselves to avoid the true ones."

"What did you actually see?" Wren asked. No softening, no preamble. Just the door, held open.

Agnes looked at her hands. They had gone white at the knuckles.

"Nothing," she said. "That was the whole design of it. I saw nothing. That is what I was paid to have seen."

The words landed in the room like stones dropped into still water, and the ripples went outward through everything Agnes had built in thirty-one years—the ordered linens, the alphabetized candles, the name she had taken instead of the one she was born with. *Marchetti.* She had kept it, even in the convent. She had told herself it was humility, keeping her family name like a hair shirt. Now she understood it had been a tether. A rope thrown backward across three decades, held taut between herself and the woman in the glass house.

"The summer of '79," she began again, slower now, feeling her way along the sentence like a woman descending stairs in the dark. "Odile was building the quarry window. You've seen it—the big one in the west hall. She'd been going out to the Calloway quarry for months. Sketching. The foreman let her set up an easel near the rim because Gerald Calloway liked having artists around; he liked being *seen* appreciating art. It made him feel civilized." Her lip curled on the word. "There was a boy who hung around the quarry that summer. He drew. Odile used to bring him paper sometimes, I think, or he'd come to the house—I remember a thin boy with charcoal on his fingers sitting on our kitchen step, and Odile talking to him the way she never talked to anyone else. Like he was a colleague."

"Danny Okafor," Wren said quietly.

"Yes." The name went through Agnes like a needle finding an old wound. "Danny Okafor. He drowned in the quarry pond in September. That's what they said. That he slipped, that he couldn't swim, that it was a tragedy and no one's fault. Nine days and the case was closed. I remember because it was nine days before Odile handed me a check and told me to take my share of the house and go anywhere I wanted. Anywhere at all. She was very generous about it. Very gentle." Agnes's voice cracked down the middle. "I thought she was buying me out because she wanted the house whole. I signed the papers and I left for the novitiate two weeks later, and I told myself for thirty years that my sister simply preferred glass to people."

"And the truth?"

Agnes closed her eyes. When she spoke again, it was in the flat, careful voice of someone reciting something memorized against her will, some catechism of horror she had never agreed to learn.

"Two years ago. Two years ago, Odile wrote to me for the first time since 1981. One letter. No return address, postmarked from a town two hundred miles north. I almost burned it. I want you to understand that—I sat with the match in my hand." Her hands trembled now, openly, and she let them. "But I was sixty-eight years old and I was tired of not knowing what I had run from. So I read it. And in the letter, Odile told me what happened at the quarry on the last day of August, 1979."

The morning room had grown dimmer; the fog was coming in off the water, muting the colored light until the floor lay in pale washes instead of blades. Agnes found she was grateful. It was easier to say these things in graying light.

"She was up on the rim with her easel. Danny was below, near the pond—he wasn't supposed to swim there, everyone knew the water was bad, cold and deep with old machinery under the surface. And a car came. Gerald Calloway's car. And Gerald walked down to the pond, and there were words—Odile couldn't hear them from the rim, only the shapes of an argument. And then—" Agnes stopped. Breathed. The discipline of small things: one breath, then another. "Then Gerald put his hands on the boy and pushed him into the water, and when Danny came up, Gerald held him under. With a length of pipe, Odile thought, or a rod from the scaffolding. She said it took less than a minute. She said the worst part was how quiet it was afterward. How the water just closed up, like a page turning."

Wren had not moved. Her face, in the gray light, was perfectly composed—but Agnes, who had spent a lifetime reading the micro-expressions of women trying to hold themselves together in chapel pews, saw the fury banked behind the lawyer's eyes like a fire shut in a stove.

"Why didn't Odile go to the police?" Wren asked. "Why didn't you?"

"Because Gerald Calloway knew she'd seen him." The words came out of Agnes like teeth pulled. "He walked up the rim road afterward and stood beside her easel—she told me this, she wrote it, I can hear her voice in it still—and he looked at the sketch she'd been working on, and he said, *'You paint what you see, Mrs. Marchetti?'* And she said yes. And he said, *'Then you'll paint the pond the way it was before this morning.'*" Agnes's breath shuddered. "And then he smiled at her and drove away, and two weeks later the first payment came. Cash, at first. Then, after a few years, routed through a company—Calloway Development, though I didn't know that until your ledger started surfacing. Monthly. For forty-five years. My sister sold her testimony one month at a time, and she hated herself so thoroughly for it that she turned herself into a monument of glass and solitude, and she let me believe she was simply heartless, because the truth would have made me complicit too."

"But the letter," Wren said. "Two years ago. Why write then?"

"Because she'd decided to stop." Agnes opened her eyes. "Because the payments had become something worse than money—they'd become instructions. In the last decade, the amounts grew, and the letters that came with them got shorter, and then they weren't letters at all, just figures, and then finally there was a note that said, *'We know you talk in your sleep.'* Odile read that as a threat against anyone she might ever tell. Including me. She wrote to warn me, and to ask my forgiveness, and to tell me that she intended to spend whatever time she had left making sure the boy was remembered properly. She said—" Agnes pressed her knuckles to her mouth for a moment. "She said, *'I have spent forty years painting around the empty space where Danny should be. I am going to paint him in.'*"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the sea worked patiently at the cliff.

"That's why the windows," Wren said softly. "All twelve. The ledger."

"That's why the windows." Agnes nodded slowly. "She encoded everything—every payment, every date—in the leading and the numbering of the panes. She told me that much in the letter, though not how to read it. She said if anything happened to her, the truth would be hanging in plain sight in her own house, where the Calloways would never think to look, because rich men don't imagine that poor women keep accounts." A ghost of Odile's own dry humor moved across Agnes's face and was gone. "She was right about that. They still don't."

* * *

When Agnes finished, Wren sat forward, elbows on knees, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"Why now?" the lawyer asked finally. "You've had two years to come forward. Why tell me today?"

Agnes considered the question honestly, which was more than she had done with any confessor in three decades. Confessors got the edited version. The sins of temper, the sins of pride, the small dishonesties of ordinary life. Never this.

"Because I chose the convent to escape complicity," she said, "and discovered too late that running is its own kind of agreement. I knew my sister was being paid for silence. I suspected why. I let myself not know, because not-knowing kept my hands clean and my conscience quiet and my God conveniently abstract." She met Wren's eyes. "Odile died carrying that secret alone because I refused to carry it with her. I will not make the same arrangement with you. Or with those children out there"—she gestured toward the terrace, toward the sound of Juno's restless feet and Marco's muffled argument—"who are strangers to me and to each other and to her, and who are, God help us all, the nearest thing either of us has left to family."

"They're not children," Wren said. "One of them is seventy."

"All the more reason they should know better than to do what I did." Agnes rose, and her legs were unsteady, but her voice had found its old choir strength, the one that carried to the back of a stone nave. "Ask me what you need to ask me. Dates, names, the letter itself—it's in my cell at St. Brigid's, in a lockbox under the floorboards, where I have kept it for two years like a coward with a receipt. Take it. Use it. Put my name in whatever record you're building. I have spent thirty-one years being nobody's witness. I find, at the end of it, that I would like to testify."

Wren stood too. Something passed between them—not warmth exactly, not yet, but the recognition of two women who had both built lives out of silence and were, at last, taking them apart brick by brick.

"There's one thing I don't understand," Wren said. "The final entry in the ledger. Dated two weeks before Odile died. Three words: *HE SAW ME.* We assumed it meant Richard Calloway—that he'd discovered what she was doing with the windows."

Agnes went still.

"No," she said slowly. "No, that can't be right. Because Odile wrote to me after that entry. Ten days before she died. The letter arrived the day before—" Her hand went to her throat. "The day before she fell on the cliff path. And in it she said the work was nearly done, and that she was frightened, but not of Richard. She said Richard was a coward, and cowards are predictable. She said—" Agnes's eyes widened, and thirty-one years of disciplined calm fell away from her face all at once, leaving something young and terrified underneath. "She said the one she was afraid of was the one who'd been watching the house. She said he'd been coming to the property line for weeks, standing among the cypress trees, and she thought at first it was a surveyor, but surveyors don't stand that still, and they don't wear jackets with the collar turned up, and they don't—" 

Her voice failed. Outside, on the gravel drive, a car engine started—distant, unhurried, the sound of someone leaving who had arrived without ever parking.

"Sister," Wren said quietly. "What did the man by the trees look like?"

Agnes stared past her, through the grayed glass, toward the cypress line at the edge of the estate, where the fog was thickening into something with weight and intention.

"Tall," she whispered. "Young. And wearing a Calloway Land Survey jacket—the same jacket I saw on a man standing at our kitchen gate in 1979, the morning after Danny drowned, delivering the first envelope. Odile never described his face to me. But she described his hands. She said he had his father's hands."
