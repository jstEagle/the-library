## 1. The Locked Crypt

The key to the crypt was heavier than it looked. Oscar Thackeray had carried it for eleven years—first as assistant to old Mr. Halloway, then, after his heart gave out among the shelving of the north vestry, alone—and in all that time she had never quite stopped noticing its weight. Brass and iron, cold even in summer, shaped like something between a key and a question mark. It lived on a ring with four others: the muniment room, the tower stair, the triforium gallery, and the little door behind the organ that no one remembered installing. But the crypt key was the one she held differently. The others opened spaces. This one opened a silence.

She came down on the first Tuesday of October, as she did every quarter, to take inventory. It was not a duty anyone had asked of her in years—the diocesan surveyor had stopped descending after his hip operation in 2016, and the dean considered the crypt "a matter for the archivist's conscience"—but Oscar kept the ritual anyway. Rituals were what she had instead of people, mostly, and this one suited her: an hour below the cathedral floor, among slabs and ledgers, where the only voice that spoke was her own, reading names aloud because names unspoken felt like small abandonments.

"Voss," she said, when she reached the third bay. "Marion Voss. 1954–2021." She paused, pencil hovering over the clipboard. "Presumed."

The word *presumed* had been added three years ago in her own hand, squeezed into the margin of the memorial record like an apology. Marion Voss had no slab. She had a line in the ledger—a name, dates, a note in red ink from the coroner's office that read, in the flat language such offices favored, *missing person; remains not recovered*. The stone masons had offered a cenotaph once, a blank tablet waiting in the yard, but the dean had demurred. Too soon, he'd said. Then, later, too strange. A memorial for a woman who might still walk in through the west door some wet Sunday, shaking rain from her coat. So Marion existed at St. Aldric's only as ink, and Oscar, who believed that ink was a kind of body—that every record was a person wearing paper—had never entirely made peace with it.

She wrote the date at the top of the inventory sheet. *Crypt inspection, quarterly. Present: O. Thackeray, sole.* The phrase *sole* always looked lonely on the page, so she let it stand.

The crypt of St. Aldric's was older than the cathedral above it by two centuries, Saxon bones under Norman skin, and it smelled the way deep places smell: stone dust and candle tallow gone to wax-ghosts, a mineral coldness, and beneath it all something faintly sweet that Oscar had decided long ago was simply the smell of time itself. Forty-one memorial slabs paved the floor, each engraved with a name and a span of years, and along the walls ran niches where older coffins had settled into their own shadows. One bulb burned overhead, forty watts, wired through a conduit that the electrician swore was safe and that Oscar privately regarded as a miracle.

She worked methodically, bay by bay, as she always did. Bay one: the Harrowgate family, six slabs, engravings legible, mortar sound. Bay two: the merchant tombs, their brasses green with patina, one loose corner noted for the mason. She knelt at each slab, checked the fixings, ran her fingers along the lettering the way a blind woman reads—not out of necessity but tenderness. Letters wore away if nobody touched them. That was true of more things than letters.

It was in bay three that she found the draft.

Not wind—the crypt had no windows—but a movement of air, cool against her ankle, coming from somewhere low and wrong. She crouched. The Voss ledger line was here because bay three had been assigned to Marion in absentia, a courtesy of the parish council, and behind the slab they'd propped temporarily against the wall—*temporarily*, three years running—there was a gap. A hand's width of darkness between the slab's edge and the Saxon masonry, where the old wall had settled away from newer stone.

Oscar told herself the draft meant nothing. Old buildings breathed; everyone said so. But her hands were already moving, already reaching into the gap with the particular courage of a woman who has spent her life touching things other people won't, and her fingers met leather.

Cold leather. Smooth, cracked at the spine, damp at one corner. She drew it out into the light and sat back on her heels with it resting on her knees, and for a long moment she did nothing at all except feel the crypt grow quieter around her, which she would not have believed possible.

A diary. Small, octavo, bound in oxblood calf gone soft with handling. An elastic band, perished, hung from the back cover like a shed skin. And stamped into the lower right of the front board, in the gilt that the cathedral shop used for its presentation gifts—Oscar knew the stamp intimately; she ordered the stock—two letters worn nearly smooth:

*M.V.*

Her breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere far above.

Marion Voss had been missing for three years, two months, and eleven days. Oscar knew the figure precisely not because she was morbid but because she was an archivist, and archivists count. Marion had last been seen on the twenty-ninth of July, 2021, a Thursday, buying a pork pie and a newspaper at the shop on Chandler's Lane, and the whole apparatus of the state had turned over the county looking for her—dogs, divers, helicopters dragging their noise across the fields—and found nothing but a woman who had apparently walked off the edge of the world at fifty-seven years old, leaving her house unlocked, her cat fed by a neighbor, her half-knitted sock still on its needles.

And the crypt had been locked since before that. Locked since the flood remediation in the spring of 2021, sealed with the dean's own padlock while the drainage work was done, reopened only in September of that year—six weeks *after* Marion vanished—and locked again thereafter, visited by no one but Oscar and her quarterly inventory.

Which meant the diary had come down here either before the sealing or after the reopening, carried past a lock, past a door, past the ordinary physics of how things moved in the world. Or else—

Or else it had been down here longer than anyone had thought to look.

Oscar sat on the cold flags with her back against the temporary slab and turned the diary over twice, examining it as she examined everything: first as object, then as evidence, then—as she could never help doing, though she knew better—as a life. The leather had been handled often. The corners were rounded with use. Someone had loved this book, or at least lived in it daily, the way you live in a coat.

She should call the police. She knew that immediately, cleanly, the way she knew cataloguing rules. Found property connected to a missing person inquiry: secure it, document it, report it. Her hand actually moved toward her phone before it stopped, hovering, because underneath the procedure there rose another feeling, older and less respectable, the feeling she got whenever she stood at the threshold of someone's private papers. Not guilt exactly. Something closer to hunger. The hunger of a woman who had spent thirty years cataloguing other people's griefs—wills and funeral books, letters from widows, the sad administrative debris of ended lives—and who had never once been permitted to open them properly. Archivists guarded intimacy without being granted any. They were priests of a religion whose sacraments were always administered to someone else.

But this was different, she told herself. This was evidence. Evidence was meant to be read.

Still, she documented first. Photographed the gap in the wall, the slab, the diary in situ—or as near to in situ as it now was, since she had already touched it, a failure she noted against herself in the margin of the inventory sheet with a small, self-punishing tick. She measured the gap: eleven centimeters. She noted the absence of dust disturbance on the flags in front of it, which troubled her more than she let herself admit, because a book pushed into a wall gap should have left a trail of itself, a smear of disturbed grime, and there was none. The flags were as clean as the day the masons had left them.

Then, because she was who she was, she opened the cover.

The endpaper was marbled, blue and white, faded to the color of a winter sky. On the flyleaf, in a round, unhurried hand—each letter upright, each word given its full space, the handwriting of someone who had been taught penmanship by a strict aunt and had never seen reason to rebel—was written:

*Property of Marion E. Voss. If found, please return to 14 Chandler's Lane. I shall be very grateful.*

*I shall be very grateful.* Oscar pressed her lips together. There was something unbearable in it—the politeness of it, the faith in strangers, the assumption that the world was full of people who would do the decent thing. And the address, of course. Fourteen Chandler's Lane, a ten-minute walk from the cathedral close, a house that had stood empty for three years with its curtains drawn and its garden going soft and wild, because no one had ever proved anything bad enough happened there to make it officially tragic.

She turned the page.

The entries began in January of 2021, ordinary as porridge. *Cold morning. The boiler man came, £180, highway robbery. Bells practice cancelled, Agnes poorly.* Names Oscar half-knew—the small constellation of people who orbited the cathedral, volunteers and vergers and the unpaid devout. Marion had been one of them: a Tuesday volunteer in the gift shop, a flower arranger on feast days, a woman who folded service sheets with crisp creases and stayed afterward to talk to anyone who looked lonely. Oscar remembered her clearly now, remembering was the trouble with archives, everything filed forever—remembered her cardigan the color of moss, her habit of humming while she worked, the way she'd once brought Oscar a thermos of coffee during a long afternoon of deed-box sorting and said, *You're always down here alone. That can't be good for a person,* and Oscar had smiled and said she preferred it that way, and Marion had looked at her with an expression Oscar had spent three years trying not to think about, an expression that said *I don't believe you, and I'm sorry for whatever made it true.*

The entries were domestic, weatherish, occasionally sharp about the parking situation outside the deanery. Oscar read standing, then kneeling, then sitting fully on the cold floor with her coat spread beneath her, losing track of the inventory, of the time, of the forty-watt bulb burning faithfully overhead. February passed. March. Marion complained about her knees, praised a sermon, worried about money in a way that was becoming less casual as the months went on—*asked again about the fund accounts, got the usual smile and no figures*—and Oscar's professional ear pricked at that, the way it pricked at any irregularity, but she kept reading because the handwriting was company, because the voice on the page was warm and wry and alive in the specific way that dead people's writing is unbearably alive.

She reached July. The twenty-ninth of July, 2021, fell on a Thursday, and the entry for that day was short:

*Saw him counting again. He thinks nobody notices. Tomorrow I'll ask my questions properly and we'll see how he likes it.*

No name. No antecedent. The sentence sat on the page like a stone dropped into a well, and Oscar waited for the splash of the next entry, the Friday, the thirtieth, the day after Marion Voss bought her pork pie and her newspaper and stepped off the edge of the known world.

There was a next entry. That was the thing that made the cold come up through the flags and into her spine, that made her look up at the locked door at the top of the stairs as if the lock itself might explain. There was a next entry, dated the thirtieth of July, and the thirty-first, and August unfolding page by page, the same round unhurried hand beginning to falter—to *falter*, the letters leaning, the spacing tightening, as though the writer's arm grew tired or frightened or cold—and the entries continued, day after day after day, twenty-three days past the date when Marion Voss had ceased, according to every official record in the country, to exist.

*They've stopped looking. Good. Now I can work.*

That was the entry for the twelfth of August. Oscar read it three times. Around her the crypt held its breath, forty-one slabs and forty-one silences, and the single bulb hummed its thin electric hymn, and somewhere far above, muffled by feet of stone, the cathedral bell rang the quarter hour with the serene indifference of institutions.

Twenty-three days. Written in a crypt that was locked. In a hand that faltered but did not stop, that kept going the way a candle keeps going in a draught, guttering, insisting, until the final entry on the twentieth of August, which was only four words long, and which Oscar read aloud without meaning to, her voice strange and small in the vaulted dark:

*"He knows I know."*

She closed the book. Her hands were not steady, and she watched them not being steady with a kind of distant interest, the archivist observing the phenomenon of her own trembling as though it belonged to someone else's file.

The police had said, three years ago, what police say: a woman of fifty-seven, finances strained, no body, no signs of struggle, probably walked away, possibly to the coast, possibly to a sister in Scotland whom she hadn't spoken to since 1998. The case had gone quiet the way cases do, folding itself smaller and smaller until it fit inside a box in a records office, and the parish had learned to leave the subject alone, and Oscar had learned to stop saying Marion's name aloud during inventory, and the temporary slab had leaned against the wall gathering no dust.

And now here was a diary that could not be here, containing words that could not have been written, in a hand that was unmistakably, undeniably, the hand of a missing woman—continuing three weeks past the end of her.

Unless it wasn't her hand.

Oscar sat with that thought, turning it over the way she'd turned the book, examining its underside. She was not a detective. She was a keeper of records, and keepers of records know one thing above all things: that documents lie. Hands can be forged. Dates can be false. A clever person with patience and a fountain pen could raise the dead on paper as easily as a medium raises them on a stage. Whoever wrote those twenty-three days had wanted them found—or had wanted them *not* found, hidden here behind Marion's own memorial line, tucked into the one place in the cathedral that only Oscar ever entered.

Only Oscar ever entered.

The thought arrived quietly, without drama, the way the most unwelcome thoughts do, and it stood beside her in the dark and would not be sent away. Because if the diary was a message, it was a message addressed to precisely one reader. And if it was not a message—if it was what it appeared to be, a dead woman's voice continuing past her death—then someone had carried it down here, past the lock, past the silence, and left it where only she would find it, which amounted to the same thing.

Either way, someone had chosen her. The invisible woman, the one who preferred it that way, the one who had told Marion Voss over a thermos of coffee that solitude suited her fine.

Oscar put the diary inside her document case, wrapped in acid-free tissue out of pure habit, and climbed the stairs. At the top she locked the door, tested the lock twice, and stood a moment in the cool grey light of the ambulatory, listening to the cathedral go about its morning above her—footsteps, a vacuum cleaner's distant drone, Tobias the young verger whistling something tuneless in the south aisle. The ordinary world, intact, oblivious.

She took out her phone and dialed the non-emergency number, and while it rang she looked down at her own hand on the rail, steadier now, and understood that whatever she reported in the next five minutes, one fact had already changed permanently: she had been noticed. Down in the dark, by whoever or whatever had placed that book, she had been seen—seen specifically, chosen specifically—with an intimacy that made her feel, for the first time in eleven years, both terrified and, God forgive her, *known*.

The line clicked. "Police, non-emergency, how can I direct your call?"

"This is Oscar Thackeray," she said. "St. Aldric's Cathedral. I need to report a finding." She paused, and heard herself add, in the careful voice she used for cataloguing damaged goods, "It concerns Marion Voss."

* * *

The diary lay on her desk in the archive office for the rest of the afternoon, wrapped in its tissue like a relic awaiting translation, while Oscar tried to work and failed. Twice she caught herself reading the final entry again through the wrapping's edge, the four words surfacing in her mind unbidden—*he knows I know*—and twice she forced her attention back to the accession register, where her entries had begun to drift, dates transposed, a deed box logged twice. By four o'clock she gave up the pretense, made tea she didn't drink, and sat looking out the narrow window at the close, where the light was going amber and Cyril the gardener was raking leaves into rows as straight as ledger lines.

Three years, she thought. Three years of quarterly inventories, of kneeling at that slab, of reading *presumed* in her own handwriting. The book had been there the whole time—perhaps the whole time—eleven centimeters behind the stone, breathing its small cold draft against her ankles, waiting for her hands.

Waiting. That was the word that wouldn't leave. Books didn't wait. People waited.

When the knock came at the office door near closing, she said "Come in" without turning, expecting Tobias with the keys, and it took her a moment to register that the footsteps were wrong—heavier, slower, shod in hard soles rather than trainers—and when she turned, a woman in a dark coat was standing in the doorway holding a warrant card, and behind her, framed in the corridor's failing light, the cathedral seemed to lean in and listen.

"Ms. Thackeray? Detective Inspector Reyes." The woman's eyes went straight past her, directly, unerringly, to the tissue-wrapped shape on the desk. "Someone downstairs said you'd found something belonging to Marion Voss." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "I'd very much like to know how it got there. Because I've read the file. And according to every record we hold—" her gaze came back to Oscar's face and settled there, "—that crypt has been locked since before she disappeared."
