## 22. What We Keep

The new archive room had once been the cathedral's coal store.

Oscar had argued for it herself—had stood before the chapter with a folder of plans and a voice that only shook twice—and now, eight months after the trial ended, she stood in the middle of it with dust on her sleeves and light coming through a window that hadn't existed in the building's nine-hundred-year history. The masons had cut it into the north wall where no window had ever been, because the coal store had never needed one, because nobody had ever imagined anyone would want to *see* inside this room.

That was the whole argument, really. That was what she'd told the dean, and the chapter, and the heritage board, and finally, when they ran out of objections, the donors: *A room without a window is a room where things are kept from people. I'd like to stop keeping things.*

The dean had sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to a battle he'd already lost twice before, and signed the faculty petition, and that had been that.

Now the room smelled of fresh plaster and beeswax and, faintly, of the lavender sachets Agnes had insisted on tucking into every drawer—"for the moths," she'd said, or rather signed, her hands moving quick and certain, "and because paper likes to be loved." The shelving was oak, pale as bone. The reading tables were old ones rescued from the school that had closed on Wexford Lane, refinished by Cyril, who had sanded them until his knuckles bled and then varnished them so deeply they looked like pools you could fall into. There were lamps with green glass shades. There was a bell-pull that connected, by some ancient wiring Tobias had coaxed back to life, to the verger's vestry.

And there, in the centre of the room, on a table all its own beneath its own small lamp, lay an open display case, and in the case, propped at the first page, lay a leather diary with a cracked spine and a water stain shaped like a bird in flight across its cover.

Marion Voss's handwriting began just below the stain. *Tuesday. Rain again. I have decided to count everything.*

Oscar had read those words perhaps four hundred times. She could recite the entries forward and backward; she knew which sentences faltered, which letters leaned, where the hand that wrote them had been tired or frightened or—God help her—happy. She knew exactly where Marion's own voice ended and Ambrose Pell's counterfeit began, the seam so fine that only someone who had lived inside both hands for months could find it. She had testified to it. She had watched a jury lean forward when she held the two volumes side by side under the courtroom's flat lights and showed them how Marion crossed her sevens and Pell did not, how grief made Marion's ink pool at the ends of words and performance made Pell's dry as August.

She had thought, during those weeks of testimony, that she would never want to see the diaries again. That they had taken something out of her that she didn't want returned.

She had been wrong about that, as she had been wrong about so many things. About being invisible. About being safe inside her catalogues. About the difference between keeping things and keeping them *from* people.

* * *

They came at four o'clock, because Agnes had decreed it—four o'clock being, she explained through Tobias's interpreting hands, the hour when the light came through the new window at its most forgiving, and also the hour when the tower bells practised, and a room should open to the sound of bells or not open at all.

Agnes arrived first, of course. She always did. She wore her good coat, the grey one with the brass buttons she polished herself, and she stood in the doorway of the coal-store-turned-archive for a long moment, her chin lifted, feeling—Oscar was sure—the vibration of the room the way she felt everything: through floors, through air, through the deep hum of the building she had served for forty-one years. Then she signed something rapid and private, and Tobias, beside her, laughed and translated: "She says it smells like a library should smell, and that most libraries don't."

Cyril came in behind her carrying a tray of tea things he had absolutely not been asked to bring, because Cyril brought tea the way other men breathed—involuntarily, constantly, as penance and offering both. He had put on weight since the trial. He had stopped flinching at doors. He still wouldn't talk about the night he'd burned Pell's blackmail papers in the incinerator behind the tool shed, but he had started talking about everything else—about roses, about his sister in Leeds, about the sermon, about anything—and Oscar had learned that this was what forgiveness looked like when it was real: not a declaration, but a man who could fill a silence again instead of hiding in it.

Tobias came in last, seventeen now and taller than everyone, still wearing his verger's cassock even though it was his day off, because he said the pockets were better. He had grown up this year in ways that had nothing to do with height. He had sat through every day of the trial in the public gallery, taking notes nobody asked him for, and afterward he had asked Oscar if she would teach him archival method, proper method, and she had said yes before he finished asking.

"You're staring at it again," he said now, nodding at the display case.

"I'm checking it."

"You checked it twenty minutes ago."

"The humidity gauge drifts."

"The humidity gauge," Tobias said, "does not drift. You drift." But he said it gently, and he set down the box he was carrying—archival gloves, the good cotton ones, ordered special—and let the subject lie.

Reyes arrived at ten past four, out of uniform, in a coat that suggested she had walked across the close rather than driven, which meant she wanted to be here as a person and not as an institution. That distinction had mattered more over the past year than Oscar could ever have explained to anyone who hadn't lived it. Reyes had fought for the reopening, then for the exhumation order, then for the charge of murder against a man whose meticulousness nearly undid them all—nearly, because it turned out that a man who documented everything documents his guilt too, in files labelled and cross-referenced with a care that the prosecution called *chilling* and Oscar, privately, in the small hours, had called *familiar*, and had hated herself a little for the familiarity, and had made peace with it eventually, the way you make peace with your own reflection showing up somewhere terrible.

"Inspector," Oscar said.

"Archivist." Reyes looked around the room, slow, taking in the shelves, the lamps, the case. Her eyes stopped on the diary and stayed there longer than anywhere else. "It's strange," she said. "I've read every word in evidence. Twice. And seeing it just—sitting there, under a lamp, like it's waiting for someone to pick it up—" She shook her head. "It's stranger than the crypt was."

"That's why it has to be here," Oscar said. "Things in evidence are dead. Things under a lamp are alive."

Reyes gave her the look she gave her—one eyebrow, half a smile, the look that said *you're a strange woman, Pemberton, and I've decided to be glad of it.* "The dean's speech is going to be unbearable."

"The dean's speech is going to be four minutes long and mostly about drainage," Oscar said. "I vetted it."

* * *

They came, in ones and twos, as the light went gold.

Volunteers first—the flower team, the stewards, Mrs. Okonkwo who ran the coffee stall and had opinions about everything and had wept openly in the gallery on the day of the verdict. Then strangers: a woman who introduced herself as Marion's cousin from Bristol, holding herself very straight, who stood in front of the display case for eleven minutes without moving and then thanked Oscar in a voice like paper tearing. A man who had been a boy in the choir when Marion sang alto, though he must have been fifty now. Two students from the university who had read about the case and come, shyly, the way people come to graves.

And the dean, who gave his four-minute speech and made it, against all odds, about memory—that a cathedral was not stone but *keeping*, that what a city chose to remember was the truest measure of it—and then stepped aside, which was itself a kind of penance, and nodded to Oscar.

Oscar had prepared remarks. They were in her pocket. She did not take them out.

Instead she stood by the case, with the whole room's attention settling on her like snow, and found—to her astonishment, after a lifetime of rehearsing invisibility—that she was not afraid of it.

"Marion Voss counted everything," she said. "That's how the diary begins. She counted the rain, the candles, the money in the restoration fund, the days. She counted because counting was how she made the world hold still." She touched the glass above the cover, lightly, the way you touch a shoulder. "For three years, the world held still around her. She was a missing person, which is a phrase that puts the missing where the person should be. And then a man who loved her in the worst possible way tried to make her live forever by writing her life for her—by keeping her, the way you keep a specimen, the way you keep a secret."

Someone in the back was crying quietly. Oscar didn't look to see who.

"But she isn't a secret anymore. She isn't a file, or evidence, or a cautionary tale. From tomorrow morning, any person who walks in off the street can sit at these tables and read her words—all of them, hers and even his, because we've kept both, because the forgery teaches us something true about how love goes wrong, and because I promised myself I would never again decide what other people get to know about their own dead." She paused. "Memory kept alone curdles. Memory kept together—" She looked at Agnes, at Cyril, at Tobias, at Reyes leaning against the doorframe with her arms folded and her face carefully neutral. "Memory kept together survives. That's the whole finding. That's my report."

Agnes raised her hands and signed, and Tobias translated, his voice cracking on the last word: "She says—ring the bells."

So they rang the bells.

* * *

Afterward, when the room had emptied and the light had gone blue-grey and the tea things sat abandoned on Cyril's beautiful tables, Oscar stayed.

She took the diary out of the display case—it would go back in the morning, under glass, but tonight it belonged in her hands—and carried it to the window, and read the first page again in the last of the light.

*Tuesday. Rain again. I have decided to count everything.*

Below it, in pencil, faint, in a hand the jury had needed magnification to believe was not Marion's: *She counted. Now I count for her.*

Pell's line. His trophy, his alibi, his warped love letter. It would stay in the published facsimile, clearly marked, because Oscar had decided that hiding it would be another kind of disappearance, and she was finished with disappearances.

But tonight, holding the book, she found she could read the line differently. Not as a monster's claim. As a warning—as a mirror held up to everyone who keeps things for the dead. She had kept Marion too, these three years: in her lamplight, in her marginalia, in the locked rooms of her own attention. The difference—the only difference, the difference that mattered everything—was the window. Was the door. Was the room built so that anyone could walk in.

Footsteps on the stair. Tobias, come back for his forgotten gloves, stopping in the doorway.

"Are you all right?" he asked. "You're doing your face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something enormous and you're pretending it's about humidity."

Oscar laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, still an unfamiliar sound in her own ears after all this time. She closed the diary and held it against her chest.

"I'm deciding," she said, "that tomorrow, when we open, I'm going to put a chair by the window. And a second lamp. In case two people want to read at once."

Tobias considered this with the gravity he gave all matters archival. "People might talk," he said. "Two lamps. Scandalous."

"Let them."

He grinned and went to fetch his gloves, and Oscar turned back to the window, where the last light lay along the shelf-tops like a blessing, and outside, high in the tower, the bells were ringing the hour—not for practice now, but plain and full, the way Agnes liked them, the way a city sounds when it remembers.

Marion Voss had counted everything. Tomorrow, anyone who wished could count along.

Oscar stood in her windowed room, visible at last, and was glad.
