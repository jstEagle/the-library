## 4. The Client's Voice

My memory is a filing cabinet that never sleeps, and the trouble with a filing cabinet that never sleeps is that it keeps pulling drawers open at three in the morning.

The client's visit had been two days past. I had gone over it — as promised, professionally, obsessively — perhaps forty times by then, and each pass yielded something new, the way a photograph held to different light gives up shadows you'd swear weren't there before. The hood. The gloves. The envelope of cash, counted out in fifties so crisp they might have come straight from the mint with a bow on them. The single instruction, delivered in a voice pitched low and roughened at the edges, like gravel poured through silk:

*Prove the murder hasn't happened yet.*

Most people would have thrown the money back. Most people would have called it a crank, a prankster, a novelist researching atmosphere. But I am not most people, and more to the point, my memory is not most people's memory. It does not blur. It does not soften. It files everything — the exact angle of the client's shoulders under the rain-dark coat, the faint smell of turpentine clinging to the wool, the way the client's left hand had hesitated half a second longer than the right when setting the envelope on my desk.

And it kept replaying the voice.

That was the thing I couldn't shake. Not the words — the words were strange enough, but strangeness is just a job description in my line of work. It was the *cadence*. The rhythm underneath the disguise. The client had spoken slowly, deliberately, spacing out syllables like a person laying tiles: careful, even, watchful for cracks. And every fourth or fifth sentence, the pitch climbed slightly at the end of a clause that wasn't a question.

I knew that habit. I had heard it somewhere, sometime, filed away under some heading I couldn't immediately retrieve. My memory never loses anything; it only occasionally mislabels things, and mislabeled things are worse than lost ones, because you know they're there, taunting you from behind the wrong tab.

So on Tuesday morning I did what any reasonable investigator with an incurable mind would do. I made tea, sat down at my kitchen table with a legal pad, and interrogated myself.

* * *

The method is unglamorous. People imagine photographic memory as a magic trick — close your eyes, see the page. In practice it's closer to being a court stenographer who can never go home. You have to cross-examine your own recollections, and recollections lie the way witnesses do: not usually in substance, but in emphasis.

I started with the voice itself. Low register. Slightly roughened — but artificially so, I was now almost certain. There had been one moment, early in the conversation, when the client had coughed, and beneath the cough the voice had come out clean and unforced for maybe four syllables before the gravel returned. A woman's range. Not deep, exactly — *contained*. The kind of voice that has spent years deciding how much of itself to let out per sentence.

Then the syntax. The client had said, "You'll want to look where everyone's already looked." Not "you should" or "you might." *You'll want to.* A construction that assumes knowledge of the listener's desires — the phrasing of someone who has studied people closely, formally, the way conservators study canvases.

And there was this: when I'd asked how long I had, the client had answered, "Until it hangs." Three words. No article. *It*, not *the portrait* — as if the painting were too painful or too precious to name. People don't name the things that have cost them everything. They say *it* and let the word stand in for the wound.

I wrote all of this down, which is a ritual rather than a necessity — writing it down tells my mind the file is open, actively worked, priority status. Then I did the part that always feels faintly obscene: I went looking through my own head for matches.

* * *

Harrow's Bend is a small town, which means its population of voices is finite, and I have spent thirty-four years — twenty-nine of them conscious — cataloguing them. Marguerite Hale's voice, warm and precise, each word chosen like a specimen for display. Rufus Vane's voice, all baritone assurance, the sound of a man who has never once been interrupted. The gala committee's voices, a chorus of cheerful evasions I'd been collecting all week like pressed flowers.

None of them matched. That was the first finding, and it was more interesting than a match would have been, because the client's cadence wasn't foreign to me. It was *familiar-adjacent*. It sat in my memory like a face seen across a crowded room — you know you know it, you cannot place it, and the not-placing starts to itch.

I closed my eyes and ran the encounter again, full sensory playback, third time that morning. The office. Rain on the window. The client standing just inside the door, refusing the chair I offered — refused it with a small motion, not a shake of the head but a slight turning away, as though sitting down would mean staying, and staying was dangerous.

"You'll know it when you find it," the client had said near the end. "You're the only one who could."

Flattery, obviously. Investigators hear flattery the way waiters hear "table for two." But the emphasis had been wrong for flattery. The stress had fallen on *only* — and there had been something underneath it, something that sounded less like confidence than like hope stretched thin over fear.

And then, as I surfaced from the playback, a fragment detached itself and drifted up from wherever misfiled things live.

A girl's voice, years ago, in the archive. Reading aloud from a finding aid, testing herself. *You'll want to start with the land grants,* she'd said, to no one in particular, to me, to the shelves. *You'll want to start with the land grants because everything else grows out of them.*

The same construction. The same assumption of desire. The same quiet certainty about how knowledge is structured — start here, because everything else grows out of it.

I opened my eyes. My tea had gone cold, which it always does when I'm working, a small tax my habits levy on the beverage industry.

The archive. The girl in the archive. Which meant the Vane estate papers, because the estate papers lived in the archive, because everything in Harrow's Bend eventually lived in the archive — Marguerite saw to that. And it meant a name I hadn't thought about in years, a name attached to a girl who used to sit cross-legged between the stacks reading conservation manuals for pleasure while other children were outside doing whatever children did in towns that hadn't taught them to love paper.

Ada Okoye.

Except Ada Okoye was dead. Had been dead since 2005, according to every record this town kept, and this town kept records the way other towns keep grudges — meticulously, and forever.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the Okoye family, retrieved now in a rush, the way a drawer sticks and then gives all at once.

Samuel Okoye, boatbuilder, came to Harrow's Bend in the late eighties. Married Marguerite — yes, *that* Marguerite, though she was Marguerite Okoye then, and after his death became Marguerite Hale, remarried briefly to a Hale who lasted four years and left her the surname and little else. Their daughter, Ada, born 1991. Samuel drowned in November 2005 — boating accident, off-season, alone, ruled accidental by a coroner who was also, I now remembered with the slow cold turn of a key in a lock, a member of the centenary committee. Ada left town within the year. She would have been fourteen.

And somewhere in the months after the drowning, the record of Ada Okoye's existence acquired a second entry. Death. I had never questioned it — why would I? Death records are the one category of document nobody forges for fun. But I remembered, distantly, the way you remember a dream from the edge of sleep, that there had been something odd about the paperwork. Filed out of sequence. Certified by a doctor whose signature I'd later see on a dozen other certificates, each identical down to the pen pressure.

A girl listed as dead who might not be. A client whose voice carried the ghost of that girl's cadence. A fee paid in advance, in cash, by someone who smelled of turpentine and moved like a person who had spent years practicing how to be no one.

I got my coat.

* * *

The county records office occupies the ground floor of the municipal building, and Mrs. Trelawny has presided over it since roughly the Jurassic period. She greeted me with the expression she reserves for me personally, which is seventy percent fondness and thirty percent suspicion that I've come to audit her.

"Felix Okonkwo. Twice in one month. Should I be flattered or frightened?"

"Neither. I need the 2005 vital statistics index. Deaths, specifically."

Something flickered across her face — quick, professional, gone. "Sad year," she said. "Lots of names."

"Lots of names," I agreed, and watched her pull the volume, and watched her hands. Her hands hesitated for a fraction of a second before opening it — not at random, but at a specific point, as though she knew which page I wanted before I said it. My memory took a photograph of that hesitation and filed it under *evidence*, pending review.

She left me alone with the book, which was either trust or instruction from above. I turned to the O section.

Okoye, Samuel A. — November 14, 2005. Accidental drowning. Coroner: Dr. E. Pruitt.

Okoye, Ada M. — December 2, 2005. Cause of death: —

Cause of death blank. Not *pending*, not *unrecorded* — blank, a field left empty the way you leave a field empty when you intend to come back and fill it in and then decide, on reflection, that an empty field draws less attention than a filled one. Certified by the same doctor. Filed, I noted, checking the accession stamps, eleven days *after* the certificate above it, out of chronological order, in a system Mrs. Trelawny ran with Prussian rigor everywhere else.

Two deaths, one family, six weeks apart. A father drowns; a daughter dies of nothing at all. And the town, which remembers everything — which has spent a century building monuments to its own remembering — had somehow misplaced both funerals. I searched my own memory for the services and found nothing. No flowers at the house. No notice in the *Bend Gazette*. For a town that turns grief into civic pageantry at the drop of a hat, the silence was deafening in a way I had apparently never noticed, because noticing it would have required asking questions, and I had been nineteen and grieving in my own quieter way and Marguerite had told me the family preferred privacy, and I had believed her, because believing Marguerite was not a decision I ever remembered making. It was simply gravity.

I copied the entries by hand, though I didn't need to. Some rituals are for the mind's benefit, not the record's.

"Find what you were looking for?" Mrs. Trelawny asked as I returned the volume.

"I'll let you know."

"That's what worries me," she said, and smiled, and the smile didn't reach whatever it was she wasn't saying.

* * *

Outside, the town was dressing itself for its birthday. Bunting along the high street. The banner over the square: HARROW'S BEND — ONE HUNDRED YEARS OF MEMORY. Someone had chosen the slogan without irony, or possibly with a great deal of it; in this town the two were often indistinguishable. Workmen were bolting a frame to the town hall facade where the restored Vane portrait would hang after the unveiling — Edmund Vane, benefactor, gazing beneficently down at a century he had, if the gossip among the gala committee's silences was anything to go by, purchased in installments.

I stood in the square and let the pieces arrange themselves, because sometimes they will, if you hold still enough.

A client who refuses chairs and smells of solvents. A voice with a girl's cadence under a stranger's gravel. A conservator hired — I'd checked the gala contracts yesterday, casually, the way you check a lock you already suspect — to clean the Vane portrait before the unveiling. A conservator whose credentials traced back to a firm in Lisbon that, when I telephoned, confirmed employment in an accent that suggested the confirmation had been rehearsed. A woman recorded dead in 2005 by a doctor who signed everything the same way.

And underneath it all, the instruction itself, which I had been treating as eccentric and was now beginning to read as precise. *Prove the murder hasn't happened yet.* Not *stop a murder*. Not *prevent*. *Prove it hasn't happened yet* — the phrasing of someone who knows that a murder occurred once already, that the town buried it, and that the burial is scheduled to be repeated. The future tense as a disguise for the past. Whoever the client was, she wasn't afraid of what might happen at the gala.

She was afraid of what had already happened, happening again.

I walked home the long way, past the boatyard where the Okoye shed still stood, paint peeling, padlocked, untouched for twenty years — the town's memory again, selective as a museum curator. And as I passed it, the last piece slid into place, the one I'd been circling for two days without wanting to touch.

The client had known my name before I introduced myself. Had known it, and used it once, softly, at the very end — *"Thank you, Mr. Okonkwo"* — and there had been something in the way she said it. Not the polite weight of a stranger's name. The particular care of someone handling a word that mattered to her, that she had maybe said to herself across years and oceans, a name connected to her father's world, to the boats, to the man who had taught a lonely kid with a strange mind that paper could be trusted when people couldn't.

Samuel Okoye had helped build half the boats in this town. He had fixed my uncle's skiff one summer, patiently, letting me hand him tools, telling me that every well-made thing was just a promise kept in wood.

His daughter had come back from the dead to hire me.

The thrill of the hunt — that clean, bright engine that had powered my whole career — stuttered, caught, and turned into something heavier. Because this was no longer a puzzle with a fee attached. This was a family. This was a name that had been taken from a living girl and nailed into a ledger, and the hand that had nailed it there belonged, in all likelihood, to the woman who taught me everything I knew about keeping secrets safe.

Marguerite had taught me that a file pulled before indexing was a kindness. I had believed her then.

Standing in front of the padlocked shed, with the bunting snapping overhead and the centenary eleven days out, I understood that I was about to find out whether she had been kind, or merely careful.

The portrait arrived at the conservator's studio tomorrow.

So, I decided, would I.
