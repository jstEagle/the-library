## 8. Marguerite's Perfect System

The Harrow's Bend Municipal Archive smelled the way it had always smelled: dust, lemon polish, and the faint vinegar of old newsprint slowly eating itself. Twenty years hadn't changed it. Marguerite Hale had seen to that. If anything, she'd improved it — new climate controls, acid-free boxes in regimented rows, a reading room with brass lamps like something out of a better-funded town. The building was a mausoleum that had been taught to hum.

I stood in the doorway a moment longer than was professional. Old habit. You don't walk into a room where someone you love works without first letting your memory take its inventory, because rooms change and memories don't, and the difference between them is usually where the truth lives.

Marguerite was at her desk beneath the tall window, sorting photographs into a shoebox-sized tray with tweezers. Silver hair pinned up with what I recognized — my memory recognized before I did, which is how it always worked — as a tortoiseshell comb she'd owned since at least 1998. Reading glasses on a chain. A cardigan the color of weak tea. She looked exactly like the woman who had taught a seventeen-year-old insurance investigator's daughter how to index a town.

"Felix Okonkwo," she said without looking up. "You still stand in doorways like you're waiting for permission."

"You always said the archive grants entry, not me."

"The archive grants nothing." She set down the tweezers and looked at me over the glasses, and her whole face did the thing it had always done — folded itself around warmth like paper around a gift. "It only remembers. Come here and let me look at you. You've got shadows under your eyes. Someone's making you work for a living."

"Someone's paying me to, anyway."

"An improvement over your mother." She said it lightly, but I felt the small barb of it go in, the way her barbs always went in — angled so you couldn't tell if they were affection or assessment. My mother had done her filing for free, out of some civic devotion Marguerite had cultivated in half the women of this town. "Sit. I'll make tea. The good tea, not the committee tea."

She disappeared into the back room, and I let myself do what I'd come to do, which was not visit an old teacher but search her archive.

Here is what people misunderstand about photographic memory. It isn't a camera. It's a filing system. Anyone can see everything; almost no one can retrieve the right thing at the right moment. Marguerite understood this before I did. *Memory without method is just suffering*, she used to say, tapping my temple with one finger. *You have a cathedral in there, girl. Give it a card catalog.* She'd built mine herself, over two years of Saturday afternoons, teaching me her system: cross-references, accession numbers, provenance notes. Every fact about every person in Harrow's Bend, filed where I could find it.

Which meant that when I walked into her archive now, my catalog opened itself automatically, and the entries came up in order:

*Hale, Marguerite Okoye-Hale. Town historian since 1991. Archivist, municipal records. Married Thomas Hale, 2007, following the death of Samuel Okoye, 2005. Chair, Centenary Committee. Keeper of the Vane papers.*

*Okoye, Samuel. Died 2005. Boathouse. Ruled accidental. File never indexed.*

There it was. Entry number whatever-it-was, sitting in my head where it had sat for twenty years, patient as a splinter working toward the surface. The file that had no accession number. The folder Marguerite had pulled from my hands one October afternoon and said, *This one comes to me, Felix. Some things aren't for the record.*

I had been nineteen. I had trusted her the way you trust gravity.

Marguerite came back with two cups and a plate of shortbread arranged with archival precision. She poured, sat, and studied me with the frank pleasure of a woman who has known you since you were small enough to be bribed with biscuits.

"So," she said. "Tell me about this client of yours. Half the gala committee says you've been asking odd questions. Rufus Vane says you've been asking *dangerous* ones, though he said it smiling, which with Rufus means he's already called someone."

"My client asked me to prove a murder hasn't happened yet."

I watched her face the way I'd been trained to watch faces — hers most carefully of all, because she'd trained me. And there it was, or rather there it wasn't: no flicker, no pause, no recalibration. Just a slow, amused arch of one eyebrow.

"A murder that hasn't happened yet. How does one investigate a negative, Felix? That's not detection, that's theology."

"That's what I told them. They paid in advance anyway."

"Cash?"

"Cash."

"Mm." She sipped her tea. "Well. People pay for strange comforts. When I curated the flood exhibit in '09, a man gave me five hundred dollars to display his grandfather's photograph facing away from the water. Said the dead don't like reminders. I took the money and turned the photograph. Comfort is a legitimate product."

"This isn't comfort. This is a prediction. Specific details. A night, a place, a method."

"And did they give you these details?" Her voice stayed light. Her hands, wrapping around her cup, went very slightly still — not frozen, just *settled*, the way a house settles at night. Most people would never have caught it. I caught it because she taught me to catch it, and because I loved her, and because those two facts had apparently spent twenty years conspiring against each other without informing me.

"They gave me enough," I said. "And when I ran the details backward instead of forward, they matched something that already happened."

The steam rose between us. Somewhere in the stacks, the dehumidifier cycled on with a sound like a long sigh.

"Something that already happened," she repeated. Not a question. A stalling maneuver, buying herself four seconds, and I knew it was four seconds because I'd timed her pauses since I was twelve.

"Samuel Okoye," I said.

I want to report that I said it gently. I didn't. Twenty years of misfiled memory had curdled into something with edges, and I let it out flat and hard, the way you'd set a document on a table.

Marguerite did not flinch. That was the first terrible thing. A woman hearing a drowned man's name after two decades should flinch, or weep, or rage. Marguerite refilled her own cup, and her hand was steady, and when she spoke her voice had gone soft in the particular way she used for grieving widows and difficult donors.

"Samuel Okoye was a tragedy," she said. "A drowning. An accident with alcohol involved, if you read the coroner's report, which I have, twice. His daughter left town and never wrote. His widow — myself, eventually, though not then — grieved. The town moved on, because towns must. What exactly is it you think you're investigating, Felix?"

"A file," I said. "October 2005. I brought it to you for indexing. You pulled it before it entered the system. I've been trying to remember why for twenty years, and yesterday I finally asked myself the right question, which wasn't *why did she pull it* but *what did she know that needed pulling*."

For the first time, something moved behind her eyes. Not guilt. Calculation — fast, fluent, practiced calculation, the kind I'd mistaken all my life for wisdom.

"You were a child," she said.

"I was nineteen."

"You were a child who saw everything and understood nothing. That's not an insult; it's a description. It's why I hired you. Why I *built* you." She set down her cup with a small ceramic click that my memory filed under *finality*. "Felix. Look at me. Whatever this client has whispered to you, whatever pattern-matching game you've played with yourself — Harrow's Bend buried Samuel Okoye the way it buries everyone it loses. Under flowers, under a plaque, under a nice sentence in the centenary book. That's not a conspiracy. That's grief management. I should know. I managed mine."

"It's not grief I'm asking about. It's paperwork."

"Then ask about paperwork." She smiled, and the smile was warm, and I hated how much warmth was still in it, how much of it was real, how much of it had been real for twenty years while — while what? While what, Felix? Finish the sentence. *While she did what?* I couldn't finish it. That was the horror of it. My perfect memory held every piece and refused to assemble them, because the assembly instructions had come from her, and she'd written the catalog to protect herself.

Or to protect the town. In October 2005, those had seemed like the same thing.

"Felix." She leaned forward and covered my hand with hers, and her fingers were cool and dry and familiar as my own pulse. "Centenary season. Do you understand what this town is right now? A hundred years of being a place worth staying in, about to be celebrated, written up, photographed. Investors coming. Young families looking at the school numbers. One hundred years of careful, decent work by people who gave their lives to this bend of the river — and you propose to dig up a sad accident from 2005 because a stranger paid you cash and spoke in riddles?"

"People don't pay cash for riddles."

"No," she agreed. "They pay cash for outcomes. Ask yourself which outcome your client wants. Ask yourself who benefits from a town tearing its own history open three weeks before its hundredth birthday." She squeezed my hand once and released it, and the release was its own kind of message: *lesson concluded*. "You were the best student I ever had. Better than I deserved. So hear me as your teacher and not your friend: some files stay closed not because they hide guilt but because opening them hurts innocent people. Ada Okoye is dead to this town. Let her rest wherever she is. And let the portrait hang."

*Let the portrait hang.*

I kept my face still. Filed the phrase. Cross-referenced it against the client's words — *the night it hangs* — and felt the two sentences click together somewhere below thought, like tumblers in a lock I couldn't see.

"I should go," I said.

"You should eat your shortbread. You never eat when you're working, and it makes you sharp in ways that cut you." But she was already standing, smoothing her cardigan, becoming the town historian again, the public monument of herself. At the door to the stacks she paused, and for one unguarded moment she looked old, and tired, and fond, all three at once, and I could not tell which of the three was the mask.

"For what it's worth," she said, "whoever this client is, they chose you because you remember everything. Remember that too, when you're deciding what to believe. Memory is only evidence. It isn't judgment. Judgment is the part you have to earn."

* * *

I didn't go home. I walked the river path past the boathouse, because my feet are traitors and my memory worse, and because there is a bench there with a plaque I can recite verbatim: *In memory of those who worked these waters, 1925–2005.* No names. The town's favorite genre of memorial — everyone, therefore no one.

I sat down and made myself do the thing I'd avoided for twenty years. I reopened the file.

October 14th, 2005. Rain earlier that day; the boards slick. I'd been nineteen, indexing flood-damage claims with Marguerite, and I'd gone to the boathouse to photograph water lines for the record. Samuel Okoye was there. He was arguing with someone I never saw — a voice, low, through the boathouse wall. Then the sound. Then the water. Then Marguerite's hand on my shoulder, arriving impossibly fast, saying *you saw nothing, Felix, there was nothing to see, some things aren't for the record* — and nineteen-year-old me, catalog-brained and loyal, had obeyed the one instruction that broke the whole system.

I had indexed everything else in my life. Everything except that.

Until now. Until a hooded stranger with a familiar cadence paid me cash to prove a murder hadn't happened yet, and my own catalog, my cathedral, my perfect system, began quietly returning results I'd spent two decades refusing to shelve.

The river moved past the pilings, brown and patient. On the far bank, the town hall windows were lit for the evening's gala planning session, and I could see them moving the portrait crate inside — the restored Edmund Vane, arriving early, waiting to hang.

Some things aren't for the record, Marguerite had said.

I stood up, and for the first time in my life I disagreed with her out loud, to an empty riverbank, in a voice that shook.

"Everything," I said, "is for the record."

Behind me, gravel crunched. I turned, and halfway up the path stood a figure in a hood, watching me — and even at forty yards, even in failing light, my memory matched the cadence of the single word they spoke next before the wind took it.
