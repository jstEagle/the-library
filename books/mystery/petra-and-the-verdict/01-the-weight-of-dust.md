## 1. The Weight of Dust

The trouble with dust, Emrys Lockridge had long ago decided, was that everyone treated it as an enemy when it was really the most honest historian in Marrow's Reach.

Dust told you what mattered. It settled thick on the things nobody touched and thin as a confession on the things everybody did. It recorded traffic the way a ledger recorded debts. And it never, ever lied — unlike the parish registers, which had been "corrected" at least twice in living memory, or the memorial plaques downstairs, one of which claimed a benefactor had died at seventy-one when every other document agreed he'd been fifty-nine and lying about it even then.

Emrys stood at the top of the archive stair with a lamp in one hand and a soft brush in the other, surveying their kingdom: three floors of shelving tucked into the cathedral's north transept like a secret kept too long to mention. The air smelled of vellum and tallow and cold stone, a smell Emrys had once heard a visiting bishop describe as "damp piety," which had struck Emrys as unfair, since dampness was the cathedral's problem and piety was mostly its performance.

"Right," Emrys said to the shelves, because talking to shelves was perfectly normal behavior for an archivist and nobody was there to say otherwise. "Inventory. Every box, every bundle, before tomorrow makes liars of us all."

Tomorrow. The word sat in Emrys's chest like a swallowed stone.

Tomorrow they would dig up Aldous Verity.

* * *

It had taken eleven years for Emrys to become invisible, and they were quietly proud of the achievement. Invisibility, properly done, was not disappearance. It was a kind of architecture. You built yourself into the structure of a place until people leaned on you without noticing you were there.

The vergers leaned on Emrys when they needed to know which vestry chest held the good altar linens versus the moth-eaten ones. The choristers leaned on them when a music part-book went missing the week before a feast day (it was always under the organ loft, third shelf, behind the box of broken candle-snuffers; it was always). Even Dean Halloway leaned on them, in his gentle, absent way, asking Emrys to "have a look" at documents that needed reading quickly and discussed never.

Emrys had arrived at seventeen with nothing but a satchel, a forged letter of recommendation from a school that had burned down — which was efficient, if morally murky, since no one could check — and a terror of being found. The cathedral had taken them in the way cathedrals take in everything: without questions, on the assumption that anyone who wanted to live among old papers must be either harmless or holy, and Emrys had worked very hard for fifteen years to be convincingly harmless.

They had learned the trick of walking so that footsteps didn't echo. They had learned which hours the nave emptied and which corners of the cloister caught the light in ways that made a person visible whether they wished to be or not. They had learned to answer questions with just enough blandness that people stopped asking them. *What's your family name, Lockridge? Where from, originally?* — answered with a shrug, a smile, a deflection about the weather's effect on sixteenth-century bindings, until even Halloway's curiosity, which was real but shallow as a baptismal font, gave up and moved on to the collection plate.

And Emrys had told themselves, all these years, that this was contentment. That a life spent among the dead men's paper was a life well spent. That the archive asked nothing of them except care, and gave nothing back except purpose, and that this bargain suited them down to the ground.

Most days, they almost believed it.

Tonight was not most days.

* * *

The inventory itself was honest work, and Emrys threw themselves into it the way some people throw themselves into prayer — with more motion than reflection.

Box forty-one: correspondence of Precentor Aldwin, 1802 to 1841, mostly complaints about the choirboys' boots. Box forty-two: the same complaints, continued, in a worse hand, suggesting the boots had won. Box forty-three contained a reliquary that was supposed to hold a fingerbone of Saint Cedd but actually held, by Emrys's private and unshakeable suspicion, a pig's toe, and Emrys dusted it anyway, because faith was not their department. Accuracy was.

They worked through the evening, and the work did what work always did for Emrys: it narrowed the world down to something manageable. A page here. A folio there. The small, clean satisfaction of a thing catalogued correctly. Out there beyond the archive windows, the town was buzzing like a knocked-over hive — Emrys could hear it even through stone, the market square filling already with folk come early for tomorrow's spectacle, hammers knocking up grandstands, children daring each other to touch the ropes around the Verity plot. But in here, the world was boxes and brushes and the soft rasp of vellum, and Emrys could pretend, for a few more hours, that the ground outside was going to stay politely closed.

Except.

Except that Emrys knew things about the Verity plot that the town did not, because knowing things was the whole of their trade, and one of the things they knew was sitting right now in the burial register, open on the lectern where they'd left it, and it would not stop looking at them.

Emrys crossed to it. They had been crossing to it all day, the way a tongue returns to a sore tooth.

The register lay open to the year 1789, and there it was in copperplate: *Aldous Verity, founder and benefactor of this church, interred in the east vault, the 14th day of October, in the presence of the Chapter.*

In the presence of the Chapter. Emrys had read that line four hundred times over the past month, since Magistrate Corven's writ had arrived demanding the exhumation, and each reading had produced the same small wrongness, the itch of a catalogue entry that doesn't match its object. Because Emrys had also read the sextons' day-books — all of them, back two centuries, because that was what an archivist *did*, that was the entire point of an archivist, to be the one person in a town who remembered what everyone else found convenient to forget —

— and the sexton's day-book for October 1789 said, in a different hand, cramped and hurried:

*Vault sealed. No viewing.*

No viewing. For the founder. For the great man whose funeral, according to the town's own cherished legend, had drawn weeping thousands. Whose coffin, according to that same legend, had lain in state for three days while the poor of Marrow's Reach filed past to kiss its lid.

Three days of lying in state, and then *no viewing*, and then a sealing done in a hurry by a sexton whose handwriting shook.

Emrys closed the register gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping. Or not sleeping. Or possibly not there at all.

"That's not evidence," they told the lectern sternly. "That's a shaky hand and a bad week. Sextons have bad weeks. Sextons have gout."

The lectern declined to be comforted, and so did Emrys.

Because there was a second thing. There was always a second thing; archives were like murders that way. The stonemasons' ledger for the same autumn recorded payment for "one coffin, lead-lined, best quality, with nameplate" — and then, three lines below, in the same week, payment for "second coffin, plain elm, no plate." Two coffins. One burial. Emrys had turned that page over in their mind so many times the paper might as well have been worn soft, and every explanation they constructed had the structural integrity of wet vellum.

Perhaps the elm coffin was for someone else. Perhaps. Perhaps a pauper buried the same week, though the poor book for that fortnight recorded no deaths at all, which was itself odd, because the poor book recorded *everything*, including a woman fined twopence for letting her goat into the churchyard, which Emrys had always felt was the truest sentence in the whole archive.

Two coffins. One grave. And tomorrow morning, in front of the magistrate and half the town, somebody was going to find out which.

* * *

Near midnight, Emrys climbed the archive stair to the little window that looked out over the east end of the cathedral, where the Verity plot lay under its iron fence and its yew trees, waiting.

Frost had begun to silver the grass. Down below, the gravediggers' tent glowed with lantern-light, and two men sat outside it drinking something from a flask, their voices carrying up in fragments — jokes, mostly, the gallows humor of men hired to do a thing nobody quite wanted done. Tomorrow at dawn they would cut the turf, lift the slabs, and break seals that had held for a hundred and thirty-odd years, and the whole town would be watching, and whatever came out of that ground would change Marrow's Reach forever, and Emrys Lockridge, keeper of its memory, would stand in the crowd like everyone else, holding nothing, saying nothing, being no one.

That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement. Emrys watched, and Emrys kept, and Emrys did not speak — and in exchange the cathedral let them stay, warm and fed and safely unnamed, and Emrys had genuinely believed, right up until this week, that the price was fair.

Now they pressed their forehead briefly against the cold glass and admitted, to the frost and the yews and the tented grave, what they had been refusing to admit all day: that they were afraid. Not of scandal. Scandal was weather; institutions survived weather. What frightened Emrys was simpler and much worse.

Whatever was in that coffin — bones, or stones, or nothing, or something — it meant the record was wrong. Their record. The one they had spent fifteen years keeping faithful, dusting and mending and defending against mice and moisture and the casual revisions of important men. If Aldous Verity was not in his grave, then somewhere between the copperplate entry and the shaking sexton's hand, the truth had been swapped out and replaced, deliberately, by people whose names Emrys probably knew, whose portraits perhaps hung downstairs, whose descendants still sat in the front pews.

And if the record could be wrong about the founder — the single most documented death in the town's history — then what else was it wrong about? How many other entries in how many other books were smooth copperplate laid over a hollow? Emrys thought of the archive below them, three floors of it, every shelf trusted, every box believed, and felt for the first time in fifteen years like a person standing on a floor that might, at any moment, prove to be a ceiling.

"You're catastrophizing," they informed their reflection in the dark window. The reflection looked pale and unconvinced. "There is a perfectly reasonable explanation involving Victorian incompetence and a misplaced corpse. Happened all the time. Whole parishes mislaid their dead. There's a village in Norfolk that buried the wrong man twice."

The reflection did not answer, which was just as well, because Emrys wasn't sure they'd have believed it either.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when Emrys finally climbed down to lock up, and it was on the bottom stair, lamp low, that they noticed the smell.

Not dust. Not vellum. Something sharper — lamp oil? No. Wax. Fresh wax, the hot-honey smell of a candle recently snuffed, drifting faintly from the direction of the crypt stair.

Emrys stood very still.

The crypt was locked. Emrys held the key, and the key was on the ring at their belt, where it had been all evening, and the door below was heavy oak banded with iron, and nobody went down there after compline except Emrys and, once a quarter, the verger to check for damp. Nobody had any business smelling of candle-wax in the crypt at two in the morning on the night before the town dug up its founder.

For a long moment Emrys considered — seriously, temptingly — going to bed. Going to bed was the archivist's move. Going to bed was the invisible person's move. See nothing, note nothing, mention nothing, survive. Fifteen years of training rose in them like a tide, whispering that whatever was happening below was above their station, that keys and ledgers were their business and shadows were somebody else's.

But the floor-that-might-be-a-ceiling feeling was still in their chest, and the two coffins were still in their head, and Emrys found that their feet had already made the decision their courage hadn't got round to.

They took the crypt stair slowly, lamp shielded, breath held. At the bottom, the great door stood exactly as it should — shut, locked, undisturbed. But through the gap beneath it, thin as a knife, ran the last grey ribbon of candle-smoke.

And as Emrys knelt, heart hammering, to look closer — as their eye caught, in the light of their own small flame, the faint fresh scuff-marks on the flagstones leading away from the door toward the oldest wall of the crypt, the wall where the founders' memorial plaques hung row upon row in the dark —

— somewhere below, deep in the stone silence, a latch clicked softly shut.

Emrys stayed kneeling on the cold step, alone in the dark with a hundred and thirty years of secrets, and understood with perfect clarity that tomorrow's exhumation was going to be the least of it.
