## 1. The Weight of Dust

The trouble with being invisible, Dominic Blackwood had learned, was that people kept handing you things.

Not the way they handed things to the dean, or the canons, or even the vergers—cassocks and croziers and the small ceremonial burdens of importance. No. They handed her *dust*. They handed her boxes. On this particular Tuesday morning in October, a verger named Tam had deposited four archive cartons at her door with the apologetic air of a man delivering something mildly unpleasant, said "These were in the triforium, Miss Blackwood, no idea what's in 'em," and vanished before she could ask how long, exactly, four boxes of unlabelled parish papers had been sitting forty feet above the nave, quietly composting.

Dominic looked at the boxes. The boxes, she felt, looked back.

"Right," she told them. "Let's see what you've been hiding."

She was the archivist of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral, which meant that to most of the cathedral community she was less a person than a function—a place you went when you needed to know whether the 1887 restoration invoices mentioned the west window, or whether anyone had ever written down why the Lady Chapel smelled of beeswax and old grievances. She was fifty-one years old, small, grey-streaked, and possessed of the particular talent of being forgettable in a room full of people who prided themselves on remembering everything. It was a gift. She had spent thirty years cultivating it the way other people cultivated orchids: patiently, in the dark, with no one watching.

What nobody at St. Aldhelm's knew—what nobody anywhere knew—was that Dominic Blackwood did not merely keep the cathedral's records.

She read them the way other people read confession.

* * *

By eleven o'clock she had the first box open on her worktable and was elbow-deep in the sedimentary layers of a parish that had been burying its paperwork almost as diligently as it buried its dead. Minutes of the Fabric Advisory Committee, 1979–1984. A receipt for two hundred hymnals. Someone's abandoned attempt at a history of the bell ringers, which stopped abruptly mid-sentence in 1991 and never explained itself, as if the author had simply walked out of his own life between one clause and the next.

Dominic made notes. Dominic always made notes. Her system was her own invention and would have horrified any properly trained archivist: three colours of ink, a private index in a locked drawer, and a habit of writing down not just what documents said but what they conspicuously failed to say. Gaps, she had found, were more eloquent than ink. A missing page spoke volumes. A signature that appeared everywhere for twenty years and then nowhere at all was practically a shout.

It was this habit—the reading of gaps—that had gotten her into trouble, though "trouble" was perhaps too dramatic a word for what happened in the privacy of her own head, over tea, at the end of long days.

Three times now, an accident at St. Aldhelm's had refused to stay an accident.

The first had been old Mr. Petchey, the boiler man, found dead at the foot of the crypt stairs eight years ago with a broken hip and a heart that had obligingly stopped. Death by misadventure, said the coroner; he'd had a weak heart and the steps were steep. And so it might have been, except that Dominic, cataloguing the sacristy accounts, noticed that someone had paid for the crypt stairs to be re-surfaced six weeks *before* Mr. Petchey died—and that the invoice for the work was signed by a hand that didn't match any name attached to the job. She'd sat with that discrepancy for a fortnight, turning it over like a stone, before concluding that whoever had arranged those repairs had known the boiler man would be going down those stairs on a particular night, and had wanted him to arrive safely enough to die of something else entirely. She had never proved anything. She hadn't needed to. She had simply written it all down, sealed it in a folder marked *PETCH — MISC*, and put it in the locked drawer where she kept the things that were true and unsayable.

The second had been quieter still: a volunteer flower-arranger, Mrs. Anand, who fell from the pulpitum steps during a power cut and hit her head on the medieval pavement. Everyone agreed it was tragic, terribly sad, the lights had gone out, she shouldn't have been up there alone. But Dominic had the electricity log, because Dominic had every log, and the power cut that night had lasted ninety seconds and affected only the north transept circuit—which had been tripped from inside the vestry, by someone who then had eleven minutes, according to the CCTV gap, to be elsewhere doing something else. She'd written that down too. *ANAND — MISC.* The folder was getting crowded.

And the third—

Dominic set down her pen and looked out the narrow window of her office, across the close, to where the tower stood against a sky the colour of dishwater. The third was only five weeks old, and it was not yet finished with her.

His name had been Callum Reeve. Thirty-four, stonemason, second-generation, the sort of cheerful competent man who whistled while he repointed buttresses and remembered everyone's children's names. He had fallen from the tower on the twenty-ninth of September—not from the top, mercifully, but from the ringing chamber gallery, a drop of perhaps thirty feet onto the leaded roof of the south aisle, and then a roll, and then stillness. Broken back, punctured lung, dead before the ambulance came. Health and safety had descended like weather. Loose scaffold board, they concluded. Inadequate edge protection. An accident, said everyone, in the tone of people closing a book.

Dominic had gone to his funeral, because she went to funerals—it was, she sometimes thought grimly, the closest thing she had to a social life—and she had stood at the back among the masons' families and watched Callum's mother hold herself together with the terrible discipline of women who have done it before. And afterward, walking home through the close in the dark, she had thought: *loose board.*

Because Dominic had the tower maintenance records. Of course she did. And the scaffold inspection for the week of the twenty-ninth of September was signed off, dated, thorough—inspected on the Monday, certified sound, every board accounted for. Callum died on the Thursday. Boards do not come loose in three days under a careful man's own feet. And there was one more thing, small, the kind of thing only she would ever have seen: the access log for the tower door showed Callum signing in at 7:40 that evening, alone, for a routine check of the weathervane fixings. And it showed one other entry, earlier, at 6:15, in handwriting that had been smudged—deliberately, she suspected—into illegibility. Someone had been in the tower before him. Someone whose name had been rubbed away with a thumb, or a sleeve, while the ink was still fresh.

She had stood in her office that night holding the log under her desk lamp for a very long time, feeling the familiar cold settle behind her sternum. Not fear, exactly. Something adjacent to fear. The recognition that the world contained people who erased their own names, and that she was the only person alive who had noticed the erasure.

She had not yet opened a folder for Callum Reeve. That was the strange part, the part she couldn't explain even to herself. Three nights running she had taken out a fresh manila folder and written nothing on it. As if some instinct in her, older than her habits, was waiting for something else to happen first.

* * *

At half past one, Mrs. Ogilvie brought her tea.

This was the closest thing St. Aldhelm's had to a ritual in Dominic's honour, and she guarded it jealously. Mrs. Ogilvie was eighty-one, had cleaned the cathedral offices since the reign of roughly the last king but one, and was the only person in the entire close who treated Dominic as though she were a person rather than a filing system. She also missed nothing, which made her either the safest or the most dangerous woman in the building, depending on the day.

"You've got your thinking face on," Mrs. Ogilvie announced, setting down the tray. "That's the face you make when you're reading something that isn't there."

Dominic blinked. "I don't have a thinking face."

"Course you don't, dear." Mrs. Ogilvie straightened a stack of folders that had been, in Dominic's opinion, perfectly straight already. "My Harold used to say the same about his poker face, and lost our house extension money learning better." She paused at the door, and for a moment her briskness faltered into something gentler. "You should eat your lunch, Miss Blackwood. You get thin when you're thinking."

"I'm always thin."

"I know," said Mrs. Ogilvie, with devastating mildness, and left.

Dominic ate her sandwich standing at the window, watching a party of tourists photograph the tower. One of them, a small boy in a red anorak, pointed up at the parapet and said something she couldn't hear, and his mother laughed, and Dominic felt the peculiar ache she always felt at such moments—the ache of the perpetual witness, the woman who saw everything and was seen by no one. Competence without witness. She had built a life on it, brick by patient brick, and most days the bricks held. Most days the invisibility felt like a coat that fit.

But lately—since the twenty-ninth of September, if she was honest—it had begun to feel more like a shroud that fit.

* * *

The afternoon gave her the fourth box, and the fourth box gave her the thing.

She nearly missed it. It was tucked inside a copy of the 1962 confirmation roll, folded into eighths, the paper gone soft and brown as old skin: a single sheet of lined notepaper, undated, unsigned, in a cramped and hurried hand.

*It is not right what was done and I cannot carry it alone much longer. If anything happens to me look in the place where the water comes in.*

That was all. No name. No year. Just those two sentences, folded away inside a record of children confirmed into the faith, hidden by someone who had evidently hoped God or posterity might do what courage could not.

Dominic sat very still for a long moment. Rain ticked against her window. Somewhere below, the organist began working through the evensong voluntaries, the same eight bars again and again, patient as tide.

*The place where the water comes in.* St. Aldhelm's had exactly one place where water came in, and it wasn't supposed to: the old cistern channel in the north wall of the undercroft, dry for decades, bricked up behind a grille that everyone assumed was Victorian and no one had ever bothered to open. Dominic had catalogued the grille itself, years ago—*iron, later repair, keyhole present, key absent*—and had noted, in her private index, the small oddity that the brickwork around it was newer than the bricks it replaced.

Someone had opened it. Someone had closed it again. And someone, once, long ago, had written down that whatever was inside was *not right*.

She looked at the note for a long time. Then she did what she always did: she took out a fresh folder, wrote a date and a single word—*CISTERN*—and reached for the locked drawer to file it beside the others.

Her hand stopped halfway there.

Five weeks of instinct finally spoke, and it spoke clearly: *not yet*. Something about the note, about the tower log with its smudged name, about the loose board that wasn't loose—something was converging, the way separate streams converge underground before surfacing somewhere no one expects. Filing it would be hiding it, and hiding it had always been safe, and safety suddenly felt like the wrong instinct entirely.

Instead, Dominic folded the note back along its creases, put it in her coat pocket, and picked up the torch from her desk drawer—the heavy one, the one she'd bought after Mr. Petchey and never explained to anyone.

The undercroft stairs were cold and smelled of stone and centuries. At the bottom, past the stacked chairs and the forgotten trestles, the little iron grille waited in the north wall, patient as everything else in St. Aldhelm's.

Dominic knelt in front of it, torch in her teeth, and tried the keyhole with the one skeleton key she probably shouldn't have copied.

It turned.

"Well," she said, to the dust, to the dark, to no witness at all. "Aren't you full of surprises."

The grille swung inward with a groan, and beyond it lay a blackness that had been waiting thirty years for someone to come looking—and, though she could not yet know it, so had someone else.
