## 5. What the Drowned Girl Kept

Cressida Ashcombe had been married into the family for twenty-six years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about grief at Ashcombe House: it was kept in the east wing, it was dusted weekly by Mrs. Tredwell, and it was never, under any circumstances, discussed after the second glass of wine.

She was thinking about this on Thursday morning while reconciling the estate accounts at the long table in the morning room, because the insurance man — Mr. Whitlock, with his careful grey suit and his eyes that moved over a room like a hand smoothing a bedsheet — had asked her yesterday, quite casually, whether the family ever spoke of Margot.

"We don't," she had said, which was true.

"Why not?" he had asked, which was also fair, and she had given him the answer everyone gave: *It was a long time ago. It was very sad. We've tried to move forward.* The liturgy. She could recite it in her sleep, and sometimes suspected she did.

The trouble with Mr. Whitlock was that he listened to answers the way other people listened to music. He didn't nod and move on. He *held* them. She had watched him hold Edmund's answer about Ruth Ellery — *died in a car crash, terrible business, 1994* — the way one holds a note to test whether it's flat.

And it was flat. Cressida had known it was flat for years.

* * *

Here is what Cressida knew, arranged not as suspicion but as bookkeeping, because bookkeeping was the only language in which she trusted herself:

Item. In 1994, the household staff roster listed a housemaid named Ruth Ellery, aged nineteen. In 1995, the trust's solicitor sent round a letter confirming that no member of the former staff remained "in contact with the family." In 1996, Edmund's mother had asked Cressida, apropos of nothing, over shelling peas, whether she thought one could be *sure* a person was dead without seeing the body. Then she had gone back to the peas and never raised it again, and Cressida had spent a decade deciding she'd imagined it.

Item. The east wing. Three rooms sealed since before her marriage — Margot's bedroom, dressing room, and the little sitting room with the bad window that let in the sea smell. Charles Ashcombe had sealed them himself, personally, with his own key, which was unlike him; Charles delegated everything, including, Cressida sometimes thought, his conscience. When Charles died ten years ago, the keys went into the library safe, and Edmund, who could not find his own spectacles without a search party, had apparently never once opened the safe.

Item. The drinking. Edmund drank the way rain falls on the moors — steadily, comprehensively, without malice toward any particular object. By seven each evening he was sentimental; by nine he was historical; by eleven he was dangerous only to furniture. And in the historical phase, twice a year, usually near the anniversary of something no one named, he would stand at the window facing the lake and say, to no one, "I was holding the rail. I was holding the rail, and Father said—" and then he would see Cressida and say, "Nothing. Pour me another, there's a good girl."

*I was holding the rail.* Not *I was standing on the steps.* Holding the rail. As though the rail were the important thing. As though someone had once needed to know precisely where his hands were.

Item. The payouts. This was the item she did not put on paper anywhere, not even in cipher, not even in the coded shorthand she used for the wine cellar. The trust paid quarterly. She had managed the books for fifteen years and the payments had never wavered, never paused, never once required anyone to prove anything to anybody. A trust like that — an inheritance clause like that — should have teeth. Should have conditions. Every will she'd ever read for fun (she read wills for fun; this was the sort of woman she was) had conditions. This one had a condition so simple it was almost elegant: *while no legitimate heir lives.*

Four deaths in thirty years, and every time, the money had simply continued, like a river that had never heard of the sea.

She added the column, found it balanced, and sat looking out at the lake, which lay flat and silver and entirely unbothered, the way it had lain in every photograph she'd ever seen of it, including the ones from July 1994.

"You're doing the face," said her daughter Clemency from the doorway.

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the accountancy face. The one where you've found something wrong and you're deciding whether it's worth the row." Clemency came in and stole a biscuit from the tin Cressida kept for exactly this purpose. At twenty-four, Clemency had inherited her mother's arithmetic and her father's talent for avoiding consequences, which made her either the most dangerous or the most useful person in the county, depending on the decade. "Is it the insurance man?"

"He's not *the* insurance man, he's an insurance investigator."

"He asked Toby if the boathouse was original to the house. Toby said yes. It isn't, it burned down in '89 and was rebuilt. Do you think he knows things like that?"

"I think," Cressida said, "that he knows everything, and is waiting politely for us to notice."

"Mm." Clemency ate the biscuit with the serenity of the young. "I liked him. He looked at Mummy's portrait like it owed him money."

"That's Margot's portrait."

"Is it? I always thought that was Aunt Margot."

"It is Aunt Margot. You've met— " Cressida stopped. Clemency had been six when the portraits were rehung. There had been no reason, in twenty years, for anyone to teach the children which silent woman in oils was which. "Never mind."

But it lodged in her, the way small things do — the splinter under the skin you only feel when the weather changes. *He looked at her like she owed him money.* No. That wasn't it. He had looked at Margot's portrait the way one looks at a person one has been introduced to and cannot place, and Cressida recognized it because she had done the same thing for twenty-six years: stood in front of that calm painted face trying to work out what the girl had known, and whom it had cost.

* * *

That evening Edmund reached the historical phase early. There had been a letter from the trust's solicitors — routine, Cressida was nearly certain, though she hadn't opened it yet — and a phone call he wouldn't discuss, and by half past eight he was at the window with a glass in each hand, giving one of them to the view.

"Holding the rail," he said.

"Yes, darling."

"Father said, we were all agreed, weren't we, we were all agreed it was the stones. Wet stones. She slipped on wet stones and I was holding the rail and that's what we told them." He turned. His face did the thing it did — the collapse from bluster to boy in the space of a breath, and beneath the boy, something older and colder that Cressida had married without ever being introduced to it. "Why is a man here asking about Ruth Ellery?"

"Because it's his job."

"It's nobody's job. She's dead. Everyone who needs to be dead is dead, that's how Father arranged it, that was the whole *point* of Father—" He stopped himself, appalled, the way a man stops a fall halfway and hangs there. "Cressy. I didn't say that."

"No," Cressida agreed. "You said nothing. Pour yourself another."

He went to the decanter, obedient, and she watched his back and felt the old war resume its positions inside her chest: love on the left flank, which was real, God help her, she loved this ridiculous crumbling man; and conscience on the right, which had been losing ground for two decades but had lately, since the arrival of a grey-suited stranger with a memory like flypaper, begun advancing again.

Because here was the item she had never entered in any ledger: she did not believe Edmund killed his sister. She had watched him lie for twenty-six years and he lied badly, floridly, with props. But she believed he *knew*. She believed he had stood somewhere holding a rail while something happened, and that his father had folded the knowledge away inside him like a letter into a drawer, and that the drawer had rusted shut, and that some part of Edmund had been drowning quietly ever since in a house where the drowned girl's rooms were dusted weekly and never opened.

And she believed — this was the newest entry, dated Tuesday — that Mr. Whitlock had come asking questions with the patience of a man who intended to keep asking until the house itself answered.

She could warn Edmund. That was the loyal thing. That was the wife-shaped thing. She could tell him the investigator was digging, and they would close ranks, the four of them plus Toby and Clemency, a nice warm wall of Ashcombes, and the wall would hold for another thirty years, and Clemency's children would grow up in a house with a sealed wing and a lake that never rippled, and Edmund would go on saying *holding the rail* to windows until the window finally answered him.

Or.

* * *

The safe was behind the atlas cabinet in the library, and the combination was Charles's birthday reversed, because Charles had trusted no one and understood everyone, and had known that the only people who might want into his safe were people who'd lived with him long enough to guess his birthday. Cressida had guessed it in her first year of marriage, out of boredom, and had never used it, and had felt vaguely guilty about knowing it, the way one feels guilty about knowing where the spare house key is buried even if one never digs it up.

The keys were in a leather folder labeled, in Charles's small precise hand, *E.W. — DO NOT ISSUE.* Which was so very Charles: not *Margot's rooms*, nothing human, just initials and an instruction, as though the whole tragedy were a stationery requisition.

E.W. Ellery? No — the maid was R.E., Ruth Ellery. E.W. Edmund Whitmore — no, Edmund was Edmund Charles. She turned the folder over. On the back, smaller: *East Wing.*

The largest key was iron, old, cold. The others were modern brass, for the padlocks Charles had added outside the ordinary locks, because a locked door apparently hadn't satisfied him; he'd wanted the rooms *denied*, double-denied, the way you deny a debt.

Cressida stood in the library for a long moment with the folder in her hands, listening to the house. Ashcombe House at night made sounds like an old dog dreaming — settling timbers, a rattle of sash, somewhere upstairs the tick of the hall clock carrying through two floors. Twenty-six years of those sounds. Her home. The only unbroken home she had ever had, and she had built it on ground she had never once allowed herself to survey.

*Complicity,* said the left flank. *You stayed. You balanced the books. You poured the drinks. You are not a witness, you're a load-bearing wall.*

*Yes,* said the right flank. *And walls can be opened. Doors are walls that changed their minds.*

She was fifty-four years old and tired of being architecture.

* * *

Mr. Whitlock was staying at the Anchor in the village, which surprised her; she'd have expected the hotel in town. It suggested a man keeping himself deliberately small, deliberately local, a man who wanted to be within walking distance of things. She wrote the note at the morning-room table the next morning, in the plain hand she used for tradesmen, and rewrote it twice because the first version sounded like a warning and the second like a confession, and what she needed was something that sounded like neither — something that sounded like a woman leaving a door open and walking away whistling.

In the end:

*Mr. Whitlock —*

*You asked yesterday whether we speak of Margot. We don't. But the house does, in its way, if you know where to listen. The east wing has been closed since 1994. The keys are supposed to be lost. They aren't.*

*Second door on the left past the linen room, third floor. The brass padlock takes the large key. Go in daylight, and mind the third stair, it complains.*

*I am not giving you this. I am merely a careless person who has misplaced a folder.*

*C.E.*

She read it through. It was, she reflected, a mad thing to send. If Edmund found out — if Miles found out — Miles, whose smoothness had always slightly frightened her, the way polished floors frighten you when you're carrying something breakable —

Well. Then she would be a careless person who had misplaced rather more than a folder.

She drove to the village herself, which drew less attention than sending anything by post from the house, and posted the envelope in the box outside the shop, next to a poster for the summer fete and a lost-cat notice that had been there so long the cat was presumably either dead or thriving elsewhere under a better name. She sympathized with the cat. She understood the appeal of a new name in a new parish.

Then she bought a paper she didn't need, exchanged remarks about the weather with Mrs. Prowse, who reported that the insurance gentleman had asked at the pub about the lake's depth, and drove home along the water road with the windows down, feeling lighter and more terrified than she had in years.

Edmund met her in the hall, sober-ish, anxious. "Anything from the solicitors?"

"The letter? I haven't opened it." This was even true; she'd left it in the car. "Darling, why does it matter? It'll be the usual. Quarterly figures, a paragraph of boilerplate, an invoice for their boilerplate."

"They rang yesterday. Asking odd questions." He rubbed his face. "Whether anyone had been in touch with former staff. Whether we kept personal papers. Whether—" He laughed, without humor. "Whether the east wing was still sealed. Can you imagine? Who asks that?"

"An investigator," Cressida said mildly, hanging up her coat. "It's their whole personality."

"Cressy." He caught her arm, and his eyes were the boy's eyes, the drowning man's eyes, and for one naked moment she saw how much of his life had been spent waiting for this knock on the door. "If someone opens those rooms—"

"Then someone will find thirty years of dust and a bad window," she said, and kissed his cheek, and went to make tea, and did not let her hand shake until she was alone in the kitchen with the kettle.

* * *

At four o'clock, from the morning room window, she watched Theo Whitlock come up the drive on foot — he walked everywhere, she'd noticed; a man conserving something, or gathering it — pause at the front steps, and then, instead of ringing, walk slowly around the east side of the house, looking up. Looking at the windows. Counting floors, probably. Finding the linen room by the light well, the way she'd told him.

He disappeared around the corner toward the side entrance, and Cressida picked up her pen, and opened the solicitors' letter, and read it twice.

It was the usual quarterly summary. But pinned to it, in a newer hand than the firm's, was a single typed line on a separate slip:

*Confirming receipt of your recent enquiry regarding disbursements. Please be advised all matters concerning the trust remain settled and are not open to review.*

She had made no such enquiry.

Which meant someone else had. Someone using her name, or the estate's, or perhaps simply testing the locks — the way Charles used to test locks, walking the house at night, checking that everything was denied.

Cressida sat very still, the slip of paper in her fingers, and thought about her son Miles, who had come home last month unexpectedly, cheerful, helpful, full of questions about the accounts — and who had, she now realized, been alone in the library for the better part of an afternoon.

Outside, faintly, from the upper floor of the east wing, came the sound of a door opening for the first time in thirty years.

She did not go up. She sat with her tea going cold and her heart going hard, and she thought: *whatever my son has been doing with this family's ghosts, whatever he thinks he's protecting — it's already too late to keep it in the dark.*

Someone else was reading Margot's diaries now. And Cressida found, to her astonishment, that she was praying he'd understand them faster than Miles could.
