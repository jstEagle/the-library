## 9. Dead Woman Walking

The photograph had been sitting in Margot's desk drawer for thirty years, and it took me the better part of a night to understand that it was not a photograph of Ruth Ellery at all.

It was a photograph of a view.

Oh, Ruth was in it — alive and smiling after her supposed death, standing on a seafront in a cardigan the colour of weak tea, squinting into a sun that had no business shining on a dead woman. But Margot, or whoever had kept the photo in that drawer all these years, hadn't framed it around Ruth. The camera's eye had drifted past her to what stood behind her: a promenade railing with a distinctive scrollwork pattern, a bandstand with a cupola painted in two tones, and behind it all, rising out of the mist like a patient old man, a church tower with a clock face that had been stuck at ten past four for as long as anyone could remember.

I knew this because I had seen that clock face before, in a newspaper I'd read eleven years ago and never forgotten, because I have never forgotten anything, which is less a gift than most people imagine. A piece about coastal towns losing their post offices. The caption had mentioned the church of St. Aldhelm's, Wexcombe-on-Sea, "where the tower clock has been frozen since the storm of '89."

Two counties away. A four-hour drive if the A-roads behaved, which they never do.

I sat in my flat until two in the morning with the photograph propped against the teapot, memorising it properly this time — not just the clock but the angle of the light, the direction of Ruth's shadow, the fact that she stood where the promenade met the slipway by the lifeboat station. Shadows fall toward the photographer in morning light from the east. Morning. She'd been photographed in the morning, on a day fine enough for tourists, which meant summer or early autumn. The cardigan said September. Everything says something, if you let it talk long enough.

Then I did something I'm not proud of, which is that I slept beautifully, like a man who has solved a crossword rather than a man who has located the only living witness to a drowning, in a county where the last investigator who came asking about her had, according to Edmund Ashcombe's cheerful account over sherry, "gone for a swim one night and never come back." Edmund had said it the way you'd mention a neighbour's new greenhouse. *Terrible thing. The sea doesn't forgive carelessness.*

* * *

Wexcombe-on-Sea announced itself an hour before I arrived, in the form of a road sign whose second line had been defaced by someone with strong feelings about the local council. The town itself was the kind of place that tourism had visited once, in 1972, and never called again: a crescent of guest houses with names like *The Anchorage* and *Sea Breeze*, half of them converted to flats, the other half holding on through sheer stubbornness and a loyalty scheme at the garden centre.

The church of St. Aldhelm's stood exactly where memory said it would, its clock still faithfully reporting ten past four to a town that had stopped listening decades ago. From the churchyard gate I could see the promenade, the railings, the slipway. The geography of the photograph assembled itself around me like a sentence completing.

Now, here is where I should confess to a certain professional clumsiness. I am very good at finding people and remarkably bad at knowing what to do when I find them. My plan, such as it was, consisted of: locate hospice, observe, approach with delicacy. What I actually did was walk into the town's only remaining café, order a full English breakfast I didn't want, and ask the waitress — a woman of about sixty with the unguarded candour of someone who has lived in the same town her whole life — whether she knew of any care homes nearby.

"Hospice, you mean? St. Brannoc's, up on the hill. Lovely place. My sister had her hip done there before it went over to palliative." She refilled my tea without being asked. "Visiting hours are two till four. Who are you visiting?"

"A friend of the family," I said, which was true in the sense that everything is connected if you pull hard enough.

"Anyone I'd know?"

"Ruth Ellery," I said, and watched her face do something complicated.

The waitress set down the teapot with exaggerated care, the way you handle a thing you've decided not to drop. "Don't know the name," she said, in the tone of a woman who knew the name extremely well. "Sister Agnes Ward, though, up at St. Brannoc's — she's been there years. Poor soul, cancer. Her sister died young, car crash, terrible family luck." She wiped a table that didn't need wiping. "You'll be wanting the number twelve bus. Or a taxi, if your family's worth it."

I left too much money for the breakfast. She looked at the extra coins, then at me, and said quietly, without looking up: "Whatever you're selling, love, nobody up there wants it. Last man came asking questions about Sister Ward drowned off the point. Inquest said cramp." A pause. "He was a strong swimmer, mind. Lifeguard in his youth, they said."

So Edmund hadn't been exaggerating. There had been another investigator, and the sea had indeed forgiven him nothing.

I thanked her. She said, "Mind how you go," in a voice that managed to be both a pleasantry and a warning, and possibly a prayer.

* * *

St. Brannoc's Hospice sat on the headland above the town like a benevolent lighthouse that had given up on ships and taken up gardening instead. It was the sort of building that had begun life as a Victorian convalescent home — high ceilings, tall windows, a veranda facing the sea so that the dying could look at something older than themselves. The gardens were immaculate. Someone clearly loved this place.

At reception, a young man with kind eyes and a lanyard asked how he could help.

"I'm here to see Sister Agnes Ward," I said. "Family friend."

He checked a list. "Are you expected?"

"No," I admitted. "It's rather a surprise visit."

"Ah." He said this the way doctors say *hmm*. "I'm afraid Mrs. Ward — we don't use the honorific, it confuses the sisters, the actual religious ones — Mrs. Ward has asked not to receive visitors she isn't expecting. It's in her notes. Quite firmly." He smiled apologetically. "She's very clear about it. There's a whole paragraph."

"May I leave a message?"

"You can leave a note and I'll pass it on, but I'll be honest with you" — and here he lowered his voice, leaning across the desk with the conspiratorial warmth of a man who spent his days among the dying and had decided honesty was the only decent currency — "she won't read it. We get one or two a year. People who knew her Before. She burns them. Well — asks us to burn them. She can't manage the matches anymore." He said this without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "There's a reason she came here, you know. To a town where nobody knows her. Some people are running *toward* something, but most are running away, and she struck me as the second sort."

"And if what she's running from," I said carefully, "is something that happened to other people — something unjust —"

"Then she's made her peace with it, hasn't she, or she'd have spoken by now." He straightened up, kindness reasserting itself over candour. "I shouldn't have said even that much. Visiting hours are two till four. The garden's open to anyone. If you happen to be in the garden at the same time as a resident, well." He shrugged, magnificently innocent. "We can't police the hydrangeas."

I liked him enormously. I tipped the charity box accordingly.

* * *

The garden at St. Brannoc's at half past two in October was populated by three residents wrapped in blankets, a nurse pushing a medication trolley, and a woman in a wheelchair positioned at the far end of the terrace with the deliberate solitude of someone who had arranged her exile herself.

I knew her instantly, though the photograph had been taken forty years ago and illness had hollowed her since. Memory is like that; it doesn't age its subjects. The woman in the wheelchair was smaller than the girl on the promenade, folded into herself like a letter, with a blanket over her knees and hands that lay in her lap with the stillness of things that had learned not to move. But the profile was the same. The set of the jaw was the same. And when the wind lifted the grey hair back from her face, I saw the same squint against the same sun, forty years on, still looking at something just out of frame.

I did not approach directly. I am clumsy, but I am not suicidal, and besides, I wanted a moment first. So I sat on a bench some fifteen feet away, within the etiquette distance for strangers sharing a view, and I looked at the sea, and I let the silence do what silence does.

For twenty minutes, nothing. A robin investigated the hydrangeas. The trolley nurse came and went. Somewhere below, the town's one pub opened its doors with a clatter audible even up here.

Then, without turning her head, the woman said: "You're the one from the café."

My heart performed a small athletic manoeuvre. "You were in the café?"

"I'm dying, not blind. Ada telephones everyone about everything. She rang the hospice before you'd finished your eggs." Her voice was dry as paper, unhurried, faintly amused in the way of people who have run out of time to be anything else. "Said a young man was asking after Ruth Ellery. Said he had a look about him." Now she turned, and her eyes — pale grey, sharp as gulls — went over me like a customs officer. "Insurance, are you?"

"How did you —"

"You've got the look. Careful hands, careful coat, and you sit like you're taking notes even when there's nothing to write on." She turned back to the sea. "They sent one before you. Michael Prentice. Nice boy. Asked his questions polite as you please. They found him on the rocks under the point with his watch stopped at twenty past eleven." A pause. "Cramp, the inquest said. In October. In a flat sea. The coroner was Charles Ashcombe's oldest friend, but I expect that's coincidence."

"I'm not here to make you testify," I said. "Not yet. Not today."

"Then why are you here?"

Because of a photograph in a dead girl's drawer. Because of diaries I had memorised down to the ink blots. Because somewhere there might be a child — Margot's child — growing up thinking no one had ever wanted her. Because I had spent my whole life outside windows looking in, and I could not bear the thought of another person locked out of their own story.

What I said was: "Because you wrote letters. For thirty years. Confessions to a crime you witnessed, mailed to strangers, hoping one of them would be careful enough to use them. That's not a woman at peace, Mrs. Ward. That's a woman keeping a vigil." I leaned forward. "I found the discrepancy. The witness in the confession is listed as dead. Nobody else caught it. Maybe nobody else ever will. I'm asking you not to burn my note. I'm asking you to let me sit here, sometimes, until you decide."

The wind came off the water and moved the hydrangeas. Somewhere inside the hospice, a bell rang for something — medications, perhaps, or prayers, or simply the hour.

Ruth Ellery — Sister Agnes Ward — dead woman walking — studied me for a long moment, and I watched her weigh me the way you'd weigh a strange dog: not for danger, exactly, but for what it might cost to feed it.

"The last one," she said finally, "also started polite. Also said *not yet, not today*." Her hands tightened on the blanket, just slightly, the only tremor in her. "Three weeks later he was dead, and I got a bunch of flowers at the nurses' station with no card. Lilies." Her voice flattened. "Charles Ashcombe always sent lilies. He's dead now himself, God rot him, but somebody still knows I'm here, Mr. Whitlock. Somebody has always known. Why do you think I burned every letter and stayed put in a town with one bus?" She looked at me then, fully, and there was terror in her face, yes, but underneath it — worn smooth as sea glass by forty years of polishing — something else.

Hope. Terrible, stubborn, unfed hope, the kind that survives on almost nothing.

"If you're the one they sent," she said quietly, "then I'm a fool, and I've been a fool before. But if you're the one I've been writing to for thirty years—" She stopped. Swallowed. Her eyes went to the sea, to the point, to the rocks where a nice boy named Michael had stopped swimming.

"Come back Thursday," she said. "Two o'clock. The garden. If I don't change my mind between now and then, I'll tell you what I saw on the boathouse steps." A ghost of the smile from the photograph crossed her face and vanished. "And Mr. Whitlock — don't take the coast road home. Take the inland route. Trust me on that."

I walked back down through the immaculate gardens with my heart going like a drum, and it was only at the gate that I remembered the detail that turned my blood cold:

I had never told her my name.

* * *

I drove the inland route. Of course I drove the inland route; I am many kinds of fool but not that one. The lanes wound through wet October fields, and I rehearsed Thursday in my head the way I rehearse everything, cataloguing questions, sequencing them, discarding the ones that sounded like interrogation.

It was somewhere past the third crossroads that the thought arrived, the way the important thoughts always do — sideways, unwelcome, complete.

Ada had telephoned the hospice. Ada had told a dying woman that a stranger was asking after Ruth Ellery by name. Which meant that whoever else listened to Ada's telephone calls — and in a town that size, everyone listened to everyone — now knew that someone had come asking. And Ruth had known my name without introduction, which meant either the hospice staff had mentioned me, or—

Or she had already been told to expect me.

I pulled into a farm gateway and sat with the engine ticking, doing what I do best and hate most: remembering. The paragraph in her notes at reception — *no visitors she isn't expecting.* Not *no visitors.* Visitors she wasn't *expecting.* As though expectation were the operative condition. As though someone had arranged, in advance, precisely which visitors she should receive.

The last investigator who came asking drowned. But he had gotten as far as the questions, hadn't he? He'd been allowed to ask them. Allowed to get close. Allowed to believe, perhaps, that he was being trusted — right up until twenty past eleven on a flat October sea.

Hope arriving wrapped in fear, I had thought, walking out of that garden. But standing in that gateway with the rain starting to stipple the windscreen, I understood I had it backwards.

It was fear, wrapped neatly in hope. And I had just been invited inside it.

Thursday, then. Two o'clock. The garden.

If either of us lived that long.
