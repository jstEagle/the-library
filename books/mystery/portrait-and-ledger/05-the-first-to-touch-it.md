## 5. The First to Touch It

Ilsa Brandt had been a police detective long enough to know that the first forty-eight hours of any death were a negotiation between what happened and what people wanted to have happened. In Halloway Falls, that negotiation usually ended quickly, because nobody here wanted anything complicated on the record. The town had spent a hundred years curating itself like a display case. Dead men in shallow creeks did not fit the glass.

She got the call at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, from dispatch, in the flat voice dispatchers used for bodies.

"Male, sixties, found in Miller's Run behind the old tannery property. Caller's a jogger. Fire and EMS are on scene."

"Identity?"

"ID says Wendell Marsh."

Brandt sat with that for a second. Wendell Marsh. She'd met him eleven days ago, across her desk, while he fidgeted with a ball cap he never took off. He was the one who'd handled the paperwork for the exhumation — not the permit itself, that went through the county, but the site survey, the grave location verification, the old plat maps. Retired gravedigger, forty years in the Halloway Falls cemetery, knew where every body was supposed to be because for four decades he was the man who put them there.

He'd come to her office voluntarily, which already made him unusual. Most people came to her office under escort or under subpoena.

"I want it noted," he'd said, "that I signed off on the location. Plot 114, east row. That's where the founder was buried. I dug the marker footing myself back in '98 when they replaced the headstone." He'd turned the cap in his hands. "So if there's questions about how the coffin got moved or emptied, those questions aren't about me."

"Nobody said they were, Mr. Marsh."

"People say things," he'd said. "Towns say things." And then he'd left, and she'd written in her notes: *nervous. knows more than he offered. re-interview.*

She hadn't gotten around to the re-interview. Now she wouldn't need to.

* * *

Miller's Run wasn't much of a creek — knee-deep in spring, ankle-deep by October, running brown and slow through a corridor of dead nettles behind the derelict tannery. Wendell Marsh lay on his side in perhaps eight inches of water, one arm folded under him, the other flung out across the stones as if reaching for the bank. His cap had floated free and lodged against a fallen branch two yards downstream.

The fire captain met her at the tape. "Jogger found him at five-fifty. No pulse. Water's maybe eight inches deep where he is."

"Any signs of struggle?"

"You tell me."

Brandt crouched at the edge of the creek and looked. She was not the medical examiner — Dr. Ferro would do the formal work — but she'd stood over enough bodies to read the first draft of a scene. Marsh's knuckles were abraded, both hands. There was a smear of mud along his jaw that didn't match the clean silt of the creek bed. His boots were muddy above the waterline, which meant he'd walked through something wetter before he got here, or he'd been walking after dark when the ground was damp, or —

Or someone had walked him here.

"Coroner's ETA?" she asked.

"Forty minutes. You want us to keep the jogger on scene?"

"Yeah. Get his statement before he talks to anyone else. Anyone else meaning anyone."

The fire captain gave her a look that said he understood exactly who *anyone* meant in a town of nine thousand people, and went off to do it.

Brandt worked the bank slowly. Ten feet upstream there was a flattened patch of nettle stalks, broken at consistent heights, wide enough for two sets of feet or one set dragged. On a stone near the waterline: a scuff mark, fresh, the gray rock exposed beneath like a wound. And half-buried in the mud at the very edge, something small and metallic that she photographed in place before bagging.

A button. Brass, military-style, but not military — the face stamped with a tiny V inside a wreath. She recognized the design without wanting to. It was the Vasse commemorative button, struck for the centennial, handed out at the historical society's kickoff event three weeks ago. Half the town had them pinned to coats or rattling in junk drawers.

Which meant nothing. Which meant everything. Evidence was like that — a coin with two faces, and you didn't get to know which side was up until the whole stack was counted.

She stood, knees cracking, and looked down at Wendell Marsh, and thought: *You came to my office because you were scared. I wrote it down and I let you walk out. That's on me.*

* * *

The ruling came back in four days. Accidental drowning. Acute alcohol intoxication as a contributing factor. Blood alcohol of .19, which Dr. Ferro delivered over the phone in the tone of a man reading a verdict he didn't entirely believe.

"Wendell Marsh drank," Ferro said. "Everybody knew that. His liver looked like a road map of a bad decision. He could have had a few, taken the shortcut home along the run, slipped on the bank."

"Could have," Brandt said.

"That's what I said."

"What I'm asking is whether you believe it."

There was a pause on the line. Ferro was careful, methodical, and — this was the thing she liked least about him — a man who valued his standing in Halloway Falls. "The autopsy doesn't show trauma inconsistent with a fall. No defensive wounds. No ligature marks. Abrasions on the knuckles are consistent with catching himself. If you've got evidence of foul play, Detective, bring it to me and I'll amend. Until then, my finding stands."

She hung up and stared at the file. The problem with small towns was not that they hid things. The problem was that they hid things so well they forgot where they'd put them, including the truth.

Accidental. A drunk old gravedigger fell in a creek. Fine. Except:

Except the blood alcohol could have been consumed anywhere, including poured down his throat somewhere other than a bar. Except the flattened nettles suggested company. Except the brass button, which Ferro hadn't even known about because she hadn't told him yet, because she wanted to see whose coat was missing one first.

And except the detail that kept snagging in her mind like a burr in wool: Wendell Marsh had been sober at her desk eleven days ago. She'd smelled exactly zero alcohol on him, and she always smelled it; her father had been a drinker and her nose had been trained by self-defense. A man quits drinking for eleven days and then turns up at .19? Possible. People relapse. But people also get plied.

She pulled the case notes from the exhumation and reread her own handwriting. *Marsh re-interview — pending.* Underlined twice. She'd been waiting for the anniversary circus to die down. The town had been a wall of noise for weeks — TV trucks, heritage committees, the mayor's office calling daily about "narrative management," a word the mayor used without irony.

*Narrative management.* She underlined it again now, in different ink, and felt the word change temperature.

* * *

She went to see the widow on Thursday. Doreen Marsh lived in a narrow house on Cordwainer Street with a garden gone wild since whatever day Wendell had stopped tending it. She answered the door with red eyes and a dish towel over her shoulder like armor.

"Detective. You're the one he talked to. Before."

"He mentioned me?"

"He mentioned everybody to me. That was the deal. Forty years of digging holes for this town, and at the end he tells me everything, because he says towns only remember what gets written down." Doreen's mouth twisted. "Coffee?"

"Please."

The kitchen smelled of burnt toast and grief. Doreen poured two cups without asking how Brandt took hers, which was correct, because Brandt took it however it arrived.

"He stopped drinking eleven days before he died," Doreen said, sitting down across from her. "Cold. I want that in your report, because the coroner's man called me yesterday and used the word *intoxicated* like it explained everything, and it doesn't explain a thing. Wendell quit because he wanted a clear head. He said he had thinking to do."

"What kind of thinking?"

"The kind he wouldn't share with his wife of thirty-eight years, apparently." She laughed, and it broke in the middle. "He got a phone call. About a week before — no. Nine days. Nine days before he died, he got a phone call, and after it he was different. Quiet. Then he quit drinking overnight, which for Wendell was like quitting breathing, and then he started going out in the evenings. Told me he was walking. Walks don't take three hours."

"Did you hear the call? See who it was from?"

"It was evening, I was at choir. But he wrote things down. Old habit — gravediggers keep records, he said, because the ground forgets but paper doesn't." Doreen got up, opened the drawer under the wall phone, and brought out a small spiral notebook, its cover soft with handling. "I'm not giving you this because I think somebody killed him. I'm giving it to you because I think somebody killed him, and I don't know how to say both of those things at once."

Brandt took the notebook with both hands, the way you take something that might be load-bearing.

Most of it was mundane — plot numbers, weather, a grocery list. But the last three pages were different. Tighter handwriting, pressed hard into the paper, the letters of a man writing fast and sober.

*Called by person unknown. Knew things about the Vasse job. Asked what I saw in '24. Told them nothing. They said everyone tells eventually.*

Then: *Went to the hotel. Talked to the Pemberton boy at the desk. Sharp kid. Grandfather was Tomas P., bellhop back then. Kid has the diary, I'd bet money. Everyone keeps something.*

Then, the last entry, dated the night before he died:

*Meeting tonight. They want to know if I'll sign a statement saying the coffin was intact when I surveyed it in August. It wasn't. Ground disturbance. Someone had been down there before the county ever opened it. I won't sign lies for anybody, not even scared ones. If anything happens to me, look at who benefits from quiet.*

Brandt read it three times. The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere outside a dog barked at nothing, the way dogs in Halloway Falls had been barking at nothing for a century.

"Doreen," she said carefully. "When Wendell talked to Rafael Pemberton at the hotel — did he say when?"

"About ten days ago. He liked the boy. Said concierges and gravediggers were in the same business — keeping track of who came and who went."

* * *

She found Rafael Pemberton at the front desk at four in the afternoon, folding maps with the crisp economy of someone who could fold maps in their sleep. Up close, the concierge had the watchful stillness of a person who noticed everything and admitted to none of it. Brandt had interviewed enough witnesses to recognize professional composure when it was wearing a name tag.

"Detective Brandt." She laid her card on the marble. "Do you have a minute?"

"For you? Several." A polite smile that didn't reach anywhere important. "Is this about Mr. Marsh? Terrible news. He was in here not long ago."

"So I understand. What did you talk about?"

"The exhumation, mostly. He wanted to confirm some dates — when guests had checked in around the ceremony, that sort of thing. Hotel memory is part of the service." The maps got squared into a perfect stack. "Why? Was there something irregular about his death?"

"The coroner ruled accidental drowning."

Something flickered across Rafael's face — quick, controlled, but Brandt caught it, and it wasn't surprise. It was confirmation. As if Rafael had already done the arithmetic and was waiting for the county to catch up.

"But?" Rafael prompted.

"But I don't rule anything accidental until I've ruled out everything else, and right now everything else includes a phone call, a meeting, and a man who suddenly quit a forty-year drinking habit because he had, quote, thinking to do." She watched Rafael's hands. Steady. Too steady. "Did he ask you anything odd?"

Rafael paused a beat too long. "He asked about my grandfather."

"Tomas Pemberton. The bellhop."

"You've done your homework."

"It's my whole job. What did he want to know?"

"Whether Grandfather had left any papers. Diaries, letters. He seemed to think the family might have kept things from 1924." Rafael's eyes came up, level and unreadable. "I told him I'd have to look. I haven't looked yet. It's been a busy season."

It was a lie. Brandt was almost certain it was a lie, and she was equally certain she couldn't prove it, and she filed the certainty away in the drawer where she kept all the things Halloway Falls expected her not to notice. Everybody in this town was holding a piece of the story and deciding, daily, whether to hand it over.

"Mr. Pemberton. When you do look — and I think we both know you will — anything you find that touches on Edmund Vasse, the burial, or the finances of this town, I want to see it. Not because I suspect you. Because two people are now dead-adjacent to this file — one drowned, one missing from a coffin — and I'd rather the next person who touches it does it with me standing next to them."

"Dead-adjacent," Rafael repeated. "That's an interesting way to put it."

"I'm an interesting woman. One more question." She slid the evidence photo across the desk: the brass button, magnified, the wreathed V sharp against the mud. "Recognize this?"

Rafael studied it longer than recognition required. "Centennial buttons. They were everywhere at the kickoff event. Three hundred of them, I'd guess. The mayor handed them out like communion."

"Mm. And your grandfather's diary — hypothetically — would it mention buttons?"

The pause stretched just past comfortable. "Hypothetically," Rafael said, "you'd have to ask someone who's read it."

Brandt collected her photo, nodded, and walked out into the gold October light feeling the shape of the case shift beneath her like ground settling over a fresh grave. Wendell Marsh had known the coffin site had been disturbed *before* the exhumation. Someone had asked him to lie about it. He'd refused, and nine days later he was in eight inches of water with a stranger's button in the mud and .19 of alcohol in his blood that his wife swore couldn't be his own doing.

Someone in Halloway Falls was managing the narrative. And they had started pruning the cast.

Her phone buzzed as she reached the cruiser. Dispatch.

"Detective, got a call from St. Alder's Hospital. Edith Crane — the historian? Fell down the archive stairwell last night. She's alive, but she's not talking to anybody."

Brandt closed her eyes for one second, the length of a curse.

"Not talking," she said, "or unable to?"

"That's the thing," dispatch said. "The nurse says she's able. She's just — scared. Won't say of what. Or who."

Brandt opened the cruiser door and got in, and did not start the engine right away. Two incidents in two weeks, both involving people who had touched the Vasse file. A pattern was a thing you needed three points to draw, and she had two and a half, and the half was a concierge at the front desk of the Vasse Grand holding a dead man's curiosity and a family secret behind a locked drawer.

She started the engine. Three points, then. She'd go collect the third.
