## 11. The Trimmed Page

The archive opened at nine. Rafael was on its steps at eight-forty, holding a coffee he had no intention of drinking and a manila folder he had every intention of using.

He'd told himself, walking over through the cold gold light of a November morning, that this was a courtesy call. That a concierge who'd spent twenty years anticipating the needs of strangers could reasonably anticipate the needs of one more — that Marisol Quist would want to know, as a matter of professional respect, that he intended to spend the day with the Vasse ledgers. He'd even rehearsed a version of it in which the words *I'm sorry* appeared.

But the truth was simpler and uglier: he wasn't going to ask permission anymore. He was going to look at what she didn't want him to see, and he was going to do it under her own roof, in her own light, surrounded by her own careful order. If she caught him, she caught him. There were worse things than being caught by someone you suspected. Being wrong about them, for instance. Being right and having done nothing.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was. Marisol Quist did not believe in locked doors; she believed in catalogued ones.

"Rafael." She looked up from a reading table stacked with acid-free boxes, her glasses pushed into her gray-streaked hair, and smiled the smile he now understood was load-bearing. "You're early. The centennial exhibit won't assemble itself, but I suppose you're not here for that."

"The ledgers," Rafael said. "1923 through '25. I want to sit with them."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "Those are fragile. Vellum-bound, foxed at the margins. I'd rather handle them myself and bring you what you need."

"What I need is to turn the pages myself." He kept his voice at concierge pitch: pleasant, immovable, the voice that said *the suite is not available but I will find you something better.* "You've been generous enough already. I don't want to take more of your time than I have."

A pause long enough to measure. Then she rose, smoothed her cardigan, and led him back into the stacks with the graciousness of a woman conducting a tour of her own home. She pulled the three volumes from their shelf cradle herself, laid them on a reading table beneath the good lamp, and set down weights, gloves, a page lifter shaped like a slim silver tongue.

"You'll forgive me if I hover," she said. "Some of these pages have been repaired. Repairs fail when amateurs are enthusiastic."

"I'm never enthusiastic," Rafael said. "It's the first thing they teach you at the desk."

She laughed at that — a real laugh, or an excellent copy — and went back to her table, where the sound of her working resumed like a metronome: the whisper of tissue paper, the soft click of a box lid, the pen scratch of her pencil in the margins of her own mind.

Rafael gloved up and opened the 1924 ledger.

* * *

He had seen the page before, twice, and both times it had been the page itself that bothered him — not the numbers, which were dull as gravel, but the physical fact of it. Every other leaf in the volume ended in a ragged deckle edge, the natural roughness of old paper. This one ended clean. Straight. As if the ledger had been bound by a different hand for a single page and then changed its mind.

Now, under the good lamp, with the neighboring pages fanned out beside it for comparison, the difference stopped being a feeling and became a measurement.

Rafael took out his phone and photographed everything: the trimmed page, the pages flanking it, the binding gutter, the ghost of offset ink where the removed leaf had pressed against its neighbor before it was cut away. He measured the margins against a ruler borrowed from Marisol's own supply drawer — she watched him do it and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer — and wrote the figures in his notebook in shorthand only he could read, an old hotel habit. You didn't leave legible notes about guests. Apparently you didn't leave legible notes about archivists either.

The math was simple. The trimmed page sat between folio 214 and folio 216. Folio 215 was gone. Not missing — *removed*, deliberately, with a blade, after binding, because the stub of it was still visible in the gutter if you knew to press the spine open gently and look. And pressed open gently and looked, Rafael found what remained: the bottom third of a line of figures, and beneath it, half a signature.

*—asse.*

Edmund Vasse had signed the page that no longer existed.

He sat very still for a moment, listening to Marisol's metronome across the room. Tissue paper. Box lid. Pencil.

Then he did the thing he'd come to do, the thing he'd been circling for two days the way you circle a room where something has died. He compared cuts.

The trimmed edge of folio 215's neighbor — because whoever cut the page had also shaved the facing margin to hide the stub's shadow — was visible under raking light if you tilted the lamp. Rafael tilted it. The cut gleamed. Not torn, not guillotined, not the chewed edge of scissors. A single draw of a very thin, very sharp blade, the kind of cut that leaves a polished line on paper the way a skater leaves a line on ice. He had seen that line before. He had seen it last week, in fact, on the repair edges of two documents Marisol had restored for the centennial exhibit, work she'd done herself, proudly, because — as she'd told him over tea, in her warm rehearsed way — *there are four people in this state qualified to use a conservator's blade on vellum, and only one of them works within forty miles of this town.*

Only one.

Rafael closed the ledger, carefully, the way she'd taught him without ever teaching him, and sat with his hands flat on the table and let the certainty arrive. It didn't crash in. It settled, like snow, like dust on a coffin lid. Marisol Quist had cut that page. In this room. With the blade in the drawer ten feet from where she sat. And whatever Edmund Vasse had signed away on folio 215, she had decided a century later that no one else should read it.

She looked up then, as if the weight of his attention had crossed the room and touched her shoulder. "Finding what you hoped for?"

"Finding what's here," Rafael said. "That's all any of us can do."

"Mm." She returned to her boxes. But she didn't put her glasses back down, and she didn't stop watching him in the reflection of the glass case beside her table, and Rafael understood that the courtesy portion of the morning was over. They were two professionals now, each pretending the other was a guest.

He stayed another hour. He turned every page of 1924 and most of 1925, photographing methodically, building nothing yet — just gathering. And it was near noon, as he was returning the final volume to its cradle, that he saw the folder.

It lay in the flat-files cart, one of a dozen Marisol had pulled for the exhibit, labeled in her neat hand: *VASSE ESTATE — RECEIPTS & DISBURSEMENTS, 1924–1931.* It was open an inch. Inside, on top, a single sheet in a plastic sleeve, already scanned, already captioned for display: *Receipt of payment, estate of E. Vasse to T. Pemberton, services rendered, October 1924.*

T. Pemberton.

Rafael's hands went cold inside the gloves.

* * *

He didn't touch it. That was the discipline, the whole discipline of the front desk distilled: you can look at anything, you can know anything, but your fingerprints stay off the china until you've decided what you're going to break. He leaned, he angled, he read.

The receipt was brief. Dated October the ninth, 1924 — six days after the burial. *Received of the Estate of Edmund Vasse the sum of Two Hundred Fifty Dollars ($250.00) in consideration of services rendered during the week of September 30th, same to be final settlement of all claims.* And below it, in a hand Rafael knew better than his own — a hand he'd traced in ballpoint on scrap paper as a child, practicing his name because it felt like holding his grandfather's fingers —

*Tomas Pemberton.*

Signed. Witnessed by a second signature he didn't recognize. Stamped by the probate court.

Two hundred and fifty dollars in 1924. Rafael did the arithmetic without wanting to. A bellhop earned perhaps fifteen dollars a week. His grandfather had taken home nearly four months' wages in a single envelope, six days after carrying a heavy guest to a grave at midnight, and had signed a paper promising that the services rendered were finished — *final settlement of all claims* — forever.

The diary hadn't mentioned it. The diary, which confessed sandbags and medals and a living man watching from a waiting car, had never once mentioned money. Tomas Pemberton had written down the crime and buried the payment, and Rafael understood, standing in the archive's hush with his grandfather's forged-into-legend name glowing on a display caption, that he had spent his whole life curating a man instead of reading one.

*Services rendered.* What services? Carrying the coffin? Or keeping silent? Both. Obviously both. The diary entry about the midnight burial was dated October second. The receipt was dated October ninth. A week apart. Long enough for fear to ripen into price.

And there — arriving uninvited, unwelcome, punctual as a wake-up call — came the thought he'd been outrunning since the exhumation: *if Tomas took the money, what else did he take? What else did he agree to? Who else did he tell, or promise not to tell? Was my grandfather a witness, or was he staff?*

The vertigo from the diary came back, but colder now, personal. He had built a shrine out of that leather book. He had kept it in a locked drawer the way Marisol kept her blade in a locked office, and for the same reason, maybe. Because some things you preserve aren't tributes. They're evidence.

"Remarkable, isn't it," Marisol said.

He hadn't heard her cross the room. She stood beside the cart, looking down at the receipt with an expression of mild scholarly pleasure, and Rafael realized with a lurch that she had left it open on purpose. That the inch of gap had been bait, or a message, or a test.

"Tomas Pemberton," she said. "Your grandfather. The only witness to the burial. The town owes that man a statue, honestly — he's the reason we have *any* record of the interment. And here he is, in his own hand." She tapped the sleeve lightly, reverently. "History is mostly receipts, you know. Everything else is decoration."

"Is that why you keep them?" Rafael asked. "The receipts?"

Her eyes came up to his. For a moment — a full, load-bearing moment — the warmth went out of her face entirely, and what remained was neither cruel nor kind. Curatorial. The look of someone assessing an accession: condition, provenance, value.

"I keep what's true," she said. "Someone has to. Towns are very bad at it." She slid the folder shut with two gloved fingers, squaring its edges to the cart. "You'll be at the dinner, of course. Guest historian. The mayor is thrilled." A small smile returned, reassembled, flawless. "Do bring the diary, if you decide to share it. Original sources are so much better than memories. Memories fade." She turned back toward her table. "Paper doesn't. Paper keeps. That's the whole tragedy of paper, really — it keeps *everything,* whether we want it to or not."

Rafael left at twelve-forty. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said, "Thank you, Marisol. For the access."

"Any time," she said pleasantly, not looking up. "You know where everything is now."

It was not, he reflected on the steps outside, in the cold bright air, a sentence a guilty person says.

It was a sentence a person says who wants you to know she isn't afraid of you finding things. Because she had already decided what you'd find, and what you'd do with it, and how long you'd be given to do it.

* * *

He walked back to the hotel the long way, past the green where the exhumation hole had been refilled and a temporary marker stood waiting for the centennial reinterment — *EDMUND VASSE, FOUNDER, 1849–1924, RESTS IN THE TOWN HE BUILT.* Except he didn't rest anywhere. He'd died in some other town, under some other name, probably, decades ago, and the town he built had spent a century singing to a box of sandbags and medals.

Rafael stood at the marker for a while. Then he took out his phone and called Detective Brandt.

She answered on the second ring, no greeting. "Pemberton."

"I need to show you something," he said. "Not at the station. Somewhere without windows."

A pause. He could hear her deciding. "The diner on Corliss Street has a booth in the back," she said finally. "Bad coffee. No cameras since the remodel. Four o'clock."

"There's a condition," Rafael said. "What I show you stays between us until I say otherwise. There's a name in it that isn't mine to give up yet."

Another pause, longer. "Everyone who talks to me has a condition," Brandt said. "Four o'clock. And Pemberton — if the name turns out to be someone I should already be looking at, our deal changes shape. Understand?"

"I understand," Rafael said, and hung up, and stood a moment longer in front of the marker, doing the last piece of arithmetic, the one that hurt.

Because here was the thing he couldn't file anywhere: the trimmed page and the signed receipt fit together too well. Folio 215 — the page Vasse signed, the page Marisol had cut away — sat in the autumn of 1924, the same season as the hush payment, the same season as the midnight burial. Whatever deal had emptied that coffin had run through those papers like a wire through a wall, and Marisol Quist had spent her career tracing wires. She knew where this led. Maybe she'd known for thirty years.

Which meant Rafael was no longer investigating whether she'd hidden the truth.

He was investigating what she'd done to the people who got close to it — and he had just spent the entire morning getting close to it, in her building, under her lamp, while she watched him in the reflection and let him.

He checked his watch. Three hours until the diner. Enough time to make copies of everything, seal the originals in the hotel safe behind the guest ledger, and write one more entry in his grandfather's diary — the first one in his own hand.

*Grandpa,* he wrote, sitting in the lobby with the leather book open on his knee while the afternoon shift flowed around him. *I think I know who's keeping your secret. And I think she's keeping it the way you kept hers.*
