## 16. The Third Name

The murder board in Ilsa Brandt's office had outgrown the corkboard two days ago and now colonized the wall beside it, index cards fanned across drywall with pins she'd stolen from the community theater's prop drawer. She stood in front of it at seven in the morning with a cup of station coffee going cold in her hand, and she was looking at dates.

Not names. Dates.

She'd been staring at them for twenty minutes, and the pattern had finally stopped being a coincidence and started being a schedule.

* * *

It began, as these things did, with a phone call she almost didn't take.

"Detective Brandt? This is Dolores Fenwick, county records office." A voice like tissue paper over a cough. "You requested the Vasse land purchase abstracts last week."

"I did."

"Well, I pulled the file, and — I don't know if this matters — but there's a request card stapled inside from 1994. Somebody asked for the same abstracts back then. Same file. And there's another one from '87." A pause, the sound of paper turning. "Three requests in forty years for one land file. That seemed like something you'd want to know."

Brandt sat down slowly. "Who made the earlier requests?"

"That's the thing. The '94 card is signed. R. Calloway. He was our records clerk here until—" The voice wobbled. "Until he passed. Ninety-four. It was very sad. Very sudden."

"How sudden?"

"He came in on a Tuesday and requested that file himself. Signed the card, initialed the log. Wednesday he didn't come in. Thursday they found him." Another pause. "In his car. Off Route 9. Exhaust. They called it a suicide."

"You sound like you have doubts."

Dolores Fenwick took her time answering, and Brandt could hear her deciding, decade by decade, whether to keep carrying this alone. "Rennie Calloway was the most cheerful man I ever worked with. He sang in the church choir. He'd just booked a cruise — he told everyone about it, he had the brochures taped up in his cubicle. You don't book a cruise and then kill yourself in a parking area off Route 9 four days later." Tissue paper tearing now. "But nobody asked me. Nobody asked anybody. His sister wanted it investigated and the sheriff at the time said the note was conclusive."

"There was a note?"

"A typed one. One line. *I'm sorry for what I found.*" A breath. "That always struck me strange. Rennie typed everything in lowercase. He said capitals were shouting. Even his Christmas cards."

Brandt wrote it down anyway, though her hand already knew what it was writing: *typed note, wrong register, no signature analysis on file.* "Ms. Fenwick, do you still have the request cards?"

"Forty years of them, detective. We're not allowed to throw anything away. Nobody ever asks us for anything, so nobody ever tells us to clean house."

"Can you read me every name that touched the Vasse financial files? Land purchases, bank correspondence, anything with Vasse money in it. Going back as far as the cards go."

There was a long silence on the line, the silence of a woman understanding, at last, why she'd made the call.

"Yes," Dolores Fenwick said. "I can do that."

* * *

She spent the afternoon building the timeline, and by evening the timeline had stopped being a list and become an argument.

**1987.** Request card, unsigned, for the Vasse land purchase abstracts. Two weeks later, Harlan Voss — retired county surveyor, seventy-one — died of a stroke at his kitchen table. Natural causes, no autopsy, no questions. Voss had been the last living person to survey the original cemetery plot boundaries. His name appeared nowhere else in any case file Brandt had. But he appeared on the request card's witness line, because in 1987 the records office still required a second signature for historical financial documents.

**1994.** Rennie Calloway. Requested the same file. Typed suicide note in a register he never used. Found four days later.

**2011.** No death. Just a card — unsigned again — requesting the Vasse estate correspondence. And in the margin of the logbook, in pencil, someone had written *not available — conservation.* Brandt checked the conservation logs at the town archive. The Vasse correspondence had indeed been sent out for conservation that year. Sent out by whom?

Marisol Quist. Newly appointed head archivist. Her first year in the position.

And in 2011, the person who would have been best positioned to notice anything odd about the estate correspondence — the deputy archivist, a meticulous man named Peter Okafor who kept duplicate finding aids in his home office — resigned suddenly and moved to Ohio, where he still lived, where Brandt had called him that morning and heard him say, carefully, politely, in the tone of a man reciting lines he'd rehearsed for fourteen years: *"I left for personal reasons, Detective. I don't really remember the details anymore. Some things are better left archived."*

Some things are better left archived. She'd written that down too, underlined twice.

Then this year. The exhumation permit touches three hands: the gravedigger, Emil Sartorius; the historian, Dr. Vivian Ashe; and the archivist whose office processed the paperwork. Sartorius drowned in six inches of water. Ashe fell down archive stairs and hadn't spoken above a whisper since, except once, to Brandt, when she'd gripped her wrist through the hospital bed rail and said *ledger* like it was the only word left in her mouth.

Four access events across thirty-seven years. Three bodies or near-bodies. One constant custodian.

Brandt stepped back from the wall and looked at the whole shape of it, and the shape looked back.

This wasn't a killer who panicked. Panicked killers left trails like spilled milk — wide, wet, obvious. This was pruning. Each cut made years apart, each one timed to an access event, each one dressed to look like accident or grief or coincidence. Thirty years of patience. Whoever this was hadn't been protecting a secret since the exhumation. She — Brandt was done pretending the probability didn't lean hard one direction — she had been tending it like a garden since before Rafael Pemberton was old enough to work a front desk.

And the worst part, the part that made Brandt set down her coffee and press both palms flat against the wall, was that the patience explained everything that had bothered her. Why the gravedigger's drowning had looked so perfectly accidental. Why Ashe's fall happened on a night with no cameras working in the archive stairwell — maintenance scheduled, Brandt now noticed, by a work order filed through municipal records. Why her own office had been searched so cleanly, nothing taken, everything photographed, a note quoting her case notes verbatim. That wasn't arrogance. That was a curator's confidence. Someone who believed, deep in the bone, that she managed the record, and the record was reality.

You couldn't arrest someone for being patient. But God help her, you could finally see the pattern, and patterns were the one thing patience couldn't protect.

* * *

Rafael Pemberton arrived at eight-fifteen, uninvited, carrying a bakery box and the particular expression of a concierge who has decided a police station is simply another lobby to be worked.

"Custard or apple," Rafael said, setting the box on Brandt's desk. "I bribed the dispatcher with the apple ones, so those are spoken for."

"You can't keep bringing baked goods into an active investigation."

"I can't keep *not* bringing them. You look like you've been sleeping in your chair." Rafael glanced at the wall, and Brandt watched the professional composure do its little dance — eyes moving card to card, cataloguing, the way they always did, the way hotel people did, reading a room the way other people read a page. Then the eyes stopped. On the 1987 card. On the 1994 card.

"That's not from this year," Rafael said quietly.

"No."

"Those are old deaths."

"Old deaths connected to the same file." Brandt pulled the photocopy of the request cards from her desk and laid them out. "Records clerk named Rennie Calloway, 1994. Asked to see the Vasse financial abstracts on a Tuesday. Dead by Friday. Typed suicide note that doesn't match how he wrote anything else in his life. And before him, a surveyor, 1987, dead two weeks after co-signing an access card for the same material."

Rafael stood very still. Brandt had learned to read that stillness over the past weeks — it wasn't emptiness, it was compression, a whole argument happening behind a closed door. When Rafael finally spoke, the voice was careful.

"The archivist's office processes all historical document requests. Access cards, conservation orders, finding aids. Everything routes through her."

"Everything routes through her," Brandt agreed. "Thirty-seven years of paper, and one pair of gloves touching all of it."

"And the clerk's note. *I'm sorry for what I found.*" Rafael's jaw tightened. "He found the embezzlement. In ninety-four. Twenty years before anyone else thought to look."

"Or he found something else. Something worse." Brandt turned back to the wall. "Here's what keeps me up. The embezzlement explains killing to protect the Vasse fortune. It doesn't explain thirty years. Aldous Vasse is fifty-eight. Marisol Quist has been in that archive since her twenties. Whatever she's protecting, she started protecting it long before the centennial gave her a deadline."

Rafael set down the bakery box with exaggerated care, the way people do when their hands need a job. "There's something I should probably—"

The desk phone rang.

Brandt picked it up, listened for ten seconds, and felt the floor of the investigation shift beneath her. She thanked the caller, hung up, and stood there a moment.

"What?" Rafael said.

"Dolores Fenwick. County records." Brandt picked up a pen. "After we talked this morning, she went back through her request cards one more time. Nervous, thorough woman. She found a fourth card she'd missed. From 1979. Before her time — it was in a box of pre-digitization material that got misfiled during a renovation."

"A fourth request for the Vasse files?"

"Not the files." Brandt wrote the year on the board, and her handwriting, usually a cop's scrawl, came out small and precise. "A request for the *original burial ledger*. The one from 1924. The one that lists who carried the coffin, who dug the grave, who witnessed the interment." She capped the pen. "The card is signed. The signature belongs to a woman named Celeste Marchand."

Rafael frowned. "Should I know that name?"

"She was the town accountant's daughter," Brandt said. "The accountant who was framed for Edmund Vasse's embezzlement in 1925 and died ruined. She requested the burial ledger in 1979, the year she turned nineteen." She let that sit. "Celeste Marchand married a man named Quist two years later."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere down the hall, a radio played weather.

"She wasn't protecting the Vasse secret," Rafael said at last, barely above a whisper. "She inherited it. As a child. She's been reading that ledger her whole life — she knows exactly what the town took from her family, and she's been—" Rafael stopped, restarted. "The pruning isn't defense. It's curation. She's editing the record. Removing every hand that ever touched the truth except hers."

"Which means," Brandt said slowly, "she doesn't think of herself as destroying evidence. She thinks of herself as the evidence. The last living entry in a hundred-year-old ledger." She looked at the wall — 1987, 1994, 2011, this year — four cuts in a row of decades, each one placed like a conservator places a blade. "And Rafael — the dinner is in five days. If the pattern holds, if she prunes on schedule—"

"She won't wait for the toast," Rafael finished. "She'll clear the room before it's raised."

Brandt reached for her coat. "Then we stop planning around her timeline and start forcing her onto ours. Tonight. You and me. Because there's one more thing Fenwick found on that 1979 card."

Rafael waited.

"A return-date stamp. The ledger was due back in thirty days." Brandt opened her office door. "It was returned in nine. Which means somewhere in this town, Marisol Quist has held that 1924 burial ledger in her hands — and whatever she learned from it, she learned it standing exactly where you're about to stand. In the archive. After hours."

Rafael paled, and Brandt understood why. Because Rafael had an appointment there. Tomorrow morning. With the archivist herself.

"You're not going alone," Brandt said.

"I was never going alone," Rafael said. "I just hadn't told you yet."
