## 5. A Letter in Ainsley's Hand

The archive at St. Aldhelm's kept its own hours, and they were mine. I arrived before the vergers lit the nave lamps, when the cathedral was still a held breath, and I worked until the radiators ticked themselves quiet. For three days after Wrenn's last visit, I had done nothing but my ordinary work—accession numbers, condition reports, the small honest labor of keeping other people's pasts legible—and I had told myself that this was discipline. It was not. It was hiding, and I had gotten so good at hiding that I could do it inside my own head.

But the dead man's hands would not leave me alone.

Ink-stained, like mine. The particular stain of iron gall, brown-black at the cuticles, the mark of someone who has spent years with a dip pen or a restoration brush. A stranger wearing my coat, drowned at the tideline, with an archivist's hands. Either he had been killed because of what he knew about records, or he had been killed by someone who assumed that anyone who touched the 1974 file would eventually have to be erased. Both possibilities led to the same place: the gap in the crypt register, the pages excised so cleanly that only a professional would know they had ever existed.

So on the fourth morning I stopped pretending and went looking for what the fire hadn't burned.

* * *

Canon Ainsley taught me cataloguing the way other men teach prayer: by repetition, by silence, by occasionally saying something devastating as if it were nothing at all. He died four years ago, and I still caught myself turning to ask him things. *Where does this folio go? What do we do with a record that shames us?*

He'd have answered the second question with a shrug and a proverb about dust. He was very good at proverbs about dust. I had thought, for thirty years, that this was wisdom. I was beginning to suspect it was technique.

The fire of 1974 had gutted the muniment room above the south transept. The official story—the one in the diocesan yearbook, the one repeated in every guidebook—was that the pre-1970 crypt registers had been destroyed. Water damage, then smoke, then the collapse of the shelving. A tidy sentence. Tidy sentences are how archives lie.

I pulled the fire inventory instead. Every disaster generates paperwork; grief is no exception. The assessor's list from November 1974 ran to forty-one pages, and item thirty-three read: *Crypt burial registers, vols. IV–IX, water-damaged, consigned for drying.* Consigned for drying—not destroyed. Drying is a process, and processes leave trails. Someone had signed for those volumes at the drying facility in March 1975. The signature was a scrawl, but the accession stamp beside it was unmistakable: the archive's own oval stamp, inked in the violet that Ainsley used his whole life because he said black ink was "too final."

Ainsley had signed for the damaged registers. And volumes IV through IX did not exist in my catalogue. Not as lost, not as destroyed, not even as a stub entry with a note of disposal. They had simply never been entered—which meant whoever processed them afterward had chosen not to enter them. That is not carelessness. That is authorship.

I sat with that for a long while, listening to the building settle around me. Then I began searching the collection for anything misfiled. If someone had removed six volumes from the record, they might also have hidden something else—something too dangerous to destroy and too shameful to keep visible. Mislabeling is the oldest concealment in my trade. You don't lock a thing away; you give it the wrong name and let fifty years of respectful indifference do the guarding.

I started with the music. Choir libraries are the attics of cathedrals: everything ends up there eventually, wrapped in whatever binding was nearest to hand.

* * *

It took me two days. On the second evening, in a folio labeled *Anthems, misc., 19th c., duplicates*, I found a folder of loose leaves that were not music at all. Pressed among faded settings of the Magnificat was a letter, folded twice, sealed but never posted. The wax had cracked along its seam. The hand on the outside was Ainsley's—smaller than I remembered it, cramped, written fast.

My own name was not on it. No name was. Only the date: 9 October 1996.

I broke the seal because there was no one left in the world with the right to keep it whole.

* * *

*I write this because I cannot say it aloud, and because I am old enough now to know that unsent letters are just confessions with better manners.*

*In August of 1974 I went down into the crypt at night. I will not pretend I don't remember why. There had been talk—a young man talking, mostly, the organ scholar, Daniel, who had found things in the accounts that embarrassed certain gentlemen of this parish, and who would not stop finding them. I went down because I suspected he used the crypt as a place to think, and I wanted to warn him off. I liked him. He played the voluntaries like a man arguing with God and winning.*

*I heard them before I saw them. Two voices, one of them Voss's—I knew his voice anywhere, we had served at the altar together for twenty years. Then a sound I will not describe, because describing it makes it real, and I have spent my life making things unreal. When I came down the stair they were gone and Daniel was at the foot of it, in the water that collects there, and he was not breathing, and Edgar Voss was standing over him holding the ledger Daniel had shown me, and Voss said: "He fell. You saw him fall."*

*I saw nothing. That is the truth. I saw nothing, and I said I had seen a fall, and the register for that month was damaged in the fire that autumn—damaged, yes, though I dried those pages myself and read every name on them before I put them where no one would look. I told myself it was mercy. Mercy to the boy's mother, who need never learn her son died in a scuffle over parish money. Mercy to the church. Mercy, mercy. I have learned that word is a door men hide behind when the house behind it is on fire.*

*If you are reading this, then you have found what I hid, which means you are either braver than I was or luckier, and I am sorry for both. The registers are in the ossuary chest—no. I will not write where. Some doors should only be opened once.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Dust keeps accounts longer than God does, I think. I only ever wanted to be forgiven by the boy, and he is beyond asking.*

*A.*

* * *

I read it three times. The third time my hands shook badly enough that I set it flat on the table and weighted the corners with lead cloth weights, the way I would treat any fragile document, because procedure is what remains when everything else gives out.

Voss. Edgar Voss, who taught me to pick a wax seal intact with a heated blade when I was nineteen. Who brought me shortbread on my birthday every year without fail. Who stood beside Ainsley at the funeral and wept openly, and I had thought: *there is a man who loved him.* I had thought: *there is a man like me.*

Daniel. The organ scholar. Somewhere under my feet, in the dark below the crypt floor, a young man who argued with God through an organ console had been lying in standing water for fifty years while the parish sang over him every Sunday. While I catalogued. While Ainsley taught me proverbs about dust.

And Ainsley—Ainsley, who had watched a murder and helped bury it, who had dried the evidence page by page with his own careful hands, the same hands that had corrected my letterforms, who had then hidden the proof inside a music folio and carried it for twenty-two years, unable to send it and unable to burn it. An archivist's sin, in the end. He couldn't destroy the record either. He just made sure no one would ever find it.

Except someone had. Someone had found it—or known of it—before me. The excised pages. The drowned stranger with the archivist's hands. The killer was not hunting the truth; the killer was hunting the witnesses to a cover-up, and the cover-up had more authors than Ainsley. *Two voices,* the letter said. One was Voss's. The other—

I looked down at the letter, and then at the folio, and felt the cold arrive all at once, the way tide comes into a channel.

Because the folio had been rewrapped. I could see it now that I was looking: the string tied in a bow rather than the archive knot I taught volunteers, the tissue replaced with a newer stock. Someone had opened this folder recently. Recently enough that the paper was still settling.

Someone had read Ainsley's confession, and put it back, and left it for the next person to find—or left it as bait.

I checked the access log. Three entries in the past week. Mine, mine, and one I did not recognize: a reader's ticket number, valid, issued eleven days ago.

To a name from the 1974 parish register.
