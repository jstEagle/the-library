## 9. Confession at Low Water

**FELIX**

The tide came in the way it always did, patient and indifferent, filling the channel beneath the quay until the water pressed against the old sea-stairs that descended into St. Aldhelm's crypt. I had known about those stairs for eleven years. Ainsley had shown me them my first winter, lantern in hand, saying, *Everything in this cathedral drains somewhere, Felix. Remember that.* I had thought it a lesson about damp.

I crouched in the dark of the ossuary passage, three meters from the lead-lined chest, with a recorder the size of a matchbox warm against my sternum. My knees ached. My mouth tasted of copper. For twenty-three years I had made myself a man no one looked at twice, and now I was betting my life on the single certainty that had survived everything else: whoever killed Daniel Reeve—and Tom Brackley—read this archive the way I did, line by line, hungering for what the gaps concealed.

The forged entry had been in place for six days. *Ossuary chest C-4: contents sealed 1974, pending diocesan review. Key held Canon Ainsley.* Written in ink I had aged myself. Filed in the ledger Voss himself had helped me catalogue when he still visited, before his retirement, before his hands shook.

At 11:40, the door at the top of the sea-stairs opened.

Not the crypt door. The *water* door—the one I had sealed with wax and recorded in no ledger, because some knowledge you keep off the page entirely. Edgar Voss came down the steps sideways, one hand on the wall, an electric torch in his other hand and something long and dark under his arm. He was eighty-one years old and he moved like a man who had done this descent before, many times, in another century.

He set the torch down. Pried the chest open with a crowbar he must have carried through half a mile of night.

"Empty," he said, to no one. Then, louder, into the dark behind him: "You can come out, Felix. I heard your breathing from the stairs."

* * *

**WRENN**

Wrenn sat in the unmarked car on Quay Lane with the engine off and her phone screen dimmed, watching the faint glow she'd told the constable to ignore. The anonymous note had arrived Tuesday, typed, postmarked local: *Tonight, low water, 2330. Crypt sea-door. Bring someone who can swim.*

She'd run it past her superintendent, who'd said the words *fishing expedition* twice, but she'd come anyway, because Brackley's staged drowning had been too clean and because Felix Nakamura's eyes, across two interviews, had held the particular misery of a person carrying a weight they couldn't put down. She'd posted Constable Adeyemi at the head of the crypt stairwell inside and taken the lane herself.

Her phone buzzed. Adeyemi: *Movement below. Two voices.*

She was out of the car before the second message arrived: *It's Voss.*

* * *

**FELIX**

I stepped out of the passage with the recorder running and my hands visible, the way you do when you want a frightened animal to stay calm. Except I wasn't afraid of him, not exactly. I was afraid of what he would say, because I had loved him once, in the small dry way you love the people who teach you things.

"You taught me locks," I said. "You never taught me why."

Voss turned. The torchlight caught his face, and it was just an old man's face, wet-lipped, tired, furious. "Ainsley's ledger," he said. "Where is the rest of it? The entry names a key."

"There is no key. There is no evidence in that chest." I kept my voice level. "There's only what I wrote, Edgar. Because I needed you here, and I knew you'd come if the record said so. You've always trusted paper more than people."

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise. Respect, maybe, which was worse.

"In 1974," I said, "a young organist named Daniel Reeve died in this crypt. He didn't drown. The pathologist's report on the body from the tideline says blunt force. He was twenty-two, and he found out the restoration fund was being skimmed, and he wrote to the bishop about it. And you were the sub-deacon who kept the keys."

"Daniel Reeve was a boy who understood nothing," Voss said, and his voice cracked open like ice on a pond. "He understood nothing about what that money *did*. The roof, the school wing, the widows' pensions—all of it balanced on a ledger he wanted to burn down for principle. Principle." He spat the word. "We argued. He swung at me. I pushed him. His head hit the step." He pointed the crowbar at the stairs, at forty years of stone. "Right there. And then Canon Ainsley came down with his lantern, and do you know what that good man did? He looked at the body, and he looked at me, and he said, *The tide will take accounts.*"

My stomach turned over. Ainsley's phrase. The lesson I'd carried like scripture.

"He showed me the sea-stairs," Voss went on, quieter now, almost gentle, which was the most terrible thing of all. "We weighted the boy and gave him to the water. And Ainsley cut the pages from the register himself—he was better with a blade than I was—and we let fifty years pass, and it worked, Felix. It *worked*, until your stranger washed up wearing your coat, and Brackley started asking questions he'd promised a dying friend he'd ask, and—" He stopped.

"And you killed him," I said. "Tom Brackley. In the stairwell."

"He was going to the police. He had a page." Voss lifted the crowbar, and the respect in his face curdled into something older. "And you, clever boy, have spent a week telling me where the proof sleeps. So open the chest properly. Show me what Ainsley left. Or I will finish what the tide started with you."

* * *

**WRENN**

She took the sea-stairs fast and stupid, no backup beside her, because Adeyemi was sealing the top exit and there was no third constable. The passage light was thin. She heard Voss's voice rise, heard Nakamura say something steady and unbearable, and then the scrape of metal on stone.

"Nakamura!" Her own voice, echoing wrong in the low vault. "Police. Drop it, Edgar."

Voss spun, crowbar high, and for one suspended second Wrenn saw the geometry of it all: the old man between her and the archivist, the black water lipping at the stair-foot, the open chest gleaming like a mouth. He lunged—not at her, at Felix—and she closed the distance and took his arm and drove him against the wall, and they went down together onto wet flagstones, her knee, his shoulder, the crowbar ringing away into the dark. He was strong the way dying fires are strong. She cuffed him to a drainage ring bolted there by masons two centuries dead, and then she sat back, breathing hard, and looked at Felix Nakamura, who stood very still with one hand pressed flat over their sternum.

"It's recording," Felix said. "All of it. From the beginning."

Wrenn nodded slowly. Outside, the tide had turned. She could hear it—a long, low exhale through the sea-stairs, the sound of water giving back what it had borrowed.

"Daniel Reeve's family," Felix said quietly. "Someone should tell them he's been down here all along. That his name goes back in the register."

"Yes," Wrenn said. And then, because she was who she was: "Nakamura. We're going to talk about how you knew all this before I did."

Felix met her eyes, and for the first time since the body came ashore, didn't flinch. "Tomorrow," Felix said. "Tonight the tide keeps accounts."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone, the cathedral bell began to toll midnight—and somewhere out in the dark channel, something that had waited fifty years shifted against its moorings, ready at last to be given a name.
