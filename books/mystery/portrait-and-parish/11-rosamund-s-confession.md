## 11. Rosamund's Confession

The note arrived the way all dangerous things arrive in Ashen Vale: politely, and by hand.

It was tucked into my copy of the parish register when I returned from morning prayers I had attended for no reason except that a man who is being watched should be seen doing something blameless. The service had been interminable. Reverend Ashe preached on the parable of the talents — burying what you are given in the ground being, apparently, the one sin God cannot forgive — and I sat in the third pew doing arithmetic on the backs of my hands while half the congregation glanced at me over their hymnals as though I were a fox that had wandered into the henhouse wearing a very good coat.

The note said only: *The folly at four o'clock. Come alone. — R.*

I considered, briefly, not going. Then I considered the alternative uses of my afternoon, which consisted of rereading ledger columns until my eyes crossed, and went.

* * *

Hale Folly stood on the eastern rise above the village, a Georgian conceit built by some ancestor with more money than sense and more sense than taste — a colonnaded shell with no rooms inside, erected purely so that one might walk up to it and admire the view of everything the Hales owned. Which was, historically speaking, nearly everything.

Rosamund Hale was already there when I crested the hill, standing beneath the columns with her back to me, watching the vale below. She wore grey. Of course she did; she had worn grey to every occasion since I'd arrived, mourning for a woman whose death she could not quite bring herself to perform.

"You came alone," she said, without turning. "Good. I watched the path."

"Thorough."

"My father taught me thoroughness before he taught me to ride." She turned then, and I saw that whatever composure she'd assembled for the occasion was load-bearing and beginning to crack. Her eyes were red-rimmed. She had either been crying or had been standing in this wind long enough to fake it convincingly, and I had learned enough about Ashen Vale to know that nothing here was done unconvincingly.

"Miss Hale," I said. "Your note was admirably brief."

"I've been drafting it for three days." A ghost of a smile. "Brevity was the only version I could bear to send."

Below us the village lay in its valley like something arranged for a photograph — the church spire, the crooked lanes, the smoke rising straight up in the still air. It occurred to me, not for the first time, that Ashen Vale did not look like a place where terrible things happened. It looked like a place where terrible things were *tidied*.

"Mr. Montrose," she said. "Do you know why I asked you here?"

"I have three theories," I said. "Two of them end with you asking me to leave, and one ends rather differently, and I confess I'm hoping for neither."

"The first two, then." She folded her hands. "But you won't leave. So I'll skip to the part where I tell you something, and you do with it what your profession requires, and I spend the rest of my life discovering whether that was wisdom or ruin."

"That's a remarkably clear-eyed framing."

"I've had seven years of practice being clear-eyed about my family." The wind caught her hair and she didn't trouble with it. "You found the ledgers. Don't bother denying it — Father knows, which means everyone knows, which means I know. Round sums, forty years, a fund with no beneficiaries. You've been walking around the village looking like a man doing sums behind his eyes, and last week you spent an entire afternoon in the crypt with the memorial plaques, and now you look like a man who has finished his sums and doesn't like the total."

I said nothing. In my experience, silence invites confession far more reliably than questions do, and it has the additional advantage of being honest.

She took a breath. "There was a man," she said. "His name was Thomas Merrick. He was a labourer on the enclosure works — the year they fenced the common land. My father wasn't running it yet; my grandfather was, but my father was there, learning the trade. And Thomas Merrick objected to losing the grazing rights his family had held since before anyone kept records, because he was one of those tiresome men who believes the law applies to landowners too."

"And?"

"And there was an argument. At the boundary stone, in November. Witnesses disagree about what happened next, which is convenient, because witnesses can be paid to disagree." Her voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who had rehearsed this in the dark until the words wore smooth. "Thomas Merrick fell. Or was struck. He struck his head on the stone. And my grandfather's men carried him off the common land that night, and he never came home, and in the spring his wife was told he'd taken work in the north and abandoned them. There was no body. There was no inquest. There was a burial record signed by the vicar of the day — private ground, the Hale plot's edge, unmarked — and there was a payment to the parish poor fund that appears in the ledgers you've been reading. The first of them. The template for all the rest."

The wind moved through the empty colonnade, and for a moment the only sound in the world was that wind, and I understood why people built follies: so that there would be somewhere to stand when the ground gave way.

"He's buried there," she said. "Under the yew hedge, past the last stone. Forty years, Mr. Montrose. My father visits him. I've seen him do it. He stands at the hedge with his hat off, and I used to think he was praying for my mother."

* * *

I made myself ask the question, though I already knew its shape. "And Marguerite Hale? What does she have to do with Merrick?"

Rosamund turned away, back to the view, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — quieter, and younger somehow, as if the years between then and now had collapsed.

"She found the burial record. Seven years ago, when she was cataloguing the archive — she volunteered, you know, everyone thought it was eccentric of her, the widow-to-be with her head in the parish papers. She found a burial entry with no grave, no plot number, paid for out of the fund. And she was Marguerite, so instead of bringing it to the vicar like a sensible person, she brought it to the vicar like *Marguerite* — she trusted him. She showed him what she'd found and asked him, gently, whether the parish ought to know." Rosamund's hands tightened on the column beside her. "He told my father within the hour. I know because I heard them — I was sixteen, and I eavesdropped the way sixteen-year-olds do, and I heard my father say, 'Then we do what we did before,' as if he were discussing drainage."

"And three days later she vanished."

"And three days later she vanished." Rosamund closed her eyes. "Everyone says she ran off, or drowned herself in the mere, or was ruined by some scandal we're too delicate to name. But I was there, Mr. Montrose. I saw her face at church that last Sunday. She wasn't a woman planning to run. She was a woman deciding something. And I have spent seven years knowing — *knowing*, the way you know a bone is broken — that whatever happened to her, my father's face was the last thing she trusted before it did."

The confession hung in the cold air between us. Below, a cart moved along the lane, unhurried, its driver whistling. The village went on being beautiful. That, I thought, was the true obscenity of Ashen Vale: not the buried man or the bought vicars, but the whistling.

"Why tell me?" I asked. "You must know what I'll do with it."

"Do I?" She laughed, and there was nothing humorous in it. "I've watched you for weeks. You're the first person to come into this valley in seven years who isn't for sale. Do you know how I know? Because my father offered, and you asked for a night to think about it, and any man who can be bought would have taken the cheque on the spot. Only an honest man hesitates. Dishonesty is always so much more decisive."

I felt that remark settle somewhere under my ribs, uncomfortably close to the truth of how tempted I had actually been. She saw it land. She was, I was learning, a great deal more perceptive than her father gave her credit for — which was perhaps the only reason she was still capable of confession.

"There's another reason," she said. "Marguerite left a will. Everything to 'the one who finds me.' Everyone assumes it's madness, or a joke, or a trap. But I've read it a hundred times, and I think—" She stopped. Started again. "I think she knew exactly what she was doing. I think she wanted someone to find *this*. Not the money. This." She gestured downward, at the whole green valley, at the yew hedge miles away that we both pretended not to see. "And if that's true, then whoever finds it is carrying out her wishes, and I am the daughter of the man who destroyed her, and I don't get to choose which of those facts is heavier."

"So you want—"

"I want you to burn it." She faced me fully now, and her eyes were dry at last, and fierce. "The ledger, the encoded plaques, whatever proof you've assembled. Burn it. Let Thomas Merrick rest. Let my father die in his bed instead of in a courtroom. Let the village keep its church and its vicar and its story about itself, because I promise you, Mr. Montrose, the truth will not heal this place — it will crack it open like ice in spring, and everything underneath will drown."

"Pity," I said quietly, "that the truth is not yours to burn."

"It isn't yours to publish, either!" The fierceness cracked, and underneath it was just a young woman, exhausted. "You've been here five weeks. I've been here twenty-six years. I know the widow whose pension comes from that fund. I know the families on the common land whose deeds would be dragged through ten years of litigation. I know old Mrs. Petchey, who thinks Reverend Ashe walks on water, and I know what it does to a person to learn their water-walker forged a burial record. You talk about justice like it's a document. It isn't. It's fire, and fire doesn't check whose house it's in."

"No," I agreed. "But arsonists always describe themselves as firefighters."

She stared at me. And then — I will give her this — she almost smiled. "Marguerite would have liked you."

"You knew her well?"

"Better than anyone guessed." Rosamund looked down at the valley again. "She used to let me help in the archive. I was fifteen, sixteen. She taught me paleography and let me drink sherry and told me that most evil is just bookkeeping that nobody audits." A pause. "She also told me that if I ever found myself standing on the folly asking a stranger to burn evidence, I should remember that she'd have hated me for it."

"Did she."

"She said mercy that protects the guilty is just cowardice wearing gloves." Rosamund's voice dropped. "She said it about my father. She said it *to* me, once, gently, when I cried after the enclosure anniversary service. And I hated her, and then I grew up, and now I'm standing here proving her right, and I hate that most of all."

* * *

We stood a while longer in the wind. I took out my notebook — partly for effect, mostly because my hand wanted something to do — and wrote nothing in it.

"If I refuse," I said, "you could go to your father. Tell him I know about Merrick. Buy yourself his gratitude."

"Yes," she said simply. "I could. I've decided not to, and I'd thank you not to make me explain why, because I only have the one speech and I've used it."

"Then hear mine, since you've earned it. I won't burn the ledger. Not because I love the fire, but because a dead man named Merrick has waited forty years under a yew hedge for someone to say his name aloud in daylight, and I am constitutionally incapable of letting him wait a forty-first. But—" I held up a hand before she could speak. "But I am not indifferent to widows' pensions or Mrs. Petchey's water-walker. If it can be done narrowly — if the principals can answer and the village can be spared — then I will spend every ounce of my stubbornness trying to do it that way. I make no promises. Justice, as you correctly observe, is not a document. But neither is it a bonfire, and I intend to find out whether it can be anything gentler than either."

Rosamund studied me for a long moment, the way one studies a contract one intends to sign but hasn't yet forgiven.

"Narrowly," she repeated. "You sound like a lawyer."

"I'm given to understand it's my defining flaw."

"My father thinks it's your price tag."

"Your father," I said, "has been wrong about the value of things for forty years. It's rather the family business."

That startled a real laugh out of her — brief, surprised, gone almost at once, but real. She pulled her coat closer and looked east, where the light was starting to fail over the hills.

"One thing more," she said. "A warning, since I seem to be in the business of giving things away today. My father doesn't know I'm here. But Reverend Ashe knows I left the house agitated, and Reverend Ashe notices everything, and Mr. Montrose — the vicar is not the gentle fool he performs. Whatever Marguerite found, she found it in *his* archive, under *his* nose, and she disappeared three days later. Think about what that means about who moves fastest in this parish when they're frightened." She began to descend the hill, then paused. "And Mr. Montrose? If you meet her — if there's any chance, any at all, that she's alive — tell her I was fifteen and I didn't understand, and I'm sorry, and I never burned anything."

She went down the path without waiting for an answer, grey against the greening grass, and I stood alone under the columns of a house that had never contained anything, holding a notebook full of nothing, thinking about yew hedges and water-walkers and the remarkable fact that in Ashen Vale, the first honest words I'd heard had come from the heiress.

I was still standing there when I saw him — a figure in black at the foot of the hill, where the path met the lane, standing beside a bicycle he made no move to mount. Even at that distance I knew the set of the shoulders. Reverend Ashe tipped his hat to me pleasantly across four hundred yards of open ground, the way a man does when he wants you to know that distance is no protection at all.

Then he pedalled slowly toward the church, and I started down the hill after him, because there was one question I suddenly needed answered before nightfall: what else, besides a burial record, had ever been forged in that archive — and whether the diary that kept finding its way back to me had been safe there all along, or whether I had been carrying the conspirators' shopping list in my own coat pocket.
