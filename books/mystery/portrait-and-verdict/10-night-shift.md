## 10. Night Shift

There is a particular hour in a cemetery that no living person visits willingly—the one just after midnight, when the frost begins its slow work on the grass and the stones stop being monuments and become, briefly, their own shadows. Ansel Okafor had spent thirty-one years learning that hour the way other men learned golf or fishing. He knew which way the wind came off Miller's Pond at two in the morning (from the east, carrying the mineral smell of silt), knew which of the old oaks creaked like a porch swing and which held their silence, knew that the Harrow plot's iron fence sang a single low note when the temperature dropped below forty degrees, as if the whole enclosure were tuning itself for something.

He was sitting in it now.

The folding stool was his wife's—had been his wife's, he corrected himself, though after eleven years the correction came automatically rather than painfully—and it groaned under him in a way that made him feel less like a watchman and more like an old man playing at one. He had a thermos of coffee gone bitter hours ago, a blanket over his knees, and a view of Edmund Harrow's reopened grave framed between two headstones like a stage set waiting for its actor.

The hole had been refilled after the exhumation—of course it had; the county didn't leave open pits in consecrated ground—but the earth sat wrong. Too dark, too loose, too new. It looked like a wound that hadn't decided whether to scar. Ansel had re-tamped it himself twice this week, out of pure professional embarrassment, and both times the ground seemed to settle crooked again overnight, as if something underneath were rearranging itself for comfort.

*Something underneath.* He took a sip of the terrible coffee and let himself think it plainly, because there was nobody to hear him think it: Daniel Voss had lain in Edmund Harrow's coffin for fifteen years, and Ansel Okafor had signed the paper saying otherwise. Every night since the opening, he lay awake composing the sentence he would someday have to say aloud—*I certified the death of a man who was not dead*—and every morning he got up and made his eggs and alphabetized something, because routine was the only rope he'd ever had, and he was not yet ready to let go of it.

But tonight was not about penance. Tonight was about the second grave.

* * *

Mara Quist had called him at six o'clock with the news, her voice clipped in the way he'd come to recognize as her version of excitement. "Ashford plot. The 1849 one, the infant. Opened sometime last night, refilled, no damage to the fence. And Ansel—" she'd paused, and he'd heard paper rustling, "—there was a card inside. Typed."

"Typed," he'd repeated.

"Index card. Three-by-five. It says: *Cross-reference 98-0417. See also 09-1130.* That's all."

Ansel had closed his eyes. 98-0417. Lily Ashford. Drowned at fourteen in Miller's Pond, ruled accidental by coroner Elias Wren, Ansel's mentor, in the last year of Elias's life. 09-1130. Daniel Voss. Filed by Ansel himself, fifteen years ago next month.

"Who else has seen the card?"

"The committee knows there's been a disturbance. They don't know what was written on it." A beat. "I haven't told them the case numbers mean anything. I'm not sure they'd recognize them anyway. But whoever left it wanted *you* to read it, Ansel. Not me. Not the sheriff. You."

He'd said something noncommittal—he was good at noncommittal; it had been his professional language for three decades—and hung up and stood in his kitchen looking at the phone as if it might explain itself.

Because here was the thing he had not told Mara, could not yet tell anyone: the numbers weren't just correct. They were formatted *his* way. The county's filing system used a different convention—year first, then a letter code. Ansel's private cross-referencing habit, the one he'd inherited from Elias and refined into something almost liturgical, put the year last. Nobody used that format anymore. Nobody had *ever* used it except the two of them, and Elias had been dead for twenty-four years.

Which meant the person leaving typed index cards in empty graves knew how Ansel Okafor thought. Knew it intimately. Was, in some sense, thinking *with* him.

So tonight he sat in the cold with his dead wife's folding stool, watching a hole in the ground, waiting for the auditor to come collect his thoughts.

* * *

The hours passed the way cemetery hours do—slowly, then all at once. At one-fifteen a fox crossed the path by the Calloway stones and gave him a look of frank editorial disapproval before dissolving into the hedge. At two he ate the sandwich he'd wrapped in foil, roast beef, slightly frozen in the middle, and decided this too was part of the vigil's necessary indignities. At three the wind shifted and the iron fence sang its low note, and Ansel felt the hair rise on his forearms with an anticipation he was almost ashamed of.

At twenty past three, someone came through the gate.

Not the main gate—that would have meant the road, the lights, the risk. This was the pedestrian gate on the pond side, the one whose latch Ansel had oiled every spring because a squealing hinge was a dereliction of duty. The figure moved along the row of young maples with the unhurried confidence of someone who had walked this ground many times, and Ansel's heart did something complicated: half triumph, half dread. *Here. Finally. Here.*

The figure went straight to the Harrow plot. No hesitation, no wrong turns. Straight to the fresh-turned earth of Edmund Harrow's grave, and knelt.

Ansel rose from the stool with the exaggerated care of a man trying not to exist, folded the blanket, set down the thermos. His knees announced themselves but he ignored them. Twenty feet. Fifteen. The moon was behind thin cloud, giving everything the soft focus of an old photograph, and he could see now that the figure was slight, narrow-shouldered, wearing a dark coat too big for it—

Too big for it. Young. The shoulders hadn't finished growing into the coat.

"—Theo?" he said, before he could decide not to.

The boy spun. For one suspended second they looked at each other across the grave of Edmund Harrow, founder, liar, occupant-of-nothing, and Ansel saw the face clearly: Theo Harrow, nineteen years old, great-grandson of the man in the ground, white as the frost on the headstones, clutching something against his chest.

"I wasn't—" Theo started, and then did the thing that confirmed everything: he ran.

It was not a graceful escape. He caught his hip on the iron fence—the fence sang its note again, louder this time, almost amused—stumbled, recovered, and bolted for the pond gate with the desperate speed of youth. Ansel did not chase. Thirty years of tending graves had taught him exactly what his body would and wouldn't do, and pursuit at three-thirty in the morning was firmly in the *wouldn't* column. Instead he walked to where the boy had knelt and looked down at what had been left behind.

A small bouquet of white chrysanthemums, arranged with more feeling than skill, the stems wrapped in a wet paper towel. And beneath them, weighted against the wind with a stone, a single sheet of paper.

Ansel crouched. He did not touch it at first. He had learned, these past weeks, to look at documents the way you look at a sleeping animal—with respect and a certain fear.

It was a photocopy. Photocopy of a photocopy, third or fourth generation, the gray getting grayer each time, but legible. It was a page from a case file. He recognized the format instantly, because it was *his* office's format, the old pre-digital intake sheet, and the header read:

**CASE 09-1130 — VOSS, DANIEL R.**

His own case number. His own formatting. And in the upper corner, where the original bore the coroner's signature block, the copy showed a signature he knew better than his own reflection—the careful, upright letters, the little forward lean on the final *r*.

*Okafor, A., Coroner.*

Below it, someone had typed a single line, centered, in the same font as the index cards:

**THE RECORD IS NOT FINISHED.**

* * *

Ansel stood in the frost for a long time, holding the page by its edges like evidence, which is what it was, though evidence of what he couldn't yet say.

Theo Harrow. He turned the name over. The boy had grown up four houses down from the cemetery, had ridden his bike through these gates every summer afternoon until he was twelve and his mother decided it was morbid. Ansel remembered him—a serious child, quiet, the kind who asked questions adults answered carelessly and then regretted. He remembered, with a lurch, that Theo had been nine years old in 2009. Nine, when Daniel Voss disappeared. Nine, when the town closed around its secret like a fist.

And now the great-grandson of the founder was sneaking into the cemetery at three in the morning to leave flowers on the empty grave of a man who'd never been buried there, along with a page of a file that should not exist outside the coroner's office.

Flowers. That was the detail that kept snagging. The killer—the auditor, the typist, the ghost in the files—left index cards. Clean, typed, clinical. This boy had left *chrysanthemums*, stems in a wet paper towel so they'd last until morning. Grief leaves flowers. Guilt leaves flowers. The killer left citations.

Unless the flowers were theater too. Unless Ansel was being handed exactly the distinction he wanted to make, precisely so he'd make it.

He looked at the grave. The dark, crooked, restless earth.

"You're full of people who aren't you," he told it quietly. It was the kind of thing he said to graves sometimes, alone, and had never once said in company. "I'd like to know how you got that way."

Then he folded the photocopied page into his breast pocket, picked up his wife's stool, and went home to wait for morning, because there was one thing he knew about nineteen-year-olds: they slept late, and they could be found.

* * *

He found Theo Harrow at eleven the next morning, sitting on the steps of the shuttered boathouse at Miller's Pond, which was either a coincidence or a message, and Ansel had stopped believing in coincidences somewhere around the second empty grave.

The boy saw him coming and didn't run this time. He just watched, hands jammed in the pockets of the too-big coat, jaw set in a way that was unmistakably Harrow—all the portraits of Edmund had that same stubborn line. Up close he looked like he hadn't slept, which was fair; neither had Ansel.

"Mr. Okafor." Theo's voice cracked once and he pushed through it. "You're the coroner."

"I'm retired from the title. The habits stay." Ansel lowered himself onto the step below the boy, which took longer than it used to and which he pretended it didn't. For a while they both looked at the pond—flat and silver and innocent-looking, the liar. "You left flowers on my crime scene."

"It's not a crime scene. It's a grave."

"It's a grave with the wrong man in it. Which makes it both." Ansel kept his voice mild. Mildness was a tool; he'd sharpened it over decades of telling families hard things. "You want to tell me why you're visiting Daniel Voss?"

Theo flinched at the name. Actually flinched, shoulders jumping like the syllables had been thrown at him. "You know who he is."

"I signed his death certificate. So yes."

"That's—" The boy turned and looked at him fully then, and there was something in his face that Ansel recognized with a sinking feeling, because he saw it in the mirror some mornings: the look of someone carrying a weight they've decided not to put down. "That's exactly it. You *signed*. Everybody signed. My family, the Calloways, the Ashfords, your office. Everybody signed and initialed and stamped and filed, and everybody agreed it was finished. And it isn't. It was never finished."

The wind came off the water and Ansel smelled the silt, the mineral cold of it, the same smell as the night a girl named Lily Ashford went under, though he'd been too young then to be anything but a bystander to that particular silence. Bystander, and later, custodian.

"Theo," he said carefully, "who gave you that file page?"

For a moment—just a moment—the boy's eyes flicked away, toward the tree line, toward town, toward something he wasn't ready to name. And then he stood up, brushing off his coat with sudden brisk movements, and Ansel understood that whatever door had opened a crack was now being shut, hard, by a kid who'd learned caution somewhere and recently.

"I have to go."

"Theo—"

"They said you'd ask questions." He was already walking backward up the path, hands back in his pockets, chin up. "They said you'd be polite about it, too. They were right."

"*They?*" Ansel got to his feet. "Who said? Who's *they*, Theo?"

But the boy was gone, quick down the lane, and Ansel stood alone at the edge of the water where a girl had drowned and a journalist had asked his last questions, holding nothing but the memory of a flinch and a flicker of the eyes toward town.

Back home, he laid the photocopied page on his kitchen table under the good lamp and studied it again, and this time he saw what exhaustion had hidden from him the night before. The copy was fourth-generation gray, but the original it descended from had been creased—folded and unfolded many times, the fibers broken along two lines. Someone had carried this page for a long time. Someone had taken it out and looked at it, again and again, the way you check a wound.

And in the bottom margin, so faint the copier had nearly eaten it, there was a pencil notation in a hand that was not typed, not printed, not official. Small, cramped, hurried:

*ledger p. 41 — see E.W.*

E.W. Elias Wren. Ansel's mentor. Dead twenty-four years, keeper of the drowned girl's file, signer of the lie that started everything.

And a ledger. Page forty-one. A ledger Ansel had never seen, referenced in a hand he didn't know, carried by a boy who wouldn't say who *they* were.

He sat down slowly in the kitchen chair and heard himself say, to the empty room, in the tone he usually reserved for misfiled paperwork:

"Well. There's another book."
