## 9. Exhumation, Reversed

The lie I told was a small one, as lies go. It fit on a single sheet of county letterhead, and it said that on the morning of October 14th, during routine seismic surveying for the courthouse foundation repair, workmen had discovered human remains in a sealed brick alcove beneath the east stairwell — remains consistent with a male of advanced age, interred approximately fifty years ago, tentatively identified as Edmund Voss.

I forged the coroner's seal myself. Thirty years of stamping other people's deaths had taught me exactly how the ink should pool at the edges of the eagle's wing. Ida Marsh taught me that, actually, back when my hands shook too badly to hold a stamp straight. She'd stood behind me at this same desk, her chin nearly on my shoulder, and said, *Press like you mean it, Rafe. A hesitant seal is a confession.*

I pressed like I meant it.

Then I did the hardest part, which was not the forgery at all. It was leaving the report where she would find it — face-up on my desk, under the paperweight shaped like a raven that she'd given me for my twenty-fifth birthday, in an office whose lock I had quietly oiled so it would no longer catch. Ida Marsh had been reading my mail, my files, and my silences for longer than I cared to count. She would read this too. And she would do what she had always done: she would go and make the problem disappear.

The only question was whether I would be standing there when she tried.

* * *

"You look terrible," Cora Lin said, which was her way of saying she'd been worried sick. She'd come to my house after dark, badge tucked away, two cups of gas-station coffee sweating in her hands. "You know you're suspended. Officially, showing up here is a bad idea."

"Officially," I agreed, and took the coffee anyway.

She sat across from me in the kitchen, under the light Ida had helped me pick out years ago — brass, warm, the kind that makes everyone look kinder than they are. Cora looked tired enough to be kind and furious at once.

"The courthouse thing," she said slowly. "That seismic report. I called the survey company this afternoon."

I held her eyes over the rim of my cup. "And?"

"And the dispatcher said they hadn't surveyed anything in Hollowmere since June." She set her cup down with a click. "Rafael. If that report is fake, then whoever moves on those 'remains' is moving because they were told to. You're fishing."

"I'm fishing," I admitted.

"With yourself as bait."

"With myself as everything," I said. "It's the only lure I've got left. They took my office, my cases, my good name — well, partially took. The point is, Ida doesn't trust institutions anymore. She trusts me. Or rather—" I turned the cup in my hands, watching the coffee shiver. "Rather, she trusts that I'll protect her, because she's spent forty years making sure I owe her. Which I do. Which is the whole problem."

Cora was quiet for a while. Outside, the wind moved through the yew trees along my fence line, the ones I trimmed every spring because Edmund Voss's grave sat just beyond them and the dead deserve tidy neighbors.

"If you're wrong," Cora said finally, "if it isn't her—"

"It's her." I said it plainly, and something in my chest went quiet and cold as I said it, the way a room goes cold when a door is left open in winter. "The portraits are hers. The brushwork — I finally placed it. She painted a copy of a Vermeer for my graduation from the academy. Same hand. Same stubborn little highlights in the eyes. She signed Lila's name inside the frame of one of them, Cora. She wanted me to find her eventually. I think maybe she's been waiting to be found for fifty years."

"That doesn't make it less dangerous."

"No," I said. "It makes it more."

* * *

The crypt beneath the old courthouse chapel smells of cold stone and candle grease, and has smelled that way since before either of us was born. I got there first, of course. I'd told Cora to wait for my call — a call I wasn't sure I'd be able to make — and I'd told Sheriff Dunmore nothing at all, because Dunmore believed whatever was easiest to believe, and right now the easiest thing to believe was that Rafael Fairweather had finally gone round the bend.

I sat on the steps above the east alcove, where my forged report said the bricks had been opened, and I waited in the dark with a flashlight I didn't turn on and a heart that would not behave.

She came at eleven.

I heard her before I saw her — the particular rhythm of her walk, unhurried, heel-first, the walk of a woman who had never once hurried toward a body because the body wasn't going anywhere. The door at the top of the stairs opened. Light spilled down. Her shadow stretched long and thin across the floor toward me, and for one absurd moment I thought: *she looks like she used to, coming down into the morgue to check my work.* Then the moment passed, and it was just a shadow.

"Rafe," she said, unsurprised. Of course she was unsurprised. "You always did leave your desk too tidy. Nobody organizes their pending files alphabetically unless they want someone to read them."

"Hello, Ida."

She came down the stairs without her cane, which frightened me more than anything else could have. She'd hidden how bad the arthritis had gotten. How much else had she hidden? She stopped three steps above me, and in the dim wedge of light from the doorway I saw that she held a boning knife — the short, curved one from her old kit, the one she'd used to teach me the first incision of my life.

"You forged that report," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you'd have destroyed the evidence either way," I said. "I wanted to be here when you did it. I wanted to see your face when you decided."

"My face." Something moved through her voice — not quite laughter. "You always watched faces, Rafe. It made you a good coroner. People think we read bodies. We don't. We read the living, the ones who come in to identify, the ones who lie about how much they drank, the ones who sigh with relief when we say *natural causes*. The dead are honest. It's everyone else who takes work."

"Ida." My voice cracked on her name, and I let it. "Lila Voss. Your sister."

The knife did not waver. But her breath did — one small catch, like a stitch pulled tight.

"She was born Lila Marsh," Ida said. "Voss was the name he gave her when he decided she belonged to him. Like a tool. Like a deed." She descended another step. "You've been to the ledger. You found her."

"I found the hole where she should have been."

"That's how he liked his women. Holes in records." Another step. We were nearly level now, close enough that I could see her eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "Fifty years ago I stood in this building — upstairs, in his office, with his blood on my hands because I'd found him dying of the stroke that killed him, and Lila already three days dead in the icehouse below, and nobody — Rafe, *nobody* — would hear it. Not the sheriff, who owed Voss money. Not the judge, who'd been a guest at his table. Not the doctor, who signed whatever the founder's family put in front of him. A servant girl drowns, they said. Girls drown. Accidents happen to girls like that."

"So you buried him alive in the record instead," I whispered. "You erased his death and kept the town hostage to its own lie."

"I kept *him* from becoming a monument!" The word rang off the stone. "Every street, every school, every smug portrait in the courthouse rotunda — I couldn't burn any of it. So I burned the man. I let Hollowmere bury an empty box and mourn a monster, and I made sure the file that could undo it stayed cut apart, page by page, in an archive only two people ever read." She looked at me then, and the knife lowered an inch, trembling. "You, and me."

"Until someone started killing people according to those pages."

"I didn't kill anyone." Flat. Immediate. Then, quieter, terribly: "Not with my hands."

"But you knew. You watched them die in the pattern and you let it happen, because every death closed another gap in the story. Because the men in that file — the ones who wouldn't listen, the ones who helped him — they were the loose threads, and someone was pulling them, and you *let them pull*, Ida, because it tidied things up."

"They were going to dig up the truth anyway," she said, "and the truth would have buried you with it. You were my apprentice when I altered the first record. Your signature is on the transfer order, Rafe. You were nineteen and you trusted me and you never even knew what you signed. Exposing Voss meant exposing you. I have spent fifty years keeping you clean." Her voice frayed. "I have spent fifty years keeping you *mine*, if I'm honest, because you were the only good thing I ever grew in that awful garden."

The knife came up again.

"Step away from the alcove, Rafe."

"No."

"*Step away.*"

"There are no remains under there, Ida. There's a brick wall and fifty years of dust and a flashlight in my pocket. The only evidence in this room is what you say next." I stood up slowly, hands open, an old habit from a hundred tense identifications — show the grieving your palms, show them you hold nothing, you take nothing. "So say it. Say all of it. For Lila. Not for the file. For her."

For a long moment the crypt held nothing but our breathing and the tick of cooling stone.

Then Ida Marsh, the finest coroner this county ever produced, sat down on the step beside me like an old woman at the end of a very long shift, and laid the knife flat on the stone between us, handle toward me.

"He killed her," she said, "because she told him she was leaving. That's all. That's the whole of it. She was twenty-two and she wanted to see the ocean, and he couldn't bear to lose a possession." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "And I have carried her name in my mouth for fifty years like a stone, and I am so tired, Rafe. God help me, I am so tired."

I reached for her hand, and she let me take it, and that was when the lights came on at the top of the stairs — white, blinding, official — and Cora Lin's voice came down the stairwell, steady and hard:

"Ida Marsh. Don't move. Rafael — step away from her."

I didn't step away. I held on.

And Ida, looking up into the light with tears cutting through fifty years of dust, began — softly, horribly — to laugh.
