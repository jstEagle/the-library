## 3. What the Register Doesn't Say

The letters sat on my desk for two days before I touched them again.

I told myself it was method. An archivist doesn't rush a document; an archivist lets the document settle, the way you let ink dry or varnish cure. But that was a lie dressed in professional clothing, and I knew it. The truth was simpler and uglier: I was afraid of what I'd find, and I had spent forty-one years building a life with no sharp edges precisely so I would never have to be afraid of anything.

St. Aldhelm's archive is kept in the undercroft beneath the north transept, a low-vaulted room where the temperature never rises above sixteen degrees and the light comes through slit windows the color of weak tea. It is, by any reasonable measure, a tomb for paper. I had made it my tomb too, and I had been content—or whatever passes for content in a person who has trained themselves out of wanting.

On the third morning I came down early, before the vergers unlocked the nave above, and I took the letters out of their drawer and read them again, side by side, under the lamp.

*I killed a man in the crypt below St. Aldhelm's in the winter of 1974.*

*Find what was written twice, and you will understand what you are owed.*

Same hand. Same slant to the ascenders, same odd little break in the crossbar of the *t*, as if the writer's pen had hesitated at exactly the same point both times. Two letters, one hand. Either one person was playing both sides of a game whose rules I didn't know, or—

Or someone knew me. That was the part I kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. Not the murder, not the money. The fact that whoever wrote those letters had addressed them to *Yara Thackeray, Archivist, St. Aldhelm's Cathedral* — a name that appeared on no staff listing, no public register, no document outside these walls. I was careful the way only invisible people are careful. Someone had seen through it.

And they had used my surname. Thackeray. A name I'd taken from a mother who died when I was four and left nothing behind but it.

I put on my white cotton gloves, because ritual matters even when no one is watching—especially then—and went to the burial registers.

* * *

The cathedral's interment records run unbroken from 1541 to the present, forty-seven volumes in calf binding, each one indexed in a different hand according to the fashion of its decade. The 1974 volume was catalogued under BUR/REG/1974–79, and I had handled it perhaps six times in eleven years, always for genealogists tracing grandparents, always routine. Burials at St. Aldhelm's had grown rare by the seventies; the crypt had been sealed to new interments in 1968 by order of the consistory court, which meant the volume should have been thin. Mostly ash interments in the garden of remembrance. Mostly administrative dust.

I turned to January 1974 and began to read.

It was ordinary work, the kind of work I loved—the kind where the world reduces itself to columns and dates and the small human music of names. Edith Mary Prowse, aged eighty-one. Arthur Kellaway, aged sixty-four. Onward through the winter, page after page, the dead of a city that had mostly forgotten them.

I found her on the ninth of February.

Not because the entry announced itself. It was formatted identically to every entry around it—same ruled lines, same clerk's italic, same officiant's initials in the margin, *R.A.B.*, the Reverend A. B. Cuthbertson, who signed everything in this volume. What stopped me was not the format but the name.

**THACKERAY, Vivienne Margaret.** Aged thirty-four. Interred: Crypt, Bay VII, North Range.

I read it three times. My own name looked back at me from the page like a stranger wearing my coat.

Vivienne Margaret Thackeray. My mother's name—I knew it the way you know a word in a language you no longer speak; it lived in me without meaning. She had died when I was four. That was the story, the whole story, the story I had been given by the aunt who raised me and never asked to be contradicted: *Your mother was ill, Yara. Your father wasn't in the picture. She's buried in the north, somewhere. It was a long time ago.*

But here she was. Not buried in the north. Buried here—in the crypt I had walked over every working day for eleven years, in Bay VII of the North Range, nine feet below the flagstones of the ambulatory where tourists paused to photograph the misericords.

And dated the ninth of February, 1974. The winter named in the first letter. *I killed a man in the crypt below St. Aldhelm's in the winter of 1974.*

My hands were steady. I remember noticing that, the way you notice strange weather—an archivist's hands don't shake, they wait—but there was a sound in my ears like wind moving through a long empty corridor, and I understood distantly that the corridor was me.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that made the wind.

Bay VII of the North Range had been surveyed in 1991, when the Friends of the Cathedral commissioned a full architectural record of the crypt ahead of some long-abandoned restoration scheme. I had catalogued that survey myself. Every bay, every shelf, every loculus, every nameplate photographed and transcribed. Bay VII contained eleven loculi, ten of them occupied, and the survey listed every occupant by name.

Vivienne Margaret Thackeray was not among them.

Ten names in the survey. Eleven loculi. One empty. And a woman entered in the burial register as lying in that bay, in that crypt, under my feet, who was not there.

* * *

I did what I always do when the ground moves: I reached for procedure. Verification. Cross-reference. The archive's own logic, which has never once lied to me, which is more than I can say for people.

First, the duplicate register. Since 1923 the cathedral has kept a fair copy of all interments, lodged separately against fire and flood—a second version of the same truth. I fetched it from the metal cabinet in the east alcove and turned to February 1974.

**THACKERAY, Vivienne M.** Ninth February. Crypt, Bay VII. Officiant R.A.B.

Identical. Down to the spacing. Which meant either the fair copy had been made honestly from the original, or both had been made from the same false source. A duplicate proves consistency, not truth. Any archivist knows this. Most people never learn it.

Second, the mortuary rolls—the daily record of bodies received into the church, kept by the verger on duty, a humbler document, less formal, less likely to be curated. The mortuary roll for the ninth of February, 1974, recorded two receptions: a morning funeral for one Reginald Otterway of Henleaze, and—

Nothing. No evening reception. No body logged for the Thackeray interment. The roll simply skipped from Otterway's funeral to the tenth of February, as if the day between had been folded shut.

A burial without a body received. A grave in the register with no occupant in the stone. A name in my family's hand-shaped absence, written into the permanent record of a place I had given my whole life to.

I sat down on the low stool by the reading bench, because my legs had opinions of their own, and I let the undercroft be quiet around me for a while. The pipes ticked. Somewhere above, the first verger was dragging a vacuum across the nave floor, the small domestic thunder of a building waking up.

Here is what I had believed about my mother for thirty-seven years: that she had been ill, that she had died far away, that she had left me nothing but a surname and a face I could no longer reconstruct except from a single blurred photograph I kept in a tin. Here is what the register now said: that she had been buried in this cathedral, in the sealed crypt, in the winter a stranger claimed to have killed a man there—at thirty-four years old, which would have made her twenty-nine when I was born, which made her realer than she had ever been, which made the lie bigger than I could hold.

Unless the register was the lie. Unless she had never been buried at all.

Buried alive in paperwork. The phrase arrived in my head uninvited and stayed, cold and exact. Someone had written her into death here without ever putting her body in the ground. Someone had used my cathedral—my archive, my registers, my responsibility—as the instrument of her erasure. And the erasure had worked so well that her own child, keeper of the very records that held her name, had never once thought to look.

I had looked, though. That was the thing I couldn't get around. In my twenties, grieving something I couldn't name, I had searched the death registers of half the counties in England for Vivienne Thackeray and found nothing, and I had concluded—comfortably, terribly—that she had died abroad, unrecorded, beyond even the reach of paper. I had grieved an absence. I had built my whole self inside that absence like a hermit crab in a shell.

She hadn't been absent. She had been *here*. Written twice, once in each register, consistent as scripture and just as unverifiable.

*What was written twice.* The second letter's phrase surfaced, and my skin prickled. I had assumed it referred to the duplicate registers—it was the obvious reading, the reading an archivist would leap to. But sitting there in the cold with my mother's false grave open in front of me, I felt the assumption crack. Maybe it meant something else. Maybe it meant the ledger the second letter promised. Or maybe it meant her name itself, written twice, once as a lie and once as—what? A message? A marker? A door left ajar?

* * *

I worked until the light through the slit windows went from tea-colored to amber, and then to nothing. I pulled every document the 1974 volume touched: the interment fee book, where the Thackeray plot showed payment in full, £84, receipted in cash—cash, unusual for a family interment, unusual enough that the clerk had added a small marginal note, *pd. by solr.,* paid by solicitor. The consistory court file authorizing the reopening of Bay VII for a single interment, granted on the petition of *Thackeray & Thackeray, Solicitors, of Clifton*—the family firm, the name I had spent my adult life pretending was just a word. The faculty paperwork bore two signatures: the chancellor's, and beneath it, as petitioner's witness, a name I recognized from the endowment boards in the south transept, brass letters polished by a century of hands.

Ambrose. No—not this Ambrose. An older one. The dean's grandfather, if the family resemblance ran true through institutions the way it runs through faces.

I sat back and looked at what I'd assembled across the reading bench: five documents, laid out in sequence, telling a story in official language. A woman dies. A solicitor pays cash. A sealed crypt is reopened by special faculty. A body enters the register but not the mortuary roll. A bay is resealed. The paper trail is complete, orderly, and wrong in exactly one place—the place where a body should be.

Whoever arranged it had known this archive intimately. Had known that the mortuary roll was kept by tired vergers who wouldn't question a gap. Had known that a duplicate register would launder the lie into permanence. Had known that no one—no one in fifty years—would go looking for a woman named Thackeray in the records of a cathedral where the Thackerays were benefactors, because benefactors' names are furniture; you stop seeing them.

Except me. They hadn't counted on me, because they hadn't counted on me existing. Or—they had. One of my two correspondents had counted on exactly this: that I would pull the volume, find the name, feel the floor tilt. The confession letter said *winter of 1974*. The promise letter said *find what was written twice*. Between them, they had aimed me at this page like a hand aiming a lamp.

I should have felt manipulated. I did feel manipulated. But underneath the manipulation was the other thing, the thing I'm ashamed of even now: the thrill. For thirty-seven years I had been a footnote in other people's buildings, tending documents about strangers. And now the archive had turned around and shown me my own face in its glass—one entry, one line, one date—and I was in the record. I was *in* it. The vertigo and the belonging arrived in the same instant and I could not separate them, and I am not sure I want to.

* * *

Near midnight I climbed the spiral stair out of the undercroft and crossed the ambulatory to stand over the place where Bay VII lay beneath the flags. The cathedral at night is a different animal—all that stone breathing cold, the windows black, the pillars going up into dark like the legs of some patient animal. Tourists think cathedrals are about God. Archivists know better. Cathedrals are about time, and time keeps everything, including the things people pay to have it keep quietly.

"Nine feet down," I said aloud, to no one, because saying it made it more real and I needed it real. "You're nine feet down, or you're nowhere."

The flags said nothing. Flags are good at that.

But standing there, I became aware of a second silence inside the first—the particular quality of a building that contains a listening person. I turned. At the head of the ambulatory, half in the spill of light from the night desk, stood Dean Ambrose in his overcoat, keys in hand, clearly returned late from some engagement he'd rather not describe. He was looking at me with an expression I would spend the next several days learning to read: courteous, warm, and underneath it, watchful as a bird dog.

"Miss Thackeray," he said pleasantly. "Burning the midnight oil. Historical curiosities?"

And I heard myself answer, lightly, with a smile I did not feel:

"Just routine cataloguing, Dean."

It was the first lie I had told in years. It came out smooth as vellum, and it frightened me far more than the empty grave had.
