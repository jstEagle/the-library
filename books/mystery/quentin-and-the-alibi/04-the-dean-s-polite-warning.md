## 4. The Dean's Polite Warning

Dean Ambrose kept a decanter of sherry in his office for exactly two kinds of visitors: donors, and problems. He had long ago stopped pretending to himself that the categories were distinct.

Yara Thackeray was both.

He watched them come across the close from his window, that familiar hunched walk, shoulders drawn in like someone perpetually apologizing for taking up pavement. The archivist moved through the cathedral precincts the way water moves through limestone—present everywhere, visible nowhere, slowly dissolving the foundations of things. Ambrose had been dean for eleven years and he could count on one hand the number of times he'd seen Yara at a service, a meeting, a supper. And yet he knew, with the particular unease of a man who has inherited other men's secrets, that Yara spent more hours inside these walls than anyone alive.

The archive was under the Lady Chapel. That was the thing people forgot. Beneath the loveliest part of St. Aldhelm's—the candlelight and the carved stone and the choir loft where tourists gasped—there was a room where the cathedral kept everything it preferred not to look at. Baptismal registers going back four centuries. Mortuary rolls. Deeds, dispensations, letters of complaint from clergy dead two hundred years who had complained about precisely what clergy complain about now. And below all of it, down a flight of steps most maps didn't bother marking, the crypt.

Ambrose poured two glasses of sherry and set them on the low table by the fire, then thought better of it and removed one. No. Two. Kindness first; you caught more flies. He arranged himself behind his desk anyway—not to loom, he told himself, but because the desk was where the light was good, and he wanted to see Yara's face when the moment came.

The knock came at half past three, precise as a metronome.

"Dean." Yara stood in the doorway holding a folder against their chest like a shield. Grey cardigan, grey eyes, hair the colour of dust in a sunbeam. Ambrose had once heard a verger describe them as *the ghost who files*, and had laughed, and had not entirely enjoyed laughing.

"Thackeray. Come in, sit down. Sherry?"

"I don't—thank you, no."

"Nonsense." He pressed the glass into their hand anyway, noting how they held it without drinking, the way a hostage holds an object they've been told not to drop. "You've brought the Hollis inventory. Good. I confess I only asked for it as a pretext."

A flicker across the grey eyes. Not fear. Calculation. Ambrose found that interesting. He had expected the ghost to be startled by candour; instead the ghost was doing arithmetic.

"A pretext," Yara said.

"For asking you up here. For seeing your face." He smiled his parish smile, the one calibrated between warmth and authority, the one that had soothed three bishops and a hostile press officer. "You do extraordinary work down there, Thackeray. Extraordinary. And nobody thanks you. I intend to be somebody."

*Lie one,* said a small clear voice in Ambrose's head, the voice of the young curate he had been forty years ago in Liverpool, before ambition taught him that truth was a tool best used sparingly, like a chisel. *You asked them up here because Merridew telephoned.*

Merridew. The solicitor. The name arrived in Ambrose's mind wearing its usual smell of old money and older fear. The telephone call had come that morning, urbane as ever: *Julian, we understand there's been some activity in the archive. Some requests. Historical curiosities. We know how thorough your archivist is—we'd hate for thoroughness to become a burden.*

*We.* Always *we*. Ambrose had never met the full constellation of Thackerays, only their solicitor and their money, and this arrangement suited everyone, including, he suspected, the archivist currently sitting across from him not drinking sherry.

"The Hollis inventory is complete," Yara said. "Cross-referenced against the 1968 audit. There were discrepancies in the plate holdings—I've flagged them—but they predate my tenure and probably predate yours."

"Probably mine too." Ambrose laughed, warm as toast. Then, gently, the way you turn a key: "And what else have you been flagging lately?"

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them a door slammed in the cloister and Yara flinched—a small, involuntary contraction, the flinch of a creature that has lived alone too long among paper. Ambrose catalogued it with something uncomfortably close to tenderness.

"Nothing unusual," Yara said. "Routine requests."

"Mm." He rose, went to the window, gave them his back—the oldest trick in the confessor's book, making yourself easy to speak to because you aren't looking. "Do you know, when I came here eleven years ago, my predecessor gave me some advice about this place. He said: Julian, St. Aldhelm's is like an old ship. Beautiful on the water. But she has ballast, and the ballast is not for looking at."

"That's a strange thing to tell a dean."

"It's a strange cathedral." He turned back. "I'm telling you because I consider you a friend, Thackeray. An ally. This institution runs on people like you—quiet, meticulous, loyal to the records rather than the record-keepers. And I would hate—" he let the pause stretch, just enough, "—I would hate for some historical curiosity to cause you distress. These old families, these old disputes. They generate correspondence. Correspondence generates work. Work generates worry. You strike me as a man"—he saw the slight stiffening and adjusted without missing a beat—"as a person who has quite enough worry already."

He watched the word land. *Correspondence.* Watched Yara's fingers tighten around the untouched glass until the knuckles went pale.

"You've seen something," Yara said quietly. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite an accusation, and Ambrose understood suddenly that he had misjudged the ghost. There was steel down there in the stacks. There always had been; why else would the family pay attention?

"I've seen nothing," Ambrose said pleasantly. "That's rather my point."

*Lie two.* He had seen plenty. Eleven years ago, in his first month, a letter had arrived at the deanery in a brown envelope, no return address, postmarked Bristol. He remembered the date still—the feast of the Conversion of St. Paul, which had struck him even then as someone's idea of a joke. The handwriting had been careful, upright, almost beautiful. It had contained six sentences. Four of them were questions. One was an apology. The last was a warning: *Some doors are locked because what's behind them was never meant to be found. You are the doorkeeper now. Choose what kind.*

Ambrose had read it twice, gone cold in a warm room, and burned it in the grate of his lodgings before dawn, feeding it page by page—or rather sentence by sentence, since it was a single sheet—into the flames, watching the ink lift off the paper in little black ghosts before the paper itself went. He had told no one. He had accepted the endowment renewal that spring with a clear-ish conscience, telling himself that a cathedral with a leaking roof and a deficit served God better than a cathedral with clean hands and a collapsed nave. He had repeated this to himself for eleven years, and the repetition had almost worked.

Almost.

Because now there was a second letter somewhere—he was nearly certain of it—and it had gone not to the deanery but down those stairs to the archive, to the person least equipped to burn it and most likely to follow it.

"Dean?" Yara had set the glass down. "Why did you really ask me here?"

Ambrose considered his options the way he considered all difficult things: briefly, and in favour of charm. But something stopped him—something about the straightness of Yara's spine, or the arithmetic still running behind those grey eyes, or perhaps simply the accumulated weight of eleven years of burning. Confession, his training insisted, was good for the soul. His experience suggested it was excellent leverage.

"Because someone wrote to me once," he heard himself say. "Years ago. About this cathedral. About its... arrangements."

He watched the stillness take Yara completely, the way stillness takes prey animals—not calm, but listening with the whole body.

"What did it say?"

"That I should be careful what I go looking for." Ambrose smiled, and was surprised to find the smile cost him something. "I took the advice. I burned the letter. I never answered it. And in eleven years, nothing has fallen on my head. That is the entire content of my wisdom, Thackeray, and I give it to you freely: whatever curiosity has taken you, put it down. Curiosities in this building are never idle. They are baited."

"Baited," Yara repeated.

"Like a trap." He came around the desk, softened his voice, laid one hand—briefly, kindly—on the archivist's shoulder. He felt them not relax. "Whoever is writing letters wants something. When they get it, they will not need you anymore, and they will not be gentle about the discarding. I have watched this cathedral chew up gentler people than either of us. Let the dead keep their secrets. It's what they're for."

For a moment—one unguarded moment—Yara looked at him with such naked, terrible hope that Ambrose felt it like a blow. Hope that he might be an ally. Hope that the kindness was real. And underneath it, already forming, the beginning of the other thing: the understanding that a man who burns letters is not a man who protects people. A man who burns letters is a man protecting himself.

"You received a letter," Yara said slowly, "and your response was to destroy it. Without answering. Without asking anyone. Without—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—without wondering who wrote it, or what they wanted, or whether they were in danger."

"I wondered," Ambrose said. "I wondered very much. And then I wondered about the roof, and the organ fund, and the eighty souls on our payroll, and I made the calculation deans make." He spread his hands. "I am not proud of it. I am not offering it as a moral example. I am offering it as a map of the terrain. This building survives on buried things, Thackeray. Its beauty is load-bearing. You of all people must understand—you keep the burial records."

Something crossed Yara's face then that Ambrose couldn't read, and would think about later, much later, with the benefit of hindsight and scandal: not fear, not anger, but recognition. The look of someone hearing their own suspicion confirmed by the last person they'd wanted to confirm it.

"The Hollis inventory," Yara said at last, standing, retrieving the folder from their lap. "Is on your desk. If there's nothing else, Dean."

"There isn't." He walked them to the door, hand hovering near their elbow, not quite touching. "Thackeray. One more thing, freely given. If another letter comes—if anything else arrives that you can't account for—bring it to me first. Before you act on it. Promise me that."

Yara paused in the doorway. The corridor light behind them made a thin bright edge of their silhouette, and for a heartbeat Ambrose thought: *they're going to refuse, they're going to see right through me, the ghost is going to haunt me after all.*

"Of course, Dean," Yara said.

It was perfectly polite. It was absolutely convincing. And Ambrose, who had spent a career reading faces across negotiation tables and deathbeds, realized only after the door had closed that he hadn't believed a single word of it.

He returned to his desk and sat for a long time, looking at the sherry glass Yara hadn't drunk from, the faint grey fingerprint of dust on its bowl. Then he opened the bottom drawer, took out the box of matches he kept there for candles, and struck one—not to burn anything, just to watch the flame, to remind himself what it looked like, the small orange appetite that had eaten his courage eleven years ago and left him this: a charming man in a beautiful building, keeping the ballast down.

The match burned to his fingers. He dropped it in the empty glass, where it hissed out against the dust.

Downstairs, beneath the Lady Chapel, he knew, the archivist was already working. And upstairs, in the drawer of his desk, beneath the matches, lay the one thing he had never managed to burn: the brown envelope itself, kept against every resolution he'd ever made, because some part of Julian Ambrose had always known that a day would come when someone would ask him what the letter had said, and he would need to prove he'd read it.

He had lied to Yara about that. Lie three. The letter was not destroyed.

Only the words were.

* * *

In the archive, Yara stood over the mortuary rolls with the dean's voice still in their ears—*baited, like a trap*—and did not feel warned. They felt, for the first time in years, awake.

If the letter was bait, then something down here was worth catching.

They turned to the 1974 volume, and began.
