## 1. The Client Who Paid for Nothing

The rain had been coming down on Marlow Street since noon, and by four o'clock it had worked its way into the building through the gap above my window frame, leaving a brown stain shaped like a handprint on the plaster. I'd been staring at it for an hour. It was the kind of stain a normal person would forget by morning. I would not. In thirty years, if I lived that long, I would still be able to tell you the exact shape of that handprint — five fingers splayed, the thumb shorter than it should have been, a drip running from the ring finger like something bleeding.

That's the deal I made with my own head without ever signing anything. Everything stays. Nothing goes.

So when the elevator chimed at 4:17 and the doors opened on Ambrose Vane, I registered him the way I register everything: completely, involuntarily, forever.

He was old the way furniture is old — not broken, just finished. Tall once, folded now into a camel coat that had been expensive in a decade I could name. Silver hair combed with the precision of a man who'd been groomed by other people his whole life. His shoes were leather so soft they made no sound on my floorboards, which somehow bothered me more than anything else about him. Old money doesn't walk. It arrives.

"Mr. Coldharbour." His voice matched the shoes. Soft, worn smooth, no edges left. "I'm told you're the best."

"I'm told a lot of things." I didn't get up. Getting up is a negotiation tactic, and I hadn't decided yet whether I wanted this case or not. "You're Ambrose Vane. Vane Holdings, the shipping interests, the estate outside Corrow Bay."

Something flickered across his face — not surprise exactly. Recognition of recognition. People who've spent their lives being known find it unsettling when a stranger does it back.

"You know me," he said.

"I read the financial pages. You sold the last of the fleet in '09 and the papers called it the end of an era. They were wrong. The era ended when your father died; you just kept the office open out of habit." I gestured at the chair across from my desk. "Sit down, Mr. Vane. Tell me what you want."

He sat. He took his time with it, the way old men do, arranging himself like a man settling into a decision rather than a chair. Then he reached into his coat and produced an envelope — heavy stock, cream-colored, sealed with wax, because apparently we were doing this in the nineteenth century — and laid it on my desk between us.

"Before I open that," he said, "I want to ask you a question. Have you ever taken a case you couldn't believe?"

I thought about it honestly, which is my only setting. "Every case starts unbelievable. Husbands who swear they were home all night. Companies that swear they never knew about the leak. Believing comes later, if the evidence cooperates."

"And if the evidence can't cooperate? If the evidence hasn't happened yet?"

Outside, the rain picked up. I heard it change pitch against the glass, and some filing clerk in the back of my skull noted the time — 4:23 — and filed it away where I couldn't reach it to delete.

"Mr. Vane," I said, "what did you bring me?"

He leaned forward and slid the envelope toward me. "Open it."

I broke the wax seal. Inside were two documents. The first was a retainer agreement, already signed, already dated, with a figure in the payment line that made me do something I almost never do, which is stop breathing for a second. Not because I need the money — I do fine, better than fine, and I take maybe six cases a year precisely so I never have to take one I don't want. But that number wasn't a fee. It was a message. It said: *whatever this is, it's worth more than your curiosity costs.*

The second document was a life insurance policy.

I read insurance documents the way priests read scripture — slowly, looking for the heresy. This one was issued by Meridian Mutual, a solid, boring company I'd done work for twice. Policy number, insured party, beneficiary structure, all of it standard. Standard until the fourth line of the schedule of particulars, where the underwriter had entered, in clean typed characters, the anticipated date of death:

**21 December — Winter Solstice.**

This year. Three weeks out.

"This policy was written eleven months ago," I said. My voice came out flat, which is what it does when the rest of me is moving fast. "Meridian doesn't issue policies against scheduled deaths. There's no such product. Either this is forged, or—"

"Or someone convinced them it was legitimate," Vane said. "Look at the endorsements. Page three."

I turned to page three. There it was, tucked into the endorsement history like a splinter: a rider, executed eight months after issuance, amending the policy to reflect a *known and documented mortality date*, supported by an actuarial certificate, a physician's attestation, and — this was the part that made the back of my neck go cold — a certified copy of a family trust record confirming the date as *a matter of established record*.

Someone had walked into Meridian Mutual with paperwork so clean, so bureaucratically perfect, that the company had insured a man against a death they believed was already certain, priced accordingly, and gone to lunch.

"Who filed this?" I asked.

"The trust archivist. A Mr. Pell. He handles our family records. He's been with the family longer than I've been alive." Vane's hands were folded in his lap, and I noticed — the way I notice everything, whether I want to or not — that his knuckles were white inside their calm. "Mr. Coldharbour, I'm going to say something now, and I'd take it as a professional courtesy if you'd let me finish before you decide I'm mad."

"I decide people are mad for a living. Finish."

"My family keeps records. We have kept them for a hundred and forty years — births, marriages, deaths, everything, maintained in a private trust at Vane House. Sealed documents. Certified. And three weeks from tonight, according to those records, I die. The entry exists already. It's been in the archive for years — cause, hour, place, all of it written down, witnessed, sealed. And every prior entry of its kind in my family's history has come true."

The rain filled the silence for him. I let it.

"Four times," he went on, quieter. "My uncle. My cousin. My aunt. My grandfather's brother. Each one recorded before it happened. Each one dead exactly as described. And now there's an entry with my name on it, and an insurance policy built on top of it, and I have spent eleven months telling myself it's a coincidence, a ritual, a joke played by a dying family on itself. And then last month I found the marginalia in my uncle's file. Notes in the archivist's hand. Corrections." He looked up at me, and his eyes were dry but ancient. "You don't correct a prophecy, Mr. Coldharbour. You correct a ledger."

I set the policy down. I have a rule — I've had it since my first year in this business, and it has saved me more trouble than the retainer on my desk was worth: *never take a case about the future.* The future can't be investigated. It can't be interviewed, subpoenaed, or caught in a lie. Cases about the future are just fear wearing a briefcase.

But this wasn't a case about the future. That was the thing my mind had already seen and was patiently explaining to the rest of me while Vane watched me with the patience of a man who'd waited eleven months and could wait ten more minutes.

This was a case about paper. Paper has a past. Every document is a confession — of who touched it, when, why, what they were afraid of. Someone had forged an actuarial certificate. Someone had persuaded a physician to attest. Someone had carried a folder into Meridian Mutual and smiled at a clerk. All of that had already happened. All of it was sitting somewhere, waiting to be remembered by somebody.

And underneath the paperwork, cold and patient as the rain, was the simpler fact I hadn't said aloud yet: entries don't come true. People make them come true. If four members of the Vane family had died on schedule, then somewhere in that decaying house outside Corrow Bay was a person who had killed them — or arranged them — and filed the results. And that person had three weeks of work left.

"Prove my murder hasn't happened yet," Vane said. "That's the job. Absurd phrasing. I chose it deliberately. Because either the phrase is absurd and you'll refuse me, or it isn't, and you're the only man in this city who'll understand why it isn't."

I looked at the retainer again. I looked at the policy, at the typed certainty of **21 December**. Somewhere behind my eyes, unbidden, the image arrived: a page in a sealed archive, an old man's name, a winter date, a neat line of ink describing the end of him. Filed. Waiting. Patient as paper.

I felt it then — the flicker, quick and unwelcome, under the professional chill. Dread. Not of the case. Of the shape of it. Because I keep everything, Mr. Vane, I thought. Every face, every lie, every corpse I've ever stood over. And in three weeks, if you're wrong about hiring me in time, I will keep you too.

"Here's how I work," I said. "I don't investigate futures. I investigate records, and records live in the past. So I'm not taking a case about your death. I'm taking a case about the documents that predict it — who wrote them, who certified them, who profits. If someone is playing God with your family's archive, they've left fingerprints in the ink. Ink is the past. Ink I can do."

Vane exhaled. It was the sound of a man putting down something heavy he'd been carrying alone.

"One condition," I said. "You sign nothing else. No amendments, no codicils, no polite little papers anyone brings you. From tonight, your signature is a weapon someone wants to use. Keep it holstered."

"I haven't signed anything in eleven months," he said. "Not even birthday cards."

"Good. We'll start tomorrow. Nine a.m., I drive out to Corrow Bay, and you show me the archive."

He rose, slowly, and shook my hand. His grip was stronger than the rest of him suggested — a ship's grip, an old captain's hold. At the door he paused, half-turned, the rain silvering the window behind him.

"Mr. Coldharbour. The entry in the archive — it describes the weather, too. On the day I die." He almost smiled. "It says snow."

"It's been raining for six hours," I said. "Forecast says it won't stop until Thursday."

"Yes," Ambrose Vane said softly. "My uncle's entry predicted rain as well. Right down to the hour it stopped."

The elevator took him down. I sat for a long time in the gray light, the retainer on my desk and the policy beside it, and behind my eyes the whole meeting replayed itself — every word, every pause, the white knuckles, the soundless shoes — filed away, permanent, complete.

Then I got out a fresh notebook, wrote today's date at the top, and beneath it, in block letters I would be able to read verbatim for the rest of my life:

*Prove the murder hasn't happened yet. Find out who's keeping the books.*

Three weeks to the solstice. Outside, the rain went on falling, steady as a deadline.
