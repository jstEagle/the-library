## 1. The Prepaid Fare

The rain came down on Bellhaven like it had a grudge to settle, and August Thackeray sat in the dispatch office of Yellowbird Taxi with a cup of tea gone cold an hour ago and a crossword puzzle they had no intention of finishing.

Tuesday nights were the town's exhale. The weekend drunks were a memory, the Monday errands were done, and by eleven o'clock the only people who needed a cab were insomniacs, nurses coming off shift, and one retired schoolteacher who took a ride to the all-night diner every Tuesday just to sit somewhere that wasn't her kitchen. August knew all of this the way other people knew their own phone numbers. Three years on night dispatch would do that to you — not because August was nosy, but because staying invisible required knowing exactly what everyone else could see.

That was the whole trick of it. August Thackeray was a person the town had agreed, without ever discussing it, not to look at too closely. The dispatcher's voice on the radio: warm, competent, forgettable. The face behind the bulletproof glass at the pickup window: pleasant, unremarkable, already half-forgotten by the time you drove away. August had spent three years perfecting the art of being nobody, and the first rule of being nobody was simple: *don't leave marks.*

Which is why, when the phone rang at 11:47 p.m., August answered it the way they answered everything.

"Yellowbird Taxi, this is August. Where can we take you?"

The voice on the other end was young. That was the first thing. Young and trying very hard to sound older, the way people do when they've practiced a sentence in their head forty times before saying it out loud.

"I need — I'd like to book a fare. Prepaid."

"Happy to help. Pickup address?"

A pause. Long enough that August checked the line wasn't dead. Rain drummed against the office window like someone politely but persistently asking to be let in.

"Hollow Bridge," the voice said. "Three a.m."

August's pen, which had been idling little circles in the margin of the crossword, stopped.

Hollow Bridge was not a destination. Hollow Bridge was a place you *passed over* on Route 9, a narrow span of gray stone where the creek underneath ran fast and dark even in summer. Nobody got picked up there. There was no shoulder to wait on, no streetlight within a quarter mile, nothing but the bridge itself and the woods pressing close on either side. In three years of dispatch logs, August could count the Hollow Bridge entries on one hand, and every one of them had been a stranded motorist.

"Three a.m.," August repeated, keeping the voice level and pleasant. "At the bridge itself? There's not much room to pull over."

"I know." Another pause. "I'll be standing on the east side. By the railing."

"Can I get a name for the booking?"

Silence again. August heard breathing — quick, shallow, the kind of breathing that comes right before you do something you're afraid of.

"Wren," the voice said finally. "Just Wren."

"Just Wren," August echoed, and made themselves smile, though there was no one to see it. Smiling changed the shape of your voice; it was a trick learned early. "And where will we be taking you from the bridge, Wren?"

The question landed wrong. August felt it land wrong the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. Because the answer should have been instant — *the bus station, my sister's place, the airport* — and instead there was a silence so long and so freighted that August understood, with a cold slide of certainty, that Wren didn't have a destination. Wren had only an origin point. A place to be picked up from. As if the pickup itself were the entire plan.

"Away," Wren said at last. "Just... away. Before it happens."

August's hand went still on the desk. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the window.

"Before what happens?" August asked, gently, the way you'd ask someone if they'd left a stove on.

The line clicked. Not hung up — August knew the difference; a hang-up had a finality to it, a little death of the dial tone. This was a click, deliberate, like a door being closed softly by someone who didn't want to wake the house. Then dead air, and after ten seconds the automated tone telling them the caller had ended the call.

August sat back in the chair. The springs creaked their familiar complaint. On the desk, the crossword stared up with half its squares empty, and August looked at it without seeing any of it.

*Before it happens.*

Here was the thing about August Thackeray: they had built a life out of noticing things and then carefully, deliberately, forgetting them. It was a two-part skill, and the second part was harder than the first. Anyone could notice that Councilman Petrossian's cab rides always ended at the same house on Delmore Street, never his own. Anyone could notice which officers of the Bellhaven PD requested copies of dispatch records, or that certain calls got logged as "prank" when they very clearly weren't. Noticing was easy. The discipline was in the letting go — writing nothing down, remembering just enough to stay safe, and never, ever becoming a person who *knew something*, because people who knew something eventually became people who *said* something, and people who said something in Bellhaven had a way of having accidents.

So August noticed things and let them go, like releasing birds one by one from a cage, until the cage was empty and clean and no one could prove there had ever been birds at all.

But this call. This call had a weight to it that wouldn't release.

August got up, crossed the small office, and poured the cold tea down the sink. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the block, the diner sign buzzed its eternal pink hum. Everything was exactly as it always was, and yet the room felt different now, the way a room feels after someone has said your name in their sleep.

*Before it happens.* People said strange things on late-night calls. August had heard them all: the weeping, the drunk philosophizing, the woman who'd once called nine times in a row to ask if the driver could stop at the cemetery "just for a minute, I won't be long." Late-night words were cheap. They meant nothing most of the time.

But the trembling hadn't been drunk-trembling or grief-trembling. August knew those well enough. This had been fear with a deadline attached. Fear with a schedule. And nobody schedules their fear unless they know something concrete — a time, a place, an event — and are counting the hours until it arrives.

August returned to the desk. Sat down. Pulled the logbook toward themself.

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the road, small as anything, the kind of fork you don't even see until you're looking back at it years later. The rules of survival said: log the booking as routine. Prepaid fare, 3 a.m., Hollow Bridge, east side. Driver assignment pending. Done. A routine entry, boring as oatmeal, leaving no mark.

Instead, August found themself writing, in their small precise handwriting:

*11:47 p.m. — Prepaid booking received via phone. Caller self-identifies as "Wren." No surname given. No callback number offered (caller ID blocked). Pickup: Hollow Bridge, east side railing, 3:00 a.m. Destination: unspecified — caller stated "away." Caller declined follow-up questions. Voice assessed: young adult, male or female indeterminate, notable distress. Caller stated request was to be transported "before it happens." No further contact attempted.*

August read it back twice. It was, they realized, the most honest entry they'd written in three years. Every other line in every other logbook was a translation — real life rendered into harmless bureaucratic soup. This one was a photograph.

"You idiot," August murmured to themself, without heat. It was almost affectionate. Almost.

Because the truth was, some part of them — some stubborn, buried, badly-supervised part — had decided already. If Wren called back, if Wren showed up at 3 a.m. shivering under the east railing of Hollow Bridge, then somebody was going to be in that car besides a driver. Somebody was going to ask the right questions. Somebody was going to make sure "away" turned into somewhere safe. August had spent three years refusing to be that somebody, and apparently all it took was one trembling voice on a rainy Tuesday to undo the whole architecture.

Or maybe it wasn't undoing. Maybe it was just that the architecture had always had a door in it, and August had pretended not to see the door, and tonight someone had knocked.

August assigned the fare to Quentin Hale.

This required a moment of internal negotiation, because Quentin Hale was, by any rational measure, the worst possible choice. He asked questions. He *noticed*. He'd been a detective once, back before whatever happened happened — the whole town knew the outline of the story and none of the details, which in August's experience meant the details were bad — and he'd come back to Bellhaven driving nights for Yellowbird like a man serving out a sentence he'd imposed on himself. He was the only driver who read the notes section of the log entries. He was the only driver who'd ever called dispatch back to say, "You flagged this one quiet. Why?"

Most drivers saw the notes field as decoration. Quentin treated it like evidence.

But that was exactly why, wasn't it. If Wren was trouble — real trouble, the kind with teeth — August wanted a driver who'd recognize it. And if August was being honest, which was an activity they tried to limit to special occasions, they wanted a driver who'd notice if August themself started acting strange about this fare. A witness. An alibi. A...

August declined to finish that thought. Some sentences were better left unstarted.

They wrote the assignment into the log: *Driver: Q. Hale. Flagged: standard caution — remote pickup, prepaid, unverified client.* Then, after a pause, they added the note they'd regret or bless later, depending on how the night went:

*Client may be frightened rather than difficult. Please be kind.*

It was the kind of note no dispatcher wrote. It was the kind of note that left a mark.

Outside, the rain kept falling, steady and indifferent, working its way toward 3 a.m. the way water works its way toward the sea. August looked at the clock — 12:15 — did the arithmetic — two hours and forty-five minutes — and reached for the cold teapot out of pure habit before remembering they'd emptied it.

The phone rang at 1:03 a.m.

August picked up on the second ring, heart doing something unprofessional. "Yellowbird Taxi, this is August."

Static. Rain static, wind static, the sound of a phone held outdoors in weather. And beneath it, that same young voice, thinner now, stretched tight as wire:

"It moved," Wren whispered. "It wasn't supposed to happen tonight. You have to tell the driver — don't come to the bridge. Don't—"

The click again. Soft as a closing door.

August sat in the humming pink dark of the office, pen hovering over the logbook, and understood that whatever Wren had been afraid of had a schedule after all — and the schedule had just changed.

Against every rule they'd ever written for themself, August picked up the pen and noted that too.
