## 9. Hollow Bridge, 3 A.M.

The rain had stopped an hour before midnight, which Quentin took as a bad sign. In his experience the weather only cleared for two reasons: funerals and ambushes.

He stood in the shadow of Hollow Bridge's eastern abutment with Wren pressed against the cold stone beside him, both of them listening to August's voice come through the radio clipped to his collar — a small, warm thread of sound in all that dark.

"Dispatch to all units," August said, for the fourth time, each repetition pitched slightly differently, like a musician tuning toward the exact note. "We have a fare requesting pickup at Hollow Bridge, river side, three a.m. Client identifies as Wren. Repeat: Wren. Client states they have documentation regarding the Calloway drowning and wishes to deliver it to the county coroner's office at first light."

It was beautiful, really. Quentin had spent eleven years as a detective and never once heard anyone lie so honestly. Every word in August's broadcast was false except one — *Wren* — and that was the hook, baited with the killer's own scripture. Dr. Edwin Voss, former coroner, current ghost, had built his whole theater out of August's logs. So August had written him a final entry, alphabetized and filed, inviting him to attend his own closing argument.

"You're sure about this part?" Wren whispered. Their teeth were chattering, though not entirely from cold. They'd refused the blanket Quentin offered twice, then accepted it on the third offer without comment, which told him everything about what kind of witness they'd been for six years — proud, starved, and practiced at pretending otherwise.

"No," Quentin said. "But I'm sure about him."

"How can you be?"

"Because men who rehearse murders don't improvise well." He shifted his weight off his bad knee. "Voss has been performing the same show since 2019. Tonight he thinks he knows every line." He glanced down at Wren. "He doesn't know we changed the ending."

Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "You two have known each other how long? A week?"

"Nine days."

"And you're already finishing each other's felonies."

"That's usually how you know," Quentin said, and meant it more than he'd planned to.

In his ear, August kept talking to the empty night, steady as a lighthouse. *Dispatch to all units.* Quentin closed his eyes for half a second and let the voice hold him up. Nine days ago he'd been a man driving in circles around his own failures. Now he was crouched under a bridge waiting for a killer, and instead of fear he mostly felt — of all the ridiculous things — grateful.

Then the scanner in his pocket murmured, and every soft feeling in him went to iron.

A car door. Far off, on the far bank, but under the bridge's acoustics it carried like a gunshot. Then footsteps on gravel, unhurried, measured — the walk of a man who had arrived early to his own rehearsal many times before.

"Positions," August said quietly, off-channel now, just to him. "He's here."

* * *

Dr. Edwin Voss came down the river path carrying a black medical bag, because of course he did. Some men couldn't help their props. He was smaller than Quentin remembered from the trial coverage — a neat, gray, unremarkable man, the kind of face you forgot while looking directly at it. That was the trick of him, Quentin thought. The town had spent six years forgetting him on purpose.

"Hello, Wren," Voss called out, pleasant as a dentist. "I know you're there. Your dispatcher was very thorough. It's one of the things I've always admired about this company. Such careful records. Such a complete account of everyone's little movements." He set the bag down on the gravel. "I only ever wanted the town to keep records too. Accurate ones. Is that so monstrous?"

Quentin stepped out of the shadows with Wren behind him, and turned on his flashlight — not into Voss's eyes, but onto his own open palm, showing them empty. An old detective's courtesy.

"Evening, Doctor," Quentin said. "You're early."

"I'm punctual. There's a difference." Voss squinted at him. "Ah. Quentin Hale. The detective who couldn't prove what everyone knew." He smiled without warmth. "They gave your old desk to a woman with better handwriting."

"They gave my old desk to a plant," Quentin said. "But yes, also her."

"August Thackeray is on the radio right now," Voss went on, conversational, circling slightly, "telling some lovely story about documentation and first light. Do you know what I heard in that broadcast, Detective? I heard someone who read my script and thought they could write a better ending." His voice thinned. "Nobody writes a better ending. The drowning happened the way it happened. I merely corrected the paperwork."

"The paperwork said accidental," Wren said, stepping forward despite Quentin's arm. Their voice shook and held anyway. "I watched you hold him under. I was fourteen years old and I watched you kill Daniel Calloway, and then you looked across the water at me, and you wrote a report that said the river did it."

For a moment — just a moment — Voss's composure cracked, and underneath it Quentin saw something worse than rage. He saw relief. Six years of solitary performance, and here at last was his audience, his witness, his missing scene.

"There you are," Voss said softly. "Do you know how long I've waited for the second act?"

And that was when the second police cruiser's lights swept the far road — Marsh, arriving late and wrong, exactly as August had predicted — and Voss moved.

Not toward Wren. Toward Quentin's blind side, toward the bad knee, toward the bag he'd set down within reach of a man who'd walked this ground forty times in his head. The knife came out of the medical bag like it had always lived there, and Quentin got his forearm up instead of his throat, which was the entire difference between a long scar and a headline.

Pain opened white and total. He grabbed Voss's wrist with both hands and they went down together into the gravel, and Quentin learned in a very immediate way that a coroner knows exactly where everything important is kept. The knife point wandered toward his stomach with terrible patience, guided by a man who'd signed a hundred autopsy reports and knew precisely what he was shopping for.

"Hale!" Wren's voice. Gravel skittering. And then — absurdly, gloriously — Wren threw the blanket over Voss's head, the same blanket Quentin had offered three times, and the knife stroke went wild and opened Quentin's side instead of his gut.

Quentin rolled, elbow, weight, the move they teach you once and you pray you remember — and the knife skated away into the dark. Voss thrashed free of the wool and came up on his knees, breathing hard, hair wild, no longer a coroner at all. Just an old man kneeling by a river with nothing left in his hands.

That was when August's voice filled the night — not from the radio now but from everywhere, because August had driven the cab onto the near rise and keyed the external speaker, broadcasting to the bridge, the river, the arriving cruisers, the whole listening town:

"*Dispatch to all units. We have a confession in progress at Hollow Bridge. Speaker identifies himself as Edwin Voss, former county coroner. Go ahead, Doctor. Everyone's recording.*"

Voss froze. Looked up at the cab's silhouette on the rise. Looked at the red-and-blue wash beginning to strobe through the trees as Marsh's cruiser — and behind it, mercifully, two state troopers August must have summoned with whatever leverage she'd pried out of Marsh hours ago — came down the access road.

"You," Voss said to the darkness, to August, to the whole architecture of records he'd once loved. "You were supposed to be invisible."

"So were you," August's voice answered, and there was something in it Quentin had never heard before — not fear, not even anger. Just a person standing fully in the light, daring the world to look. "Turns out we're both bad at it."

Voss started to speak — Quentin would wonder forever what the first word was going to be — and then the troopers were on him, and it didn't matter anymore. The sentence finished itself in handcuffs and gravel and the flat procedural language of an arrest, and Quentin lay back against the cold stones and laughed until his ribs complained.

* * *

August found him ten minutes later, sitting in the open door of an ambulance while a paramedic taped his forearm and interrogated him about tetanus shots. She still had the radio in her hand. She looked like a woman setting down something enormous and discovering her arms still worked.

"Hey, driver," she said.

"Hey, dispatcher." He lifted the bandaged arm. "Your passenger requests a ride home."

"You're bleeding on my upholstery."

"It's a premium service."

She sat down next to him on the ambulance bumper, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them said anything. Across the water, the troopers were walking Voss to a car. Wren stood wrapped in the blanket again, giving a statement to a state investigator with a face like sunrise — six years of silence coming out of them in a long, steadying exhale.

"He confessed," August said finally. "On an open channel. Every unit, the scanner feeds, the whole nine yards. There's no version of this where it goes away."

"Yeah." Quentin flexed his fingers. They worked. Everything worked. It felt suspicious. "Marsh is going to try to claim he was running his own investigation."

"Let him. I have three years of logs that say otherwise." She paused. "Speaking of which."

He looked at her. She was staring at the river, at the spot where a boy had drowned and a report had lied and a witness had grown up hollowed out by it.

"After tonight," she said quietly, "that archive is evidence. Every secret in this town, subpoenaed and read aloud in a courtroom. The affairs, the bribes, the small rotten things people paid me to drive past. I keep thinking about what it does to them. To all of them." Her jaw tightened. "And I keep thinking about what it cost us to get here."

Quentin heard the shape of what was coming and felt his chest do something complicated.

"August—"

"And I also keep thinking," she went on, over him, "that I've spent three years making sure nobody could see me, and the first time I was ever actually visible was nine days ago, to a disgraced detective bleeding on my floor, and I'd like to know if that's—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "What happens tomorrow, Quentin?"

He looked at her — at the woman who'd baited a killer with her own voice and hadn't let it shake until now — and told her the truth.

"Tomorrow," he said, "the department calls me. Reinstatement, commendation, the works. And tomorrow night I'm going to stand in front of a mirror and practice saying no." He bumped her shoulder with his. "Because I hear the night shift here pays terribly and the dispatcher is impossible."

August laughed — wet-eyed, disbelieving, real. Then her radio crackled, and the dispatch channel that had been theirs alone for nine days carried a new voice, calm and unfamiliar, asking for a pickup at Hollow Bridge.

From a caller identifying itself as *Wren.*

Except Wren was fifty feet away, still talking to the troopers, and had not touched a phone.

August and Quentin looked at each other, and the peace drained slowly out of the morning.
