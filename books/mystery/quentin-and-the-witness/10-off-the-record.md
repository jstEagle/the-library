## 10. Off the Record

The confession tape ran eleven minutes and forty seconds. August knew because they'd listened to it four times, once for the words, twice for the voice, and a fourth time at two in the morning when sleep refused and the rain came back, softer now, like an apology.

Dr. Edwin Voss — former county coroner, twenty-two years retired, the man who had signed a drowning certificate with hands that had never touched water that night — talked the way men talk when they've rehearsed a speech their whole lives and finally found an audience. He recited dates. He recited tide tables. He recited, at one point, a dispatch log entry from March of the previous year, word for word, including August's own typo.

*Hollow Bridge. 3:04 a.m. No pickup. Caller did not answer callback.*

He'd said it like scripture, because to him it was. And then he'd said the rest of it — the drowning, the payment, the report he'd falsified because a man named Aldous Crane had asked him to, and because Voss had debts, and because nobody ever checks the dead man's paperwork. Nobody except a night dispatcher with three years of insomnia and a filing system built out of fear.

The tape went to the state police on Thursday. By Friday, Voss was in custody. By Saturday, so were Detective Marsh's superiors, who had spent a week being fed a false suspect by a killer who understood their systems better than they did. Marsh himself resigned before they could take his badge, which everyone agreed was the most dignity anyone involved had managed all month.

And Wren — whose real name turned out to be Marisol Vega, nineteen years old, who had been fourteen when she watched a man go into the reservoir and stayed silent because staying silent was the only survival skill anyone had ever taught her — gave her statement in a room with a victim's advocate and a window that actually opened. Quentin drove her there. August rode along. Nobody in that car spoke much, but nobody needed to.

* * *

The archive lived on a hard drive in a fireproof box under August's desk, backed up twice, labeled in August's handwriting as TAX RECORDS 2021–2024, which was even technically true.

Three years of nights. Three years of calls logged not as calls but as patterns — the councilman who booked rides to a house on Delmore Street every second Tuesday; the pharmacist whose "deliveries" ran after midnight; the judge who paid cash and never looked at the driver; the covered-up drowning, reconstructed call by call until August could have drawn the whole thing from memory. August had built it the way a child builds a wall of pillows against the dark: not to hurt anyone, just to make sure nothing could sneak up again.

Now the state investigator wanted copies. All of it. Everything relevant, and everything adjacent, and everything adjacent to the adjacent, because that was how investigations worked — they ate outward.

August sat at the dispatch desk on Sunday afternoon, the office empty except for the hum of the radio and the smell of cold coffee, and held the drive in one hand like something that might bite.

"You don't have to decide today," Quentin said from the doorway. He'd brought sandwiches. He'd been bringing sandwiches for six days, as if food were a language he was still learning to speak fluently.

"Yes, I do. That's what deciding is."

"Fair." He came in and sat on the edge of the desk, which was against about four company policies, none of which August had ever enforced. "For the record, I think you should give them everything. I'm an ex-cop. Completeness is my love language."

"I know." August turned the drive over. "That's exactly why I can't."

"The councilman—"

"Cheated on his wife with a schoolteacher and then voted for the crossing guards anyway. The pharmacist — half her after-midnight deliveries are insulin for people who can't afford it at the counter price. She rounds down. Every time." August heard their own voice go quiet. "I catalogued this town's sins for three years, Quentin. And the longer I look at the file, the more I notice that most of the sins are just people surviving badly. Voss wasn't in here because he was evil. He's in here because I noticed the wrong kind of pattern, and then a murderer used my noticing as a map."

"So delete it."

"I swore I'd never spend those secrets. Spending them is how we got Marsh manipulated, how Wren got taken—" August stopped. That thread still pulled tight in the chest. The night the killer came through the open channel, reciting logs verbatim while they listened helpless, and afterward Quentin had said *I've got you* in a voice that hadn't sounded like reassurance so much as surrender, and August had said *I know* back, and that had been that. Love, admitted mid-crisis, the way fires get reported. "Someone already got hurt because I kept this. Now someone else gets hurt if I hand it over. There's no clean option. There's just the one I can live with."

Quentin was quiet for a moment. Then he reached over, took the drive, and held it up to the light like a jeweler assessing a stone.

"State police already have the Voss tape," he said. "They have Marisol's statement. They have Crane's financials, which took them all of nine hours to pull. Your archive isn't evidence anymore, August. It's just memory." He set it back on the desk. "Memory's allowed to be merciful."

August deleted it at 4:17 p.m., which felt right — a shift-change hour, a between-time. The progress bar took eleven seconds. August watched every one of them.

Then they sat back, and the silence where three years of secrets used to be was enormous, and light, and terrifying, and fine.

"Huh," August said.

"Yeah?"

"I keep waiting to feel naked."

Quentin smiled. "Give it a minute."

* * *

The reinstatement offer came on Wednesday, in a manila envelope delivered by a lieutenant who kept glancing at Quentin's knife scar like it was a job posting.

Full restoration. Back pay considered. A commendation for "assistance rendered in the Vega matter," with a paragraph carefully worded so that no one would ever learn that the assistance included broadcasting a fabricated murder narrative over an open taxi channel to bait a confessed killer onto a bridge at three in the morning. The department wanted Quentin back. What the department did not want was the story of how.

Quentin read it twice at August's kitchen table, then folded it along its original creases and put it in his jacket pocket, where it stayed through dinner, through the dishes, through the long comfortable hour when neither of them talked and both of them listened to the rain start up again.

"You're going to burn it," August said finally.

"I'm going to burn it."

"You spent your whole career trying to get back to that room. The badge, the title, the—"

"I spent my whole career thinking that room was who I was." Quentin got up, opened the wood stove, and fed the envelope into the coals with the unhurried care of a man burning a letter rather than a future. It caught, curled, and was gone in under a minute. "Turns out I was just killing time between fares."

"That is the least romantic way anyone has ever described falling in love."

"And yet." He sat back down. His knee found August's under the table and stayed there. "You understand what this means. No pension. No shield. Just me, a cab, and a dispatcher who deletes things."

"A dispatcher," August corrected, "who is considering a career change. Somebody has to run the night desk properly now. Someone with a documented history of noticing things." They paused. "Also the owner offered me a partnership. Apparently solving a murder is good for business. We're fully booked through spring."

"Fully booked." Quentin shook his head, wonderingly. "Three years of keeping this town invisible, and now you're its favorite attraction."

"Not invisible." August looked out the window, at the wet street, at the streetlight doing its patient work on the dark. "Just — private. There's a difference. Took me a while to learn it."

* * *

They drove the last fare of the night together, because the schedule said one driver and neither of them cared. Hollow Bridge route, 2:50 a.m., a nurse heading home from the late shift who fell asleep in the back seat before they cleared Delmore Street.

The bridge was just a bridge now. Wet stone, black water, a single lamp with a moth auditioning for it. August watched it pass and felt — nothing sharp. Something healed-over, tender when pressed, but closed.

At the depot, Quentin parked, killed the engine, and didn't move to get out. Neither did August. The radio murmured static, that soft ocean sound, the sound of a channel with nothing urgent on it.

"Marisol called today," Quentin said. "She's enrolling in the community college. Wants to study forensic science."

"Of course she does."

"She asked if you'd proofread her application essay."

August laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised them on the way out. "Tell her yes. Tell her she doesn't need it, but yes."

The static breathed. Somewhere across town, a phone was ringing in an empty dispatch office, and tomorrow someone would answer it, and it would be an ordinary call, a ride to the airport or a forgotten grocery run, and that was the whole miracle of it: that most calls were ordinary. That August had spent three years forgetting that.

"Hey," Quentin said quietly.

August turned. In the dark of the cab, with the lamp-light coming through the windshield in pale bars, Quentin Hale looked at them the way he'd started looking at them somewhere around the night on the bridge — like something worth protecting, and also like something perfectly capable of protecting itself, and like both of those facts delighted him.

"I see you," he said. "You know that? Fully. Off the record, on the record, whatever record's left."

August reached over and laced their fingers through his. Outside, the rain kept up its gentle work on the town, washing nothing away, changing nothing, just making everything shine a little under the lights.

"I know," August said. "I've got you too."

The radio crackled once — a fare request, 3:04 a.m., pickup at Hollow Bridge.

They looked at each other.

"Nope," they said, in perfect unison, and drove home the long way, past all the sleeping houses, keeping the town's secrets by the simple mercy of never writing them down.
