## 1. The Grave That Kept Its Fare

The rain had been threatening since dawn, and by the time the backhoe's bucket broke the first layer of sod over Edmund Voss's grave, it had committed fully to the promise. Dominic Iversen stood beneath a black umbrella at the edge of the cemetery's older section, where the stones leaned like tired men waiting for a bus, and watched the water pool in the trench before anyone had dug deep enough to call it a grave.

They should have waited for better weather. Everyone said so. The funeral home director said so, wringing his hands the way he probably wrung the flowers. The groundskeeper said so, twice, once in English and once in the language of spitting. But Maren Voss had flown in from somewhere with better weather and worse manners, and she had said so in the opposite way—*now*—and when the founder's daughter said *now*, the town of Harlow Bend said *how high*.

Dominic had not wanted to be here. That was the first lie of the day, and Dominic catalogued it the way they catalogued everything: filed, timestamped, shelved. Twelve years of dispatch work did that to a person. You learned that every statement had a log entry behind it, and every log entry had a truth behind *that*, and most people never got past the first page.

The truth was that Dominic could not have stayed away. You don't send someone else to watch your own grave being opened.

Not your grave. Edmund Voss's grave. Keep the file straight.

The umbrella handle was cold and slightly tacky against their palm. Around them, the small audience of the exhumation arranged itself the way small audiences do: the sheriff's deputy with his hat dripping, the two men from the funeral home in rain-slick suits, the backhoe operator who kept his engine running because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant looking at the sky. Maren Voss stood closest to the trench in a coat that cost more than a taxi's annual insurance, her hair dark under the rain, refusing the umbrella a man held over her like he was being paid to be ignored.

And Dominic stood apart, because Dominic always stood apart, in the gray space between witness and furniture. That was the trick of invisibility. It wasn't that people didn't see you. It was that they saw you and their eyes slid off, the way water slid off the waxed hood of a cab. Dominic had spent a lifetime learning the angle of that slide.

"Careful," the funeral home director said, uselessly, as the bucket came down again. "Careful with—"

"It's a box in the ground, Gerald," Maren Voss said. Her voice carried flat and clear over the rain. "It's been in the ground twelve years. It knows what weather is."

Dominic's stomach did something small and mechanical at the number. Twelve years. The bucket bit down through clay.

*Twelve years*, and the date had been sitting in Dominic's chest since Maren filed the petition, since the court order came through with its official letterhead and its official date, since Dominic read the exhumation date and felt the floor of their ordinary life tilt one degree—not enough to fall, just enough to know the room was no longer level. Because the date was not just a date. The date was the night of the call.

*The call.* As if it were a single object, a stone in the shoe. Dominic had spent twelve years not thinking about it with the discipline of a monk, and the discipline had worked the way discipline works, which is to say it had worked right up until it hadn't.

Rain ticked against the umbrella. Somewhere beyond the cemetery fence, a Voss Taxi cab idled at the light on Corliss Street, its roof light a soft amber smudge in the gray. Dominic knew it was there without looking. Dominic knew where every cab in Harlow Bend was, had known for twelve years, the way a spider knows its web—not by watching every thread but by being the thing the threads were strung from. Dispatch was like that. You sat in the chair and the town spoke to you, one voice at a time, and you spoke back, and between the voices you kept the map of everything moving.

The bucket came up with clay and then, all at once, with less clay. The operator killed the engine. The silence after was enormous, filled only with rain on plastic and rain on satin and rain on the tarp they'd laid beside the trench.

"Hand shovels," the deputy said. "From here."

The men climbed down into the hole. Dominic watched their shoulders work. There is a particular posture people take when digging up something they'd rather not find, Dominic thought—a hunching, a keeping of the head low, as if the thing below might notice them. The rain ran off the brim of the deputy's hat in a steady thread.

Maren Voss had not moved. She stood at the lip of the trench with her hands in her coat pockets and her chin up, and Dominic studied her the way one studies a fare who's given an address they don't quite believe: not suspicious, exactly. Attentive. The daughter had demanded this exhumation on the strength of an anonymous letter, the town gossip said—something delivered to her hotel in an envelope with no return address. The town gossip said a great many things. The town gossip was, in Dominic's professional assessment, the only dispatch channel that had never once gone quiet.

The shovel struck something that wasn't soil. The sound carried—a dull knock, wood on wood, the coffin lid answering.

"Okay," the director said. "Okay. Okay."

They worked the lid free in the way of men who have done this before but not often enough to make it easy, and the rain fell directly into the opened coffin, and everyone leaned in, and everyone went still.

Dominic went still a half-second before they did. Not because Dominic saw it first. Because Dominic had known, had known since the court order arrived, in the way you know a fare is going to be trouble before he says a word—the set of his shoulders, the way he counts his money. You know. You log it. You drive anyway.

The coffin was empty.

Not disturbed. Not robbed. *Empty*—lined in its rotted satin, its pillow of decayed lace, its brass handles green with twelve years of earth, and holding nothing at all. No Edmund Voss. No remains. Not even the honest indignity of bones. The rain pooled in the bottom of it, filling the shape of a man who had never been put there, and the water lay flat and gray and reflective, and for one absurd moment Dominic had the vertiginous sense of looking into a mirror laid in the ground.

Someone made a sound. The director, maybe. A small syllable of professional ruin.

Maren Voss did not make a sound. She looked down into the coffin the way a person looks at a bill they've been expecting—confirmation, not surprise—and then her head turned, slowly, and her eyes moved across the small circle of onlookers with a thoroughness that reminded Dominic, unpleasantly, of a dispatcher logging calls. Taking names. Marking times.

Her eyes passed over Dominic and stopped.

They did not slide off. That was the thing. Everyone's eyes slid off Dominic; it was a law of nature, tested daily, never once repealed. Maren Voss's eyes stopped on Dominic the way a cab stops at a corner—deliberately, engine running, waiting to see who comes out of the building.

Dominic gave her nothing. Twelve years of practice gave them that much: the face of professional detachment, the dispatcher's mask, the expression of a person who is here because the company sent a representative, because Voss Taxi was the deceased's own company and someone from the office ought to attend, and Dominic was the one who attended things. Dominic was very good at being the one who attended things. It was the whole architecture of their life.

"Twelve years," Maren Voss said, to no one, to everyone. Her voice was steady in a way that cost her something; Dominic could hear the cost under it, the way you can hear a bad universal joint under a smooth-sounding engine if you've spent enough nights listening. "Twelve years, and you people buried an empty box and patted yourselves on the back."

"Maren," the director began, "if we could all just—there are procedures—"

"There are procedures," she agreed. "There are always procedures. That's rather the point, isn't it. Someone followed the procedures, and the procedures produced this." She looked back at the coffin, at the rain filling it. "Who was here? Twelve years ago. Who handled the burial?"

Silence, the special kind that funerals and exhumations produce, in which everyone is calculating how much truth the moment can hold.

"I was," Dominic heard themself say.

It came out level. It came out calm. Inside, behind the mask, something with cold hands turned a page.

* * *

The date was October 9th.

Dominic had not needed the court order to tell them that. The date lived in them now the way a scar lives in the body—not remembered so much as *weathered with*, aching before storms. October 9th, twelve years ago, a Tuesday, the night shift, 11:47 p.m., when the dispatch phone rang and a voice that did not give its name asked for a cab at the service gate of the Voss estate, no lights, cash only, and Dominic—twenty-two years old, three years on the desk, still learning the trade from a man named Aldous Reeve who taught filing like scripture—took the call.

Took the call, logged the call, dispatched the call. And then, at some hour past two in the morning, with the rain coming down the same way it was coming down now, took the log page and erased the call.

Not tore. Erased. There was a difference, and Aldous Reeve had taught them that too: *You never tear a page, kid. Tearing leaves an edge. You make the page clean. You make it look like the minute never happened.* Dominic had not asked, that night, why. That was the part that lived deepest, the part the rain could not reach: not the call, not the erasure, but the not-asking. The willingness. The way a seventeen-year-old runaway who'd been given a cot above the garage and a chair at the drivers' table learns that gratitude has a currency, and the exchange rate is never posted.

Aldous Reeve had stood beside them while they did it. Had put one hand on their shoulder, light as a bird landing, and said, *Good. Now it's done, and it never was.*

And then, three weeks later, Aldous Reeve was gone. Left no note, left his house, left his coffee mug on the desk with a ring still in the bottom. The company said he'd moved on. The drivers said he'd moved on. Dominic had spent twelve years not asking that question either, and the two not-askings had grown together like roots under a sidewalk, and now the sidewalk was lifting.

The rain fell into the empty coffin. The deputy was on his radio, and his voice had the clipped quality of a man who has just discovered that his afternoon has become his year.

* * *

They let the onlookers go, eventually. There was nothing to see. That was the whole problem; there was nothing to see, and therefore there was everything to see, and the cemetery was suddenly a crime scene with yellow tape and a sheriff's SUV grinding up the wet gravel with its light bar throwing blue smears across the rain.

Dominic walked. Not toward their car, at first—toward the fence line, the long view, the place where the cemetery's older stones gave way to weeds and the town beyond was a watercolor of roofs and wires. The umbrella had become a liability; Dominic folded it and let the rain hit, cold and businesslike, flattening their hair against their skull. Let it. The rain was honest. The rain logged itself.

Twelve years of professional detachment. That was the phrase Dominic reached for, the way a driver reaching for the visor finds the registration where it always is. *Professional detachment.* It had served through a hundred small corruptions—the fares that never got logged, the names that got softened, the nights Aldous Reeve's ledgers balanced a little too beautifully. Detachment was the job. You could not dispatch a town's secrets and stay soaked in them. You kept the map dry.

But the map was wet now. The coffin was empty, and empty coffins do not stay private; they metastasize. By morning there would be a reporter from the county paper. By noon, the sheriff would want records. Dominic knew this the way they knew a fare was going to dispute the meter: not prediction, just pattern. Sheriff Okonkwo was a careful woman, and careful women follow paper. Paper was Dominic's country. Paper was where Dominic had lived, invisible, for twelve years—and paper was where the erased minute waited, patient as a fare who knows you'll come.

The cold that moved through Dominic then was not fear exactly. Fear is hot; it runs. This was colder and slower, a settling, like the temperature dropping in a cab when the heater quits on the far side of town. It was the cold of arithmetic. Someone had put an empty coffin in the ground twelve years ago, and someone had known it was empty, and someone had let the town hold a funeral for a box. And now the box was open, and the town was looking in, and the first place every looking eye would go was the dispatch logs.

The logs Dominic kept. The logs Dominic had cleaned.

Dominic stood at the fence line with the rain running off their jaw and did what they had always done, the thing Aldous had drilled into them across a hundred midnight shifts: they ran the board. *Who's moving. Who's watching. What's the next call.* Maren Voss, at the graveside, logging faces. Okonkwo, inbound, logging tape. The drivers—Ray, and the others, the family, God help them, the family who had given a runaway kid a bed and a name and never asked the price—each of them a thread, each of them now attached to the same trembling web.

And somewhere out there, past the fence, past the watercolor roofs, whoever had mailed Maren Voss a dead man's cufflink was watching the rain fall into an empty coffin, and watching it with interest.

Dominic turned up Corliss Street in the wet dark, past the office with its amber sign, its lit window, its chair. The chair was the thing. The chair had always been the thing. Whoever sat in that chair knew the town's whole moving map, knew every fare, every silence, every minute that never happened.

Twelve years ago, someone else had sat in it.

Dominic unlocked the office door, hung their wet coat on the hook like any other night, and stood a long moment in the doorway, listening to the rain on the roof and the radio's idle hiss—the sound of a channel waiting to be spoken into.

Then they crossed to the file room, knelt at the bottom drawer, and pulled the October box from twelve years ago.

The folder was there. The pages were there. The log ran 11:31, 11:39, 11:52—clean, continuous, a night with no gap at all, a night in which nothing had ever happened.

And clipped inside the cover, where no clip had ever been, was a single sheet of yellow legal paper, folded once, in handwriting Dominic knew better than their own.

*Hello, kid,* it said. *Told you never to tear a page.*
