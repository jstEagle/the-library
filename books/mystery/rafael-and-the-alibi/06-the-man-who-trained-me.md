## 6. The Man Who Trained Me

The house at the end of Corliss Street had the look of a held breath.

Dominic had driven past it a hundred times over twelve years without stopping—out of habit, out of something they refused to name—and each time the house had looked the same: shutters drawn, gutters sagging under a decade of pine needles, the mailbox listing at an angle that said *nobody home, nobody coming home*. It was a house that had been allowed to fall out of the world gently, the way things fall when no one is watching. Dominic had always found a strange comfort in that. A house that kept its silence. A house that understood the job.

Tonight, Dominic stopped the car.

The rain had moved off an hour ago, leaving the street washed and gleaming under the sodium lamps, every puddle holding a small trembling copy of the streetlight. Somewhere a dog was reporting the weather to another dog. The kind of night, Aldous Reeve used to say, when the radio gets honest—when people call not because they need a cab but because they need a voice.

*You always answer, kid. Even when there's nothing to say. Especially then.*

Dominic sat with both hands on the wheel and looked at the house and did not get out for a long time.

Because here was the thing Dominic had not said aloud, not to Okonkwo, not to Maren Voss, not to the drivers at the coffee-stained table in the break room: the empty coffin had a shape, and the shape was familiar. A box meant to hold a man, holding nothing. A record meant to hold a night, holding a gap. Dominic had spent twelve years being the gap. And now the gap was out in the open air, under floodlights at the cemetery, being photographed by people who did not know what they were looking at.

But Dominic knew. And knowing had a gravity. It pulled.

It pulled toward Corliss Street.

* * *

Aldous Reeve had taught Dominic the filing system the way other men taught their children to pray—by repetition, by rote, by a kind of tender tyranny.

*Fare cards first, alphabetical by surname, then chronological within the letter. Radio logs in triplicate, the pink copy to the box, the yellow to the drawer, the white to you. And the white copy, kid, the white copy is the one that never lies, because it's the one nobody ever asks for.*

Dominic had been seventeen, three weeks off a Greyhound with a duffel bag and a fake confidence, sleeping in the back of cab 14 because Ray Calvo had looked at them across the dispatch counter and said, *You look like somebody who needs a job more than a story,* and hadn't asked for either. The night dispatcher before Dominic had quit mid-shift—just stood up, hung his headset on the peg, and walked out into the rain—and Aldous had slid into the chair beside Dominic like a man taking a seat that had always been his.

"Can you alphabetize?" he'd asked.

"Yes."

"Can you keep your mouth shut?"

Dominic had hesitated, and Aldous had smiled—warm, crinkle-eyed, a face like a retired teacher's—and said, "Good. Hesitating means you know the value of a thing. The ones who answer too fast, they give everything away. Now. Fare cards first."

For six years, Aldous Reeve had been the closest thing Dominic had to a map. He taught them the town the way dispatchers know towns—not by landmarks but by phone numbers, by the voices attached to them. *That's Mrs. Halloran on Birch, she calls every Friday at ten, don't let Ray take her, he drives too fast and she pretends to hate it but she doesn't. That's the quarry office, they only call when somebody's hurt, so when you hear that number, wake up all the way.*

*Wake up all the way.* Dominic still said it to themselves at the start of every shift.

And then, in the autumn twelve years ago, in the same month that held the call Dominic had erased—the call with the driver's voice and the location and the ninety seconds of silence—Aldous Reeve had cleaned out his desk.

No farewell party. No note. He'd left the chair pushed in, the headset hung on its peg, and a tin of peppermints in the top drawer, and he'd walked out of Voss Taxi the way the other dispatcher had walked out years before, and this time Dominic had been the one left holding the headset, wondering if the chair was cursed or if the town simply ate its dispatchers one at a time.

Dominic had filed his departure the only way they knew how. Neatly. Alphabetically. Under R.

And had never once said his name out loud since.

* * *

The gate hinge made a sound like a question.

Dominic stood at the edge of the yard, and the yard was wrong. That was the first thing. Twelve years of pine needles should have made a soft brown carpet, should have swallowed footfalls and forgotten them, but the walkway from gate to porch was swept. Swept recently—there were bristle marks in the thin film of new-fallen needles, a broom's signature, days old at most. And the flowerbed under the front window, which Dominic remembered as a graveyard of plastic geraniums Aldous's late wife had left behind, was turned. Dark soil, fresh-turned, holding the smell of it even through the damp: that black, mineral, living smell of earth that has been opened.

Somebody was gardening at a dead man's house.

Dominic's body made the decision before the rest of them did. They moved along the side of the house, past the rain barrel with its lid of floating oak leaves, to the kitchen window—and stopped, because the kitchen window had a curtain. It had never had a curtain. Aldous had kept that window bare for thirty years so he could see the drive while he drank his morning coffee; he'd said a dispatcher's eyes should always have a runway. Now there was a curtain, a cheap one, a hotel curtain, the kind that comes folded in plastic and smells of other people's sleep.

Dominic stood very still in the wet grass and listened the way Aldous had taught them to listen—not for sounds but for the absence of them. The particular silence of a house that is empty has a texture. It hums. It settles. This silence had a held quality, a *listening-back* quality, like the ninety seconds on the erased call, like a channel open with no one keying the mic but everyone, somehow, on the line.

They went around to the back door.

It was locked. It was also, Dominic noted with a cold, precise, professional part of the mind that never stopped taking dictation, recently locked—the deadbolt's throat gleaming with oil, a single bright drop of it on the strike plate like a bead of mercury. Rust does not get oiled. Abandoned houses do not get oiled. And the storm door, when Dominic tried it with one gloved finger, swung on hinges that whispered rather than screamed.

*Somebody got here first.* The thought arrived with the same vertigo as the missing backup tape, the same sick drop, as if the floor of the world had a trapdoor in it and Dominic had been standing on the seam all along. First to the archive. First to the grave. First to the house. There was a pattern taking shape, and the shape of it was a person—one step ahead, always, patient as filing, thorough as triplicate.

Dominic should have left. Every instinct that had kept them invisible for twelve years said *leave, leave, you were never here, sweep your own footprints.* But the back porch had a chair on it, and the chair had a blanket folded over its arm, and the blanket was wool, army-green, and Dominic knew that blanket. Had slept under it once, feverish, nineteen years old, while Aldous sat up all night reading radio logs aloud in a low voice like a lullaby, because he said the numbers would keep the bad dreams in order.

Loyalty, Dominic thought, standing on that porch with the rain starting again, fine as mist, beading on their jacket. Loyalty is a debt. And debts, in this town, in this business, accrue.

They broke in.

It was almost embarrassingly easy—a credit card Aldous himself had shown them how to use, years ago, laughing, *in case you ever lock yourself out, kid, and never for any other reason, you hear me?* The tongue of the lock gave with a click that sounded enormous in the wet dark.

Inside, the house was warm.

Not warm the way old houses hold warmth, stubbornly, out of spite. Warm the way a house is warm when heat has been running in it. The air smelled of coffee—not old coffee, not the ghost of coffee, but coffee, recent, its oils still hanging in the room—and under the coffee, faintly, tobacco. Aldous had quit smoking the year Dominic started. Or said he had.

Dominic did not turn on the lights. They stood in the kitchen with the curtain drawn against the world and let their eyes adjust, and the kitchen assembled itself out of the gloom: the table cleared and wiped, one mug in the drying rack, upside down, still beaded with water. One mug. One chair pulled slightly from the table, at the angle of a man who had just stood up.

On the table, in the exact center, the way Aldous had taught them to place fare cards for a driver's morning pickup, sat a single sheet of paper.

Dominic looked at it for a long time before touching it.

It was a radio log page. Old stock, the cream-colored triplicate paper the company hadn't used in a decade, the kind with the faint pink and yellow ghosts of the copies beneath. It was dated twelve years back—to the week. The entries were in Aldous's hand, that beautiful, patient, schoolmaster's cursive, and they were ordinary: a fare to the hospital, a fare to the Legion hall, a fare to the quarry road.

The quarry road.

And beneath the last entry, in the same cursive, in ink the same age as everything else—or so it wanted to look—was a line that had not been in any log Dominic had ever filed, any log they had memorized in the long insomniac years of reconstructing that night:

*22:41 — Return fare, quarry rd. Passenger: A. Reeve. No fare collected.*

Dominic read it four times. The rain found a rhythm on the roof, patient, metronomic, like someone tapping a pen on a counter, waiting.

A planted page. It had to be a planted page—left out on a table in a house that was warm, in a house that had been swept, oiled, curtained, *prepared*, the way a stage is prepared. Someone wanted this page found. Someone wanted it found by whoever came looking, and Dominic understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they had been meant to come looking. That the swept walkway was an invitation. That the whole house was a log entry, and the entry read: *come in, kid, sit down, I've been expecting you.*

Because that was the other thing. The page was not evidence against Aldous. It was evidence *by* Aldous—a page in his hand, placing him at the quarry on the night in question, a confession-shaped thing, or an accusation-shaped thing, or a trap-shaped thing, and Dominic could not tell which, and that was the cruelest part. Aldous Reeve had taught them that a good log entry answers three questions: who, where, when. This page answered all three and still told them nothing, because it was a page written by a man who had spent his whole career teaching one student that paper lies beautifully when the hand behind it is patient enough.

Dominic took out their phone and photographed it—flash off, three exposures, the way they'd photograph anything they might someday have to testify about—and then, hands steady in the way that cost something, they set it back exactly where it had been. Center of the table. Angle true.

And that was when they saw the second thing.

The mug in the drying rack was not just wet. It was a company mug—white, heavy, chipped at the handle—and printed on it in the old logo, the one retired before Dominic ever arrived, were the words VOSS TAXI: *ANYWHERE IN THE VALLEY, ANY HOUR.* And beneath the logo, hand-painted in small careful letters, faded but legible, a name.

*A. REEVE.*

His mug. His actual mug, from the dispatch desk, that had vanished with him, that Dominic had privately mourned along with everything else. It was here. It had been used. It was drying on a rack in a house that was supposed to be a shrine to absence, and it was warm-adjacent, and the chair was pulled out at the angle of a man who had just stood up, and somewhere in the walls or the dark or the twelve years, Aldous Reeve was close enough that his coffee was still wet.

Dominic left the way they'd come, carding the lock shut, wiping the storm door, sweeping the walkway bristles smooth behind them with a pine bough, restoring the house to its held breath. Professional. Invisible. All the old training holding like a wall against the sea.

Then they sat in the car at the curb and let the shaking come, and it came quietly, the way everything in Dominic's life came quietly.

Because the planted page was not a confession. Dominic turned it over in their mind, entry by entry, the way Aldous had taught them to audit a shift, and saw it now for what it was: a message. *I was there.* Not *I did it.* Not *I didn't.* Just *I was there, and I can write myself into any night I choose, and so can you, and so can anyone, and you know exactly how, because I'm the one who taught you.*

The rain thickened. The streetlight shivered in its puddle.

Dominic thought of the backup tape, missing from an archive only three people had ever had the key to. Thought of the empty coffin, handled by two sets of hands. Thought of the warm kitchen, the one mug, the pulled chair. Thought: *he's not hiding from me. He's been waiting for me. He's been reading my whole life like a log.*

And then the radio in the car—the company radio, the old channel, the one the drivers joked was haunted—crackled.

Not static. Not the drivers.

A voice, low and warm and crinkle-eyed even through the compression, a voice Dominic had spent twelve years not saying aloud, keyed the retired dispatch channel and used a call sign that had been dead for a decade, a call sign that had once belonged to the night shift, to the chair, to the man who taught them everything:

*"Dispatch to Cab One. Come in, kid. You can stop sweeping the walkway. I saw your headlights from the kitchen."*

* * *

Dominic did not answer. Their hand hovered over the mic and did not move, because some part of them understood that the moment they keyed that channel, the moment their voice went out over the air, the twelve years of invisibility would end—not in the way they'd always feared, exposed and ruined, but in the way Aldous had always intended: *found.*

The voice waited. It was very good at waiting. It had, after all, been trained by no one; it had done the training.

*"I left the page for you,"* it said gently. *"I knew you'd audit it. You always were my best student. Go home, kid. Get some sleep. Wake up all the way tomorrow."*

A click. The channel closed. The rain came down on Corliss Street like static, like static, like static, and Dominic sat in the dark with their heart going like a struck bell, knowing two things with terrible certainty: that Aldous Reeve was alive, near, and had been watching them for twelve years—

—and that tomorrow morning, Sheriff Okonkwo was expecting the old dispatch logs on her desk by nine.
