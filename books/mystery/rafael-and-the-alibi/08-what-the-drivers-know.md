## 8. What the Drivers Know

The rain had stopped by morning, but the depot still smelled of it—wet wool and old asphalt, the particular damp that lived in the cinder-block walls of Voss Taxi no matter what season it was. Dominic arrived at six, as they always did, and found the break room already occupied: Ray Calvo at the corner table with his coffee going cold in front of him, Sal Ferreira asleep upright in the vinyl chair by the radiator, his cap tipped over his eyes like a man pretending the world had ended and he'd rather not watch.

Dominic stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary. Twelve years of mornings had taught them to read this room the way other people read weather. Today the pressure was wrong. The air held the flat, waiting quality of a dial tone.

"Morning," Dominic said.

Ray didn't answer. He was fifty-eight now, gray coming in at the temples like frost on a windshield, hands wrapped around his mug as if it were something he'd been asked to guard. His knuckles were scarred from thirty years of doors and fenders and one memorable fistfight outside the Elks lodge in 1999. Those hands were not steady this morning.

"Ray."

"Hm." He looked up, and for half a second—half a second, no more—there was something in his face that Dominic had never seen there before. Not guilt exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who hears his own name called from a room he thought was empty.

"Coffee's fresh," he said finally, and looked back down at his mug.

* * *

Dominic had come to Voss Taxi at seventeen with nothing but a duffel bag and a name nobody could pronounce properly, and the drivers had fed them, housed them, taught them to drive in the cemetery lot where the only thing you could hit was the dead. They had never once asked where Dominic came from. That was the covenant: *we don't ask, we don't tell, we keep each other.* It had felt like grace for a long time. It had taken twelve years and an empty coffin for Dominic to understand that the same covenant cut both ways—that a family built on not-asking is also a family that cannot be asked.

Now the sheriff wanted statements, and the family had closed like a hand around a coin.

Dominic spent the morning doing what Dominic always did: listening. The dispatch desk sat behind a half-wall of frosted glass in the center of the depot, and every conversation in the building passed through it eventually, the way water passes through limestone, leaving traces.

At seven-fifteen, Marisol Vega came in early for her shift, which she never did. She hung her coat on the hook by the door, took it down, hung it again. She was forty-one, precise, the kind of driver who logged her mileage to the tenth of a mile. When she passed the dispatch window she didn't do her usual two-finger salute off her brow. She kept her eyes forward, jaw set, and went straight to the board to check her runs.

"You hear about the exhumation?" Teddy Brandt said from the bench where he was lacing his boots. Teddy was twenty-six, the youngest driver, hired three years ago, innocent of everything except an appetite for gossip that bordered on pathology.

"I hear enough," Marisol said.

"They dug him up and the box was empty, Ted. Empty. Like a—" He made a gesture, palms up, expanding. "Like a takeout container somebody already ate out of."

"Teddy." Sal's voice came from under his cap, gravelly, without moving otherwise. "Some things you don't joke about in this building."

"It's not a joke, it's news—"

"In *this building,*" Sal repeated, and lifted the cap an inch. One eye found Teddy and held it. The eye was bloodshot. It had been bloodshot for days, Dominic noticed. Sal Ferreira had slept maybe four hours a night since the letter about the exhumation ran in the *Courier*, and he wore the deficit like a uniform.

Teddy shut up. Marisol checked her runs twice. And Dominic sat behind the glass with their heart going slow and heavy, cataloguing it all, because cataloguing was what they knew how to do.

* * *

The fragments had been arriving for years, actually. Dominic understood that now. They had simply never assembled them, the way you can live beside a river your whole life and never once think about where it goes.

There was the night in October—twelve years ago, though Dominic hadn't marked it at the time—when Ray had come back from a run at nearly four in the morning, long after last call, long after anything legitimate, and washed his car himself in the bay, at night, with the hose running so hard it drowned out whatever else he might have been doing in there. Dominic had been sleeping in the cot room behind dispatch, seventeen years old and used to the sounds of the building, and had registered only the water, and gone back to sleep.

There was the jacket. Sal had a habit, every first warm day of April, of burning brush behind his place out on Kettle Road, and one year—Dominic couldn't have said which year until recently—he'd thrown something onto the fire that wasn't brush. Something that smoked black and stank of melted synthetic fiber, and when Sal's wife, Luanne, had asked what it was, Sal had said *an old coat* in a voice that ended the conversation. Luanne told the story at the depot Christmas party once, laughing, calling him a sentimental fool who couldn't throw anything away. Sal hadn't laughed. Dominic remembered that now with terrible clarity: Sal hadn't laughed, and had refilled his glass, and changed the subject to the plow schedule.

And there was the argument. Two weeks after the exhumation request became public, Dominic had stayed late reconciling the fuel accounts and heard voices in the garage bay—low, urgent, the register men use when they don't want to be overheard but are too angry to manage it. Ray and Sal. Dominic caught only pieces through the roll-up door's gap.

"—not going to say another word about it, that's final—"

"She's his daughter, Ray. She's standing at that grave every Sunday like—"

"That's not ours to fix."

"Whose is it, then? Whose is it, if not—"

"*Nobody's.* Nobody's. You want to end up like—" A pause. A sound Dominic couldn't identify, a breath or a swallow. "You remember what we agreed. We agreed."

Then silence, and the click of the bay lights, and Dominic had stood very still among the fuel ledgers, feeling the floor tilt beneath a building they'd known for half their life.

*We agreed.* Agreed to what? To silence? To a story? To a version of one October night that all of them carried like stones in the same pocket?

* * *

Dominic tried the direct approach the way they tried everything—sideways, gently, disguised as routine.

At ten o'clock, Ray came to the window to turn in his trip sheets, which he did every day at ten, which he had done every day at ten since before Dominic could legally drive. His handwriting had gotten shakier lately. The numbers wandered on the line like men walking home drunk.

"Ray," Dominic said, keeping their voice at dispatch pitch, neutral, professional, "you remember much about October twelfth? Twelve years back?"

The pen stopped.

It was a small thing, the stopping of a pen. But in the depot's acoustics, behind the frosted glass, it sounded like a lock turning.

"Why?" Ray said.

"Sheriff's office pulled the old logs. I'm doing a completeness check. Standard."

"Standard," Ray repeated. He set the pen down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a gun. "You know what my father used to say? He used to say, a man who goes digging in old ground better be ready for what's buried there." He picked the pen back up. "I don't remember October. I've driven eleven hundred nights since then, kid. They all run together."

He walked away. At the door to the lot he paused, half-turned, and for a moment Dominic thought he was going to say something else—something true, something that would crack the whole wall open—and Dominic leaned toward him across the counter without meaning to, hungry, hungry for it.

"You should eat something," Ray said instead. "You're looking thin."

* * *

In the afternoon, Dominic went through the motions of the shift while the real work happened underneath, the way it always had. They pulled the October file for twelve years ago—not the erased minute, they weren't ready for that yet, or not brave enough—but the surrounding pages, the ordinary traffic of that week, looking for the shape of the drivers' lives inside it.

Ray had worked the night of the eleventh and the night of the thirteenth. The twelfth—the night of the call Dominic had erased—showed Ray off shift. Which meant either he'd truly been home, or someone had written him out of the record, or he'd taken a fare that never got logged at all.

Sal had been on days that week. Marisol hadn't worked for Voss yet—she'd been driving for the school district. Teddy was in middle school.

So it was Ray. And Sal, who hadn't driven that night but had burned a jacket sometime after, and had argued with Ray about it two weeks ago, and had not slept since.

Dominic closed the file drawer softly, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

* * *

At four, Maren Voss's sedan pulled into the lot.

Dominic saw it from the dispatch window and felt the room change temperature. Maren got out slowly, in her dark coat, and stood for a moment just looking at the building—the sign, the bays, the row of Crown Victorias with their dented bumpers—with an expression Dominic couldn't read from that distance. Grief, maybe. Or appraisal. Or the look of a woman visiting a place she'd been told stories about her whole life and finding it smaller than the stories.

Sal came out of the break room fast, wiping his hands on his trousers, and met her at the door before she reached it.

"Ms. Voss," he said, too loudly, too warmly. "Ma'am. We're honored. Can I get you coffee? Teddy, put a pot on."

"I'm not staying, Mr. Ferreira." Her voice carried through the glass, clear and cold as well water. "I wanted to see it. Where he started it." A pause. "Where he loved to say he started it."

"He did start it," Sal said. "Nineteen sixty-one, one car and a secondhand meter. Everybody knows that story."

"Do they," Maren said. It wasn't a question. She looked past Sal's shoulder, through the doorway, into the depot—at the break room, the board, the frosted glass behind which Dominic sat very still—and her gaze snagged on the dispatch window the way a sleeve snags on a nail. Held there. Then moved on.

"Well," she said. "Thank you for your time. All these years. My father thought highly of his drivers."

"He thought highly of you too, ma'am," Sal said, and something crossed his face when he said it—a flinch, quick as a blink, gone before it finished happening—and Maren saw it. Dominic watched her see it. Watched her go quiet in a new way, recalibrating, filing Sal Ferreira's flinch somewhere behind her eyes next to the anonymous letter and the cufflink and the empty box.

She drove away. Sal stood in the doorway a long time, watching the taillights, one hand braced against the frame as if the wind were stronger than it was.

* * *

Dominic found Ray in the wash bay at closing, running the hose over a car that was already clean. Water drummed on the concrete, loud enough to hide things. Dominic stood in the entrance and waited, and eventually Ray shut the water off, and in the sudden silence the drip from the ceiling counted seconds between them.

"You washed a car once," Dominic said. "October, twelve years ago. Four in the morning. With the hose loud."

Ray didn't turn around. His shoulders, broad under the gray work shirt, seemed to get broader, the way animals make themselves bigger. "Kid—"

"I'm not the kid anymore, Ray. I'm the one who keeps the logs. Everything that ever happened in this company went through me, one way or another. You know that. So whatever it is you're all carrying—whatever you agreed to—it's sitting in my building, in my drawers, and the sheriff is coming for those drawers whether we like it or not." Dominic heard their own voice shake and let it. "I'm not asking you to confess. I'm asking you to tell me how bad it is, so I'm not standing there blind when Okonkwo asks."

Ray turned off the hose reel slowly. When he finally faced Dominic, his eyes were wet—not crying, just full, the way old pipes fill before they fail.

"You were a baby," he said. "Seventeen years old, sleeping on a cot, eating our leftovers. We made a decision that night, all of us, and the decision was that you were the one clean thing in this outfit, and we were going to keep you that way even if it cost us." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "That's the only thing I'll say. Not because I don't trust you. Because I love you. There's a difference, and someday—I hope to God never—you'll understand it."

He hung the hose on its hook, squared it, aligned it perfectly with the others, and walked out into the dark lot.

Dominic stood alone in the wash bay with the water dripping, doing the arithmetic they'd been avoiding for two weeks. Ray had lied to protect someone. Sal had burned something to protect Ray. Marisol knew enough to be afraid. And none of it—not one fragment, not one flinch, not one four-in-the-morning car wash—had ever been about protecting Edmund Voss.

It was about protecting each other. The family, closing ranks around a secret the way a body closes around a splinter, growing tissue over the wound until the wound and the body were indistinguishable.

Loving and suffocating, both at once, and no way to cut one free of the other.

Dominic was still standing there, hands braced on the wash bay's cold steel table, when the office phone rang—the old landline, the one that rang only after hours, the one whose number appeared nowhere public. They dried their hands and answered it.

"Voss Taxi, night dispatch."

A pause. Then a voice, dry and papery and horribly familiar, a voice Dominic had last heard through a headset twelve years ago:

"Hello, little dispatcher. I hear you've been going through the October files."
