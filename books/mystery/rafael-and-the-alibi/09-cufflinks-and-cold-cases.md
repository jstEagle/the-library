## 9. Cufflinks and Cold Cases

Maren Voss had learned, over forty-one years of living with her father's name, that grief was a kind of weather. It came in systems. It had fronts and pressure changes. Some days it sat on the chest like fog off the lake; other days it arrived as a dry wind that made everything crackle and catch. The morning she decided to go to the taxi company, the grief was a hard blue cold—the kind that sharpened rather than dulled—and she dressed for it like an expedition: wool coat, her father's scarf, the letter in a ziplock bag because she'd read somewhere that evidence should be handled that way.

The envelope had come eleven days after the exhumation, postmarked from a town two counties over whose name she'd had to look up. Harwick. Population, according to the internet, something under four thousand. No return address. Inside, on a sheet of lined paper torn from a legal pad, five sentences in a small, upright hand:

*You asked the right question at the grave. Ask the next one: who signed for the fare? The answer is in the ledgers. Bring gloves.*

And beneath it, wrapped in tissue paper like a gift, her father's cufflink. Gold, monogrammed E.V., the clasp bent as if it had been pried from fabric by someone in a hurry—or someone being careful not to tear anything.

She had turned it over two hundred times since. She had held it to the light. She had slept with it under her pillow one night and woken furious at herself, because her father had been dead—absent, missing, *unaccounted for*—for twelve years, and whoever sent this had known where the cufflink was all that time. That was the part that made her hands shake. Not the sentiment. The custody.

So she went to Voss Taxi.

The building looked exactly as it had when she was nine years old and her father lifted her onto the counter so she could count the keys on the pegboard. Same brick, same green awning faded almost white, same smell of engine oil and burnt coffee that hit her at the door like a hand on the shoulder. What had changed was the people. A big man with silver in his beard looked up from a crossword and nodded at her without curiosity. A younger woman argued cheerfully with a radio. And behind the dispatch desk, in the corner chair that had been her father's, sat the person she'd come for.

Dominic Iversen. Small, watchful, gray-eyed, with the stillness of someone who had spent a lifetime learning not to be noticed. Maren had seen that stillness before—in animals, in witnesses, in herself during the worst years of her marriage. She recognized it the way you recognize a scar pattern: kinship of a sort.

"You're the daughter," Dominic said. Not a question. The voice was quiet, low, careful around the edges.

"I'm the daughter." She set the ziplock bag on the desk between them. "I need ten minutes and I need it to be off the record."

Dominic looked at the bag for a long moment. Then, without a word, reached over and clicked the radio's volume down to nothing—a gesture so practiced, so fluent in the grammar of this room, that Maren understood immediately she was talking to the person who ran the place, whatever the org chart said.

"Break room's quieter," Dominic said.

* * *

The break room smelled of diesel and microwave popcorn. A corkboard held a child's drawing of a taxi with wings, a funeral notice for someone named Petey Kowalczyk, and a photo of six drivers at a Christmas party, arms slung over shoulders, laughing at something outside the frame. Maren studied it while Dominic closed the door.

"That's the family," Dominic said, following her gaze. There was no irony in it. "You wanted off the record."

Maren took out the letter and laid it flat. Then the cufflink, still in its tissue. "This came Monday. Postmarked Harwick."

Dominic did not touch either item. But Maren saw the change—the way the gray eyes went from the paper to the cufflink and stopped there, fixed, the pupils tightening like a camera aperture. Whatever Dominic had expected, it wasn't this. Or maybe it was exactly this, and the shock was in being right.

"Read it," Maren said.

Dominic read it. Maren watched the face do very little—that was the eerie thing about this person, everything happened below the surface—but the hands told the truth. They came to rest flat on the table, fingers spread, pressing down as if the table might lift off otherwise.

"'Who signed for the fare,'" Dominic repeated.

"You know what it means."

"I have an idea."

"Then say it."

Dominic looked up, and for the first time Maren saw something unguarded move through the gray eyes—not fear exactly, but its cousin. "Your father kept dispatch ledgers. Paper, even after we went digital. He liked the permanence. Every fare, every signature, every cash run written out longhand by whoever was on shift." A pause. "I file them. I've filed them for twelve years."

"And?"

"And the ledgers are in the archive. In the basement. Under lock."

"Then let's go look."

Dominic stood so fast the chair scraped. "Not together."

"Why not?"

"Because if someone is watching this company—and they are, they sent you that cufflink, which means they've had physical access to things that were buried—then the last thing we should do is walk into my archive side by side like a delegation." Dominic's voice stayed level, but the words came faster now, and Maren realized she was seeing the first honest thing all day: this person was afraid, and had been for a while, and had gotten very good at functioning inside the fear. "You go home. I'll pull the ledger for the relevant year. Tomorrow, same time, I'll tell you what I find."

"No," Maren said.

Dominic blinked. It was such a small reaction, but on that controlled face it landed like a shout.

"I've spent twelve years being managed by people who thought they knew what was good for me," Maren said. "My father's lawyers. His board. The sheriff's office, who treated me like a grieving hobbyist until I forced the exhumation through a court order. I am done being sent home. We go now, or I go alone, and if I go alone I'll be handling original documents with no gloves and no idea what I'm looking at, and if your killer is watching, I'll be doing it badly and alone." She picked up the cufflink and held it out. "You knew him. You worked for him. Help me."

For a moment the break room was silent enough that Maren could hear the radio hissing faintly through the wall, the tick of the clock above the corkboard, the big driver's crossword pen scratching in the next room. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a business that had gone on running for twelve years over a hole in the ground.

Dominic took the cufflink. Turned it over once, thumb moving across the monogram with a strange tenderness, then set it back in the tissue with the precision of someone returning a relic to an altar.

"Aldous Reeve filed those ledgers before me," Dominic said quietly. "He taught me the system. He disappeared the same month your father did."

* * *

The basement archive smelled of paper and cold concrete, of toner and the particular vanilla sweetness of old glue. One bare bulb swung gently on its cord when Dominic pulled the chain, throwing their shadows huge against the shelving. Maren hugged her coat around her and watched Dominic work—watched the small hands move down the boxes with total fluency, reading spines the way other people read street signs.

"Reeve's system," Dominic said. "Chronological by year, alphabetical by driver within each month, cross-referenced by account number. He built it in 1987 and nobody's been allowed to change it since. He used to say a filing system is a promise you make to the future." A pause, barely a breath. "He also used to say everyone lies on paper eventually. You just have to know which entries are the lies."

"Charming man."

"He was the best teacher I ever had." Dominic said it flatly, factually, and somehow that was worse than any confession of affection would have been. "Here. 2013."

The box came down heavy. Inside, ledger after ledger in green cloth bindings, the years stamped in gold leaf gone dull. Dominic laid them on the worktable and opened the October volume, and Maren leaned in close enough to smell the pages—dust and ink and something faintly metallic underneath, like blood remembered.

"October ninth," Dominic said. "That's the night. Your father's last logged activity was a call from the estate at 9:47 p.m. Requesting a car to—" the finger stopped. Moved back. Stopped again.

"What?"

"The entry's been amended." Dominic's voice had gone very soft. "Ink's different. Look." The page tilted toward her. Most of the column marched along in a confident blue-black ballpoint, but one line had been overwritten in a finer, darker hand, the letters small and upright and perfectly formed: *destination amended — quarry rd — acct 4411.* "Someone changed the destination after the fact. And the handwriting—"

Maren had already taken out the letter. She laid the ziplock beside the open ledger and looked from one to the other, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the basement.

The small, upright hand. The perfectly formed letters. The same narrow *g* with its flattened loop. The same distinctive *4*, open-topped, like a chair missing a leg. On the ledger line: *acct 4411*. On the letter: *ask the next one*. Side by side, they might have been written by the same sitting, the same wrist, the same life.

"It matches," she said. Her own voice sounded far away. "It's the same hand."

Dominic didn't answer right away. When Maren glanced over, she found the dispatcher staring at the page with an expression she couldn't name—something older than fear, something like recognition, like a person hearing a dead relative's voice on an old answering machine tape.

"Say it," Maren said. "Whatever you're thinking. Say it out loud."

"This is Aldous Reeve's handwriting." Each word placed carefully, like stones across water. "I spent three years reading it eight hours a night. He corrected my forms in this hand. He wrote me notes in this hand. Nobody writes a *4* like that; he said it wasted motion to close the top." Dominic's thumb hovered over the amended line without touching it. "He amended this entry himself. After the fact. Which means Reeve didn't just vanish the month your father died. He was here. That night. At this desk or near it, changing the record."

"Then he's alive." Maren heard her own heartbeat. "The letter came from Harwick. Two counties. If he's writing to me, he's close enough to watch me open it."

"He's close enough to watch all of us." Dominic straightened slowly, and Maren watched something happen in that guarded face—a decision being made in real time, visible like weather. "There's more. If he amended the ledger, he may have amended other things. Tapes. Logs. I keep backups of my backups because he taught me to, and I never asked myself why until right now."

"Because some part of you always knew," Maren said.

Dominic looked at her then—really looked, the first time since the break room—and something in the gray eyes shifted from suspicion to something rawer. "Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Act like you know me. You don't. I've been invisible in this town for twelve years on purpose. Being known has never once worked out well for me." The words came out level but fast, and Maren understood she was hearing a door opening and slamming in the same sentence.

"Then close the door," Maren said. "Or leave it open. But decide, because I can't do this alone and neither can you. Whoever wrote this letter wants us both looking. He's playing us against each other or toward each other, and either way, we're stronger comparing notes than hiding them." She tapped the ledger. "He taught you the system. He's using the system. You're the only person alive who can read his moves inside it. I'm the only person with standing to demand answers about my father. That's not partnership. That's just arithmetic."

The bulb hummed. Somewhere overhead, a cab door slammed and an engine caught, muffled through the floor.

Dominic exhaled. And then—Maren would remember this precisely, the way you remember the exact second a storm front passes—something in the posture let go. Not much. An inch. The shoulders came down from around the ears. The hands unclenched on the table's edge.

"Account 4411," Dominic said. "It's a house account. Cash only, no name attached, opened the spring before your father died. I've wondered about it for twelve years and never once pulled the file, because pulling the file meant admitting the night existed." A short, humorless breath. "Reeve used to say the archive only keeps what you're brave enough to ask it for."

"Then ask it."

"Tomorrow. First light, before the drivers are in. I'll pull 4411 and every tape from that week." Dominic began closing the ledger with the reverence of a priest shutting a reliquary, then paused, one hand flat on the green cloth cover. "One condition. Okonkwo doesn't hear about this from anyone but us. When we have something real—not a feeling, not a handwriting match, something *real*—we bring the sheriff in together. Until then, it stays between us. Because the last time someone in this building found something in these records, they erased it, and I'm not giving anyone the chance to erase us."

"Us," Maren repeated.

Something flickered across Dominic's face—alarm, then resignation, then, buried deepest, the faintest ghost of relief, like a muscle releasing a cramp it had held for years.

"Us," Dominic said. "God help us both."

They climbed the stairs into the late afternoon light, and Maren carried the letter and the cufflink out to her car, and only when she was buckled in did she let herself shake. Because she had gotten what she wanted—a partner, a lead, a name—and the name was Aldous Reeve, and Aldous Reeve had written to her like a man keeping score.

*Bring gloves,* the letter said.

She hadn't asked yet what he'd wanted her to find that required gloves. But driving home past the quarry road turnoff, its gravel mouth dark under the pines, Maren Voss began to suspect that the answer was already waiting for them—buried, patient, and closer than either of them knew.
