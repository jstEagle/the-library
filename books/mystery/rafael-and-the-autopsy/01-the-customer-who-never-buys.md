## 1. The Customer Who Never Buys

The rain came down on Nystrom & Daughters Books the way it always did in October — sideways, with intent, as if it had a personal grudge against the awning and was determined to see it through to the end. The awning, for its part, held the same opinion of itself it had held for eleven years: that it would last one more season out of spite.

Jonah Nystrom watched this standoff from behind the register with a cup of coffee he'd reheated twice and a ledger open to a page that refused to improve no matter how many times he looked at it. Rent was due Friday. The register contained enough for rent if you rounded generously and ignored the word *enough* entirely.

There were no daughters. There had never been daughters. His father had named the shop in 1974 in the optimistic belief that sons would follow, and when only Jonah arrived — one son, divorced, childless, retired from thirty-one years of county homicide with a bad knee and a worse sleep schedule — the name had simply stayed, the way names do, like a scar or a promise nobody remembers making.

The bell over the door jingled at 2:14 in the afternoon.

Jonah looked up with the practiced neutrality of a man who had learned that hope at the sound of that bell was a tax he could no longer afford. In eleven years of ownership, the bell had announced: three actual customers on Tuesdays, one lost tourist per week, Mr. Anand every day at four o'clock sharp (who bought nothing but read an entire shelf of poetry standing up, which Jonah had decided counted), and Priya, his sole employee, who was currently at the back table with her headphones on, alphabetizing a cart of paperbacks by feel because she claimed she could tell a Koontz from a King blindfolded. Jonah had never tested this. He suspected she was right, which was somehow more annoying.

The woman who came in out of the rain was maybe thirty, maybe thirty-five, wearing a green coat that had been good once and a expression that had been hopeful once, though both were running low. She stood just inside the door and dripped with the particular patience of someone who has been walking through rain all day and has stopped minding.

"Are you open?" she asked, even though the OPEN sign was lit, even though she was already inside. People did that. Rain made people polite in strange places.

"Barely," Jonah said. "But yes."

She moved into the shop the way people move through churches — slowly, head tilted back, reading spines. Jonah let her. He'd learned long ago that hovering killed sales faster than clutter did, and his shop had plenty of both. He went back to the ledger, where the numbers sat exactly where he'd left them, being terrible.

He heard her stop somewhere near the local history section. Heard her pull a book out, put it back. Then her voice came across the quiet, careful and a little too casual:

"Do you buy books here? I mean — do people ever sell *to* you?"

"Sometimes. Estate lots, mostly. Why?"

"My mother had a lot of books." A pause. "I'm trying to find out what happened to them."

That was the kind of sentence Jonah had spent three decades learning to hear underneath. *What happened to them* was never about the books. He set down his pen.

"What's your mother's name?"

The question landed harder than he'd meant it to. He saw it land — saw her shoulders come up half an inch, saw her hand go flat against a shelf for balance.

"Elise Varga," she said.

* * *

Here is the thing about names: a detective spends thirty years filling drawers with them, and then he retires, and he thinks the drawers are closed. They are not closed. They are merely shut, and they open at the worst possible moments, usually in bookshops, usually on rainy Tuesdays when the rent is due.

*Varga.* Jonah knew the name before he knew why he knew it, the way you know a face from a dream. Elise Varga. Missing persons, filed — what, six years ago now? Seven? He hadn't worked it; he'd been two years gone by then. But he'd heard about it the way retired cops hear about everything, over coffee with men who should have known better than to talk in front of him. A woman walks out of her house on a Sunday evening, leaves her purse, leaves her car keys, takes nothing. Her car turns up three days later in a park-and-ride off Route 9, burned. No body. No note. No bank activity, no credit cards, no sightings. The file closes fast — too fast, someone had said, and then had said it quieter, and then had changed the subject, which in Jonah's experience was the sound a case makes when it has teeth.

And now here was a young woman dripping on his floor asking after her mother's library.

"You're Mara," he said.

She stared at him. "Do I know you?"

"No. But you have her chin." It was true — the same narrow jaw, the same set of the mouth, like a sentence someone had decided not to finish. "Elise Varga came in here sometimes. Years ago. Before your time, probably."

Mara Varga crossed the shop in four steps and stopped at the counter, and up close he could see the week she'd been having written all over her — the coat buttoned wrong by one, the eyes doing that dry-beyond-tired thing that grief does when it's been going a while.

"You knew my mother?"

"I knew *of* her. She sold us some books once. Mystery hardcovers, mostly. Good condition." He remembered it now with strange clarity: a woman in her fifties, silver-streaked hair pinned up like she'd done it during an argument, who haggled over a box of Ross Macdonalds with the seriousness of a woman negotiating a hostage release. She'd won. "That would've been — eight, nine years ago."

"And after that? Did she come back?"

"A few times. Browsed. Bought a paperback once." He frowned, reaching. "A western, of all things. Zane Grey. I remember because she apologized for it, like she was cheating on somebody."

Something moved through Mara's face — not quite a smile, something older and sadder that a smile had once lived in. "That sounds like her."

Behind them, Priya pulled one earbud out without turning around, which was how she listened to everything important. Jonah pretended not to notice. This was their arrangement.

"Look," Mara said, and then stopped, and started again, and Jonah recognized the maneuver — the approach run people make when they're about to ask for something they're ashamed of needing. "This is going to sound strange. I haven't seen my mother in six years. We didn't — we weren't speaking. And then she disappeared, and everyone acted like it was solved, like she just *left*, and maybe she did, maybe that's exactly what happened, but—" Her hand flattened on the counter. "Nobody ever found anything. Not a body, not a letter, not a suitcase. You don't just evaporate. Somebody knows something. And I keep thinking, if she was selling her books, if she was *getting rid of things* — that means she was planning something. Leaving is planning. Disappearing is planning. So I'm going backwards through her life, place by place, and this shop is on the list."

It was a good theory. It was, in fact, almost exactly the theory Jonah himself would have built, and hearing it come out of a stranger's mouth produced in him a sensation he hadn't felt in years — a small, warm, unwelcome ignition, like a pilot light catching in a furnace everyone had agreed to decommission.

"I can't help you," he said.

Her face fell so completely, so honestly, that he immediately regretted the sentence — not because it was untrue, but because it was true in a way he couldn't afford.

"I mean," he amended, "I can't help you find her. If she wanted to be found, there are professionals for that, and believe me, you don't want them. But—" He exhaled. Somewhere in the back of his mind, a voice that sounded suspiciously like his ex-wife said, *here we go.* "I might still have the intake slips. From when she sold us the books. Dates, titles, what she took in trade. It's not much."

"It's more than I have," Mara said. "I have a postcard and a police report that's four pages long and says nothing four different ways."

*A postcard,* Jonah thought, and filed it, and hated himself a little for filing it. Some instincts don't retire. They just get a smaller office.

"Give me your number," he said. "I'll dig tonight. If I find the slips, I'll call you tomorrow."

She wrote it on the back of a receipt from a gas station two towns over, and her handwriting was quick and slanted and pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — a woman used to being ignored, he thought, writing so the world couldn't pretend she hadn't spoken.

At the door she paused, rain-coat collar up, and looked back at him with her mother's unfinished-sentence mouth.

"Why are you helping me?" she asked. "You don't know me."

Jonah considered several answers. *Because the rent is due and distraction is free. Because you look like someone who hasn't eaten. Because thirty years ago I learned that the cases that close too fast are the ones that come back with interest.*

"Because you asked a bookseller about books," he said finally. "We're suckers for that."

She almost smiled. Then the bell jingled, and the rain took her, and the shop settled back into its Tuesday silence like water closing over a stone.

* * *

Priya appeared at the counter approximately four seconds after the door shut, which was her standard response time for anything interesting. She was seventeen, chronically under-slept, and possessed of the unshakeable conviction that Jonah's entire life was a novel she was reading one chapter at a time whether he liked it or not.

"That was a plot development," she said.

"That was a customer."

"She asked about a missing person. That's a plot development. That's chapter one behavior." Priya leaned on the counter, radiating curiosity like a space heater. "Varga. Varga. Wasn't there something—"

"There wasn't."

"You did the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you say 'there wasn't' and then stare at the middle distance like the middle distance owes you money." She tapped the counter twice. "You're going to stay late tonight. You're going to go through those boxes in the back that you pretend are inventory but are actually your feelings."

"They're inventory."

"Organized by year of emotional damage," Priya said. "I've seen the labels."

Jonah looked at her. She looked back, unrepentant, one earbud dangling, a girl who shelved books by instinct and read people the same way. Eleven years ago the shop had been empty except for him and the debt. Now there was this — a teenager who'd wandered in at fifteen looking for shelter from a foster placement gone sideways and had simply never been evicted, and Mr. Anand arriving at four o'clock daily, and Deshawn calling every Sunday to check that Jonah was eating vegetables. He had never planned any of it. Found families, he'd learned, were like water stains: they spread where the structure was weakest.

"Four o'clock," he said. "Mr. Anand will want his chair."

"He always wants his chair." Priya retreated toward the poetry shelf, then paused. "For the record, I think you should look. Whatever it is. You've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one you get right before the shop stops making money for a month."

* * *

He stayed late. Of course he stayed late.

The intake slips lived in a filing cabinet older than Priya, in a drawer labeled MISC 2010–2016 in his father's handwriting, which meant it could contain anything from receipts to love letters to, once, memorably, a live wasp nest. Jonah dug past estate lots and church sale remnants until he found it: a yellow slip dated March 14th, nine years back. *Varga, E. — 22 hc, various, mystery/crime. Trade: $40 store credit. Took $12 cash.*

Nine years ago. Three years before she vanished. A woman quietly converting her library into cash and credit, one box at a time, apologizing for a Zane Grey.

He should have stopped there. He told himself he was going to stop there. Instead he pulled the ledger — the real one, the old inventory book from the years before he bought the shop, when it had belonged to a quiet accountant named Tomas Varga who'd died suddenly and left his widow a building full of books she apparently didn't want.

Jonah sat with that fact for a long moment in the lamplight, rain ticking against the dark windows.

Tomas Varga. Elise Varga. And now Mara Varga standing in his shop asking questions the police report wouldn't answer.

He turned to the ledger's spring pages, nine years back, and ran his finger down the purchase orders from suppliers he'd never heard of — names that sounded plausible and weren't, amounts that were odd in the specific way that amounts are odd when they're not really about books at all. $317. $317 again, six weeks later. $318. He'd assumed, when he'd bought the place, that old Tomas had simply been a sloppy bookkeeper.

Sloppy bookkeepers repeat themselves. Codes do.

Outside, the rain kept up its argument with the awning. Upstairs, his apartment was cold and the coffee was dead and the sensible thing — the thing a retired man does — was to lock the drawer, turn off the lamp, and go to bed.

Jonah looked at the name *Varga* sitting there in faded ink, three times in one day like a knock at a door he'd painted shut years ago.

Then he got out a fresh legal pad, wrote *ELISE VARGA* at the top of it, and underlined it twice.

The dormant thing in his chest, the one he'd told everyone — including himself — was dead, turned over in its sleep and opened one eye.
