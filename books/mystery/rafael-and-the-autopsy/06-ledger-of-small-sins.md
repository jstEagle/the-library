## 6. Ledger of Small Sins

The hymnal had been sitting on shelf 12, row C, for longer than I'd owned the shop.

I know this because it had my predecessor's pricing pencil on the flyleaf — a faded *$4* in a hand that wasn't mine — and because when I pulled it down at half past six in the morning, before the kettle had even finished its argument with itself, a small brown spider the size of a comma relocated to the far end of the shelf with what I can only describe as dignity.

*The St. Augustine Hymnal, Revised Edition, 1968.* The kind of book nobody buys, nobody steals, and nobody — under normal circumstances — leaves wedged spine-inward between a water-damaged atlas and a three-volume history of canal engineering that I have never once sold, not even to the canal enthusiast who comes in every November.

But the diary had mentioned it. Entry number forty-one, the one dated eleven days after Elise Varga stopped existing on paper: *The music is in the old hymnal. He never changed the arrangement.*

He. Her late husband, Tomas Varga, who'd owned this shop before me, who'd died the year before I bought it, and who — according to the inventory records I'd spent two sleepless nights with — had purchased stock from suppliers who did not appear to exist.

I set the hymnal on the counter, made tea I forgot about, and opened it to the index of first lines, because that's what the diary's later entries kept doing. Page numbers. Dozens of them, tucked into mundane sentences like loose change in a coat pocket.

*Page 112 was lovely this week. He still prefers 87. I told him 203 was too much, and he agreed.*

Hymns. Page numbers. Payments.

The inventory ledger from 1999 through 2003 — the years Tomas ran the shop — sat open beside it, and I'd already flagged the anomalies. Purchase orders from *Meridian Book Supply* of a town that didn't have a Meridian Book Supply. Invoices in round, odd amounts: $112.00. $87.00. $203.00. Amounts that made no sense for book orders, because book orders come with tax and shipping and the general messiness of commerce, and these were clean as church windows.

I put my finger on page 112 of the hymnal.

*Amazing Grace.*

I put my finger on 87.

*Just As I Am.*

And 203.

*Beneath the Cross of Jesus.*

Three hymns. Three invoice amounts. Three purchase orders from a supplier that had never shipped a single book to a single shop, dated at intervals of roughly six weeks across four years, each one for a few thousand dollars more than the last.

"Well," I said to the spider, who had not come back down. "That's not inventory."

* * *

Here's what I want to be clear about, because I've had twenty years of people assuming detectives spend their days having revelations. We don't. We spend our days having hunches we can't prove, and then we spend weeks — months — doing the boring, unglamorous, soul-grinding work of turning a hunch into a fact. The revelation, when it comes, is almost always smaller and worse than you hoped.

So I did it the boring way. I made a grid.

I took every hymnal page number in Elise's diary — and once I started looking, they were everywhere, threaded through entries that read like a woman describing her week at choir practice — and I wrote each one on an index card. Then I took every anomalous invoice in Tomas's ledger and wrote those on cards too. Then I laid them out on the floor of the shop, between the poetry section and the biography section, in a pattern that would have gotten me committed if any of my regulars had walked in.

Priya arrived at nine, stood over the grid with a cinnamon roll in one hand, and said, "Are we doing witchcraft now? Because I'm into it, but I have a chem test."

"Inventory audit," I said.

"On the floor."

"The floor has better light."

She crouched, scanned it with the terrifying speed of a teenager who reads everything, and pointed at two cards. "These match. 112 and 87. Same dates, almost. Is that on purpose?"

"Maybe."

"You're doing your face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a face you do when you're not telling me something. It's your only face." She took her cinnamon roll and her chem test and her terrifying perceptiveness to the register, and I got back to work.

By noon I had forty-one matched pairs. Forty-one hymn pages in the diary corresponding to forty-one invoices over four years. And the amounts — this was the part that made my hands go cold in a way I pretended was the drafty shop — the amounts climbed. $4,100. $4,800. $6,200. Small money, at first. Money that could hide inside a bookshop's books the way a hymn hides inside a Tuesday.

Money that was not book money. Money that was *payment* money.

* * *

The dates were what undid me.

I'd been treating the invoices as a timeline of Tomas's activity — when he was paid, how often, by whom. But when I laid them against the calendar instead of against each other, a different shape emerged. The payments didn't cluster around Tomas's convenience. They clustered around *court dates*.

I know this because I keep a private habit I never broke, even after retirement: I read the county court calendar the way other men read the sports page. Twenty years of it. The sealed cases don't appear, of course — sealed is sealed — but the *gaps* appear. The docket numbers that skip. The hearings that vanish from the public index between one week and the next. A detective learns to read the silences in a document the way a musician reads the rests.

Invoice of $4,100, dated March 14th, 1999. On March 11th, 1999, a civil suit against the county — one I remembered, vaguely, involving a jail transport — was pulled from the docket and sealed.

Invoice of $6,200, dated the week a wrongful-death filing against a county corrections officer disappeared into a sealed file.

Invoice after invoice, each one landing within days of a case going dark. Forty-one sealed or vanished proceedings across four years, and forty-one payments routed through a bookshop on Delmore Street, disguised as hymns.

I sat down on the floor, in the middle of my own grid, and let that settle.

Tomas Varga hadn't been taking bribes. Bribery is transactional — you pay, you get a thing, the thing arrives. This was something else. This was a *schedule*. A maintenance fee. Someone was paying, month after month, year after year, to keep cases sealed. And Tomas Varga's little bookshop was the pipe the money flowed through, and Elise — Elise who had kept her husband's books after he died, Elise who had sold me this shop with a handshake and a box of receipts I'd never fully audited because I trusted her — Elise had known.

The diary said so. Not in those words. In hymn numbers. In entries that read, on their surface, like a woman missing her husband's opinion on music: *He always said 203 was too much. I finally agree.* Dated the week after a sealed case involving — I checked my cards three times to be sure — a settlement of $203,000, paid quietly to a family whose name I recognized from a newspaper story I'd read over coffee a decade ago and never forgotten, because the story had ended with the sentence *the county admitted no wrongdoing.*

* * *

It was the last card that broke me.

I'd saved it for the end, the way you save the last bite of something you're afraid to finish. The largest invoice in the ledger — $22,000, dated October of 2001, from Meridian Book Supply, hymn page 447.

There is no hymn on page 447 of the St. Augustine Hymnal, Revised Edition, 1968. I checked. Page 446 ends with *Abide With Me*, and page 448 begins the order of service for funerals. Page 447 is blank — a deliberate blank, a page left empty between the hymns and the rites, the way a book leaves room for the reader.

Somebody had chosen a blank page on purpose. A payment that was also an absence.

And October of 2001 — I didn't need my cards for this one. I needed my memory, and my memory has never once cooperated by letting me forget the things I'd prefer to lose. Twenty-two years ago this autumn, a man named Samuel Okonkwo died in county custody. Found unresponsive in a holding cell at four in the morning. The official finding was positional asphyxia, the official story was that he'd been drunk and combative, and the official investigation lasted nine days before closing with the tidy efficiency of a machine built for exactly that purpose. I was a year into detective work then, junior, nowhere near it, but the whole floor had heard about it, the way a whole floor hears about the things nobody says out loud.

The case had been sealed. I'd never known why. I'd never asked. That was the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone — not that the payment existed, but that I'd spent twenty-two years a few doors down from the answer and never once knocked.

$22,000. Twenty-two years. I told myself the number was coincidence. I am a professional, and professionals know that numbers lie to you when you want them to mean things.

Then I looked at the hymnal, open on the counter to page 447, blank as a held breath, and I stopped telling myself things.

* * *

Priya found me there at closing time, still on the floor, the cards spread around me like a man who'd fallen asleep building a house of evidence. She didn't say anything at first. She just looked — at the grid, at the hymnal, at my face, which I will concede was doing its face — and then she sat down across from me, cross-legged, the way you sit with someone at a campfire.

"Is this about the missing lady?" she asked. "Mara's mom?"

"Maybe."

"The invoices are fake, aren't they. The suppliers." She'd helped me shelve the ledger weeks ago; of course she'd read it. I'd stopped being surprised by Priya around the time she reorganized my mystery section by *probability of the narrator being unreliable*, and it had improved sales.

"They're real invoices," I said. "For books that never existed."

"Money laundering." She said it the way she'd say *long division* — a thing she understood the mechanics of and found mildly tedious. "In a bookshop. *This* bookshop."

"Before my time."

"Everything bad is before your time. That's how you all get to be surprised." She picked up the card for page 447, turned it over, put it down. "Whose money was it?"

"That's the question."

"And whose was it *before*?" she said, and I looked at her, and she looked back with the flat patience of the young, who have not yet learned that some questions are better left in the dark, and who are usually right anyway.

* * *

I closed up late. I put the hymnal back on shelf 12, row C, spine-inward, exactly where it had lived for twenty years, because I am enough of an old detective to know that a thing which has stayed hidden in one place for two decades is sometimes safest left looking undisturbed. Then I sat in the back room with the diary and read the entries around page 447 one more time.

*He said the blank page was the most honest one in the book. I didn't understand him then. I do now.*

And below it, three entries later, in that handwriting that was almost hers but moved wrong, like a stranger wearing her coat:

*447 again this year. Same as always. Some pages never change.*

Same as always. Which meant the payments hadn't stopped with Tomas's death. Which meant the ledger in my back room wasn't the whole of it — it was the *early* years, the small sins, and somewhere out there was the rest of the account, twenty more years of blank pages, including whatever had been paid — and to whom — in the weeks before Elise Varga walked out of her life and a diary kept writing itself in her hand.

The kettle had gone cold on the counter, untouched since morning. Outside, the rain had started again, soft and indifferent, the way rain is when it has no idea what it's falling on.

I got out a fresh index card, wrote *October — 447 — who collects?*, and pinned it to the corkboard above my desk, where the shop's old delivery schedule used to hang.

The board had one other card on it. Mara Varga's, with her number, from the day she'd walked in asking about books her mother might have sold.

I looked at it for a long time.

There was a version of the next phone call where I asked her about her father's suppliers, and there was a version where I didn't, and I stood there in the dark of my own shop — Tomas's shop, Elise's shop, a building that had been laundering secrets through its shelves since before I ever turned the sign to OPEN — trying to decide which call I could live with.

Then the phone rang, and the decision made itself. Mara Varga's voice, small and steady: "Mr. Nystrom? I found something in my mother's things. A postcard." A breath. "It's dated three weeks after she disappeared."
