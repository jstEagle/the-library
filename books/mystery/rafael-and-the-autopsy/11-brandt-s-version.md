## 11. Brandt's Version

Ilsa Brandt had learned a long time ago that the best place to watch someone was from inside their own routine. People guarded their doors. They never guarded their habits.

So she'd been coming to Nystrom & Daughters Books every Thursday for three years, buying nothing, browsing everything, learning the geography of the man. The creak of the third floorboard on the way to the register. The way Jonah Nystrom alphabetized mysteries under "N" instead of by author, because he liked finding his own name in the stacks. The chipped blue mug he drank tea from, and the fact that he made it too weak, always, like a man punishing himself in small ways.

Tonight she came at closing time, when the rain had thinned to a fine gray mist and the streetlights were coming on along Delancey Avenue, smearing gold across the wet pavement.

Jonah was behind the counter with the ledger open in front of him — her ledger, though he didn't know that yet — and a pencil tucked behind his ear like a man who still believed problems could be solved by hand.

"Closing time," she said, and watched him startle. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Detective Brandt." He didn't look up right away. When he did, his face did the thing it always did — the careful blankness of a man rearranging furniture behind his eyes. "We're technically closed."

"I know. I called ahead." She hadn't. She let him wonder about that. "Mind if I look around? Old habit."

"You've looked around every Thursday for—" He stopped himself. Good. So he *had* noticed. That was better than she'd hoped and worse than she'd feared.

"Three years," she finished for him. "You count your customers?"

"The ones who never buy anything, yeah. They're memorable." He closed the ledger, casual as a card player covering his hand. "Can I help you find something tonight, Detective? We just got a shipment of—"

"Ilsa," she said. "Off duty, it's Ilsa. And no, Jonah. I'm not here for a book."

The rain ticked against the front window. Somewhere in the back, the ancient radiator knocked twice and went quiet, like even the building was holding its breath.

She walked the aisle slowly, trailing one finger along the spines — poetry, local history, the sad little shelf of self-help nobody touched — until she reached Shelf 12, Row C, where the county records and old municipal annuals gathered dust. She pulled down a hymnal. 1987 edition. Water-stained. Worthless.

"Page 214," she said, setting it on the counter between them. "That's 'Abide With Me,' if you're curious. Lovely hymn. Very popular with people who need to remember things."

Jonah went very still. It was a small stillness, controlled, professional — twenty-six years of homicide work doesn't leave a person — but she saw it. She'd been trained to see it by the same department that taught him.

"That's an odd thing to bring up," he said.

"It's an odd book you're running here." She sat down on the customer side of the counter, uninvited, making herself comfortable the way you do when you intend to stay a while. "A bookshop that processed payments for two decades. A dead man's inventory system used as a washing machine. Hymnal page numbers keyed to amounts, dates keyed to court filings. You found it faster than I expected, honestly. I gave you six weeks. You did it in eleven days."

The radiator knocked again. Jonah's hand drifted toward the ledger and stopped, deliberately, halfway.

"You've been reading my mail," he said quietly.

"I've been reading *her* diary. Or trying to. Mara Varga showed it to me last week — don't look surprised, she's scared, and scared people talk to anyone wearing a badge." That was true, or close enough. "Elise Varga was my informant. For four years. And then she disappeared, and the file got closed in nine days by a detective who didn't interview a single witness, and everybody upstairs acted like that was normal. So now I'm asking questions off the books, in a bookshop, on a rainy Thursday. That's where we are."

* * *

He didn't believe her. Of course he didn't believe her. She watched him not believe her — politely, carefully, the way you handle something that might be loaded.

"Your informant," Jonah said. "Elise Varga. A retired schoolteacher who ran a laundromat and sold this shop after her husband died."

"A retired schoolteacher whose husband laundered hush money for people in this building, and who spent twenty-two years collecting evidence against them after he died. Yes. Her." Brandt leaned back. "You want to hear how it started? Four years ago, cold case review. I pulled the Okonkwo file — Daniel Okonkwo, died in county custody, 2003, ruled accidental. Something in it bothered me. The transport log. One deputy signed for a prisoner transfer that never appears in any other record. I started asking quiet questions. Two weeks later, Elise Varga walks into my office unannounced, sits down, and says: 'You're going to get yourself killed. Let me help.'"

Jonah's eyebrows moved. Barely. "And you trusted her."

"I verified her. Everything she gave me checked out. Dates, amounts, names. She'd been keeping copies of everything for two decades — she told me her husband made her promise, before he died, that if anything happened to him, she'd finish what he couldn't." Brandt let that sit. "She was careful, Jonah. Meticulous. She never gave me anything directly — drops, coded notes, the works. And then eight months ago she went dark. Missed a drop. Then another. Then her car turned up burned out past the fairgrounds, and Detective Okafor closed the case before the paint cooled."

"And you think—"

"I think somebody upstairs found out what she was doing, and I think the same somebody has been feeding me just enough cooperation to keep me leashed." She met his eyes. "I also think you've been pulling morgue intake records, which is a very interesting hobby for a bookseller, and that case number 4471 is going to be cremated in nine days unless somebody does something about it."

There it is, she thought. Watch his hands. Watch his breathing. A guilty man relaxes when you name the wrong detail; an honest man leans in.

Jonah leaned in. Fractionally. Maybe it was real. Maybe he was better than she was.

"Why come to me?" he said. "If you're running an investigation, why walk into a bookshop and show me your whole hand?"

"Because you have the diary, and I don't. Because Mara trusts you, and she doesn't trust me — can't, professionally. And because—" She paused. This was the part that mattered. This was the part she'd rehearsed in the car for forty minutes. "Because whoever killed Elise Varga knew our protocols. Knew when her drops were scheduled. Knew how to close a case without triggering review. That's a short list, Jonah, and I'm on it whether I like it or not. If I go to Internal Affairs tomorrow, I'm either the investigator who solves this or the suspect who runs. There's no version where I stay neutral. So I'd rather be the first one."

The lie of omission sat comfortably in her chest. It had been sitting there for years. It weighed less than it used to. That was the part that frightened her, late at night — not the weight, but how little she felt it anymore.

"So that's it," Jonah said slowly. "An alliance. Off the books. Between a retired detective running a failing bookshop and an active homicide detective who may or may not be telling the truth."

"When you say it like that, it sounds insane."

"It sounded insane the other way too."

Despite herself — despite everything — Brandt laughed. Short, genuine, surprised out of her. She saw it land on him, soften him half a degree. Good. Trust wasn't built on evidence; evidence came later. Trust was built on small human moments, and she was good at manufacturing those. She'd learned from the best. She'd learned from the man she was protecting.

"Here's what I can offer," she said, standing, buttoning her coat. "Access. Sealed files, the original Okonkwo transport logs, the internal phone records from the week Elise vanished. Things you can't get without burning this shop down around you. In exchange: I see everything you find. The diary entries. The quarry reference — yes, I know about the quarry, don't ask how. Full transparency. Both directions."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I keep investigating alone, you keep investigating alone, and one of us gets to the truth second." She smiled, and let it reach her eyes, because she meant the next part. "And the person who gets there second, Jonah, in cases like this — historically — ends up in the ground next to the witness."

The rain had stopped. Through the window, Delancey Avenue shone like a spilled secret. Somewhere down the block, Priya's bicycle rattled past, the kid heading home with a bag of returns, oblivious.

At the door, Brandt paused with her hand on the frame. One more thing. The hook, the gift, the poison pill — she'd decided on the drive over which one it was, and she still wasn't sure.

"One more thing you should know," she said, not turning around. "The postcard Mara received. Dated after the fire. Everyone assumes it means Elise was alive when it was mailed." Now she turned, and put every ounce of sincerity she owned into her face, and the terrible thing, the thing she would think about later, was that some of it was real. "It doesn't. I've seen that handwriting before — in Elise's drop notes. Someone was practicing it. Someone close enough to copy it well and badly at the same time. Whoever sent that card wanted Mara looking. Wanted *you* looking. Think about who benefits from a retired detective with an old grudge digging into a twenty-year-old case."

She let the door swing shut behind her, the bell chiming its small bright note, and stood a moment in the wet dark listening to the silence inside.

Through the glass she watched Jonah stand motionless behind the counter, the hymnal lying open between his hands like a Bible at a funeral, and she thought: *I just handed him a reason to trust me and a reason to fear everyone else, and both of them are true, and only one of them is mine.*

Then she walked to her car, sat in the driver's seat without starting it, and took out her phone. One contact, starred, labeled only **DAD**.

She typed: *He has the ledger. Moving faster than expected.*

Her thumb hovered over send. Outside, the streetlight flickered once, twice, and held.

In the bookshop window behind her, faint through the rain-beaded glass, Jonah Nystrom picked up his phone too — and Ilsa Brandt felt, for the first time in years, the cold specific sensation of being watched by someone who had finally decided to stop reading and start writing.
