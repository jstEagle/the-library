## 13. Priya Reads Everything

The thing nobody understood about Priya Chandra was that she read everything.

Not just books — though she'd read most of the shop by now, alphabetically, out of a kind of stubborn completeness that Mr. Nystrom called "a cry for help" and she called "professional development." She read the receipts. She read the shipping labels. She read the sticky notes Jonah left on the register, the ones with phone numbers and half-finished sentences like *call D about the* and then nothing, because apparently retired detectives ran out of steam mid-thought the same way everyone else did.

She read the notes he didn't know he was leaving.

That was the thing about Jonah Nystrom: he thought of himself as a tidy man because his shop was tidy, but Priya had worked here eleven months now and she knew the truth. The man was a hurricane contained entirely within a three-foot radius of whatever surface he was currently standing at. He shed paper the way other people shed skin. Index cards, legal pads torn into strips, the backs of envelopes from the electric company covered in handwriting that started confident and got progressively more cramped, like the words were trying to escape off the edge of the page.

And every night when she closed up — or some mornings when she came in early and found the aftermath of one of his 2 a.m. sessions — she gathered it all up. Not to throw away. She'd asked him once, in her second week, whether she should bin the scraps, and he'd said, "Leave them," in a voice that meant *don't touch them*, so naturally she'd started reading them instead.

It wasn't snooping. It was shelving. There was a difference. Shelving was putting things where they belonged, and if you were going to put things where they belonged, you had to understand what they were. That was basic. That was *Dewey Decimal*. Nobody accused librarians of snooping.

So for two weeks, while Jonah and Mara Varga descended deeper and deeper into whatever grim archaeology they were doing in the back room — Priya heard the word *quarry* through the door twice and pretended she hadn't — Priya had been quietly re-shelving the investigation itself.

She kept the index cards in a cigar box behind the counter, sorted by date. The envelope-backs went in a folder labeled, in pencil, MISC (SORRY). The legal pad pages she'd flattened under the big dictionary on the reference shelf, which was ironic and also useful, because nothing flattens paper like a dictionary nobody has ever opened.

She wasn't going to say anything. That was the plan. She was seventeen, she made twelve dollars an hour, and her job description consisted of ringing up books, dusting shelf 12, and not asking questions. Especially not after the day Detective Brandt came in with her friendly smile and her wrong eyes and bought a paperback she clearly never intended to read while asking about Mara's schedule like she was planning a surprise party.

But then Tuesday happened.

Tuesdays were slow. Tuesdays were rain-and-one-customer slow, and the customer was always Mr. Anand, who came in at four o'clock sharp, bought nothing, complained about everything, and stayed until six. Priya had learned to love him in a way she'd never admit out loud, mostly because loving Mr. Anand out loud would require explaining why, and the explanation was that underneath all the complaining was a man who remembered exactly how she took her tea and had once, without being asked, stood between her and a drunk guy being loud about her name until the drunk guy lost interest.

So it was a Tuesday, raining, Mr. Anand installed in the sagging armchair by the window with a book about Balkan history he had no intention of buying, and Priya was doing the cigar box.

She did it when there was nothing else. She'd take the week's cards and lay them out on the counter in order, and she'd read them, and usually they made about as much sense as a phone book read aloud by someone having a stroke. Dates. Numbers. Names crossed out and rewritten. Little arrows pointing nowhere.

But this Tuesday, she laid them out and something snagged.

There was a card from — she checked the corner, where Jonah had gotten in the habit of dating things after she'd once suggested it, back when she still pretended she wasn't paying attention — October ninth. On it, in his tight slanted hand:

*Payments stop. Why? Ledger goes dark Oct 3–10, every year? Check.*

And below that, smaller, like an afterthought or maybe a wound: *anniversary?*

Priya stared at it.

Then she did the thing she probably should have felt guilty about, except she'd long ago made peace with the fact that guilt was for people who got caught. She pulled the folder marked MISC (SORRY) out from under the dictionary and spread the ledger transcriptions across the counter between the register and the stack of remaindered cookbooks nobody wanted.

Jonah had decoded the cipher weeks ago — hymnal page numbers, prices keyed to them, the whole elegant little system Elise Varga's husband had built into the shop's own inventory records, right here, in this building, under these floors Priya mopped every Thursday. The decoded payments were written out on a long strip of butcher paper Jonah had taped to the back-room wall, but he'd also copied chunks onto cards, and the cards were what she had, and the cards were enough.

She lined them up by year.

The shop had been doing its secret business for over twenty years, and the payments — small ones, regular ones, the bookkeeping equivalent of a heartbeat — marched across the page month after month. Except.

Except.

Priya put her finger on the first gap. Then the next. Then the next. Her heart was doing something embarrassing in her chest, and she told it to calm down, because it was just bookkeeping, it was just arithmetic, arithmetic couldn't hurt anyone.

Every year. Every single year, going back twenty-two years, the payments stopped for roughly a week in early October. And then they resumed, sometimes slightly larger, as if catching up.

"Mr. Anand," she said, without looking up.

"Hm."

"Do you know what happened in early October twenty-two years ago?"

"I know what happened in early October *every* year," Mr. Anand said. "The county fair closes, the leaves clog my gutters, and my daughter forgets my birthday." He turned a page. "Why?"

"Because I think something happened here too. Something bad enough that whoever was paying money to keep quiet about it couldn't bring themselves to pay it that week. Like a scar that opens every year."

Mr. Anand looked at her over his glasses. He had the kind of face that made you feel like you'd said something stupid even when you hadn't, and the rarer kind that could make you feel like you'd said something important even when you weren't sure you had. Right now it was the second one.

"You've been reading those," he said. Not a question.

"I shelve them," Priya said. "There's a difference."

"Mm." He closed the Balkan history book on his finger. "And have you told him?"

"He's got enough going on." She glanced toward the back room, where she could hear Jonah's voice, low, talking to Mara. "He keeps looking at me like I'm a kid who needs protecting. Which is sweet and also insane, because I'm the one who noticed the photographer."

The words were out before she'd fully decided to say them, which was how most of Priya's best decisions worked.

"What photographer?" Mr. Anand said.

* * *

Here was the thing about the photographer: he was bad at it, and that was the only reason she'd caught him.

The first time was nine days ago, a Wednesday. She'd been restocking the window table — the one that faced the street, the one with the display of local interest titles that sold maybe four copies a year — and she'd seen him across the road, under the awning of the dry cleaner's, holding up his phone like he was taking a picture of the rain. Except the rain doesn't need framing, and his framing had the shop dead center.

He was maybe fifty-five, heavyset, gray jacket, the particular posture of a man who used to stand a certain way for a living and couldn't unlearn it. Priya had grown up around cops — her uncle was one, the decent kind, allegedly — and she knew the stance. Feet planted. Weight even. Chin level. A man built for watching doors.

He didn't come in. He walked off toward the bus stop, unhurried, and Priya had stood there with a copy of *Ghost Towns of the County* in her hands feeling cold in a way the weather couldn't explain.

The second time was last Friday. Same man, different angle — he was parked down the block in a beige sedan, and he'd angled his side mirror toward the shop, which was either lazy tradecraft or a message, and Priya had spent a long time deciding which. She'd written down the partial plate. She'd drawn him, actually, on the back of a bookmark, because drawing helped her think and also because if anything ever happened she wanted a record that wasn't just her word against a cop-shaped stranger's.

She showed Mr. Anand the bookmark. He studied it for a long time.

"You drew this?"

"I'm good at faces. It's a whole thing. My mom says I remember people too well and it'll get me in trouble someday."

"Your mother may be a prophet." He handed it back. "You should tell him. Today. Not because you're a kid — because you're the only one of them who isn't looking at the ledger anymore. They're all inside the puzzle. You're the only one still looking out the window."

Priya considered this. It was annoyingly wise. Mr. Anand's wisdom was like that — it snuck up on you disguised as complaining.

"And the anniversary thing?" she said. "Should I tell him that too?"

Mr. Anand picked his book back up. "Tell him both. But lead with the photographer. Men like Jonah will chase a pattern forever and ignore a threat standing on their own sidewalk. You have to make the threat boring first, so he can't resist it."

"That's terrible advice."

"It's excellent advice. It's how I got married."

* * *

She waited until closing. Mara left first — she'd been leaving earlier all week, and Priya had noticed that too, the way Mara's hands shook slightly when she zipped her bag, the way she checked the window before crossing the street, habits you pick up fast when you think someone's hunting you. Then Jonah emerged from the back room looking like a man who'd been arguing with a ghost and losing on points.

"I'm heading out," Priya said, shrugging into her jacket. "Oh — also, your payments stop every year in the first week of October. Twenty-two years running. And there's a guy photographing the shop. Gray jacket, beige sedan, partial plate starts with K. I drew him."

She set the cigar box, the folder, the bookmark drawing, and the aligned cards on the counter in one neat stack, the way she stacked everything, and watched Jonah's face do three things in rapid succession: confusion, comprehension, and then a kind of horror that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with a man realizing the teenager had been assembling his own investigation better than he had.

"The anniversary," he said slowly. "The custody death. Ruth Okonkwo's husband died the fourth of October. Twenty-two years ago." His hand came down flat on the counter. "I had the dates. I *had* them, I wrote them down—"

"You wrote them on cards," Priya said. "You scattered them across every horizontal surface in a ten-foot radius. I shelved them. There's a difference."

For a second — just a second — Jonah laughed. It was a tired, cracked laugh, but real, and Priya filed it away as possibly the nicest sound she'd produced all year.

Then he picked up the bookmark drawing, and the laugh died.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you see this man?"

"Twice. Across the street, and parked down the block. He never comes in. He never approaches. He just... documents." She hesitated, then decided the last part mattered more than her pride. "Mr. Nystrom, I don't think he's casing us. I think he's *keeping records*. Like he's building a file on the shop. On Mara. On you. Cops write reports; this feels more like somebody keeping receipts."

Jonah looked at the drawing for a long moment. Outside, the rain kept up its soft static against the glass, and the streetlight came on, and somewhere down the block — Priya saw it in the window's reflection, and she saw Jonah see it too — a beige sedan sat with its lights off.

Neither of them moved.

"Priya," Jonah said, not turning from the window, his voice dropped low and even, the voice of a man thirty years younger, "how would you feel about working Sundays?"

"I'd feel like I want time and a half," she said.

"Done." He was already reaching for his coat. "Now lock the till, kill the lights, and stay away from the windows. I need to make a call — and then you're going to tell me everything you remember about that man, starting with the exact shape of his hands."

"The *hands*?"

"Faces can change," Jonah said, pulling open the back door into the dark of the stockroom. "Hands don't lie. And I think I've seen those hands before — holding a camera, twenty-two years ago, in a case file everybody wanted closed."

The beige sedan's headlights flicked on, once, like a blink — or a signal — and slid away into the rain.
