## 3. Cipher of Tides

The confession sat on my kitchen table for two days before I touched it again, the way you might leave a snake in a jar to see if it's really dead.

I'd memorized it, of course. That's not a boast; it's just how I'm built. Two readings and every word was filed away — the looped descenders on the *y*s, the ink bleed where the pen had paused at "accidental," the faint coffee ring someone else's mug had left on the paper before it ever reached me. My memory doesn't ask if I want to keep things. It keeps them the way the sea keeps things: without permission, without mercy, and for far longer than anyone would consider reasonable.

So while the physical letter sat there gathering my dread, the copy in my head did the actual work.

Here is what I noticed, in the order I noticed it:

First: the confession was too long. A man confessing to murder says *I did it* and maybe *here's why*. This one ran four pages of flowing, almost lyrical prose about Elena Marsh, the water, the night, the guilt. Nobody writes like that when they're confessing. People write like that when they're performing — or when they need a lot of room to hide something.

Second: certain words were wrong. Not misspelled — *chosen*. "The tide was at its **flood**." "She went down at **slack** water." "The moon stood **full** over the cove." A killer describing his crime wouldn't reach for tidal terminology three times in four paragraphs unless the tides were the point.

Third — and this was the one that made me put down my tea with a click that sounded unnaturally loud in my kitchen — the numbers. Scattered through the text like stones in a streambed: *four fathoms*, *the ninth hour*, *three hundred yards from the light*, *a depth of two and seven*. On their own, they were texture. Atmosphere. Together, they were something else entirely.

I got up, found a pencil, and wrote them out in a column on the back of an envelope from the gas company.

4 — 9 — 300 — 2 — 7

Then I went back through the memorized text and pulled every other number, however innocuous. The confession mentioned dates, distances, depths, times. Eleven figures in all, once you counted everything, including a reference to "the spring tide of that year" that gave me a specific month.

Eleven numbers. And a confession that read like it had been written by someone who'd swallowed a nautical almanac.

I stood at my kitchen window for a long time, watching the harbor do its gray morning business, and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from recognition. Because I knew what those numbers were. I'd grown up within earshot of a foghorn; I'd spent one miserable summer as a teenager working the marina fuel dock, memorizing tide tables out of boredom until the owner told me to stop showing off to customers.

They were tidal data. Depths and times and heights. The confession wasn't a confession at all.

It was coordinates wearing a confession as a coat.

* * *

The problem was that knowing the numbers were tidal didn't get me anywhere near reading them. Tidal prediction isn't arithmetic you can do in your head — well, other people can't; I can, but only after I have the tables, and even then it's the kind of party trick that gets you uninvited to parties. Every station along the coast keeps its own records, corrected for local topography, dredging history, storm damage. The same moon produces different water at every point on the shoreline. To decode the cipher, I needed to know which station's tables the writer had used.

Which meant the coastal archive.

I'll be honest about my relationship with archives: we are not friends. An archive is a building full of things I cannot forget after seeing once, arranged by people who believe organization is a moral virtue, staffed — historically, in my experience — by people who regard questions as a form of assault. I had been thrown out of exactly two archives in my career, both times for noticing things about their filing systems that the filing systems' custodians preferred unnoticed.

But the letters had said *decipher*, and the second letter had used the word *unless* in a way that still visited me around three in the morning. So on Wednesday morning I drove out past the harbor mouth to the Coastal Records Office, a low brick building squatting behind the dunes like something the sea had tried to take twice and given up on.

The place smelled the way all archives smell: dust, old glue, and the faint vinegar of degrading microfilm. A bell over the door announced me to an empty front desk. I waited. Somewhere in the back, drawers opened and closed with the contented rhythm of someone who genuinely loved this work.

"Be with you in a moment," called a voice — low, unhurried, with the kind of accent that has survived several governments.

I looked at the reading room while I waited. Long oak tables, green-shaded lamps, a wall of tide tables bound in cracked blue cloth going back sixty years. Charts in flat drawers under the windows. A framed photograph of some long-dead hydrographer looking mildly disappointed in everyone who would ever come after him.

And then the woman herself came through the doorway, and I revised several assumptions at once.

She was perhaps sixty-five, silver hair cropped close, dressed in a cardigan the color of storm water over a blouse that had clearly been ironed by someone who believed wrinkles were a character flaw. She moved like the building belonged to her and she was permitting it to stand. Her name tag said N. OKONKWO, and beneath the name tag her expression said *state your business quickly*.

"I'm looking for historical tide tables," I said. "For the harbor station. Five years back, specifically."

"Five years back for what purpose?" She didn't say it rudely. She said it the way a border guard says it.

"Private research."

"Everyone who comes in here is doing private research," she said. "Half of them are lawyers, a quarter of them are grieving, and the rest are writing novels nobody will read. Which are you?"

That startled a laugh out of me before I could stop it. "Investigator. Insurance, mostly. And possibly grieving, though I haven't decided whose funeral it is yet."

Something shifted in her face — not warmth, exactly. More like a door opening onto another locked door. "Tide tables are public record," she said. "Harbor station volumes are on the open shelf. Third case, bottom row. Sign the visitor book, don't use pens near the charts, and if you break a volume's spine I will know, because I know everything that happens in this room."

"I believe you," I said, and meant it.

* * *

The tables were exactly what I needed and exactly useless, which is the archive's signature move.

I spread the harbor station volume for the relevant year across a table under one of the green lamps and began cross-referencing the eleven numbers against predicted high waters, low waters, current speeds, chart datums. If the cipher used this station's tables, certain combinations should resolve into meaningful pairs — a time matched to a height, a height matched to a distance. I worked for an hour. I produced nothing but a headache and a growing conviction that I was reading the wrong book.

The trouble was the corrections. Tide tables give you predictions; the real sea adds its own annotations. Storm surge, barometric pressure, river discharge — every actual tide differs from its prediction, and the difference is logged separately in what the trade calls the residual observations. If the cipher's author had used predicted values, fine. If he'd used observed ones, I needed a different volume, and there were six possible volumes, and each one would take hours.

I was staring at the residuals for the fourth month running when a shadow fell across my table.

"You're reading it wrong," said Nadia Okonkwo.

I looked up. She was holding a reshelving cart she had no apparent intention of using, and looking at my spread of papers with the expression of a surgeon watching a first-year student hold a scalpel by the wrong end.

"I'm sorry?"

"The residuals." She set down the cart, came around the table, and tapped one finger on the page — not touching the paper itself; even her finger respected the rules. "You're treating them as corrections to apply. You should be reading them as a sequence. Look at the pattern of the deviations, not the values." She glanced up at me. "Who taught you charts?"

"A man at a marina fuel dock who wanted me to stop reciting them at customers."

"Hm." The syllable contained multitudes, none of them flattering. "And who taught you to look for patterns?"

"Nobody. That's the problem. It's just something my head does whether I want it to or not."

She studied me for a moment the way she'd probably study a document of uncertain provenance — checking for forgery. Whatever she saw, she apparently decided was authentic, because she sighed the sigh of a woman about to do something she'd promised herself she wouldn't.

"Wait here."

She disappeared into the stacks and returned with a second volume — not the harbor station but the smaller station up the coast at Greywater Point, the one that had been decommissioned eight years ago when they automated the light. "Your cipher," she said, setting it down beside my work. "If it's real, and if whoever built it knows this coast, they wouldn't use the harbor tables. Too many people consult them. Too many eyes." She opened the Greywater Point volume to the right year. "A decommissioned station's records, though? Those live in one place." She tapped the cover. "Here. With me."

I stared at her. "How did you know it was a cipher?"

"Because you've been here ninety minutes and you've written eleven numbers on the back of an envelope and drawn arrows between them like a man defusing a bomb." She straightened. "Also, you flinched when I said *residuals*, which means the word means more to you than it should. Investigators who flinch at words are investigators chasing something. I've seen it before." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under a boat — grief, or the memory of it. "More than I wanted to."

"What did you see before?"

"That," she said, "is not a question I answer on a first visit." She turned to go, then stopped. "The Greywater Point tables. Start with the observed highs, not the predicted. And Mr.—"

"Villalobos. Gideon Villalobos."

"Mr. Villalobos. The archive closes at five. But for documents that matter—" and here she looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, "—I have been known to stay late."

* * *

She was right, of course. That was the humiliating part, and also the exhilarating part, and I want to be precise about how rare that combination is for me.

Greywater Point's observed high-water times, laid against my eleven numbers, began to resolve within twenty minutes. Not completely — the cipher had layers, I could feel them, the way you can feel a locked door has a second lock behind the first — but the outer mechanism turned. Pairs of numbers became dates and times. Times became tide states. And when I mapped the resolved pairs against a chart of the coastline, they clustered.

Not at the harbor. Not at any of the places the confession's prose had gestured toward so lyrically.

At a cove. A small, unnamed dent in the coastline two miles north of Greywater Point, the kind of place that appears on charts as a hazard warning and in no one's vacation photographs. The numbers resolved to a position within it, and a tidal state — a specific low water, low enough to expose what higher tides would keep.

I sat back under the green lamp and looked at what I'd decoded, and the reading room was very quiet, and outside the window the actual sea went on doing whatever it was doing, keeping whatever it was keeping.

It wasn't a confession. I'd been right about that. It was a location and a condition — *this place, this tide* — wrapped in four pages of manufactured remorse. Someone had written me a treasure map and disguised it as a guilty conscience, and the treasure was buried below the tideline, available only when the sea drew breath and held it.

Which raised the question I'd been avoiding since Monday: if the writer knew where something was, and knew exactly which tide would reveal it —

He'd been there. Recently enough to remember the water's habits precisely. Or he'd put something there himself, and the confession was less a confession than an invitation. *Come and see.*

Or a test. Or bait. With this correspondent, I was learning, those three words were synonyms.

The clock over the desk said ten to five. Through the doorway to the stacks I could hear Nadia Okonkwo moving among her shelves, reshelving with the contented precision of a woman whose kingdom was in order, and I found myself hesitating over something I hadn't hesitated over in years: the question of whether to tell someone what I'd found.

My whole life, the memory has made me a closed circuit. Everything goes in; very little goes out. People find it unsettling, being remembered that completely. I've learned to keep the vault shut.

But she'd read my envelope of arrows in ninety seconds. She'd known which volume before I did. And when she'd said *I've seen it before*, something in her voice had sounded like a person standing at the edge of an old wound, checking whether it still bled.

I gathered my notes and walked back to the front desk, where she was stamping returns with small violent satisfaction.

"The Greywater Point tables," I said. "They worked. The numbers resolve to a cove north of the station. And a specific low tide."

Her stamp stopped mid-air.

For a moment she said nothing at all, and I watched her decide something — watched it happen behind her eyes like weather changing. Then she set the stamp down, aligned it precisely with the edge of the desk, and looked at me with an expression that had lost all its border-guard frost.

"A cove," she repeated. "North of Greywater Point."

"You know it."

"Everyone on this coast knows it." She took off her glasses, which I understood suddenly was the equivalent of another woman removing armor. "What I know, Mr. Villalobos, is that five years ago a girl drowned off this coast, and the file they built around her drowning was the last thing I ever requested from this archive before certain people decided I'd asked one question too many." She put the glasses back on, and the armor with them, but her voice stayed quiet and absolutely steady. "So. You're going to tell me what your cipher says, and then you're going to tell me why an insurance investigator walks in here with murder in his envelope, and then—"

She paused. Outside, the gulls were making their arguments over the harbor.

"—and then maybe I'll tell you what the official files leave out. Because they always leave something out. I made a career of finding it, and it cost me the career."

I looked at this woman I'd met four hours ago, who had corrected my chart-reading without being asked and dismantled my assumptions without raising her voice, and I felt something I hadn't felt since I was a boy: the strange, vertiginous relief of being *read* — thoroughly, accurately, and without flinching.

"The Marsh girl," I said. "Elena Marsh."

Nadia Okonkwo went very still.

"How," she said slowly, "do you know that name?"

And that, I realized, was a very good question — because I hadn't told her, and the cipher hadn't either, and the only place I'd gotten it was from a dead woman's file that somebody had already tampered with once.

The lamp hummed. The clock ticked toward five.

"I think," I said, "we'd better both sit down."
