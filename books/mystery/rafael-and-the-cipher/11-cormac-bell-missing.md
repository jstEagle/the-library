## 11. Cormac Bell, Missing

The date in the second letter was the fourteenth of March. I had it memorized by the time I finished reading it the first time — of course I did, that's the curse and the whole point of me — and I had written it on three calendars, taped it to my bathroom mirror, and set an alarm on my phone labeled with a word I won't repeat here.

I was still late.

Not fashionably late. Not late the way you're late to a dentist appointment, with a shrug and a story about traffic. I was late the way a man is late when he has spent three days chasing a ghost through paperwork instead of standing in front of the one living person the ghost had promised to kill.

Here is how it happened, because I have replayed it so many times that the sequence has worn grooves in my mind like a record needle stuck in the same eight seconds:

On the eleventh, I went to the harbor master's office to confirm Bell's shift schedule. The clerk, a young woman with chipped nail polish and the thousand-yard stare of someone who answers phones for a living, told me Bell had swapped his shifts — taken the fourteenth off, given away his pilot runs for the week. "Said he had family business," she said, and chewed her gum like it owed her money.

Family business. Cormac Bell had no family. I'd read his file twice. Divorced, no children, a brother in Aberdeen he hadn't spoken to since their mother's funeral. When a man with no family suddenly acquires some, what he's really acquired is fear.

On the twelfth, I tried his house. No answer. The neighbor — a retired postman with a dog shaped like a mop — told me Bell had left before dawn with two duffel bags and hadn't come back. "Gone fishing," the neighbor said, in the tone people use when they know it's a lie and are offering it to you anyway as a courtesy.

On the thirteenth, I drove to the marine unit to ask about Bell's boat, *Kestrel*, a twenty-six-foot pilot cutter he kept at the north moorings. The gate guard told me the officer who could help was off duty. I asked who. He told me. I wrote the name down. It meant nothing then. Names mean nothing until they mean everything, and by then it's usually too late, which is practically the motto of my profession.

And on the fourteenth itself — the day, the actual day, circled in red in a dead man's handwriting disguised as a killer's — I spent the morning with Nadia Okonkwo in the coastal archive, cross-referencing tide tables against the cipher's outer layer, because I had convinced myself that the coordinates were the key, that the water would tell me where to be, that mathematics could outrun murder.

Nadia found it first. She always finds things first; it's her superpower, along with an ability to make bureaucrats feel two inches tall using only her eyebrows.

"Gideon." She said my name the way you'd say *fire* in a crowded theater. She was holding the second letter flat under the archive lamp, her reading glasses pushed up into her gray curls. "This date. Today's date. What did the letter say would happen today?"

I recited it. Word for word, punctuation included, because my brain doesn't know how to do anything else. *"Cormac Bell will learn what Elena learned. Unless you can stop it, which you cannot."*

We were in the car within ninety seconds. I don't remember locking the archive door. I remember Nadia doing it, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that she locked doors the way other people breathe — automatically, competently, without making a production of it.

The drive to the harbor took eleven minutes. I know because I counted, and counting is what I do instead of praying.

* * *

The north moorings sit past the breakwater, past the fuel dock, past the row of lobster boats that all have names like *Second Chance* and *Enough Already*, which tells you everything you need to know about the people who work this water. *Kestrel* was tied at slip fourteen, and from thirty yards away I knew something was wrong, the way you know a face is wrong before you can say why.

Boats at rest have a sound. A small language of hawsers creaking, fenders sighing, hulls knocking gently against rubber. *Kestrel* was silent. Silent the way a stopped clock is silent — not peaceful, just wrong.

"She's listing," Nadia said beside me. "Starboard side."

I was already running. I want to be clear about that, for the record I keep in my head, the one where I am both defendant and prosecutor: I ran. Whatever else I got wrong, whatever hours I burned chasing coordinates through tidal arithmetic while a man was being taken apart somewhere in the dark, I ran when it mattered, and I was still too late, and those two facts are going to live in the same room inside me for a very long time.

The starboard gunwale was nearly touching the water. I stepped aboard — Nadia shouted something about calling the harbor police, and her voice was already shrinking behind me — and dropped through the companionway into a cabin that smelled of diesel, cold coffee, and fear.

Fear has a smell. Most people think that's poetry. It isn't. It's chemistry. Cortisol, sweat gone sour, the faint ammonia note of a body that hasn't slept. Cormac Bell had been afraid in this cabin for days, and the cabin had kept the receipt.

There was a mug on the chart table, half full, a skin across the coffee like pond ice. A chart of the outer harbor lay open beneath it, weighted at the corners with a wrench, a flashlight, and a tin of sardines he'd never opened. And on the chart, in grease pencil, someone had drawn a circle around the old beacon channel marker — the one they call the Widow's Post, because of course they do, because this coast names its dangers the way other places name their streets.

The engine was warm.

I put my hand on the housing before I understood what I was doing, and the metal was blood-heat warm, maybe warmer, the kind of warmth that means minutes, not hours. Someone had run this engine recently. Recently enough that if I had come straight here on the twelfth, or even the morning of the fourteenth instead of losing the day to tide tables —

"Stop," I said out loud, to myself, in an empty cabin. It's a trick Reyes taught me, back when he was teaching me things instead of becoming one of them. *When your head starts eating itself, say stop, out loud, like you'd tell a dog.* It works about forty percent of the time. That day it worked for maybe ten seconds.

Then I saw the rope.

It was coiled neatly on the bunk — neatly, which was the detail that turned my stomach over. A length of polypropylene line, the cheap floating kind every working boat carries, cut clean at both ends. And threaded through the bight of it, snagged where whoever cut it had been in too much of a hurry to notice, was a single strand of something pale. Hair? Fiber? I didn't touch it. Five years ago I'd have bagged it myself. Now I stood there with my hands in my pockets like a man at a funeral, and waited for the professionals, and hated every second of the waiting.

Because here's what the rope told me, in the silent language of objects: Bell had been tied. Bell had gotten loose or been cut loose or cut himself loose — the fraying at one end suggested struggle, the clean cut at the other suggested a blade — and then someone had put the rope away. Coiled it. Tidied it.

You don't coil a rope after a kidnapping. You coil a rope after a ritual. After something you've done before, more than once, until the motions live in your hands.

* * *

The harbor police arrived in nineteen minutes. I know because Nadia timed them, and later she told me the number in the gentle voice she uses when she thinks I'm about to do something self-destructive, which is most voices she uses with me, now that I think about it.

Inspector Okafor — no relation to Nadia, though the universe clearly has a sense of humor — walked the deck of *Kestrel* with the weary competence of a man who has done this walk too many times. He was polite to me in the way police are polite to civilians who find things, which is to say he thanked me twice and asked me not to touch anything a third time, and I said of course, absolutely, while internally cataloguing everything I'd already touched.

"Engine's warm," he said, crouched at the transom. His tech confirmed it: run within the last hour, maybe two. Cold-started, idled, shut down.

"So he left under his own power," Nadia said. "Or was made to look like it."

"Or the boat came back without him," I said.

They both looked at me.

"Think about it," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I intended, the way it does when the machinery underneath is running hot. "Bell swaps his shifts. Takes bags out of his house before dawn. He's running — we agree on that? He's been terrified since the parcel arrived, he knows the date, he knows what it means. So he plans an exit. Boat's the only way out that doesn't leave a paper trail. But the boat comes home. Engine warm. Rope coiled. Coffee abandoned mid-sip." I pointed at the mug above us, visible through the companionway. "You don't abandon coffee if you're leaving voluntarily. You take the cup. Everybody takes the cup. It's the first thing hands do."

Okafor wrote something down. "You're saying he came back to the boat."

"I'm saying somebody brought the boat back. Or somebody brought Bell back." I looked at the circle on the chart, the grease-pencil ring around the Widow's Post. "And drew our attention to exactly where they wanted us to look, which is either a confession or a misdirection, and with this particular correspondent it has never once been a confession."

The letters had taught me that much. The confession letter confessed to nothing true. The threat letter threatened nothing random. Everything the killer wrote was furniture arranged in a room — placed precisely so that when you entered, your eyes went where they were supposed to go.

Which raised a question I said out loud, because saying things out loud is how I keep them from metastasizing:

"How did the killer know Bell would run to his boat?"

Silence on the deck. Gulls arguing over something in the water. Okafor's pen stopped moving.

"Bell's a careful man," I went on. "Terrified, but careful. He swapped shifts quietly. He left before dawn. If he planned to run by sea, he wouldn't have told a soul. So either the killer watched him — continuously, patiently, for days — or—" and here my stomach did the thing it does, the slow elevator drop, "—or the killer didn't need to watch him, because the killer already knew what a frightened man does with a boat, because the killer has stood on this exact deck before, or one like it, wearing a uniform, asking routine questions, learning routines."

"The file," Nadia said quietly. Behind her, the sun was coming through the harbor haze and turning her silhouette into something almost architectural. "Everyone in this case keeps coming back to the file."

Okafor cleared his throat with the delicacy of a man deciding how much he wanted to hear. "Mr. Villalobos. Off the record. This business with the letters — the Marsh case connection — you've shared that with us?"

"I've shared what I can prove."

"And what you can't?"

"Is in a sealed file," I said, "that five years ago passed through the hands of every senior officer in this district, and at least one page of which has been replaced with a forgery so good that only a man with my particular defect could catch it. So no, Inspector. What I can't prove is currently beyond anyone's jurisdiction, including mine, including yours, including God's, probably."

He took that better than most would. Wrote something else down. Said he'd put out a missing persons bulletin, canvass the fuel dock, pull the marina camera footage — "though half those cameras haven't worked since the flood" — and that I should expect a call.

I should expect a call. In my experience, expecting a call is the emotional equivalent of keeping a lottery ticket in your wallet: technically hope, functionally decoration.

* * *

Nadia drove us back. I sat in the passenger seat of her ancient Volvo and watched the harbor shrink in the wing mirror and performed the exercise I hate most, which is honest accounting.

Three days. I had three days of warning — no, five, counting from the second letter's arrival — and I spent them like a rich man spends found money. Chasing coordinates. Decoding tides. Building elegant little theories about coves and spring tides and the geometry of secrets, while the actual human being the letters had named went on living his ordinary terrified life, drinking bad coffee, checking his locks, planning his escape down a channel marked with a grease pencil.

"You weren't late," Nadia said, eyes on the road.

"I was late."

"You decoded a cipher nobody else in this country could have decoded. You identified the pattern. You—"

"Nadia." I watched a gull hang motionless over the breakwater, riding air it couldn't see. "The letter gave a date. The date was today. I was at the archive today. Reading tide tables. For next year." I heard my own voice crack on *next year*, which embarrassed me, so I pressed on before she could be kind about it. "Bell is gone because I treated this like a puzzle instead of a countdown. The killer counted on that, I think. Counted on me being the kind of man who can't resist the lock more than the door."

She was quiet for a full kilometer. Nadia's silences are load-bearing; you can lean on them.

"When I was pushed out of the archive," she said finally, "there was a records clerk, a young man, who testified against me to the review board. Sweet boy. Genuinely believed he was doing right. Years later he found me at a conference and apologized, and do you know what I said?" She glanced at me, quick, then back to the road. "I said, *you were a smaller piece of a larger machine, and machines don't apologize.* And then I went home and cried for two days, because it was a generous lie. Machines are made of people, Gideon. Every gear is a person. Every person gets to decide whether they turn."

"What's your point?"

"My point is that guilt is only useful if it's fuel. Otherwise it's just rust." She pulled up outside my building, killed the engine, and turned to face me fully, which she reserves for pronouncements. "So. Fuel or rust. Choose by morning."

She left. I sat in the Volvo's cooling seat and let the harbor smell follow me in — salt, diesel, rope — and thought about the rope coiled on the bunk, neat as a hymnal.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I want to report that I tried, but honesty is the one luxury my memory affords me. I sat at my kitchen table until two in the morning with the case spread out around me like a card game I was losing, and at some point I became aware that my mind had left the room.

It does that. My memory, I mean. People assume photographic memory is a filing cabinet, tidy and obedient. It's closer to a haunted house. Every room I've ever entered is still furnished in my head, still lit, still occupied. And when I'm tired, or guilty, or afraid, the doors start opening on their own.

That night the house of my head opened onto a corridor I hadn't walked in fifteen years: the training academy, winter, fluorescent light. A younger Reyes — dark-haired then, patient, terrifyingly competent — leaning over my shoulder while I read a case file in one pass.

*"Again,"* he'd said, and I'd recited it back, and he'd laughed — actually laughed, a sound I could count on one hand across our whole acquaintance — and said, *"Do you have any idea what you are, Villalobos? Do you have any idea what we could do together?"*

*What we could do together.*

I sat with that sentence in the dark kitchen for a long time. Then I got out the second letter and read it again, slowly, letting each phrase register like a photograph developing.

*Cormac Bell will learn what Elena learned.*

Elena Marsh had learned something. Something worth killing for, five years ago. And now Bell — harbor pilot, thirty years on this water, a man whose job was knowing every current, every shoal, every hidden thing the sea kept — Bell had learned it too. Or seen it. Or carried it.

Pilots see everything that moves on the water. That's the entire job.

I looked at the grease-pencil circle in my memory — the Widow's Post, the old beacon marker, the ring drawn on Bell's chart by a hand that wasn't his. A location. An invitation. A stage already set.

And underneath the grief, underneath the rust, something in me shifted weight and began, quietly, to burn.

They had taken Bell on my watch. Fine. Then I would stop watching and start hunting — and the first thing a hunter needs is to understand the animal, and the oldest trail I had led straight back through fifteen years to a winter classroom, a laughing mentor, and the question I had never once let myself ask:

What exactly had Inspector Reyes taught me to see?

And what had he taught me not to?

I reached for the phone to call Nadia, then saw the clock — 3:47 a.m. — and put it down. Instead I pulled a blank sheet of paper toward me, and at the top, in block letters, I wrote the only sentence that mattered:

*Who benefits from a dead harbor pilot?*

My pen hovered. Somewhere out past the breakwater, the tide was turning, and the sea was holding whatever it held, patient as paperwork.

The answer, when it finally came off my pen, was going to cost me.
