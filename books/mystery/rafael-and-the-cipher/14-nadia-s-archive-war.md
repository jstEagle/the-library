## 14. Nadia's Archive War

The Coastal Records Annex smelled the same as it had eleven years ago: dust, toner, and the particular despair of a building that had been scheduled for demolition twice and reprieved twice by people who'd never set foot in it. I stood in front of the glass doors for a full minute before going in, which is the kind of thing you do when you're about to knock on the door of the institution that fired you.

Not fired. "Restructured." That was the word they used. Restructured, as though I were a wall that needed moving rather than a woman who had asked one question too many.

I pressed my palm flat against the glass. Old habit. The door used to stick in humid weather; you learned to push with your whole body, like shoving open a stubborn tide. They'd replaced the door at some point. Of course they had. Institutions will spend money on hinges long before they'll spend it on answers.

"Morning," said the young man at the desk, not looking up. His nameplate read D. FERRIS, ASSISTANT RECORDS OFFICER, and beneath that, in smaller print, as if apologizing for itself: *provisional*.

"Nadia Okonkwo," I said. "Formerly senior archivist."

That got his eyes up. Recognition moved across his face the way weather moves across water — there and gone, but unmistakable. He knew my name. Which meant someone had told him to know it, or told him to be careful of it, which amounts to the same thing in a bureaucracy.

"Ms. Okonkwo. We don't have you in the system anymore."

"I'm aware. I'm here under a research access provision. Section 12 of the Public Records Act." I laid Gideon Villalobos's letter on the counter between us — he'd written it out formally, investigator's request, case number cited, his handwriting so precise it looked typeset. "A closed drowning inquiry. Marsh, Elena. 2019. I need the sealed annex materials: coroner's supplemental file, chain-of-custody log, and any correspondence between the marine unit and the deputy coroner's office."

Ferris looked at the letter the way a man looks at a spider on his sleeve. "Sealed materials require a judicial order."

"They require a judicial order only if the requester is a member of the public. A licensed investigator attached to an active inquiry has standing under Section 12(b). And before you reach for the other objection — yes, the Marsh case is active again. Reopened informally pending review of new evidence. Mr. Villalobos can confirm it, Inspector Reyes can confirm it, and the fact that you're hesitating tells me somebody upstairs already knows all this."

He did reach for something, but it was only his coffee, and he drank from it like a man buying time. "I'll have to check with my supervisor."

"Of course you will." I smiled at him, and I made sure the smile reached my eyes, because a smile that doesn't reach the eyes is a weapon and I wasn't ready to show him mine yet. "Take your time. I've waited eleven years; I can wait until your supervisor finishes her tea."

* * *

Here is what happened eleven years ago, because you can't understand a war without knowing where the first shot was fired.

I spent thirty-one years in that archive. I started when the catalog was index cards and I ended when it was a database, and I will tell you plainly which one lied to me more often. Cards don't quietly rewrite themselves. A card is honest: it says what some tired clerk wrote in 1974 and nothing else. A database says whatever its last editor wanted it to say, and keeps no scars to show for it.

In my last year, I ran a routine integrity audit — the kind of housekeeping nobody notices and everybody depends on. Chain-of-custody logs against physical holdings. Signatures against personnel files. Dates against tide tables, because coastal records are full of dates and dates are full of lies when nobody checks them against the moon.

I found eleven discrepancies over six months. Small ones. A page renumbered here. A witness statement whose paper stock didn't match the rest of its file there. A coroner's certification signed on a day the certifying officer was, according to the duty roster, two counties away. Nothing dramatic. Nothing you could point at and shout about. Just the faint wrongness of a ledger that had been touched by hands that shouldn't have been touching it.

I reported them. In writing. Through channels. That was my mistake — through channels. Channels are how institutions digest things. My report went up the chain, was reviewed by three committees, and came back down as a directive: consolidate the audit findings into a single summary memo, omit "non-material variances," and refrain from characterizing discrepancies as "potential tampering" absent forensic confirmation.

Non-material variances. As though a forged signature were a rounding error.

I rewrote the memo four times, each time refusing to soften the language, each time having it sent back softer than I'd written it. On the fifth pass I did something unwise and unforgivable in bureaucratic terms: I sent the original, unsoftened version directly to the oversight board, cc'ing nobody.

Six weeks later, my position was eliminated in a restructuring. My access codes were revoked on a Friday afternoon. I was given twenty minutes and a cardboard box, and a security escort who couldn't meet my eyes, and I remember most clearly — isn't it always the small thing? — that they let me keep my reading glasses case but not my personal mug, because the mug was technically archive property. Thirty-one years, and the line they drew was around a mug.

The oversight board never acted on my report. I checked, later, through a friend still inside. The report had been received, logged, and marked *resolved — no further action*. Resolved. Eleven discrepancies, resolved without a single one being investigated.

You learn things from being swallowed. You learn that institutions are not stupid; they are digestive. Anything inconvenient gets broken down and passed through, and what comes out the other end is clean, odorless paperwork. You learn that the people who run archives are rarely the people who control them. And you learn — this is the lesson that matters — that whoever altered those files knew exactly which doors my report would travel through, because every one of those doors closed behind it in perfect sequence.

Somebody inside had watched me coming for six months and let me walk all the way to the edge before pulling the rug.

I never found out who. For eleven years, that unfinished sentence sat in me like a fishhook.

Then a man with a photographic memory walked into my reading room and asked me to check a tide table, and the hook began to move.

* * *

Ferris returned after forty minutes — long enough to have actually consulted someone, short enough that the consultation had been a phone call, not a meeting. Behind him came a woman I recognized with a jolt that went all the way down: Margaret Ilves, records compliance officer, who had been junior staff when I left and was now, apparently, the person who decided what the public was allowed to remember.

"Nadia." She said my name warmly. Warmth, in a compliance officer, is a tool like any other. "It's been ages. Listen, we'd love to help, but the sealed annex on Marsh is — there are complications."

"What complications?"

"Chain of custody issues." She said it smoothly, professionally, and then — and this is why I've never quite managed to hate her — she blinked, just once, a half-second too late. "The file's integrity can't currently be certified, so releasing it could compromise any future proceedings."

I let the silence sit. It's the oldest archivist's trick and the truest: say nothing, and people fill the quiet with the truth they were trying to bury.

She filled it. "What I mean is, if pages went missing or were substituted at some point, and then we released the file, and it turned out to be incomplete — that's liability. For the office."

"So the file *is* incomplete." I kept my voice level, almost gentle. "Or altered."

"I didn't say—"

"You did, Margaret. Beautifully. You said its integrity can't be certified. In eleven years of asking, that's the closest anyone has come to admitting it out loud." I tapped Gideon's letter on the counter. "Which means Section 12(b) doesn't just give us standing — it makes denial a reportable obstruction. An investigator on an active inquiry, denied access to a record your own compliance officer has just acknowledged may have been compromised? I can write that sentence tonight, and I promise you it reads worse than it sounds."

Ilves looked at Ferris. Ferris looked at his coffee. Somewhere a printer groaned through a job nobody had claimed, the way printers do in government buildings, mourning.

"Give me an hour," Ilves said finally.

"Take two. I'll be in the reading room, visiting old friends."

* * *

The reading room hadn't changed either. Same green-shaded lamps, same long tables scarred by decades of pen points and coffee rings, same radiator that knocked twice at the quarter hour like a neighbor asking if you were home. I sat at my old seat — third from the window, the one with the best morning light for reading faded ink — and allowed myself the indulgence of remembering.

Thirty-one years. I had married in that room's shadow, or near enough; my husband used to bring lunch on Thursdays and complain that I loved the dead letters more than him, and I'd tell him the dead letters never expected dinner. After he died, the archive was the thing that held me together — not the work exactly, but the *order* of it. Every document in its place. Every lie eventually findable, if you were patient enough, because paper remembers even when people don't.

Paper remembers. That was my creed for three decades. Then I discovered paper can be made to forget, and it broke something in me that took years to set.

Gideon understood that. I saw it the first week, watching him reconstruct a corrupted shipping manifest from memory alone, his eyes half-closed, reciting columns of figures like scripture. He carried the archive inside his skull, complete and helpless against the same enemy as mine: people who edit. Two custodians of memory, one external, one internal, both of us exiled by the same disease.

When I'd told him, three nights ago over soup in my kitchen, why I'd really left the annex — not the polite version, the real one — he'd put down his spoon and said, "They didn't fire you for making mistakes. They fired you for not making any." And then he'd apologized for the joke being inadequate, which was somehow the most adequate thing anyone had said to me in a decade.

Found family, my late husband's sister called such arrangements. People who find you when your own institutions have lost you.

* * *

Ilves came back in fifty minutes, carrying a gray archival box and a face carefully arranged into neutrality.

"Coroner's supplemental file, Marsh, Elena, 2019," she recited. "Chain-of-custody log. Correspondence index — the correspondence itself requires separate authorization, but the index is included." She set the box down and slid a release form across the counter. "Sign here. And Nadia—" she lowered her voice, and for a moment the compliance officer was gone and the junior staffer I'd once mentored looked out, frightened, "—the seal log on this box shows it was opened twice since interment. Once for the original inquest. Once fourteen months ago. No requester listed. No purpose listed. Just a signature code."

"Whose code?"

"That's the thing." She met my eyes. "It's a valid code. Decommissioned staff tier. It belongs to someone who retired or died — the system doesn't distinguish. But whoever used it knew the system well enough to pick one that wouldn't trigger an alert."

After she walked away, I sat very still with my hand flat on the box lid, feeling the weight of it, and thought: *there you are.* Not the person. The shape of the person. Someone fluent in the archive's grammar, patient enough to use a ghost's credentials, careful enough to leave everything looking proper — and arrogant enough, in the end, to make the one mistake that mattered.

Fourteen months ago. Before Gideon's letters. Before any of this. Which meant the tampering wasn't a response to the investigation.

It was maintenance. Someone tending a garden they'd planted five years ago.

I signed the form, carried the box to my table under the green lamp, and cut the twine with the little knife I'd carried for thirty years and been allowed to keep, because nobody thought to call it archive property.

The chain-of-custody log was the first folder, and I read it the way you read an old lover's letters — slowly, hungrily, looking for the lie between the lines. The Marsh file had passed through nine hands in the weeks after the drowning. Intake clerk. Evidence technician. Marine unit liaison. Coroner's office. Deputy coroner's office — Silas Vane's signature, neat and unhurried, certifying receipt. Back to records. Sealed.

And threaded through all of it, authorizing transfers, extending deadlines, requesting the file "for review" three separate times: the marine unit liaison's countersignature, and beside it, in the authorization column, a stamp I knew better than my own name.

Inspector R. Reyes.

I sat back. The radiator knocked its two knocks. Outside the window, gulls argued over the harbor's leavings, and somewhere down the coast a tide was turning whether anyone watched it or not.

Reyes. The man vouching for Gideon's investigation. The man whose name appeared, I now realized, in the margins of nearly every anomaly I'd flagged eleven years ago — not as a suspect, never that, but as a facilitator. Signatures. Authorizations. The quiet machinery that moves a file from one room to another.

Either Reyes had been the killer's unwitting courier for years, moving boxes he never opened.

Or he had opened every single one.

I photographed nothing — cameras weren't permitted, and besides, I had spent thirty-one years training the only camera I trusted, the one behind my eyes. I copied the log by hand, in my own shorthand, the way I'd done ten thousand times, and felt the old exile fall away from me like a coat I'd outgrown. They could restructure my position. They could take the mug. They could not, it turned out, ever really take the work.

My phone buzzed against the table. Gideon.

"Nadia." His voice was tight, controlled, the control itself a signal. "Where are you?"

"The annex. Why?"

A pause, the length of a man choosing words. "Theo just called me. He was doing his rounds past the archive dumpster this evening — he cuts through there after his last delivery. Nadia, he says there was someone burning papers behind the fence. Half an hour ago. He got close enough to see the coat."

I looked down at the chain-of-custody log, at the neat stamp of Inspector R. Reyes marching through five years of paperwork like a man walking through his own house.

"What kind of coat?" I asked.

"A police inspector's," Gideon said. "Nadia — Theo says he thinks the man knew he was there. He says the figure stopped burning, stood very still, and looked straight at him in the dark. And then went back to feeding the fire."
