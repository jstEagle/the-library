## 18. Tide Tables and Lies

I have spent forty-one years among paper, and I will tell you the secret that no one who works with their hands ever believes: documents lie. Not the way people lie — clumsily, with sweat and contradiction — but beautifully. A forged ledger balances to the penny. A falsified certificate carries the right watermark, the right embossing, the right smell of authority. Paper lies best when it has been made to lie by someone who loves it.

So when Gideon Villalobos spread the cipher across my kitchen table for the eleventh consecutive evening, I did not look at his face. I looked at the numbers.

He had transcribed them again from memory, of course, though he hardly needed to; the original confession letter lived behind his eyes in whatever terrible perfect archive he carried around instead of a soul. He'd written them out in columns because he said the columns helped him think, and I let him believe that, the way you let a proud man keep his umbrella open indoors.

"These are tidal heights," he said, tapping the left-hand column. "I told you. High water marks at some station, day after day. It's the key to the substitution layer."

"You did tell me," I said. "Twice on Tuesday. Once at two in the morning, which I would like noted was not an hour civilized people discuss tides."

"And?"

"And nothing." I turned my teacup a quarter-turn, which is what I do instead of sighing. "Except that I have been reading tide tables since before you were born, young man, and something about these has been itching at me like a wool collar."

Gideon went very still. He does that — goes still the way water goes still before it finds the crack in the wall. "What kind of something?"

"The kind you check rather than guess." I got up, because my knees announce themselves now when I rise from low chairs, and fetched the battered almanac binder I had brought home from the coastal archive three weeks ago, back when this whole affair was one mad letter instead of a countdown. "The cipher gives us fourteen days of readings. If they're genuine, they should match the published tables for whatever station the killer used. That's the elegance of it — real data as a key. Unbreakable unless you know where and when."

"That's what I thought too."

"Yes, well. You thought it and moved on. I thought it and it kept me awake." I found the page. "So tonight, while you were drawing your little columns, I checked."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about retirement: you do not stop being an archivist. The archive moves into you. Forty-one years of cataloguing other people's secrets leaves sediment in a person, and I had long ago stopped pretending mine was inert. When the Marsh case came apart in our hands — when we stood in that cove at low tide and found nothing but wet stone and our own foolish hope — I had gone home and done what I always do with an unsolved problem. I filed it. Carefully. In the drawer of my mind marked OPEN, next to the question of who burned papers behind my archive, and the question of why a deputy coroner's signature appeared on more coastal death certificates than any reasonable tide of mortality should produce.

Silas Vane. I hadn't spoken the name aloud yet, not even to Gideon. But names have a way of rising in a body like gas in a marsh, and I had been feeling the bubble travel for days.

That evening, though, the name didn't matter yet. What mattered was arithmetic.

"Gideon," I said, laying the almanac open beside his transcription, "read me your column. All fourteen values. Slowly."

He read them. He read them the way he reads everything — once, instantly, forever — but he humored me and spoke them aloud anyway, each height to the decimal, and I wrote them down myself because checking another man's memory against paper is a discipline older than either of us.

Then I ran my finger down the published tables for the following spring.

"Now the tables," I said. "For the same station. Read them to me."

He read. And I watched his voice change halfway down the column — a small hitch, barely a syllable's worth, the sound of a man whose inner archive has just flagged a discrepancy between two records that both claim to be true.

"They don't match," he said.

"No," I agreed. "They don't."

"But they're close. Off by centimeters. Small ones. The pattern of highs and lows is identical — same sequence, same intervals, same shape." His finger hovered over the two columns without touching either, as if touching might commit him to an answer. "If someone were misremembering, they wouldn't misremember the *shape*. The rhythm of a tide is the easiest thing in the world to hold onto. It's the numbers that drift."

"Which means," I said, "that whoever built this cipher wasn't misremembering anything."

I let that sit while I poured us both more tea, mostly because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. Gideon had gone into that particular silence of his that I had learned, over these weeks, to distinguish from his ordinary silences. This one had teeth in it.

"Nadia." His voice was careful. "Whose tables are those? What year am I looking at?"

I turned the almanac so he could see the cover.

His face did something I had never seen it do in all our weeks of working together. Gideon Villalobos, who had stared down a corrupt inspector and a burning dumpster and the ghost of a drowned girl without blinking, looked at a publication date and went pale.

"Next year," he said.

"Next year," I confirmed. "The cipher's tidal data matches the tables for the coming spring. Not last year's. Not this year's. Next year's. Published four months ago, in a print run of perhaps six hundred copies, most of which sit unopened in harbor offices until someone needs to know when to bring a boat in."

* * *

We sat with that for a long moment. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the street Theo's bicycle bell rang twice — his signal, these days, that he was passing and everything was ordinary, a small ritual we had established after the business with the coat and the photograph, because the boy deserved to know that someone was listening for him even when nothing was wrong.

"Explain it to me plainly," Gideon finally said. "Because there are two explanations and I don't like either."

"Good. Then we agree." I sat back down. "Explanation one: coincidence. The killer used next year's tables by accident — grabbed the wrong binder off a shelf, never noticed."

"You don't believe that."

"I have worked with accidental errors for forty-one years. Accidental errors are messy. They transpose digits. They skip lines. They do not produce fourteen consecutive readings that match a future year's predictions to within a centimeter while preserving the exact tidal character of the correct season." I tapped the page. "This isn't an error, Gideon. This is a *citation*. Whoever wrote this cipher opened a specific book, turned to a specific page, and copied it faithfully. Deliberately. Which brings us to explanation two."

"He chose next year's tables on purpose."

"He chose them," I said, "because next year's tables contain something this year's do not."

Gideon's eyes went to the window, out toward the coast we couldn't see from my kitchen but both of us could feel, the way you feel a bruise through clothing. "The spring tide."

"The spring tide." I pulled the almanac toward me and turned pages with the confidence of long acquaintance. "Every year has them — the big ones, when sun and moon line up and the sea reaches up higher than it has any business reaching, and pulls back further than it should too. But they're not interchangeable. Each year's springs fall on different dates, reach different heights. And next year's highest spring tide of the season—" I found the line, and read it aloud so there could be no mistake, no reliance on anyone's memory, not even his— "falls on the twenty-ninth of April. Coefficient 118. The biggest water this coast will see for three years."

"When?" he asked, though I could see he already knew, the way you know a thing in your chest before your head consents to it.

"Three weeks from Thursday."

* * *

I want to record, here, what happened to Gideon's face in that moment, because I have come to understand that his face is the only honest document in his possession. Everything else about him — the dry wit, the investigator's calm, the terrible tidy archive behind his eyes — is a filing system. But his face files nothing.

It went, first, to the cove. I watched him go there. We had stood on that wet stone together and found nothing, and I saw him standing there again now, doing the arithmetic that men like him cannot help doing: if the cipher's coordinates were right and the tide was wrong, then the tide was the missing variable. Whatever the sea took from Elena Marsh — whatever it held, wherever it held it — the killer knew it would take a specific, exceptional water to give it back. Or to bury it past finding. Or to make a grave of a place that had almost given up its dead.

And then his face did the second thing, the worse thing. It went to Cormac Bell.

"Bell disappeared on the date in the second letter," Gideon said quietly. "The letter named the day. I was hours late. Hours." He said *hours* the way other men say a wound. "But Nadia — Bell wasn't killed. There was no body. There was a boat with a warm engine and a man gone. What if Bell was never the endpoint? What if the second letter's date wasn't an execution date at all?"

"Then what was it?"

"A rehearsal." His hand flattened on the table. "Or a test. Or — no." He stopped himself, and I watched him do it, watched him physically arrest a thought the way you'd grab a child running toward a road. "No. Say it plainly. If the cipher points to next year's spring tide, then the letters aren't a confession and a threat. They're a schedule. Everything so far has been preparation. The drowning five years ago, Bell's disappearance, the altered file, the taunts — all of it is setup for an act scheduled for the twenty-ninth of April, timed to a tide that hasn't happened yet."

"Timed," I said, "to the only tide big enough to move what he needs moved. Or hide it. Or—" and here I made myself finish, because unfinished sentences are how fear breeds— "or expose it. Gideon. Whoever built this cipher built it around the moment the sea gives up its secrets. Maybe he's going to the cove to make sure nothing comes back. Maybe he's going to collect something. Either way, he has told us the date in the only language he trusts — numbers copied perfectly from a book most people never open."

"Why tell us at all?" Gideon's voice cracked on it, just slightly, and I understood that the crack wasn't fear for himself. It was the old question, the one that had been eating him since the third letter: *you remember everything except what matters.* "Why build the deadline into the puzzle? Why give us three weeks?"

"Because he wants an audience," I said. "Or a witness. Or—" I reached across the table and put my hand over his, which is not something I do lightly; my hands are for paper. "Or he wants *you*, specifically, standing at that cove on the twenty-ninth of April. Think about the photographs in the locker, Gideon. Months of them. He has been studying you longer than you've been studying him. A man like that doesn't set a stage without knowing who's meant to stand on it."

* * *

We worked until midnight, because dread is a fuel and neither of us had anything better to burn.

Gideon rebuilt the cipher's structure aloud while I cross-checked every assumption against the almanac, and slowly the shape of the thing emerged with horrible clarity. The killer had not merely used next year's tables as a key. He had anchored the entire cipher to the twenty-ninth of April — the reference height, the one value from which all the substitutions radiated, was the predicted peak of that spring tide. The number 7.42 meters sat at the center of the grid like a spider. Every other figure in the confession was defined relative to it.

"It's not hidden," Gideon said at last, and his voice had gone flat and cold, the voice of a man reading his own autopsy. "It's a cornerstone. He built the whole message on top of that date. Anyone who solved it fully would find it. He *wanted* the date found — eventually. By the right person. At the right time."

"Three weeks," I said. "Not sooner. He needed time to prepare, and he needed you chasing the earlier layers — the coordinates, the cove, the false trail to Reyes — so that you'd arrive at the date exhausted and doubting yourself."

"Or so that I'd arrive at the date exactly when he wants me to." Gideon rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and for a moment he looked not like the sharpest mind I had ever shared a table with, but like a tired man in his forties who had not slept properly in weeks. "Nadia, I keep turning it over. Vane's certificates. Eleven drownings in nine years, all signed off clean. If the cipher is scheduled around a future tide, then maybe the others were too. Maybe every death he's certified was timed to a tide table. Maybe the paperwork and the water have been in collusion the whole time, and nobody noticed because nobody reads tide tables against death certificates."

I felt the sediment in me shift — forty-one years of it, settling into a new configuration, the way silt rearranges after a storm.

"Gideon," I said slowly, "the archive keeps duplicate sets. Tide tables by year, coroner's registers by year. If you're right — if there's a correlation — it would be in the overlap. Dates of certification against dates of spring tides, going back nine years."

He lifted his head. And I saw it happen, the exhaustion burning off, the machine underneath re-engaging, and I confess I found it a frightening and magnificent thing to witness, the way you find a lighthouse frightening and magnificent when you are the one in the water.

"Eleven deaths," he said. "Nine years. Twenty-two possible spring tides, high and low, plus the equinoctial peaks. If even half of his certifications land within a day of a major tide—"

"—then Silas Vane doesn't use the sea to hide his work," I finished. "He uses it to *schedule* it. The tide is his calendar, his shovel, and his filing cabinet, all in one. And on the twenty-ninth of April, at 7.42 meters, he intends to file something new."

The clock in my hallway struck twelve. Down the street, faintly, a bicycle bell rang twice — late for Theo, later than his route ever ran — and both of us turned our heads toward the sound, and both of us heard it fade without stopping.

Gideon was already pulling on his jacket.

"Nadia," he said, and there was something in his voice I had not heard before — not fear, exactly, and not resolve, but the place where the two meet and shake hands. "Get your coat. We need to be sure that boy is home. And then we have three weeks to learn everything Silas Vane has ever touched — because on the twenty-ninth of April, at high water, whatever he's planned happens with or without us."

"And if we're wrong?" I asked, because someone had to.

He paused at the door, hand on the frame, and looked back at me with the ghost of his usual dry smile, the one that never quite reached the archives behind his eyes.

"Then we've ruined a perfectly good spring tide," he said. "But Nadia — we're not wrong. The sea keeps books. And he just showed us the ledger."

Outside, the wind had come around to the southeast, and it smelled of rain, and far away — so far we couldn't possibly have heard it, and yet I swear we both did — the sea was already beginning to pull itself up the shore, gathering for April.
