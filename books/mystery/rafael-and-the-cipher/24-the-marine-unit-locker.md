## 24. The Marine Unit Locker

The key fob sat on Nadia's kitchen table between a teapot and a stack of tide tables, looking exactly as sinister as a piece of stamped brass can look, which is to say: not at all. That was the trick of it. Evil rarely announces itself. It arrives as paperwork, as plumbing, as a small metal tag that jingles innocently in a teenager's pocket for three days before anyone thinks to ask where it came from.

"Marine unit," Nadia said, turning it over with one finger. "Harbor Division insignia. Serial number on the back, because of course there is. Bureaucracies can't help themselves."

"You say that with love."

"I say that with thirty-one years of experience. Every system leaves a trail. The people who build the systems forget that the trail is part of the system." She set the fob down and looked at me over her reading glasses. "The question is whether the trail still exists, or whether someone with a mop and a filing schedule has already been through."

"Marine unit lockers were decommissioned in 2019," I said. "Budget consolidation. They moved everything to the central facility and sold the old harbor annex to a developer who's been 'renovating' it into luxury flats ever since, which in this town means it's been empty and full of pigeons."

"You've been reading the municipal asset registers again. Gideon. You have to stop doing that. It's not healthy."

"It's extremely healthy. It's the only cardio I get."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the smile went somewhere private, and she picked up the fob again. "A dead man's name, you said. Theo said the figure dropped it, and you said—"

"The alias on the Marsh file's missing page. The tide log that was swapped out. The witness signature." I had it memorized, of course. I have everything memorized; it's the one party trick that stopped being fun around age nine. "R. Calloway. Ronald Calloway, dock worker, died in 2016, two years before Elena Marsh went into the water. A dead man signed a tide log attesting that he saw nothing at Gullwing Cove on the night a lighthouse keeper's daughter drowned. The dead are excellent witnesses. They never contradict you."

Nadia was quiet for a moment. Outside her kitchen window, the harbor lay flat and gray, the water doing its patient impersonation of a thing that keeps no records. I've never believed that impersonation. The sea keeps everything. It's just a very slow archive with terrible retrieval times.

"Calloway," she said at last. "Let me check something." She went to the front hall, where her personal archive lived — and I use the word *archive* deliberately, because Nadia Okonkwo's house contained more accurate records than the institution that had pushed her out of it — and came back with a ledger, a pencil, and the particular expression of a woman about to enjoy herself. "Marine unit locker assignments, harbor annex, 2015 through decommission. I copied them in 2017 when I was still allowed near a filing cabinet. They never knew I took it. They never knew I took half of what I took. That was always the joke of it — they were so busy guarding the doors they never checked what was walking out in a handbag."

"You're a criminal, Nadia."

"I'm a librarian with initiative. There's a difference, and it's mostly the paperwork."

She ran her finger down the columns. I watched her do it and felt the familiar, complicated ache of watching someone work — the pleasure of competence in a world that had lately seemed built entirely out of incompetence and malice. Calloway. Calloway. Her finger stopped.

"Locker forty-one. R. Calloway. Rented 2015, renewed annually, fees paid in cash." She looked up. "Paid in cash, Gideon. Every year. Through 2023. Seven years after the man died."

"Someone kept paying for a dead man's locker."

"Someone kept paying for a dead man's *name*. There's a difference, and it's mostly the paperwork." She closed the ledger with a small, decisive snap. "The annex is on Pier Road. The developer's been fighting the council for three years, so I doubt there's a lock on it that means anything. And I doubt," she added, in the tone of a woman who had spent her career around people who destroyed evidence and had developed an instinct for it the way sailors develop an instinct for weather, "that whoever rented locker forty-one has been back since Theo's friend started asking questions. But I doubt is not a plan. Let's go find out."

* * *

The harbor annex was a long, low building the color of old rope, its windows boarded, its signage half-torn so that it read MAR NE U IT and then, optimistically, FUTURE HOME OF PIER POINT RESIDENCES. The developer's promise had the same shelf life as the developer's fencing. Pigeons had colonized the loading dock. The salt air had eaten the door hinges down to orange lace.

Nadia had the annex floor plan in her handbag, because of course she did. "Lockers were along the east wall. Staff side. Forty-one should be—" she counted, walking, "—here."

Locker forty-one was a dented steel rectangle with a padlock that had rusted into a sculpture of itself. I could have forced it. Nadia produced a key instead.

"You have a key."

"I have *keys*. Plural. When they decommissioned the annex, the locksmith who changed out the core locks was a man named Ferro, and Ferro's daughter was doing a dissertation on municipal record retention, and Ferro's daughter needed archive access, and I am a generous woman." She fitted the key. "Everything in this town is connected, Gideon. People think that's a blessing. It's mostly a filing system."

The lock turned. The door swung open with a sound like a small, tired sigh, and the smell came out first — salt, mildew, and beneath it something older, the dry mineral smell of paper that has been shut away from air for a long time. My heart was doing something unprofessional.

Inside: a metal shelf, a rusted tin, a coil of rope gone stiff as wire. And on the shelf, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the kind of knot that speaks of either a fisherman or a very careful man, a bundle.

Nadia looked at me. I looked at Nadia.

"You want to do the honors," she said, "or shall I? Because I've been doing honors for thirty-one years and my arms are tired."

I untied the knot. I remember the knot — I'll always remember the knot, a double sheet bend, the kind you use when you want something to hold but also to be untied by a knowing hand. Whoever had packed this bundle had packed it to be found by someone who knew knots. Or had packed it in the only way they knew how, and I was reading intention into rope, which is the occupational hazard of a mind like mine: everything is a message if you stare long enough. Sometimes a knot is just a knot. Sometimes it's a confession.

The oilcloth fell open.

Three things.

The first was a journal. Small, cheap, water-swollen at the corners, the cover stamped with a lighthouse — a child's souvenir or a keeper's gift. The name inside the front cover, in a slanted, careful hand, made the harbor go quiet around me.

*Elena Marsh.*

Nadia made a sound I had never heard her make. Not a gasp. Something smaller and worse — the sound of a woman who has spent five years suspecting the truth and has just been handed the physical object of it. She sat down slowly on an upturned crate, her hand pressed flat against her sternum.

"That's her hand," she said. "I've seen her hand. She used to leave notes at the archive desk, requests for shipping records, tide almanacs — she was doing her father's logbooks, helping him, and her handwriting was—" She stopped. Started again. "That's her handwriting, Gideon."

The second thing was a duplicate key. Not a door key — a cipher key. A card of typed letter-pairings, laminated against the damp, and my mind went into the cipher we'd been chewing for weeks and the tumblers began to turn. The tidal tables, the letter-pairings, the outer layer we'd cracked at the cove. This was the inner ring. This was the key to the key.

The third thing was a photograph.

It was me.

Not a surveillance shot, not a candid through a window. A photograph of me standing outside the records office, taken from across the street, in the flat gray light of a morning I could date to the week I'd first pulled the Marsh file. I was looking down at something in my hands — the file, I realized; I was looking down at the Marsh file — and whoever had taken the picture had taken it from the angle of someone who had been waiting. Patiently. For a long time.

There were others beneath it. Months of them. Me at the archive. Me at the harbor wall. Me leaving Nadia's house. Me at Bell's pontoon, the day I'd interviewed him, my face turned three-quarters toward the camera, and in the corner of that one, in small block letters, a date and a single word: *SOON.*

The pigeons shifted on the loading dock. Somewhere out on the water a gull made its idiot complaint. I stood in a decommissioned locker in a dead man's name, holding my own face in my hands, and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from the understanding of how long you have been watched without knowing it.

"Gideon." Nadia's voice, steady, pulled me back. "How long?"

"Eight months. Maybe nine. The earliest one is from last spring." I laid the photographs out on the shelf with the care you use for evidence, or for the belongings of the dead. "Whoever rented this locker has been photographing me since before the first letter arrived. The letters weren't the beginning, Nadia. They were the middle. I was being studied the way you study a tide chart — for pattern, for predictability, for the moment the water goes out and exposes what's underneath."

"Then the letters weren't a confession at all."

"They were a test. A controlled experiment. Write to the man with the perfect memory, give him a cipher, and watch what he does. Watch how he thinks. Watch who he goes to." I looked at the photograph of me leaving Nadia's house and felt something go hard and cold in my chest. "Watch who he loves."

Nadia took off her glasses and cleaned them on her cardigan, a slow, deliberate motion, the motion of a woman buying herself three seconds of calm. "Then we assume the locker is known. We assume everything in this room has been seen, catalogued, and left here for us to find. A dead man's name, a dead girl's journal, a cipher key, and a gallery of you. It's a stage set, Gideon. Someone dressed it."

"Or," I said, "someone hid it here years ago and never came back, and the watcher and the renter are two different people."

"Which do you believe?"

I looked at the knot. Double sheet bend. Tied by a careful hand, tied to be untied.

"I believe," I said slowly, "that the alias on the missing tide log page and the name on this locker are the same, and that is not a coincidence, because I don't believe in those anymore. I believe that R. Calloway was a real man who died in 2016 and whose identity was harvested like everything else in this case — like Elena's fear, like Bell's terror, like my memory. Someone built a paper person out of a dead man and used him to sign a lie, rent a locker, and hold a door open. And the journal—" I picked it up, and it was lighter than it should have been, the way important things always are. "The journal is either the one true thing in this room or the bait in the trap. And I won't know which until I read it."

"Then we read it," Nadia said. "Here? Or—"

"Here. If it's being watched, I want to know what we look like when we find it. If it's not, no one follows us home." I opened the cover. The first page was dated five years and two months ago, and the handwriting was young and level and afraid in the way that careful handwriting is afraid — control stretched over panic like paint over rust.

The first line read: *I have to write this down somewhere my father can't find it, because if he finds it he'll do something brave, and brave is what gets people drowned here.*

I read it aloud. Nadia listened with her eyes closed, the way she listened to tide reports, the way she listened to anything she intended to keep.

Elena Marsh had seen something at the harbor. Boats running dark, cargo moved at the wrong hours, men whose names she knew and whose faces she was careful never to look at directly. She had written it down in the only place she trusted, which was a cheap journal hidden from a dying father who loved her too much to be safe. And on the last dated page — the last page, the ink stopping like a clock stopping — she had written a single sentence that I will carry in my memory until the memory goes out of me, which will be never:

*I told the inspector. He said he'd handle it. I believe him. I think I believe him.*

Nadia opened her eyes.

"Which inspector?" she asked, though her voice said she already knew there was no name on the page. There wasn't. Elena had been too careful even here, at the end. She had trusted one man with the truth and had not trusted him enough to write his name.

But I knew. The file, the access logs, the chain of custody — my memory laid them out like a hand of cards, and every card turned up the same face. Only one inspector had touched her report. Only one inspector had the authority to make a drowning tidy. Only one inspector had taught me, years ago, with such patience and such pride, how to read a file — and how, if you were very careful, to make one lie.

I closed the journal. The harbor light through the boarded windows fell in stripes across the photographs of my own face.

"Nadia," I said. "I know who the inspector was."
