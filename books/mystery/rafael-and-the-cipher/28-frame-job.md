## 28. Frame Job

The call came at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, which in my experience is the hour reserved for bad news, bill collectors, and people who want to confess to things before they've had coffee to regret them with.

It was Nadia.

"Turn on the news, boy. Or better, don't. Come down here and look at it with your own eyes, because your eyes are the only ones I trust anymore."

I turned on the news anyway. I've never been good at doing what I'm told, which is either a character flaw or the entire reason I'm still alive. The screen gave me a helicopter shot of a modest brick house on Calder Street with three police cars parked in front of it like ducks in a row, and a chyron that read: **TIP LEADS POLICE TO HOME OF RETIRED INSPECTOR — MARSH CASE REOPENED.**

Someone had given Reyes's address to the department's anonymous tip line. Not just the address — the tip line transcript, which I would get my hands on within the hour, contained details. Specific ones. The kind of details that don't come from a neighbor peeking through curtains.

*Subject observed burning documents in metal container in rear yard, October 14th and again November 2nd. Subject in possession of original Marsh case materials. Subject known to have altered official record.*

Every word of it was either true or true-adjacent. That's what made my stomach drop. A good lie needs a spine of truth to hang on, and this one had a whole skeleton.

I put the kettle on out of pure habit, watched it without seeing it, and then drove to the archive, where Nadia was already waiting with two coffees and the expression of a woman who has been awake since four and intends to make it everyone's problem.

* * *

"Read this," she said, slapping a printed page on the reading table. "Then tell me what you see."

I read it twice. The first time as an investigator. The second time as the man with the memory that never lets him have a quiet moment.

"Three tips," I said. "Placed over six weeks, all from different payphones — the department rotates the recording, so whoever did this knew to spread them out, make it look organic. The first tip is vague, almost useless. Just enough to get a flag on the file. The second names the Marsh case explicitly. The third — the one that triggered this morning's circus — gives dates, details, and the phrase 'altered official record.'"

"Which is language from *your* reconstruction," Nadia said. "The one you wrote up after you identified the swapped tide log. The one that went into the system three weeks ago."

I nodded slowly. That was the part that had woken me at four a.m. too, though I hadn't called anyone about it. "The tipster used phrasing that exists in exactly one document in the department's system. A document with restricted access."

"So either your restricted access isn't restricted—"

"Or the tipster *is* the access."

Nia sat back and wrapped her hands around her coffee cup like she was warming them on a small fire. "I've been fighting bureaucracies for thirty-one years, Gideon. I know what a frame looks like. And I'll tell you the thing that's bothering me most, more than the language, more than the payphones."

"Tell me."

"The timing." She tapped the tip transcript. "Reyes ran last Thursday. He called you at midnight, gave you the key to his notes. That means whoever is doing this *knows* he ran. Knows the notes are out. And instead of panicking, they accelerated. They burned him *harder* — tipped the police to his house, got his name in the news, made sure that by the time his notes surface, nobody will believe a word in them."

"Poisoning the well," I said.

"Poisoning the well, salting the earth, and paving over it for a parking lot." She took a sip. "This isn't a man covering his tracks. This is a man building a monument on top of them. You don't do this unless you're confident. Confident and *patient*. This was planned before Reyes ever ran."

I stared at the transcript and felt the shape of it — the whole shape, ugly and elegant at once. Someone had spent weeks laying a trail toward Reyes. The letters to me, the cipher, the taunts — I'd assumed they were aimed at *me*, at my memory, at my guilt over Bell. But what if that was never the point? What if I was just the crowbar they'd chosen to pry the Marsh case back open, knowing that when it opened, everything inside would be arranged to point at one man?

"You remember everything except what matters," I murmured.

"What's that?"

"The third letter. I thought it was just cruelty. But it might have been a *curriculum*. Teaching me what to look at. Steer the investigator, and the investigation follows."

Nadia looked at me for a long moment. "Then the question, boy, is who benefits from Reyes burning."

I had a list. I'd made it in chapter and verse, in my own head, in the neat handwriting that only I can read. Three insiders with file access. Two eliminated by alibi. One name left, the name I'd been refusing to say out loud because saying it made it real. But Reyes had handed me his notes, and his notes had cracked that list open like an egg — Reyes himself had altered the file, yes, but to protect a witness, and his twenty years of marginalia pointed somewhere I hadn't been looking.

"Silas Vane," I said.

There. Said. The deputy coroner with the perfect record and the soft voice and the hands of a man who has never once had to grip anything harder than a pen.

Nadia didn't flinch. "The coroner who certified Elena's drowning."

"Deputy coroner. He certified eleven coastal drownings in nine years, all of them flawless. He had custody of the Marsh file at every stage. And he's the only person on my list whose alibis I couldn't break — because his alibi for the night of the third letter was *a night alone doing paperwork*, and paperwork leaves no witnesses, only product. And the product was perfect."

"Too perfect?"

"Vane's paperwork is always too perfect. That's not an accusation, it's a fingerprint."

* * *

I went to see Reyes.

Not at his house on Calder Street — that was a crime scene now, or close enough, with a bored uniform on the porch and neighbors collecting details like seashells. I went to the place I knew he'd go, because I'd known the man for fifteen years and he was nothing if not predictable in his sanctuaries: the bait shop on the river road, where he kept a rod he never used and a thermos of coffee he always shared and opinions he dispensed whether you asked or not.

He was sitting on the overturned crate by the back door, and he looked ten years older than he had at midnight eight days ago. The news had done that. Being hunted in public does something to a face that being hunted in private never manages.

"You saw," he said. Not a question.

"I saw."

"Sit down, Gideon." He poured coffee into the spare cup he'd apparently brought for me, because of course he had. He'd always brought the spare cup, back when I was twenty-three and memorizing case files faster than the department could print them. "Tell me what you see."

It was our old ritual. He'd taught it to me: *don't tell me what you know, tell me what you see.* Knowledge can be wrong. Seeing is just seeing.

I sat. I drank. I told him.

"Three tips over six weeks, payphones, escalating specificity. The language in the third tip matches my restricted reconstruction nearly word for word — 'altered official record,' the October date for the burning, which is a date only Theo, Nadia, you, and I know about, plus whoever watches the archive logs. The tips were designed to make your notes unbelievable before they could surface. That's not improvisation. That's a campaign."

Reyes nodded along, and there was something in his stillness that frightened me more than panic would have. He wasn't surprised. He'd already worked all of it out, probably before I had, probably at four in the morning in some rented room, and he'd had days to sit with the conclusion.

"And?" he said.

"And it points at Vane."

"Say the rest."

I looked at my mentor, this man who had taught me to read files the way other people read faces, who had taken a strange, friendless prodigy and given him a trade and a purpose, and I said the rest. "It points at Vane, and it points at you as the *designed* fall. The letters to me were bait. The cipher was a curriculum. I was never the target, Reyes. I was the instrument. Whoever wrote those letters wanted the Marsh case reopened by someone with the credibility and the memory to do it thoroughly — and then wanted the reopening to land on you. Every thread I pull now has been pre-arranged to end in your hands."

"And you believe that," Reyes said. "That I'm the designed fall."

"I believe it's what the evidence says."

"Your evidence." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, an old smile, the one he used when a suspect gave him a technically true answer. "Gideon. I taught you to read files. Let me teach you the last lesson, the one I held back because I hoped you'd never need it. When the evidence is *arranged* — and you've just said it's arranged — then believing the evidence is exactly what the arranger wants. You don't believe the evidence. You believe the *cost*. Who could afford this? The payphones, the patience, the six weeks, the access to your reconstruction, the access to the archive logs. Who has a life built so carefully that a campaign like this is just... housekeeping?"

"Vane."

"Vane," he agreed. "And here's what I couldn't put in my notes, because notes get found — you're living proof of that. Twenty-two years ago, when I was a young detective and Vane was a young deputy coroner, there was a drowning in the northern channel. Harriet Osei, a deckhand. I wanted it investigated. Vane certified it accidental before I'd finished my first site visit. I pushed. I was told, gently, by people above me, that Silas Vane had *never made an error* and that my record, young as it was, had two. Do you understand? He was protected then. Which means whoever protected him is probably still in a position to protect him. And it means that when I started pulling the Marsh thread five years ago, I wasn't fighting one man. I was fighting a *system* that Vane has spent decades making himself indispensable to."

The river went by behind him, brown and patient.

"So why run?" I asked. "If you're innocent and you knew the frame was coming, why hand them the fleeing-inspector narrative on a plate?"

"Because my notes were the only thing that could clear Elena Marsh, and notes in the possession of a man about to be arrested get *confiscated*, and confiscated notes get lost, and lost notes stay lost." He looked at me directly. "I ran to keep them in play. I ran so that you'd be the one holding them when the hammer came down on me. That's the whole plan, Gideon. There is no plan beyond that. I'm an old man with a rod he never uses and a thermos, and my entire strategy is that I taught you well enough to finish this without me."

I felt something crack a little in my chest. Fifteen years of hero worship and resentment and loyalty, all of it compressing into one sentence: *I taught you well enough to finish this without me.*

"Then I need one more thing," I said.

"Name it."

"The tipster used language from my reconstruction. Restricted document. I need to know who else could have seen it. Not who *should* have — who *could*. You spent thirty years in that building. You know which locks are decorative."

Reyes was quiet for a while. A heron stood in the shallows across the river, doing its impression of patience.

"Chain of custody review board," he said finally. "Every restricted document gets a quarterly access audit. The audits go to the coroner's office for certification — has to, because the coroner certifies evidence integrity. It's in the charter. Written forty years ago by a man who never imagined the coroner's office might be the problem."

My skin went cold in the warm morning air.

"So Vane doesn't need to break into anything," I said. "The audits come to him. He reads my reconstruction *as part of his job*. Legally. Boredom-proof. Invisible."

"He doesn't even have to steal," Reyes said. "He just has to show up to work. That's the thing about Silas, boy. He never breaks rules. He *is* the rules. You can't catch a man like that with evidence, because he's had thirty years to make sure the evidence system belongs to him." He stood, stiffly, and capped the thermos. "You can't catch him with evidence. You have to catch him with *appetite*. Give him something he wants so badly he reaches for it himself, in front of witnesses he doesn't control."

I stared at him. "That's — Reyes, that's a trap."

"Yes," he said. "It is. I told you I taught you well. Now go build one. And Gideon—" He paused at the door of the bait shop, an old man silhouetted against the river he'd never fish. "Whatever you find at the end of it, don't do what I did. Don't sit on it for five years trying to build a perfect case. Perfect cases are his home turf. Build a *messy* one. Build it fast. The spring tide is in three weeks, and whatever he's kept at the bottom of the sea all this time — he'll go get it. He has to. The sea is the only filing cabinet he doesn't control, and the tide is about to open the drawer."

* * *

I drove back with the windows down and the wind shredding my thoughts into ribbons I had to reassemble at every red light. By the time I reached the archive, I had the shape of it.

Nadia was waiting, because Nadia was always waiting, which is a thing I had stopped taking for granted somewhere around the second week and would never take for granted again.

"Talk," she said.

"Vane reads the quarterly access audits. My reconstruction went through his office legally. He's known every move we've made that touched the system." I sat down across from her. "Reyes says we can't catch him with evidence. We catch him with appetite. We build something he wants — a file, a page, a witness — and we leak it through the channels he monitors, and we let him come take it himself."

Nadia was quiet for a moment. Then she smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent thirty-one years watching bureaucrats destroy documents and had, until now, only ever been able to *file complaints* about it.

"A falsified file," she said.

"A falsified file. A witness who was never there, placed at the cove, dated for the spring tide. Detailed enough to be worth destroying. Wrong enough to be provably ours. And we route it so that the only person who can see it and act on it in time is the man who reads the audits."

"And when he reaches for it—"

"We log the reach. Archive timestamps, access trails, the whole chain. He's spent thirty years making the system his alibi." I felt my mouth do something that was almost a smile. "So we make the system his confession."

Nadia pulled a fresh legal pad toward her, and her pen hovered over it for a moment before she looked up at me.

"You understand what this means," she said. "To bait a man who reads everything, the bait has to be perfect. Which means we have to lie perfectly. In a system you can't fully control. In three weeks. With the police currently convinced your mentor is a murderer, and your mentor in the wind, and a boy who delivers mail mixed up in it, and a harbor pilot still missing."

"I've thought about that."

"And?"

"And I've decided," I said, "that the man who wrote me a letter promising more deaths unless I solved his cipher doesn't get to be the only one in this story who can forge a document."

Nadia laughed — a short, sharp, surprised sound — and uncapped her pen.

"We'll need tide tables," she said. "The real ones. And Gideon — we'll need to decide what our fake witness *saw*. Because whatever we put on that page, Vane will believe it. That's the trap and the danger in the same sentence. We're about to hand a murderer a reason to come to the cove at the spring tide, and we'd better be very sure about what happens when he arrives."

Outside, the afternoon was going gold and flat, the harbor lying to itself the way it always did, beautiful and full of things it had swallowed. Three weeks until the spring tide. Somewhere in the city, Silas Vane was probably at his desk, certifying something, keeping the world's paperwork tidy, waiting for the next audit to tell him exactly where we were.

He thought he was reading us.

I uncapped my pen, and began to write him a file.
