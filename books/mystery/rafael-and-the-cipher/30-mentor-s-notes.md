## 30. Mentor's Notes

The key was warm from Reyes's hand.

That's what I kept thinking about, standing in my kitchen at one in the morning with a brass key sitting in my palm like a small accusation. Somewhere out there, a man who had taught me everything I knew about reading the truth out of paper had held this key, walked to a mailbox or a dead drop or the gap under his own back fence, and left it for me. He'd been running. Fugitives don't usually make scheduled deliveries. But then, Reyes had never done anything by accident in thirty years of police work, and I had to assume this wasn't an accident either. It was a lesson. The last one, maybe.

*Read mine,* he'd said on the phone. *I taught you to read files.*

So I read them.

The key fit a padlock on a storage unit at the self-storage place out past the cannery road — the kind of facility that exists in every coastal town, full of other people's abandoned lives: exercise equipment bought with January optimism, boxes of a dead mother's dishes, jet skis that hadn't touched water since the recession. Unit 114. The lock opened like it had been waiting for me, which, in fairness, it had.

Inside there was no furniture, no clutter. A single metal filing cabinet, four drawers, and a folding chair positioned in front of it like a pew. Reyes had been coming here to sit with his secrets. I understood that more than I wanted to. There are things you can't keep in your house because your house is where you're supposed to be innocent.

The cabinet was organized the way Reyes organized everything — chronologically within category, cross-referenced, annotated in that cramped left-slanted hand that always looked like it was trying to escape the page. I pulled the first folder and my heart did something complicated, because the label read *MARSH, ELENA — PERSONAL*, and beneath it, in smaller letters: *NOT FOR THE FILE. FOR ME.*

I sat down in the folding chair. It seemed appropriate. This was, after all, a confessional, just one where the priest had left his sins in writing and gone on the run before absolution could be administered.

* * *

Here is what I expected to find, and I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I have left after this week: I expected to find guilt. I expected the notes to confirm what three letters, a missing harbor pilot, and twenty-nine days of mounting dread had been building toward — that Inspector Emilio Reyes, my mentor, the man who taught me that a file never lies but a filer might, had killed Elena Marsh five years ago and had been managing the cover-up ever since. The altered tide log. The warnings. The coat Theo saw burning papers. The gait. All of it pointed home.

The notes did not point home. The notes pointed somewhere much worse, and much sadder.

Reyes had been investigating the harbor smuggling operation since 2016 — two years before Elena died. His earliest entries were almost embarrassed, the way a man sounds when he's admitting he believes something he can't yet prove. *Cargo manifests at Pier 9 don't match customs declarations. Discrepancy runs roughly 4-7% by weight. Either someone is very bad at math or someone is moving product through legitimate shipments.* Then, over months: names. Not the names I'd expected — not the dockworkers and petty criminals who'd been arrested and released in the years since — but names with titles. A harbormaster's deputy. A shipping agent whose firm handled a third of the port's tonnage. And, circled twice in ink so hard it had torn the page slightly, a name I recognized from the coroner's office.

Silas Vane.

I should say something here about what happened to my body when I saw that name in Reyes's handwriting, dated March 2018 — fourteen months before Elena Marsh went into the water. My hands didn't shake. They never shake; that's part of the curse. Instead everything went very still and very clear, the way it does when my memory decides to show me something I filed without understanding. I remembered Vane at the inquest. Soft-spoken. Meticulous. The kind of man who apologizes when he corrects you. I remembered him certifying the drowning as accidental with a thoroughness that had impressed me at the time — every box ticked, every form countersigned, chain of custody documented down to the hour. I had thought: *here is a man who respects procedure.* I had thought that was a virtue.

Reyes had thought so too, once. His notes said as much. *Vane is careful. Too careful. Started pulling death certificates for coastal drownings going back nine years. Eleven of them. All certified by Vane. All ruled accidental. All with witnesses conveniently absent or conveniently vague. You don't get eleven coin flips landing the same way unless someone's weighting the coin.*

Eleven. I sat with that number while the storage unit's fluorescent tube buzzed overhead like a trapped insect. Eleven drownings. One deputy coroner. And a smuggling operation moving product through Pier 9 for the better part of a decade, tidying up after itself with paperwork so clean it gleamed.

But that wasn't the part that made me put the folder down and press the heels of my hands against my eyes. That came next.

* * *

August 2019. Three weeks before Elena Marsh drowned.

Reyes's handwriting changed in that entry. It got faster, messier, the letters leaning harder into each other, a man writing at speed because speed mattered. *Elena Marsh came to me today. Lighthouse keeper's daughter, works the chandlery, sharp as glass. She's been keeping her own log — dates, vessel names, times she saw light signals from the point that don't match any registered traffic. She grew up reading that water. She knows what it's supposed to look like at night, and she knows what she's been seeing isn't it.*

*I told her to stop. I told her to write nothing down, tell no one, let me handle it. She said she'd already sent a report. Asked me who she should have sent it to instead. I couldn't answer honestly, which tells you everything about what this job has made me.*

Then, dated six days later:

*Confirmed. Her report went through the intake desk and landed on Vane's review queue. She reported suspicious activity to the deputy coroner of all people, because he's the one who signs off on "irregularities," and nobody thought to tell her that the man who files the bodies is the man making them. She has no idea. She thinks she did the right thing. God help me, she did do the right thing. That's the problem.*

I read that line four times. *She thinks she did the right thing.* Five years I had carried Elena Marsh's file in my head like a stone — every page, every signature, the photograph of a young woman with salt-tangled hair and a smile that the caption called *unidentified subject, recovered at Gullwing Cove*. Five years I had believed the file, because the file was all I had, and because believing files was the only way I'd survived being the person I am.

And now a dead man's — no. A hunted man's notebook was telling me that Elena Marsh had spent her last weeks doing exactly what I would have done. Observing. Recording. Reporting. Trusting the system. Trusting an inspector.

Trusting Reyes.

* * *

Because that was the next entry, and it was the one I'd been bracing for since I turned the first page, and it still hit like cold water.

*September 14. Elena Marsh is dead. Found at Gullwing Cove. Vane certified accidental drowning within 36 hours — fastest certification I've ever seen from him, and I've seen him sit on paperwork for weeks when it suited him. The official file will close clean. Nobody will ask questions because the questions were pre-answered by the man whose job it was to raise them.*

*I did one thing. I'm writing this down so that someday, if it matters, someone knows I did one thing. The witness log — the tide log that places old Tom Ellery at the cove that night — Tom wasn't there. He was at his daughter's in Millbrook; I've confirmed it. But if I remove that page, the file shows a hole, and holes get investigated, and investigations reach Vane, and the next thing buried at Gullwing Cove is me. So I swapped it. Put in a version that keeps Tom at the cove, harmless, half-blind, sees nothing. A boring page. A page nobody looks at twice. The original is behind this entry. If anyone ever reads these notes: the alteration was mine. Not to bury evidence. To keep one more person off Vane's list. Because Elena talked to Tom the week before she died, and if Vane learns Tom remembers anything useful, Tom drowns too, and it'll be ruled accidental, and Silas will sign it with his neat little pen.*

I sat in that folding chair for a long time.

The relief came first, and it came ugly — a hot, shaking flood of it, my whole body unclenching around a suspicion I'd been carrying for a month like a swallowed hook. Not Reyes. Not my mentor. The altered page I'd reconstructed from memory, the swap I'd identified with such cold clarity in Chapter — in week thirteen of this nightmare — it wasn't a killer erasing a trail. It was a protector throwing a blanket over a witness. Reyes had committed the only crime available to him, a small bureaucratic sin, to keep an old man breathing. I laughed, actually laughed, alone in a storage unit at two in the morning, and it came out sounding like something dying.

Then the confusion arrived, right on schedule, because relief is never allowed to finish its sentence in my life.

If Reyes altered the file to protect Tom Ellery — then who wrote the letters? The confession letter used details from inside the original file. Details only an insider would know. I'd built my entire suspect web on that premise, and it was still sound. But the premise had assumed the insider was the killer. What if the insider was the protector? What if Reyes had written the confession himself — a false confession, bait, the same trick I'd tried to run on Vane with my forged file, except Reyes had thought of it first and aimed it at whoever was still sniffing around Elena's death?

No. That didn't hold either. Reyes had warned me off. Twice. Once in person, once with that midnight call. A man running his own sting doesn't warn the investigator to quit.

Unless the warning was also protection. Unless he'd seen me circling the same drain Elena Marsh had gone down and tried to pull me back the only way he knew how — by looking guilty enough to scare me away.

My memory laid the possibilities out side by side, the way it does, merciless, every scenario fully rendered and equally plausible: Reyes as author of the letters, staging his own guilt to flush out Vane. Reyes as target, framed by Vane using knowledge stolen from the file. Reyes as both — playing a game so layered that even he might lose track of which move was real. And underneath all of it, one fact sat like ballast: Cormac Bell was missing, and Bell had received a parcel delivered by a teenage letter carrier, and whatever was in that parcel had been arranged by someone who knew the harbor, knew the tides, and knew how to make a man disappear into paperwork.

I closed the drawer. Sat a moment longer. The fluorescent tube flickered, steadied.

There was one more thing in the cabinet. I'd felt it when I pulled the Marsh folder — a thin envelope taped to the underside of the top drawer, flat, the size of a photograph. I reached up and worked it free. On the front, in that escaping-leftward hand: *FOR THE BOY. WHEN HE'S READY.*

The boy. Me. Thirty-four years old and still, in Emilio Reyes's private taxonomy, the boy he'd found memorizing case files at nineteen because it was safer than memorizing faces.

I turned the envelope over. It wasn't sealed. Of course it wasn't — Reyes never sealed anything he wanted read carefully; he believed a sealed envelope invited carelessness. Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet, a photograph, and beneath the photograph, one line.

The photograph was of a page. A coroner's intake log, dated September 2019, showing the chain of custody for Elena Marsh's remains — and initialled in the review column, in neat, upright, unhurried script I would have known anywhere, was *S.V.*

The line beneath it said: *He signed for her before she was found. Check the time stamp. Then decide who taught whom.*

I checked the time stamp.

My memory held it up against the tide tables Nadia and I had pored over, against the recovery time in the official file, against the weather log for that night — and the numbers refused to reconcile. Silas Vane had initialled receipt of Elena Marsh's remains forty minutes before any boat went out to Gullwing Cove.

You cannot sign for a body that hasn't been recovered yet. Unless you already knew where it was. Unless you put it there.

The relief curdled, and the confusion burned off, and what was left was the coldest clarity I have ever felt in my life: Reyes hadn't been running from the law. He'd been running from the filing clerk of eleven drownings, carrying the only evidence that could hang him, and he'd trusted it to the one person he believed remembered everything.

The envelope trembled in my hands — not from fear. From arithmetic. Because if Vane had signed for Elena forty minutes early, then Vane had made a mistake, one mistake, in nine years of perfect paperwork.

And somewhere across town, a meticulous man was about to discover it.
