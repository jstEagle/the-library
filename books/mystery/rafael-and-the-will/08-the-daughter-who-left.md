## 8. The Daughter Who Left

There are two kinds of addresses in this world: the kind a man gives you because he wants to be found, and the kind you have to dig out of him like a splinter. Marianne Voss had given the county neither. I found her the way I find most things — by following paper. Property records, a water bill, a complaint filed with the neighboring county's animal control office concerning a dog that "howls at nothing, all hours." People think they vanish when they move forty miles. They only change the shape of their paperwork.

The drive out took me through country that got poorer the farther it got from Hollow Creek, which is usually the case. Towns like mine have a way of hoarding their wealth and their secrets in the same vault. By the time I turned onto a gravel lane lined with scrub pine, I was in a place where the houses sat back from the road like they were embarrassed to be seen.

Hers was a small white farmhouse with a sagging porch, a rusted truck, and a garden that had been tended with real care and then, judging by the weeds, abandoned mid-sentence. A dog did indeed howl at nothing, all hours, from somewhere behind the house. It sounded less like grief and more like a complaint being filed.

I knocked. I waited. I knocked again, and the door opened on a chain, and through the gap I saw a woman of about fifty with her mother's cheekbones and none of her mother's money.

"Mrs. Voss?"

"It's Delacroix now." The chain didn't come off. "Whatever you're selling, the answer's no."

"I'm not selling. I'm Wendell Fairweather. I'm the coroner of Hollow Creek."

The chain stayed on, but something behind her eyes shifted — the particular stillness people get when a word from home reaches them in exile. "Coroner," she repeated. "Is she dead?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I'd been asking it for a week and getting nothing but smooth answers and lye-smell for my trouble. Here was the first person to ask it back at me with what sounded like genuine hope or genuine dread, and I genuinely could not tell which.

"I don't know," I said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

She let me in eventually. The chain came off, the door opened onto a small front room that smelled of coffee and old upholstery, and Marianne Delacroix — born Voss, and I suspected she said that name the way you spit out a pit — poured two cups without asking if I wanted one, which I took as a good sign. People who are performing hospitality ask. People who are simply too tired to be hostile just pour.

"Start from the beginning," she said. "And don't do the polite thing where you circle around why you're here. I've had thirty years of polite circling. It's the family religion."

So I gave her the shape of it, if not the substance. A client had retained me — I did not name Lark — to look into her mother's affairs ahead of a new will. I said I was conducting inquiries into the household. I did not say I suspected the household might contain a dead woman being kept alive by a caretaker with clean fingernails and a talent for answers. I did not mention plot forty-one. I mentioned none of it, and I watched her face while I mentioned none of it, because faces tell you more when you give them nothing to react to.

What I saw was a woman doing arithmetic behind her eyes. Not guilt-arithmetic. The other kind — the kind where you're counting what it will cost you to care.

"You said a new will," she said. "She's rewriting it."

"So I'm told."

"Under the old one, I got half. Under the new one?" She laughed, a dry sound with no floor under it. "Let me guess. Nothing. Or a token. A thousand dollars and the good china, so the lawyers can say she provided for me."

"You've seen the new will?"

"I haven't seen anything. That's the point of it." She set her cup down harder than she needed to. "You know when I last spoke to my mother? Go on. Guess."

"I'd rather you told me."

"Eleven years. Eleven years, Mr. Fairweather. And the last conversation was her telling me that I'd disappointed her, and me telling her that disappointment was hereditary, and Silas Crane's voice in the background telling her the doctor was on the line, which was a lie, because I called the doctor's office the next day and there was no appointment, no line, nothing. He's been editing our calls for decades. He's not a caretaker. He's a border."

There it was. The bitterness, fresh as a opened wound and old as the hills it had been festering in. I have heard a great deal of bitterness in my line of work — from widows, from orphans, from men standing over bodies they swear they didn't make — and I've learned that bitterness is like any other evidence. Mostly it's genuine. Occasionally it's manufactured. The trick is that genuine bitterness has texture. It has specific grievances, dates, the doctor's office called the next day. Manufactured bitterness has themes. Marianne had texture to spare.

But texture isn't an alibi.

* * *

I asked her about the night of the third of October, thirty years ago, and I watched the texture change.

Because here's the thing I hadn't put in my notes yet, the thing that had been sitting in my skull like a coin in a pocket: the witness in plot forty-one, Ruth Calloway, was registered as buried on the third of October. And the original Voss will — the one that made Eleanor Voss the wealthiest widow in three counties — was witnessed by three people. Ruth Calloway was one. And a will witnessed under questionable circumstances, by a woman who then conveniently died within the month and went into the ground so shallow that the frost heaves had been arguing with her ever since — that's not a coincidence. That's a blueprint.

"October third," Marianne said. "I was twenty-one. Why?"

"Where were you?"

The question landed the way such questions always land — a half-second of stillness, the cup halfway to her mouth, the eyes going somewhere else to fetch the answer. Innocent people are slow because they have to travel back. Guilty people are fast because they've already made the trip. Marianne was slow, but she was slow in a strange way — not searching, but deciding.

"I was at home," she said. "Here — well, not here, but in the area. I'd moved out by then. I had a room over the feed store in Miller's Crossing."

"Anyone vouch for you?"

"A man I was seeing. He's dead now, before you get excited. Heart, nine years back. So it's my word and a dead man's word, which in a court of law adds up to my word." She smiled without any pleasure in it. "You're asking about thirty years ago. Why does a coroner care about a night thirty years ago?"

"Because something happened that night that someone has been paying to keep buried. Literally buried. And because I've learned this week that the burial may be empty."

I hadn't meant to say it. It came out because I was tired and because she had that way of pulling on a thread until the whole sweater came apart. But I said it, and I watched it hit her, and what I saw was not what I expected.

I expected confusion. I got recognition — a flicker of it, quickly smothered, the way you smother a match when you smell gas. She knew something. Not everything, maybe. But something.

"Empty," she repeated.

"Ruth Calloway's grave. Plot forty-one. I have reason to believe the coffin contains stones and an old coat."

Marianne stood up, walked to the window, and stood there with her back to me for a long time. The dog howled at its nothing. When she turned around, her face had been rearranged into something careful.

"My mother ruined that woman," she said. "Ruth Calloway. Whatever you think you're finding, Mr. Fairweather, start there. My mother's whole fortune — the house, the land, all of it — came through a will that Ruth Calloway witnessed, and six weeks later Ruth Calloway was in the ground, and nobody in Hollow Creek ever asked why a healthy forty-year-old woman died the same season she signed her name on a piece of paper worth a fortune." She sat back down. Her hands were not quite steady. "I asked. Once. When I was young and stupid and still loved her. I asked my mother what happened to Ruth Calloway, and she looked at me like I'd asked her to open a vein, and Silas showed me out, and that was the last honest conversation we ever had."

"You believe your mother had Ruth Calloway killed."

"I believe my mother's money did. Whether her hands did or not, I couldn't say. Silas's hands, maybe. Silas's hands do a lot of things." She looked at me straight on. "You want to know if I hate her. Yes. I hate her. And I'd still rather she was alive, because if she's dead and I never got to say what I needed to say, then Silas Crane won. He took the ending too."

That, I believed. You can fake a great many things in this world, but you cannot fake the specific hunger of the unfinished sentence. I've sat with enough grieving families to know the difference between mourning a person and mourning the conversation you never got to have. Marianne was starving for the conversation.

And yet.

* * *

"Tell me again about October third," I said. "Slowly."

"Mr. Fairweather—"

"Indulge me. You said you were in Miller's Crossing. Over the feed store. Your young man vouches for the evening. What about the morning? The afternoon? Ruth Calloway was buried on the third. Burials happen in daylight. Somebody stood at that graveside, or somebody didn't, and either way it's a small town and gravesides are theaters. Were you there?"

"No."

"Did you see the funeral procession?"

"I don't know. It was thirty years ago. I don't carry a ledger, Mr. Fairweather. That seems to be more your department."

It was, and I resented how neatly she'd said it. "The man you were seeing. The one with the heart condition. What was his name?"

"Tommy. Tommy Brandt."

"And Tommy will confirm—"

"Tommy is ash in the Miller's Crossing cemetery, and his confirmation died with him, and if you're asking me whether I had anything to do with Ruth Calloway's death, then ask it. Plainly. I've earned plain."

So I asked it plainly, because she'd earned it and because I wanted to see what plain did to her.

"Were you there that night? At Voss House, or near it? Did you see Ruth Calloway alive or dead or anything between?"

And here is where my notes get uncomfortable, because Marianne Delacroix — who had answered everything else with texture and grievance and the doctor's office called the next day — looked at me for a long moment and said:

"I don't remember."

Three words. The three most expensive words in any investigation. Not "no." Not "I was home." *I don't remember.* And her eyes, when she said it, went to the window, to the garden she'd abandoned mid-sentence, and the dog howled, and I understood that I had just been given a true answer that was also a door held shut.

"Thirty years is a long time," I offered.

"It is," she said. "It's a very long time. Longer for some of us than others."

* * *

I stayed another twenty minutes and got nothing else worth the ink. I learned that she worked part-time at the county library, that she'd been married briefly to a Delacroix who'd left her the dog and the porch and nothing else, that she kept a photograph of her mother on the mantel turned face-down — I noticed that, and I noticed that she noticed me noticing, and neither of us said a word about it, because some evidence is better left in the field.

At the door, she stopped me with a hand on my sleeve.

"Fairweather. The empty grave." Her voice had dropped, gone careful. "If you're right — if there's nothing in plot forty-one but rocks and a coat — then ask yourself who's been paying for the perpetual care. Because somebody has. Somebody's been tending that plot like it matters, for thirty years, and I can tell you it wasn't my mother. My mother didn't tend anything she'd finished with."

"Who, then?"

"That's your job, isn't it?" She let go of my sleeve. "But I'll tell you this for free, since you drove all the way out. When I was a girl, before everything went wrong, I used to see my mother and Ruth Calloway talking on the garden wall like they were friends. Real friends. Laughing. And then one year they weren't, and my mother never said her name again, and neither did anyone, and that's Hollow Creek for you. We don't bury our dead. We bury our *questions*."

She closed the door. The chain went on behind it — I heard the small metal sound of a woman making herself a fortress out of hardware.

* * *

I sat in the truck for a while, because that's what you do after a conversation like that. You sit, and you let the pieces settle, and you try to sort which ones are load-bearing.

Here's what I had. A daughter with honest bitterness and a dishonest memory. A thin alibi held up by a dead man. A flicker of recognition at the words *empty grave* that she never explained. A photograph turned face-down on the mantel, which is either grief or guilt, and in my experience the two are often wearing each other's coats.

And one more thing. As I'd stood to leave, my eye had caught the edge of a drawer in her sideboard — the kind of small domestic detail a coroner notices the way other men notice weather. The drawer was open an inch, and in it I'd seen paper. Not letters. Paper of a particular weight and color, the heavy cream stock that lawyers use, that wills are written on. The kind of paper that comes out of the offices of Brandt, Halloway & Finch, who drafted the original Voss will thirty years ago, and who I had, as of that morning, requested the archives of.

Marianne Voss, who claimed to have seen nothing and inherited nothing, had a piece of the old will's stationery in her front room.

I turned the key. The dog howled at its nothing, all hours, and I found myself thinking that the dog and I had something in common: we were both making a great deal of noise about a thing we couldn't see.

The road back to Hollow Creek ran through Miller's Crossing, and in Miller's Crossing there was a cemetery, and in that cemetery there was, according to Marianne, a jar of ash labeled Tommy Brandt. I had half a mind to stop and check whether the jar was full.

I didn't stop. Not then. Because my headlights caught the dashboard clock, and the clock said I had just enough time to get back to Hollow Creek before the county archive room closed, and because somewhere in the back of my mind a small cold voice was doing the arithmetic Marianne had been doing behind her eyes — counting what it would cost to care — and arriving at a number I didn't like.

The new will would be signed within the fortnight. The client's envelope sat heavy in my coat pocket, triple rate for a murder that hadn't happened yet. And now, forty miles from the house where a woman might or might not be alive behind a curtained window, her daughter had looked me in the eye and said *I don't remember* about the one night that mattered most.

I drove east, toward the closing archive room and the opening dark, and behind me the dog howled on — at nothing, all hours, or perhaps, I thought, at exactly the something the rest of us were still pretending wasn't there.
