## 15. Mr. Lark Unmasked

There is an old trick in my trade that I have never had occasion to use, mostly because my trade is the dead and the dead are terrible liars. It goes like this: if you want a man to tell you the truth, hand him a lie first — one that's just wrong enough to frighten him — and watch which way he runs. The guilty run toward damage control. The innocent run toward lawyers. And the men running a con of their own?

They run toward the lie itself, because they need to know how much of it you've got.

I spent two days building my lie. I wrote it out longhand on coroner's stationery, which has a certain gravity to it even when what's written there is fiction. I dated it three days in the future. I gave it a case number, a time of death, a cause. *Eleanor Voss, deceased. Asphyxiation. Body recovered from the east wing of the Voss estate. Notification pending.*

Then I folded it into an envelope, sealed it, and called Mr. Lark.

"Progress report," I said when he arrived at my office, pale as ever, hat clutched like a shield. "You'll want to sit down."

He didn't sit. He stood by the window with his back half-turned, the way he always did, as though sitting would commit him to something. "Is she—"

"Read it yourself."

He took the envelope the way a man takes a diagnosis. His fingers worked at the seal for a long moment — too long, I thought, for a man who supposedly feared this outcome. A man who truly believed Eleanor Voss was about to be murdered should have torn the paper. Should have gone white and grabbed my arm. Instead he opened it with the careful, almost professional patience of someone checking his own work.

He read it twice. And then he did the thing I'd been waiting for.

"You can't release that," he said quietly. "Not yet."

"Not yet," I repeated.

"The timing's wrong." He set the report on my desk with both hands, squaring its edges, a man arranging evidence rather than receiving news. "If the body surfaces before the will signing, everything collapses. Everything. You don't understand what's at stake."

"No," I said. "But I'm beginning to."

* * *

Let me back up, because I've earned the right to tell this in order, and because the order matters more than I understood until that moment.

After the fishing cabin — after Hollis Grange, the vanished investigator, had gripped my sleeve with whiskey-trembling hands and told me he'd seen Eleanor Voss alive six weeks after her supposed death date — I went home and did not sleep. I sat at my kitchen table with every document I'd gathered spread out like a card game I was losing, and I asked myself the question I should have asked in chapter one, so to speak: *who benefits from a murder that hasn't happened yet?*

Lark paid me triple rate to prove a crime in advance. That's not fear. Fear doesn't hire investigators; fear hires movers and lawyers and one-way tickets. What Lark had purchased, with three investigators before me and me as the fourth, was *documentation*. A paper trail. A record — and God knows I understand the power of records better than any man in Hollow Creek — establishing, in advance, through independent professional testimony, that Eleanor Voss was going to die.

You don't build a record like that to prevent a thing. You build it to survive a thing. Or to force a thing into the light so hard that no one can shove it back underground.

So I did what any self-respecting coroner does when he suspects he's been cast in someone else's play: I learned my lines better than the director knew them, and then I changed the ending.

The report I handed Lark was bait. The real work happened after he left my office that morning, because I followed him.

* * *

He walked, which surprised me. A man paying triple rates walks. He cut behind the feed store, crossed the lot of the shuttered cannery, and let himself into the old county records annex — the annex, mind you, not the active archive where I'd been pulling Voss documents for two weeks. The annex had been closed since the fire marshal condemned it in '09, but the county never locks anything it's ashamed of, and Hollow Creek is ashamed of a great deal.

I gave him ten minutes, then let myself in through the loading door, moving quiet on the rotten boards. Dust hung in the shafts of light from the broken skylights like the room was holding its breath.

Lark stood at a table under the one intact lamp, and the table was covered in paper. Not scattered — *arranged*. Files in ordered stacks, documents cross-referenced with colored tabs, photographs pinned to a corkboard leaning against the shelving. Thirty years of the Voss estate laid out like an anatomy lesson. I saw the original will of 1994. I saw the probate filings. I saw a photograph of a young woman with dark hair standing on the steps of the courthouse — Ruth Calloway, though I only knew that because I'd spent a week learning her face from a high school annual.

And I saw, on the table's edge, a ledger. My name was in it. So were three others — Hollis Grange, a man named Petrossian, and a woman listed only as "D.W., incomplete." Beside each name, amounts. Beside mine, a figure exactly triple my quoted rate, entered in a neat, patient hand.

"You're very thorough," I said.

He spun. For a moment he looked genuinely frightened, and I confess that satisfied something petty in me. Then he straightened, and the fear drained out of his face and left something older and wearier behind.

"Coroner Fairweather."

"That's the name on the invoice. Though I notice you've been billing me as an expense, which stings a little. I like to think I'm worth more than an expense."

"How much did you see?"

"All of it, I expect. But let's start with the part where you explain why a man terrified of a future murder keeps a filing system."

He looked at the table. At thirty years of paper. When he spoke again, his voice had changed register entirely — the nervous-stranger performance stripped away like paint off old wood.

"My father," he said, "was Aldous Lark. Attorney at law, Hollow Creek, nineteen sixty-one to nineteen ninety-six. He drafted the Voss will. The real one. And then, on the night of March third, nineteen ninety-four, he drafted a different one."

* * *

We sat on overturned crates because the annex has no chairs, two men in a condemned building lit by a single lamp, and he told me the story I had been assembling piece by piece for weeks — except now it had a spine.

Aldous Lark was dying in the winter of '94. Cancer, fast and mean. And according to his son, the dying made him honest in the way that nothing else manages. One night, near the end, he'd started talking — to a bottle, mostly, and to the boy who sat up with him. About the Voss estate. About old Amos Voss's true will, the one that had left substantial portions to the household staff, to Ruth Calloway who had served the family twenty years, to a trust for the town itself. About how that will had been replaced, page by page, in the hours between Amos's death and the reading, with a version that left everything to Eleanor and nothing to anyone else. About the three witnesses who signed the fraudulent instrument — and about the fourth person who had been present that night and refused to sign her name to a lie.

Ruth Calloway. Who was supposed to leave town. Who was supposed to be dead within the year, per the arrangement, and whose grave — plot forty-one — had been purchased and maintained ever since by payments routed through a law firm account that closed the same month Aldous Lark died.

"She wouldn't do it," Lark said. "Refuse to sign, I mean. She stood in that parlor with the pen in her hand and said no. My father always said it was the bravest thing he ever watched and the worst thing he ever caused. Because once she refused, she couldn't be allowed to talk. She ran. We buried stones and a coat and let the town believe what the paperwork said, because the paperwork was my father's, and nobody in this county has ever doubted my father's paperwork."

"Including me," I said. "Until recently."

"He left me the truth the way other men leave debts." Lark's mouth twisted. "A confession in his effects, unnotarized, unwitnessed, legally worthless. A dead man's word against a settled estate and a town full of people who profited from not remembering. I tried for years. Lawyers laughed. The statute ate most of it. Eleanor Voss sits in that house on money that was stolen from living people, some of whom died waiting for justice, and the law says *too late, too late, too late.*"

"So you manufactured a crisis."

"I manufactured a *record*." He leaned forward. "Think it through, Coroner. You did. I know you did, because your fabricated report is dated wrong — you put the death three days out, but you used the east wing, and the east wing detail means you've been inside the house, which means Silas Crane talked to you or failed to, either of which tells me things. You're good. You're the best of the four of you, and the other three weren't bad." He said it without flattery, the way you'd note the weather. "Here is what the scheme was. I hire investigators — reputable ones, credentialed ones — to investigate whether Eleanor Voss will be murdered before signing her new will. They find irregularities. Of course they find irregularities; I salted the ground. Empty graves. Caretakers who lie. Signatures that tremble. Each investigator produces a documented file. And then, at the right moment, those files surface — all of them, together, publicly — and the county has no choice but to open the estate, audit the probate, pull the original instruments, and follow the thread back to 1994. You cannot investigate the new murder without exhuming the old fraud. The files are a crowbar. The murder was never the point. The *investigation* was the point."

"And the actual murder?"

"There was never going to be an actual murder." He said it flatly, but his hands had gone still in his lap, and stillness, in my experience, is what fear looks like on disciplined men. "Eleanor wasn't the target. She was the *pretext*. The plan required her alive, cooperative, and signing — the new will was the deadline that forced everyone's hand. I chose her precisely because she was safe. Old, reclusive, guarded by a caretaker who owed her everything. No one was ever supposed to touch her."

"But."

His jaw worked. "But someone has been doing things I didn't arrange."

"Tell me."

"The checks. I forged the proof-of-life materials in the early phase — the signature samples, the staged sightings. Crude stuff, enough to keep the investigators convinced she was alive and in danger. But the recent check, the one dated last week — I didn't make that. I've compared it against my own forgeries. Different hand. Better hand. Someone in that house is producing fresh evidence independently, which means someone in that house knows about the investigation, which means—" He stopped.

"Which means your pretext has become a target," I finished. "Someone intends to do the thing you only pretended to predict. Your fiction hired a killer."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere in the dark of the annex, a pigeon shifted on a beam and resettled.

"I swear to you," Lark said, and his voice cracked on the second word, which is the only time in our entire acquaintance I heard anything unmanaged come out of him, "I built this whole machine so that no one would have to die for the truth again. Ruth Calloway lost thirty years of her life to my father's lie. I was not going to balance that ledger with another body. And now there's a hand in my machinery I can't identify, and the will signs tomorrow, and I came to *you*, Fairweather, because you're the only one of my hires who kept digging instead of running."

"Two of your hires ran," I said. "One vanished. I found him drunk in a cabin, terrified. He saw Eleanor alive after your staged timeline said she should be dead — which means your fictions and the house's fictions have been colliding for weeks, and neither of you knew about the other."

"I know." He passed a hand over his face. "That's what frightens me. Two deceptions operating in the same house produce coincidences, and coincidences produce bodies. I've read enough of my father's case files to know how it goes."

I stood. My knees objected; crates are unkind to men my age. On the table, the corkboard stared back at me — thirty years of a town's arranged forgetting, tabbed and cross-referenced by the son of the man who built it.

"One question," I said. "Who are Ruth's allies? You said you were hired — past tense, you said *hired by* — which implies you're working for someone besides your own conscience."

Lark hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but I've built a career on small hesitations.

"A mutual friend," he said finally. "Someone who's been keeping Ruth alive and hidden for three decades, and who agreed that the truth needed daylight. They provided the funding. The contacts. The assurance that Ruth herself would testify when the moment came."

"Silas Crane."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "Why would you say that?"

"Because someone pays perpetual care on an empty grave, and it isn't you — your ledger shows outflows, not that kind. Because Silas lies smoothly but protects fiercely. Because the guest wing smells of lye and the mausoleum has food wrappers in it, and the man who tends the dead is the only one with keys to both houses." I picked up my fabricated report from the table, folded it, and put it in my coat. "And because when I mentioned the east wing, you flinched — but not at the word *murder*. You flinched at the word *east wing*, which means you know what's in it, and you learned that from someone inside the house."

"Fairweather—"

"Tomorrow the will gets signed," I said. "Your crowbar, my report, Ruth's testimony, Eleanor's conscience, Marianne's inheritance, and whatever Silas Crane is actually doing in that house — it all comes to a head at one table. I suggest we stop pretending we're running separate investigations."

He looked at me for a long moment across the lamp-lit table, this man who had spent years constructing a lie elaborate enough to force a town to remember, and who had just discovered that his lie had grown teeth while he wasn't looking.

"Partners, then," he said.

"Cautious associates," I corrected. "I've seen what happens to your partners."

I was almost at the loading door when he spoke again, quiet enough that I almost missed it under the wind worrying the broken skylights.

"Coroner. There's one more thing. The funding — the mutual friend's funding — it stopped last week. Mid-scheme. No explanation, no message. Whoever it is pulled out right when the danger turned real." He met my eyes. "Which means either they got cold feet, or they got what they actually wanted, and the rest of us — you, me, Ruth, maybe Eleanor herself — are the part they're willing to lose."

I stood in the doorway with the condemned dark at my back and the gray afternoon ahead of me, and I thought about a check that trembled in a way signatures shouldn't, and a locked east wing, and a daughter with a thin alibi, and a caretaker who sang such careful songs.

The will signed tomorrow at noon.

I intended to be in that room — and I intended to open every locked door in that house before the ink dried.
