## 16. Storm Warning

The barometer at the coroner's office had been falling all afternoon, and by four o'clock it read like a confession. Hollow Creek's weather had a way of arriving with intent — not rain so much as a verdict — and tonight it intended to wash something clean or bury something deeper. I stood at my window watching the first gray sheets come off the ridge and thought, not for the first time that week, that I had become a man who believed in omens because the records had stopped believing in themselves.

Tomorrow, at ten o'clock, Eleanor Voss would sign her new will.

Unless someone made sure she didn't.

I had spent the day doing what I do best and worst: making lists. On the left side of my legal pad, facts. On the right side, things I could no longer call facts but couldn't bring myself to file under fiction either. The left column was short. The right column was winning.

Fact: Ruth Calloway, witness to the original Voss will, alive after thirty years of official death.

Fact: Mr. Lark, my client, was the son of Ambrose Lark, the lawyer whose ink had forged that original will — and his grand scheme to force the truth into daylight had been hijacked by somebody willing to let an actual murder happen inside it.

Fact: the recent signature purporting to be Eleanor Voss's had been traced by a left hand, and Silas Crane was right-handed, which meant someone else in that house was signing for a woman who might be alive, might be imprisoned, and might have until ten tomorrow morning.

The storm hit the window like a palm laid flat against glass. Somewhere out there, every player in this miserable drama was moving toward the same door, and I intended to be standing in front of it when they arrived.

I put on my coat, took the revolver I hadn't touched since the Hollis estate matter in '09, and went out into the rain.

* * *

Silas Crane nailed the last board over the kitchen window at dusk, and the sound of it — three strikes, then silence — felt like the only honest conversation he'd had in thirty years.

He had been preparing for this night the way other men prepare for judgment: slowly, methodically, and with the growing understanding that preparation is just fear wearing work clothes. The boards were less about weather than about visitors. He knew who would come. He'd known since the morning he found the second set of footprints in the east garden mud, small ones, a woman's, stopping below the curtained window before turning back toward the road. Marianne. Or Ruth's people. Or both.

Inside the house, the lamps were lit in the wrong rooms — deliberately, a decoy geography he'd designed years ago and never needed until now. Anyone watching from the drive would conclude the mistress of the house kept her evening in the parlor and retired up the main stair. In truth, Eleanor Voss had not climbed those stairs in eleven days. She sat instead in the room at the end of the east hall, behind a door Silas locked each night with a key he wore against his sternum like a reliquary, and she sat there because she had chosen to, which was the part of the arrangement that gnawed at him worst.

Protection and imprisonment, he had learned, are separated by a distance you can measure only from the inside.

He checked the bolt on the service door. He checked the telephone line — dead since noon, cut clean at the junction box, which meant someone had done it deliberately and recently, and neither of those facts comforted him. He poured himself two fingers of the good bourbon he'd been saving for a bad night and recognized, with the clarity of men who have lied professionally for decades, that this was it. The bad night. The one the whole careful architecture of his life had been built to postpone.

From down the east hall came a sound. Not Eleanor's cough. Not the shuffle of her slippers. A knock — soft, deliberate, three times — from a door he had locked an hour ago.

Silas set down the glass. His right hand, the honest one, trembled. His left, the one that had learned to sign another woman's name, was perfectly steady, and that difference told him everything about which half of his life was about to end.

He walked the length of the hall. He unlocked the door.

Eleanor Voss stood in the lamplight, wrapped in a shawl, thin as a struck match and twice as flammable, and her eyes were clearer than they had been in months.

"Silas," she said, "open the front door. She's here."

"Who's—"

"All of them," said Eleanor. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Marianne Voss had driven through two hours of storm with the wipers losing their argument against the rain, and she had used every minute of it rehearsing a speech she already knew she wouldn't deliver.

She had rehearsed versions of it for years, actually — in the car outside lawyers' offices, in hotel rooms in three counties, once, memorably, into the rearview mirror of a parked Buick while a stranger washed his windshield and watched her mouth move with what he probably assumed was prayer. The speech always began with *Mother, we need to talk about Grandfather's will*, and it always ended with some version of justice, and in none of its drafts did it include the words *and then I considered killing you*, which was the sentence that had been living in her chest for five weeks now like a swallowed stone.

She turned onto the Voss drive as the lightning came down in long white ropes. The house rose out of the rain the way it always had — vast, symmetrical, a building designed by people who believed grief should have good proportions — and every ground-floor window was lit, which was wrong. Her mother had kept a dark house for a decade. Her mother had kept a dark everything.

Marianne parked under the copper beech and sat with her hands on the wheel, engine ticking, rain drumming the roof like impatient fingers.

Here was what she would never say aloud, not even now, not even to herself in the privacy of her own skull where she said most things: she had come back to Hollow Creek six weeks ago intending to confront Eleanor about the forged will — the one Wendell Fairweather was now pulling apart thread by thread, God bless his obsessive little heart — and she had found instead a house performing life. A check signed in her mother's trembling hand. A caretaker who answered questions like a man reading from approved text. And a mother who, when Marianne finally got one glimpse of her through a doorway, had looked directly at her daughter and shaken her head — one slow, unmistakable motion — *not yet, not here.*

So Marianne had waited. And while she waited, she had planned. And somewhere between the planning and the waiting, the plan had grown teeth. If the new will didn't get signed — if something happened to Eleanor before ten o'clock tomorrow — the old fraud would stand forever, and Marianne would inherit nothing, and the Calloway family's stolen decades would stay stolen. She had told herself the contingency was defensive. Contingencies always tell themselves that.

She got out of the car into the deluge, ran the gravel to the porch, and rang the bell.

The door opened before the second chime. Silas Crane stood there with a lamp behind him and a face like a closed ledger.

"You shouldn't have come," he said.

"I know," said Marianne. "Let me in anyway."

Behind him, from deep in the house, a voice carried down the hall — thin, cracked, and absolutely lucid, a voice Marianne hadn't heard properly in thirty years:

"For God's sake, Silas, let my daughter in out of the rain. We're all going to be in the same room by morning whether we like it or not. We may as well start dry."

* * *

Ruth Calloway had not asked permission to leave the mausoleum, because asking permission was the habit of a lifetime she had buried along with her own name in plot forty-one.

Still, Wendell Fairweather argued with her the entire way down the hill, which she supposed was the closest thing to permission he knew how to give.

"You're seventy-three years old," he said, holding the flashlight low against the sheeting rain. "You haven't been seen in public in three decades. The moment you walk into that house, every secret in this county stops being a secret."

"That," said Ruth, gripping his arm with a strength that surprised them both, "is the general idea, Mr. Fairweather."

"The man who tried to kill you in 1971 might still be alive. The person cutting phone lines and forging signatures is certainly awake. And you want to walk into the middle of it because—"

"Because I spent thirty years being safe." Her voice came steady through the dark, pitched under the rain. "Safe in a crypt, Mr. Fairweather. Safe among strangers' names. Do you know what I did with my evenings, all those years? I practiced my testimony. Out loud, to the coffins. Every word I would say if anyone ever asked me about that night in Ambrose Lark's office. I know it better than the Lord's Prayer." She paused at the gate, lightning whitening her face — older than her years, carved by three decades of hiding, but unbroken. "Nobody ever asked. So tomorrow I'm going to stand in a room full of people and ask myself."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Then he opened the gate.

They had gone perhaps two hundred yards down the flooded lane when the headlights swept them — a truck, high-beamed, sliding to a stop beside them with a hiss of water. The window cranked down and revealed the weathered face of Amos Tuller, retired sheriff, sixty-eight years old and dressed, incongruously, in his old uniform shirt.

"Fairweather," Amos shouted over the rain. "Get in. Both of you." He glanced at Ruth, and something moved across his face — recognition, then a grief thirty years deferred. "Hello, Ruthie. I always figured you'd turn up at the end of this. Get in the truck. I've been sitting outside that estate for an hour trying to decide if I still have any authority, and I've decided I don't care."

* * *

I drove — rode, rather, since Amos drove the way he'd always driven, as though the road owed him money — with Ruth's hand still on my arm and the storm throwing branches across the lane ahead of us. My mind was doing arithmetic it didn't like.

Every player converging on one house. A cut phone line. A barricaded estate. A signing scheduled for ten a.m. with a county attorney due at nine-thirty. And somewhere in the arithmetic, an unknown variable: whoever had hijacked Lark's scheme, whoever traced that signature with a left hand, whoever wanted Eleanor Voss dead before ink met paper. Lark swore it wasn't him. Lark, notably, could not be reached tonight — his room at the Hollow Creek Inn stood empty, his car gone, and I chose to believe that was flight rather than positioning, mostly because the alternative required energy I was saving for other purposes.

"Fairweather," Amos said, eyes on the road. "When we get there, what exactly is the plan?"

"The plan," I said, "is that nobody signs anything, nobody dies, and everybody tells the truth, in roughly that order of difficulty."

"And if the house doesn't cooperate?"

"Then I'll do what I've done all case, Amos. I'll write it down accurately and hope accuracy counts for something."

He snorted. Beside me, Ruth laughed — a small, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose.

The Voss house appeared through the rain with every window burning, and standing in the open doorway, framed in lamplight like the final figure in a play neither of us had auditioned for, was Silas Crane. He wasn't barring the door. He was holding it wide.

And behind him, descending the main staircase — the staircase she hadn't climbed in eleven days, the staircase the whole town believed she'd forgotten how to use — came Eleanor Voss, upright, shawled, and looking directly at me with the expression of a woman who has been waiting a very long time to compare notes.

"Mr. Fairweather," she called over the storm. "You took your time. Come in — all of you. We have until ten o'clock, and I'm afraid we need to discuss the murder."

Lightning split the sky behind us.

"Mine," said Eleanor Voss, "isn't the one you should worry about."

* * *

We were barely through the door, dripping on the imported runner, when the knock came — heavy, official, three strikes from the front porch.

Silas and I exchanged a look over the heads of two widows, a fugitive, a disgraced sheriff, and a prodigal daughter.

The county attorney wasn't due until morning. Nobody else in Hollow Creek knocked like that.

Amos's hand went to a holster he hadn't worn in fifteen years and found nothing there. Marianne went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the storm. And Eleanor Voss smiled — a small, terrible smile, the smile of a woman whose trap had just caught something she hadn't baited.

"Well," she said quietly. "Early."

Through the frosted glass of the door, a tall shape shifted, and raised its fist to knock again.
