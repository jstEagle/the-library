## 19. Plot Forty-One, Filled

The rain came down like the town itself had finally decided to confess.

I stood in the doorway of the Voss estate watching Silas Crane cross his own gravel court with a leather satchel under one arm and an umbrella he wasn't using, because men who are running don't bother with umbrellas. Behind me, in the parlor, Marianne Voss sat with her hands cuffed to nothing at all — I hadn't cuffs, so she'd settled for the weight of her mother's stare — and Eleanor Voss herself presided over the wreckage of a signing ceremony that would never now be signed, wrapped in a shawl, looking smaller than any woman I'd ever certified as living.

And out on the drive, the caretaker was making for a car I recognized from thirty years of county records: a dark sedan registered, until last spring, to a lawyer named Ambrose Pell. The lawyer who had drafted the original Voss will. The lawyer whose son was currently standing beside me wearing the face of a man called Mr. Lark.

"Silas," I said, loud enough to carry over the rain. "You're forgetting your coat."

He stopped. He didn't turn around right away, and in that pause I understood something important about him: Silas Crane had spent three decades being the most reliable man in Hollow Creek, and reliability, when it breaks, doesn't shatter. It buckles slowly, all at once.

"I'm going for the doctor," he said. "Mrs. Voss has had a shock."

"She's had thirty years of shocks," I said. "She's had you."

* * *

Let me back up, because the last ten minutes deserve their own coroner's report.

The signing had been set for two o'clock. Attorney Hobb — young, earnest, sweating through his collar — had laid out the new will on the dining room table with the reverence of a man handling scripture. Eleanor sat at the head. Ruth Calloway sat where a witness ought to sit, sixty-eight years old and visible for the first time in three decades, her hands folded like a woman arriving late to her own funeral. I stood by the window doing what I do best, which is watch people pretend not to be watched.

Marianne had broken twenty minutes earlier. It came out of her the way water comes through a dam — not all at once but in a rising line, first a crack in her voice, then the whole structure. She'd found the forged will in her father's papers after his death. She'd confronted Eleanor, quietly, a year ago. And when Eleanor's conscience had produced this honest rewrite, Marianne had discovered that honesty, applied retroactively, disinherited her completely.

"So I let it be known," she'd said, not looking at anyone, "that Mother wouldn't survive to sign it. If the will couldn't be stopped, the testator could be." A pause. "It was a contingency. I never—"

"You hired no one," Lark had said quickly, too quickly, "but someone heard the rumor. Someone decided to make it true."

And that was when Wendell Fairweather, coroner of Hollow Creek, keeper of every grave by name, noticed that Silas Crane was no longer in the room.

* * *

Now he stood forty feet away in the rain, and the satchel under his arm was the wrong shape for a doctor's bag.

"Mr. Crane," I said again, stepping off the porch. Sheriff Amos Dill — retired, seventy-four, built like a fence post that had decided against falling — fell into step beside me without being asked. Thirty years ago Amos had been the third name on the witness list to the original will. He'd been drinking about it ever since, in the way of men who signed something they didn't read.

"Son," Amos muttered to me, "that man's got a gun in his coat. I can see it print when he moves his arm."

"Then we'll be polite," I said.

"I'm always polite. That's why I retired."

Silas turned to face us at last. Rain ran off his jaw, and up close I saw what I should have seen weeks ago in that too-smooth parlor: a man who had been performing calm so long that the performance had eaten everything underneath. There was nothing left of Silas Crane but the role.

"The proceeds," he said. It wasn't an explanation. It was arithmetic. "Under the old will, the estate passes through the trust Pell established. I'm the trustee. I've been the trustee since 1994. Everything Mrs. Voss owns is legally mine to disburse, and I am disbursing it before that—" he nodded toward the house, toward Ruth, toward the truth sitting at the dining table like an uninvited guest, "—before that circus changes the paperwork."

"That money isn't yours," I said.

"It was never anyone's," Silas said, and here his voice did something terrible. It softened. "That's rather my point, Mr. Fairweather. Do you know how Eleanor Voss built this fortune? Her husband didn't earn it. Her *father* stole it. In 1989, Gerald Voss was dying, and his sister's family had a claim on half of everything, and Ambrose Pell drafted a will that erased them, and three people were paid or frightened into witnessing it." His eyes went to Amos Dill. "Sheriff. You took four hundred dollars and a promise to forget the smell of brandy on Gerald's breath. You've been forgetting it very loudly for thirty years."

Amos's face went gray. "Gerald signed it himself," he said. "I saw him sign it."

"Gerald Voss couldn't hold a pen in March of 1989," Silas said. "His *wife* signed it. Eleanor signed her husband's name while Pell held his hand steady above the paper, and Ruth Calloway refused to swear to what she'd seen, so Ruth Calloway was going to have an accident. And I—" he laughed, one dry note, awful, "—I drove her to the bus station at midnight because I was nineteen years old and the only person in that house with a functioning conscience. Then I stayed. Thirty years, Mr. Fairweather. I kept the lie alive because I was the only one keeping *her* alive. And now they want to take even the compensation."

The rain filled the silence.

"Eleanor knew," I said. Not a question. I'd seen it in her face upstairs, in the east wing, in the room at the end of the hall where she'd been playing dead to flush out her own murderer. "She knew which parent signed. She's known for years. That's why the new will."

"Eleanor knows whatever version of history lets her sleep," Silas said. "Ask her sometime whether she signed that will out of fear or ambition. She'll give you a different answer depending on the weather." He shifted the satchel. "Now. You're a coroner, not a constable. You have no authority to stop me, and Sheriff Dill has no badge, and if either of you touches me I'll say I feared for my life, and given the state of this county's records, who do you think the paper will believe?"

There it was. The whole philosophy of Hollow Creek in one sentence: the record decides, and whoever holds the pen holds the truth.

I thought about plot forty-one. About weighted stones and a decades-old coat and thirty years of perpetual care paid on an empty grave. About how the most reliable record in this town had been a lie maintained by exactly this man, for exactly these reasons, and how some part of me — the part that had once believed paper could not perjure itself — wanted to let him walk, just to see what the world looked like when the careful man won.

Then I thought about Ruth Calloway, alive, sitting in a warm room for the first time in thirty years, waiting to give testimony.

"No," I said.

"No?" Silas raised an eyebrow.

"No, I don't think the paper will believe you. Because as of nine o'clock this morning, the paper belongs to me." I held up the folder I'd been carrying under my jacket, dry, thank God; a coroner learns to protect documents the way other men protect their children. "Affidavit from Ruth Calloway, witnessed and notarized. Statement from the vanished investigator, taken down in a fishing cabin with two cups of bad coffee between us. Bank records tracing the perpetual-care payments on plot forty-one to a trust account you control. And a forensic analysis of a certain trembling check establishing that the signature of Eleanor Voss was traced by a left-handed forger — which rules out you, incidentally, and points quite firmly at the woman currently crying in your employer's parlor."

Silas looked at the folder the way a drowning man looks at a life raft that is clearly, insultingly, someone else's.

"You've been busy," he said.

"It's what I'm paid for. Triple rate." I glanced at Lark, who had followed us out onto the porch and stood there in the rain getting thoroughly soaked and looking, for the first time since I'd met him, like an honest man — which is to say, like a fool. "Though the invoice may get complicated."

Silas moved.

I'd like to tell you I anticipated it. I'd like to tell you that Wendell Fairweather, meticulous student of human remains, read the micro-tells in the man's shoulders and stepped aside with professional grace. What actually happened is that he shoved past me hard enough to knock me into the mud, and Amos Dill — seventy-four years old, three fingers of bourbon into his morning — hit him at the waist like a fence post falling in the direction of a fence.

We went down together, all three of us, a tangle of coats and elbows and the satchel, which burst open on the gravel and scattered its contents across the drive. Passbooks. Deeds. A revolver, which skidded six feet and stopped against the porch step, and which Lark — bless his cowardly, scheming heart — put his foot on and left there, hands raised, saying "Not mine, not mine, I want that noted."

Silas got an elbow into Amos's face. I heard something in the old sheriff's nose go, a sound I know professionally, and Amos made a noise like a man agreeing with it and did not let go. Silas twisted, got a knee under himself, reached for the satchel —

And I did the thing I will think about for the rest of my life.

I had his wrist. Both my hands on it, and beneath us the gravel, and somewhere near my knee a piece of the scattered contents — a heavy brass letter-opener, Pell's monogram on the handle, fallen from the satchel like the past itself offering me a tool. And Silas was strong, and frightened, and frightened men are strongest of all, and for one clear cold second I understood that I could end this. Press his wrist down into the stone. Break something. Make him stay. Justice, outside the law, delivered by the town's most reliable record-keeper, and who would ever question it? Who audits a coroner?

Ruth Calloway spent thirty years in hiding because powerful men decided the law was a thing you could arrange. Every crime in this case — the forged will, the empty grave, the trembling check, the murder that hadn't happened yet — began with someone deciding that the proper process was slow, and inconvenient, and could be improved upon.

I let go of his wrist. I picked up the letter-opener, and I threw it — underhand, badly, into the flowerbed.

"Mr. Fairweather," Silas panted, staring at me.

"Get off him, Amos."

"He broke my nose."

"You'll live. I'll write the report myself." I hauled Silas up by the collar, mostly because my knees had opinions about staying down. Up close, bleeding from where the gravel had opened his cheek, he looked suddenly, enormously tired — thirty years of tired, surfacing all at once like a body coming up from deep water. "You're going to sit in that parlor, Mr. Crane, and you're going to tell the whole truth, including the part about which parent held the pen. And then you're going to cooperate with the actual authorities, whom I have already called, using the actual telephone in your own kitchen, while you were busy packing."

"You called the state police," he said flatly.

"I called them an hour ago. Before the signing. I've learned my lesson about this town — I document first and confront second." I allowed myself one small, unprofessional satisfaction. "They'll be here in twelve minutes. You'll note I chose restraint anyway. I'd like that noted."

Amos sat in the mud holding his nose, laughing wetly. "Twelve minutes," he wheezed. "Boy, you planned a citizen's arrest with a twelve-minute intermission."

"I planned a conversation. The arrest was his idea."

* * *

They sat us all in the parlor eventually — the state police arrived in nine minutes, which I choose to attribute to my precise directions rather than to anything as vulgar as luck — and Silas Crane told it all. Gerald Voss dying of the palsy, Eleanor signing her husband's name with Pell guiding the pen, the sister's family erased from their own inheritance, Ruth refusing to swear to a lie and marked for removal. He told it in a flat voice, staring at the carpet, and when he got to the part about driving Ruth to the midnight bus, Eleanor spoke for the first time in an hour.

"I know," she said. "I've always known. I signed that will, Silas. My hand. My choice. Whatever else happened, that part was mine." She looked at her daughter, at her caretaker, at the attorney whose father had built the machine. "The rest of you arranged things. I consented to them. Don't any of you dare carry it for me."

Silas's shoulders dropped, and thirty years of caretaking ended in that parlor with a sound like a long exhale.

The troopers took the satchel, the revolver, the passbooks, and Silas. They took statements from everyone, including Ruth, who gave hers standing up. They took Amos's, too, and he gave it with a broken nose and both hands flat on the table like a schoolboy, and when it was done he looked at me and said, quietly, "Four hundred dollars, Wendell. I was twenty-six and stupid, and I've been paying interest on it my whole life."

"I know," I said. "Interest compounds. That's rather the theme."

Outside, the rain was easing. Through the window I watched the troopers' cruiser pull away down the gravel drive carrying Silas Crane, and behind it, absurdly, the dark sedan sat abandoned with its driver's door hanging open, filling slowly with rainwater — the fraudulent will's proceeds, such as they were, reduced to a vehicle nobody would claim.

Lark appeared at my elbow. He looked wrung out, younger than his con, and oddly relieved, the way men do when a scheme collapses and they discover they'd been hoping it would.

"Well," he said. "You proved a murder that didn't happen, exposed a forgery that did, recovered a witness everyone buried, and arrested a trustee with a notarized folder and a strong arm. Any of that in your job description?"

"I'm a coroner," I said. "I determine cause of death." I looked out at the drive, at the rain-washed afternoon, at the house full of living people who had spent decades pretending otherwise. "Today's finding: everything in Hollow Creek died of natural causes. Greed, guilt, and time. Very natural."

"And what happens to Ruth now?"

I watched through the parlor door as Ruth Calloway sat beside Eleanor Voss, two old women with thirty years of unfinished business between them, and Eleanor was saying something low and urgent, and Ruth was shaking her head — not refusal, I thought. Correction. There was testimony yet to give, and it wasn't for the police.

"Now," I said, "she gives the statement that matters. Sworn, complete, thirty years late." I picked up my hat. "And I intend to be there for every word — because tomorrow morning, there's a will to sign, and this time, God help me, I'm going to watch every pen in the room."
