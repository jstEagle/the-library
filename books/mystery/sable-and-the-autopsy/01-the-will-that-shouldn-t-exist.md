## 1. The Will That Shouldn't Exist

The rain had been falling on Hallowmere for three days without pause or apology, and by the time Gideon Halloran's taxi crunched up the gravel drive of Sable House, he had stopped trying to keep his shoes dry and settled instead for keeping his dignity merely damp.

He was forty-six years old, a probate lawyer of modest reputation and immaculate habits, and he had read more wills than most people had read novels. He liked to say — usually to clients who expected drama — that a will was just a letter written too late. People revealed themselves in them the way they never did in life: petty, generous, vindictive, tender, often all four on the same page. It was, in his private opinion, the most honest branch of the law, which was rather like being the tallest building in a very flat town.

Sable House loomed out of the wet grey like something a novelist had ordered but couldn't quite pay for. Victorian gothic, half-timbered, its windows lit unevenly as though the house itself were undecided about receiving guests. A man in a dark suit stood under the portico holding an umbrella that had clearly been deployed on his behalf, not his own.

"Mr. Halloran." The man's voice was smooth as bank stationery. "Marguerite Voss. We spoke on the telephone."

"You did most of the speaking," Gideon said, shaking her hand. Her grip was firm and cold, like a door handle in January.

"I wanted to be clear about the situation." She led him inside, into a hall that smelled of beeswax and old fires. "My aunt has been dead seven years, Mr. Halloran. Declared dead. There is a court order. And yet last week a firm I have never heard of telephoned to say she'd left a new will, dated — " Marguerite paused with the precision of someone who had rehearsed this pause — "three days ago."

"That is unusual," Gideon agreed.

"It's fraudulent."

"That's also possible. Though fraud usually requires effort, and in my experience families who forge wills tend to leave themselves rather more money than this one apparently does."

She looked at him then, really looked, the way one looks at a piece of furniture one is considering having reupholstered. "You're not what I expected."

"People rarely are. Shall we?"

* * *

There were five beneficiaries gathered in the drawing room, arranged around the fireplace with the studied casualness of people who had discussed their seating at length. Gideon catalogued them out of habit: Marguerite Voss, the niece, mid-forties, wearing grief the way some women wear perfume — applied carefully, noticeable from across the room. A brother-in-law type called Desmond who kept checking his phone and putting it away as if it were contraband. Two cousins whose names he would need to check against the file because they introduced themselves simultaneously and he caught neither. And at the edge of the group, a young woman with ink-stained fingers and red eyes who was the only person in the room not looking at him.

Everyone else was looking at him. That was the first thing that snagged, small and sharp, like a splinter under a fingernail.

In thirty years of readings, Gideon had developed a theory: you could tell everything about a family by where they looked during the reading of a will. The honest ones watched the lawyer. The guilty ones watched each other. This family watched *him* — all five of them, with the fixed attention of an audience waiting to see whether the magician would notice the loaded deck.

"Before we begin," Marguerite said, "I want it noted that we attend under protest."

"Noted," said Gideon, who had no authority to note anything and no intention of doing so. He opened his case folder. "The document before me purports to be the last will and testament of Vivienne Anne Sable, of Sable House, Hallowmere. Dated — " he checked, though he had already memorized it, had turned the date over in his mind the entire taxi ride like a stone with something written on it — "the eleventh of October. Last Tuesday."

A silence followed, the kind with weight to it. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a clock chimed once, indecisively.

"My aunt died in 2018," said the cousin on the left, whose name might have been Peter.

"So the court record indicates," Gideon said mildly. "Presumed drowned. Body never recovered, if I recall the file correctly, which I do, because I read files twice." He looked around the room. "And yet here we are. Someone delivered a will to my office bearing her signature, witnessed by two parties I intend to locate, and accompanied by instructions that I personally conduct the reading. Whoever she was, Vivienne Sable went to considerable trouble to be dead and then, apparently, un-dead."

"Then you agree it's nonsense," Marguerite said. It wasn't quite a question. It also wasn't quite a statement. It was, Gideon thought, a test dressed as a statement, and he had failed enough tests in his life to recognize the posture.

"I agree it's irregular," he said. "Nonsense is a finding for a court. My job today is to read it aloud. Unless someone wishes to instruct me otherwise?" He let the question sit there, polite and immovable, like a tea trolley blocking a doorway.

Nobody instructed him otherwise. But Desmond's phone came out again, and this time it stayed out, angled away, and Gideon filed that away too.

He began to read.

The will itself was short — suspiciously short, the work of someone who knew exactly how wills worked and wanted no ambiguity. The estate, entire, was left in trust. The income to the beneficiaries named in the room, in shares he recited while they sat very still. But the capital — the house, the investments, the whole substantial body of Vivienne Sable's money — was held for a period of one year, pending "the resolution of a matter known to my executor," after which it would pass entirely to a fund for "the memorialization of the drowned."

"The drowned," repeated Marguerite. "What drowned? What does that mean?"

"The will doesn't specify," Gideon said. "Wills rarely explain themselves. They're the last word, not the full conversation." He turned the page. "There is a residuary clause. And an appointment of executor."

"Who?" asked the young woman with the ink-stained fingers, speaking for the first time. Her voice cracked on the single syllable.

Gideon looked down at the name and felt the odd sensation of a floor shifting beneath a chair he had sat in comfortably for years.

"Gideon Halloran," he read aloud. "Of Halloran & Wren. That is — " he cleared his throat — "that is me."

The room did not erupt. That was the strange part. In his experience, surprises in wills produced eruptions — shouting, weeping, at least one dramatic exit. Instead, five faces simply tightened, in near-unison, like a fist closing slowly. They had known. They had known he would be named executor, and they had wanted to see his face when he found out, and now they were watching to see what he would do about it.

"Why you?" the ink-stained girl asked, softer now.

"I haven't the faintest idea," Gideon said honestly. "I never met your aunt. To my knowledge, I've never drawn a breath of air in the same county as your aunt." This was true, and it was also, he realized with a small internal lurch, no longer entirely certain to remain true.

"There must be dozens of lawyers in this city," Marguerite said. "Better ones."

"Almost certainly," Gideon agreed warmly. "But I'm the one who's here."

* * *

The reading concluded. The beneficiaries dispersed into the rain with the efficiency of people who had somewhere else to be — together, he suspected, and soon. Only the ink-stained girl lingered, hovering by the door.

"She used to write letters," the girl said abruptly. "Aunt Vee. Hundreds of them. She said a letter was the only way to say a true thing, because you could say it to someone who wasn't in the room to interrupt." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, smearing nothing. "If she wrote you anything, Mr. Halloran — you'll read it properly? Not skim it. People skim her. Everyone always skimmed her."

"I don't skim," Gideon said. "It's a professional deformity."

She nodded once, as if that were the correct answer to some question she hadn't asked aloud, and left.

The housekeeper showed him to the study, where the estate papers waited in a single locked box, per the instructions that had come with the will. Gideon worked alone for two hours in a pool of lamplight while the rain performed its endless percussion on the glass. Deeds. Insurance policies, curiously recent. Bank statements showing an account that had been active — actively active, transactions monthly — until six weeks ago. For a dead woman, Vivienne Sable had been remarkably solvent.

At the bottom of the box, beneath a false bottom that was less a concealment than an invitation, lay two envelopes.

They were identical: heavy cream paper, sealed with wax, each addressed in a slanted, confident hand. One read *To Gideon Halloran — to be opened alone.* The other read *To Gideon Halloran — to be earned.*

He sat very still for a long moment, listening to the rain.

Now, Gideon Halloran was a man of rules. He had perhaps eleven of them, honed over decades, and rule number four — learned expensively, early, from a case involving a disputed farm and two brothers who no longer spoke — was this: *never take the unwinnable case.* A lawyer who couldn't close a file carried it forever, like a stone in the shoe of his soul. Dead women don't sign wills. Dead women don't retain lawyers. Whatever this was, the sane response was to telephone the registrar in the morning, report an anomaly, and go home to his cat and his single malt and his orderly, winnable life.

He picked up the first envelope anyway.

"To be opened alone," he murmured. "Well. I am famously alone."

He broke the seal.

The handwriting inside was the same slanted script, pressed hard enough to emboss the page, as though the writer had needed the words to physically exist. It began without greeting:

*Mr. Halloran. By the time you read this, I have been dead for seven years, which I have found to be quite long enough. I am writing to confess to a murder. You will know it — everyone in Hallowmere knows it. On the fourth of November, 2017, Thomas Reeve went into the water below the old mill bridge and did not come out. The police closed the case. The town closed the case. I am writing to open it again, because I am the one who killed him, and because the truth has been sitting in my chest for seven years like a swallowed stone, and I find at the end I would like to put it down.*

Gideon set the page down on the desk. His hands were perfectly steady, which surprised him; inside, something was moving around like a bird trapped in a church.

Thomas Reeve. Even the name arrived with weather attached. Every lawyer in the region knew the Reeve drowning the way coastal towns know their worst storm — the wealthy industrialist, the flood year, the detective who'd wrapped it up in eleven days and been decorated for it. Gideon had been a junior associate then. Aldous Wren — his mentor, his north star, the man whose framed photograph still occupied the only space on Gideon's desk not occupied by paperwork — had followed the case the way other men followed football.

He picked up the second envelope. Turned it over. *To be earned.*

He didn't open it. Some instinct older than rule number four told him that this one was the lock, and the first letter merely the key turning in it — and that whatever was promised inside would cost him something he hadn't yet agreed to pay.

Instead he folded both letters into his breast pocket, against his heart like a sentimental fool, and told himself it was evidence protocol.

Outside, the rain went on. Upstairs, the indecisive clock finally committed to striking midnight. And Gideon Halloran, who never took the unwinnable case, sat down in a dead woman's study and began, methodically, to make a list of every fact that didn't fit — starting with the date on the will, and ending, though he didn't know it yet, with himself.
