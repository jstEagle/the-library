## 7. What Aldous Knew

The Wren & Halloran archive lived in the basement of the old probate building, in a room that smelled of dust, pipe tobacco, and the particular melancholy of paper that no one expected to read again. Aldous Wren had kept meticulous files for forty years—every retainer, every correspondence, every billable hour accounted for in his small, upright hand. Gideon had inherited the archive along with the practice, and in eleven years he had gone down those stairs perhaps a dozen times, always for something mundane.

He went down now at seven in the morning, before the office opened, with a thermos of coffee and a flashlight he was slightly embarrassed to need.

*Vivienne Sable.* He started with the S drawer, because he was a lawyer and lawyers believe in alphabetical order the way priests believe in liturgy.

Nothing.

He tried *Reeve*. Nothing. He tried *Ashcombe*, on the off chance, and found only a land dispute from 1994 involving a man who had sued his own brother over a fence. Gideon read three pages of it before catching himself, because Aldous's voice came back to him across the years: *You're stalling, boy. When you can't find what you're looking for, ask yourself what you're afraid of finding.*

Gideon sat down on a crate of 1980s conveyances and drank his coffee and asked himself the question honestly.

He was afraid of finding that Aldous Wren—the man who had taught him that a will was a promise made in advance, who had once driven two hours through a snowstorm to hand-deliver an amendment to a dying widow because "a signature shouldn't have to wait"—was mixed up in whatever Vivienne Sable had been doing for the last seven years.

So he stopped looking for her name and started looking for dates instead. The will was dated three days before its reading. If Sable had been alive until last month—if she'd walked into some office somewhere and signed her name in front of witnesses—then someone had notarized it, filed it, arranged the reading. Someone with access to the machinery of probate.

Someone like Aldous Wren.

Gideon pulled the files from the year of Reeve's drowning—2017—and worked backward through them page by page. It took him four hours. He found nothing about Sable, nothing about Reeve, nothing out of place at all, which was itself strange, because Aldous's files were never this clean. There were always loose threads, carbon copies, notes to self. The 2017 box was tidy as a coffin.

It was too tidy. And Gideon might have missed it entirely if he hadn't knocked the box off the crate reaching for his coffee.

The false bottom slid six inches, and underneath it lay a single folder, unmarked except for a date: March 2017. Two months before Thomas Reeve drowned.

Inside was a retainer agreement.

Gideon read it twice, then a third time, his hands not quite steady. The parties were listed as: *Aldous J. Wren, Esq., acting on behalf of an unnamed client (hereinafter "the Principal") in matters pertaining to Reeve Hydrological Ltd., including but not limited to estate planning, identity management, and the disposition of assets arising from the Ashcombe Reservoir Project.*

Identity management. Estate planning. A client whose name didn't appear anywhere—not even in the signature block, which bore only a small, precise initial: **V.S.**

And at the bottom, in Aldous's handwriting, a note that had nothing to do with law:

*A — You know what she lost. You know what they took. Some promises are worth keeping even when they're lies. Do not open this file again. — A.W.*

Gideon sat in the dark of the archive for a long time, listening to the pipes tick in the walls.

His mentor had helped her disappear. Had built her a new name, a new death, a clean legal ghost. And had kept the evidence hidden one floor above where Gideon worked every day, trusting him—or perhaps testing him—to never go looking.

*Some promises are worth keeping even when they're lies.*

Gideon folded the retainer into his coat pocket, next to the two sealed letters, and climbed the stairs into the gray morning light feeling as though he were carrying something radioactive.

* * *

Marguerite Sable-Voss was waiting in his office.

She sat in the visitor's chair with her ankles crossed and her coat still buttoned, as though she'd been shown in by Mrs. Pell and had declined to make herself comfortable on principle. On his desk, square in the middle, sat a plain cream envelope.

"Mr. Halloran," she said pleasantly. "I hope you don't mind. Your secretary is very obliging."

"Mrs. Pell is very well trained," Gideon said, hanging up his coat with deliberate slowness, keeping the pocket with the retainer turned away from her. "To what do I owe the pleasure?"

"You've been busy." Marguerite smiled. She had her aunt's cheekbones, Gideon thought, and none of her aunt's warmth—or rather, all of her aunt's warmth, deployed with the same precision, which amounted to the same thing. "Ashcombe. The county archive. Poor Mr. Fenwick—he does so love company, but he mentioned you asked some pointed questions about a photograph."

"The archive is public record."

"Mm. And Elena Marsh. You visited her on Tuesday." Marguerite unfolded her hands, folded them again. "Widow of Detective Marsh. That struck us as an unusual line of inquiry for a simple will reading."

"There's nothing simple about this will, Ms. Sable-Voss. You know that better than anyone. You've told half the county it's fraudulent."

"I have." She said it without a flicker of shame. "And I'll tell you now why I'm wrong to say it, and why I'm right, and why neither of those things matters." She nudged the envelope an inch toward him. "Open it."

Gideon opened it. Inside was a check. The number had enough digits in it to make him sit down without quite deciding to.

"That's a retainer," Marguerite said. "For your continued professional discretion. Not silence—discretion. There's a difference, and I'm told you appreciate differences. You may keep investigating. You may even close the estate. What you may not do is pursue the second letter. Burn it. File it unread. Lose it. I genuinely don't care how. My family has spent seven years burying my aunt once already, Mr. Halloran. We would prefer not to dig her up."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'm afraid the check becomes something else." Her smile didn't move but changed temperature entirely, the way a room goes cold when a door opens onto winter. "A record of improper contact between counsel and a beneficiary. A complaint to the bar. An allegation—credible, detailed, regrettable—that Gideon Halloran solicited a bribe to suppress evidence in a contested estate. Your mentor's name is already tangled in this, isn't it? We know about Aldous. We've known for years. One more compromised Wren man won't surprise anyone."

The office was very quiet. Somewhere below, the archive pipes ticked on, indifferent.

Gideon looked at the check for a long moment. Then he did something he would later be unable to fully explain, even to himself: he laughed. Not loudly—a short, tired breath of a laugh—but Marguerite flinched as though he'd thrown something.

"You misunderstand the situation," he said. "You think you're offering me a choice between money and trouble. But I've spent two weeks reading letters from a woman who planned her own death twice, and do you know what I've learned? She doesn't leave choices. She leaves traps that look like choices." He slid the envelope back across the desk. "Your family never contested this will. You could have. You had grounds, lawyers, money. Instead you let me read it, watched me like hawks, and now you're here buying my discretion before the second letter opens. You're not fighting the case, Ms. Sable-Voss. You're trying to close it. Which tells me exactly one thing."

"And what's that?"

"That whatever's in that second letter frightens you more than any court ever could."

Marguerite stood. For just a moment—half a heartbeat—the rehearsed composure slipped, and beneath it Gideon saw something raw and exhausted and almost young. Fear, yes. But grief too, real grief, worn smooth by seven years of carrying it.

"She was my aunt," Marguerite said quietly. "Whatever you think we are, remember that we loved her. And remember that loving someone sometimes means letting their lies stand." She buttoned her coat. "You have until Friday."

The door closed behind her. Gideon looked at the check, at the two letters in his coat, at the retainer agreement hidden upstairs in a place Marguerite clearly already knew about.

Then he noticed what she'd left behind: the envelope, empty, propped against his blotter. Written on the inside flap, in a hand he recognized from the will—small, upright, unmistakable—were five words:

*Boathouse. Thursday. Come alone. — V.*

Gideon stared at it, heart hammering, and understood that Marguerite hadn't come to threaten him at all.

She'd come to deliver the invitation—because Vivienne Sable had ordered her to.
