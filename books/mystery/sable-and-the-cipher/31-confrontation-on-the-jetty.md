## 31. Confrontation on the Jetty

The storm came in off the bay around four o'clock, the way November storms do — not arriving so much as being revealed, as though it had been standing offshore all day waiting for permission. By five the windows of the Meridian's lounge were streaked and shivering, and by six the power had flickered twice, and by seven Theo Marsh had found me on the jetty.

I should say I went to him. That would be more honest, and honesty is the currency I've been trying to learn to spend. But the truth has a shape, and its shape is this: he was standing at the end of the jetty in a long coat with his collar up, watching the water hammer the pilings, and when I came out onto the boards with no good reason to be there, he didn't turn around. He just said my name.

"Ines."

Two syllables, warm as a lobby fire. He had always had that gift — making your name sound like an invitation.

"You shouldn't be out here," I said.

"Neither should you." He turned then, and the wind took his words sideways, and we both leaned a little into it, two people pretending the weather was ordinary. "But here we are. Isn't that always how it goes? Here we are."

Behind us the hotel glowed against the dark, every window lit because the reunion party was still going — the glasses, the laughter, the five survivors of August 1994 drinking very good wine in a room where none of them would say Sable Voss's name aloud. I could hear it faintly, the murmur of it, like a radio in another room. Like a party at the bottom of the sea.

"I've been thinking about Marlowe," Theo said.

I kept my hands in my pockets. The rain had begun in earnest now, fine and needling, and the jetty's old boards were slick as soap beneath my shoes. Thirty years ago they'd replaced the railings — I'd read the work order myself, in the hotel's basement archive, dated October 1994, six weeks after a boy went into the water. Someone had wanted new railings very quickly. Someone had wanted the old ones gone.

"He used to stand right here," Theo said. "Did you know that? End of the season, after the last guests left. He'd walk out to the end with a thermos and just — look. I asked him once what he was looking for." Theo smiled, and even soaked through, even at the edge of everything, the smile worked. It had always worked. "He said, *the tide brings back whatever you can't afford to keep.*"

"That doesn't sound like Marlowe," I said. "That sounds like something you'd put in a brochure."

The smile thinned but held. Wind shoved between us, and somewhere down the beach a shutter banged loose and began keeping its own frantic time.

* * *

Let me tell you what I carried out onto those boards, because I carried it deliberately, the way Marlowe taught me to carry anything that mattered: close to the body, weight distributed, invisible.

In the inside pocket of my coat, wrapped in a plastic sleeve against the rain, was a photocopy of a boy's diary. Sable Voss had written in it from June until the second week of August 1994, in handwriting that slanted forward like someone leaning into a race. I had read it eleven times. I knew entries by heart the way other people know prayers. And I had spent the last three days understanding, entry by entry, what Theo Marsh had done, and what he thought he still might get away with, and why he had invited five frightened people to drink his wine tonight under the banner of *reunion*.

He thought one of them had written the letters. He still thought that. He had spent thirty years believing his danger lived inside that circle, contained, manageable — and he had never once looked at the concierge pouring his scotch.

Marlowe had looked at me, though. Marlowe had bet on me. Two years dead, and I was still doing exactly what he'd trained me to do: noticing everything, owning nothing.

Until tonight.

* * *

"Theo," I said. "Why are we out here?"

"Because the jetty's the only place in this town where nobody can hear us." He said it lightly, but his eyes did something they almost never did — they stopped performing. For a moment I saw the sixteen-year-old underneath, the one in the photograph on the jetty, laughing with his arm around a boy who had eight weeks left to live. "And because I'm going to say some things, Ines, and I'd rather say them once."

The water hit the pilings below us, black and muscular. Spray climbed the boards and died.

"I know what you've been doing," he said. "The registers. The archives. Dana Okonkwo. Ruth Voss." He watched my face carefully when he said that last name, and I gave him nothing, which cost me. "You have to understand — I don't blame you. Marlowe trained you. You're the best reader I've ever met; I've known that since the first time you checked me in and told me my usual room was available before I asked. That's not criticism. That's admiration." He stepped closer, boots ringing on wet wood. "So let me offer you the same thing I offered him, all those years. Except better. Because unlike Marlowe, you haven't done anything yet. There's nothing to forgive. There's only a choice."

"Say it plainly," I said.

"I will." Rain ran off his jaw. "There's a position opening at the Marsh Foundation. Director of historical preservation. It pays more than you'll make in ten years at that desk. Your job would be exactly what you've already been doing — sorting the past into something presentable. The Meridian itself comes with it. New ownership, real investment, the whole property restored. Wren keeps her name. Dana keeps her quiet little life. Nobody else has to lose anything." He spread his hands, and the gesture was genuinely beautiful, genuinely sincere — that was the horror of Theo Marsh, that he believed his own mercy. "You'd be protecting people, Ines. The living ones. Isn't that what you do?"

For a moment — I want to be honest about this — the offer landed. Not the money. The *restoration*. Twenty years I had kept the Meridian alive with tape and prayer and the sheer force of noticing, and here was a man offering to save the only home I'd ever had, if I would just agree to keep one boy drowned.

That's how it works. That's how it has always worked in this town. They never ask you to do evil. They ask you to do housekeeping.

"No," I said.

Theo exhaled slowly. Something behind his eyes closed, like a register drawer sliding shut.

* * *

"You recite things when you're nervous," he said. "Did you know that? Little facts. Room numbers, tide tables, dates. Marlowe said you learned it from him, but I think he learned it from you." He shook his head, almost fondly. "Go ahead, then. Whatever speech you brought. I can see it in your pocket, Ines. I can see the shape of it."

So I took out the diary pages, and I didn't open them, because I didn't need to. I had them in me. I stood at the end of the jetty in the teeth of the first real storm of November and I gave Sable Voss his voice back.

"June twenty-ninth," I said. "*Dad drove me out to the Marsh place today. Mr. Marsh senior gave me a Coke in a glass bottle and asked me about school like he actually cared.*" Theo flinched — barely, millimeters, the kind of movement only a concierge would catch, and I caught it, and I hated that I caught it, and I kept going. "July eleventh. *Saw something at the boathouse I wasn't supposed to. Mr. Voss giving Mr. Finch an envelope. Finch looked sick about it.*"

"That's—" Theo started.

"July nineteenth. *Wren knows too. She saw the envelope. She made me promise not to tell.*" My voice cracked on her name and I let it crack; there was no use anymore in a smooth voice. "August third. *Something's wrong. Dad's acting weird about the sale. Everybody's smiling too much. I feel like I'm the only one who isn't in on something.*"

"Stop," Theo said.

"August ninth. His last entry. *If anything happens to me it wasn't an accident.*" The wind tore the words away and I threw them harder. "He wrote that, Theo. A fourteen-year-old boy wrote *if anything happens to me* and then he walked out onto this jetty with the five people he loved best in the world, and one of them shoved him, and the rail was already rotten, and he went into water cold enough to stop his heart in four minutes. And then your father bought the hotel, and Edmund Voss bought the silence, and the coroner bought a boat, and Marlowe bought—"

"Don't," Theo said, and his voice had changed. All the charm had drained out of it, leaving something flat and young and terrified. "Don't you dare talk about what Marlowe bought. You don't know what he—"

"He bought me." I said it steadily. "My tuition. My winter coat. The orthodontist. Every kindness of my entire life, paid for out of an envelope passed at a boathouse. I know exactly what Marlowe bought, Theo. I *am* what Marlowe bought. And I am telling you that it stops tonight."

He lunged.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. He didn't lunge for me. He lunged for the pages — for the photocopy in my hand, the way a drowning man lunges for driftwood, without thought, without dignity. His hand closed on the plastic sleeve and wrenched, and I held on, and for one absurd suspended second we were two grown people playing tug-of-war with a dead boy's diary in a rainstorm, and then his foot skidded on the wet boards and the world tilted.

The railing caught him.

The new railing — the 1994 railing, the replacement, the one installed six weeks too late — took his full weight across the chest, and groaned. I heard it groan. Wood does that, old wood, treated wood gone soft at the bolts: a sound like a word trying to be spoken. Thirty years ago another rail must have made that exact sound, and given way, and a boy had gone over into the black water, and everyone who heard it had agreed forever afterward to call it something else.

Theo hung there. Half over. His coat flapping, his hands scrabbling at wet wood, his feet finding nothing. Below him the bay heaved and slammed, white-eyed, hungry, patient. The same water. The identical water, come back on schedule, the way tides do.

And every instinct I owned said *let go*.

I want to be honest about that too, because I promised myself honesty even if it costs the last thing I have. Every instinct said let go. Some colder, older part of me — the part that had catalogued twenty years of secrets without touching them — calculated it in half a heartbeat: his hands slipping, the storm covering everything, one less liar, Wren free, Dana safe, the ledger speaking for itself. The sea would do what the sea had always done for this town. The sea takes the blame for everything.

Then I heard Sable's voice in my head — not the last entry, not the fear. June. Early June, bright and funny, the entry about the gull he'd named Reginald, the entry about teaching Wren to skip stones, the entry that ended *best summer ever starting NOW*. A boy who loved this water. A boy who would not have wanted it fed.

I dropped the diary pages. I heard them slap the wet boards, and I seized Theo Marsh's wrist with both hands, and the shock of it went up my arms like current — the bones, the heat, the terrible ordinary humanity of him.

"Pull!" I shouted, and he couldn't, he was past pulling, so I hauled, boots braced against a piling, back screaming, rain in my teeth. His chest scraped the rail. His knee came up and hooked wood. And then he was over the top and on the boards beside me, on his hands and knees, coughing spray, alive, and I knelt there gasping with his pulse still hammering in my palms as though I'd never quite let go of it.

We stayed like that for a long moment. Two men on their knees at the end of the jetty, the storm coming down on us like judgment or like weather, which may be the same thing.

"Why," Theo rasped. He lifted his head, and there was salt and rain on his face, and his eyes were wide and wet and for the first time in thirty years completely unguarded. "After everything. Why would you—"

"Because I'm not you," I said. "That's the whole answer. That's the only one there is."

* * *

Footsteps on the boards. Running, uneven, desperate.

She came out of the dark with no coat and her hair plastered to her face, and she was shouting my name before I could see her — *Ines, Ines, someone said you went down to the water* — and then she saw us, Theo sprawled and gasping, me kneeling with my hands still shaking, the diary pages fanned across the wet planks like a hand of cards the sea had dealt.

Wren Aldous stopped three feet away. The wind pushed her hair back and I saw her face do something I had never seen it do in thirty years of knowing her: it stopped hiding.

She looked at Theo. She looked at the pages. She looked at the rail — the rail, the groaning, guilty, innocent rail — and I watched thirty years leave her all at once, not gently, the way a tooth leaves.

"It's over," she said. Not a question. Her voice was steady in a way I understood was costing her everything. "It's over, Theo. I called Detective Reyes from the lobby phone. Ten minutes ago. Everything — the fight, the cover-up, all of it. My statement's already typed." She swallowed. "You don't have to run. Please don't run. Not again. Not into the water, not into the money, not anywhere. Just — stand up. Stand up and be a person for five minutes before they come."

Theo Marsh, owner of half the town, knelt on his own jetty in the rain and began, very quietly, to laugh. It was not a pleasant sound. It was also, I realized, the first honest sound I had ever heard him make.

Down the beach, headlights swung onto the coast road. Two vehicles, moving fast, no siren yet — Reyes, patient to the last, choosing arrival over pursuit. Behind us the hotel blazed against the storm, every window gold, the reunion party still murmuring inside, unaware or pretending to be, and above the door the old sign buzzed and guttered and held.

I gathered the diary pages out of the rain, one by one, careful as handling a guest's mail. Then I stood, and gave Wren my hand, and the three of us waited on the jetty for the law to arrive — the guilty, the broken, and the concierge, while below our feet the tide kept coming in, faithful as ever, carrying up everything it had ever been asked to hold.
