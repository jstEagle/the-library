## 5. The Boy Who Waited

The morning the podcast found me, I was chumming herring guts off the dock behind Marsh's Bait & Tackle, and the gulls were conducting their usual committee meeting over my head. That's how I like my mornings — loud, stupid, and honest. Nothing about the rest of that day would be any of those things.

I'd been listening to true-crime shows for a couple of years by then. Don't ask me why. A man who spent thirty years not talking about something has a funny relationship with other people talking. It's like watching someone else scratch an itch you've had since you were nineteen. Mostly it's torture. Sometimes it's company.

The show was called *Cold Open*, which I thought was a little cute for a program about dead girls, but the host had a voice I didn't mind — low, a bit sardonic, the kind of voice that made you feel like they were telling you a secret instead of selling you one. Dominic Montrose. I'd looked them up once, out of professional curiosity, which is what I called it when I couldn't sleep.

That morning they said: *"Grayhollow, Maine. Population 2,300 give or take, depending on the season and how honest people are feeling with the census."*

I stopped scraping the bait barrel.

*"In June of 1994, a seventeen-year-old girl named Sable Wren rode her bicycle up Cliff Road toward the Vail property where she worked summers, and she never came back down. Her shoes were found on the rocks below the overlook. Her bicycle was in the weeds at the shoulder. The tide took everything else — or so the file says. The file, incidentally, was closed in six days."*

Six days. He got that right. Six days and change, and Sheriff Dell Hargrove stood in front of the whole town at the firehouse meeting and told us the sea keeps its own records. People nodded. People wanted to nod. Nodding was easier than the alternative, which was asking why a drowning investigation needed to be closed before the Fourth of July fireworks.

I stood there with fish blood on my hands and listened to a stranger say her name into a microphone, and I'll tell you something nobody tells you about guilt: it doesn't age. It just waits. Thirty years, and the moment somebody said *Sable Wren* out loud in a tone that wasn't grief or gossip — a tone that was actually *listening* — my whole chest went hot, like I was nineteen again, standing on the bluff with a letter going brown in a coffee can fire.

I turned the radio off. Then I turned it back on. That's more or less been my relationship with this entire affair ever since.

* * *

Here's what I remember about the summer of 1994, and I want to be clear that I have spent three decades trying to remember it accurately, because memory is a liar that mostly lies to make you comfortable, and I decided early on I wasn't going to let it.

Sable Wren started working at the shop when she was fifteen. Her mother sent her down with a note written on the back of a church bulletin — *Theo will look after her, he's a good boy* — which was funny, because Mrs. Wren had never met me, and "good boy" was doing a lot of load-bearing work in that sentence. But I hired her. She showed up the next morning at seven sharp, hair dark as wet rope, and asked me where we kept the squid, and when I told her we kept it in the cooler and it smelled like God's own regret, she laughed. Not a polite laugh. A real one, surprised out of her.

She worked three summers. She learned the register, the tides, which tourists tip and which ones photograph you like you're part of the scenery. She could gut a mackerel faster than boys twice her size and she read books on the slow afternoons — paperbacks with cracked spines, science fiction mostly, stories about people leaving impossible places. I used to tell her she read like she was digging a tunnel.

Third summer, she wasn't laughing much.

I noticed it the way you notice weather. Small things first. Long sleeves in July. A bruise on her forearm shaped like nothing in particular, which is exactly what bruises are shaped like when you're inventing excuses for them. She flinched when the screen door banged. She stopped taking the shortcut past the Vail house even though it saved her ten minutes, and Sable Wren did not give up ten minutes for anything short of a hurricane.

Raymond Vail owned half the waterfront and leased me my dock, which is a fact I've thought about every day for thirty years, because it means that even my silence had a mortgage on it. Raymond came into the shop sometimes. Big man, easy smile, the kind of charm that works on everyone except the person standing closest to him. He'd buy tackle he didn't need and call Sable "sunshine" in a voice that made my skin crawl in a way I couldn't have explained then and have never once managed to explain since without sounding insane.

One afternoon in May — late May, fog sitting on the water like a lid — she rang up his purchase and he put his hand on her shoulder and left it there one second too long, and I saw her face do something I'd only seen on animals caught in leg traps. And I did nothing. I bagged his lures. I made change. I said *have a good one, Mr. Vail,* because that's what you said, because he was Raymond Vail and I was nineteen years old and worked in a bait shop.

After the door closed, Sable went into the back room and stayed there twenty minutes. When she came out her eyes were dry and hard as ball bearings, and she said, "Theo, if anything ever happens to me, don't let them say it was the sea."

I laughed. God help me, I laughed, because what else do you do with a sentence like that when you're nineteen and the fog is lifting and the day is ordinary? I said, "Nothing's going to happen to you, kid."

She looked at me for a long time. Longer than the moment deserved, or maybe exactly as long as it deserved, and I've replayed that look ten thousand times and it has never once gotten shorter.

"Okay," she said. And went back to the register.

* * *

Present day. The bell over the shop door jangled, and I said "Be with you" to whoever it was, because that's the liturgy of retail, and finished tying off a jig for old Pete Callahan's grandson, who was going to lose it to a rock within the hour, the way all grandsons do.

When I looked up, the customer was a woman in her thirties, canvas jacket, city boots that had already given up pretending to be hiking boots. She picked up a tide chart, put it down, picked up a lure, put it down. Tourists who buy nothing but touch everything. I gave her the standard Grayhollow welcome, which is to say I grunted in her general direction.

"You're Theo Marsh," she said. Not a question. "You knew Sable Wren."

And here's the thing I need you to understand, because it's the whole engine of this story: I had been waiting for that sentence for thirty years. I'd rehearsed answers to it in the shower, on the boat, at three in the morning. I had a whole repertoire prepared — the sad headshake, the *nice girl, terrible thing, small town never got over it.* I could perform grief like a man playing a hymn on piano, all the right notes, no soul required.

What I had not rehearsed was hearing her name come out of a stranger's mouth while I was holding a fishing knife.

"Everybody knew her," I said. "Small town."

"I'm doing a series on her disappearance." She set her phone on the counter between us, and the screen was showing a waveform, red and jagged, and I realized with a lurch that she might have been recording since she walked in. "Dominic Montrose. I do a podcast called *Cold Open*. Maybe you've heard it?"

Maybe I've heard it. Friend, I had heard episode one eleven times. I'd heard it while netting bait, while driving to Rockland, while lying awake listening to my own house settle around me like a man confessing under his breath.

"Can't say I have," I said. "Radio's mostly weather out here."

Something flickered across her face — not suspicion exactly, more like a fisherman watching a bobber that dipped but didn't go under. She knew I was holding something back. She just didn't know what, or how big.

"The sheriff says the case was straightforward," she said. "Drowning. Closed in six days."

"Dell Hargrove says a lot of things."

That one slipped out before I could stop it, and I watched her eyes sharpen like she'd felt a tug on the line. Thirty years of guilt compressed into a smile — that's what I gave her. I've gotten very good at it. It uses muscles in your face that eventually start to ache, and I suspect that ache is the only honest part of the expression.

"He also called her 'that poor girl' before I mentioned her name," Montrose said. "Which is interesting, because in a town of two thousand three hundred, you'd think a missing teenager would be famous, but nobody here talks about her like she's famous. They talk about her like she's *recent.*"

I tied off another jig that didn't need tying. "We're a polite town," I said. "Politeness looks like a lot of things from the outside."

"It does," she agreed, and smiled back, and hers was better than mine, because hers was real. "Thanks for your time, Mr. Marsh. If you think of anything — anything at all —" she slid a card across the counter, "— I'm easy to find."

She bought the tide chart. Paid cash. Bell jangled. Gone.

I stood there holding her card like it was ticking. Then I walked to the back room, sat down on the same stool Sable Wren once sat on reading about starships, and listened to my heart do something arrhythmic and humiliating.

On the wall above the workbench hangs a photograph from 1993. Fourth of July, the whole waterfront crew lined up on the dock. Dell Hargrove in his uniform, squinting. Cole Ives leaning against the ferry rail. Me, nineteen, sunburned, grinning like the future was a place worth going. And at the end of the row, half-turned toward the camera, Sable Wren, seventeen, holding up a mackerel like a trophy, laughing at whatever Cole had just said.

Everyone in that photograph is still alive. I want that on the record. Whatever else happened, whatever I did or failed to do — everyone in that photograph is still alive, and one of them is living sixty miles north under a name that isn't hers, and I have kept that fact like a stone under my tongue for thirty years.

Montrose was in town now. Which meant the clock was running.

* * *

The night she vanished, I burned a letter.

I've never told anyone that. Not Dell, not Cole, not Ruth Calloway with her courthouse drawers full of other people's secrets. It's mine, the way a scar is yours — proof of something, explanation of nothing.

It was June 14th, 1994. Fog again; that whole week was fog, like the town was being kept under glass. Around nine o'clock Sable came to the shop after hours and knocked on the back door, and when I opened it she was soaked through and shaking, and not from cold. She had a envelope in her hand, sealed, my name on it in her slanted handwriting.

"If I'm not here tomorrow," she said, "burn this. Don't read it. Burn it, and forget you ever saw me tonight."

"Sable—"

"*Burn it.*" And then, quieter, in a voice that scared me worse than the shouting: "Please, Theo. You're the only one who ever believed me about anything."

I didn't believe her about anything. That's the part that kills me. I believed she was scared, which is a different animal entirely, and I fed the difference to myself for years like it was a meal.

I took the envelope. She looked at me for that long look one more time — the last time, though neither of us knew it — and said, "Tell my mother I loved her. Not yet. Someday." And she went into the fog toward the water, and I stood in the doorway like a fence post until the fog closed behind her, and then I did the single most cowardly intelligent thing of my life.

I didn't burn it. Not right away. I sat on the floor of the back room with that envelope in my hands for two hours, turning it over, holding it up to the light like an idiot in a movie, wanting with everything in me to open it and knowing — knowing in the marrow, in the way you know a storm is coming — that opening it would make me a person who knew things, and people who knew things ended up in the newspaper or the water.

At midnight I put it in a coffee can with some newspaper and struck a match, and I watched my name curl black off the front of it, and I told myself that whatever she was doing, wherever she was going, she'd chosen it, and my job was to keep her secret, and keeping her secret was love, and love was enough.

Then the search parties started, and the shoes were found on the rocks, and Dell Hargrove closed the file in six days, and I understood — slowly, over weeks, in the way you understand a trick — that the shoes and the bicycle weren't evidence of a drowning. They were evidence of a *staging.* Somebody had arranged a death for her. Somebody with access to her things, knowledge of the tides, and a reason to make the sea the villain.

And I stood at her memorial service with the ash smell still faintly on my jacket, singing a hymn to a girl I knew was alive, surrounded by five hundred people mourning a lie I was helping to maintain, and I have never in my life felt closer to God or further from forgiveness.

* * *

That was Tuesday. On Thursday, Montrose came back.

Not to the shop — smarter than that. I was locking up at dusk when their rental car pulled alongside the dock road and the window came down, and Montrose said, "Mr. Marsh. One more question. Off the record, if you want."

I should have kept walking. Any sensible man would have kept walking. But thirty years is a long time to stand at a door you're not allowed to open, and when someone finally puts their hand on the handle beside yours, even a stranger's hand, you stop.

"What did Sable Wren ask you to do, the night before she disappeared?"

The fog was coming in off the harbor, softening everything, making the streetlights into halos, making the whole town look like a photograph of itself. I gripped the padlock in my fist until the teeth bit my palm.

"Off the record," I said.

"Off the record."

I looked at her for a long moment. Nineteen-year-old me looked too, from inside, furious and pleading.

"She asked me to burn a letter," I said. "And I did. And I've regretted it every day since — although not," and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized, went rough and low, "not for the reason you think."

Montrose went very still. "What reason, Mr. Marsh?"

Out on the water, the buoy bell rang — Grayhollow's oldest resident, saying the same thing it always says. I unlocked the shop door instead of answering, and held it open, and heard myself say the words I'd been rehearsing in the shower for thirty years without ever believing I'd say them:

"Come inside. And turn that recorder *on.* Because if I'm finally telling this story, I'm telling all of it, and I'm only going to have the nerve once."
