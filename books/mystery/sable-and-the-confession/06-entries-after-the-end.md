## 6. Entries After the End

There is a particular kind of insomnia that comes from owning a dead girl's diary. I don't recommend it. It's not the gothic dread you'd expect — no whispering in the margins, no ink that runs when you look away. It's worse than that. It's *banal*. Sable Wren wrote about weather. She wrote about the price of eggs. She wrote about a dog named Biscuit who was afraid of thunder. And every few pages, tucked between the ordinary and the ordinary, she wrote things that made no sense at all.

I'd been up three nights running with the diary spread across my kitchen table like an autopsy, and by the third night I'd stopped drinking coffee and started drinking it cold, straight from the pot, which is how you know a project has become a problem.

Let me back up.

The final entry — the one dated fourteen months after Sable Wren walked off the face of the earth — had seemed like a bombshell on its own. *Grocery lists.* Flour, lamp oil, thread, batteries. Written in the hand of a girl the state of Maine had declared drowned. But a single entry is an anomaly. Anomalies get explained. A misdated page, a sentimental forgery, a grieving friend keeping her memory alive on paper. People do strange tender things with the belongings of the dead; I knew that better than most, having built half a career on them.

But it wasn't one entry.

Once I stopped treating the last page as the ending and started reading backward through the whole book — really reading, the way you read a letter from someone you love instead of the way you skim a document — I found them. Entries after the end. Plural. Not many. Eleven of them, scattered across fourteen months like stones dropped in a creek, each one written in that same small slanted hand, each one doing its level best to look like nothing.

They didn't succeed. They succeeded only in looking like nothing *to someone who wasn't looking*. And I was looking. Looking was, at this point, more or less my entire personality.

* * *

The first thing you have to understand about the post-disappearance entries is that they're coded — but not well. Not spy-craft coded. Teenager-coded. The code of a girl who needed to write something down and needed it to be deniable if her mother found the book, or her employer, or God forbid the sheriff. It's the cipher of someone hiding from one specific person, not from the world.

She used names, but wrong ones. Or rather: right ones, bent slightly out of shape, the way you'd bend a name so it could pass as a nickname or a typo.

"The quarry" she called *the Q*, sometimes just *Q*, once memorably "the hole where the town keeps its secrets," which is less a code than a confession. Fine. Easy. Grayhollow has exactly one quarry, a flooded granite pit two miles inland, famous locally for two things: the water being cold enough to stop your heart in ninety seconds, and the summer some kids dove off the high ledge and found an old Model T resting on the bottom like a sleeping whale.

"The chapel ruins" she called *the C-house*, then *the chapel*, then, in one entry, simply *where we said the words*. That last one stopped me for a while. Said what words? Vows? Prayers? Some private ceremony? There are ruins of a nineteenth-century Methodist chapel up past the salt marsh, roofless walls and a bell tower stub, a place teenagers went to drink and make promises they wouldn't keep. Everyone in Grayhollow under sixty had kissed somebody there. It was almost too obvious to be a clue, which is exactly why it took me a day to take it seriously.

And then there was the third name. The one that turned my blood from room temperature to something considerably less comfortable.

*"The Judge's boat."*

Not *the judge's boat* lowercase, generic, any judge. Capital J. *the Judge's boat,* underlined once, in an entry dated March 1995 — eleven months after Sable Wren drowned.

Here's the thing about Grayhollow in 1994, according to every scrap of coverage I could pull: there was no judge. Not a resident one. The county seat handled the courts; Grayhollow got a visiting magistrate twice a month and a justice of the peace who mostly notarized fishing licenses. Nobody in that town was *the Judge.*

But everybody in Grayhollow knew a family named Vail. Raymond Vail owned the charter operation Sable worked summers, the one whose dock she was last seen walking toward. And Raymond's younger brother — this I learned from a 1998 newspaper clipping about a courthouse appointment, filed away in the same box as everything else — was Ambrose Vail. Newly elected district court judge. Ambitious. Charismatic. The kind of man whose photograph always caught him mid-laugh, as though life were a joke he'd generously agreed to be in on.

In March 1995, Ambrose Vail was the only judge within fifty miles of Grayhollow.

A dead girl was writing about his boat.

* * *

I want to be honest about the next part, because podcasters are professional liars by omission and I promised myself I wouldn't be one on the page. The next part took me four days, and for the first two of them I accomplished nothing except rearranging my kitchen and developing strong opinions about my neighbors' leaf blower.

Because here's the question that kept breaking over me: *if these entries were real, what were they for?*

Sable didn't come back. That was the settled fact of the case — no body, sure, but also no sightings, no withdrawals, no letters home, nothing. If she'd survived, if she'd been writing grocery lists somewhere in 1995, then either she came back to Grayhollow repeatedly, secretly — or someone brought the diary to wherever she was, and she filled pages and sent it back. Either possibility required accomplices. Quiet ones. Long-term ones. The kind of people who keep a secret for thirty years without spending it, even once, even for money, even for absolution.

Or — and this was the theory I hated, the one that sat in the corner of the room like a guest nobody invited — the entries were planted. Written later, backdated, by whoever wanted the world to believe Sable lived. A forgery inside a forgery. A ghost story with a ghost story inside it.

I needed Marta Okafor again. Fortunately, Marta had reached the stage of acquaintance where she answers my calls with "What now," which I choose to interpret as affection.

"It's the same question as before," I told her. "Same hand, same period. But now it's eleven entries across fourteen months, and I need to know if they could all have been written at different times. Ink aging. Is there variation? Would a forger slip?"

Marta sighed the sigh of a woman whose expertise keeps getting subpoenaed by true crime. "Dominic. Do you understand what you're asking? You're asking me to distinguish between ink applied in June 1994 and ink applied in August 1995, in the same book, stored who-knows-where, by a hand that doesn't vary because it's the same hand."

"Yes."

"And if I say the chemistry is consistent throughout?"

"Then either she wrote it all before she vanished, planning ahead like a novelist, or she really did keep writing after."

"And if I say there *is* measurable variation consistent with time gaps—"

"Then she really did keep writing after."

"So either answer gives you what you want." But she was already laughing, dry as old paper. "Send me high-resolution scans. All of them. And Dominic — if this is real, if this girl was alive and writing in 1995, then somebody in Grayhollow has known for thirty years. Think about what that means before you knock on any doors."

I thought about it. That was the problem. Thinking was all I did for two days.

* * *

While waiting on Marta, I did the thing I'm actually good at, which is maps. Give me a stack of documents and a wall and I will happily disappear into it like a spider into its own web.

I pulled the 1994 search records — the official ones, obtained through a public records request that took six weeks and cost me forty dollars in copying fees, plus the unofficial ones, which is to say the newspaper accounts, which are often more honest because reporters write fast and edit slow. And I laid out every location searched in the week after Sable Wren disappeared, and then I laid out the decoded place names from the post-disappearance entries, and I put the two maps side by side on my kitchen wall.

They matched.

Not loosely. Not approximately. *Exactly.*

The quarry: searched on day two of the official investigation. Divers in the water, dragged the full perimeter. Sheriff Dell Hargrove told the *Grayhollow Courier* at the time — I had the clipping — that the quarry was searched "as a matter of thoroughness." Standard procedure, he said. You search the deep water so you can tell the family you searched the deep water.

Except. Here's what I know about missing persons work, and I know a fair amount, having spent years marinating in other people's worst weeks: you don't lead with the quarry. The quarry is two miles inland. Her bicycle was found on the cliff road, her shoes on the rocks below. Every indicator pointed seaward. A competent investigator searches the water first and the quarry eventually, maybe, if the tide of tips pushes him there. You do not send divers into forty-degree flooded granite on *day two* unless something has told you to look there.

Something had told Dell Hargrove to look there.

The chapel ruins: searched on day three, with volunteers, a line of townspeople walking shoulder to shoulder through the marsh grass. Again — plausible. Volunteers always search the ruins; it's where teenagers go, and a missing seventeen-year-old is presumed to be a teenager first and a mystery second. But the search party that day included, per the Courier's photo caption, a broad-shouldered young man identified only as "a local business owner." I zoomed in until the pixels gave up. Broad shoulders, dark hair, a jaw you could open envelopes on. I couldn't prove it was Raymond Vail. I also couldn't unsee it.

And the boat. This is the one that kept me up on night four, staring at the ceiling while the radiator knocked like it wanted in.

On day five of the search, the harbor was combed. Every mooring checked, every hull inspected, the Coast Guard auxiliary running grid patterns out past the point. And among the vessels noted in the log — I found the auxiliary's report, or a summary of it, buried in a county archive box labeled MISCELLANEOUS MARINE 1990–96, which is where bureaucracies hide the things they can't throw away and won't face — among the vessels noted was a thirty-two-foot sport fisher registered to R. Vail Charters. Cleared. No sign. Moving on.

So the Judge's brother's boat was searched on day five. And eleven months later, a dead girl underlined the words *the Judge's boat* in a diary that officially did not exist.

You see the shape of it. I saw the shape of it, standing in my kitchen at two in the morning with cold coffee and a wall full of string. The map had turned itself inside out. Those weren't places the searchers happened to check. Those were places the searchers checked *because someone already knew their significance* — or, darker, places that mattered enough to Sable, afterward, that the people who helped her vanish had made sure the official record touched them first. Search the quarry early, log it thoroughly, and no one ever asks why the quarry mattered. Control the map and you control the territory. Control the search and you control the story.

Someone in Grayhollow hadn't been searching for a body in September 1994.

Someone had been tidying.

* * *

There was one more entry I haven't mentioned. The last of the eleven, dated October 1996, and it's different from the others. Shorter. Steadier. The handwriting of someone who has stopped being afraid, or has gotten very good at pretending.

It reads, in full:

*"Biscuit died in the spring. I didn't cry until Thursday. R. is in the water now and I am sorry, I think, the way you're sorry for weather. The book goes back in the ground. If you're reading this, little ghost, you were never supposed to find me. Be kind to Theo. He carried more than his share."*

*R. is in the water now.*

Raymond Vail drowned in October 1996. Boating accident, alone at night, no witnesses, ruled accidental by a coroner's office that spent, per the file, less than a page on it. I'd had the file for a week and barely glanced at it, because Raymond's death had always been a footnote to Sable's disappearance — the tragic coda, the town's bad luck compounding.

Now it was a headline.

She was sorry "the way you're sorry for weather." What does a person mean by that? Grief without ownership. Regret without guilt — or regret *with* guilt, worn so thin it reads flat. And "the book goes back in the ground" — buried? Hidden? Was the diary I bought at an estate sale the same book, dug up decades later by some unknowing heir clearing out a property? Whose property?

And *be kind to Theo.* Theo. One name, no surname, offered like a coin tossed to a stranger. In a town of four thousand people, that narrows things less than you'd hope — but combined with everything else, with the boy who worked the bait shop counter beside her the summer she disappeared, it narrowed plenty.

I sat with that entry for a long time. Longer than the others. Because the others were coordinates, and coordinates are exciting, and excitement is easy. This one was a voice. A living, breathing, grown-up voice, apologizing to a future reader for a death, asking kindness for a friend, signing off like a letter that knew it might never be delivered.

Dead girls don't apologize for weather.

* * *

Marta called on Friday. I let it ring twice, which is the maximum amount of cool available to a person in my position.

"I'll give you the short version," she said, "because the long version involves mass spectrometry and you'll pretend to follow along. The eleven entries show iron-gall ink degradation profiles consistent with application across an extended period. Not days. Not a single session. Months, minimum. Possibly the full span the dates suggest." A pause, the kind experts take before handing you the part that matters. "Could a skilled forger replicate that? With period-appropriate ink stock, controlled aging, and access to the original book? Yes. Anything's possible. But Dominic — the handwriting pressure patterns in the later entries differ subtly from the earlier ones. Heavier. Slower. An adult's hand wearing a teenager's style. Which is what you'd expect if—"

"If she grew up."

"If she grew up," Marta agreed. "Forgeries usually fail in the opposite direction. Forgers exaggerate the youth. They make the hand tremble. They give you the dead girl they imagine. These entries give you a woman who got older." Another pause. "Whoever wrote those pages, Dominic, was alive in 1995 and 1996. I'd stake my license on the direction of that finding, if not the certainty."

I thanked her. I hung up. I stood in my kitchen and looked at the wall of string and felt the specific vertigo of a story eating its own outline.

Here's what I had, as of that Friday night:

Sable Wren, declared drowned in September 1994, was almost certainly alive through at least October 1996. She moved through Grayhollow during that window — or someone moved her words through it — visiting the quarry, the chapel ruins, the harbor. She wrote about the Judge's boat eleven months into her afterlife. She wrote, with terrible calm, about Raymond Vail drowning two years after her own death. And every single place her pen touched had been searched, logged, and closed by the town of Grayhollow before her pen ever got there.

Either the town had been searching for a living girl and helping her stay lost —

— or the town had been searching for a dead one, and helping bury the truth of how she got that way.

Both possibilities required the same thing: people who knew. Plural. Committed. Patient. The kind of silence that gets passed down like property.

And hovering over all of it, capital-J, underlined once in a dead girl's hand: *the Judge.* A man who held a courtroom's worth of power over a small coastal town, whose brother employed the missing girl, whose brother's boat was searched and cleared, whose brother ended up in the water while Sable Wren was still writing grocery lists somewhere beyond the edge of her own obituary.

I booked a flight to Maine that night. Then, because I'm a professional, I booked a rental car. Then, because I'd listened to too many episodes of my own show, I emailed myself copies of everything — scans, maps, the coroner's file, the marine log — to three separate addresses, with instructions to a lawyer friend about what to do if I stopped answering my phone. It felt dramatic. It also felt, in the pit of my stomach where the cold coffee lived, proportionate.

Grayhollow had questions to answer, and I intended to start at the top of the food chain. Retired sheriffs forget their lines. Ferry captains and bait shop owners can be rattled. But judges — judges are trained to sit still while people tell them terrible things, and to decide, afterward, what happens next.

Ambrose Vail had been on the bench for twenty-five years. I wanted to watch his hands while I asked him about his brother's boat.

I had no idea, yet, how carefully he'd already rehearsed his answers — or that he'd ask me a question of his own that would keep me awake in a different way entirely.

But that's the next chapter.
