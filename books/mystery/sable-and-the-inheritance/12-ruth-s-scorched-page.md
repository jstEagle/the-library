## 12. Ruth's Scorched Page

Ruth Vane had kept the page for thirty years in the same place she kept everything that frightened her: inside the false bottom of her sewing tin, beneath a layer of buttons that had outlived three coats and two husbands' worth of mending. She had told herself, all this time, that she was preserving evidence. That one day it would matter. That Judge Ellery Sable — *Mr. Ellery*, as she had never once called him aloud, though she thought of him that way in the privacy of her own skull — would come back from the dead and ask for it, and she would hand it over with both hands and be done with the weight of it.

He had not come back. And now he was six weeks in his grave, and the town was tearing itself apart over his will like dogs over a bone, and Ruth woke at four every morning with the sewing tin on her mind the way other people woke with chest pains.

She dressed in the dark, as she always did. Wool skirt, high collar, hair pinned so tight it gave her a faint permanent headache she had long since stopped noticing. In the kitchen she put the kettle on without lighting the burner — an old habit of mornings when she only needed the ritual of it, not the tea — and stood at the window watching the fog come off the Bend in slow gray ropes.

Cyrus Coldharbour's house sat at the edge of her view, down where Cemetery Hill sloped toward the water. A light burned in an upstairs window. It always did, at this hour. The boy — no, not a boy anymore, a man of forty-one who still moved through the world like someone apologizing for taking up space — kept coroner's hours the way monks kept prayers.

*My boy.*

The judge had said it once, only once, in Ruth's hearing, thirty years ago, standing soaked to the skin in his study with ash on his cuffs. She had spent three decades telling herself she'd misheard. That grief or brandy or the lateness of the hour had bent the words into something they weren't.

But she had kept the page anyway. Because you do not keep things for thirty years unless some part of you knows exactly what they are.

---

She let herself into Cyrus's house at half past seven, using the key under the loose brick by the kitchen door — a key he believed she didn't know about, because Cyrus needed to believe people were more private than they were, the way some people need to believe the dead are at peace. She found him at the table with tea going cold beside him and the ledger open under his hands, and she saw at once that he hadn't slept. There was a particular grayness to sleeplessness, a translucence, and Cyrus wore it like his father had worn it, God rest the man.

"You've been at the rubbings," she said.

He looked up. He had his mother's eyes, wide-set and dark, and they flinched before they settled. "Ruth. I didn't hear—"

"No. You wouldn't have." She hung her coat on the hook he'd never used, because hanging a coat implied staying, and staying implied being wanted, and Cyrus Coldharbour had built a whole life on the architecture of neither. "I brought bread."

"I'm not hungry."

"I know." She set the loaf down anyway. Then she took off her gloves, finger by finger, which was what she did instead of wringing her hands, and said: "Sit down properly, Cyrus. What I've come to say won't keep, and I'm too old to say it twice."

He sat. She almost smiled at that — the obedience of him, the deep-buried training of a child raised in a house where the judge's word settled matters like weather. Almost. Her hands weren't steady enough for smiling.

"Thirty years ago," she began, "on the night of the fourteenth of October, Mr. Ellery came home past midnight soaked through to his skin. Not river water. Pond water. I knew the difference because I'd grown up on the millpond and the pond leaves a green smell on wool that the river doesn't. He went straight to the study and he burned papers in the fireplace, and he was crying, Cyrus, and I want you to understand that I had worked for that man eleven years by then and I had never seen him cry, not at his wife's funeral, not when the child was lost, not ever. And he kept saying a name over and over, but it wasn't a name, it was just — *my boy*. My boy saw. My boy can't see. God forgive me, my boy."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, a crow called twice from the elms along the cemetery fence, and stopped, as if listening too.

"He sent me to bed," Ruth said. "And in the morning the fireplace was cold and swept, and he was himself again, or wearing himself, which is nearly the same thing in a man like that. But he'd missed one page. It had gone under the fender, curled and scorched, and I found it when I cleaned, and I read it, and then—" Her voice caught on something old and sharp. "Then I hid it. Thirty years, Cyrus. I have committed a small cowardice every single day of my life since, and I am here this morning to stop."

She reached into her handbag and laid the fragment on the table between them.

It was smaller than she remembered — memory inflates relics, she supposed, the way it inflates grief. Half a page, brown as autumn, its edges charred to lace. The judge's handwriting marched across it in that upright, judicial slant she could have picked out of a crowd of a thousand hands:

*...was no accident. I saw the body before Pryce prepared it and there is a mark I cannot name in any verdict I am willing to sign. If the truth comes out it will destroy — [the rest burned away] — and I will not allow it. Let the record say drowning by misadventure. Let it say nothing else. My boy must never —*

There the paper ended, torn or burned, the sentence amputated mid-word.

Cyrus did not touch it. His hands stayed flat on the table on either side of it, framing it, the way he framed bodies on his slab — respectfully, at a small distance, as if the thing might object to being handled.

"Read it," Ruth said.

"I've read it."

"Read it again. Out loud. Some things need air."

So he read it, in a voice that started level and did not stay that way, and Ruth watched the words go into him like cold water into cloth, spreading, darkening, until he reached *my boy* and stopped, and the silence after it was the loudest thing in the room.

"The millpond," he said finally. "1994. The drowning he ruled accidental."

"You know about that."

"I know the ledger points there. I know a name was chiseled off the family monument. I don't yet know whose." He looked up at her, and there was something terrible in his face — not fear, exactly, but the moment before fear, the held breath of it. "Ruth. When the judge said *my boy* —"

"Don't," she said.

"— how old was I in 1994?"

She had known this question was coming from the moment she'd taken the page out of the tin. She had rehearsed answers on the walk over, honest ones, evasive ones, kind ones. In the end she gave him the only one she had.

"You were eleven. And you spent that October at the Sable house, because your mother was ill and I minded you, and Mr. Ellery took you walking most evenings, and one evening you came back without him and you were white as milk and you wouldn't say why, and the next day the whole town was talking about a body pulled from the millpond." She drew breath. "And the judge locked his study door that night and burned papers until dawn."

Cyrus stood up. It seemed involuntary, the way a man stands when the floor tilts. He walked to the window with his back to her, and Ruth saw that his shoulders had gone rigid in a way she recognized from the slab — the posture he took over the young ones, the drowned ones, the ones that cost him something.

"He protected me," Cyrus said. It wasn't a question.

"He lied for you. There's a difference, and I expect you'll be finding out exactly what it is."

"For thirty years. Every verdict he signed after that, every — Ruth, he built his whole reputation on speaking for the dead, and he put a lie in the ground and left it there, and he did it because an eleven-year-old boy saw something he shouldn't have." Cyrus turned. His eyes were wet and furious and grateful all at once, three currents fighting in one channel. "Do you understand what that means? Everything I am — this house, this work, the way he trained me, the way he *made* me — he did all of it to keep me close. To keep me safe. To keep me quiet. I can't tell anymore which parts of my life were love and which parts were containment."

"And that," Ruth said quietly, "is why I waited thirty years to show you that page. Because I knew you'd ask yourself that question, and I knew there'd be no clean answer to it."

She rose and gathered her gloves, needing her hands busy. "But hear me now, Cyrus Coldharbour, because this is the part I came to say and I'll not soften it. That page names a witness. Whoever killed that man in 1994 has lived in this town thirty years believing the witness was buried under the lie. The judge is dead. The archivist's files are gone — yes, I hear things, I'm a housekeeper, we're the telegraph system of this county. Which means somebody is cleaning house. And if they learn the witness was never the judge at all — if they learn it was a child, and the child grew up, and the child is now the coroner holding a decoded ledger —"

She stopped. She made herself finish it.

"— then you are not the man investigating the murders, Cyrus. You are the next one."

He was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the professional calm she knew was armor, and she hated it and understood it and loved him for it all at once.

"Then I need to move faster than they do." He slid the scorched page toward himself at last, touching it now, claiming it. "Ruth — the payee. The survivor. You helped hide the child, didn't you? I can see it in your face. Don't answer yet. Just — think about whether you're ready, because I'll need it. All of it. Before trial week."

Ruth nodded, and put on her coat, and let herself out through the kitchen door into the raw morning. Behind her, through the window, she saw Cyrus stand alone at the table with the burned fragment in both hands, head bowed over it like a man praying over a grave —

and she saw, beyond him, at the edge of the yard where the fog still clung, a figure standing motionless among the elms, watching the lit window, watching the house, watching *him* —

and when she blinked, the figure was gone.
