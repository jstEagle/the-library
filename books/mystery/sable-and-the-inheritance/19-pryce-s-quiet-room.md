## 19. Pryce's Quiet Room

The funeral home of Wendell Pryce sat at the end of Chapel Street like a well-mannered lie — white clapboard, black shutters, a sign with gold letters so tasteful they seemed almost apologetic. Cyrus had passed it ten thousand times in his life and had been inside it perhaps five. He knew the building the way he knew all the buildings where the dead were kept: by their thresholds, by the particular hush that lived just past the door.

Tonight he did not knock.

He had told no one where he was going. Not Mara Quist, who would have insisted on coming and whose badge on his shoulder would have turned this into an interrogation rather than what it needed to be — a conversation between two men who spent their lives handling other people's endings. The letter from Pryce had come that morning, tucked inside the funeral home's own stationery, heavy cream stock with a blind-embossed wreath at the top. *Come tonight after eight,* it said. *Come alone. I will tell you about your coat.* No signature. None was needed. Only one man in Marrow's Bend still used paper like that, and only one man had dressed both bodies — the stranger from the Bend, and, thirty years ago, the boy pulled blue and silent from the millpond.

Cyrus climbed the three porch steps. The boards did not creak; Wendell Pryce maintained his silence even in the wood.

The door opened before he reached it.

"Coldharbour." Wendell stood in the frame, small and neat in a gray cardigan, his white hair combed with the same precision he gave to the hair of the dead. His eyes were red-rimmed, though whether from grief or sleeplessness or something older, Cyrus could not say. "You came alone. Good. I wasn't sure you would."

"You said you'd tell me about my coat."

"I said I'd tell you about your coat." Wendell stepped back to let him in. "There's a difference between saying a thing and meaning to keep the promise. Most men break promises in the direction of cowardice. I'm trying not to."

* * *

The room Wendell led him to was behind the chapel, through a door marked PRIVATE in brass. It was a small sitting room, furnished decades ago and never updated: a wingback chair worn smooth at the arms, a lamp with a green glass shade, shelves of funeral registers bound in black leather going back further than Cyrus's memory. On the wall hung photographs of confirmation classes and church picnics, faces gone amber with age. A kettle sat on a side table beside two cups already poured.

"Chamomile," Wendell said. "I don't keep anything stronger in the house anymore. Learned that lesson late."

Cyrus did not sit. He stood with his hat in his hands — he realized he was holding his hat, wringing its brim like a nervous boy, and made himself stop. "Wendell. The man they pulled out of the Bend. He was wearing my clothes. My coat, my shirt, my boots. Clothes that went missing from my closet six weeks before he surfaced. I have spent every day since asking myself what kind of message that is, and every answer I've found has been worse than the last."

"Yes." Wendell lowered himself into the wingback with the care of a man whose joints had begun keeping their own ledger. "Sit down, Cyrus. Please. This is a long story and you'll want to be seated for the middle of it."

Cyrus sat. The tea was too sweet. He drank it anyway.

"The dead man's name was Emil Roos," Wendell said. "He came to town in the spring. Told people at the Gable Inn he was a surveyor. He was not a surveyor. He was a collector of debts — the kind of man who finds leverage the way water finds cracks. Aldous Sable hired him."

"Aldous." The name came out flat.

"Aldous hired him to find dirt on Ruth Vane. Anything that would disqualify her as heir under the judge's language — 'the one who kept my ledger.' Aldous believed, correctly, that if the ledger surfaced and pointed anywhere, it might point at Ruth. He wanted insurance. Something to bury her with, figuratively or otherwise." Wendell's hands were folded in his lap, knuckles pale. "Emil Roos was very good at his work. Too good. Within a month he'd found something Aldous hadn't bargained for."

"The millpond."

"The millpond." Wendell closed his eyes briefly. "Roos got into the county archive before anyone else thought to look. He found the 1994 file's ghost — the index card that survives when the file itself is pulled. He traced the payments. And then he did what collectors do. He stopped working for Aldous and started working for himself. He wanted the whole inheritance, Cyrus. Not a finder's fee. All of it. He told Aldous he'd go to probate court himself, name the survivor, expose everything — the fraud, the payments, Aldous's hiring of him — unless Aldous signed it over."

"And Aldous killed him."

"Aldous didn't have the stomach for it, if that's any comfort. It won't be." Wendell looked at the green lamp as if the light might soften what came next. "Aldous came to me. Three weeks ago. He was half-mad with fear — Roos had threatened to send everything to the *Marrow's Bend Courier*, to the court, to the sheriff. Aldous said, *Wendell, you've known me since I was in short pants, you have to help me make him go away.* And I —" The old man's voice caught, and he pressed his lips together until it passed. "I told him there was a way. I shouldn't have. God forgive me, I told him there was a way, because I have spent forty years making problems disappear quietly and I had forgotten that some problems are people."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the house a clock counted seconds with the patience of the embalmed.

"How did he die?" Cyrus asked. His voice sounded far away to himself, clinical, the coroner's voice he used over tables. "The official finding was drowning. Alcohol involvement suspected. But I examined that body, Wendell. I held his heart in my hand. There was water in his lungs, but the petechiae were wrong, the timing was wrong. He was dead or nearly dead before he went into the river. I couldn't prove it. I've been living with that inability for weeks."

"Heart failure," Wendell said simply. "Digitalis. From the foxglove that grows along the millpond path — poetic, isn't it, how things circle. Aldous put it in a bottle of bourbon and left it where Roos was drinking. Then he called me." Wendell's hands unfolded, refolded. "He said, *make it look like the river took him.* And I did what I do. I prepared the body. I cleaned it. I dressed it."

"In my clothes."

Wendell met his eyes then, and Cyrus saw that the old man had been waiting for this question the way a condemned man waits for the reading of the sentence — dreading it, needing it, unable to begin without it.

"In your clothes," Wendell said. "Yes. That part was mine alone. Aldous never asked for it. He wanted the body found, found wet, found far from anywhere he'd been seen. That was all. But I knew something Aldous didn't know. I knew you'd taken the rubbings. I knew you'd been to the archive — Mara Quist isn't the only one who notices things in this town, Cyrus, I've buried this town for fifty years and I notice everything. And I understood, before anyone else did, where the ledger was pointing. Not at Ruth. Not at Aldous. At the survivor. And at the man who protected the survivor." He paused. "At Ellery Sable. And through Ellery Sable, at you."

Cyrus set down the cup. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "So you dressed a drowned man in my coat."

"To warn you off." Wendell's voice dropped to nearly nothing. "It was the only language I could think of that you would understand. You are not a man who frightens, Cyrus Coldharbour. I've watched you handle the worst nights this town produces without turning a hair. But I thought — I gambled — that seeing yourself on a dead man's back would reach someplace argument couldn't. That you'd understand someone was telling you: *we know who you are, we can dress death in your skin, stop digging.*" He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "It was even true, in a way. It was me telling you. I just didn't have the courage to sign it."

* * *

For a long moment neither man spoke. The lamp hummed. Outside, rain had begun against the windows, soft and indifferent, the same rain that fell on the cemetery, on Judge Sable's stone, on the millpond where a boy had died and a verdict had been born to cover it.

"Why tell me now?" Cyrus asked finally. "Why not stay silent? Aldous is exposed, the trial starts Monday, and you've managed forty years without confessing anything. One more week and maybe none of this touches you."

"Because you'll find it anyway." Wendell said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "That's the thing about you, Coldharbour. Everyone in this town thinks you're invisible — the little coroner who walks among the graves talking to the dead. But you see everything. You saw that Roos's heart didn't fail honestly. You'll see the rest. And I am seventy-eight years old, and I have lied to a courtroom twice, and lied to a widow once, and lied to myself every single day since 1994, and I find that I cannot carry one more secret across the threshold of a grave. Do you know what I do all day? I make the dead presentable. I close their eyes, I fold their hands, I choose words that comfort the living. And every body on my table for thirty years has been a small accusation. Because I know which ones died honest and which ones didn't, and I say the same kind words over both."

He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the shelf of black registers. He ran his finger along the spines and stopped at one near the bottom, older than the others, its leather scuffed to suede at the corners.

"August 1994," he said. "I prepared that boy. Fourteen years old. Pulled from the millpond with a gash on his temple that no rock made. I shaved him and closed his eyes and I listened to Ellery Sable stand in my preparation room and tell me the ruling would be accidental drowning, and I said nothing. I sewed the town's silence shut with my own needle." He drew the register down and laid it on the table between them, open to the page. The entry was in his younger hand, precise and beautiful: a name, a date, *cause of death: accidental drowning.* Beneath it, added years later in the same ink but a shakier hand, a single line:

*Forgive me. It was not the river.*

"Wendell." Cyrus leaned forward. "The survivor. The child who lived. The payments. Do you know who —"

"I know what Ellery told me, which may be all there is." Wendell sat again, suddenly looking every year of his age, a man deflating like a lung giving up its last breath. "The boy in the pond had a sister. Eleven years old. She was there that night — she saw it happen, saw who did it, and she ran, and she fell, and she nearly joined her brother in the water. Ellery pulled her out. Literally pulled her out of the millpond, soaked to the bone, and carried her home past midnight, and Ruth Vane helped him hide her, and the next month the child was gone from Marrow's Bend as if she'd never been born. Sent away. New name. New life. And every year after, on the first of August, a payment went out through channels Ellery built like a watchmaker. To keep her safe. To keep her quiet. To keep her *alive*, Cyrus — because whoever struck that boy in 1994 would have struck the witness too, if the witness had stayed."

"Who struck him?" Cyrus asked, though his blood had already begun to whisper the answer, had been whispering it for days, in the shape of a charming man's smile slipping just enough to show teeth.

Wendell shook his head slowly. "Ellery never said the name aloud to me. But he said something else, the night he came here soaked and burning papers. He said, *Wendell, I have spent my whole life weighing men's souls on a scale, and tonight I learned that the scale was rigged from the start — that the law is a lamp we hold over everyone except our own blood.*" The old mortician's eyes filled, and he let them, too tired for dignity. "His own blood, Cyrus. Think about who that is. Think about who inherits if the ledger names no one. Think about who hired a debt collector to dig up the past and then killed the man when the past dug back."

Cyrus thought of Aldous in his office, the offer of money, the charm thinning over something hungry. He thought of the chalk outline drawn on his morgue floor around empty space. He thought of the archivist, Mr. Fenwick, lying in Cyrus's own cold room with a heart that had stopped too neatly, too conveniently, taught to stop by someone who understood staging — and Wendell Pryce was the only other man in Marrow's Bend who understood the grammar of staged death as fluently as Cyrus did.

"Fenwick," Cyrus said quietly. "Was that Aldous too? Or was that you?"

Wendell flinched as if struck. "No. God, no. I've done enough." He gripped the armrests. "But I know it wasn't Aldous — Aldous hasn't the nerve, he hires things done. Someone else is cleaning up around the edges of this, someone older and more patient, and that frightens me more than Aldous ever did. That's the other reason I sent for you tonight. Cyrus — whatever you think you know about who killed Emil Roos, hold it loosely. There are hands in this that neither of us has counted yet."

* * *

Cyrus walked home through the rain without hurrying. Chapel Street emptied into River Road, and River Road ran dark and silver under the lamplight, and the river itself moved below the bank with the sound of pages turning.

Pity was a strange thing to feel for a man who had dressed a corpse in your own clothes, but Cyrus felt it, walking there in the wet dark. He pitied Wendell Pryce — the broken accomplice, the needle and thread of the town's long silence, the old man who had finally chosen confession over comfort and would spend what remained of his life paying for it. He pitied Ellery Sable, dead and judged, whose love had curdled into fraud, whose protection had become a prison built of payments. He even pitied Aldous, a little, the way one pities a man drowning within sight of shore because he cannot stop grasping at driftwood that pulls him under.

But pity was not mercy, and Monday was coming.

On the stand, under oath, he would lay out the decoded ledger, the rubbings, the phantom entries mapped onto hymn numbers, the annual payments to a girl who had grown into a woman somewhere with a new name. He would speak the fraud aloud. He would name the survivor. And the truth would cut in every direction at once — at Aldous, at the judge, at Ruth Vane who had helped hide a child, at Wendell who had sewn the silence, and at Cyrus himself, who had loved the judge like a father and had chosen, night after night, decade after decade, not to ask the questions that now stood in the courthouse waiting for him like witnesses of their own.

At his door, he paused. Down the street, a figure stood beneath the dead elm at the corner — still, unhurried, unmistakably watching. Rain fell between them. When Cyrus took one step forward, the figure turned and walked away with the easy, unerring stride of someone who knew these streets blindfolded.

Someone older. Someone trusted. Someone whose hands he had not yet counted.

Cyrus stood a long time in the rain, and then he went inside, and took out the ledger, and began again at the first page — because there was one payment, the earliest one, dated August 1995, that he had never been able to match to any hymn number at all.

Someone had written it in a different hand.
