## 16. The Third Letter

The third letter did not come by post.

That was the first thing Odile noticed about it, standing in the dispatch office at six in the morning with the kettle just off the boil and the radio muttering its overnight summary to no one. The first letter had come through the mail slot like any honest piece of correspondence. The second had been intercepted—addressed to Hélène, routed through the taxi company's PO box because someone in this town still believed in old habits. But the third letter was simply *there*, wedged under the dispatch console's clipboard, between the shift-change log and a takeout menu for a Chinese restaurant that had closed before Odile learned to drive.

No stamp. No postmark. No envelope.

Just three pages of blue-ruled paper, folded twice, and on the topmost page, in handwriting that made Odile's stomach drop through the floor and into the harbor below:

*Sable.*

Not signed. Written. The word itself, alone on the first line, like a hand reaching across twenty years of dark water.

Odile did not touch it at first. They stood with their hand hovering, the way you hover over a stove burner you're not sure is hot, and they listened to the building—the tick of the wall clock, the hum of the radio's standby light, old Perrin coughing somewhere in the records room—and they thought: *someone was inside here.* Someone had walked past Gaspard's empty chair, past the corkboard with its faded photo of the company picnic from 2011, past the mug Odile kept pens in (the one shaped like a lighthouse, chipped at the top, a gift from Hélène when they got the dispatcher job), and left this.

Or. Or or or.

Or Marguerite Vasseur had walked into the one place in Saint-Aubin-sur-Mer where every secret in town eventually washed ashore, and set down her confession like an offering, and walked back out before the tide of daylight could catch her.

Odile picked up the pages.

* * *

The handwriting changed as it went. That was the second thing Odile noticed, later, after they'd read it four times and made tea they never drank and sat down at the console with the radio turned low so the static wouldn't interrupt. The first page was written in a careful, schoolroom hand—the kind of penmanship taught by nuns, all upright strokes and disciplined loops. By the second page the letters leaned. By the third they were running downhill, urgent, the ink blotting where the pen had pressed too long in one spot, the way a hand does when it stops caring how it looks and only cares about getting out what's inside.

Odile read it again, aloud, to the empty office, because some things need a voice to be real.

*"Sable.*

*I don't know if you remember me. I think you do. I think there are nights you can't help it, the way I have nights I can't help it, and if we are both lying awake in different beds in the same town remembering the same summer, then maybe neither of us is as alone as we've been pretending.*

*My name is Marguerite Vasseur. I have not used it since August 2004. Before that I used it every day for nineteen years, and most of those days I wished I hadn't, because names are handles and handles let people pick you up."*

Odile's thumb found the edge of the page and pressed until the paper dented.

*"You were eight the summer your parents disappeared. I was eleven. You will not remember how we met, so I will tell you: you were sitting on the sea wall by the bait shop, crying, because a boy had told you your father's boat wasn't coming back, and I sat down next to you and said nothing, because I didn't know what to say either, and after a while you stopped crying and asked me if I knew how to read tides. You'd heard your father say it. Read the tides. Like they were a book. I said yes, which was a lie, and we spent that whole summer learning to lie to each other beautifully, and it is the only summer of my life I would live again exactly as it was.*

*I called you Sable because you were always finding sand in places sand shouldn't be. Your shoes. Your hair. Once, impossibly, inside a closed tin of biscuits. You laughed so hard you fell off the breakwater, and I had to fish you out, and you came up spitting salt and grinning, and I thought: this child. This child is going to be all right no matter what happens. I clung to that thought for a long time. It was wrong, but it was load-bearing, and I want you to know I'm sorry for what it held up."*

The clock ticked. Somewhere outside, the first gulls of the morning started their arguments over the harbor wall. Odile became aware, distantly, that their face was wet, and did nothing about it, because the office was empty and the sea had seen them cry before, in a manner of speaking, and had never once mentioned it.

*"Here is what happened, and I will tell it straight because you have earned straight things and been given crooked ones all your life.*

*On the night your parents disappeared, I was on the breakwater. I went there most nights that summer; it was my place before it was ours. I saw a car come down the coast road with its lights off, which is itself a confession—no honest driver kills their lights on that road unless the road is doing something they don't want witnessed. I saw your parents' car already parked near the old slipway. I saw two people walk down toward the water. And I saw the man who got out of the dark car, and Sable, I knew him. Everyone in Saint-Aubin knew him. He was Hélène Roche's brother, Aurélien, and he was the sort of man who smiled at children and was kind to dogs and kept his cruelty the way other men keep a hunting rifle: oiled, unloaded-looking, and always within reach.*

*I watched him kill them.*

*I won't write how. Not here. Not on paper that might be read by anyone but you. But I watched, and I ran, and I made noise doing it, and he heard me. And for nine days afterward I lived in the space between him finding me and him not finding me, and on the ninth day I understood that he would find me, eventually, because I was eleven and he was patient, and so I did the only thing an eleven-year-old can do against a patient man.*

*I drowned.*

*There is a spring tide in late August that runs hard through the channel past the point—it was in the tables, Sable, you'd have found it yourself by now, I know how you work, you get it from your father—and I let it take me. Or appear to. There is a cove past the point, the one the fishermen call the Pocket, where anything the tide takes comes ashore, and I swam it half-dead with cold and crawled out on the far side of being a girl named Marguerite Vasseur. Dr. Roche signed my death certificate. I have wondered for twenty years whether she believed it. I think now she must have, or she chose to, and both of those are their own kinds of drowning.*

*Aurélien searched for me. I know because I watched him do it, from windows, from behind hedges, from the particular invisibility of a child everyone believes is dead. He searched for weeks and then he stopped, and I told myself he'd stopped believing, but the truth is worse: he stopped needing to believe. A dead girl is a closed door. He walked away from it whistling.*

*And then, Sable, then came the part I cannot forgive myself for, and I won't ask you to, and I'm going to write it anyway because a confession with the worst part left out is just a longer lie.*

*They gave you to Hélène Roche.*

*I learned it the way you learn things in a small town—through walls, through overheard kitchen talk, through the parish grapevine that moves faster than any radio dispatch ever has. The orphaned child, given to the sister of the man who orphaned her. And I could have said something. I was twelve by then, hiding in the convent at Les Mouettes under the name Sister Bernadette gave me, and I could have written a letter, made a call, walked into the gendarmerie soaked and shivering and told them everything. I told myself I couldn't, that no one would believe a dead girl, that Aurélien would only come looking again, that saying nothing was how I kept us both safe.*

*Some of that was even true. But underneath the true parts was a simpler one, and I am done dressing it up: I was afraid, and fear is a country, and I emigrated, and I sent you no letters from abroad for twenty years while the sister of your parents' killer raised you at her own table with her own hands and, God help her, I believe she loved you truly, which is the cruelest thing Aurélien ever built—that his crime should wear love as its disguise, so that exposing it would feel like tearing something living out of your own chest.*

*You grew up in that house. You learned to drive on roads he paved with silence. And when Émile Tanguy started asking questions last spring—because Émile remembered more about that night than anyone guessed, and he'd been selling pieces of his memory to Aurélien for years, and last spring the price went up and Aurélien decided memory was cheaper to end than to buy—when Émile died on the coast road, I finally understood that the door I'd let close behind me had been closing on you too, all this time, and that staying hidden hadn't kept you safe. It had just kept the danger comfortable.*

*So. Here is what I propose, and it is not a proposal so much as a surrender.*

*Spring tide comes in nine days. The same tide that carried me to the Pocket will run high and full, and I will be at the old breakwater past the point—you'll know the spot; it's where the photograph was taken, the one I sent half of, though I suspect that half never reached you intact—at mid-tide, when the water covers the lower rocks and there is nowhere to stand except beside another person. I will be there at mid-tide, and I will wait one full hour, and I will tell you everything I have, everything I saw, everything Émile knew, everything Hélène knows whether she admits it or not. Bring whoever you trust. Trust almost no one. Aurélien reads the mail routes the way I read tides—he always has; it's how he's stayed uncaught for twenty years—and so I brought this myself, at dawn, past your sleeping night porter, because paper can be intercepted but a girl who's been dead for twenty years leaves fewer tracks.*

*If you don't come, I'll understand. I have no right to expect anything from you, least of all a meeting. But I hope you come. I hope it selfishly, because I have been alone with this for twenty years and the weight has bent me into someone I don't recognize, and I think—I think if I put it down in front of you, even once, I might stand up straight again before I die.*

*You found sand in impossible places, Sable. Find me. I'll be the impossible place.*

*M."*

* * *

Odile sat for a long time after finishing it the fifth time.

The kettle had gone cold hours ago, or minutes—time had gone strange, the way it does in dispatch when a call comes in that changes the shape of the whole night. Outside, the morning had committed fully to being morning: fishing boats knocking against their moorings, the bakery van making its rounds, the ordinary machinery of a town that had been keeping a secret the way the harbor keeps water, without effort, without even noticing.

Grief arrived first, quietly, the way it does when it's been waiting a long time and doesn't need to make an entrance. Grief for the eight-year-old on the sea wall. Grief for the eleven-year-old on the breakwater, watching, running, choosing the sea over the man. Grief for Hélène, whose love Odile now understood had been real and also a room built over a grave. Grief, strangely, for Émile Tanguy, who had tried to turn his knowledge into a pension and died of arithmetic.

And underneath the grief, arriving together the way the letter promised, relief—vast and shameful and undeniable. Because for two weeks Odile had been investigating alone in the dark, pulling threads with no idea whether the garment was a sweater or a net, and now, suddenly, there was a shape. There was a name. There was a date, nine days out, and a place, and a person who had been dead for twenty years and was willing to stop being dead in front of witnesses.

*Bring whoever you trust. Trust almost no one.*

Odile thought of Gaspard, who had confessed in fragments and wept into his coffee. Thought of Hélène, who had asked, over roast chicken, what Odile owed the people who raised them. Thought of Aurélien Roche, whom Odile had met perhaps six times in twenty years—a big warm man who fixed things, who showed up at Christmas with tools and good wine, who had once spent an entire afternoon teaching ten-year-old Odile to skip stones, laughing, patient, *oiled, unloaded-looking, and always within reach.*

"I skipped stones with him," Odile said aloud, to the lighthouse mug, to nobody. "He taught me the flat ones."

The radio crackled. Morning traffic, nothing. A trawler reporting engine trouble off the point—off *the point*, and Odile's head came up like a dog's.

Nine days. Spring tide. Mid-tide, at the old breakwater past the point.

Odile pulled the tide tables across the console, found the date, and circled it twice in red pencil, pressing hard enough to tear the page slightly. Then they took the third letter, all three pages, and did the thing they'd been avoiding since dawn: they locked it in the dispatch safe, behind the cash box and the spare keys, because Marguerite had been right about one thing above all others.

Paper could be intercepted.

And somewhere in Saint-Aubin-sur-Mer, a man who read mail routes the way other men read weather reports had been reading along this whole time—which meant that when spring tide came, Odile would not be walking to that breakwater alone.

The question was who else would be there first.
