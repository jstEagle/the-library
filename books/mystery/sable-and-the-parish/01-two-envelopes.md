## 1. Two Envelopes

The morning Xanthe Dunmore received the two letters began, as most of his mornings did, with the small ceremonies of a man who had arranged his life to avoid surprise.

The kettle first. He filled it from the tap in the back room of the coroner's office—a room that had once been a vestry, judging by the ghost of a bracket on the wall where a cross had hung, and which now smelled of dust, old paper, and the bergamot of the cheap tea he had drunk every day for eleven years. The office stood at the edge of Sable Fen's single square, wedged between the ironmonger and a shop that sold wool and, inexplicably, fishing bait. From its one window he could see the churchyard wall, the yew tree that leaned over it like a mourner who had been standing too long, and beyond that the flat grey shimmer of the marsh, which in October seemed to breathe.

He was forty-six years old and he had been in Sable Fen for eleven years, and in that time he had perfected the art of being unremarkable. It was a discipline, like any other. He rose at six. He drank his tea while the light came up slow and watery over the fens. He opened the post at half past eight, because the post came at half past eight, and in eleven years the post had never once contained anything that mattered.

There were death certificates to countersign, an inquest file on a farmer named Hollis who had died of a heart in his own beet field, and a letter from the parish council about the state of the mortuary's guttering. Xanthe worked the way he did everything—carefully, quietly, without lifting his head. He had a good hand for forms. He had a better hand for silence. The parish had accepted him the way a body accepts a splinter that has gone in deep: not warmly, but without rejecting him, and after enough years the tissue simply grew around the fact of him.

He was halfway through Hollis's file when he heard the van.

It came up the lane at ten past nine, which was wrong—everything about it was wrong—and Xanthe set down his pen and listened the way he had once listened to rooms, back when listening had been his trade. Doors. A footstep on the gravel. Then the flap of the letterbox, and the particular soft slap of an envelope landing on the mat, and a second one, a heartbeat later, and then nothing.

The post had already come. He had watched the postman, a round, cheerful man named Tredgett, pedal away an hour before. Xanthe sat with his pen suspended over the form and looked at the two envelopes on the mat for a long moment before he got up to retrieve them.

The first was ordinary. Cream, typed address, franked in the city—Norwich, by the postmark. The second was not ordinary. It was smaller, grey, unfranked, and it had been left by hand. There was no stamp because no one had carried it through any postal system. Someone had walked it up the lane, past the ironmonger, past the window where Xanthe sat at his desk, and had pushed it through the flap and gone.

He stood holding them in the grey light and felt, for the first time in years, something move in him that he had thought was dead. It was not quite excitement. It was older than excitement, and colder. It was the instinct that had once made him good at his work, back before he had learned that being good at his work was the most dangerous thing a man could be. Curiosity, the coroner's disease. He had spent eleven years starving it.

He carried the letters back to the desk and opened the typed one first, because it was the lawful one, the expected one, the one with a return address and a franked stamp and all the small honest machinery of the ordinary world attached to it.

The paper inside was cheap, lined, torn from a spiral notebook. The handwriting was large and laborious, the letters of a man who did not often write, formed with the careful effort of a child or a craftsman. It was signed at the bottom, twice, as though once had not been enough to bind him.

*I, Silas Crane, of 4 Ferry Cottages, Sable Fen, do confess to the killing of Ada Merrick in the month of June 2004. I put her in the river at the weir below the mill. I held her under until she was dead. I have carried this sin for twenty years and I can carry it no longer. I did it and no one else and I am sorry to God and to her.*

Xanthe read it three times.

The name Ada Merrick went through him like a draught through a badly hung door. He knew the name. Everyone in Sable Fen knew the name, the way everyone knows the name of the one wound a place has never let close. Twenty years ago—a girl of twenty-three, found in the river below the old mill weir on a June morning, her lungs full of water, her death ruled accidental drowning by the coroner of the day. There had been talk, of course. There is always talk. A girl, a river, a summer night. But there had been no evidence of anything but water, and in time the talk had silted over, and Ada Merrick had become a story mothers half-told and a headstone in the churchyard that some invisible hand kept in flowers.

Silas Crane. Xanthe knew him too, by sight at least—a big, slow, grey-faced man who worked the fish dock down at the sluice, who drank two pints at the Ferryman on Fridays and never more, who had a sister in the village and a boat with a bad engine and a reputation for harmlessness so complete that it had become almost a form of invisibility. Xanthe had passed him a hundred times on the lane. The man had never once met his eye.

He set the confession down and looked at it the way he had once looked at bodies: without hurry, without flinching, letting the thing speak.

And the thing said, quietly, in its laborious hand: *I am not true.*

It was not one detail. It was the whole grain of the document. A man confessing after twenty years of silence writes, if he writes at all, in a fever—crossings-out, digressions, the ink of guilt. This was clean. This was composed. It read like a statement dictated to someone and copied out, each sentence placed like a stone in a wall. *I did it and no one else.* No one writes that unless someone has told him to write it.

And there was the date. *The month of June 2004.* The file—the old file, which Xanthe had read in his first year out of thoroughness and never touched again—said the body was found on the morning of the fourteenth of June. But the night of the drowning had never been fixed. The last person to see Ada alive had seen her on the evening of the twelfth. The river had kept its own counsel about the rest. A man confessing from memory might say the fourteenth, might say the thirteenth. Only two kinds of people in the world would write *June 2004* with that smooth, unplaced confidence: a man who had never thought about the date at all because he had not been there—and a man who had been told exactly what to write by someone who had read the file.

Xanthe sat very still. Outside, the marsh light shifted, and a heron lifted off the sluice with a sound like a sail filling.

He had not let himself think like this in eleven years. Thinking like this had consequences. Thinking like this had once cost him— he pressed the thought down where it lived, in the sealed room at the back of his mind, and turned instead to the second envelope.

The grey one. The hand-delivered one.

He cut it open with his letter knife and slid out a single card, heavy stock, good quality, the kind sold by stationers in the city. The handwriting on it was entirely different from Silas Crane's—small, upright, controlled, the writing of someone educated, someone who wrote often and well. No signature. No salutation. Four words and then four more.

*Find her. The witness lives.*

Xanthe read it once, and then, because his hands had gone very slightly cold, he read it again.

*The witness lives.*

The old file had listed no witness. He remembered that much without looking: the Merrick inquiry had been a model of its kind, brief and tidy, a drowning with no suspicious circumstances, certified by the parish doctor, closed by the coroner, mourned by the church. No witness had ever come forward. No witness had ever been sought. The river had been the only witness, and the river had said what rivers say, which is nothing.

Which meant that whoever wrote this card knew something the file did not contain. Knew it, and had carried it, and had chosen this morning—this grey, unremarkable October morning, twenty years and four months after a girl went into the water—to put it through the letterbox of a coroner who had done nothing for eleven years but countersign the deaths of farmers.

Unless, said the cold voice at the back of his mind, unless the card was not an invitation at all. Unless it was a test. Unless someone wanted to know what Xanthe Dunmore would do when he was handed a thread, and whether he was the kind of man who pulled it.

He got up and made a second cup of tea he did not want, because his hands needed something to do. Through the window, the square was going about its morning: Mrs. Petchy unlocking the wool shop, two children running along the churchyard wall, the church clock striking the half hour with its flat, cracked note. Sable Fen, doing what Sable Fen did. A parish that had buried a girl and grown grass over her and called it peace.

He came back to the desk and laid the two letters side by side. The confession and the card. The lie and the truth, or the truth and another lie—he could not yet say which was which, and that, he admitted to himself, was the thing that made his pulse lift.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not leave him alone: Silas Crane's confession, if true, closed the only wound this parish had. It gave the Merrick case a killer, a method, a motive of the simplest kind, and it asked nothing of anyone. It was a gift. And the card, if true, took the gift away and replaced it with a question, and questions in Sable Fen were not gifts. Questions were debts, and this parish collected.

He looked at the confession again, at the phrase *I did it and no one else*, and found himself thinking about who benefits. It was an old habit of mind. A false confession is never charity. Someone had paid for it—paid in money or in fear or in both—and someone had wanted the Merrick file shut with a finality that twenty years of silence had somehow failed to provide. Which meant that somewhere behind the laborious hand of Silas Crane stood a second hand, a patient one, a hand that wrote small and upright and controlled.

He set the card beside the confession and compared them, though comparison was almost absurd—the two scripts were as different as a ploughed field and a ledger. And yet the card had come an hour after the confession, hand-carried, by someone who knew his letterbox, knew his morning, knew that the post came at half past eight and that he opened it at half past eight. Someone who had watched him for longer than a morning.

Xanthe Dunmore looked out the window at the yew tree, at the churchyard wall, at the graves he could name without opening a single record—Hollis, Petchy, old Mrs. Tregale, and there, under the yew's longest arm, the stone with the fresh flowers that no one ever saw being placed.

Ada Merrick. 1981–2004. *At rest in the river's keeping.*

He had walked past that stone a thousand times. He had never once asked who brought the flowers.

He took a clean sheet of paper, and after a moment's thought did not write anything on it. Instead he unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk—the one that held the files no one ever asked about—and drew out the Merrick file, thin and brown and cold as a caught fish, and laid it on the desk beside the letters.

The clock struck ten. Somewhere down the lane, a dog began to bark, and did not stop.

Xanthe poured his cold tea into the potted fern he kept for the purpose, filled the kettle, and sat down to read a twenty-year-old file with the feeling—the first in eleven years—that the water ahead of him was deeper than it looked, and that something below it had just turned over.
