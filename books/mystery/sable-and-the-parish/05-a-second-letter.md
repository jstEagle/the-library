## 5. A Second Letter

The second letter came on a Thursday, which meant nothing, and arrived with the morning post, which also meant nothing, and yet Xanthe Dunmore stood in his doorway holding it the way a man holds a snake he has not yet decided is venomous.

The first envelope had been ordinary. This one was not. It was the same cheap cream stock as the confession—stationery from the stationer's on Marsh Lane, sold in packs of fifty to anyone with two pounds and a grievance—but this time there was no stamp. No postmark. The envelope had been carried by hand and left in his brass letter slot sometime between midnight and six, which narrowed the sender's habits considerably: either an early riser or a sleepless one.

Xanthe knew something about sleeplessness. He had made a profession of it, in a way, though he would never have phrased it so dramatically. He simply did not sleep well in Sable Fen, had never slept well anywhere since—well. Since. He set the kettle on out of habit, then stood watching it instead of reading, letting the anticipation ripen like fruit he wasn't sure he wanted to taste.

The note inside was brief. Brief notes were always worse than long ones; brevity implied confidence.

*Six weeks. The trial opens the fourteenth of March. She will be found by then or not at all.*

No signature. No instruction beyond that terrible conditional. *Or not at all.* As though the finding of her were a matter of weather, of tide, of some impersonal clock already running—and as though Xanthe were merely the instrument through which the finding must occur, or fail to.

He read it three times. Then he did what he always did when his mind refused order: he laid things out.

The confession, unfolded flat beside the sink. The first note—*Find her. The witness lives.*—beside it. Now this. Three documents, arranged in chronological order on his scarred kitchen table, weighted at the corners with the salt cellar, a spoon, and the tin of loose tea he kept for days that required ceremony rather than routine.

He made the tea properly. He sat. He looked at them the way he looked at bodies: without flinching, without inventing, asking only what they insisted upon and nothing more.

What they insisted upon was this: someone wanted Ada Merrick's death reopened. Someone knew there was a witness—a living witness, twenty years hidden—and someone believed Xanthe Dunmore, of all the people in the parish of Sable Fen, was capable of finding her.

That last part was the part that chilled him, once he let himself feel anything at all.

Because why him?

He had been coroner here eleven years. He was competent, thorough, unloved, and deliberately unremarkable. He attended the parish council meetings because attendance was required, spoke only when procedure demanded speech, and had cultivated with great care the reputation of a dry, bloodless man who counted bones and filed certificates and could be relied upon never to make trouble. He had built his invisibility plank by plank, the way a drowning man builds a raft. It had taken years. It was the single greatest achievement of his adult life, this careful erasure.

And now two letters had found him anyway. Not the police—not Talbot, who held actual jurisdiction over suspicious deaths. Not the newspapers, who would have paid for such a story. Him. The reclusive coroner. Which meant the writer knew him. Knew him specifically, personally, intimately enough to believe that beneath the dry exterior there remained a man who could not leave a wrong thing lying crooked in the road.

Xanthe drank his tea and admitted, with a sensation like setting down a heavy bag he'd forgotten he was carrying, that the writer was right.

* * *

He spent the morning trying to work and failing at it in instructive ways.

There was paperwork—the perpetual sediment of any coroner's office: a certificate awaiting countersignature for old Mrs. Petch, dead at eighty-nine of the unsurprising failure of her heart; a form for the lad who'd fallen from the quarry scaffolding in November, finally cleared through the insurers. Ordinary mortality. He signed both, and his hand was steady, and he noticed that his hand being steady had become something he monitored, the way a man with a limp monitors his gait on stairs.

At half past ten he caught himself standing at the window, looking down at the street, watching for anyone who paused too long outside his door.

The office occupied the ground floor of a narrow brick building wedged between the solicitor's clerk and a shop that sold fishing tackle and, inexplicably, candles. His front door opened directly onto the pavement. Anyone could post a letter. Anyone could stand across the lane under the awning of Tredgett's Tackle and watch his window, and he would never know, because he had spent eleven years teaching everyone that his windows showed nothing worth watching.

He tested the lock on the front door. It was a decent lock—he had installed it himself when he took the position, a private superstition rather than a practical measure, since no one in Sable Fen had shown the slightest interest in entering his office uninvited in over a decade. But the back door, the one that opened onto the alley where the bins stood and the cats conducted their parliament—that lock was original to the building, a tarnished thing older than his tenure, and it turned with the loose, apologetic click of metal that had given up caring whether it engaged.

Xanthe looked at it for a long moment.

Then he put on his coat and walked to the ironmonger's and bought a new deadlock, screws, and—after a pause at the counter that he pretended was indecision and was not—a door chain. The ironmonger's boy, a freckled youth named Wilf who delivered paraffin to half the parish, watched him wrap the hardware in brown paper and said, "Trouble, Mr. Dunmore?"

"Precaution," Xanthe said.

Wilf nodded with the solemnity of the young, and Xanthe walked back through the cold bright morning aware that he had just become, in however small a way, a story. *Dunmore's bought new locks.* In London no one would have noticed. In Sable Fen it would travel the parish by nightfall, gathering embellishments the way a snowball gathers snow.

Let it travel, he thought. And then, more honestly: I am telling myself that, and I do not believe it.

He fitted the deadlock himself that afternoon, kneeling in the doorway with a brace and bit while the February light went thin and blue outside. The drilling was loud in the quiet street. Twice he glanced up, certain he'd heard footsteps, and twice the pavement was empty. The third time it was Mrs. Aldous from the candle shop, wrapped in a coat the color of wet slate, watching him with frank parish curiosity.

"You're securing yourself in, Mr. Dunmore," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Securing my records," he said. "Confidential material."

"Mm." She had lived in Sable Fen for seventy years and had buried a husband and both of the Aldous boys' fathers, and she looked at him with eyes that had seen every kind of lie the parish produced, including the polite ones. "Mind you don't secure yourself in too tight. Some doors only lock from the inside."

She moved off toward the churchyard with her shopping bag, and Xanthe knelt there holding the screwdriver, feeling the sentence settle into him like a splinter.

* * *

That evening he did something he had not done in years: he took the file home.

Not the Merrick file itself—that stayed locked in the cabinet, photographed now page by page in a development he hadn't fully examined even in his own mind—but a folder of his own making, plain and unlabeled, containing his transcriptions, his timeline, his questions. He wrapped it in oilcloth against the damp and carried it home through streets going dark, conscious of its weight under his arm the way one is conscious of a heartbeat.

His cottage stood at the end of Ferry Lane, backing onto the drainage cut, a low slate-roofed place he had chosen precisely because no neighbor's window overlooked it. He lit the lamp, built up the fire, and spread his work across the kitchen table again, and this time he added the second letter to the arrangement.

Three documents. He read them as a sequence now, and the sequence told him things.

The confession had been composed by someone literate, legalistic, familiar with the case record—it contained details that had never appeared in the newspapers. The first note was different: shorter, urgent, almost frightened. *Find her. The witness lives.* Present tense. *Lives.* Whoever wrote it knew the witness was alive as a fact, not a hope.

The second note was calmest of all. A date. A deadline. A conditional. The voice of someone managing an operation.

Xanthe sat back and let the thought form fully, the way he let rigor pass into a body before beginning—because you could not work on a thing until it stopped pretending to be alive.

Someone was running this. Someone had arranged the confession, was monitoring the trial calendar, knew the witness existed and possibly where, and had selected Xanthe as the instrument. Two possibilities presented themselves, and he wrote them in his neat coroner's hand at the top of a fresh page:

*1. The writer wants the truth out and is using me to force it.*

*2. The writer wants me to find her first—for other purposes.*

He looked at the second line for a long time. *Other purposes.* He did not write down what those purposes might be. He found he didn't need to; the words *or not at all* sat in the room with him like a third presence.

And beneath both possibilities lay the question he had been circling all day, the one that had sent him to the ironmonger's with his dignity disguised as errand:

How did the writer know him? Not his office—his *character*. Knew that Xanthe Dunmore, who had spent eleven years declining to make waves, would take the bait. Knew that the dry man was not dry at all underneath. That knowledge was not available to strangers. It was the knowledge of someone who had watched him for years—or someone who had known him before Sable Fen.

Before.

The word opened onto a darkness he kept locked even within himself. There were reasons a man became invisible. There were reasons a man chose a fen at the edge of the map, a parish of mud and eels and churchyard fog, where nobody asked about the life a coroner had led before he was a coroner. He had assumed those reasons were his alone, sealed and buried. But the letters had a way of speaking to him that suggested the writer understood something of burial. Of things interred alive.

He banked the fire, poured the last of the tea, and forced himself through the discipline that had saved his judgment a hundred times: separate what is known from what is feared.

Known: the trial date, the six weeks, the existence of a living witness.

Known: the letters came by hand, locally. Cheap paper, Marsh Lane stock.

Known: whoever wrote them had access to the case record and to the court calendar.

Feared: everything else.

He blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the drainage cut chuckle over its stones, and somewhere past midnight he heard, or thought he heard, footsteps on Ferry Lane—slow, unhurried, passing his gate without pausing, continuing on toward the river. He did not get up. He lay with his eyes open and his breath shallow and counted the steps until they faded, and told himself it was Tredgett walking his dog, because Tredgett walked his dog at strange hours, because everyone in Sable Fen was entitled to their strangeness.

But in the morning, before the post came, he was at his window, and there in the frost on the pavement outside his office door—when he arrived, early, keys in hand—was the small dark rectangle where an envelope had lain and been taken away again, melting the frost around it, leaving its ghost.

Someone had posted a letter in the night. And someone else—or the same someone—had collected it before dawn.

Xanthe stood on the cold pavement a long moment, breath smoking, looking at the melted outline as though it might explain itself. Then he unlocked his three locks, went inside, and began, methodically, to make a list of everyone in the parish who rose before six.

It was, he reflected grimly, going to be a short list. And at the top of it, though he did not yet know why, he would eventually write the name of the parish's oldest and most beloved institution: Dr. Edmund Voss, who had certified Ada Merrick's drowning accidental, and who—Xanthe had learned this only yesterday, from the chemist's delivery book, idly, the way one learns things that mean nothing until suddenly they mean everything—rose every morning at five to walk by the river before his surgeries began.

Six weeks. She will be found by then, or not at all.

Xanthe turned the new deadlock, heard it seat home with a clean, decisive sound, and reached for his coat.
