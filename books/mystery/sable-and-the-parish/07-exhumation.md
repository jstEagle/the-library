## 7. Exhumation

The petition took eleven days to move through channels that had not moved in years, and Xanthe Dunmore spent every one of them listening to the parish tell him what he already knew: that he was a fool.

They told him in the polite registers Sable Fen had perfected over centuries. Mrs. Aldous at the post office let his letters sit an extra day in the sorting tray. The clerk at the district office in Marsham read his exhumation application twice, then a third time, and said, "You're certain you want this on your record, Mr. Dunmore?" as though he were applying for something shameful rather than lawful. Even the vicar, old Reverend Hale, who had buried half the parish and christened the other half, came to his office uninvited and stood with his hat in his hands like a man at a bedside.

"Ada Merrick has been at rest twenty years," Hale said. "There are things older than law, coroner. The peace of this ground is one of them."

"The peace of that ground is a lie," Xanthe said, "if what lies beneath it was put there by violence."

Hale had looked at him for a long moment—not with anger, Xanthe noticed, but with something wearier and more frightened—and left without another word. That frightened him more than protest would have. Anger defends itself. Fear already knows.

He filed the petition anyway. He wrote it out in his small, exact hand, citing the statute, citing the confession of Silas Crane, citing—though he did not say so aloud—the second letter, which he had photographed and locked in the drawer beneath his inkwell. *Find her. The witness lives.* He did not mention the letter because mentioning it would mean explaining why he believed it, and he could not yet explain that even to himself. It was instinct. Twenty years ago instinct had ruined him; he had spent two decades teaching it to sit still, like a dog beaten too often. Now it stood at the door again, trembling, wanting to be let out.

The approval came down from the diocesan consistory court on a Thursday, over the signature of a chancellor who had never seen Sable Fen and could not have found it on a map. By Friday the whole parish knew.

* * *

They came to watch. Of course they came to watch.

It was a grey morning in late October, the kind where the fen exhales its damp breath and the sky sits so low it seems to press against the reeds. Xanthe arrived at St. Brannoc's churchyard at seven to find perhaps forty people already gathered beyond the lychgate, standing in the lane in their coats, silent in the way crowds are silent when they have agreed together not to speak. An old woman held a bunch of chrysanthemums against her chest as though carrying them to a fresh grave rather than watching an old one opened. Two men from the dock stood with their arms folded. Someone had brought a thermos, and the small domestic sound of its cap twisting off carried across the whole yard.

Constable Talbot was there too, in uniform, ostensibly to keep order. He did not look at Xanthe when Xanthe passed through the gate. That, too, was a message.

The gravediggers were brothers, the Petch boys, third-generation fen men with forearms like mooring rope. They worked in silence, spades going into the earth with a rhythm older than any of the institutions gathered to observe them. Xanthe stood at the head of the plot with his clipboard and did not pretend to be anything other than what he was: the man who had ordered this done.

The earth came up dark and rich, smelling of iron and rot and something sweeter underneath—the deep smell of the fen itself, which never quite leaves anything. At four feet the spade struck the coffin lid, and one of the Petch boys looked up at Xanthe, waiting.

"Go on," Xanthe said.

The lid was elm, swollen black with twenty years of fen water. They levered it carefully, and the smell that rose was not the smell people expect—it was cold and mineral, like a cellar, like a well. Ada Merrick lay beneath it in the dress she had been buried in, blue cotton gone grey-green, her hands folded on her breastbone in the attitude the undertaker had arranged two decades ago. Her hair remained, fine and brown, clinging to the skull in delicate whorls. The flesh had gone. The bones were patient.

Xanthe felt the crowd's attention on his back like weather. Somewhere in the lane a child asked a question and was hushed.

"Everyone back behind the rope," he said quietly, and the pathologist's assistant from Marsham—a young woman named Okonkwo with steady hands and no history with the parish, which was precisely why he had requested her—began photographing the grave before anything was disturbed.

Dr. Edmund Voss did not come. Xanthe had half expected him to stand at the gate in his tweed coat, dignified and wounded, the beloved physician forced to witness the desecration of a patient. His absence was more eloquent. A man confident in his lie watches it tested. A man afraid of the test stays home.

Or, thought Xanthe, watching the mist curl along the yew hedge, a man very sure of himself sends others to watch for him. He scanned the faces in the lane once, twice. No one met his eyes.

* * *

The examination room at the Marsham mortuary smelled of disinfectant laid over something older, and the fluorescent lights hummed at a frequency that made Xanthe's back teeth ache. He had driven the forty minutes with the coffin strapped in the estate car behind him, checking the mirror at every bend, though he could not have said what he expected to see there.

Okonkwo worked methodically while he observed, and it was she who spoke first, because he had learned long ago to let the bones do their own talking before anyone interpreted them.

"Left parietal," she said, angling the skull under the light. "Depressed fracture. Here—" she traced a line with a gloved finger, "—and here. Radial fissures running out from the point of impact."

Xanthe leaned close. The bone was stained the colour of weak tea, but the injury was unmistakable: a concavity the size of a shilling's edge, sharp-edged, with fine cracks spidering outward. Not post-mortem damage. Post-mortem breaks are pale, clean, brittle things; the bone had been alive when this was done. The edges were rounded by healing—by the body's own desperate attempt, in whatever hours remained, to begin repairing the wound.

"Healed?" Xanthe asked.

"Partially. Days, not weeks. She was struck, and she lived a little while after." Okonkwo straightened. "This isn't consistent with a fall into water, Mr. Dunmore. A fall produces linear fractures, or nothing. This is a focused impact. Blunt instrument, narrow striking surface. A hammer. A boat hook. Something with an edge."

Xanthe wrote it down in his small hand, and his hand did not shake, and he was grateful for that, because everything else in him was shaking.

"And the lungs?"

Okonkwo had already sectioned them, such as remained. She weighed the samples, consulted her notes. "Fluid present. Diatoms consistent with the river water—she did aspirate some. But not enough." She looked up. "Not nearly enough to cause death by drowning. If she drowned, she drowned in a thimble. There's fluid in the airways, yes, but the degree of congestion, the distension—" she shook her head. "A drowning victim's lungs are heavy. Waterlogged. Hers weren't. She got water in her mouth, maybe her throat—she was breathing, or trying to, when she went in—but the water didn't kill her."

"What did?"

Okonkwo hesitated. She was young enough to still believe the answer should be simple. "I can't give you a single cause with certainty after twenty years. But blunt force trauma to the head, followed by immersion while unconscious or semi-conscious—" she spread her hands, "—that's a homicide pattern, Mr. Dunmore. That's a staging pattern. Someone hit her hard enough to fell her, put her in the water, and let the river finish the story."

*Let the river finish the story.* Xanthe wrote that down too, though he would remember it word for word without needing to.

He thought of the certificate in the archive. *Death by misadventure — accidental drowning.* Signed, certified, countersigned. Dr. Edmund Voss, who had examined the body—or claimed to have—who had found no wound on the head of a woman whose skull bore a depression wide enough to hold a coin. Either Voss had examined her and lied, or he had signed without looking, and Xanthe knew which possibility sat better in a man like Voss. Careless men sign without looking. Careful men lie beautifully.

Twenty years. The certificate had hung in the record room for twenty years, and Silas Crane had carried the weight of it for six months before the trial collapsed on other grounds, and Ada Merrick had lain under the yews with a hole in her head while the parish brought flowers and called it accident, called it fate, called it the river taking what rivers take.

The past refuses its burial, Xanthe thought. It waits. It keeps its evidence in the one place no one thinks to search—in the ground, in the bone, in the body's own stubborn testimony. Bones don't forget. Bones don't accept money. Bones don't recant.

"I'll need your report formally written," he said. "Signed. Dated."

"You'll have it tomorrow." Okonkwo paused, wrapping the samples. "Mr. Dunmore—may I ask? This case. It's twenty years old. Why now?"

Because a stranger sent me a letter, he almost said. Because a frightened man confessed to a murder with details only the dead woman's doctor should know. Because someone in this parish has tended a grave for two decades out of guilt or love or terror. Because I have spent twenty years being nobody on purpose and I am tired of the weight of it.

"Because the file didn't close," he said instead. "Files that close stay shut. This one kept breathing."

* * *

He drove back to Sable Fen in the late afternoon, the light going copper over the reed beds, and stopped first at his office to lock the report in the drawer with the letters. Then, though he could not fully account for it, he walked to the churchyard.

The crowd was gone. The lane was empty. The Petch boys had refilled the grave and tamped it flat and set the headstone back true, and if you did not know—if you had not watched—you would never guess the earth had been opened at all. That was the fen's whole genius, Xanthe thought. It closed over things. It smoothed the surface and kept the depth.

Ada Merrick's stone stood clean against the dusk:

**ADA MERRICK**
**1981 – 2004**
*SHE HEARD THE RIVER AND WENT TO IT*

And at its base, fresh that day—he was sure of it, the stems still firm, the petals unwilted despite the cold—a small posy of white flowers. Snowdrops, out of season. Someone had grown them under glass, or bought them dear, or driven miles for them. Someone had come, knowing the grave would be opened today, and left flowers either as apology or defiance or farewell.

Xanthe crouched and did not touch them. He looked at the cut ends of the stems, clean and angled, cut with scissors not fingers. A careful person. A person who planned.

"Who tends you?" he murmured to the stone. "Who remembers you well enough to do this, and won't say so?"

The churchyard gave no answer, only the wind moving through the yews with a sound like slow water. But as he rose, he saw, across the lane at the edge of the gathering-dark, a figure standing by the wall—a slight woman in a long coat, grey-haired, who had been watching him. The moment their eyes met she turned and walked away up the fen road, quickly, her coat hem swinging, and she did not look back.

Xanthe stood a long time in the cold, his breath smoking, his heart doing something quiet and enormous in his chest.

He knew that walk. He had seen it, years ago, leaving the school gates—Edith Carrow, retired now, the schoolteacher who had taught half the parish to read. Who had known everyone. Who had, perhaps, known Ada Merrick.

The bones had spoken. Now, it seemed, the living were beginning to as well—one flower at a time, one turned back, one hurried figure vanishing down a road at dusk.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would buy a bunch of flowers himself. And ask the florist exactly who else did.
