## 15. The Fire at the Archive

The fire started, as far as anyone could later agree, sometime after midnight, in the north wall of the parish record room, where the oldest ledgers were kept and the wiring was older still. By the time the first bell rang out across Sable Fen, the building that held the parish's memory was breathing smoke through every seam of its roof like a thing waking from a long sleep and deciding, all at once, not to be a thing at all.

Xanthe was awake when the bell began. He was often awake at that hour; sleep had never been a country he could settle in, only visit on short, uneasy visas. He heard the bell and stood at his window with a cup of cold tea, watching the orange light climb the sky over the church like a second, corrupt dawn, and he knew — before he dressed, before he ran, before he smelled it — exactly which room was burning.

Not the vestry. Not the nave. The record room.

He was out the door in his coat over his nightshirt, boots unlaced, the frost biting through the thin soles. The lanes of Sable Fen at three in the morning are made of stone and silence, and his footsteps sounded obscene against them, a man running through a sleeping congregation. Ahead, the churchyard gate stood open. Beyond it, the low annex built against the church's north flank — the room where every birth, marriage, death, and certificate of the parish had been filed for a hundred and sixty years — was vomiting fire through its single window.

There were perhaps a dozen people gathered by the time he arrived, in nightclothes and coats thrown over, their faces lit from below by the blaze so that they all looked like figures in some medieval painting of judgment: lit by hell, watching without expression. Constable Talbot was there, directing two men with a hand pump and a bucket chain that was less a firefighting effort than a ritual, an offering of water to something that had no intention of accepting it. The nearest hydrant was a quarter mile off, and by the time a proper engine came grinding up from the county road, the roof of the annex had gone, and with it the greater part of what lay beneath.

Xanthe did not join the bucket chain. He stood at the edge of the churchyard, among the graves, and watched the fire eat.

He watched the way a coroner watches — which is to say he watched the body of the thing, not its spectacle. He noted where the fire had begun. He noted that it had begun in the north wall, where the shelving stood, where the death registers were shelved, where the certificates of 1998 through 2011 were filed in the cabinets against that same wall — and nowhere else. He noted that the window of the record room, which had been painted shut for as long as anyone could remember, was open. He noted the smell beneath the smoke, faint but unmistakable to a man who had spent a career among the chemistry of endings: accelerant. Paraffin, or something like it.

And he noted, standing a little apart from the crowd, at the edge of the firelight where the light did not quite reach, a figure he could not quite resolve into a name. Tall. Still. Watching the fire with the same professional attention Xanthe was giving it. When Xanthe took two steps toward that edge of the crowd, the figure took two steps back into the dark between the headstones, and did not so much leave as become part of the churchyard, the way the churchyard makes everything part of itself eventually.

By dawn the annex was a black ribcage open to the sky. The fire had done in four hours what twenty years of careful filing had built. The registers of the parish — the births, the weddings, the deaths — were ash and sodden pulp. And among them, gone now beyond any recovery, were the death certificates of Ada Merrick and eleven others whose names Xanthe had spent three weeks photographing one by one in a room that smelled of dust and candle grease, while the parish slept and believed its history was safe.

* * *

He had photographed them because he was, by nature and by trade, a man who did not trust the world to keep its promises.

It was a habit older than Sable Fen. In the years before he came here — in the years he did not speak of, the years that had made him a man who took a post at the edge of a marsh rather than a career at the center of things — he had learned that documents lie in two ways: by what they say, and by how easily they can be made to stop existing. A record is only as durable as the honesty of the people who keep it. He had learned this the way one learns most true things: expensively.

So three weeks ago, on a grey afternoon when the fen was the color of old pewter, he had let himself into the record room with the sexton's spare key — the sexton owed him for a kindness involving a death certificate issued without fuss to a family that needed one quickly — and he had photographed, with the old camera he kept in his desk drawer, every certificate signed by Edmund Voss in the years surrounding Ada Merrick's drowning. Eleven certificates. Eleven deaths certified as accident, as misadventure, as the quiet failures of aged hearts — every one of them signed by the parish's beloved doctor, every one of them bearing small discrepancies that meant nothing alone and everything together. A date of death amended in a different ink. A cause of death written in a hand that trembled where Voss's hand did not. A widow's name spelled two different ways on the same page, as though two different men had filled in the form, or one man had filled it in twice, on two different days, with two different purposes.

He had made three sets of prints. One he kept in his office safe. One he had posted, in a plain envelope, to a solicitor in the county town whom he trusted the way one trusts a man one has paid to be trustworthy. And one he had hidden in the one place in Sable Fen no one would ever search, because no one in Sable Fen would ever go there willingly: the cold drawer of his own mortuary, beneath the tray where he laid out the unclaimed.

He told no one this. Not Talbot, whose loyalty he had come to suspect was a rope with a collar at one end. Not Edith Carrow, whose hands shook at the sight of an anonymous letter and who had enough to carry. The photographs existed the way a splint exists on a broken limb — quietly, close to the bone, invisible to everyone but the man who set it.

Standing in the churchyard at dawn, watching the smoke rise off the ruin of the annex in a grey column that leaned east with the wind, Xanthe felt something that was not quite fear and not quite relief. The fire had taken the originals. The originals were now, in the eyes of any court, any inquiry, any skeptical barrister, nothing at all — a gap in the record, a rumor of paper. But the fire had also told him something the certificates themselves could never have told him with such clarity.

Edmund Voss was afraid.

A man who is innocent watches a fire with horror. A man who is clever watches it with indifference. But a man who sets a fire to burn twenty years of his own signatures, and then stands in the crowd to watch it done — that man is afraid, and fear, in a man as controlled as Voss, was the most damning document Xanthe had ever handled.

* * *

The parish gathered that morning the way water gathers in a hollow, without effort or decision.

By eight o'clock there were forty people in the churchyard, and by nine there were sixty, and the talk among them had already found its shape, the way talk does in a place where the shapes are old and well-worn. Faulty wiring, they said. The annex was old; the wiring was older; the county had been warned for years. A tragedy, they said — all those records, all those names, gone. People's histories. Their grandparents' marriages, their children's baptisms. A tragedy.

And then, with the smoothness of a current finding its channel, the talk turned.

Shame about the timing, someone said — not loudly, not to Xanthe, but in the carrying voice people use when they want to be overheard without being seen to speak. Shame, with an inquest pending and a trial coming, that the records should burn now. Almost as though someone wanted them gone. And eyes, not all of them, but enough of them, slid toward the man standing alone at the churchyard wall in a coat over his nightshirt, the man who had dug up Ada Merrick against the parish's wishes, the man who asked questions in a town that had survived two hundred years by not asking them.

Xanthe heard it. He had spent his life hearing what people said underneath what they said; it was the only part of speech he had ever found reliable.

Mrs. Aldous came to him at the wall, her face composed into the expression of a woman delivering a kindness. She was the wife of the churchwarden and the parish's unofficial minister of tone.

"Terrible business, Mr. Dunmore," she said. "The records. Though I suppose—" and here she smiled, a small, dry, terrible smile, "—you'll have your copies. You being the thorough sort."

"I keep copies of everything," Xanthe said. "It's the trade."

"Mm," said Mrs. Aldous. "Well. Some things are perhaps meant to rest. The dead, I mean. And their papers." She touched his arm, two fingers, briefly, a gesture that in another parish might have been comfort and here was a warning dressed as one. "The doctor's taking it very hard, poor man. Twenty years of his signatures, all gone. He sat with the sexton till dawn."

"Did he," said Xanthe.

"He's devoted to this parish, you know. Always has been. It's a comfort, in times like these, to have a man like that." She patted his arm again and moved away, and Xanthe understood that he had just been told, in the parish's gentlest dialect, that the fire was being spoken of in two languages — accident for the county, accusation for him — and that Edmund Voss had already chosen which one he would grieve in.

Across the churchyard, Voss stood by the blackened door of the annex with the sexton, exactly where Mrs. Aldous had placed him, his silver hair bright in the flat morning light, his hands clasped behind his back, surveying the ruin with the expression of a man at a graveside. When he saw Xanthe looking, he did not look away, as a guilty man might. He inclined his head — a small, courteous, unhurried acknowledgment, one professional to another — and then returned his gaze to the ashes with an air of sorrow so complete it might have been painted on.

Xanthe looked at the north wall. At the shelving that was now a sculpture of charcoal. At the window, open, its paint seal broken cleanly outward, as if from the inside — or as if a careful man had cut the seal with a knife and opened it to give the fire air, and closed the door behind him, and walked out through a churchyard he knew every stone of, and stood at the edge of the crowd to watch his own history burn, and inclined his head to the one man in the parish who knew what he was looking at.

* * *

The county fire investigator came on the third day, a tired man from the city with a case of instruments and the manner of someone who had long ago stopped expecting the truth to cooperate. He spent two days in the ruin, sifting, and on the afternoon of the second day he sat in Xanthe's office with a cup of tea he did not drink and told him, in the flat voice of a man reading a verdict he did not like, what Xanthe had known since the first morning.

"Origin point was the north wall. Accelerant — paraffin, most likely, common enough out here. The window was opened from inside; the paint seal was cut, not cracked. No forced entry anywhere else in the building." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the desk. "Which means whoever did it had a key, or knew where one was kept, and knew the building well enough to know where the fire would do the most work."

"And?"

The investigator looked at him with the weary directness of his profession. "And nothing, Mr. Dunmore. That's the finding. Origin, accelerant, entry. What I can't give you is a name, because there's no witnesses, no prints, and a parish full of people who all saw nothing at all." He paused. "Off the record, since we're both men who read bodies for a living — the fire was set by someone calm. No haste marks. The accelerant was laid in a pattern, not splashed. That's not a burglar covering a theft. That's a man erasing a ledger. Somebody burned a very specific twenty years, and they took their time about it, and they slept well afterward."

Xanthe walked him out. The fog was coming up off the fen that evening, the way it did in late autumn, rolling in from the marsh in slow white banks that swallowed the lanes one by one, until Sable Fen was a parish of voices and lamplight and nothing else.

"Mr. Dunmore," the investigator said at the gate, shrugging on his coat. "One more thing, off the record. The cabinet that burned hottest — the one against the north wall, the death certificates, ninety-eight to eleven — the lock was already open when we got in. Unforced. Somebody had been through those files recently. Recently enough that the dust was disturbed in a fan pattern, like pages being flipped." He gave Xanthe a long look. "You wouldn't know anything about that, I suppose."

"I photograph everything," Xanthe said. "It's the trade."

The investigator almost smiled. "So I've heard." He went out into the fog, and the fog took him, and Xanthe stood a while at his office door listening to the parish settle into its evening sounds — a dog, a gull, the far bell of the church marking nothing in particular — and understood that he was now the only archive left. The originals were ash. The copies in the solicitor's hands were one thread, and threads could be cut. The copies in his safe were another. The copies beneath the mortuary tray were a third. And somewhere out in that fog, a man who had just burned twenty years of paper was doing the arithmetic of what remained, and would arrive, as Xanthe had arrived, at the same sum.

The sum was Edith Carrow.

* * *

He went to her cottage that night, because the arithmetic would not leave him alone.

The fog was thick enough to drink. He walked the lane with his torch beam bouncing back at him off the white air, past the dark windows of houses where lamps burned behind curtains and he could feel, without seeing, the attention of the parish on him like heat. They knew about the fire. They knew what it had burned. And they knew — the way a body knows weather — that the quiet coroner and the fire were part of the same story now, whatever the story was, and that the story was going to get worse before it got better, and the best place to stand when it did was far from Xanthe Dunmore's shadow.

Edith's cottage sat at the end of Marsh Lane, the last house before the fen took over, a small stone place with a garden gone wild and roses — of course, roses — climbing a frame by the door that had once been white. There was a light in the front window. That much was right. Edith kept a lamp burning in the front window every evening; she had told him once, in the careful voice she used for the true things, that she did not like coming home to a dark house, and had not for twenty years.

He knocked.

No answer.

He knocked again, and put his hand on the latch, and found the door unlocked, and felt the cold walk up his spine on small feet, because Edith Carrow locked her door the way she left her lamp burning — always, without exception, a ritual of a woman who had spent two decades being reminded that she could be reached.

"Edith?" He pushed the door open. "It's Xanthe Dunmore."

The lamp was lit in the front window. The fire had been laid in the hearth but not lit. Her knitting sat in the basket by the chair, needles crossed mid-row, a row half-finished, a row that no one had put down so much as abandoned. On the table, a teacup with a skin of cold tea on its surface, hours old. Her coat was on its hook. Her purse was in the drawer where she kept it — he knew because she had shown him once, the week after the second letter, when he had come to ask her about the handwriting and her hands had shaken and she had said, *I only liked her, that's all, I only liked her* — and the purse was full, the flower money untouched, the small hoard of notes she set aside every week for the roses still lying where she left them.

No struggle. No note. No Edith.

Xanthe stood in the middle of the small, lamp-lit room, in the smell of cold tea and lavender and twenty years of a careful life, and felt the last of the fog close around the cottage outside until the world was this room and nothing else. The fire had been the message: *I can burn what you know.* This was the message after it: *I can burn what you have left.*

He thought of the boathouse by the river — derelict these fifteen years, its door hanging, its floor rotten — and of Voss's evening walks, which followed, every night, the same route along the water, past the slipway, past the ruin, a habit of forty years that no fire and no trial had yet been able to interrupt.

He turned off Edith's lamp — because a lamp burning in an empty window was a signal, and he would not leave signals burning for men who read them — and went out into the fog, and began, at a pace just short of running, to follow the river.
