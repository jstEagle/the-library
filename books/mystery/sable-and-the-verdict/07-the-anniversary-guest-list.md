## 7. The Anniversary Guest List

Three days before the sesquicentennial dinner, Dominic Iversen sat in the archive's reading room with a legal pad and the particular misery of a man auditing his own life.

He had done this kind of work a thousand times — provenance checks, chain-of-custody reconciliations, the patient archaeology of who had touched what and when. It was the discipline he loved most about the archive: that every document carried its history like sediment, layer over layer, and if you were careful you could read the strata. He had never imagined applying it to himself.

The question was simple. The answer was not.

*Who had access to the restricted stacks between March 2009 and last Tuesday?*

He started with the lock itself. The archive door had been re-keyed twice in fifteen years — once in 2011 after a water main break flooded the sub-basement, once in 2019 when the old brass mechanism finally seized. Three keys existed for the current lock. His own, on his ring, had never left his person except for the six days in February when he'd lain in St. Ambrose Hospital with a perforated ulcer, and during those six days the ring had sat in his desk drawer, locked, in a room that was itself locked.

Which meant someone else's key had opened his drawer.

Or someone had never needed a key at all.

He wrote the names down because writing made things honest. The pad's blue lines took the ink without complaint.

**Canon Aldric Verity.** Honorary archivist emeritus, a title the cathedral council had bestowed on him twenty years ago with much ceremony and little thought. The canon kept a key. Of course he did — he was Aldous Verity's grandson; the town would have rioted if he hadn't. But in fifteen years, Dominic could count on one hand the times the canon had actually come down here. Twice for tours. Once to retrieve a photograph of his father for a memorial service. Once — Dominic remembered this now with a cold, slow clarity — on an afternoon in October three years ago, when the canon had asked to see the burial ledgers and stayed nearly two hours alone in the stacks while Dominic catalogued acquisitions upstairs.

He had offered the canon coffee. The canon had declined. *I know where everything is,* he'd said, smiling. *You've made this room legible, Dominic. That's rarer than you think.*

At the time it had been the finest compliment of his professional life.

**Tam Rooker.** The sexton. Keeper of keys to the crypt, the bell tower, the columbarium, and — by an administrative accident no one had ever bothered to fix — a duplicate to the archive, issued in 2014 so Tam could access the interment records without walking them up from the cemetery office in the rain. Tam came down maybe once a month. He read the way he did everything else: slowly, thoroughly, with his lips moving slightly. He was the only other person alive who knew where the 1962 ledger was shelved without looking it up.

And **Dominic Iversen.** Who had access to everything, always, and whose signature sat on a forged exhumation order dated to a week he'd spent unconscious in a hospital bed.

Three names. He stared at them until they blurred.

The arithmetic was ugly. If the alterations spanned years — and they did; he'd found files touched as far back as 2010 — then whoever had done it had needed sustained, repeated, unobserved access. Not a single night of opportunity but a decade of small permissions. That narrowed the field in one direction and widened it in another. A burglar couldn't do this. A stranger couldn't do this. Only love could do this — the patient, proprietary love of someone who treated the archive as theirs.

He flipped the page and wrote a second list, smaller, more terrible:

*Who benefits from the founder's coffin being empty?*

Underneath it, after a long time, he wrote: *Who benefits from me being blamed?*

Because that was the shape of it now, wasn't it. The forged order bore his name. The alterations required his kind of knowledge. When Maren Sable finished pulling the thread — and she would; he'd watched her work, she pulled threads the way tide pulls sand — every clue would lead back to the man who had spent fifteen years alone with the records. Himself.

Unless one of the other two names was guilty. And unless one of the other two names wanted him to take the fall.

---

That evening he walked up through the cathedral close to see Tam. They had known each other fifteen years, sharing the strange jurisdiction of the dead — Tam below ground, Dominic above it in paper, two halves of the same custodianship. Tam was in his cottage behind the sexton's shed, eating soup and watching the local news rehearse its sesquicentennial coverage.

"I need to ask you something," Dominic said, "and I need you not to be offended."

"Offended's my default setting. Go on."

"The archive key. The duplicate. Where is it right now?"

Tam set down his spoon. "On my ring. Same ring as the crypt." He fished it out and held it up — a fat iron ring carrying perhaps forty keys, each tagged in his careful hand. The archive duplicate hung there between the columbarium gate and the boiler room, unremarkable as a pebble.

"When did you last use it?"

"March. Rain. Wanted the weather logs for the drainage report." Tam squinted at him. "This is about the coffin."

"It's about the coffin."

They looked at each other across the small warm room, and Dominic saw Tam arrive at the same arithmetic he had. Three keys. Two other men. One of them dying, one of them digging graves, and one of them already named on a forgery.

"You think it was the canon," Tam said quietly.

"I think the canon knows call numbers by heart. I think he asked to see the burial ledgers three years ago and stayed two hours. And I think—" Dominic stopped. He had promised himself he wouldn't say it aloud yet, not until he'd verified it, but Tam's face was open and waiting and the secret had grown too heavy to carry alone. "I think something happened in 1962. Something the records were changed to hide. And I think the changes started the year your duplicate key was issued."

Tam was silent for a long moment. Then he said, "My father dug the founder's grave."

Dominic went still. "What?"

"Second grave. The real one." Tam said it flatly, the way he reported weather damage. "He never told me what was in it or why there were two. He told me to forget it, and I did, mostly, for fifty years. Until that coffin came up light as a bread loaf and I started remembering." He looked at his hands. "There's a spot in my garden, Dominic. Under the quince tree. My father planted that tree the spring of '63 and never let anyone dig near it. Never let *me* dig near it."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship does when the swell changes.

"Tomorrow," Dominic said. "First light. Before anyone's awake."

But Tam shook his head. "Tonight," he said. "I've waited fifty years. I'm not sleeping on it again."

---

They didn't make it to the garden.

Dominic's phone rang as they crossed the close, the floodlights of the cathedral washing the flagstones bone-white ahead of them. Maren Sable's name on the screen. He answered, and her voice came through stripped of all its usual dryness, running fast and low.

"Dominic. Where are you?"

"The close. I'm with Tam Rooker, we're—"

"Stay where you are. Don't go into the crypt. Don't let him go into the crypt." A breath, ragged at the edges. "A groundskeeper found the lower door open twenty minutes ago. There are lights on down there. And Dominic—" another breath, and he understood, before she said it, exactly what she was going to say, the way you understand a chord change before it lands. "Tam's coat is at the bottom of the stairs. There's blood. We can't find Tam."

Beside him, in the white wash of the floodlights, Tam Rooker stood holding his key ring in one fist, and Dominic saw with a lurching, vertiginous horror that the archive key was missing from it — clipped off clean at the ring, the little brass tag for the archive dangling empty beside the columbarium gate.

"He's here," Dominic said. "Maren, Tam is standing right next to me."

The silence on the line lasted three full seconds.

"Then whose blood," Maren said slowly, "is at the bottom of my stairs? And why does your voice sound like a man who just realized he's the last name left on the list?"

Down in the dark beneath their feet, somewhere past the stone and the silence, the lights in the crypt burned on — and the second coffin, the one nobody was supposed to know existed, waited under the quince tree for morning.

* * *

Dominic lowered the phone without ending the call. Maren's breathing filled the small space between them, tinny and fast.

"Tell her I'm coming down," Tam said. "Tell her nobody touches anything until I've seen the stairs myself. Fifty years I've kept those steps. If someone fell them tonight, I'll know whether it was an accident by the scuff marks alone."

"That's exactly what a suspect says," Dominic said, and immediately hated how it sounded.

"Then it's good we're all suspects together." Tam was already moving toward the crypt door, soup forgotten, grief and fury braided into something that walked faster than either. "Come on, archivist. You keep the records. I keep the stones. Let's go find out which one of us the truth kills first."

Behind them, at the edge of the floodlights, a figure stood among the yew trees — still, patient, watching them walk toward the door they should not open. By the time Dominic turned fully around, the close was empty, and the only proof the figure had ever been there was a single sheet of paper pinned to the cathedral door with a rusted tack, fluttering.

He crossed to it. Unpinned it. Held it under the light.

It was a page torn from the 1962 burial ledger — the original, pre-correction, the entry he had filed away years ago as *amended per family request.* And across the bottom, in handwriting he recognized instantly, having spent three years admiring its elegance in the margins of donation letters:

*One of you buried the truth. One of you is standing on it. Come down and I'll show you which is which.*

"Aldric," Dominic whispered.

The crypt door stood open at the bottom of the steps, and the smell rising out of it was old stone and fresh blood, and somewhere below, in the dark where the founder's empty coffin lay like a lie given wooden form, someone was waiting for them with the lights left deliberately on.

Maren's voice crackled up from the phone he'd forgotten he was still holding. "Dominic. What does the note say?"

He looked at Tam. Tam looked at him.

"It says," Dominic said, "that we're expected."
