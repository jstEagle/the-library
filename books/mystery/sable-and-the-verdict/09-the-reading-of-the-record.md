## 9. The Reading of the Record

**Dominic**

The lectern had been set up at the head of the long table like an altar, which, in a way, it was. One hundred and fifty years of Verity, arranged in candlelight and white linen, and at the center of it all a folder of documents that Canon Aldric Verity had carried in himself that afternoon — Dominic had watched him do it, watched the old man's hands steady on the rail as he descended into the archive one last time.

He'd taken the sanitized history from the locked case. The true records were still there. All Dominic had to do was make the exchange before eight o'clock.

His hands were not steady. He noticed this with clinical detachment, the way he noticed everything now, cataloguing his own deterioration the way he'd once catalogued water damage in the map room. Tremor, onset this evening, probable cause: what he was about to do to the only institution he had ever loved.

Maren stood across the hall in her dark suit, talking to the mayor's wife, laughing at something. She hadn't looked at him once all evening. That was the discipline they'd agreed on. If he glanced over and she was already watching him, it meant stop. She wasn't watching. So he kept moving.

The folder sat inside his jacket, against his ribs, warm as a living thing. Burial ledger, 1962. Death certificate, amended. The sexton's statement, unsigned but dated. Fifteen years of silence, bound in acid-free mylar because even lies deserved preservation — no. Because *he* had preserved them, without knowing what he was preserving, and that was the wound he would carry out of this room tonight no matter how it ended.

The canon held court near the fireplace, thin as a candle itself, propped in his chair with a blanket over his knees that someone had tried to make look like an elegant lap robe. His color was bad. Everyone pretended not to notice. He had maybe weeks; everyone knew that too, and it was why the town had come tonight in such numbers, why the dinner felt less like a celebration than a wake that refused to admit its own name.

"Archivist." The canon's voice found him across the room the way it always did, precise as a call number. "A word before we begin."

Dominic crossed to him. Up close the old man smelled of camphor and old paper, and Dominic hated that he still found the smell comforting.

"You'll want to check my pages," Aldric said, smiling faintly. "You're the sort who checks."

"I trust your reading, Canon."

"No, you don't. You never have. It's why I chose you for the archive, thirty years ago — you were the only applicant who told me the collection had errors in it. To my face. At your interview." His eyes were bright and terrible. "I have loved this town my whole life, Mr. Iversen. Tonight I give it a gift. See that it's delivered properly."

*See that it's delivered properly.* Dominic carried the sentence back across the hall like something with edges. Did the old man know? Had he always known? Was this a blessing or a test or simply the vanity of a dying man who assumed every hand in the building moved at his instruction?

Eight o'clock came. The mayor spoke. The choir sang the founding hymn, the one about seed and stone. And then the canon was wheeled to the lectern, and the folder was placed before him, and two hundred people leaned in the way congregations lean, and Dominic began to move through the servants' corridor toward the archive stairs with Maren falling into step beside him without either of them saying a word.

* * *

**Maren**

She heard the canon's voice through the floor as they worked — amplified, sonorous, reading the founding history the way it had been read at every civic occasion for a century and a half. *Aldous Verity, who gave us the cathedral and the mill and the law of our streets.* She'd pulled the file that morning. She knew what the real record said. Listening to the beautiful lie drift down through the boards while she stood in the cold archive holding the truth made her feel like she was hearing a eulogy delivered over the wrong body.

"Fast," she said.

Dominic's hands, remarkably, were steady now. He lifted the true folder from the case where it had slept for fifteen years and set the canon's sanitized pages in its place, aligning the corners, squaring the stack, and she understood suddenly that this was how he prayed — order imposed on chaos, one last time.

"Ledger first," he said. "Then the certificate. Then the sexton's statement. In that order. He'll try to talk over the ledger. He can't talk over three documents."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since the coffin came up empty." He looked at her then, and in the dim light of the stacks his face was stripped of everything archival, everything institutional. Just a man. "Whatever happens up there — I need you to know I'm not doing this to save myself."

"I know what you're doing it for," Maren said. "Go."

They took the stairs two at a time. Through the service door, into the warmth and candlelight and the smell of roast pheasant, and the canon's voice rolling on — *and so he was laid to rest beneath the floor he consecrated, and the town grew around his sleep* — and Maren peeling away left while Dominic went right, closing the pincers, moving through the crowd the way she'd moved through a hundred rooms looking for the one person who wouldn't meet her eyes.

Half of them weren't meeting her eyes. Guilt, or goose fat, or the canon's voice. She drifted. Waited.

Dominic reached the lectern's side. The canon paused to turn a page, sipped water with a trembling hand, and in that small domestic gap Dominic slid the true folder onto the lectern and took the false one away, smooth as a card trick, and stepped back into the shadow of a pillar.

And the canon, without breaking stride, began to read the burial ledger.

* * *

**Dominic**

Later, he could never say whether Aldric knew at the first line or the tenth. Whether the old man's composure cracked gradually or all at once. What he remembered was the sound.

*"Interment record, fourteenth of March, 1962. Remains received: male, adult. Identity as recorded: Aldous Verity, founder."* A pause. The rustle of a page that should not have been there. *"Correction entered in later hand: remains received do not correspond. Interred individual unidentified. Original remains disposed by family request, location unrecorded."*

The hall went quiet in stages, like a tide going out. Forks stopped. The choir director turned around in her chair.

The canon's voice continued, because a voice trained on forty years of sermons does not know how to stop. *"Death certificate, amended: cause of death, blunt force trauma. Amending physician's note: original certificate recording cardiac failure superseded. See attached statement of T. Marsh, sexton, undated: 'I buried what they told me to bury and I signed what they told me to sign and I have waited fifteen years to say so.'"*

"Aldric." The mayor was half-risen. "What is this?"

The canon looked up from the page. Looked out at two hundred faces he had christened and married and buried, the whole living architecture of the town his father had built on a dead man's name. And Dominic, standing in the pillar's shadow with the false history folded under his arm, saw the exact moment the old man decided — saw it land in him like a key turning.

"He died angry with me," Aldric said. His voice had gone small, unamplified, human. "My father. We argued about the mill workers' wages and I struck him and he fell against the hearthstone, and when I knelt down he was already—" The breath caught. "I was twenty-six years old. I put him in the ground behind the sexton's cottage and I put another man in the founder's tomb, and then I built a town on top of both of them, and I have read the sanitized history aloud at every anniversary for fifteen years because if I said the true words out loud even once, I believed God would finally take me. And He wouldn't. That was the punishment. He just — wouldn't."

Someone screamed, or laughed, or both. Chairs scraped. Maren was already moving through the crowd, and Dominic watched her go with the strange calm of a man watching the thing he set in motion arrive exactly where he aimed it.

The canon's hand came off the lectern. He swayed. The blanket slipped from his knees.

"He's collapsing—" 

"Doctor! Is there a doctor—"

They got him flat on the parquet among the dropped napkins, and the town physician pushed through, and above the noise of it Aldric Verity found Dominic's eyes across the room — found them with the unerring precision of a man who had memorized the catalogue of everyone he'd ever met — and mouthed two words.

*Delivered properly.*

Then his eyes closed, and Maren's voice cut through the hall, clear and official: *"Nobody leaves. Canon Verity is under arrest for the murder of Aldous Verity, and this building is now a scene."*

* * *

**Maren**

The paramedics took him at 9:40. By ten the hall was half-empty and echoing, candles guttering in their holders, pheasant going cold under the chandeliers. Maren stood at the lectern where the canon had fallen and read the rest of the record to the forty people who stayed — the ones too stunned or too loyal or too hungry for truth to leave — and when she finished, nobody applauded. They just sat there, in the ruins of the sesquicentennial, holding the new weight of their own name.

Dominic appeared at her elbow. He still had the false history tucked under his arm like a hymnal.

"You'll need to book the documents into evidence," he said. "All of them. Including the ones I handled tonight."

"Including those." She looked at him. Under the chandelier he looked ten years older than he had that morning at the crypt, and lighter, somehow, at the same time. "You understand what happens tomorrow. Statement, deposition, probably a bedside hearing given his condition. You'll be sworn in front of the whole town."

"I've been rehearsing in front of the whole town for thirty years," Dominic said. "Tonight was the first time anyone listened."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she took the false folder from under his arm, logged it mentally as exhibit whatever-it-would-be, and said the thing she'd been carrying since the midnight coffee, since the first thread pulled taut:

"When this is over — the archive. The locks. I want to help you open it. All of it. To everyone."

Dominic looked at her for a long moment, candlelight moving in his eyes.

"Then bring a request slip," he said. "I'll pull the first file myself."
