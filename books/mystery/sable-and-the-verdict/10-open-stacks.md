## 10. Open Stacks

The second burial was nothing like the first.

There were no brass bands, no mayor in ceremonial sash, no choir singing the hymn Aldous Verity had supposedly loved. There was a gray October morning, a hole dug to proper depth by men who were paid by the hour, and a small pine box carried on the shoulders of six pallbearers who had volunteered rather than been appointed.

Dominic stood at the edge of the grave with his hands folded, watching them lower what remained of the town's founder into ground that had already held him once, unnamed and unrecorded, in a plot behind the sexton's cottage.

The rector kept her words short. *We commit these remains to the earth, not as a symbol, but as a man.* She had refused to read from the official founding history. She had asked Dominic instead for the true record — the death entry dated 1962, the coroner's fragmentary report, the ledger line in Canon Verity's own hand: *Father interred privately. Public service deferred indefinitely.*

*Indefinitely* had turned out to mean sixty-three years.

The box went down. The first spade of earth hit the lid with a sound like a book closing, and Dominic felt something in his chest loosen that he hadn't known was clenched.

Maren stood beside him, out of uniform, wearing a coat he'd never seen before. She didn't speak during the committal. But when the small crowd began to disperse — half the town had come anyway, drawn by the same instinct that draws people to fires — she said quietly, "You wrote the words she read."

"I filed them," Dominic said. "Fifteen years ago I corrected a discrepancy because correcting it was my job. It took me until this month to understand that filing isn't the same as telling."

"No," Maren said. "But it's where the telling starts."

They watched the gravediggers work. Somewhere behind them, a child asked her mother why the coffin was so small, and the mother gave an answer that was true and incomplete, the way most answers are.

* * *

Canon Aldric Verity died on a Tuesday, eleven days after the reading, with the trial scheduled to begin the following Monday.

He never entered a courtroom. He died in the cathedral's infirmary — the same building where he had spent decades quoting call numbers from memory, where he had taught two generations of altar boys the alphabet of the dead — and he died without retracting anything. His confession at the anniversary dinner had been complete, almost serene: the argument in 1962, the fall, the decision made in the space of an hour that the family name mattered more than the truth. The staged burial. The forged documents, refined over years, perfected using an archive he knew better than its own archivist.

Better than its own archivist. Dominic turned that phrase over many times in the weeks that followed. It should have wounded him. For a long time it did. But grief, he discovered, is just love with nowhere left to file itself, and eventually he found a shelf for it.

The canon's last request, relayed through his solicitor, was that his papers be donated to the archive. All of them. Unrestricted.

Dominic catalogued them himself. It took four days. On the final evening he sat alone among the boxes of a man who had loved the collection as fiercely and as corruptly as anyone could, and he understood at last that the archive had never failed. Archives don't fail. They only wait. Every crime Aldric Verity committed had left a paper trail precisely because the man couldn't bear to destroy a document. He had altered, swapped, shifted dates — but he had kept everything, because keeping was the one commandment he honored above all others.

The truth had been in the building the whole time. It had simply been waiting for someone to stop guarding it and start reading it aloud.

* * *

The notice went up on the cathedral door on a Monday morning, typed on plain paper, laminated against the weather:

**THE VERITY ARCHIVE IS OPEN TO THE PUBLIC.**
**Research hours: Tuesdays through Saturdays, 9 to 5.**
**All records available upon request. No exceptions.**

*No exceptions.* Two words that would have cost Dominic his position fifteen years ago, five years ago, one year ago. Now they cost him nothing but the particular loneliness of being the only key.

The first week, three people came. A graduate student writing about nineteenth-century burial customs. An elderly woman looking for her grandmother's naturalization papers. A boy of perhaps twelve who wanted to know if the founder really murdered anyone, and whom Dominic sat down and showed the actual file, because a child old enough to ask is old enough to read.

By the third week there were more. Word traveled the way word does in small towns — imperfectly, then all at once. People came in wary, as though expecting the locked cabinets of rumor, and found instead a reading room with good light and a sign that said ASK ANYTHING.

Dominic had replaced the lock on the front door with one that required no key at all. He kept the old one in a drawer, next to the forged exhumation order, which he had decided not to destroy either. Evidence, yes — but also history. The archive did not curate itself into comfort anymore. It kept what happened.

Some nights he still caught himself reaching for keys that weren't on his belt anymore. Some nights the silence of the stacks felt less like peace than like an audience waiting. But the days were different. The days were full of other people's questions, and every question was a small act of trust he answered with a slip of paper and a call number.

He was reshelving the 1962 correspondence on a Thursday afternoon when the door opened.

* * *

She came in carrying a request slip filled out in her own hand — blocky, unhurried letters, the handwriting of someone accustomed to being read.

Maren Sable set the slip on the reference desk the way she'd once set case files on interview tables, and Dominic picked it up and read it with the formality the moment deserved.

*Requestor: Sable, M. Subject: Verity family, complete holdings. Purpose: personal.*

"Personal," he said.

"The case is closed. The trial won't happen now." She shrugged, but her eyes stayed on his. "Turns out I've been reading this town's files for months and I still don't know how it ends. How it goes on, I mean. That part was never in the record."

"It will be," Dominic said. "I'm writing it. Not the sanitized version. The one with the empty coffin and the garden bones and the dinner where an old man told the truth because he ran out of time to keep lying." He slid the slip back across the desk. "It'll be filed under V. Everything about this town always is."

"And access?"

"No exceptions."

Maren smiled — the rare one, the one he'd seen maybe four times, each time in this building after dark. "Then I'll need a table," she said. "And probably coffee. And possibly company, if the archivist isn't too busy."

"The archivist," Dominic said, pulling out the chair across from his own, "has been waiting weeks for a serious researcher."

She sat. He went into the stacks — his stacks, the town's stacks now — and lifted down the first file. Not the murder record, not the founding myth, not any of the documents that had defined the last fifteen years of both their lives. Just the oldest thing they had: Aldous Verity's own hand, aged nineteen, a letter home describing the land he intended to build on. *A place worth telling the truth about,* the letter said, though the young man writing it could not have known what those words would cost.

Dominic carried it to the table and placed it in front of her, open to the first page.

"Start here," he said. "Everyone else did."

Outside, the light was going gold over the rooftops of the town that had finally been given back its own story. Inside, two people bent over a single page, and the archive — unlocked, unguarded, alive — held them the way it had always held everything.

Patiently. And in the open.
