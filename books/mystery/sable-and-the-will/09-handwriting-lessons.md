## 9. Handwriting Lessons

The trouble with forging a stranger's handwriting, Sable Wren had learned by her second night at the Hotel Vesper, was not the letters themselves. Letters were easy. Letters were geometry — the angle of a crossbar, the depth of a loop, the slant of a line against its ruling. She could deconstruct anyone's alphabet in an hour. She'd done it for juries, once, standing beside an overhead projector with two documents projected side by side while twelve bored citizens watched her make a man's signature appear and disappear like a card trick.

No, the trouble was rhythm.

Handwriting wasn't drawn; it was performed. It lived in the speed of it, in where the pen hesitated and where it flew, in the small hesitations that no forger thought to fake because no forger thought of them as anything but accidents. A person's hand was a recording of their body's habits, their fatigue, their mood at eleven p.m. on a Tuesday. To forge it you had to stop being yourself for long enough to become someone else's muscle memory.

And August Sterling, Sable was fairly certain, would notice a forgery the way she noticed everything else — instantly, silently, and without ever letting on that she'd noticed at all.

Sable sat in Room 412 with the lamp turned low and the curtains drawn to a finger's width, and spread her materials across the writing desk like a surgeon laying out instruments. The hotel stationery. A fountain pen she'd bought from the gift shop downstairs because the Vesper's own pens were too distinctive, too heavy, too *theirs*. And in the center, weighted flat under the brass ashtray she never used: three pages of notes she'd collected over nine days of watching the concierge work.

She had started collecting them almost by accident.

It was what she did — it was what she *was*, underneath the travel-writer costume she wore the way other people wore raincoats. Forensic accountants were trained to see paper the way detectives saw crime scenes, and Sable had spent eleven years reading documents the way other people read faces. Every institution left a fingerprint in its paperwork. Every fraud did. You just had to know which details were load-bearing.

The Hotel Vesper's paperwork sang to her from the moment she checked in. It sang in a register most people couldn't hear — a quiet, disciplined wrongness, like a piano slightly flat in a room full of people pretending not to notice. The guest registry behind the front desk. The ledgers in the back office she'd glimpsed through a door held open a half-second too long. The way certain names appeared in the book, stayed three nights, spent nothing visible, and vanished without folios or incident reports. The way room numbers repeated across months in patterns that made no commercial sense — 214, always 214, twice a month, cash rate, no luggage service, no restaurant charges, as though the room were being rented the way other businesses rented a parking space.

Sable had come to the Vesper chasing a thread from a bankruptcy filing four hundred miles away — a shell company whose only physical address, listed across fourteen years of filings, was a post-office box rented from the lobby of this hotel. She had expected to find a small node in someone else's network. What she'd found instead was a network that seemed to have no outside edges at all. The Vesper didn't launder money for criminals. The Vesper *was* the machine. Everything ran through the guest book.

Which meant the guest book was the ledger. Which meant everyone who touched it mattered.

And the person who touched it most, who stood beside it ten hours a day like a lighthouse keeper tending a light nobody else could see, was August Sterling.

* * *

Sable had catalogued her the way she catalogued everything, in the margins of her working notebook, in a shorthand only she could read.

*Concierge. F., approx. 40s. Name tag: A. STERLING. Uniform immaculate — pressed daily, cuffs clean, shoes polished but heels worn unevenly (right outer edge — stands with weight shifted, listening posture). Never writes at the desk without angling the registry away from guests. Left-handed? No — right-handed, but holds pen high, near the nib's throat. Distinctive capitals. Crosses sevens. Does NOT dot i's with a circle (checked — common affectation here, she doesn't share it).*

That last detail had taken her three days to confirm, and it had cost her a deliberately misdirected piece of luggage and a very patient bellman named Dev.

Dev was interesting too. Young, watchful, loyal in the way of people who had decided August Sterling was the best thing this building had produced. He adored her openly and she pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of data. Institutions like the Vesper survived on people pretending not to notice things. Sable had begun keeping a list of everything the staff politely failed to see, and the list was getting long enough to be damning all by itself.

But it was August the diary entries kept circling back to.

Because here was the problem Sable had been turning over for two days now, the problem that had finally driven her to steal a page of the woman's handwriting in the first place:

Sooner or later, they were going to take her.

Not might. *Would.* She'd seen the signs stacking up for a week now — the way the night porter's face went carefully blank when she asked about the east wing; the way the general manager, Edmund Vesper III, had appeared at her elbow during breakfast with a smile like a locked drawer and asked, so gently, whether her article was going well, and whether there was anything at all the hotel could do to make her stay more *comfortable*. The word comfortable had landed on the table between them like a coin on a bar. She'd understood it perfectly. So, apparently, had he, because he'd picked up his coffee and left before she could answer.

Sable had worked cases where the subjects got nervous. Nervous subjects hired lawyers. Nervous subjects shredded documents, moved accounts, fled jurisdictions. But the Vespers were older than nervousness. They were third-generation confident, the kind of family that had never once needed to explain itself to a jury because no jury had ever gotten close enough. When people like that decided you were a problem, they didn't hire lawyers.

They had a condemned east wing with a freshly oiled lock.

She'd noticed that too, on day six. Officially sealed since the seventies, according to the plaque and the dust and the plywood over the lobby-side windows. But the hinges on the service corridor door gleamed. Someone oiled them regularly. Someone used that door often enough to maintain it, and maintained it quietly, and quietly was the whole grammar of this hotel.

So: they would take her. Probably soon. Probably at night, probably through that door, probably with Tomas the porter looking the other way because Tomas looked the other way the way other men breathed.

And when they took her, every scrap of evidence she'd gathered — the notebook, the photographs, the copies of registry pages she'd sewn into the lining of her suitcase — would vanish into the same silence that had swallowed whatever else the east wing had swallowed over ninety years. Unless.

Unless she left something behind that would be found. Not by the police — she had no faith the police hadn't already been folded into the Vesper's guest book like everyone else. Not by a journalist — too slow, too public, too easy to intercept. It had to be found by one specific person. One person inside the building, above suspicion, with access to everything, and — this was the essential part — with eyes good enough to read a message that would look like nothing at all to anyone else.

There was exactly one candidate.

* * *

The page she'd stolen had come from the concierge desk itself, lifted on day eight while August walked a lost child back to its frantic mother in the dining room. A guest request slip, filled out in August's own hand: extra blankets, Room 307, *at your convenience* — and beneath it, the concierge's notation in the margin, that quick private shorthand Sable had watched her use a dozen times: initials, a time, a small precise annotation.

Sable had studied it for two hours that night, and what struck her wasn't the letterforms. It was how *economical* the hand was. August Sterling wrote the way she presumably did everything else — no wasted motion, no flourish, every stroke doing exactly one job. The capitals were tall and narrow, almost architectural, like columns. Her lowercase e's didn't close fully; there was a gap at the top, a tiny unfinished doorway in every single one. Her t-crossings were short and slightly angled downward, left to right, like small decisive gestures. And she crossed her sevens — Sable had confirmed this across four separate documents — with a single flat strike that extended past the stem on both sides.

Anyone could copy those features. That was the trap of forgery: amateurs copied features. Experts copied *flow*. And flow couldn't be faked directly. Flow had to be practiced until it stopped being performance and became habit.

So Sable practiced.

She filled page after page of hotel stationery with August Sterling's alphabet. *At your convenience. Extra blankets. Room 307.* Over and over, slowing down first, then speeding up, then forcing herself to write without looking at the exemplar, then writing with her eyes closed, then writing fast, slightly tired, slightly distracted — because whoever found the message later would need to believe it was written by a busy woman at the end of a shift, not by a forger at midnight with a lamp and a ruler.

By the third night her hand ached in unfamiliar places, which was how she knew it was working. You always ached in new places when you learned a new body.

The strange part — the part she hadn't anticipated — was what the practice did to her head. Somewhere around the hundredth repetition of *A. Sterling*, copying the firm architectural capitals, she realized she was beginning to think in the woman's cadences. She'd catch herself annotating her own observations in that clipped shorthand style. She'd imagine how August would file away a detail — not with alarm, not with excitement, but with that terrible patient competence, the look of someone adding a fact to a collection she'd been building her entire life.

Sable had spent her career studying people through their paperwork. She had never before felt *studied back* by a document. But that was what practicing August's handwriting felt like — as though each letter she forged was also a small portrait assembling itself, stroke by stroke, of the woman who'd made the original.

She liked her, Sable realized somewhere around the two-hundredth line. That was inconvenient. Sympathy was a liability in fieldwork. Sympathy got agents made, sources burned, operations collapsed. And yet there it was: the growing, unshakable sense that if August Sterling ever saw what Sable had seen in the registry — the flat piano note, the rooms rented like parking spaces, the whole quiet machinery of it — she wouldn't look away, and she wouldn't run to Edmund Vesper either. She'd do what she always did. She'd notice. She'd wait. She'd decide.

The question was whether Sable could give her enough to decide with before the east wing door closed behind her.

* * *

On the ninth night, she designed the message.

It couldn't say anything outright. If the diary or the note were discovered by the wrong eyes, it had to read as mundane — a concierge's routine annotations, a guest's forgettable scribbles. The real content had to live in the layer only August could read: the numbers.

Sable had cracked the margin code on day five, and it remained the most elegant thing she'd found in the building. The registry's marginal notations — those little clusters of digits beside certain guests' names — weren't room extensions or billing codes. They were coordinates into the registry itself. Initial-position, date-offset, entry-number. A self-indexing system, hidden in plain sight for decades, pointing from any given name to the other names it was entangled with. Debts. Favors. Payments. A web of obligation written in the hotel's own public handwriting, legible to anyone who knew the key and invisible to everyone who didn't.

Whoever built it had been a genius. Whoever maintained it had been careful. And whoever inherited it — Edmund, she assumed, or Edmund's father, or some gray eminence behind both — had never once imagined that a forensic accountant posing as a travel writer would spend her evenings cross-referencing a guest book for fun.

The message would use the same grammar. She'd leave it in the diary she'd bought the day she arrived — the one she wrote in openly, the one that would inevitably be found and read, because a missing woman's diary was the first thing anyone looked for and the last thing anyone could resist. Let them read it. Let them find a travel writer's banal observations about lobby flowers and bad coffee. But the final entries — the ones dated *after* whatever happened to her — would carry the margin-code numbers, arranged in clusters that meant nothing to a casual reader and everything to the one person in this building who handled the registry ten hours a day.

They wouldn't be in Sable's handwriting. That was the masterstroke, the part she'd worked out at three in the morning with her hand cramping and the wastebasket full of ruined pages. They'd be in *August's*. Because when August Sterling opened that diary and found entries in her own hand — entries she had never written, dated after a woman had vanished from her hotel — she would not rest. Curiosity could be managed; professional pride could be managed. But a document that shouldn't exist, in your own handwriting, about your own hotel? That was a hook no watcher could ignore. Sable was counting on the one flaw in August Sterling's armor: she could not abide a record that didn't reconcile.

*If they take me,* Sable wrote in her own hand, on a page she would later destroy, rehearsing the sentence she'd encode, *the ledger names the buyer.*

Then she set the page aside, uncapped the pen, and began the slow, deliberate work of writing those words in a dead woman's voice — except the woman wasn't dead yet, and the hand wasn't hers, and the whole plan rested on the hope that imitation, done precisely enough, becomes a kind of introduction.

*Dear August,* the forged entries would say, in everything but words. *You don't know me. I've been watching you the way you watch everyone. Here is what I found. Here is where I'll be.*

* * *

Near dawn on the tenth day, Sable sat back and surveyed the finished pages drying on the desk, and allowed herself the first honest assessment of her situation in a week.

The forgeries were good. Not perfect — August herself might feel something faintly off, some rhythm a half-beat slow, some hesitation where none should be — but good enough to survive a glance, and even a study, provided the reader's suspicion was pointed outward rather than inward. Nobody suspects a diary of being forged *by its own subject*. The trick worked because it was absurd.

The plan was less good. She knew that. The plan required her to keep the diary discoverable but not seized prematurely; required her to hide it somewhere the police would look and the Vespers wouldn't; required her to survive long enough to write the final entries *after* the disappearance began, which meant she had to be present, coherent, and unobserved during the exact window in which she expected to be drugged, detained, or worse. There were failure modes stacked on failure modes, and she'd catalogued every one of them at 4 a.m. and decided, in the end, that a flawed plan executed was worth more than a perfect plan imagined.

What she hadn't decided — what she was still turning over as the sky behind the curtains went from black to the color of old pewter — was the last entry. The thank-you. Whether to write it now, in advance, in her own hand, and hide it where only a thorough search would find it. *Thank you for noticing. Thank you for finishing it.* Whether a woman walking into a building's basement should get to say goodbye to someone she'd never actually met.

She was still deciding when the floorboard in the corridor outside creaked — the third stair-soft step past the elevator, the one she'd mapped on day two, the one that meant someone heavy and unhurried was passing toward the east wing corridor at an hour when no guest had business there.

Sable closed the diary, slid it into the false bottom of her suitcase among the sewing and the spare shoes, and turned off the lamp.

Through the finger-width gap in the curtains, she watched the gray light come up over the city, and listened to the Hotel Vesper wake around her — the boiler's cough, the kitchen's clatter, the front desk bell ringing its bright indifferent note — and she thought, with a calm that surprised her: *Three days. Maybe four.*

Downstairs, August Sterling would be pinning on her name tag about now, angling the registry away from the morning's first guests, noticing everything, suspecting nothing — or suspecting everything, it hardly mattered yet.

Soon, Sable thought. Very soon, you're going to read something in my handwriting that isn't mine at all.

I hope you forgive me for the lesson.
