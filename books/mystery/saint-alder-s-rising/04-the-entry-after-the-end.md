## 4. The Entry After the End

The diary sat on Jude's kitchen table like something rescued from a drowning.

He had not turned on the overhead light. The single lamp above the stove threw a cone of amber across the table's scarred pine, and beyond that cone his apartment receded into the kind of darkness he preferred — the darkness of a man who had spent twelve years learning that most truths arrive quietly, at the edges of things, and are lost to anyone standing in full light. He made tea he did not drink. He put on reading glasses he did not need and did not take off. And then, with hands that were steadier than he expected them to be, he opened Marisol Vane's diary for the second time.

The first time had been in the basement of the Meridian, crouched behind the opened wall while Renzo the drywaller stood over him asking questions Jude hadn't heard. He'd read perhaps four pages before Sheriff Okafor's deputy arrived to seal the scene, and in those four pages he had found a woman's voice — wry, watchful, a little lonely — describing the town he thought he knew. She wrote about the fog the way he thought about it: as a collaborator. She wrote about the hotel. She wrote about *him*, once, briefly, in an entry from March of 2013: *The new concierge at the Meridian watches everyone the way I do. I think we would have been friends if I'd stayed.*

Jude had read that line three times before the deputy's flashlight found him.

Now, alone, he began again at the beginning, because he was a man who believed in beginnings, in sequence, in the alphabet of small towns where every name had its place and every place its history. The early entries were ordinary in the way that only lives about to end are ordinary. Grocery lists folded into reflections. Complaints about the cannery's smell in August. A lover referred to only as *G.*, described with a tenderness that curdled, entry by entry, into something more like inventory: *G. lies about small things. G. lies about the boat. G. says he was in Coos Bay but the ferry log says otherwise.* Jude recognized the method immediately, because it was his own method — the patient accumulation of small wrongnesses until they formed a shape no one could deny. Marisol Vane had been keeping a file. Just like him.

*She would have understood me,* he thought, and then corrected himself: *she did understand me,* because she had written it, in March of 2013, eleven weeks before she walked out of the Meridian's lobby at 9:40 on a Tuesday night and ceased, officially, to exist.

He knew the date without looking it up. That was the thing people never understood about him, the thing that made them call him strange behind his back and meticulous to his face — he kept the whole town in his head, cross-indexed, dated. April 17th, 2013. A Wednesday, though the papers got it wrong and said Tuesday. Rain earlier in the day, clearing by evening. She had checked out of Room 214 — no, she hadn't checked out; she had simply stopped being there, her key left in the door, her car still in the lot, her bill unpaid for two more days until management noticed. The last confirmed sighting was the lobby camera, which captured her crossing toward the front doors in a gray coat, and then nothing. No body. No note. No bank activity after May. A woman dissolved into the coastal fog like sugar into tea, and Saint Alder, which forgot nothing else, had somehow forgotten her.

Except Jude hadn't. He had a page for her in the green ledger he kept in the concierge desk's lower drawer — the one Okafor had once joked was the town's real census. *VANE, MARISEL E., Rm 214, long-stay, quiet, tips well, watches the pier at low tide.* Twelve years, and the page had never been closed out, because Jude did not close pages on unresolved entries. It felt like a lie.

So when the renovation crew opened the wall between the old service corridor and what had been, in 2013, the back office — when Renzo pried loose a section of lath and plaster and a oilcloth-wrapped bundle dropped into the dust — Jude had known whose it was before anyone said her name. Some things you know the way you know weather coming.

*

The lamp hummed. Outside, rain had started again, soft against the window, and somewhere down the block the Anchorlight's last-call crowd was spilling onto the sidewalk with the particular loudness of people avoiding their homes. Jude turned pages with a letter opener, careful of the paper's damp-swollen edges, careful of everything.

The entries through March and early April of 2013 grew shorter. Tighter. The handwriting, which had been loose and looping in January, compressed itself, letters standing closer together like people in a crowd sensing danger. She wrote less about G. and more about being watched. *Someone went through my room. Not housekeeping — housekeeping folds the throw differently.* On April 12th: *I'm going to write down what I know and put it somewhere no one will look, because I don't think I'm going to be here much longer, and if I'm not, someone should find this.* On April 15th, the final entry in the wall bundle, her hand suddenly hurried, the pen pressed so hard it had torn through in places:

*If you're reading this, I didn't leave. Everyone will say I left. Check the dates. Check everything. He counts on people not checking.*

That was the last page of the bundle. Jude set it down and looked at it for a long time, the way you look at a door you're about to open knowing the house behind it has changed.

Because here was the problem. Here was the thing that had been sitting in his chest since the basement, cold as a stone swallowed whole: the bundle was not all one document. He had noticed it downstairs and dismissed it as damage, as damp, as twelve years against old plaster doing what water does. But now, under the lamp, with the diary laid open beside the bundle, he could see it plainly. The paper was different. The first eighty-odd pages were a consistent stock — cream, faintly ruled, the same brand throughout, aging uniformly. But the final signature of pages, maybe fifteen of them, bound into the same spine, were a half-shade whiter. Newer. As if the book had been opened and fed, like a bird's nest lined with stolen feathers.

And the dates on those newer pages continued past April 2013.

They continued for years.

Jude turned to the first of them and read the date twice, then a third time, mouthing it like a word in a language he'd almost forgotten: *June 4th, 2016.*

Three years after she vanished. Three years after the search parties, the candle vigil on the pier, the article in the *Saint Alder Sentinel* that ran under the headline VANISHED WITHOUT A TRACE and then, two weeks later, a smaller one: CASE GOES COLD. Marisol Vane — or someone claiming, in her voice, in her hand, to be Marisol Vane — had written an entry dated June 4th, 2016, describing the fog rolling in off the channel, describing the smell of the cannery, describing *the same gray coat hanging in the same closet.*

His tea had gone cold in its cup. He poured it out without looking, a habit of tidiness so deep it operated without him.

He read on. June 2016 became September 2016. There was a gap — months, sometimes a year — and then another entry, 2018, noting that the Meridian's elevator had finally been replaced, *the old cage gone at last, it used to stick between floors and I hated the jolt.* The elevator had been replaced in October of 2018. Jude had signed the work order himself. He remembered the contractor's coffee order. He remembered the jolt.

Another gap. Then March of 2021, during the worst of the empty season, when the town had been shut down and the hotel nearly empty: *The halls are so quiet you can hear the boiler thinking. He came by again tonight. He thinks I don't see him watching the building, but I see everything. I learned that from him, after all.*

Jude sat very still.

The refrigerator cycled on. Rain worked at the glass. Down the street, someone laughed too loudly and the sound broke apart in the wet air. He was aware, distantly, of his own heartbeat, steady and slow, the metronome he trusted more than any clock.

*He came by again tonight.*

The entries were not the ramblings of a woman alive in hiding — or they were not only that. They were addressed. Somewhere in the middle of the later pages the voice shifted, the way a letter shifts when the writer remembers who will read it. She began leaving instructions between the observations, small and sly, like notes passed under a door: *Check the guest register for the week of the regatta, 2014. Ask the night porter who signed in as Mr. Gray.* Once, chillingly: *You always double-lock your drawer. You'll want to check it after this.*

Jude's eyes went to the lower drawer of the concierge desk, twelve blocks away, locked inside the dark hotel, and his skin prickled with the certainty that whatever waited in that drawer had been waiting for him to understand exactly this.

Because that was the impossible arithmetic at the center of it all. The entries were dated after her death — after her disappearance, he corrected himself, discipline, discipline — and they contained details that could only be verified by someone who had been in Saint Alder continuously since 2013. The elevator. The boiler. The regatta register. Whoever wrote those pages had been here. Had been watching the Meridian. Had been watching *him*.

Either Marisol Vane had never left, and had spent twelve years writing into a wall she could not have reached —

— or someone else had written her, patiently, across a decade, feeding false pages into a sealed book like a man feeding coins into a machine, waiting for the wall to come down, waiting for the diary to be found, waiting for a particular reader with a particular memory to sit down under a particular lamp and start checking dates.

*He counts on people not checking.*

But someone had counted on him checking. Someone had built this entire apparatus — the wall, the oilcloth, the placement between studs where only a demolition crew would find it — as a message. And the message required a reader who knew the town the way the writer knew the town. There was exactly one such reader.

The lamp buzzed. Jude took off the glasses he didn't need and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations.

Then he went back to the beginning of the false pages and read them the way he read everything: slowly, twice, with a pencil.

*

It was past two in the morning when he found the entry that ended his ability to sit still.

It was near the end of the inserted pages — the newest paper, the least aged, though even that told him little except that whoever bound these in had access to old stock, or patience, or both. The handwriting here had changed subtly. Still Marisol's hand — the distinctive backward-leaning *f*, the way she crossed her *t*'s with a single impatient stroke — but looser now, faster, as if the writer had grown confident, fluent, comfortable wearing another woman's script the way you grow comfortable in a coat.

The entry was dated nine days ago.

Jude read it once at normal speed and then again with the pencil hovering, not writing anything, because there was nothing to annotate. There was only the fact of it, sitting on the page like a stone at the bottom of a clear glass.

*He's close now. Closer than he knows. In three days the tide will be low enough at Cannery Pier to see what the winter storms uncovered, and he'll have to come, because he can't stand to leave a record unbalanced any more than I could. If anyone is reading this — if you're the one who checks — be at the pier. Or don't be. But either way, by Friday one of us will finally be done hiding.*

Friday. Three days from now. Low tide at Cannery Pier at 6:14 in the morning — he knew that too, knew the tide tables the way he knew checkout times, because a concierge learns the sea's schedule along with the trains'.

Jude set the pencil down. His hand, he observed with detached interest, was not shaking. Twelve years of Tuesdays, twelve years of registering other people's arrivals and departures, other people's lies delivered across the front desk with a smile — none of it had prepared him for being written into someone else's ledger. And yet some cold, competent part of him, the part that had kept the green ledger current through two ownership changes and a flood, was already moving, already sorting: *verify the tide table, verify the paper stock, verify the ink age, verify the hand.*

Verify the hand. That was the thread to pull. Handwriting could be practiced — clearly it had been — but practice leaves seams, and seams show under magnification, and Jude knew exactly one person in Saint Alder who owned a loupe, a light box, and a grudge against unsolved cases: Sheriff Adaeze Okafor, who had worked the Vane disappearance as a rookie deputy in 2013 and had never, in twelve years, taken the maroon case file off the shelf behind her desk. He'd seen it there a hundred times, delivering packages, settling disputes over billing, standing in her office while she pretended not to notice him noticing. *Cold File 2013-0417.* She'd labeled it herself, he was sure, with the same stubborn precision he brought to his ledgers. Two watchers in a town that preferred to sleep.

She would not welcome what he had to tell her. She would like it even less that he'd handled evidence before the scene was processed — though technically the diary had come to him in the dust of an active renovation, technically Renzo had handed it to him, technically, technically. Okafor had a way of looking at the word *technically* the way a fisherman looks at a fish that's mostly bones.

It didn't matter. Tomorrow morning he would carry the diary to the sheriff's office in a plastic sleeve, and he would lay it on her desk beside the cold file, and he would say the sentence he had already begun composing in his head, the sentence that would change the shape of everything:

*Sheriff, the last entry is dated nine days ago — and it predicts a death on Friday.*

Outside, the rain thickened. Jude stood at his window with the lamp off, looking down at the wet street, at the few lit windows of the Anchorlight going dark one by one, at the black mass of the harbor beyond the rooftops where Cannery Pier reached out into the channel like a finger pointing at something no one had bothered to read.

Somewhere in this town, someone had spent twelve years writing a dead woman's diary by hand, entry by entry, year by year, keeping the story alive the way he kept his ledgers — faithfully, obsessively, waiting. Waiting for the wall to open. Waiting for him.

*By Friday one of us will finally be done hiding.*

Jude realized he was whispering the words aloud to the dark room, and that he already knew, in the place where he kept the things he wasn't ready to know, whose handwriting the diary had really been teaching him to recognize all along.

He just wasn't ready to say the name yet.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would take it to Okafor, and they would open the cold file together, and the name would have to come out of the dark whether he was ready or not.

He packed the diary carefully, wrapped it in a clean cotton shirt, and set it by the door next to his shoes — a concierge's instinct, everything staged for the morning's departure — and then he lay down on top of the covers, fully dressed, and listened to the rain, and did not sleep at all.
