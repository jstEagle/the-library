## 8. Pages That Predict

The rain came in off the water around nine that night, the kind of rain Saint Alder specialized in—not dramatic, not cleansing, just a fine gray insistence that made the town feel like a photograph left too long in a drawer. Jude Lockridge stood at the concierge desk with the diary open in front of him, a sheet of archival glassine between his fingers and the page, and read the entry dated March 4, 2019.

Six years after Marisol Vane walked into the sea—or was taken into it, or walked out of town on the last bus and was never seen again, depending on which version of the file you believed. Six years after the search dogs lost her at the tideline and the case went soft as old fruit.

The handwriting was hers. He was sure of it the way he was sure of most things: not with his heart, but with his eyes, trained for twelve years on the small betrayals of the human hand. The way she made her lowercase *g* with a flat descender, like a hook that had given up fishing. The way she crossed her *t*s with a single unhurried stroke, left to right, never doubling back. The way her ink pressure lightened at the end of a line, as though her thoughts ran out of breath before her sentences did. He'd spent enough evenings with the sample pages Sheriff Okafor had let him photograph—the grocery lists, the postcard to her sister, the half-finished letter found in her room at the Meridian—to know the hand the way he knew the sound of the freight elevator.

And the entry was dated March 4, 2019. Written in a fountain pen. In a hand too precise, too consistent, too *hers* to be forged.

Jude turned the page with the glassine and read it again.

*The fog came in early today. He thinks I don't notice him noticing me, but I've had twelve years of practice at being invisible in a small town. You learn the alphabet of it. I know which of the staff count the silverware and which count the guests. I know that Room 311 has been let twice this year to a man who signs the register as a name that isn't his. I know what he keeps in the wall.*

Jude read that last sentence four times. The lobby clock ticked behind him. Somewhere in the kitchen, the night cook was arguing with a delivery man about oysters, their voices rising and falling like weather. None of it touched him. He was somewhere else entirely—back in 2013, standing in this same lobby, watching Marisol Vane cross the marble with her canvas bag and her camera, the woman who had once told him, over a cup of the Meridian's terrible decaf, that every small town had an alphabet and the trick was learning to read it.

*What he keeps in the wall.*

The diary had been found behind a wall. During the renovation of the east wing, in a cavity between the studs of what had once been a linen closet on the third floor, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as a prayer. The foreman had called Sheriff Okafor before he'd called anyone else, which in Saint Alder was either diligence or theater, depending on who you asked.

But the diary was dated 2013. The last entry—the one the newspapers had quoted, the one that had made the case famous for a season and then forgotten—was dated October 11, 2013, three days before Marisol Vane was reported missing. The wall was part of the original 1927 construction. It had been sealed, as far as anyone knew, since the Nixon administration.

So how did a diary found in a sealed wall contain entries written six years after its owner disappeared?

Jude had turned that question over for two days the way a man turns a stone in his pocket, and tonight, alone in the lobby with the rain writing its long gray sentences on the windows, he finally let himself look at the answer.

There were two possibilities. Either the wall had been opened before—opened, written into, resealed—and someone in the trades knew about a cavity that appeared in no blueprint he'd ever seen. Or the diary had never been in the wall at all until recently, and its discovery was not an accident but a delivery.

A message.

He closed the diary and pressed his palms flat against the leather cover, feeling the faint ridges of the tooling, the way the spine had been broken in and re-broken. Then he opened his eyes and did what he always did when the world refused to make sense: he went to his records.

* * *

The records lived in a locked drawer beneath the desk, in eleven black notebooks with marbled covers, each one indexed by year and cross-referenced by a system of his own devising that no one had ever seen and no one would ever understand. To the staff, Jude was the concierge who remembered everything—your anniversary, your allergy, the name of your daughter's horse. What they didn't know was that the remembering was not a gift. It was a discipline, practiced nightly, in ink.

He pulled the 2013 volume first. Marisol Vane, guest, Room 214, checked in September 2, checked out—no. The entry read: *V., M. — 214 — Sept 2 – Oct 14 — no checkout. Key returned by housekeeping Oct 15, on the pillow, aligned with the headboard.* He remembered that. He remembered standing in the room after, looking at the pillow, at the key lying there like a period at the end of a sentence, and feeling the particular cold that comes when a person leaves a place the way you leave a sentence unfinished.

He had catalogued her for six weeks. Her comings and goings, her visitors (three, all women, all local), her phone calls (made from the lobby phone, always, though the rooms had phones—she'd said the lobby phone was "honest"), her habits (she photographed the harbor at low tide, every day, same hour, same pier). She had watched the town the way he watched the lobby. He had recognized it in her the way one swimmer recognizes another in open water.

He flipped forward through the years. 2014. 2015. The investigation, such as it was, had gone quiet by summer. Sheriff Danforth had retired; Okafor had come up from the coast with a reputation for closing cold files and a way of looking at Jude that suggested she'd already read his notebooks in her imagination and found them wanting.

Then, in the 2019 volume, his own handwriting: *Renovation, east wing, begins March 1. Crew of nine. Foreman: D. Pell.*

March 1. The diary's impossible entry was dated March 4. Three days after the renovation began.

Jude sat very still. The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere upstairs a guest ran a bath, and the pipes sang their old iron song through the walls—the same walls, he thought, that had kept their secret for six years or six weeks, depending on which lie you preferred.

He pulled the diary open again and turned past March 4. There were more entries. He had read them all, of course—he had read everything twice—but now he read them as a sequence, as a document with a purpose, and the purpose made his mouth go dry.

March 9, 2019: *He moved the boat today. The gray coat came to the pier at dusk and stood where the cannery used to be. He knows I know.*

March 14, 2019: *Room 311 again. He signed the log as R. Mott. R. Mott has been dead eleven years. I checked the cemetery myself.*

And then the last entry. The one that had made Sheriff Okafor sit down slowly in her chair when Jude showed it to her, the one that had turned a curiosity into an emergency.

*He'll do it again on the 22nd. Low tide, the pier, after the centennial fireworks. He always works when the town is looking at the sky.*

The 22nd. Three days from now.

Jude looked up at the lobby calendar, the brass frame, the card that the morning shift changed by hand. *June 19.* Three days to the centennial. Three days to low tide. Three days to a prediction written six years ago—or six weeks ago, or yesterday, it hardly mattered—in the hand of a woman who had been dead or gone since 2013.

The diary wasn't a record. It was a script. Someone was feeding pages into the file, into the wall, into the investigation, and every page was a move in a game whose board was the whole town. And the next move was scheduled.

* * *

He should have called Okafor. He picked up the phone twice. Both times he set it back down.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told her, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: the diary's entries weren't just predictions. They were *accurate*. He had spent the last two days verifying, quietly, without telling anyone, the way he did everything. The man signing the register as R. Mott—dead eleven years, and Jude knew it because he had attended the funeral and catalogued the mourners—had checked into Room 311 on the dates the diary gave. Twice. The gray coat at the cannery pier: Jude had seen it himself, twice this spring, a figure in a long gray coat standing at the pier's end at dusk, gone before you could cross the street. He had logged it both times, in his neat hand, filed under *anomalies*.

Whoever was writing the diary wasn't inventing the town's secrets. They were *reporting* them. Which meant the writer was here. Watching. And knew the town's alphabet fluently enough to write sentences in it that only a native speaker could verify.

There were perhaps five people in Saint Alder who could have written those entries. Jude had spent the evening narrowing it down, and every road led somewhere he didn't want to go.

He took out the 2019 volume again, and beneath the renovation entry he found what he'd been circling all night, the note he'd written on March 4 without knowing its weight: *Site visit, east wing, 4 p.m. D. Pell walked me through. Wall cavity found behind linen closet, 3rd flr. Diary recovered. Present: Pell, two crew, Sheriff Okafor, and —*

He had written the name and then, uncharacteristically, drawn a single line through it. Not scratched out. Drawn through, neatly, the way you cross out a word you've decided not to say aloud.

Jude stared at the line through the name for a long time. He remembered the afternoon of March 4 with terrible clarity, the way he remembered everything, whether he wanted to or not. He remembered who had been standing in the third-floor corridor in the plaster dust and the work lights. He remembered the moment the diary came out of the wall, wrapped in its oilcloth, and how the air in the corridor had changed—not with surprise, which would have been natural, but with something quieter. Something like satisfaction, quickly folded away, the way a dealer folds a good hand so no one sees it.

The person whose name he'd crossed out had not looked surprised. They had looked like someone receiving a delivery they had been expecting.

And they were the one who had taught him, years ago, over terrible decaf and long shifts, how to notice everything. How to read a lobby like a tide chart. How every small town had an alphabet, and the trick—the whole trick—was learning to read it.

Marisol Vane had taught him that. But Marisol Vane was gone. Which meant the person in the corridor on March 4, the person who had watched the diary come out of the wall with satisfaction folded behind their face, had learned the same lessons from the same teacher—or had taught them, once, to someone else.

The phone rang. Jude let it ring twice, then answered.

"Sheriff Okafor," said the voice on the line, flat and unhurried. "I've been going through the diary again. There's something you should see. The ink on the last three entries is different from the rest. Fresher. And Lockridge—" a pause, the sound of her breathing, rain on her end of the line too, "—the last entry isn't in Marisol Vane's handwriting at all."

Jude closed his eyes. Outside, the rain kept writing its long gray sentences on the windows, and somewhere across town, low tide was coming in three days, right on schedule.

"Whose is it?" he asked.

"That's the problem," Okafor said. "It's yours."
