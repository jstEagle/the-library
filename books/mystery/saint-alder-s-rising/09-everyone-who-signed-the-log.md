## 9. Everyone Who Signed the Log

The Meridian's guest registers lived in the basement, in a room Jude had privately named the reliquary: a windowless chamber off the old boiler corridor where the hotel kept what it could not bring itself to throw away. Eleven ledgers stood on steel shelving in strict chronological order, their spines stamped in faded gilt, and beneath them, in acid-free boxes, the loose registration cards from the years before the hotel went digital in 2019. Jude had spent the better part of two decades in this building, and he had spent the better part of this particular night among the dead paper of it, wearing white cotton gloves he'd bought at a pharmacy on Harbor Street because the archival supply shop had closed years ago and no one in Saint Alder noticed such things anymore.

He worked by lamplight, cross-referencing.

The diary lay open beside him on a folding table, propped flat between two brick paperweights he'd taken from the lobby fireplace. The pages Marisol Vane had written in the months before her disappearance were easy to date against the registers—she had been meticulous, almost compulsive, about noting where she was and whom she'd seen, and the hotel log confirmed her again and again. *Tuesday, March 12. Dinner at the Meridian, table by the window. The concierge remembered my name.* He had, too. He remembered it now. He had remembered it for twelve years, which was precisely the problem.

But the pages after the end—those were the ones that mattered, and those were the ones that would not reconcile.

Jude had built himself a system overnight, because systems were how he kept the world from sliding. A legal pad divided into columns: date of diary entry, event described, event as the Meridian's records remembered it, and a fourth column he had left blank until an hour ago, when he'd finally written a header for it in small, careful capitals: *WHO BENEFITS.*

The first three columns were damning enough.

The entry dated October 4, 2016—three years after Marisol Vane walked out of Saint Alder and out of every record—described a man checking into the hotel under a name she spelled out in full: *Corliss, Room 214, paid cash, asked twice about the fire exits.* Jude had pulled the ledger for that week. There was no Corliss in Room 214. There was no one in Room 214 at all; the room had been closed for a plumbing refit from late September through mid-October of 2016. Jude knew this because he had been the one to apologize to the two guests who'd wanted it, because it had the best light on the eastern face, and because Jude Lockridge apologized for the Meridian's small disappointments the way other men breathed.

The entry was wrong. Or the room was. And a diary that lied about rooms was a diary written by someone who knew the hotel—knew it well enough to be almost right, and not quite.

He turned pages with his gloved fingers, slow as a priest.

*November 19, 2017. A woman in a gray coat at the bar, alone, reading. Left a book behind. Never came back for it.*

Jude remembered the gray coat. He remembered it the way he remembered weather. But the woman in the gray coat had come back—had come back four times that winter, and had finally left in February, and Jude had carried her bag to the taxi himself. The diary had taken one true evening and amputated its ending. That was the signature he kept finding, now that he knew to look for it: not invention but surgery. Every fabricated entry was built from something real, trimmed at exactly the joint where the truth would have exonerated someone or implicated someone else. The forger hadn't just known the hotel. The forger had known *his* observations of the hotel, secondhand or otherwise, and had cut them to fit.

He wrote in the fourth column and did not like what his hand produced.

* * *

By four in the morning the reliquary was cold enough that his breath showed faintly in the lamplight, and Jude allowed himself the ritual: he stood, stretched until his spine cracked like green wood, and made the walk down the boiler corridor to the staff kitchen for coffee he would not finish. The corridor smelled of lime and old iron. The Meridian at night had a sound he thought of as the building's pulse—the boiler's tick, the elevator's distant sigh, the settling of a structure that had been standing since 1911 and intended to outlast them all. He had come to find it comforting. Tonight it sounded like a witness clearing its throat.

He carried the coffee back and did not drink it.

Instead he did the thing he had been circling all night the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. He pulled the registration cards for the summer of 2013—the last summer of Marisol Vane—and laid them out across the folding table in the order they'd been signed, which was not the order the sheriff's office had ever bothered with. Sheriff Okafor's file organized the world by relevance. Jude's organized it by sequence, and sequence was where lies got tired.

One hundred and eleven cards. He had counted them twice, because counting was a form of prayer and he was not above praying to arithmetic. One hundred and eleven people had checked into the Meridian between June 1 and September 14, 2013, the window the sheriff's office had established for the disappearance. Okafor's file—Jude had seen it, had held it, two nights ago in the sheriff's office with the smell of cold coffee and old tape—listed forty-one persons of interest drawn from those cards. Witnesses, staff, the fishermen, the real estate man, the cousin from Tacoma. Forty-one names, and the file had gone cold around them like a pond going to ice.

But Jude had not stopped at forty-one. Jude had never stopped at forty-one. He had spent twelve years noticing, and what he noticed now, laying the cards side by side under the lamp, was the thing he should have noticed in 2013 and had not, because he had been thirty-one years old and grieving in the private way of men who grieve for guests: three of the one hundred and eleven had signed with handwriting that leaned the same way.

Not the same hand. That would have been crude, and whoever had written the false diary entries was not crude. But three cards, spaced across the summer—July 2, August 9, August 27—bore signatures with the same distinctive break in the capital letter, the same hesitation before the final stroke, as if each writer had learned penmanship from the same strict teacher. A Mrs. E. Danforth of Portland. A Mr. T. Voss, address illegible. A Ms. R. Calloway, no address at all, paid cash, stayed one night, requested a room at the back.

Jude sat with that for a long time.

Then he did what he always did with a fact that frightened him: he checked it against everything else he knew. He pulled the bar tabs from the same weeks—copies he had kept, because the hotel discarded them and Jude had never in his life been able to discard anything. Mrs. Danforth had taken her meals in her room. Mr. Voss had eaten at the bar on the one evening of his stay, sitting in the corner seat with his back to the wall, and had asked the night porter one question: whether the hotel kept records of long-term guests. Ms. Calloway had not appeared in the bar or the dining room at all. A guest who never eats, Jude had written in his own notes at the time, in his own small capitals. *Watch this one.*

He had watched her, for one night, and then she had gone, and he had filed the note away in the cabinet of his mind where twelve years of Tuesdays lived, and he had not thought of it again until the wall came open and Marisol Vane's diary slid out into the dust and the light.

Three signatures. One penmanship. One question about records. And a diary, found behind a wall, containing pages written after the writer's death in a hand too precise to be forged—too precise, the sheriff's examiner had said, and Jude now understood that the examiner had been precisely wrong. The hand was not too precise to be forged. The hand was too precise, period. It was a hand that had been *practiced*, the way Jude had practiced his own observations, the way a musician runs scales. Someone had learned to write as Marisol Vane the way Jude had learned to read a lobby: patiently, obsessively, over years.

And someone had needed to get those pages behind the wall. Which meant someone had needed access. Which meant someone had needed to be, at some point, on the other side of the front desk.

Jude pulled the employment files.

The Meridian's staff roster for 2013 was short—nineteen names, and he had signed the payroll himself as acting manager every second Friday when Mr. Ellery's health failed. He read the names the way he read everything, twice, and then a third time aloud in the empty room, because the ear catches what the eye forgives. Housekeepers, a chef, two porters, the night auditor, the gardener, Ellery himself. And then the last file in the drawer, the one that was not quite an employment file because the woman in question had never been quite an employee.

Consultant. That was the word they'd used. Hospitality consultant, retained spring of 2012 through winter of 2014, to review the Meridian's guest experience, its security, its—Jude read the phrase twice—*its practices of observation and record-keeping.*

His coffee had gone cold in the mug and he did not remember pouring it.

* * *

He knew her. Of course he knew her. He knew her the way you know the person who built the house you live in, the way you know the voice that taught you your own name. She had come to the Meridian when he was twenty-nine and hungry in the way that hotel men are hungry—not for money, but for mastery—and she had stood in the lobby with her gray coat still buttoned and looked at him for a long moment and said, *You watch people. That's rare. Most people only look.*

Her name was Ash Meridian. The hotel's name was a coincidence of history—the founding family had died out decades ago, or so everyone said—and she had arrived with a briefcase and a reputation and a way of standing very still while the world moved around her. For two years she had taught him things he had thought he would take to his grave as gifts: how to read a registration card for what it concealed; how a guest's shoes told you their intentions before their mouth did; how the most important person in any room was the one pretending not to be there. She had taught him the alphabet of small towns, she had said, was written in who signed the log and who refused to.

She had left in the winter of 2014, without ceremony. A final invoice, a taxi, a wave from the window that he had returned like a fool, grateful, devoted. He had built his whole method on her bones. Every system in this room, every ledger cross-referenced against every instinct, was a cathedral built on her foundation, and he had never once questioned the architect.

Now he sat in the cold with three signatures leaning the same way and a diary full of surgically altered truth, and he made himself write the question he had been refusing to write since midnight. He wrote it in his small capitals, because capitals were for things that had to stand up straight no matter what.

*WHO BENEFITS.*

And beneath it, because the fourth column had never been about strangers:

*WHO TAUGHT ME THAT THE LOG IS THE TOWN?*

The boiler ticked. The elevator sighed. Somewhere above him the Meridian stood in the dark with its hundred and four rooms, and Jude thought about the phrase from her final report, the one he'd filed and forgotten, the one that had seemed like wisdom then and seemed like a confession now: *A hotel that keeps perfect records keeps perfect power.*

The diary's next entry was dated three days from today. It described, in Marisol Vane's practiced hand, a death at the cannery pier at low tide.

And Jude realized, with the cold certainty of a man reading his own handwriting in someone else's book, that the entry had been left for him to find—that the whole wall, the whole diary, the whole twelve-year architecture of it, had been built by the only person alive who knew exactly how he would read it.

The lamp buzzed. He turned to the last page of the false entries, and there, in the margin, in a hand he had seen a thousand times across a lobby desk, were three words he had taught himself never to expect again:

*You're close, Jude.*
