## 7. Heir Apparent

Mara Villalobos had been in Harrow Creek for exactly four hours when she decided the town was hiding something, and another forty minutes before she decided Sebastian Okoye was hiding it.

She'd taken the Wren estate file as a favor to her old firm — a probate dispute, a missing body, a life insurance payout frozen by a court order. Open and shut, her supervising partner had said. Six weeks of billable work in a town that smelled like cut grass and denial. Mara had driven out from the city with a rental car full of case law and a thermos of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration, and she had expected boredom.

What she got instead was a records office that looked like a church, an insurance investigator who never wrote anything down, and a sheriff who watched her cross the town square like she was checking Mara's teeth for livestock.

The estate's counsel had retained her specifically because she was nobody. No history with Harrow Creek. No fondness for its founding families. No reason to look at Elias Wren's empty coffin and see anything but what the paperwork said: a legal anomaly, an exhumation gone strange, a claim that couldn't be paid until someone proved what was in the ground.

But Mara had spent eleven years as a paralegal learning one fundamental truth about documents: they lie beautifully, and then they slip.

She found him on her second morning, in the reading room of the records annex, standing over an open box of files with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a funeral viewing. He didn't hear her come in — or he pretended not to. He was tall, mid-forties, with the stillness of someone who had trained himself out of fidgeting, and when he finally turned, his eyes did something she'd only seen once before, in a deposition from a witness who turned out to have been coached: they went somewhere else first, some internal room, before coming back to her.

"You're the paralegal," he said.

"Villalobos. Estate side." She set her bag on the table without asking permission. "You're the investigator who doesn't take notes."

"I take notes."

"Where?"

He tapped his temple. "Up here."

"Convenient."

"Occasionally inconvenient." He closed the box lid with a soft, deliberate sound. "Mostly convenient."

Mara pulled out a chair and sat, unfolding her copy of the exhumation order. "Then you won't mind walking me through it. Ten years ago, Elias Wren is buried in front of half the town. Last month, a claimant contests the death benefit — says the death certificate doesn't match the burial record. Court orders exhumation. Coffin opens. Sandbags." She looked up. "Whose claimant? Because I've read the filing four times and the name is redacted in three places, which is not how redaction works unless somebody wanted it unreadable."

Something flickered across his face — not surprise, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man finding a word he'd been searching for.

"That's the right question," he said.

"It's the *only* question. Everything else is theater. The sandbags are theater. The court order is theater. Somebody filed that contest knowing it would crack this town open like an egg, and they redacted their own name badly enough that we'd notice, which means they *wanted* us to notice." She leaned forward. "So. Who is it?"

Sebastian Okoye studied her for a long moment. She had the distinct feeling of being read — not skimmed, but read, cover to cover, footnotes included.

"You came here yesterday," he said. "You checked into the Bellamy House under your firm's travel account, then changed to paying cash. You ate dinner at the diner and sat facing the door. You asked the waitress about the Wren family and she told you Elias Wren built the grain cooperative and died beloved, and you let her talk for six minutes because you were counting how many times she used the word 'beloved.' It was five."

Mara kept her face still, mostly. "And that tells you what?"

"That you don't trust anyone here either. Which means we can work together." He opened the box again. "The claimant's name isn't in the file. It was removed after filing. I remember the original — I filed the intake myself, ten years ago, when the premiums were still being paid."

"Premiums." She caught it instantly. "Past tense. Whose premiums?"

"Elias Wren's. A whole-life policy through Mutual Fidelity, written in 1971. Someone kept paying on it for years after his death. Cash payments, no payer of record." He slid a folder across the table. "I can't prove any of it anymore. The payment ledger pages were cut out of the archive last week. Cleanly. With a blade."

Mara took the folder and felt the temperature of the room change. "Someone got into the records office."

"Someone got into the records office *before me*," Sebastian said, and there was something underneath the words — not fear, but its cousin. "That hasn't happened in twenty-two years."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and it went the way most first collaborations go: badly, then well.

Mara's method was aggressive. She pulled everything — probate filings, tax assessments, the grain co-op's articles of incorporation, cemetery plot maps going back to 1954 — and spread them across two tables until the reading room looked like a conspiracy board. Sebastian's method was to stand at the edge of the paper field and *remember*, supplying dates and names and page numbers from a memory that made her feel, professionally speaking, both grateful and deeply uneasy.

"The burial was October 14th," he said, eyes half-closed. "Open casket. Four hundred people through the funeral home over two days. He looked — " A pause. "He looked wrong. Too smooth. I was thirty-four and I thought grief was making everyone sentimental. Now I think the mortician was working around something."

"Around a body that wasn't there."

"Or around a body that needed help looking presentable." He opened his eyes. "There's a difference, and I can't tell yet which one it is."

"That's a horrible sentence."

"Yes," he agreed mildly. "It is."

By five o'clock they had established three things. One: the estate's assets were larger than the will accounted for, by a margin of roughly two hundred thousand dollars in unexplained land value. Two: the grain co-op's original parcel had been assembled from smaller holdings between 1958 and 1963, and the deed transfers for those smaller holdings were — every single one — marked as exempt from public index, a designation so rare Mara had seen it twice in her career and both times it had meant fraud. Three: Elias Wren had no surviving family on record. No children. No spouse living. No next of kin.

Which was why, at 5:40 in the evening, Mara dropped the fourth thing on the table like a stone.

"Wren had a grandchild."

Sebastian went very still. It wasn't dramatic stillness; it was the stillness of a machine recalculating. "That's impossible. I've read the estate file. There's no issue listed, no—"

"There's no issue *listed*. Look at this." She pulled a photocopied page from her bag — she'd requested vital records from the county seat that morning, driving forty minutes each way while he'd assumed she was at lunch. "Elias Wren, married 1952 to Constance Wren, née Aldous. One child: Thomas Wren, born 1954. Thomas married in 1976 — marriage license, county records. One child born 1979. Birth certificate lists the mother as Diane Wren. Then—" she flipped to the second page, "—Thomas Wren dies, 1981, car accident. Diane remarries within the year and moves out of state. And the child's birth record gets amended in 1984. Name change. Mother's petition, father deceased, no objection filed."

"Amended to what?"

Mara slid the page across. "That's the part I can't get past. The amendment order sealed the new name. Juvenile confidentiality, standard for the era. The child exists in the record as a black bar." She watched him read it. "Elias Wren buried a granddaughter somewhere out there under a different name, and whoever drafted his estate papers — and I've read the drafting attorney's work, it's meticulous — simply never mentioned her. As if she didn't exist. As if someone made sure the file said so."

Sebastian set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

"I archived that estate file," he said quietly. "Eleven years ago. Every page. I can recite it. And there was no amended birth record in it."

"Then either it was never filed—"

"Or it was removed." His jaw tightened. "Same as the premium ledger. Same as the claimant's name. Someone has been editing this town's memory, and they're using my archive to do it."

Mara studied him. Here it was, she thought. The moment where the local expert either closes ranks or opens the door. She'd bet her fee on the door.

"Who has access?" she asked.

"Three people. Me. Sheriff Brandt. And Ruth Calloway, my archive partner. Twenty-six years. She taught me the filing system." He said it flatly, but Mara heard the cost of the flatness. "She also warned me yesterday to drop the case."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you counted the waitress's 'beloveds' and paid cash at the hotel, and because you found in one day what I missed in twenty-two years, which means you're either brilliant or you started from a place I couldn't — outside." He met her eyes. "I need outside. My memory tells me what *was*. It can't tell me what should have been. You read documents like they're lying to you. Mine trust me too much."

It was the closest thing to humility she'd heard in this town, and she decided, on the spot, to trust it approximately sixty percent.

"Fine," she said. "Partnership. But rules. One: I subpoena everything before you 'remember' it, so we've got originals. Two: nobody knows what we find — not the sheriff, not your Ruth, not the waitress. Three:" she gathered her black-barred birth certificate and held it up, "we find the granddaughter before whoever's editing these files does. Because if they've erased her once, they'll erase her again, and next time it won't be with a seal order. It'll be with something less reversible."

Sebastian nodded slowly. Then he paused, head tilting, that internal-room look crossing his face again.

"What?" Mara said.

"The funeral," he said. "October 14th, ten years ago. Four hundred mourners. I remember faces — I always remember faces. And there was a woman near the back, third pew from the rear, who cried like family cries. Not politely. Ugly. The kind of crying that embarrasses people around it." His voice dropped. "Everyone assumed she was staff. Catering. But catering staff don't wear mourning clothes, and they don't leave before the casket does."

"You remember her face?"

"Perfectly. That's the problem. I've run it against every resident of this town for ten years and she's not in it. She was a stranger at Elias Wren's funeral, grieving like blood." He looked at the black bar on the birth certificate. "Born 1979. She'd have been thirty-one that year."

Mara felt the spark catch — the first real one, the kind that turns two people with separate agendas into something like a team.

"A granddaughter who knew she was a granddaughter," she said. "Who came to say goodbye to a grandfather the town never knew she had."

"Or a granddaughter who was saying goodbye," Sebastian said grimly, "to the man who erased her mother's family from the record. There's more than one reason to cry like that at a funeral."

Outside, the light was going amber over the square, and somewhere across town the records annex stood dark, holding whatever it held. Mara packed her files with quick, efficient hands.

"First thing tomorrow," she said, "we pull the sealed amendment. Judges unseal things for estate interests all day long. We get a name."

Sebastian was already shaking his head, and the look on his face stopped her cold.

"We don't need a judge," he said. "I remembered something else just now — something I filed and forgot I filed, because it seemed like nothing at the time. Three months ago. A records request. Handwritten, mailed in, no letterhead. Someone asking for certified copies of *every document relating to the Wren birth amendment of 1984.*"

Mara stared at him. "And?"

"And the return address on the request," Sebastian said, "was a post office box in a town two hundred miles east. Under a name I've been turning over all evening trying to place — because I know I've seen it before, recently, somewhere it didn't belong." He picked up his keys. "The request was denied, pending proof of interest. But whoever sent it knows the amendment exists. And Mara — the handwriting on that request form?"

He held the door open onto the cooling evening.

"It matches the handwriting on the premium payments. The ones made in cash, after Wren was already dead. Our secret granddaughter has been keeping her grandfather's policy alive — and now she's looking for herself in the archives. Which means whoever cut those ledger pages out isn't hunting us."

He stepped out into the dusk ahead of her, and Mara caught the last of it over his shoulder:

"They're hunting *her*."
