## 9. The Fire at the Annex

I smelled the smoke before I saw the fire.

That's the thing nobody tells you about a memory like mine — it doesn't just hold faces and dates and the exact number of steps between the records office and the annex (one hundred and twelve, counting the cracked one). It holds smells. And at 2:47 in the morning, standing in my kitchen with a glass of water I hadn't drunk, I caught something on the night air that did not belong: creosote and burning paper and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the particular chemical sweetness of accelerant.

I have catalogued that smell exactly once before in my life. I was nine years old, and the Okoye family store on Delancey Street burned to its foundations while my father stood in the street holding a ledger he'd run back inside for. He never told me why the ledger mattered more than his own hands. I understood later. Some things you can rebuild. Some things, once they're gone, take every truth with them.

The annex was eleven blocks away. I made it in six minutes.

* * *

Fire does strange things to light. It doesn't illuminate so much as it interrogates — throwing orange interrogation beams across the faces of everyone gathered behind the sawhorses, and I knew those faces, all of them, because I know every face in this town of four thousand people, and what I saw in them as I arrived was not horror.

It was curiosity.

The old records annex sat at the end of Founders Row like a tooth that had been left in too long — a 1930s brick building the county had stopped using for anything but overflow storage twenty years ago, when Ruth Calloway and I moved the active archives into the modern office above the bank. Every claim older than 1995 lived in that building. Every policy, every death certificate, every premium ledger from the town's first century. Elias Wren's founding-era files. The displaced family's land records. Everything the cipher had begun pointing toward, stacked in acid-free boxes on steel shelving that some county engineer in 1974 had certified as fire-resistant.

Fire-resistant. Not fireproof. There's a difference, and the difference was currently eating through the roof.

"Sheriff." I found Ilsa Brandt at the command truck, her face lit in flickering orange, jaw set the way it set whenever she saw me coming and couldn't think of an excuse fast enough. "How long has it been burning?"

"First call came in at two-twenty." She didn't look at me. She watched the roofline, where the flames had found the ventilation gap and were working it like a seam. "Night watchman at the feed store. You?"

"I smelled it."

"You *smelled* it." Now she looked at me, and there was something in her expression I'd been seeing more and more lately — the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Eleven blocks, Sebastian."

"Accelerant," I said. "Under the woodsmoke. Gasoline or kerosene, poured low, probably along the east wall where the windows are boarded. The fire's climbing from the floor up, not spreading across — see how it's columning at the northeast corner? That's where it started."

She stared at me for three full seconds. Then, grudgingly, the way she gave me everything: "Arson investigator says the same thing. Won't confirm till morning." A pause. Her voice dropped. "You want to tell me why somebody would burn down a building full of forty-year-old insurance paperwork?"

"Because paperwork is the only thing in this town that can't lie forever."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have right now."

The heat reached us even at fifty yards, a dry pressure against the face like a hand pushing back. Behind the sawhorses, the crowd murmured. I scanned it out of habit — Mrs. Ferro in her robe, the Delgado brothers, Pastor Lund with his arms folded tight, Mara Villalobos standing apart from everyone with her phone out, recording, her eyes finding mine across the firelight with a question in them I couldn't answer yet.

And then I stopped scanning. Because a thought had entered my head and refused to leave, the way the true ones always do.

Nobody had called me.

* * *

Let me explain what that means, because it took me another hour to understand it myself.

When the Wren exhumation order came through, Sheriff Brandt called me within the hour — resentfully, but she called. When the missing pages turned up in the Wren death file, she showed up at my door. This town knows I'm the man who remembers. If a building full of the town's oldest secrets caught fire in the middle of the night, someone — the sheriff, a deputy, Mara, anyone — should have thought: *Sebastian will want to know.*

Unless someone made sure the news reached me late.

Or unless someone wanted me to arrive at a specific time. To a specific scene. To stand in front of a specific crowd, smelling like smoke, looking exactly like a man with something to hide.

I filed the thought where I file everything — precisely, coldly, in the cabinet at the back of my mind marked EVIDENCE AGAINST MYSELF. It was getting crowded in there. The planted initials in the altered files. The shifted dates only three people could have shifted. Someone was building a case against me the way I built cases against claimants: patiently, document by document, using my own archive as the murder weapon.

And now they'd burned the archive itself. Which meant —

"Sebastian." Mara appeared at my elbow, phone still recording, voice low. "You're doing the thing where your eyes go still and everyone around you gets nervous. What is it?"

"The fire isn't destruction," I said. "It's editing."

"Explain."

"Think about what burns tonight. Everything predating 1995. Wren's original policies — if they're still here. The founding ledgers. The land transfer records for the homestead parcels." I watched a section of roof collapse inward and felt the crowd flinch as one body. "Whoever set this isn't erasing evidence. They're deciding which version of history survives. After tonight, whatever we claim existed in that building exists only because we say so. Our word against ash."

Mara lowered the phone slowly. In the firelight her face had gone very sharp, very awake. "Then we'd better hope something survived."

"Something will survive." I heard my own voice go flat and certain, and I didn't know yet where the certainty came from. "It always does. Fire's lazy. It takes what's easy."

* * *

They let the structure burn until four, then knocked it down to embers by dawn. By five-thirty, when the arson investigator finally waved off the tape for the salvage crew, the annex was a black ribcage open to the grey sky, steam rising off the wreckage like the building exhaling its last hundred years.

Sheriff Brandt met me at the tape line. She looked like she hadn't slept, which she hadn't, and like she'd been waiting for me, which she had.

"One condition," she said. "You don't touch anything without gloving. And you don't go past the interior line."

"Agreed."

"And Sebastian —" she hesitated, and for a moment the resentment dropped away and there was just a tired woman standing in wet ash, "— if you know why this happened, if any part of you knows, you tell me. Not tomorrow. Not after you've worked it out. Now."

"I don't know why," I said. "I know *who*. Or close enough. But knowing who without proof is just a rumor wearing my face, and we both know whose face they've been borrowing lately."

She held my gaze a moment longer, then jerked her chin toward the ruin. "Go. Be careful."

I went in through what had been the loading door on the west side, stepping where the investigator chalked, into a world remade in charcoal and water. The smell was total — soaked ash, burnt glue, the ghost of gasoline. The steel shelving units stood in rows like survivors of something, their contents either collapsed into black drifts or fused into brick-hard masses of carbonized paper. The east wall, where I'd predicted the origin point, was gone entirely, open to the sky.

Row A: county tax records, 1912–1968. Gone. Row B: church registries and cemetery plots. Gone. Row C — the row where the pre-1995 claims overflow had lived, including the boxes Ruth and I had transferred during the great reorganization of 2011, the boxes containing duplicate copies of the town's earliest policies —

I stopped walking.

Because I remembered. Of course I remembered; I remember everything. On March 14th, 2011, Ruth Calloway and I had spent a Saturday moving forty-one boxes from the annex to the new office, because she'd said — I could hear her voice saying it, hear the rain against the van windows — *"These originals shouldn't live in a building with a leaky roof, Sebastian. Paper remembers longer than people do. Let's keep the important ones close."*

Forty-one boxes. We'd catalogued them together. Box 17 through Box 22 had contained the founding-era policies. Elias Wren's original book of business. The documents the cipher pointed to. They weren't in the annex.

They were upstairs in our office, three feet from my desk, and had been for thirteen years.

So why did the arsonist burn the annex?

Unless they didn't know. Unless whoever set the fire believed the originals were still here — believed it because they'd checked the catalogue, and the catalogue said Row C, Shelf 3, Boxes 17–22, ANNEX — because thirteen years ago, someone had updated the location index and gotten it wrong, or half-wrong, or deliberately wrong —

Ruth.

The name arrived in my head with no emotion attached, the way all my worst discoveries arrive, clean and clinical, before the feeling catches up. Ruth had overseen the index. Ruth had signed off on the transfer manifest. Ruth, who warned me twice to drop the case. Ruth, whose alibi for the exhumation window was airtight in the way that alibis are airtight when someone builds them in advance.

But if Ruth thought the originals burned tonight, then the fire wasn't Ruth's work. The fire was meant to destroy what Ruth had protected. Two hands on this board, moving against each other, both of them convinced they were playing the same side.

My head was spinning and I kept walking, because there was one more thing to check, and my feet already knew the way.

* * *

The far corner of the annex, past Row F, had been a dead zone since before my time — a windowless alcove where the 1950s flood-damaged files had been quarantined and forgotten. Nobody catalogued the flood boxes. Nobody wanted them. Even I had only entered the alcove twice in fifteen years, and both times to retrieve nothing.

Which is why what I found there hit me the way it did.

A single box, sitting on the lowest shelf, dust disturbed around its base in a fresh semicircle, as though it had been slid out recently and pushed back. No water damage. No charring — the alcove had shielded it, barely, the fire having died four feet short. Plain grey cardboard, no label except one written in ballpoint in a hand I knew better than my own:

**WREN — ORIGINALS — DO NOT FILE**

Ruth's handwriting. Ruth's instruction. A box hidden outside the system, outside the index, outside the catalogue — invisible to anyone who searched the archive honestly, invisible to an arsonist working from the records, invisible to everyone in the world except the two people who knew it existed.

One of whom was me. And I had never seen this box in my life.

Someone had put it there recently. Recently enough to leave a dust shadow. And they'd used Ruth's handwriting to do it — a good forgery, maybe, or maybe not a forgery at all.

I knelt. I gloved. I lifted the lid.

Inside, wrapped in plastic sheeting against the damp, lay a thick folder of documents — brittle paper, typewritten headers, wax-sealed signatures. Original policies. Elias Wren Insurance & Assurance, est. 1947. Policy numbers matching the cipher fragments Mara and I had decoded two nights ago at her kitchen table until dawn. And clipped to the inside cover, one additional page that stopped my breath:

A premium schedule in Wren's own hand, running forty years forward — premiums paid on a dead man's life, entries continuing year after year in different inks, different pressures, different hands. Ten years past the burial. Cash payments, each annotated with a single initial in the margin.

The same initial, over and over, in handwriting I had spent the last week convincing myself I couldn't place.

I knew that initial. I had filed payments bearing it. I had stood in the archive with the receipt in my hand and drawn a blank on the payer's face — the first hole in my perfect memory, the crack everything else had poured through. Because the payer had never shown me a face. Only a signature. Only an initial. Only a hand I'd trusted for fifteen years, signing for a dead man's life.

The heat of the ruined building pressed in around me. Somewhere behind me, the salvage crew shouted coordinates. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock.

I had come to the fire expecting grief — survivor's guilt, the loss of a century of paper, the town's memory going up in smoke. Instead I was kneeling in the ashes holding the one thing the fire was supposed to destroy, delivered to me like a message, planted in the one blind spot only an insider could exploit.

*They wanted me to find it.* That was the thought that finally landed, cold and complete. Whether it was bait, or confession, or a dying woman's last act of protection, I couldn't yet say. But someone had walked into this building before the fire, in the dark, and placed the truth exactly where the flames couldn't reach and where my instincts would carry me — because whoever did it knew how my mind works.

Knew I'd check the alcove. Knew I'd trust Ruth's hand. Knew I'd kneel.

Only one person alive knew all of those things about me.

I closed the box, stood, and walked out of the annex into the grey morning light, past the sheriff, past the crew, past Mara waiting at the tape with a question already forming on her lips.

"Mara," I said. "We need to talk about Ruth Calloway. Tonight. And Mara —"

I glanced back once at the smoking ruin, at the box under my arm that should not exist.

"— I think she's trying to tell us she's sorry."
