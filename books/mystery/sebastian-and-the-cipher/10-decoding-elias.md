## 10. Decoding Elias

**Sebastian**

The records office at midnight smelled like cold coffee and old paper, and I loved it the way some men love a church.

Mara Villalobos had claimed the long table by the east window, spreading Wren's founding-era ledgers across it in a fan. She'd rolled her sleeves to the elbow, tied her hair back with what looked like a rubber band from a newspaper, and set up an operation that would have made a quartermaster proud: three lamps, two legal pads, one thermos of something that smelled like it could strip paint.

"You said you found anomalies in the premium columns," she said, not looking up. "Show me."

I pulled the 1898 ledger toward me and opened it to March. "Here. And here. And here." My finger moved down the column without my having to search — the numbers were already in my head, had been since Tuesday, arranged in their rows like faces at a funeral. "Every seventh entry, the final digit is wrong. Not illegible. Wrong. A four where the arithmetic says seven. A nine where it should be two."

"Bookkeeping errors."

"In a ledger kept by a man who never made bookkeeping errors. I've read eleven hundred pages of Elias Wren's hand. The man ruled his columns with a straightedge and wrote his eights like a draftsman. He didn't slip."

Mara leaned over the page, close enough that her shadow fell across the numbers. "So he did it on purpose."

"He wanted someone to find it. Eventually. Someone patient."

"And you're nothing if not patient." She glanced up, and there was something in her face that wasn't quite a smile — more like a door opening an inch. "Okay, Okoye. Walk me through it. What's the pattern?"

I'd been turning it over for three days, mostly while pretending to sleep. "The wrong digits, read in sequence, form a number string. But it's not a date or an account number — I checked both. It's too long and it repeats every forty-one entries." I tore a sheet from her legal pad before she could object and wrote out the sequence as it lived in my memory:

4-9-2-7-1-8-3-6

"That's eight digits," she said. "Repeating?"

"Repeating, but shifted. The second cycle starts one position later. Third cycle, two positions later." I wrote them stacked:

49271836
92718364
27183649

"It's a rotation," Mara said slowly. "Like—" She stopped. Her pen tapped the table twice. "Like a combination lock. You're not reading the numbers, you're reading how they move."

I stared at the three lines and felt something turn over in my chest, that specific click when a shape I'd been holding wrong suddenly fits the hand. "A key," I said. "It's not the message. It's the key to the message."

"To what message?"

"To all of it." I swept my arm across the ledgers. "Forty years of premium columns. Thousands of entries. If this sequence is the key, then every digit in these books might be carrying freight we never checked."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Outside, a truck ground past on the county road, headlights sweeping the ceiling, and I watched her decide something. When the light passed, she said, "My grandmother used to say the town was built on a bargain nobody would name out loud. I thought it was a saying. Like people say about any small town."

"What do you say now?"

"Now I think maybe my grandmother was being literal." She squared the ledgers in front of us. "Let's read the mail."

* * *

**Mara**

We worked until two in the morning, and I'll be honest about what I expected: I expected the great Sebastian Okoye, the man whose memory was a local legend, to simply *see* the answer, the way people said he could walk into a room once and own it forever.

He couldn't. That surprised me, and then it didn't, because watching him work I understood the legend was wrong in an interesting way. He didn't see answers. He saw everything else — every digit, every smudge, every inconsistency — and the answer was whatever survived after everything else got eliminated. It wasn't magic. It was labor, performed at a speed that looked like magic from the outside.

My job was slower and dumber: I did the arithmetic. For each entry, subtract the key digit from the recorded premium digit, using the rotation pattern he'd mapped. Write down the remainder. Most remainders were garbage. Some weren't.

At quarter past two, my column produced its first word.

"Sebastian." My voice came out strange. "Look at row sixty-one."

He came around the table fast. I'd written the decoded remainders down the margin in my cramped hand, and there they were, where the encoded column had hidden them:

H-O-M-S-T-E-A-D

"Homestead," he read. Then, quieter, like he was testing whether the floor would hold: "Whose homestead?"

"We don't know yet. Keep going."

We kept going. The next decoded fragment came from the 1901 ledger, buried in a run of fire-insurance premiums: *NORTH QUARTER*. The next, from 1903: *DEED 114*. The next was just a single letter, repeated across six consecutive entries, and when Sebastian laid the six pages side by side and read the letters in order, he went very still.

"A-C-K-E-R," he said. "Acker. The Ackers were—"

"I know who the Ackers were." Everyone in Harlow knew who the Ackers had been, in the way everyone knows about things that are no longer there. There was an Acker Road that led nowhere now, ending in scrub brush and a collapsed wellhead two miles north of the reservoir. There was a line in the fourth-grade history unit about the family that sold their land to Elias Wren in 1903 and moved west. Sold. That was the word in the textbook. *Sold.*

Sebastian sat down slowly, like a man whose chair had moved.

"The deed number," he said. "114. The county archive keeps the original grantor-grantee index. If Wren recorded a deed from the Ackers—"

"Then it was a sale, and the textbooks are right, and this is a very boring evening." I was already pulling on my jacket. "But if there's no deed—"

"Then he insured land he never bought." Sebastian stood, and I saw something cross his face that I'd only seen on clients signing papers that ended marriages: the look of a man reading a document he wished said something else. "Forty years of premiums, Mara. On land he never owned. You don't pay insurance on stolen property unless the stealing is the point. Unless the insurance is how you make the theft *clean*."

"How does insuring it clean it?"

"Because a policy requires provenance. An insured parcel has a paper trail — appraisals, title searches, affidavits. You build the file, and the file becomes the history. Forty years later, nobody asks whether the first deed existed. They ask whether the policy lapsed." He grabbed his coat off the back of the chair. "The county clerk's office opens at eight."

"It's two-thirty."

"Then we have five and a half hours to finish decoding." He was already back at the table, pulling the 1905 ledger. "There's more in here. I can feel the shape of it. The fragments aren't random — they're getting longer, more confident. He was getting better at hiding it. Or more desperate to be found."

I sat back down. I told myself it was because the case needed it.

That was maybe half the truth. The other half was that I'd grown up on Acker Road — on the street named for people who were a paragraph in a textbook — and if Elias Wren had done what the ledgers were whispering, then every building in this town sat on a story somebody had paid to bury. Including the courthouse where I worked. Including my own apartment.

"You okay?" Sebastian asked. He'd noticed. Of course he'd noticed; noticing was the whole engine of him.

"My grandmother's maiden name was Acker," I said.

His hands stopped moving.

"She changed it when she married. Everybody did, back then. It never meant anything to me. It was just a name on a christening record I looked up once for a school project." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it does when I'm deposing a hostile witness and the hostile witness is myself. "Keep decoding, Okoye."

* * *

**Sebastian**

By dawn we had the whole skeleton of it, laid out on Mara's legal pads in her tight hand and my margins full of cross-references I wouldn't need written down but wrote anyway, for her.

Elias Wren arrived in the valley in 1897 with a surveyor's license and no capital. By 1910 he owned four thousand acres. The ledgers explained the difference. Beginning in 1901, he carried fire and casualty policies on parcels the county records could not connect to him by any deed, mortgage, or lien — and the premiums on those phantom policies were themselves insured, reinsured through a company in Cincinnati that folded conveniently in 1912, taking its files with it. The cipher fragments named the parcels. *North Quarter. Acker Homestead. Deed 114.* Deed 114 did not exist. We would confirm that at eight o'clock, but I already knew it the way I know a forged signature — the absence had a shape.

And the premiums were never paid by Wren.

That was the detail that turned my stomach, standing in the grey light at the east window while Mara slept twenty minutes in her chair, her chin on her fist. Every premium on the phantom policies was remitted by a third party — marked in the margin with a small notation, *per H.A.* Per H.A. Forty years of payments, *per H.A.*, and the last entry, dated 1941, in a shakier hand: *per arrangement concluded.*

H.A. Hannah Acker? The family Bible in the historical society listed a Hannah Acker, born 1885, died — the record said — 1902, age seventeen. Died the same year the first phantom policy was written.

Except the beneficiary fragment we hadn't decoded yet. Except the fact that dead girls don't pay premiums for forty years.

Mara stirred. "Time?"

"Quarter to six."

She rubbed her face and looked at the legal pads, at the little empire of evidence we'd built out of wrong digits, and her expression hardened into something I was beginning to recognize as her working face — the one that came after the human face, armor over skin.

"So the founder of this town took a family's land, buried the girl who could have contested it — one way or another — and spent forty years laundering the theft through insurance paper so thick that nobody ever dug to the bottom of it." She said it evenly, like reading a charge into the record. "And the town put his name on the school."

"On the school, the library wing, and the reservoir," I said. "Wren everything."

"And nobody ever caught it because the proof was locked in a cipher inside the one archive nobody imagined could hide anything." She looked at me then, and there was an edge under the exhaustion. "You've filed ten years of claims in this room, Okoye. You memorized these ledgers. How did you miss this?"

The question landed harder than she probably intended, because I'd been asking it myself since two a.m., and I had an answer I didn't like.

"I didn't miss it," I said. "I saw the wrong digits years ago. Flagged them as clerical errors in a memo. Filed the memo." I closed the 1898 ledger. "And then I forgot to wonder about them. Which I don't do. I don't forget to wonder."

"You're telling me your perfect memory has a blind spot exactly the size of this cipher?"

"I'm telling you someone taught me not to look." I heard the words come out and wished I could take them back, because of where they pointed. Three people had full access to this archive. Me. Sheriff Brandt. Ruth Calloway. And Ruth had taught me the archives, entry by entry, year by year — had taught me what mattered and what was dust, which anomalies were clerical noise and which were worth a memo.

Ruth had told me, more than once, that the founding-era ledgers were dust.

Mara was watching me do that arithmetic. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to; the silence between us had gotten fluent overnight.

At eight o'clock we drove to the county clerk's office in two cars, because Mara still didn't entirely trust me and I respected that. The grantor-grantee index for 1903 filled a single bound volume. I found the page in four seconds flat — I'd read the volume cover to cover during a title dispute in 2019, and it was all still there, every entry in its place.

Grantors A through C. Adams, Aldous. Ashford, Peter. Baker, Cornelius.

No Acker. No deed 114. No transfer of the north quarter, ever.

Behind me, Mara read the page over my shoulder, and I heard her breathing change. "It's real," she said. "It's actually real. He stole it. All of it. The whole town is sitting on—"

She stopped. Because I'd gone still, and she'd learned overnight what my stillness meant.

Something was wrong with the page. Not missing — altered. Entry 113, Baker, Cornelius, was correct; I could see it in my head from 2019, down to the clerk's ink blot in the lower corner. But the ink of the blot was different now. Fresher. And between Baker and the binding, where my memory held a blank half-inch of margin, there was now a faint ghost of erased writing — something removed carefully, professionally, within the last few days.

Someone had gotten to the county index before us. Someone who knew exactly which page we'd need, and had cleaned it just ahead of our arrival.

"Mara," I said quietly. "Don't touch anything. Go outside and call Sheriff Brandt."

She looked at the page, then at me. "What is it?"

"Whoever emptied that coffin," I said, "isn't following behind us anymore."

I touched the ghost of erased ink with one fingertip, careful as a man touching a wound.

"They're reading over our shoulder."
