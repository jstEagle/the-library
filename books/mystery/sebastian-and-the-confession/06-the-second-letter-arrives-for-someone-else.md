## 6. The Second Letter Arrives for Someone Else

The bell above the bookshop door had a cracked note in it, a small wounded sound that Astrid had been meaning to fix for three years and never had. On the fourth morning after the letters arrived, it rang at nine minutes past eight, while he was still crouched behind the counter with the day's first cup of coffee going cold beside the register, and he came up too fast, spine protesting, heart already running ahead of him.

He had not slept well. That was putting it gently. He had lain awake in the flat above the shop listening to the rain work its way through the guttering, and every time he closed his eyes he saw Cora Vane's face as it had looked in the photograph on Edith's mantel—seventeen years old, sunlit, laughing at something just outside the frame—and then he saw his own signature at the bottom of the incident report, neat and blue and damning, closing the case like a lid.

So when the bell rang, some animal part of him braced for Bramholt. For a cruiser idling outside with its light bar off. For the chief's voice, warm as bathwater, saying *Astrid, old friend, we should talk.*

Instead it was Nell Merrick.

She stood just inside the door with her umbrella dripping a dark circle onto the floorboards, and she looked—there was no kinder word for it—haunted. Nell Merrick was sixty-one and had carried her husband's death the way she carried everything, upright and quietly, in a good wool coat and sensible shoes. Aldous had been gone fourteen months now. The pharmacy on Wren Street had been shuttered since spring, its green cross sign dark, its windows papered over with a notice from the bank that everyone in town had read and no one had mentioned to her face.

"Astrid," she said. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not. She held them clasped around her handbag like a woman holding shut a door against wind. "I need—I'm sorry to come so early. I saw your light."

"You're always welcome." He came around the counter. "Tea? I've got coffee that's been sitting since seven, which is either an insult or an act of mercy."

"No tea." She did not move further into the shop. Rain ran off her umbrella and pooled. "I've been standing across the street for twenty minutes trying to make myself come in. I want you to understand that. I nearly went home twice."

"Nell."

"I found something." She said it fast, as if speed could blunt it. "In Aldous's papers. I've been sorting them—the bank wants the premises cleared by autumn, and there's thirty years of receipts and ledgers and God knows what, and I've been going through box after box like a widow in a bad play, and last night—" She stopped. Breathed. "Last night I found a letter."

The word landed between them like a dropped glass.

Astrid kept his voice level, though everything beneath it had begun to move. "What kind of letter?"

"That's the thing." She reached into her handbag, then hesitated, her fingers white on whatever was inside. "I don't know what kind. That's why I'm here. Because you'll know. Because if anyone in this town will know what this is, it's you." A pause, and then, lower, almost ashamed: "And because I couldn't bear to open it again alone."

*Again.* The word snagged in him. She had opened it more than once. She had sat up all night, probably, reading it until the words wore grooves in her.

"May I?"

She drew it out and set it on the counter between them rather than handing it over—an instinct he recognized, the instinct of someone who has decided to show evidence and cannot quite bring herself to surrender custody of it.

It was a plain envelope, cream-colored, heavy stock. Unremarkable except that Astrid's stomach dropped through the floorboards of his own shop, because he knew that stock. He had held an identical envelope four mornings ago and turned it under the lamp and weighed it in his palm and felt the particular expensive blankness of it. The same paper. The same size. And the address—his own address, written in the same hand he'd spent three nights studying: a slanted, disciplined script, fountain-pen ink, the loops of the *l*s closed like little doors.

Except this one was addressed to *Dr. A. Merrick, Wren Street.*

And it had been posted eleven months ago. Eleven months before Aldous died.

"Nell," Astrid said carefully, "has anyone else seen this?"

"No one. I found it in a box of prescription duplicates, of all places—tucked inside a folder marked 2013, which is why I nearly threw it out unopened. I thought it was patient correspondence." Her eyes came up to his, and they were wet and furious and frightened all at once. "Astrid, what is it? Because I read it, and I read it again, and it's a confession. It's a confession to killing Cora Vane. In my husband's handwriting—or near enough to break my heart—and I have spent the whole night trying to decide whether my Aldous murdered a girl and hid it from me for ten years, or whether something worse than that is happening, because I cannot for the life of me imagine what could be worse."

Astrid looked down at the envelope and felt the shape of the whole morning change.

"I need to tell you something," he said. "And I need you to hear all of it before you say anything else."

* * *

He made tea anyway. It gave his hands something to do while he decided how much truth a frightened widow could hold, and in the end he decided she could hold all of it, because Nell Merrick had the kind of face that got harder, not softer, when it was afraid.

He told her about the two letters. The confession. The second one—*the witness is still breathing; you have until the equinox*—which he had told no one about, not even the postmistress's gossip mill. He watched her take it in, watched her set her cup down with exaggerated care so it wouldn't rattle against the saucer.

"And you didn't go to the police," she said.

"The police are Bramholt's police."

"Yes," she said simply. "They are." She wrapped both hands around the cup. "Go on."

He told her about the ink. About the three evenings he'd spent with a magnifying loupe and the confession letter, comparing the hand against everything he could find—old pharmacy advertisements, a signed get-well card Aldous had sent Edith Vane years ago, the memorial book from the library. The similarity was close enough to hurt. Close enough that Astrid had spent two days believing, with a sick certainty, that Aldous Merrick had written the confession—that the town's gentlest doctor had carried Cora Vane's death inside him for a decade and finally mailed it out into the world before dying.

But there had been things wrong with it. Small things. The *t*-crosses were slightly too long. The pressure of the nib varied where Aldous's writing, in every sample Astrid could find, flowed with a pharmacist's even hand. And the ink itself—he'd taken a cutting to a friend in the county who knew more about pens than was strictly healthy—was a Montblanc royal blue, whereas Aldous had used Pelikan 4001 his entire working life; Nell confirmed it now with a hollow little laugh, saying he bought it by the case and complained whenever she suggested anything else.

"Somebody imitated him," Astrid said. "Well enough to fool me for two days. Well enough to fool anyone who wasn't looking for a forgery."

"But why?" Nell's voice cracked on it. "Why forge a confession in a dead man's hand? What does anyone gain?"

"That's what I intend to find out." He turned the envelope toward her. "Nell, look at the postmark."

She leaned in. Grey-blue ink, half faded: the regional sorting office, dated eleven months back.

"Eleven months ago," Astrid said, "Aldous was alive. This was sent to him while he was alive. Which means whoever wrote it wanted him to read a confession to murder—a confession in his own hand—and wanted him to carry that however he carried it, for whatever time he had left." He let that sit. "Fourteen months later, he was dead."

Nell had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice was thin and precise, the voice of a woman walking along a ledge. "His heart. The doctor said his heart."

"Heart failure can be a very convenient diagnosis in this town. You know that better than most."

"Don't." The word came out sharp. Then softer: "Don't say that yet. Not until we know. If I start down that road and it isn't true, I'll never find my way back to him being innocent of anything."

"Then we won't say it yet," Astrid agreed. "We'll do this properly. One fact at a time."

"One fact at a time," she repeated, like a prayer or a discipline, and he understood suddenly that she would be all right—not fine, perhaps never fine, but upright. She had been married to a man who falsified things for a living, in a small way, in the way pharmacists sometimes did for friends and powerful people; Astrid had suspected it for years without ever having cause to act on it. She might know more than she was saying. Or she might know nothing at all. Either way, she had walked through the rain to bring him this instead of burning it, and that told him what he needed to know about Nell Merrick.

"There's one more thing," she said. She reached into the handbag again and produced a second object, smaller: a sheet of cream stationery, folded once. "This was with it. Inside the same envelope. I didn't notice it at first—it was stuck to the back of the letter."

Astrid unfolded it. Four lines, in the same forged hand:

*You are not the only one who knows what happened at the lake. Others received their copies this week. Consider what your silence purchased, and whether it was worth the price. You have until the equinox.*

The paper trembled slightly in his grip, and he realized the trembling was his own.

*Nell,* he started to say, *this says others received—*

"I know what it says." She had gone pale but her eyes were dry now, burned clear. "I've had all night to read it, Astrid. All night, and most of the walk here." She straightened her shoulders inside the good wool coat. "Others. Plural. Copies. Someone has written a confession to Cora Vane's death and mailed it—to you, to my husband, and to whoever else was part of it. And they're giving us all the same deadline." She laughed, short and terrible. "*Us.* As if I were part of it. As if knowing nothing for thirty years makes me guilty too."

"It may be exactly the point," Astrid said slowly. The thought was assembling itself as he spoke, piece by piece, cold and clean. "Think about who received these. Not the people who did it—the people who *enabled* it. The ones who signed things. Looked away. Took payments they didn't ask questions about." His own name rose in his throat like bile; he swallowed it. "Someone isn't confessing. Someone is *distributing*. Like a pharmacist filling prescriptions." He heard himself say it and winced. "Forgive me."

"No," Nell said. "Say it. It's true, isn't it? Whoever wrote this thinks in lists. Names, addresses, doses." She stared into her cooling tea. "Aldous used to say the town was like a formulary. Everything in its place, everything measured out, nothing dispensed without authority." Her jaw tightened. "I thought it was a joke."

* * *

After she left—he walked her to the door, held the umbrella over her to the corner, watched her go with her spine straight as a lamppost—Astrid stood behind his counter for a long time, doing what he had done for twenty-five years as a detective and had sworn never to do again: building the thing in his head.

Two letters to him. One to Aldous Merrick, eleven months ago, with a follow-up note. How many more?

He laid it out on the counter in his mind like evidence bags on a table. If the sender had targeted him, they knew his role—knew he had signed the ruling, knew about the doubts he'd buried. If they'd targeted Aldous, they knew about the autopsy, or the prescriptions, or whatever arrangement had existed between the pharmacy and the chief's office. Who else had touched the case? He made the list without wanting to, the way you probe a bruise.

Chief Bramholt, who had leaned on the ruling. The coroner, Dr. Pell, retired now to the coast, who had signed the death certificate with suspicious speed. The lodge owner, Harrow, whose boathouse had been locked and whose staff had all developed sudden amnesia. And—

And whoever had paid for it all to be quiet. Because somewhere under the accidental drowning and the closed file and the grieving mother who was never allowed to see the full report, there had been money. There was always money. He had never followed it. That had been the deal he'd made with himself without ever admitting he'd made it: *don't follow the money, and you can keep telling yourself you were only tired.*

Now someone was following it for him. Someone was mailing the ledger of the town's sins out one cream envelope at a time, timed—why?—to some clock only they could see. The equinox. Three weeks away. And Daniel Bramholt coming home next month to announce his candidacy, if the county paper's rumor column could be believed.

*Detonating the secrets systematically.* The phrase arrived in his head fully formed, and he distrusted how neatly it fit. But it did fit. This wasn't blackmail—no demands had been made, no money requested. Blackmail wanted silence. This wanted the opposite. This wanted everything opened at once, all the envelopes torn open on the same morning, the whole rotten formulary spilled across the counter of the town.

Which meant the sender didn't want money or revenge in any simple sense.

They wanted a reckoning. Public, total, and on a schedule.

And they had chosen Astrid Underhill—disgraced, retired, hiding behind a till full of unsold novels—as either an ally, a target, or a fuse.

He looked down at the counter, at Nell's envelope lying beside his own two, three pieces of cream-colored paper like three cards dealt from the same deck. Eleven months ago, someone had put a forged confession into a dying man's hands and let him sit with it. Whatever Aldous Merrick had done in those last months—whatever guilt or fear or resolve had moved through the closed rooms of the pharmacy on Wren Street—it had begun with that envelope. Had the letter killed him? Had it *freed* him to die? Astrid turned the question over and could not answer it, and the not-answer sat in his chest like a stone.

One thing he could do. He took out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote, in his own careful hand, the list of everyone connected to Cora Vane's death, alive or dead, near or far. Twelve names. He looked at them for a long moment.

Then he circled the six who were still living in Hollowmere, and asked himself the only question that mattered:

*How many mailboxes on this list had already been opened?*

Outside, the rain had thickened. Down the street, a boy in a yellow slicker was pedaling the postal route, satchel bouncing, sliding envelopes into slots one by one with the cheerful indifference of someone delivering nothing but bills. Astrid watched him stop at the house opposite—Bramholt's house, the chief's flag flying damp and defiant over the porch—and drop something through the slot.

Something small. Something cream-colored, maybe. Impossible to tell at this distance.

Astrid stood very still behind his counter, the bell silent, the coffee cold, and understood that the town had just become a board game and someone else was moving all the pieces.

Three weeks to the equinox. And the post was still coming.
