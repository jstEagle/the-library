## 14. Equinox Eve

The letters had arrived on four different days, spread across three different months, and it had taken Astrid Underhill nearly all of that time to see what was printed on the corner of every envelope.

Not written. Printed. The return address was the same each time—*P.O. Box 114, Harrow Bay*—but the postmark was the tell, and he had been too busy reading the words inside to study the ink outside. Tonight, with the equinox less than a day away and the bookshop dark around him except for the green-shaded lamp on the counter, he laid all four envelopes side by side on the glass like specimens on a dissection tray, and finally he looked.

Harrow Bay was a resort town forty miles up the coast, a place of saltwater taffy and shuttered cottages, and its postmark was a double-ring stamp that read *HARROW BAY / 9:30 AM* in the same tired font every day. But these weren't Harrow Bay postmarks. Three of the four read *GREYHAVEN*. The fourth, the earliest, read *GREYHAVEN* as well, though the strike had been smeared by rain into something almost illegible—almost, but not quite, and Astrid had spent twenty years training himself to read the almost.

Someone was using a Harrow Bay post office box as a shield and mailing the letters from Greyhaven, ninety minutes north along a coast road that clung to the cliffs like a nervous thought.

Greyhaven. He said the name aloud in the empty shop and it sounded like something out of an old chart—*grey* for the granite, *haven* for the lie of safety the harbor offered boats that trusted it. He had never been. He knew it only as a smudge on the coastal maps he sold in the travel section, a village of a few hundred souls that the highway had forgotten in the sixties and that had apparently preferred it that way.

The witness was still breathing. The witness was in Greyhaven, or close enough to it to walk letters to a postbox in the fog.

Astrid took off his reading glasses and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until the dark behind his lids bloomed orange. Tomorrow was the deadline. *You have until the equinox.* The phrase had sat in him for weeks like a swallowed hook, and every morning he woke expecting to feel it pull. But pull toward what? The letter hadn't threatened him, not exactly. It had *summoned* him, the way a coroner's summons summons—without permission, without appeal. And now, with the deadline arrived, he understood that he had a choice with only one real shape to it: he could stay in Harrow's Crossing, behind his counter, among his alphabetized ghosts, and wait to find out what the equinox would bring to him—or he could drive north and meet it himself.

He had spent ten years waiting for things to come to him. It was how a man hid.

He locked the shop at nine, stood a moment in the rain-gray street with his collar up, and went home to pack a bag he had no logical reason to pack. A change of shirt. A toothbrush. The notebook with the three sets of footprints. And, after a long pause at the shelf by the door, the ledger Nell Merrick had given him, wrapped in a flannel shirt at the bottom of the bag like a thing he was smuggling across a border. Which, in a sense, he was.

He did not sleep. He sat in the armchair by the cold fireplace with a glass of whisky he never finished and listened to the house tick and settle, and somewhere past two in the morning he caught himself speaking aloud to the dark, to Cora Vane, to the girl he had failed to look at properly a decade ago.

"I'm coming," he said. "I should have come ten years ago. Forgive me the difference."

The house said nothing. The whisky said nothing. Outside, the wind had begun to come up off the water with a low, patient sound, like something breathing at the door.

* * *

He left before dawn, and the fog was already waiting for him.

It lay over Harrow's Crossing like a held breath, thickening as he drove out past the last streetlights, and by the time the road began to climb toward the headlands he was moving through a white world with the wipers going and the headlights reduced to two pale smears that seemed to illuminate nothing so much as their own failure. The coast road north was a bad road in good weather—single lane in places, guardrails rusted through, shoulders that fell away into gorse and then into nothing. In this fog it was a road for people with reasons.

He had a reason. He held it in front of his mind the way a man holds a lantern: *the witness is still breathing.* Everything else—the fear, the memory of Bramholt's soft voice in his office, the image of his own name waiting in a ledger he had already read—everything else he folded away and sat on, the way he had once sat on doubt, and looked at how that had ended.

The fog did strange things with time. He would round a bend and a farmhouse would materialize, complete and detailed, a lit window, a dog barking somewhere unseen—and then it would be gone behind him as if it had never existed, and he would drive for ten minutes through pure white with nothing but the sound of the engine and the sea, far below and invisible, working the rocks. Twice he stopped at pull-offs to let a logging truck groan past, its amber lights swimming toward him like jellyfish, and twice he sat with his hands on the wheel and felt the truck's passing shake the car, and thought: *this is what it is to be small in a large machine.* He was driving into a story that had already killed one woman and exiled another, that had bent a police chief and a doctor and a coroner and, yes, a detective, all of them ordinary men who told themselves ordinary lies—and he was doing it alone, in fog, on a road with no guardrail worth the name.

There was a version of him, ten years younger, who would have called someone. A journalist, a state trooper, anyone. That version had died in a conference room with Bramholt's hand on his shoulder and a scandal in his lap that he still, to this day, was not entirely certain he hadn't deserved. The version that remained had learned the hard arithmetic of small towns: you call for help, and help arrives wearing a familiar uniform, and the man inside it knows exactly who signed the drowning report.

So: alone. He had made his peace with it somewhere on the headlands, or told himself he had.

The fog began to thin as the road descended toward the coast proper, not lifting so much as losing its appetite, and by seven the world resolved in grays and greens: wet stone walls, sheep standing in fields like punctuation, the sea suddenly present at his left, vast and indifferent, glittering under a sky the color of dishwater. And then the road turned a final headland and dropped, and there was Greyhaven.

He had expected a village. What he saw was a held place—a crescent of slate roofs around a stone harbor, a church tower, a breakwater with a light at its end, fishing boats tilted on the mud of the ebb tide like sleeping animals. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys and was immediately flattened seaward by the wind. It was beautiful in the way of places that had stopped trying to be anything but themselves, and Astrid sat in the parked car at the top of the hill for a full five minutes and looked at it, because some part of him understood that he was looking at the last ordinary thing he would see for a while.

There was a shop at the foot of the hill with a post office sign in the window. Of course there was. He had known there would be, the way you know the murderer in the third act.

* * *

The shop smelled of bread and paraffin and old newsprint. A woman behind the counter was weighing out flour for a fisherman, and a radio on the shelf behind her was giving the shipping forecast in that calm, liturgical voice that made gales sound like psalms. Astrid browsed the narrow aisles—rope, tinned goods, postcards of the harbor—until the fisherman left, and then he set a postcard of Greyhaven's breakwater on the counter and said, casually, to the bell above the door and the fog still clinging to his coat:

"I'm trying to trace a correspondent. Letters I've been receiving—return address is a box in Harrow Bay, but the postmarks are all from here."

The woman's hands did not stop moving. She was perhaps sixty, with a face like weathered oak and eyes that had looked at a great many things across this counter and forgotten most of them on principle.

"Post office doesn't discuss its customers," she said. "Even retired ones."

"I'm not from the post office."

"No," she agreed. "You're not. You've the look of a man who used to be something official and is trying very hard not to look it." She finished with the till, rang it shut, and only then looked at him fully. "Four letters, was it? There'd be four."

The floor seemed to shift very slightly, the way a boat shifts. "You've seen them?"

"I've seen the hand that wrote them." She began rearranging a tray of pencils that did not need rearranging. "Every Tuesday for a month, come the morning boat, and post a letter, and buy a stamp, and never say a word to a soul. I mind it because nobody posts letters here anymore except the funeral home." She looked up. "Small woman. Dark coat. Kept her collar up even inside. Eyes like she was counting the exits." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down. "You'll be the one the letters were to, then."

Astrid felt the old instinct rise—the detective's reflex to give nothing away, to ask rather than answer—and set it down deliberately, like a tool he was learning to stop using. "Yes," he said. "I'm the one the letters were to."

"Then you'll want the house on the cliff path. The one with the blue door, past the church. She walks up from the harbor every evening at dusk, rain or no rain, and she stands at the rail a while, looking at the water." The woman turned to face the shelf, taking down a tin, her back to him, her voice going flat and practical. "I never asked her name and she never gave it. But I'll tell you what I told myself the first week she came: that's a woman posting letters she's been carrying a long time. Whatever's in them, it isn't malice. Malice posts from the harbor and takes the afternoon boat back. That one walked up the hill like a woman laying flowers."

Astrid stood very still. "You're telling me where to find her."

"I'm telling you where a woman takes her evening walk." She set the tin down and finally turned, and there was something in her face now that was not the shopkeeper at all—something older and wearier and kinder. "This village keeps its secrets because it's had to. But it doesn't keep them for other people's convenience, and it never kept them for men from Harrow's Crossing." She nodded once, toward the door, toward the fog-bright street. "Dusk's at half six. You'll want to be early. The cliff path's a devil in the dark."

He bought the postcard. He wrote nothing on it. He paid with coins and his hand was not quite steady, and the woman watched it happen and said nothing, and the bell above the door rang him out into the wind.

* * *

The day was a long gray wait, and he spent it the way he had once spent the hours before a warrant was served: walking, watching, letting the place tell him what it was.

Greyhaven in the off-season was a village holding its breath. He ate breakfast at a café where three old men stopped talking when he came in and resumed it, carefully, when he left. He walked the harbor wall and read the memorial stone by the slipway—*the men of Greyhaven lost to the sea*—and counted nineteen names and noticed that the newest was eleven years old, and thought about how a town like this would regard a stranger asking questions, and decided the old men in the café had probably already telephoned someone. Good, he thought. Let her know I've come. Let her decide whether to open the blue door.

He climbed to the churchyard in the early afternoon and stood at the wall looking down at the harbor, and the wind came off the sea with salt in it, and he found himself doing the thing he had sworn off doing, which was talking to the dead.

"I signed the report," he said quietly. "I want you to know that I'm not pretending otherwise anymore. Not to you, not to your sister, not to the inquest if it comes to that. I looked at the file and I saw the edges didn't fit and I signed it anyway, because a man with a chief's hand on his shoulder can convince himself that a drowned girl is a tragedy and not a crime, if the alternative is losing everything." The wind took his words and flung them at the sea. "I'm done losing things on purpose. It turns out it costs more than the truth."

A gull screamed somewhere and wheeled away, and he took it, if he was honest, as the closest thing to absolution the coast was likely to offer him.

He walked the cliff path at five, while the light was still holding—a narrow track of packed earth and sheep-cropped turf above a fall of dark rock, the sea booming below with a sound like a great door being tested. The house with the blue door sat where the path bent inland: small, stone, squat against the wind, a garden gone wild and beautiful with sea pinks, a single window lit against the coming dark. No car in the lane. No name by the door. Just the blue paint, freshly done, vivid as a wound in the gray.

He did not knock. He had thought about this all afternoon, turning it over the way he'd turn a case over in the old days, and he had concluded that a woman who had spent years gathering proof against the most respectable families in Harrow's Crossing, who had summoned him by letter and given him a deadline, would not want him hammering on her door at dusk like a debt collector. She would want to see him first. She would want the advantage. She had earned the advantage.

So he found the spot the shopkeeper had described—a rail of weathered timber at the path's edge, above the long drop to the water—and he leaned on it, and he waited for dusk, and dusk came down over Greyhaven like a lid being lowered.

The light went from gray to pewter to a deep blue iron, and the harbor lamp came on at the end of the breakwater, a small yellow defiance, and the first stars showed and were immediately smothered by cloud rolling in off the sea. The temperature fell. He stamped his feet, put his hands in his pockets, felt the ledger's wrapped shape against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and watched the path.

She came at half six exactly, as promised, though he suspected she had been watching him for an hour from some window and had chosen her moment the way she chose everything.

She came up the cliff path with her collar up and her stride long and unhurried, a small woman in a dark coat, and she did not startle at the sight of him, did not break stride, did not do anything so ordinary as hesitate. She simply walked to the rail six feet from him and put her hands on it and looked out at the dark water as if he were a fence post that happened to have arrived that morning.

"You got my letters," she said. Her voice was low and even, with a roughness in it, like wind over stone.

"Four of them," Astrid said. "I read every word. I checked the postmarks. I checked the ink. I checked the footprints you left in my own files when you took them." He turned to look at her, and she turned her head, and in the blue dark he could see her eyes, and they were Cora Vane's eyes, and they were not Cora Vane's eyes at all—harder, and older, and lit from somewhere deep with a fire that had been burning a long time without anyone to warm.

"You signed the report," she said. It was not a question, and it was not an accusation. It was a door, held open.

"I did," Astrid said. "And I've come to un-sign it. However that has to happen."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the sea said everything, booming and booming against the rocks below. Then Lark Vane—the woman who had been dead for ten years, the witness who was still breathing—nodded once, toward the house with the blue door, and said:

"Then you'd better come in, Detective. There's something you need to hear before the equinox. And there's a car parked down the lane that's been sitting there since noon, and it isn't from Greyhaven."
