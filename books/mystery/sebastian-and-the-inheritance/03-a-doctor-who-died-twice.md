## 3. A Doctor Who Died Twice

The death certificate arrived at Ulyana's motel room in a manila envelope that smelled faintly of mildew and old toner, the way all official documents in Grayhollow seemed to smell, as though the town's paper trail had been stored in a root cellar for a century. She spread it across the desk beneath the lamp's yellow cone and let her memory do what it always did: photograph.

Dr. Emmett T. Voss. Licensed 1961. Cause of death: cardiac arrest secondary to congestive heart failure. Time of death: 11:47 p.m., October 3rd. Attending physician's signature — a looping, confident *E.T.V.* with a flourish on the terminal V that swept back under the name like a scythe.

She had seen that signature before. Not once. Twice.

Ulyana closed her eyes and pulled the first image forward from the archive of her mind: the wall of certificates in the funeral home's front office, framed and laminated, the undertaker's professional credentials displayed like hunting trophies. Among them, third from the left, a medical license bearing the same sweeping signature. She could render it stroke for stroke. The ink pressure heavier on the downstrokes. A small tremor in the crossbar of the T that suggested either age or haste.

And the second image — newer, stranger. Last night, unable to sleep, she had asked the motel clerk, a boy of perhaps nineteen with acne mapped across his jaw like a constellation chart, whether there was a doctor in town she should know about, some official she'd need to liaise with regarding the exhumation order. He'd shrugged and said Grayhollow hadn't had a doctor since Doc Voss passed on.

"Passed on when?" she'd asked.

"Before my time. My mom says he delivered half the town, though. Died — oh, must be two years back now. There was a big funeral. Whole town turned out."

Two years back.

Ulyana opened her eyes and looked at the certificate again. October 3rd. This year. Signed by a man who had been in the ground — or in a coffin, or wherever the people of Grayhollow kept their dead — for twenty-four months.

Her pen hovered over her notepad. She wrote one line, boxed it, and underlined it twice:

*Dead men don't certify deaths.*

Then, because her mind refused to leave a thread loose, she added:

*Unless someone is holding the pen for them.*

* * *

The county records office occupied the old Carnegie library building on Ashfall Street, its sandstone facade furred with lichen, its steps worn into shallow bowls by generations of feet. Inside, the air was cool and dust-heavy, lit by green-shaded lamps over long oak tables. A woman named Mrs. Abernathy — seventy if she was a day, reading glasses on a beaded chain, hands like crumpled tissue paper — presided over the vital records with the serene authority of a woman who had outlasted every elected official in the building.

"I need everything you have on Dr. Emmett Voss," Ulyana said. "Death record, obituary, burial location."

Mrs. Abernathy peered at her over the glasses. "You with the insurance?"

"I'm with the truth," Ulyana said, and immediately regretted how it sounded. "I'm an investigator. Aldous Wren's claim."

Something flickered behind the old woman's eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition. As though Ulyana were the fourth person this month to ask, and the others had been less polite about it.

"Aldous Wren." Mrs. Abernathy said the name the way people in mill towns said the names of weather systems. She rose, unhurried, and led Ulyana into the stacks, where the smell of aging newsprint thickened until it was almost edible. "Voss died in March. Two years ago. Heart gave out in his garden, they say. Found among his tomatoes." She pulled a bound volume of the *Grayhollow Courier* from a shelf and set it on a reading table with a thump that raised ghosts of dust. "March fourteenth edition. Front page. He was beloved."

The obituary ran nearly a full column, bordered in black. Ulyana read it once and owned it forever: *Emmett Theodore Voss, 84, physician to three generations of Grayhollow families... survived by no immediate family... services at Wren Chapel Cemetery... In lieu of flowers, the family requests donations to the Grayhollow Volunteer Fire Department.*

No immediate family. Which meant no son or nephew or grieving colleague with a similar hand who might plausibly have signed anything. No one whose pen could wander onto a death certificate by accident or inheritance.

"Who handled his estate?" Ulyana asked.

Mrs. Abernathy's lips thinned. "There wasn't hardly anything to handle. Little house on Corvus Lane. Sold at auction. His practice records went to the county for retention." She paused, and then, as if deciding something, added: "You'll want to talk to whoever filed the paperwork on Mr. Wren. That would've come through Harlan Pike's office. Pike handles everything in this town worth handling."

"Pike. The lawyer."

"The lawyer, the executor, the notary, and half the town council," Mrs. Abernathy said dryly. "In Grayhollow we don't believe in separating our powers."

Ulyana copied the date of Voss's death, the funeral home listed (Halloway & Sons — the same establishment currently holding Aldous Wren's empty coffin), and the officiant named in the obituary (Reverend Amos Teague, Wren Chapel). Three names. Three threads. She was beginning to understand that in Grayhollow, every thread eventually tied back to the same knot.

One more question. "Mrs. Abernathy — did Dr. Voss keep a register? Patient logs, house-call books, anything like that?"

"They're in the basement. Retention archive. But—" the old woman hesitated, and the hesitation itself was data, filing itself neatly in Ulyana's memory beside the flicker of recognition earlier — "they've been checked out. All of them. Since summer. By the sheriff's office."

"Checked out by whom?"

"Deputy Colter signed for them himself." She lowered her voice, though the basement door was thirty feet away and made of solid oak. "Which struck me as odd, because Dane Colter never struck me as much of a reader."

* * *

Sheriff Dane Colter stood at the window of his office above the hardware store and watched the woman walk up Ashfall Street toward the courthouse annex, and felt his jaw tighten the way it did before a storm rolled off the ridge.

Underhill. That was the name on the insurance company's letterhead, the name his dispatcher had repeated three times this morning like a warning bell. Ulyana Underhill, claims investigation, regional office. He'd run her through every database he had access to, which wasn't much, and come back with a personnel file so clean it looked laundered. Fourteen years with the company. Commendations. A note in her file — he'd memorized it, because he memorized everything, a habit his father had beaten into him alongside the fear of God — that read simply: *Subject possesses exceptional recall. Treat verbal statements as documentary evidence.*

Exceptional recall. Wonderful. The one investigator in the whole damn region with a head like a court stenographer, dropped into his town two weeks after they'd put Aldous Wren in the ground.

Except they hadn't put him anywhere. Colter knew that better than anyone breathing. He'd stood at the graveside and watched the casket lower, and he knew what weighed nothing and what weighed eighty kilograms of founder, and the difference between those two things had been sitting in his chest ever since like a swallowed stone.

He watched Underhill pause at the corner of Ashfall and Millrace, look up at the courthouse clock, and stand perfectly still for eleven seconds. Just standing. Doing nothing. And Colter understood, with the particular dread of a guilty man, that she wasn't doing nothing at all. She was looking at the courthouse. At his office window. Maybe at him.

He turned away from the glass before she could catch him watching.

On his desk lay the folder he'd signed out of the county basement in June — Dr. Voss's patient registers, forty years of house calls and deliveries and dying breaths, and somewhere in their pages, twenty years back, a September entry Colter had spent three nights searching for and finally found: *S. Wren, age 9. Laceration, right palm. Treated on site, Wren estate.* Dated six days before the drowning.

A boy who cut his hand the week he died. A burn scar on file from a kitchen accident at age seven. Medical details that could identify a drowned child grown to manhood — details that lived now in a folder locked in the sheriff's own bottom drawer, because Colter had not been able to make himself destroy them, and had not been able to make himself return them either.

He'd told himself he'd taken the registers to protect the town. To keep the past buried where burying it had cost everyone so much. That was the story he'd been telling himself since June, and he was almost good enough a liar to believe it.

But the truth was smaller and meaner: Aldous Wren had held a page of Colter's father's history for thirty years, and when the old man died — when he *supposedly* died — Colter had assumed the leverage died with him. Then the empty coffin business started, and the anonymous letter came, unsigned, typed, postmarked local: *We all agreed on the price, Sheriff. Prices don't expire.*

Someone was keeping the machine oiled. Someone was still collecting.

Colter sat down heavily, pulled the Voss folder from the drawer, and stared at the signature on the last register's cover sheet. Emmett T. Voss. Dead two years. And yet the man's handwriting had certified Aldous Wren's death as neat as you please, which meant somebody in this town had practiced that scythe-swept V until they got it right.

That took time. That took access. That took more than one pair of hands — a doctor's sample signature to copy, a funeral home willing to file without questions, a notary to stamp it, a clerk to accept it, a sheriff to look the other way.

Not fraud. Fraud was one desperate man with a pen.

This was a machine.

And machines, Colter knew from the mill, from his father, from thirty years of watching Grayhollow work — machines didn't break down on their own. They got shut off. Or they got fed.

His radio crackled. Dispatch, hesitant: "Sheriff? There's a Ms. Underhill downstairs. Says she'd like a word about the Voss estate."

Colter looked at the folder in his hands. At the drawer. At the window, where the afternoon light was going copper over the rooftops of a town that had sold its soul in installments and called it prosperity.

"Send her up," he said.

* * *

She climbed the stairs to find him standing, hand extended, wearing the smile of a man greeting a bill collector. Big through the shoulders, sun-cured skin, eyes the flat gray of the millpond in November. His handshake was calibrated — firm enough to seem honest, brief enough to end the conversation early.

"Sheriff Colter. Thanks for seeing me."

"Heard you've been making the rounds." He gestured to a chair. "Funeral home. Records office. Now here. You get around for a routine claim."

"Nothing about this claim is routine." She sat, declined the coffee he didn't offer, and laid her notepad on her knee like a shield. "I'm verifying the attending physician on Aldous Wren's death certificate. Dr. Emmett Voss."

"Doc Voss. Fine man. Terrible loss." The words came smooth and pre-packaged, a eulogy in miniature.

"When did you last see him?"

The pause lasted half a second too long. "At his funeral, I suppose. Two years back. Whole town was there."

"That's what I understood." Ulyana let the silence stretch, watching his face the way she'd watch a document — for alterations, erasures, telltale pressure marks. "So I'm sure you can help me with my confusion, Sheriff. Dr. Voss signed Aldous Wren's death certificate five weeks ago. Cardiac arrest, 11:47 p.m., October third. In his own hand, by the look of it. A very distinctive hand."

Colter's expression didn't change, but everything behind it did — a shutter dropping behind the gray eyes, a recalculation running at speed. "Signatures get copied," he said finally. "Nurses sign for doctors all the time. Office staff. Happens everywhere."

"Not on death certificates. Not legally. And not in a hand that precise unless it's been practiced." She tilted her head. "You signed out all of Dr. Voss's patient registers from the county archive in June. Every volume. They're still checked out to your office."

Something moved through the room then, cold and slow, like water finding a crack in a foundation. Colter crossed to the window, put his back to her, and looked out at the street.

"You've been busy," he said.

"It's my job. Here's my problem, Sheriff. A dead doctor certifies a dead founder's death. The coffin comes up empty except for a child's shoe. And the one man in this county with the authority to investigate any of it has been hoarding the victim's medical files since before anyone knew there was a crime." She stood, tucking the notepad away. "From where I sit, that's not an investigation. That's a containment operation."

"You're new here," Colter said, still facing the glass, "so let me explain how Grayhollow works. Things settle here. That's not corruption, that's geology. You start digging up settled ground, you don't find answers. You find gas pockets." He turned, and for one unguarded instant she saw not a lawman but a tired man carrying something heavy up a long hill. "Take my advice. Verify your claim, write your report, go home. Some holes are empty because what fell in them never existed in the first place."

"Coffins aren't metaphors, Sheriff. They're boxes. Boxes are either full or they're empty." She paused at the door. "One more thing. The shoe in the grave. Size nine, child's, left foot, leather cracked along the sole seam. Does that mean anything to you?"

For the first time, Dane Colter flinched. It was small — a contraction around the eyes, a fractional shift of weight — but Ulyana's memory caught it and preserved it in amber, timestamped, indexed, ready for whatever courtroom or reckoning might someday require it.

"No," he said. "Can't say it does."

"Then you won't mind if I keep asking around."

She let herself out. Through the window, Colter watched her descend the courthouse steps into the copper light, a slim figure with a notepad and a head full of evidence, walking straight through the middle of his town like she had every right to be there.

He reached for the phone, then stopped, hand hovering. Twenty years ago he'd have known exactly who to call. Tonight he wasn't sure the list still worked — or worse, that he was still on it.

Down the street, unaware she was being watched by two separate sets of eyes, Ulyana Underhill paused at the corner of Millrace Road and opened her notepad to a fresh page. At the top she wrote:

*Voss — dead 2 yrs. Signature forged. Registers withheld by Colter. Shoe = message, not accident.*

Below it, after a moment's thought:

*A machine needs builders. Find the builder.*

And below that, circled twice, the name Mrs. Abernathy had given her with such pointed dryness — the lawyer, the executor, the notary, half the town council:

*Harlan Pike.*
