## 4. The Groundskeeper's Hands

The cemetery at Grayhollow sat on a hill like a man who owned it, which in a way it did. Every family worth the name had bought its plot from the Wren estate, and the iron gates bore the founder's initials — A.W. — worked into the scrollwork so many times that the letters had stopped meaning anything and become pure pattern, decoration without memory. Ulyana Underhill stood before those gates at nine in the morning with a court order folded in her coat pocket and let her memory do what it always did: take everything.

Frost on the north-facing railings only. South side clear. Gate hinges recently oiled — the smell of machine grease under the cold iron. Gravel drive raked within the last day; the tine marks ran parallel and unbroken except for one set of boot prints going out, none coming back, which meant someone had walked out backward or the rake-man had started at the far end and worked toward her. The yew trees flanking the path were older than the town charter. The grass over the newer plots was greener than it had any right to be in November — fertilized, then, and not cheaply.

She catalogued all of it without deciding to, the way other people breathed. It was the one inheritance she'd never resented: a mind that kept everything, filed everything, and offered nothing back up until she asked. Most people found it unsettling once they figured out what she could do. She'd learned to stop telling them.

The verification exhumation wasn't until Thursday — Judge Abernathy wanted three days' notice to the estate, and Harlan Pike's office had returned the paperwork with a signature so florid it looked less like consent than a flourish of a cape. But Ulyana had never trusted documents to tell her what ground could tell her faster. Paper lied by omission. Dirt just lied, period, but it lied clumsily, and she knew how to catch it.

She pushed through the gate.

The groundskeeper was already there, of course. Groundskeepers were always already there; it was practically the job description. He was kneeling between two rows of markers with a spade and a hand trowel, doing something careful to the base of a headstone — resetting the footing, maybe, or clearing frost-heave. He wore canvas coveralls over a flannel shirt, gloves tucked in his back pocket despite the cold, and his hands were bare and black to the wrist with cemetery soil.

"Morning," he said, without looking up. "Grounds are open. Visitation hours are dawn to dusk."

"I'm not here to visit."

That got him. He turned his head, and Ulyana felt the world tilt slightly on its axis, the way it did perhaps twice in a career, when something the conscious mind hadn't yet assembled arrived whole anyway, like thunder before you've counted the seconds.

He had Aldous Wren's eyes.

She had studied the founder's face for two days now — the portrait in the funeral home's viewing room, the photograph on the memorial card, the oil painting behind the county clerk's desk that every official document in this town seemed obliged to pass beneath. Pale gray eyes, almost colorless, set deep under heavy brows, with a slight downward pull at the outer corners that gave even the youngest photographs of Aldous Wren an expression of patient disappointment. The man in the ground — allegedly in the ground — had looked out of every image as though he were waiting for the world to explain itself and fully expected to be unimpressed when it did.

This boy — young man, late twenties, early thirties — had those exact eyes. Not similar. Exact. The same pale gray, the same depth of socket, the same faint downward drag at the corners, so that even now, caught mid-turn with dirt on his face, he looked mildly disappointed to see her.

Genetics did this sometimes. Towns intermarried; founders' blood spread thin and wide through the local population like cream stirred into coffee. She told herself that. She was very good at telling herself things.

"You'd be the insurance investigator," he said. His voice was low and unhurried, with the flat vowels of someone who'd grown up within fifty miles and made no effort to leave them behind. "Word travels. Small town."

"Ulyana Underhill. Meridian Mutual." She showed him the laminated ID out of habit, though he hadn't asked, and watched him look at it longer than an innocent man needed to. Not suspiciously long. Memorizing long. She recognized the behavior because it was hers.

"And you are?"

He wiped one hand down his thigh, leaving a brown smear, and extended it. "Sebastian."

Just Sebastian. No surname offered. His grip was dry and strong and calloused across the palm in the specific pattern of tool work — spade, rake, the worn wood of wheelbarrow handles — rather than machinery. Mill workers had different hands. She'd spent yesterday afternoon shaking them.

"Sebastian," she repeated, letting it sit in the air between them. "Just the one name? Like a rock star?"

"Like a groundskeeper." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of where a smile might have been buried. "Name tag's on the coveralls if you want the full legal treatment."

She glanced down. There was indeed a plastic-tagged pin above the breast pocket, the kind sold in hardware stores for four dollars, and it read SEBASTIAN in block capitals that someone had labeled themselves, the letters slightly uneven, pressed hard enough to dent the plastic.

No last name on the tag either.

* * *

She made herself walk the cemetery properly first. Professional discipline: never let the interesting thing make you skip the boring things, because the boring things were where bodies got hidden — sometimes literally. She took the eastern row, where the oldest stones stood, their inscriptions softened by a century of weather into suggestions. WREN. WREN. VALE. FINCH. PIKE. COLTER. The founding families, laid out in stone like a board game, and she noted without surprise that the plots nearest the gate — nearest the road, nearest convenience — belonged to the names that still appeared in the county's business filings. Death, too, had real estate values here.

The groundskeeper followed her at a respectful distance, ostensibly resuming his work, actually keeping pace. She could feel his attention on her back the way you feel sun through a window.

"Aldous Wren's plot is the mausoleum?" she asked, stopping at the granite structure near the hill's crown. It was modest as mausoleums went — a single-chamber affair, granite gone gray-green at the base, the door secured with a brass seal bearing the funeral home's stamp. Pending the court-ordered opening, it remained sealed. Someone had left fresh flowers against the door. White chrysanthemums, cut within two days, stems wrapped in wet paper towel and foil — the improvisation of someone who'd carried them a distance without a vase.

"His plot," Sebastian agreed, from somewhere behind her left shoulder. "Though I hear he's not using it much."

She turned. He was leaning on his spade with an ease that looked practiced, watching her with those colorless eyes, and his face gave away precisely nothing — but his hands, his beautiful telltale hands, had tightened on the spade's shaft until the knuckles went pale.

"You heard about the discrepancy," she said.

"Everybody heard. Undertaker fainted at the pre-check. Marlowe. Big man, faints like a Victorian heroine." He said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. "Empty box and a kid's shoe. That's the story going around the diner, anyway."

"Is that how you'd describe it? A story?"

"I'd describe it as above my pay grade." He nodded at the tag on his chest. "I dig holes and plant people. What they put in the holes beforehand isn't really my department."

"No," Ulyana said. "But you'd know if anything had been taken *out* of this mausoleum. You're here every day. You'd have seen traffic."

Something flickered across his face — quick, quickly suppressed, but her memory caught it and held it up to the light: not guilt. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a question he'd been expecting for years and finding it strange, finally, to hear it aloud.

"Traffic," he repeated.

"People coming and going. Vehicles at odd hours. Disturbed ground."

"It's a cemetery, Ms. Underhill. All the ground's disturbed. That's sort of the theme." He straightened off the spade. "You want to check the seal log, the funeral home keeps one. Marlowe signs every entry. I don't have keys to the chamber. Never have."

*Never have.* Three words, delivered smoothly, and yet her memory flagged them the way a bank's software flags a transaction — not because they were wrong but because they were *ready*. Rehearsed-ready. People who answered questions honestly answered them messily, with hesitations and corrections and irrelevant detail. People who answered questions they'd prepared for answered them clean.

She filed it. She filed everything.

* * *

She spent another forty minutes walking the rows, and he spent those forty minutes being exactly what he appeared to be: a groundskeeper. He reset the footing on the Pike marker. He cleared leaves from the drainage channel along the northern wall. He loaded a broken branch into a wheelbarrow with economical movements that spoke of years of repetition. If she hadn't seen the eyes, she'd have written him off inside five minutes, and she suspected that was the point of him — the coveralls, the tag, the flat local vowels, the whole costume of ordinariness worn with the confidence of long practice.

It was the hands that undid him.

Not the soil on them — that was the job. It was what was underneath. When he stripped his right glove to work the trowel into frozen ground, she saw the back of his hand, and there, running from the webbing between thumb and forefinger up past the wrist bone and disappearing under the sleeve, was a scar. Old. Burn tissue, glossy and pulled, the kind that came from contact heat — a hand pressed against something hot, not splashed, not spilled. Pressed. Held.

Ulyana stood very still among the dead of Grayhollow and opened a file in her memory that she had closed eleven years ago and sworn she wouldn't reopen.

She'd been twenty-three, newly licensed, working claims triage in the regional archive in Ashford, and one of the boxes that crossed her desk was ancient — a fire claim from 1968, the old Wren mill annex, plus a rider file she probably shouldn't have been reading because it wasn't part of her assignment. Family incident report. A stove. A nine-year-old boy, left-handed, who'd put his right hand flat on a cast-iron stove door that someone had left open, and held it there — the report was ambiguous about whether he'd been pushed or had fallen or simply hadn't known, and the ambiguity had bothered her even then, twenty-three years old and already allergic to ambiguity. The injury had been documented because the family physician had treated it and the insurer had required photographs for the medical rider.

A burn scar, contact-pattern, right hand, webbing to wrist. On a nine-year-old boy named Sebastian Wren, declared drowned six years later in Blackwater Creek, body never recovered, claim paid on a presumption-of-death certificate signed by Dr. Emmett Voss.

The same doctor whose signature she couldn't place on Aldous Wren's death certificate. The same doctor whose obituary she'd pulled from the *Grayhollow Gazette*'s morgue file last night, dated two years before the founder supposedly died.

Coincidence stacked on coincidence stops being coincidence and starts being architecture.

"Hot stove," she said quietly.

Sebastian went still. Not dramatically — a groundskeeper pausing in his work, anyone would say. But she was watching his hands, and the trowel stopped moving a half-second before the rest of him decided to notice.

"Excuse me?"

"The scar. On your hand." She kept her voice level, conversational, almost idle. "Contact burn. Stove pattern. You'd have been a kid."

For a moment the only sound was wind moving through the yews, that dry whispering like paper being turned. Then he laughed — short, genuine-sounding, easy. Too easy.

"Woodstove, actually. Out at my uncle's place, when I was seven. Whole town's got a scar story. Cold winters, old stoves." He flexed the hand, displaying it with the casualness of a man showing off an old fishing injury. "You always this interested in groundskeepers' hands, or am I special?"

*Yes,* she thought. *Special is exactly the word.*

Because here was the thing her memory would not release, the thing it kept turning over like a stone to check what lived underneath: the 1968 incident report had specified a *woodstove*, and the treating physician's notes — Voss's notes, she realized now, Voss again, the man was threaded through this town's paperwork like a wire through insulation — described the scar's healing as complicated by the fact that the boy had kept using the hand. Left-handed child, forced to favor the burned right one, re-injuring it for weeks. And the boy standing in front of her had just instinctively reached for the trowel with his left hand. Had handled the spade left-handed. Had loaded the wheelbarrow left-handed while holding the branch in his right, protecting it without appearing to protect it, the way a person protects an old injury the body has never quite forgotten even after the skin forgot.

Twenty years later, the habit survived the wound.

"You're special," she said, and smiled, and watched him decide whether to smile back. He did, eventually, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it frightened her more than the eyes had, because she believed it, some traitorous part of her believed it instantly and completely, and belief was a liability she could not afford.

* * *

She made herself walk away. That was the discipline — when the hook sets, reel slowly, or you tear the mouth and lose the fish entirely. She thanked him for his time, told him she'd be back Thursday for the exhumation and hoped the grounds would be as well kept, and received a nod that managed to be both deferential and amused, as though she'd said something charmingly unnecessary, like hoping the ocean would stay wet.

At the gate she paused, one hand on the iron scrollwork, and looked back down the gravel path.

He hadn't resumed work. He was standing where she'd left him, spade forgotten, watching her go, and his expression — this time she caught it clean, before he could bury it — was not the blankness of a stranger. It was the expression of a man watching something arrive that he had been waiting for. Relief and dread in equal measure, balanced so finely that together they looked almost like hope.

Their eyes met across sixty feet of graves.

Then he tipped an imaginary hat, turned back to his headstones, and became ordinary again, so thoroughly and so fast that a lesser observer might have doubted what she'd seen.

Ulyana Underhill walked out the gates with her heart beating faster than the climb justified, and her memory, faithful and merciless, laid the evidence out in a row for her like cards on a table:

Eyes matching the founder's portrait, exact, not approximate. A self-labeled name tag bearing the name of a drowned boy, no surname, the letters pressed hard enough to dent plastic. A contact-burn scar matching a documented 1968 injury, on the correct hand, healed in the correct pattern. Left-handedness consistent with the physician's notes. Rehearsed answers about keys and seals. Fresh flowers at a sealed mausoleum, carried a distance by someone without a vase. And underneath all of it, the thing that wasn't evidence at all but weighed more than any of it: the look on his face when he thought she couldn't see. The look of a man who had been alone with a secret for twenty years and had just heard footsteps.

She stopped at the crest of the hill road and looked back once. He was a small dark figure among the stones, bent to his work, indistinguishable from any groundskeeper in any town in America.

Except that the grave he tended best — the one he weeded most carefully, whose footing he'd checked twice while she watched, whose flowers he'd replace tomorrow, she'd have bet the deed to her own life on it — was empty.

And the boy everyone said had drowned in Blackwater Creek in 1974 was pulling on his glove, pressing the burned hand into the canvas, and going back to digging.

Ulyana took out her notebook and wrote a single line, then underlined it twice:

*Find out who else knows what a nine-year-old looks like grown up.*

Because if she was right — if the mystery had just acquired a face, and the face belonged to a dead man — then somebody in Grayhollow had gone to extraordinary lengths to keep that boy buried on paper. And the shoe in the coffin meant somebody had recently gone to equally extraordinary lengths to dig him back out.

Thursday, the coffin would come up out of the ground in front of a judge.

She intended to be watching the groundskeeper's hands when it did.
