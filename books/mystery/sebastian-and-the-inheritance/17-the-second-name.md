## 17. The Second Name

The rain came in off the hills the way it always did in Grayhollow that time of year — not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn slowly across the valley. Ulyana Underhill watched it from the window of the Wren Diner, her coffee untouched, her notebook closed but full.

*Corliss Finch. Second name on the ledger. Vice president, Grayhollow Savings & Trust, est. 1911.*

She had read the name so many times in the past two days that it had worn a groove in her memory, the way a coin wears smooth in a pocket. The ledger page Sebastian had slipped her listed six names, six sums, six dates stretching back to 1974. Finch's entry was the second-largest. Payments made quarterly for eleven years, then nothing — the silence of a debt considered paid.

And now the man himself was missing.

She'd learned it that morning, not from the sheriff's office — Colter hadn't called her, wouldn't call her, hadn't spoken more than four words to her since the night he ordered her out of town — but from the diner itself, from the way conversation died when she walked in and then resumed in a lower register, like a radio turned down but not off.

"—found the boat out past the weir—"

"—engine cold, gas tank half full—"

"—his wife says he never came home Tuesday—"

Ulyana turned her cup a quarter rotation on its saucer. She had a habit, catalogued and never corrected, of arranging small objects into grids when she was thinking. Four coasters, aligned. Salt, pepper, sugar, napkin dispenser. The waitress, a girl of perhaps nineteen with chipped nail polish and a name tag reading DELPHINE, refilled her coffee without being asked and without meeting her eyes.

"You heard about Mr. Finch?" Delphine asked the booth behind Ulyana instead.

"Boat was still running when they found it," a man's voice answered. "Circling. Like somebody left the wheel and walked off the edge of the world."

"Or was walked," said another voice, older, and then a silence that had weight to it.

Ulyana paid for the coffee she hadn't drunk and went out into the rain.

* * *

The Grayhollow marina was less a marina than a gravel ramp, a fuel pump, and a long shed where a dozen boats sat on trailers under tarps like sleeping animals. The Finch boat had been hauled out and tied to the dock, and even from thirty feet Ulyana could see why it had drawn a crowd.

It was a handsome craft, a twenty-two-foot cuddy cabin with *MAREN'S BOY* lettered on the transom in flaking gold. The name snagged something in her memory — she let it snag, let her mind open the drawer. Maren's boy. Sebastian. The boat was older than the drowning, older than the disappearance. Corliss Finch had named his boat after the dead child of the woman whose fortune he'd helped bury.

Or after his own guilt. With men like Finch, Ulyana thought, the two were often the same thing.

The boat rocked gently against the tires of the dock. The cabin door stood open. She could see, even at this distance, the details that mattered: a coffee thermos on the bench seat, upright and unspilled; a rain jacket hanging from a hook, dry; a set of keys — boat keys, she'd learn later — still in the ignition.

Nobody leaves a running boat in the rain without a reason. And nobody falls overboard without disturbing the thermos.

"You're not law enforcement."

The voice came from her left. A heavyset man in a rubber bib and a hooded sweatshirt stood under the shed's overhang, arms crossed, watching her with the flat assessment of a man appraising livestock.

"Insurance investigator," Ulyana said. "Ulyana Underhill. And you are?"

"Ray Dolson. I run this ramp." He didn't offer a hand. "You're the one been poking at the Wren grave."

"I'm the one the court sent to verify a death. The grave did the rest." She stepped closer to the boat, and Dolson didn't stop her. "You pulled it in?"

"Me and my boy. Wednesday morning." He nodded at the water, gray and patient beyond the dock. "Out past the weir, engine at idle, nobody aboard. Rain coming down like the sky had a grudge. My boy wanted to leave it, but I figured Mr. Finch would want his boat back."

"When did you last see Finch?"

"Monday evening. He come down regular — Mondays and Thursdays, always alone, always out past the point and back before dark. Twenty years, near enough." Dolson's jaw worked. "Man's seventy-one years old and he handled that boat like he was born on it. Whatever happened out there, he didn't fall. I'll tell the sheriff that till I'm blue."

"What makes you so sure?"

Dolson looked at her then, fully, and something moved behind his eyes — not fear exactly, but its first cousin.

"Because the ladder was down," he said. "On the port side. You drop the ladder to swim. You don't drop it in October rain." He paused. "And because when we winched her out, there was blood in the scupper. Not much. A cup, maybe. But blood dries brown, and this was brown."

* * *

The sheriff's office occupied the ground floor of the municipal building, between the water department and a room where the Grayhollow Garden Club kept its trophies. Ulyana had been in twice before. The first time, Colter had been grudgingly civil. The second, he had told her, in front of his deputy, that the county line ran both directions and she'd be wise to remember which side of it her jurisdiction ended on.

This time the deputy on desk duty — a young man named Pell, whose ears went red when he was nervous, which was always — looked up at her with something worse than hostility. He looked up at her with relief.

"Sheriff's out at the Finch place," Pell said, before she'd asked anything.

"Then I'll wait."

"You could—" Pell lowered his voice, glanced at the empty corridor. "Ma'am, there's something you ought to know. People are saying things. About the founder."

"The founder is a skeleton in a borrowed coffin, Mr. Pell. Or an absence in one."

"That's just it." He came around the desk, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Old Man Wren's grave. My grandmother goes out every Sunday, puts flowers on the family plot — her mother worked the mill house. She says the ground over the grave's been disturbed. Fresh-turned. And the headstone—" He swallowed. "The headstone's been moved. Half an inch, maybe. But moved. And there's wet dirt on the *inside* of the stone, ma'am. Like something pushed it from below."

Ulyana held his gaze and did not let her face do anything at all. She knew, better than anyone in this building, that the coffin was empty, that the grave was a message Maren Wren had arranged, that the founder was — if the widow was to be believed — alive somewhere far from Grayhollow. But the town didn't know any of that. The town knew only what it could see: an empty grave, a vanished banker, and a legend with room in it for anything.

"Thank you, Deputy," she said. "Has anyone checked on Mrs. Finch?"

"Mrs. Finch is with her sister in Harlow Bend. Sheriff sent a car."

"And the blood from the boat? Lab work?"

Pell's ears went redder. "County lab's backed up," he said, which was county-lab language for *the sheriff hasn't sent it*.

* * *

The Finch house sat on Wren Hill, in the crescent of large homes the mill's management had built for itself in the fifties — the same street where the Pikes lived, where the Vosses had lived, a street Ulyana now understood as a map of the conspiracy, each house a name on the ledger. Corliss Finch's place was brick, two stories, with a copper beech in the yard that had been old when the house was young.

Colter's cruiser was in the drive. Ulyana parked behind it and walked up through the rain, and Colter met her at the door before she could knock, as if he'd been watching her come.

"No," he said.

"I haven't asked anything."

"You were going to." He stepped out and pulled the door shut behind him. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with a face that seemed permanently braced for bad weather, and today the weather was winning. "Finch is missing. That's a county matter."

"Finch is the second name on a hush ledger connected to a faked drowning and an empty coffin. That's my matter, Sheriff, and you know it."

Colter looked past her at the rain, at the copper beech thrashing in the wind, and when he spoke again his voice was lower. "You want to know what I know? I know a seventy-one-year-old man went out on his boat Monday and didn't come back. I know there's blood in the scupper and the lab's backed up. I know his housekeeper says nothing's packed, his wallet's in the drawer, his pills are in the cabinet — heart pills, Ms. Underhill, the man doesn't miss a dose. And I know that every old-timer in this valley is telling every other old-timer that Aldous Wren has come back to collect."

"You don't believe that."

"What I believe doesn't matter." He said it flatly, and for a moment — a moment she would keep, the way she kept everything — something in his face opened like a door onto a lit room, and then shut. "What matters is that this town believes it. And a town that believes a dead man is walking doesn't need an investigator. It needs a priest."

"Or a sheriff who sends blood samples to the lab."

Colter's jaw tightened. He turned and went back inside, and the door closed with the soft, final sound of a man deciding not to answer.

* * *

She found the groundskeeper at the cemetery, because she had known she would, because she had begun — and she admitted this to herself with a professional's cold honesty and something less cold underneath — to know his rhythms the way she knew the rhythms of documents. He worked the east slope in the mornings and the old rows in the afternoons, and in the hour before dusk he was always near the Wren plot, as if drawn there by a gravity the headstones couldn't account for.

Sebastian was raking leaves into a canvas tarp when she came up the gravel path. He didn't stop working. He'd stopped pretending not to know why she came, weeks ago; now he simply acknowledged her presence the way the ground acknowledges weather.

"Corliss Finch," she said.

The rake paused for half a second, then resumed. "What about him?"

"Second name on your ledger. Eleven years of payments. He's been missing since Monday. His boat came back without him, with blood in the scupper and the swim ladder down."

Now the rake stopped for good. Sebastian leaned on it and looked out over the cemetery, at the rows of stones going gray and gold in the dying light, and Ulyana watched him with the attention she gave to difficult texts.

"You think I don't know?" he said. "Half this town comes through those gates. They talk like I'm a fence post." He turned to her. "You think I did it."

"I think someone is killing the names on that list. Harlan Pike was the first. Finch is the second. And I think whoever it is knows that list exists — which is a short list of people. You. Me. Maren. And the six."

"Five," Sebastian said. "Six minus two."

"Minus three, if Finch is dead."

"Then we're down to a race." He said it without drama, and that was the frightening part — the flatness of a man who had spent twenty years being dead and had run out of the instinct to be alarmed. He looked at her. "You should hear what they're saying. Down at the diner, at the feed store. That the founder walks. That he's come back for what he's owed. Old Mrs. Tabb swears she saw a tall man in a long coat standing at the Wren plot at midnight, and when she called out he turned and he had—" Sebastian's mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "Aldous's face. She's eighty-four and she's certain."

"People need the story more than the facts. Facts don't have a shape you can pray to."

"Is that what you do, Ms. Underhill? Pray to facts?"

"I don't pray at all. I *file*." She took a step closer, gravel crunching, and lowered her voice even though the nearest living soul was a hundred yards away among the stones. "Sebastian. If Finch is dead, then the killer has two of six, and the pace is accelerating. Pike died eleven days after you gave me the ledger. Finch vanished four days after that. Someone is working down the list, and they're not being careful anymore — the ladder was down, the blood wasn't cleaned. Either they're arrogant, or they *want* it found. Want the town telling ghost stories. Want everyone looking at the grave instead of the living."

Sebastian was quiet for a long moment. Rain dripped from the bare branches of the row of lindens along the wall, each drop striking the leaves on the ground with small, exact sounds she would remember all her life without trying.

"There's something I haven't told you," he said finally. "About Monday night."

Her pulse didn't quicken — she'd trained that out of herself — but something behind her sternum did, and she noted it the way she noted everything, precisely and against her will.

"Monday night I closed the gates at seven. Standard. I was doing a last walk of the wall line when I heard the gate chain again, around nine. I came out of the shed and there was a car at the curve — big, dark, idling with its lights off. And there was a man standing at the Wren plot. Tall. Long coat. Hat." He met her eyes. "I called out. He turned. And Ulyana — I know Aldous Wren's face better than anyone alive, because it's half of mine. It wasn't him. It was a man *wearing* him. The build, the carriage, even the way he stood with his weight back on his heels like the old man used to. Someone who'd studied him. Or someone related to him."

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the hill, a dog began to bark and was silenced.

"Did you see the car?"

"Make of nothing. But the plate was county — and it was a dealer frame. *WREN MOTOR CO.*" He let that sit. "There's one Wren left with access to that lot, Ulyana. The brother. Merritt. The one name that isn't on the ledger, because he was never bought. He was never bought because he was never *paid* — and in my family, being cut out was worse than being bought."

Ulyana stood very still, rain running off her chin, her mind opening drawers at speed: the ledger, six names; the torn page Colter had given her; the initials she hadn't yet spoken aloud; the empty coffin as *message*; and now a ghost in a long coat, a car from the Wren lot, a town tipping over into superstition like a boat past its weir.

"Merritt Wren," she said. "The executor. The man who benefits if the inheritance is never claimed and every witness to the fraud is dead."

"He's not killing to hide the secret," Sebastian said. "He's killing to *own* it. Every name that dies makes the legend bigger. And a legend, Ulyana—" he looked down the hill, at the town coming alight below, windows kindling yellow in the gray, "—a legend doesn't need a body. It needs a believer. And this whole town is on its knees."

She turned to go, then stopped.

"The shoe," she said. "The child's shoe in the coffin. I matched it. It was yours."

"I know," Sebastian said quietly. "I put it there. Twenty years ago, when we emptied the grave. It was the only thing they ever let me keep from that boat." His voice didn't break, but it bent, the way a beam bends before it decides. "Someone put me in that boat to drown, Ulyana. And now someone is putting other people in the water. And I need you to find out if it's the same someone — because if it is, then the person who tried to kill me when I was nine years old is still here. Still patient. Still *family*."

She walked down through the cemetery as the last light failed, and behind her she heard him lock the gates — the long rattle of the chain, the click of the padlock, the sound of a man securing a kingdom of stones.

At the bottom of the hill, pinned under the wiper of her car, a single envelope waited, rain-spotted, unaddressed.

Inside, in block capitals, was a photograph of the Finch boat taken from the water — taken *Monday night*, from an angle no witness had mentioned — and on the back, four words:

*STOP DIGGING. SIX REMAINS.*
