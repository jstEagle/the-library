## 19. Odette, Alive

The postcard had been mailed from Grayhollow, but the address it was sent *to* was the thing that mattered — a return address in faded pencil on the back of Odette Vale's own handwriting, forty years stale and still legible if you knew how to look: *Mrs. E. Marsh, The Gullwing House, Pelican Row, Cape Verity.*

Ulyana found it in the county archive's dead-letter drawer, misfiled under 1987, an envelope that had bounced back from an address that no longer existed and been forgotten by everyone except the filing cabinet. Everyone except her. Her memory did not forget. It never forgot. It only waited.

Cape Verity was three hours north along a coast that seemed embarrassed by its own beauty — gray water, white gulls, houses the color of old candy leaning into the wind. She drove it in a rented sedan with the radio off, rehearsing questions she already knew the shape of, because with Odette Vale there would be no second interview. Women who changed their names and vanished for four decades did not grant follow-ups.

The Gullwing House sat at the end of Pelican Row like the last tooth in a jaw of them — three stories of weathered cedar, a widow's walk, geraniums in coffee cans on every sill. A hand-painted sign advertised ROOMS BY THE WEEK, MEALS INCLUDED. A woman in a man's cardigan was sweeping the porch, and when Ulyana asked for Mrs. Marsh, the sweeping stopped.

"You police?"

"Insurance," Ulyana said, which was true, and let the word do its work. Insurance sounded harmless. Insurance sounded like paperwork. Insurance sounded like nothing anyone needed to run from.

The woman — landlady, sister-in-law of somebody, keeper of the gate — studied her for a long moment with the flat appraisal of someone who had turned away strangers before. "She don't take visitors."

"I'm not a visitor. I'm from Grayhollow."

The broom handle creaked in the woman's grip. Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Recognition. As if *Grayhollow* were a diagnosis.

"Back room," she said finally. "Second floor. I'll tell her you're coming up, but I won't tell her twice." She leaned close, and her voice dropped into something almost tender. "Go easy. She's been waiting for this a long time, whatever she says otherwise."

* * *

The stairs smelled of salt and lamp oil. Ulyana climbed them slowly, cataloguing as she went — the habit that had made her career and cost her everything softer than a career. Fourteen steps. A banister worn pale at the turn where a hand had gripped it ten thousand times. A door at the landing, painted green once, now the color of sea glass. Through it, the sound of a radio playing something orchestral and old, turned low, as if the listener wanted the company without the noise.

She knocked.

The music stopped. Silence, long enough that she counted her own heartbeats against it. Seven. Eight.

"Who is it?" The voice was thin and level, a voice that had practiced being calm for so long the practice had become structural.

"Ulyana Underhill. I'm investigating the death of Aldous Wren."

Another silence. Then, quietly, with something that might have been laughter in it if laughter hadn't died in this room decades ago:

"Aldous Wren's death. Well." A pause. "Come in, then. It's about time somebody came about Aldous Wren."

* * *

Odette Vale was small. That was the first thing — not frail, not broken, but *small*, the way trees are small when they've grown all their lives against a wind. She sat in a chair by the window with a knitted shawl over her shoulders and her hands folded in her lap like two birds that had learned not to fly. Her hair was white and cut short and practical. Her eyes were gray and absolutely unmissable, because they were the eyes of a girl in a 1974 witness statement, unchanged, still wide, still seeing something behind Ulyana that wasn't there.

On the wall above her bed hung a single framed photograph: a shoreline. No people. Just water and rocks and the particular quality of light that said *this place mattered enough to keep.*

"Sit," Odette said, indicating the bed's one other chair. "You'll want to sit. Everyone always wants to sit down after."

"After what?"

"After I tell you." She looked out the window at the water. "You found me through the postcard. The dead-letter file. I wondered who'd think of that." Her mouth curved, dry. "I mailed it to myself, you know. Or to the name I used to be. Every year on the anniversary. One postcard, to an address that didn't exist anymore. It was the only way I could stand to remember — to send the memory somewhere it couldn't come back from." Her hands tightened on each other. "And then last month I mailed one to Grayhollow instead. To *them.* To say: I'm still here. I remember. Stop it, whoever you are. Stop doing whatever you're doing."

"And someone read it," Ulyana said. "Someone in town knew what it meant."

"Oh, they knew." Odette's voice didn't rise. It went *flatter*, which was worse. "There are maybe four people alive who'd understand that postcard, and I'd have said three of them were dead. So either I'm wrong about the dead part—" her gray eyes came around and fixed on Ulyana, "—or there's a fourth person I never knew about. And either way, Miss Underhill, that means the thing I ran from has started moving again. Which brings us to why you're really here."

"The empty coffin."

"Yes." Odette closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, but her voice held. "I saw the news clipping. My landlady brings me the paper. *Founder's grave found empty,* it said, and I laughed until I cried, and then I cried until I couldn't stand up. Because that coffin was supposed to stay shut forever. That was the whole point. That was the *price we agreed to pay.*"

"Where were you standing," Ulyana said gently, "on the night Sebastian Wren drowned?"

Odette looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed again, that terrible dry laugh. "No preamble. No warming up. You're not like the sheriff's men who came in '74. They asked me if I'd been drinking. I was fifteen." She unfolded her hands, folded them again. "I was on the mill footbridge. The old one, before they tore it down. I used to go there to smoke cigarettes I stole from my father and pretend I was somewhere else. And on the fourth of October, 1974, I watched a boat go out onto the mill pond in the dark, and I watched a man put a boy into it."

The room seemed to lean in. Even the gulls outside went quiet.

"Aldous Wren," Ulyana said.

"No." Odette's answer came fast and absolute, and it knocked something loose in Ulyana's chest that she hadn't known was loaded. "Not Aldous. Everyone assumes Aldous, because Aldous was the monster everyone could see. But I knew Aldous's silhouette — the whole town did, he stood on that mill balcony like a monument every God-given day. This man was different. Taller. Narrower. And he carried the boy like—" her voice caught, steadied, "—like luggage. Like something being put away. He set Sebastian in that boat, and he pushed it out onto the pond, and he took the plug out of the hull first, because I heard the water start, that soft swallowing sound, and I was fifteen and stupid and I thought — I thought it was a game. A test. Some rich-family cruelty. Boys got dunked in that pond every summer. I told myself it was that. I told myself that for thirty years."

"But it wasn't."

"It wasn't." Odette's hands had gone white inside each other. "The boat went out past the pilings and it settled low and the boy didn't swim. He *couldn't* swim — I learned that later, everyone learned that later, the grandson of the man who owned the swimming hole couldn't swim, isn't that a joke. And the man on the dock just stood there. Watching. Hands in his pockets. He watched that boat go down and he watched the spot where it went down for a full minute, and then he walked away, calm as Sunday, and I hid behind the bridge rail with my heart going like a hammer and I swore I'd take it to my grave."

"Did you see his face?"

"No." Odette shook her head, slow, frustrated, the frustration of four decades. "Dark. Hat. But I'll tell you what I saw, Miss Underhill, what I've never told a living soul, because telling it felt like signing my own death warrant." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the fine bones of her old face, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "When he turned to leave, the porch light off the boathouse caught his hand. His left hand. There was a ring on it — a heavy signet ring, dark stone, gold band. And I'd seen that ring my whole life, because I worked summers cleaning offices at the mill, and there was exactly one man besides Aldous Wren who wore a ring like that and had reason to be on that dock at midnight."

Ulyana's memory laid it out before Odette could say it, the way it always did — the estate photographs, the funeral, the ledger page Sebastian had pressed into her hands, six names, five accounted for, and the torn-away sixth that Colter's confession had failed to restore.

"Merritt Wren," Ulyana said.

Odette flinched as if the name had struck her. Then, slowly, she nodded. "Aldous's brother. The one nobody talked about. The one who signed things in the back office and smiled too much and wore that ring like it was a crown he'd been promised." She exhaled, long and shaking. "Aldous was cruel, but Aldous was *open* — everyone knew what he was. Merritt was the kind of cruel that files papers. The kind that waits. And when the boy went in the water, I understood even at fifteen that this wasn't rage. This was *administration.*"

* * *

They sat in silence while the light through the window went from silver to copper. Downstairs, faintly, the landlady rattled pans. Somewhere out on the water a boat engine coughed and settled into its drone.

"Why didn't you come forward?" Ulyana asked, though she dreaded the answer, because she already knew its shape. She'd seen it in Maren's riddles, in Colter's shame, in Harlan Pike's panic. Grayhollow ran on debts, and fear was just another currency.

"Come forward to *whom?*" Odette said simply. "The sheriff was Aldous's creature. The doctor was on the mill payroll — I checked, even at sixteen, I checked, because I'm careful, being careful is the only talent I've ever had. The coroner ruled drowning, accident, case closed, and the family held a funeral for a boy whose body they never found, and do you know what that told me, Miss Underhill? It told me the *family* was in on it. You don't bury an empty box by accident. Somebody chose that coffin. Somebody chose that grave. And if the mother and the grandfather and the whole machinery of that town agreed on the story, then a fifteen-year-old mill worker's daughter with stolen cigarettes in her pocket was going to change precisely nothing, except the direction of the next boat." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ulyana had seen in weeks of sad things. "So I left. Changed my name. Married a good man who died too soon. Kept the postcards. Kept the memory. Kept the *ring*, in my head, where no one could burn it or bury it or buy it."

"The others," Ulyana said. "Harlan Pike. Corliss Finch. The foreman, Dade Ruthers. They helped fake the death — Maren Wren's scheme, to hide the boy from Aldous. But if Merritt actually tried to drown him—"

"Then the rescue and the murder happened on the same night, and neither side knew about the other." Odette's eyes had gone sharp, bright, the fog of age burned away by the clarity of an old terror finally given a shape. "That's it. That's the whole rotten machine. Maren paid her conspirators to make the boy disappear *safely* — and Merritt, or whoever that man was, meant to make him disappear *permanently*, and the plan Maren built accidentally saved the child from the plan Merritt built. Two plots, one pond. And everyone who signed on to the first plot has spent forty years believing they were party to a mercy." Her voice dropped. "Until someone started killing them. And now they're learning what else was in the water that night."

"Is Merritt alive?" Ulyana asked. "Merritt Wren — is he alive, is he in Grayhollow?"

"I don't know." Odette spread her small hands. "He vanished from town in '75, a year after. Sold his share, some said. Disgraced, others said. I assumed dead — I *prayed* dead. But that postcard I sent last month..." Her gaze drifted to the window, to the darkening water. "Somebody answered it, Miss Underhill. Did I mention that? Three days after I mailed it, a letter came to this house. No return address. Postmarked Grayhollow. One sentence inside." Her hands began, very slightly, to tremble. "*Some graves hold more than you think. Stay out.*"

Ulyana felt the cold move up her spine with the slow certainty of tide water. "May I see it?"

"It's downstairs. In the flour tin where I keep the things that scare me." Odette almost smiled. "It's a large tin."

* * *

But when they reached the bottom of the stairs, the landlady was waiting in the hall, and her face had gone the color of the fog outside.

"There was a man," she said, low and fast, before Odette could speak. "Half an hour ago. While you two were talking. Came to the door asking about weekly rates — big fellow, gray coat, kept his hat on indoors. I told him we were full." She swallowed. "After he left, I found this wedged in the screen door."

She held out a piece of paper, folded once. Ulyana opened it with fingers that did not tremble, because hers never did, and read it, and her photographic mind stamped it permanently in the space of a heartbeat:

*Ask her about the ring. Then ask yourself who else knows you came.*

No signature. Cheap paper. And beneath the ink, pressed deep enough to feel with a fingertip, the ghost of a signet seal — a dark stone, a crest worn nearly smooth.

Odette made a small sound behind her. Ulyana turned. The old woman had gone rigid in the hallway, one hand braced on the wall, her gray eyes fixed on the note with forty years of recognition draining the blood from her face.

"That's his paper," she whispered. "That's mill stationery. That's the watermark from the back office." She looked up at Ulyana, and her voice cracked clean through. "He knows I talked. He *let* you find me, child — don't you understand? Nobody finds me unless he wants me found. You weren't following a trail." Her hand slid down the wallpaper, and outside, far down Pelican Row, an engine started in the dark. "You *are* the trail."

Ulyana was already moving for the door.
