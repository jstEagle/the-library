## 24. Maren Wren

The Wren house sat at the end of Founders' Row like a held breath.

Ulyana had walked past it a dozen times since arriving in Grayhollow — the kind of house that made other houses look like sketches of themselves. Three stories of gray granite, slate roof gone soft with moss, windows tall enough that a person standing in one could watch the whole town without being seen. She'd catalogued it the way she catalogued everything: gable angles, shutter hardware, the hairline crack running from the second-floor cornice down through the lintel like a vein. But she had never been inside, and standing on the porch now, her knuckles an inch from the doorbell, she understood that the house was not architecture. It was testimony.

Maren Wren opened the door before she rang.

"I wondered when you'd come." The old woman's voice was dry and even, like paper turning over in wind. She wore black — she always wore black, Ulyana had noted at the funeral home, though the widow's grief had struck her then as oddly weightless, grief performed by someone who had already finished grieving years ago. "You have your mother's habit of standing too straight, Miss Underhill. It makes people want to knock you over."

"My mother died when I was six."

"Yes," Maren said. "I know. Come in."

The foyer swallowed sound. A staircase curved upward into shadow; a chandelier hung dead above them, its crystals filmed with dust, as if the house had decided decades ago that light was a courtesy it no longer owed anyone. Maren led her through a parlor furnished in the style of 1960 and into a small room at the back that faced the mill pond. Books everywhere. A cold fireplace. Two chairs positioned with the precision of chess pieces.

"Sit," Maren said. "Would you like tea?"

"No."

"Good. I've stopped making it for guests. It was becoming a lie I told too often." She lowered herself into the chair facing the window, hands folded in her lap, and studied Ulyana with eyes that were pale as river ice. "You've been busy. Harlan Pike is dead. Corliss Finch is missing, presumed drowned, which this town finds poetic. And you've been to see Odette."

Ulyana kept her face still, but her pulse ticked up. *She knows about Odette.* "You've been watching me."

"I've been watching everyone for fifty years. It's what you do when you're the only one holding the whole picture." Maren's gaze drifted to the window, to the dark water beyond the lawn. "You found the ledger page. Sebastian gave it to you. Don't bother denying it — I know his handwriting better than my own late husband's, and I know he's been waiting twenty years for someone worth giving it to."

"You knew he was alive."

"I raised him until he was nine," Maren said simply. "Of course I knew."

The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way rooms do when a fact you've been carrying finally sets down its true weight. Ulyana had suspected. Suspecting was her profession. But suspicion was a map, and this was the territory.

"Then you can tell me why the coffin was empty."

"I can tell you more than that." Maren turned back to face her, and something moved behind the ice — not warmth exactly, but the place where warmth had once been, like a hearth long cold that still bore the scorch mark of fire. "But first, a question. You're an investigator. You deal in motive. So tell me — what did you conclude? Why would a woman help fake her own grandson's drowning?"

Ulyana laid out her reasoning the way she'd built it, brick by brick. "Greed didn't fit. The will left everything to Sebastian; faking his death didn't move the money anywhere useful. Protection fits better. Someone wanted the boy gone — hidden, erased — and the drowning was the cleanest way to do it. The conspirators on the ledger were paid to keep silent, which means they weren't accomplices to a crime against Sebastian. They were accomplices to a crime *for* him. Or for someone connected to him."

"And who benefits most from a child being declared dead?"

"The child himself. If someone was hunting him." Ulyana paused. "Or the person hiding him."

"Mm." Maren unfolded her hands, refolded them. "You're close. Closer than Dane Colter ever got, and he had twenty years and a badge. But you keep circling the wrong center of gravity, Miss Underhill. You think Aldous Wren was the author of that night. The town thinks it. The legend thinks it — the founder so ruthless he drowned his own heir rather than let the fortune pass. It's a good story. It has a villain with a name on the water tower." Her voice thinned. "It's also the story I let them believe, because the truth was worse, and the truth had a use for staying buried."

"Then tell me the truth."

Maren rose. It took her a moment — the old woman's body negotiating with itself — and she crossed to the mantel, where a row of framed photographs stood in a line like witnesses. She picked up the last one: a boy of eight or nine, gap-toothed, squinting into sun, standing on the mill pond dock with a fishing pole taller than he was.

"Do you know what my husband loved?" she said. "Not the mill. Not the town. Not me, though he was fond of me the way a man is fond of a well-built house. He loved *the will*. He wrote it himself, eleven times over thirty years, and each version was a little cathedral — clauses and contingencies, trusts nested inside trusts, every dollar assigned a duty. He called it his immortality. 'Money dies when it stops moving, Maren,' he used to say. 'My will keeps it moving forever.'" She set the photograph down. "And there was one flaw in his cathedral. One stone he couldn't carve around. Sebastian."

"The inheritance clause."

"The inheritance clause," Maren agreed. "Everything, unconditionally, to the grandson — provided the grandson lived. Aldous wrote it after our son died, because grief makes men superstitious and my husband was the most superstitious rationalist I ever met. He'd convinced himself the fortune was cursed to die with any bloodline but that boy's. So he bound it to Sebastian. Irrevocably. He showed me the final draft in the spring of 1974 and he was *glowing*, Miss Underhill. Like a man who'd finally gotten God to sign a contract."

Ulyana's memory laid the timeline flat: the will executed in spring, the drowning in August. Four months. "What changed?"

"Aldous changed. Or rather, Aldous stopped pretending." Maren came back to her chair but didn't sit; she stood over it, gripping its back, knuckles bloodless. "That summer he began to fail — the heart, the doctors said, though I knew it was something else. Something eating him from the inside that no stethoscope could find. And the closer he came to dying, the more unbearable the will became to him. Do you understand? He had built his immortality, and now he could feel death coming, and the will said that everything he was — the mill, the money, the *name* — would pour into a nine-year-old child. A child who cried at funerals. A child who once released every fish he caught because he couldn't stand their mouths opening and closing." Her mouth twisted. "Aldous began to call him *the leak*. As in, everything drains away through the leak."

"That's not hatred. That's—"

"It's arithmetic," Maren said. "In his mind. And in July of 1974, he sat across from me at that table—" she nodded toward the parlor, "—and he explained, very calmly, the way he explained everything, that there was a provision. An obscure one, from the third draft, never removed. If the named heir died before the testator, the estate reverted to the testator's absolute discretion. No conditions. No trust. He could burn it, bury it, leave it to the state. He had checked with Harlan Pike twice. It was iron." Her grip tightened on the chairback until the wood creaked. "And then he said, 'The boy swims alone on Thursdays. Nobody watches the north dock.'"

The room went very quiet. Outside, a crow called once from the elms and thought better of it.

"He was going to kill him," Ulyana said. Not a question. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, flattened, as if the words had passed through machinery.

"He was going to drown his grandson in the mill pond on a Thursday afternoon, and I was going to be a widow with a convenient heart condition and a locked study, and the town was going to weep for the poor founder's loss, and Aldous Wren was going to live another ten years spending every cent of a fortune he'd murdered to keep." Maren finally sat, and the sitting looked like surrender. "So I made sure Thursday came first."

* * *

Ulyana had interviewed hundreds of subjects. She knew the taxonomy of confession: the relieved, the defiant, the bargaining, the broken. Maren Wren was none of these. She confessed the way a surveyor reads off coordinates — precise, unhurried, as if the terrain had always been there and Ulyana was simply the first person to ask for the map.

"There were six of us who knew the whole shape of it," Maren said. "Harlan, because the reversion clause needed to be quietly neutralized — he drafted a codicil killing it, backdated, and Aldous signed it without reading, because by then he trusted his own legend more than his lawyer. Corliss Finch moved the money that night — real transfers, real accounts, so that if anyone ever audited the estate there'd be a financial trail consistent with a living heir. Emmett Voss certified the drowning and signed the death certificate; he owed Aldous his practice and hated him for it, which made him reliable. Dane Colter's father had debts Aldous owned like a leash, and Dane was a deputy who knew how to lose a report. And Odette Vale saw a man put a boy in a boat at dusk, and we paid her to say she'd seen nothing, and later to say she'd seen *Aldous*, because a witness pointing at the founder closed the case forever."

"And the sixth name on the ledger."

Maren's eyes came up slowly. For the first time all evening, something flickered there that wasn't control. "You've seen the initials."

"M.W."

"Then you know."

"I know what the initials spell. Initials aren't proof. Half this town is M-something W-something." Ulyana leaned forward. "Say it."

"I am Maren Wren," the old woman said. "I put my grandson in that boat myself. I rowed him to the north shore while Odette watched from the tree line, because a witness who believes she saw a murder tells a better story than a witness who suspects a rescue. I gave him to a family in Marquette who owed the mill three generations of debt and asked no questions. I came home, I let my husband hold me while I pretended to weep, and I watched him sleep that night knowing that in the morning he would wake up having lost everything he wanted — and never know why the pond had given him nothing." Her voice did not break. It *narrowed*, like a blade being drawn. "He spent two weeks trying to find out who'd taken the boy. He hired men. He threatened. And then the codicil surfaced, and Harlan showed him the reversion clause was dead, and I watched Aldous Wren understand, all alone at his own breakfast table, that his wife had beaten him. That his immortality had a hole in it shaped exactly like a child he'd tried to erase. He never touched the will again. He never touched *me* again. We lived in this house for nineteen more years like two generals in a frozen war, and when he finally started to actually die — really die, two years ago — he looked at me from that bed and said, 'You'll outlive me, Maren. You always were the stronger animal.' And I said, 'Yes, Aldous. That was always the plan.'"

Ulyana sat motionless. Her memory, that relentless archive, was filing everything — the exact cadence, the light through the west window, the photograph of the boy on the dock — and beneath the filing, something human was reeling. Every theory she'd built had assumed a monster at the center. The monster was here. The monster had saved a child's life by destroying his childhood, by making him a ghost, by teaching a nine-year-old that the safest thing he could ever be was dead.

"You didn't protect him," Ulyana heard herself say. "You erased him. Twenty years. He grew up with no name, no family, digging graves in the town that thinks he's dead. You call that salvation?"

"I call it *alive*," Maren said, sharp as a slap. "You want to weigh costs, investigator? Weigh them. One boy loses his name, or one boy loses his throat under four feet of pond water while his grandfather stands on the dock timing it. There was no third option. I looked for one for six weeks. There was no court that would take a Wren's word against a Wren's, no sheriff — Aldous *owned* the law here, he owned the bank, the church deacons, half the county bench. The only protection strong enough was death itself. Nobody hunts a dead child. Nobody sues a dead child. Nobody *drowns* a dead child twice." She drew a breath, and when she continued, the blade was sheathed again, and underneath it was something older and wearier. "I visited him. Twice a year, in Marquette, as 'a friend of the family.' I watched him grow up through a kitchen window. He was happy, for a while. Then the questions started — children always ask eventually — and the family got frightened, and he ran at sixteen, and I've tracked him ever since. When he came back to Grayhollow three years ago and took the groundskeeper's job at the cemetery—" her mouth did something that was almost a smile, "—I confess I admired it. Sleeping in the graveyard. Hiding inside his own legend. It was the most Wren thing he could possibly have done."

"Why are you telling me this?" Ulyana asked. "You've stayed silent for twenty years. Colter warned me off. Pike is dead. Finch is missing. Someone is killing the conspirators, and every hour I sit here, you're handing me the rope to hang you with. What do you want?"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. The light through the window had gone amber; the mill pond beyond had turned to hammered copper.

"Because the killings aren't mine," she said, "and they aren't random, and I'm an old woman who has run out of time to fix what I broke." She turned to face Ulyana fully. "Voss died two years ago — before any of this started, natural causes, I attended the funeral. Which means whoever signed Aldous's death certificate last month forged a dead doctor's hand. Think about that. Think about who would know Voss's signature well enough to forge it, who would have access to his old letterhead, his seal. And think about *why* — why would someone fake Aldous Wren's death now, twenty years after the fact, using methods only the original conspirators would recognize?"

Ulyana's mind was already moving, laying cards on the table of herself. "To reopen the wound. To make the town dig. To flush out—"

"To flush out *Sebastian*," Maren said. "Every death, every clue, every empty coffin — it's a trail of bread crumbs leading straight to my grandson. Someone wants him to surface. And someone wants the people who could contradict the new story dead before he does." She rose again, slowly, and stood looking down at Ulyana with those ice-pale eyes, and for the first time Ulyana saw what lived beneath fifty years of composure: terror, worn smooth as river stone. "I built one lie to save him. Someone is building a bigger one to destroy him. And there is a piece of this I have never told anyone, Miss Underhill — not Harlan, not Dane, not you — because saying it out loud makes it possible, and I have spent my whole life keeping impossible things impossible."

"What piece?"

Maren walked to the window. Her reflection floated in the glass, faint as a watermark.

"When Aldous lay dying two years ago," she said, "he didn't die."

The words dropped into the room like stones into deep water.

"He was past saving — the doctors gave him days. And on the last night, I sat with him, and he gripped my wrist with those terrible strong fingers, and he told me he'd known about the boat since the week it happened. Knew I'd done it. Had always known. And he laughed, Miss Underhill — a dying man laughing at me from his deathbed — and he said, 'You think you won. But I've been dead for twenty years, Maren. Dead men don't need heirs.' And in the morning the bed was empty."

Ulyana felt the floor of the world shift. "He was cremated. There's a certificate. There's a grave—"

"There's a grave with nothing in it," Maren said. "There has *always* been a grave with nothing in it. I filled the coffin with the shoe because a message should be legible — I wanted whoever opened it to understand that everything buried in this town was a lie. But Aldous—" her voice caught, the first fracture in all that ice, "—Aldous walked out of this house the night he died, or was carried, or was helped, and I have spent two years telling myself that a man that sick could not have survived, that grief and guilt were inventing ghosts. But the coffin was empty, and the certificate was forged, and the killings started, and every road in this mystery leads back to a man who should be ash." She turned from the window. "You wanted the truth about the drowning, investigator. Here is the rest of it: my husband may have faked his own death before I ever faked my grandson's. And if Aldous Wren is alive — if he has been alive, watching, waiting, arranging — then everything I did to save that boy was for nothing, because the one person who wanted him dead has had twenty years to learn patience."

Outside, far across the copper-colored water, the old mill clock began to strike the hour — six slow bells rolling over Grayhollow like a verdict.

And in the silence between the strokes, Ulyana remembered, with her perfect, merciless recall, the groundskeeper's face at dusk by the burning barrel — and the figure she had dismissed that same night as a trick of the light, standing at the cemetery gate, tall and gaunt, watching the flames with the posture of a man attending his own funeral.

She had filed the image and forgotten it.

Her memory never forgot anything.
