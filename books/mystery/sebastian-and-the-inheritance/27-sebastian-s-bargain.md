## 27. Sebastian's Bargain

The rain had stopped by the time Ulyana reached the cemetery, but the ground still drank at her shoes, and the yews along the north wall dripped like slow clocks. She came without a plan. That was the thing she could not stop noticing about herself lately — Ulyana Underhill, who cataloged everything, who filed every face and footprint in a memory that never slept, was now walking into a graveyard after dark with no plan at all, only a name on a page of a mill ledger and the knowledge that three men were dead and a fourth was missing and the ink on all of it pointed, eventually, to the man who kept this ground.

He was at the chapel, not the cottage. Light leaked under the door in a thin gold line, and through it she heard the small industrious sound of a nib on paper.

She knocked once.

The scratching stopped. A long silence, long enough for her to count the raindrops still falling from the eaves — eleven — before the door opened and Sebastian Wren stood in the gap with a candle behind him and his grandfather's eyes looking out of a tired young face.

"You shouldn't be here," he said.

"Neither should you." She stepped past him into the chapel, because standing in doorways in Grayhollow had become, in her professional opinion, an excellent way to die. "Harlan Pike died in a doorway. Or near one. The staging was sloppy."

"I heard." He closed the door and set the candle on the vestment table, and she saw what he'd been writing: several sheets covered in his careful hand, the first one headed with words that stopped her breath.

*To whom it may concern, I, Sebastian Aldous Wren—*

She read fast. Her memory always read fast; that was its curse and its gift. By the time he turned back from the candle she had absorbed the whole first page — a confession of identity, a renunciation of claim, a public statement surrendering any right to the Wren estate, witnessed by no one, dated today.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard me out."

"I don't need to hear you out. I've read your suicide note." She kept her voice level, professional, the voice she used with grieving beneficiaries, and hated how much practice she'd had. "That's what this is. You sign this, you announce it, and within a week there'll be a fourth body, and it won't matter what you've signed because dead men can't testify to their own renunciations."

Sebastian looked at the pages as if seeing them for the first time. Then he sat down on the old pew, elbows on knees, hands hanging between them — those hands, scarred and earth-stained, the groundskeeper's hands that had held hers once across a table scattered with ledger paper.

"Then tell me what else there is," he said quietly. "Tell me what I'm supposed to do, Ulyana. Corliss Finch is missing off the water. Walt Grady is dead in his own vat room. Harlan hanged himself — or someone hanged him and called it peace. Every name on that ledger is being crossed out, and mine is written above them all in my grandfather's hand." He looked up. "I'm the inheritance. That's all I am to whoever is doing this. An obstacle with a pulse."

"Then we protect the pulse."

"With what? Colter's compromised. Maren won't leave her house. Odette is gone, scared half to death, and I did that — I did that by trusting the wrong instinct, by telling you where she was because I thought—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I thought someone was watching me. I thought if I moved fast enough I could outrun it. And now her boarding house is ash and she's running again at seventy years old, and that's on me."

"That's on the person who burned it," Ulyana said. "You don't get to carry other people's crimes. God knows this town has tried that trick for fifty years and look where it got everyone."

He almost smiled. It was terrible to see, that almost-smile, because it reminded her of the photograph in the 1974 file — a boy of nine on a dock, squinting into sun, mid-laugh, one shoe already off. She had matched that shoe to the one in the coffin. She had matched everything. That was her function in the world: to match things, to make patterns hold. And the pattern forming now was one she wanted desperately to break.

"Listen to me," she said, and sat beside him on the pew, close enough that the candlelight put their shadows together on the far wall, one shape. "Think about what the killer actually wants. Not revenge — Merritt's grievance is real but it doesn't explain the method. Not silence — the silence was bought decades ago and the buyers are dying, which destroys the silence. What's left?"

Sebastian frowned. "The estate."

"The estate. Unclaimed. If every conspirator dies, the trust arrangements collapse back to the executor. But if *you* die — publicly, verifiably, this time with a body — then it doesn't matter how many conspirators are left breathing. The will names you. No you, no claim. The fortune passes through probate into the executor's hands clean as a whistle." She watched him arrive at it, watched the color leave his face by degrees. "Your renunciation doesn't save you. It just saves the killer the trouble of staging another drowning. You'd be doing the paperwork for your own murder."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The candle guttered and steadied. Somewhere outside, water dripped from the yews, patient as arithmetic.

"So I'm trapped either way," he said finally. "Live, and I'm a target. Sign, and I'm a faster one."

"No." Ulyana took the pages from the table and held them up. "This is the trap because it's *private*. A quiet renunciation helps no one but the killer. But a public one—" She let the idea form as she spoke it, letting him watch her think, which was a thing she had never done for anyone. "A public one, announced, witnessed, reported, changes the math. Whoever is killing wants the inheritance unclaimed *quietly*. They want the town never quite sure Sebastian Wren existed. The moment you stand up in front of the whole of Grayhollow and say *here I am, and here is what I refuse*, you stop being a rumor they can erase and become a fact they'd have to explain."

"And facts," Sebastian said slowly, "have witnesses."

"Facts have witnesses," she agreed. "And witnesses have names, and this killer has been very careful so far to kill alone, in the dark, one at a time. Publicity is the one weapon they can't poison."

He looked at her for a long time. In the candlelight his grandfather's eyes were less eerie than they'd seemed the first day, when she'd seen them over a shovel among the headstones; they were just eyes, tired and stubborn and, underneath, afraid in a way he would never say aloud.

"There's a will reading," he said. "Harlan's successor — a young lawyer named Pruett, came down from the county — he's convening it. End of the month. Whole town will come, half for grief and half for the show." His mouth twisted. "My grandfather would have loved that. He built this place on shows."

"Then that's your stage."

"You're asking me to walk into a room full of people whose families spent twenty years pretending I drowned, and announce myself to them, while a murderer counts heads."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "That's exactly what I'm asking."

"It might get me killed."

"It might." She heard her own voice go quiet. "It also might be the only version of this where you're alive in December. I've run it every way my memory can run it, Sebastian, and every private road ends with your name on a stone. This is the only exit I can find that isn't a coffin."

He reached over and took the pages from her hand. For a moment she thought he was going to tear them, and something in her chest clenched — not at the loss of evidence, she realized, but at the loss of him, and the clarity of that feeling frightened her more than anything Merritt Wren or Sheriff Colter or the whole rotten architecture of Grayhollow had managed in two months of digging.

Instead he set them on his knee, took up the pen, and began to revise.

"Not a renunciation," he said, writing as he talked. "Not yet. A conditional one. I'll offer it — publicly, at the reading, in front of God and the mill and everybody. My full claim, surrendered, on one condition: that the killings stop. That whoever is doing this understands they've won. There's nothing left to kill for." He glanced up. "It's a lie, partly. I'd be surrendering to a murderer. But it might make them believe they've won, and believing they've won is when they get careless."

Ulyana stared at him. "That's — actually clever."

"Don't sound so surprised."

"I'm surprised you've been holding out on me. That's strategy. That's investigator thinking."

"I had twenty years," he said, "of sitting in a graveyard watching this town lie to itself. You learn to think about what liars need." He wrote on, the nib scratching, and she memorized the sentences as they formed — she couldn't help it, her mind filing each line even as she wished, briefly, absurdly, that she could experience a single moment of her life unrecorded. *I, Sebastian Aldous Wren, being of sound mind and tired heart...*

When he finished, he read it aloud to her, twice, and she corrected a date, and he initialed the correction, and then they sat in the candle-smelling dark of the little chapel like two conspirators of an entirely different kind.

"There's one more thing," she said.

He waited.

"When you stand up at that reading — when you say your name out loud for the first time in front of this town — I want you to look at Merritt Wren. Don't accuse him. Don't stare. Just look at him the way family looks at family, and watch his hands."

"His hands?"

"Guilty men guard their hands. I've watched it in forty depositions. Innocent men gesture. Guilty men keep them still, like they're afraid of what they might pick up." She stood, suddenly restless, suddenly aware of how late the hour had grown and how many nights were left before the end of the month. "And Sebastian — the condition in your statement. The killings stop. Make sure Pruett reads it word for word. Because if our killer hears that their work is finished, they may believe it. And if they believe it—"

"They stop," he said.

"And if they stop," Ulyana said, "they start covering. And covering means records, travel, mistakes. Twenty years of patience ends the moment they think the game is won. People relax. People talk. People book train tickets."

Sebastian rose too. Up close he smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil and cold earth, the smell of the cemetery, the smell she had begun — unforgivably — to associate with safety.

"Ulyana." He said her name carefully, like a document he didn't want to crease. "If this goes wrong. If I stand up in that room and I'm wrong about the room—"

"Then I'll be standing next to you," she said, "with every fact I've ever memorized, and I promise you I have never once lost an argument with a fact."

He laughed — really laughed, short and rough, the first she'd heard from him — and for a moment the chapel felt less like a tomb and more like a room where people lived.

She left him copying the statement in fair hand, three copies, one for Pruett, one for the county record, one she would carry herself, sewn into the lining of her coat where the intruder with Colter's gait had never thought to look. Outside, the clouds had torn open and the moon lay across the graves like a sheet of ice, and she walked the rows slowly, reading names she already knew by heart.

It was halfway to the gate that she stopped.

Because her memory, faithful and merciless, had just finished cross-referencing the evening — the candle, the pages, the dates, the reading scheduled for the end of the month — against one small detail from the county archive, from a folder she had handled six weeks ago and photographed with her mind the way she photographed everything.

The will of Aldous Wren contained a codicil. A single paragraph, added in the final year, in handwriting she had noted at the time as *similar to testator's but pressure lighter, rightward slant increased.*

She had assumed age. Illness. A trembling hand.

But Aldous Wren had supposedly died strong, sudden, mid-stride — no illness, no decline. And a lighter-pressure hand, slanting rightward, belonged to a different writer entirely.

The codicil — the paragraph that governed how the estate passed if the grandson could not be found — had not been written by Aldous Wren at all.

It had been written by the executor.

Ulyana stood in the moonlit cemetery with her breath smoking in the cold, and understood, with the terrible completeness that was both her gift and her punishment, that Sebastian's bargain was worse than useless — that the reading itself was the trap, that the stage had been built for him, that every road she had found out of the coffin led directly back into it.

Someone had designed a will with a hole in it shaped exactly like a living Sebastian Wren.

And the reading was in nine days.

* * *

Nine miles away, in a rented room above the feed store where the lamp burned low and the fair copy of his statement dried under a weight of books, Sebastian Wren paused over his work.

He had heard her footsteps fade down the gravel path, counted them out of habit — forty-one steps to the gate, the pause, the gate's iron complaint, then nothing. He had expected nothing else. That was the shape of their evenings now: an hour of papers, an hour of talk, and then the long solitary dark in which he kept the only vigil left to him.

But the pause at the gate had lasted too long. Forty seconds. Fifty. Long enough for a woman with a perfect memory to remember something.

He looked down at the statement, at the words *the killings stop*, and felt the old cold move through him, the cold of the lake water in 1974, the cold he had carried in his chest for twenty years like a swallowed stone.

She knows something, he thought. She remembered something tonight, standing at my gate, and whatever it is, she went out into the dark carrying it alone rather than bring it back inside.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the blackness, listening to the yews drip, and made himself a promise that frightened him more than any grave: whatever Ulyana Underhill had remembered, he would not let her face it alone.

Even if facing it meant standing up in front of the whole lying town and saying his name.

Even if it killed him.

Especially then.
