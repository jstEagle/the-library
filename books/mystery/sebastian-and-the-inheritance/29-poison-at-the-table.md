## 29. Poison at the Table

The reading of Aldous Wren's will was held in the estate office on Wren Street, a room Ulyana had catalogued twice before and now read again with the particular hunger of someone who knows she will need every detail later.

It was a long room, taller than it was wide, built in the mill's prosperous decades when the town's money wanted to be seen. Dark wainscoting. A marble fireplace that had never, in living memory, held a fire. Portraits of three generations of Wrens hung along the north wall, their painted eyes following the folding chairs that Harlan Pike's successor — a pale, sweating man named Ellery Dunn from the county seat — had arranged in four uneven rows. Forty-one chairs. Ulyana counted them without meaning to. She counted everything now; it was the way her mind breathed.

The whole town had come. Not because they loved Aldous Wren — most of them had hated him quietly for fifty years, the way you hate a dam that holds back your river but could also drown you — but because the will was the last page of a story Grayhollow had been telling itself since the coffin came up empty. They sat shoulder to shoulder in funeral clothes borrowed or resurrected from closets: mill families, shopkeepers, the Vales' distant cousins who'd driven in from Kestrel County, old men who remembered the founder's handshake and young women who only knew his name from the water tower.

Maren Wren sat in the front row, black-gloved, her spine straight as a fence post. Sheriff Dane Colter stood by the door with his arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man guarding a room he wished he could burn down. And in the third row, aisle seat, sat Sebastian Wren — the drowned boy, the groundskeeper, the ghost made flesh — in a gray suit that fit him like an apology, his burn-scarred hand resting open on his knee where anyone could see it.

He had insisted on coming openly. *If I hide now,* he'd told her two nights before, *I'll be hiding for the rest of my life.* Ulyana had argued, then stopped arguing, because she recognized the thing underneath his voice. It was the same thing that had carried her through foster homes and filing rooms and twenty years of other people's paperwork: the need to stand in a room and be counted.

She should have trusted her own instincts instead. Her instincts had been screaming since dawn.

* * *

The morning had begun wrong in small ways, and her memory had filed each one without asking permission.

Ellery Dunn arriving forty minutes late, flustered, saying his car had trouble on the ridge road — though there was no dust on the undercarriage, no mud on the tires, nothing to suggest any road at all. The estate office unlocked but empty when they arrived, smelling faintly of something medicinal beneath the usual paper-and-wood. The pen.

That was the detail that kept snagging. The pen.

Dunn had produced it from the executor's desk drawer with a little ceremony, a heavy black fountain pen with a silver clip, announcing that protocol required the heir to sign a formal renunciation or affirmation of claim before the will could be executed — a document Sebastian had drafted himself, per their bargain. *I surrender my claim publicly if the killings stop.* He'd signed it at the desk while Dunn witnessed, and Ulyana had stood three feet away and watched the ink dry, and something about the ink had bothered her all morning without resolving into thought. It had dried strangely. Slowly. With a faint iridescent sheen, like oil on water, like —

Like the residue she'd seen, once, photographed in evidence, inside the preservation vats of the old Wren mill.

She hadn't known it yet at nine a.m. That was the cruelty of her gift: it recorded everything and explained nothing until explanation arrived, and by then it was often too late to matter.

* * *

Dunn cleared his throat and began to read.

Ulyana half-listened, tracking the room instead. Maren's gloved hands folded and still. Colter's weight shifting from left foot to right. Corliss Finch's widow dabbing her eyes — grief or performance, impossible to say, and Finch himself still missing these eleven days, his boat found drifting off Gull Rock with the tiller lashed straight. The former foreman's brother-in-law sitting rigid, angry, a man waiting for a verdict. In the back row, half-hidden behind a pillar, a face Ulyana did not know and could not place, which itself was a kind of alarm; she placed faces the way other people placed phone calls.

"—and to the town of Grayhollow, in trust, the sum of—"

Sebastian stood.

He'd been asked to rise when his name was read, and he rose, and Ulyana saw it happen in fragments her mind would replay ten thousand times afterward: the slight sway first, so small it might have been the chair. His hand going to the edge of the pew-back in front of him. The color leaving his face not gradually but all at once, like a tide pulled out too fast. Then his knees.

"Sebastian—" She was moving before the word finished leaving her mouth, shoving past the foreman's brother-in-law, catching him as he went down. He was heavy in her arms, heavier than a falling man should be, and his skin under her hands was already cold and wrong, clammy in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

His eyes found hers. They were Aldous Wren's eyes, the ones she'd noticed across a grave the first week, and they were dilating, the pupils swallowing the gray-blue whole.

"The pen," he said. Or she thought he said. His lips moved around the words and what came out was mostly breath. "Ulyana. The—"

Then the convulsion took him, and the room erupted.

* * *

Chairs scraped. Someone screamed. Colter shouted for everyone to stay back and simultaneously pushed forward, and for one suspended second the room was pure noise and motion, forty-one chairs and forty-two people and none of them knowing what to do with their hands.

Ulyana knew. Her body knew before her mind finished assembling the sentence. She rolled Sebastian onto his side as the seizure racked him, wedged her jacket under his head, and heard herself shouting in a voice she didn't recognize as her own: "Somebody call the ambulance! Now! Tell them poisoning, tell them convulsions, tell them—"

Maren Wren was suddenly beside her, gloves off, kneeling on the hardwood in her funeral dress without a moment's hesitation, and whatever else Maren was — architect of a fake drowning, keeper of a twenty-year silence — her hands were steady and her voice was iron. "Turn him, girl. Keep his airway. I've seen this before." A beat, and her eyes flicked up, and Ulyana saw the recognition land in them, cold and total. "The vats," Maren whispered. "Dear God. It's the vat compound."

The ambulance took nine minutes. Ulyana would know; she counted every one of them against the sound of Sebastian's breathing going shallow and ragged against her knee, against Maren's low counting — *breathe, boy, breathe* — against Colter's radio crackling at the door. Nine minutes is either an eternity or nothing, depending on whose heartbeat you're listening to.

When the paramedics loaded him, Ulyana climbed in after without asking anyone's permission, and no one asked her to leave.

* * *

They worked on him in the back of the rig and she stayed out of the way, pressed against the wall beside the oxygen tank, and her mind — her treacherous, magnificent, merciless mind — went to work.

Because that was the thing about catastrophe. It didn't scatter her. It focused her. Every crisis she'd ever survived had taught her the same lesson: when the world catches fire, the only thing you can save is the record. So she saved the record.

The pen. Start there. The pen.

Heavy black barrel, silver clip, kept in the executor's desk drawer — no. Correction. She replayed it frame by frame, the way she could replay anything: Dunn reaching into the drawer, yes, but the pen had been lying on top of a leather blotter, presented rather than retrieved, as if placed there in advance. Placed. By whom? Dunn had arrived late, flustered, claiming car trouble with no dirt on his car. Had Dunn prepared the room? Or had someone prepared it for him?

The ink. Watch the ink. Sebastian signing, the nib whispering across the page, and the line drying slow, too slow, with that faint oily iridescence — and here her memory reached back through weeks and pulled the photograph forward, the evidence photo from the mill, the preservation vats drained and rust-streaked, and along the rim of the nearest vat a dried residue with exactly that sheen, exactly that refusal to dry clean. The compound the mill had used to treat timber, the compound the coroner had found in the foreman's blood, the compound that killed slow at low doses and catastrophically fast in concentration.

In ink. Someone had compounded it into ink.

Her stomach turned over. Because she understood then, with the terrible speed of a mind that only ever needed one fact to finish a building, exactly how it had been done. Sebastian had signed a confession — a public surrender of his claim, drafted to stop the killings. Whoever killed him didn't want the killings to stop. Whoever killed him wanted the inheritance unclaimed, and a dead claimant accomplishes in one stroke what six murders had failed to do cleanly. And better than dead: dead *by his own signature*. Dead holding a document that said, in his own hand, *I give up everything* — a confession the killer could produce afterward as proof of consent, of settlement, of a life closed out neatly on paper.

Killed by paperwork. It was almost beautiful. It was the most obscene thing she'd ever imagined, and she had spent her career imagining obscene things done with documents.

Who touched the pen? Run the tape.

Dunn, presenting it. Dunn, capping it after. Sebastian, signing. Herself, standing three feet away, watching the ink dry. And behind Dunn — she dragged the frame wider, wider, her memory pulling peripheral detail out of storage like drawers opening — behind Dunn, near the door, Colter, arms crossed, watching. And passing behind the desk in the minutes before the reading began, while Dunn fussed with his papers—

A figure. Half-second of footage her brain had filed and flagged and never resolved. Tall, unhurried, gray at the temples, pausing at the executor's desk for the length of time it takes to set something down or take something away. She had registered it as nothing. Staff. An attendant adjusting the room.

But the estate office had no staff that morning. Dunn had come alone. She'd verified it, because she verified everything.

The figure turned its head slightly as it passed the window, and the light caught the profile, and even now, even in the ambulance with Sebastian's hand going cold in hers, her memory refused to resolve the face — because the angle had been wrong, the light had been wrong, the half-second had been too short. But the build. The carriage. The way the man's shoulders sat, square and proprietary, like a man in a room he considered his.

She'd seen that posture before. At the funeral home, the first day, standing apart from the mourners. At the cemetery, at a distance, hat brim low. Once outside the county archive, once outside the diner, once — she was sure of it now, sure with the sick certainty of a pattern completing itself — outside Odette Vale's boarding house, the night before it burned.

Every scene. Every single scene. The man had been at every scene, patient as weather, and she had filed him as background because background was what he made himself.

And the posture, the shoulders, the proprietary stillness — she knew where she'd learned to read that posture. From the portraits. Three generations of Wren paintings on the north wall of the estate office, and the third generation, the founder's younger brother, cut from the will sixty years ago and never mentioned since, hung at the end of the row with his arms crossed and his chin lifted and his shoulders set exactly, exactly like that.

Merritt Wren.

The name surfaced with the force of a door breaking open. The estranged brother. The executor of record before Harlan Pike, retired from the role years ago but still, according to the probate file she'd memorized, holder of keys to the estate office. Holder of a key to the desk drawer. Holder of sixty years of grievance, compounding like interest.

The sixth name. The torn page. *M.W.*

"It's Merritt," she said aloud, and the paramedic looked up from the monitor. "The poison, the pen, the fires — it's Merritt Wren. He's been in the room the whole time."

"Ma'am, you need to sit back—"

"He's the executor emeritus. He has keys. Check the sign-in ledger at the estate office, check who accessed the desk—" She was talking too fast and she didn't care. "Where's the ambulance going?"

"County hospital. Twenty minutes."

Twenty minutes. She looked down at Sebastian. The paramedics had him intubated now, lines running into his arm, the monitor stuttering through rhythms she didn't want to learn the names of. His face was gray. Twenty years ago this town had let him die on paper to protect its fortune, and today someone had tried to finish the job with a fountain pen, and if he died in the next twenty minutes then every document she'd ever memorized would amount to exactly nothing, because the truth only mattered if someone lived long enough to hear it.

His fingers twitched against hers. She squeezed them.

"Hold on," she said. "You hold on. You've done it before."

* * *

The ambulance screamed through Grayhollow's streets, past the shuttered mill, past the water tower with the founder's name peeling off it letter by letter, and Ulyana Underhill sat in the corner with a dying man's hand in hers and began, methodically, silently, to build the case.

Not the poison case. That would need labs, chain of custody, the coroner, time. She was building the other case, the one that lived entirely inside her head, the one no one could burn or steal or tear a page from: the full architecture of Merritt Wren's twenty-year war. The pen traced to the estate office. The keys traced to Merritt. The compound traced to the mill vats — and who had managed the mill's chemical stores in its final operating years? She dredged the employment records she'd photographed in week one. Foreman under Aldous. Then, after the closure, custodian of the site. Industrial access, twenty years of it, unwatched.

The ledger payments — six names, hush money flowing monthly for two decades — and Merritt cut out of the will entirely, cut out of the conspiracy, cut out of everything except the knowledge of it. He hadn't needed to be paid. He'd needed only to wait, and watch, and keep the keys.

And Aldous himself. Maren had told her: *he fled after the scheme collapsed.* Fled where? Or — Ulyana's blood went cold — fled *when*? What if the founder hadn't fled at all? What if the empty coffin wasn't Maren's message alone? What if Merritt had gotten to his brother years ago, quietly, the same way he'd done everything — patiently, without witnesses — and the empty grave was the one piece of evidence Merritt had never been able to retrieve?

The ambulance hit a pothole and Sebastian's monitor stuttered. The paramedic swore softly and adjusted something.

"Pressure's dropping," the other one said.

Ulyana leaned close to Sebastian's ear, past the oxygen mask, past the smell of antiseptic and the copper tang of emergency.

"Merritt Wren," she whispered. "I know. Do you hear me? I know who it is. Stay alive so you can watch me prove it."

His eyelids fluttered. Whether he heard, whether it was the drug or the dying or some last stubborn ember of the boy who'd survived a staged drowning and twenty years of graves, she couldn't say. But his fingers curled around hers, weak as a child's, and held.

At the hospital doors, orderlies swarmed the gurney and the world became corridor-light and shouted vitals and swinging doors, and Ulyana was peeled away into a waiting room that smelled of floor wax, and she stood there alone with her coat still smelling of him and her mind already turning toward the estate office, toward the desk drawer, toward the pen that would still be there unless Merritt Wren had already gone back for it.

She checked her watch. 11:47 a.m.

The reading had ended at 10:15. Ninety-two minutes. Enough time for a careful man to return to an empty room, collect his instrument, and vanish.

Or enough time for a careful man to believe the job was finished — and stop being careful.

Ulyana walked out of the hospital, flagged the first deputy she saw, and said five words she'd been saving for someone:

"Arrest warrant. Merritt Wren. Murder."

The deputy stared at her. Behind him, at the far end of the hospital parking lot, a gray sedan that had been idling near the exit eased out onto the road and drove, unhurried, toward the ridge.

Toward the estate.

Toward the pen.
