## 31. Merritt's Reckoning

The reading room of the Wren estate office had once been the mill's counting floor. You could still see it in the bones of the place — the ceiling beams hewn from old-growth fir, the iron hooks where ledgers had hung like cured meat, the tall windows set high enough that no clerk could look out and dream of anywhere else. Someone had papered over the brick with a genteel cream stripe and hung oil portraits of dead Wrens, but the room remembered what it was. Ulyana Underhill felt that memory pressing against her like weather.

Thirty-one people sat in folding chairs arranged in a shallow arc. She had counted them without meaning to, the way she counted everything — the way she had counted them at the funeral home, at the graveside, at every stage of this slow unspooling. Thirty-one chairs. Fourteen empty, because fourteen people in Grayhollow had decided, in the last three weeks, that they had somewhere more urgent to be. She knew their names. She knew, in several cases, why they weren't coming.

Sebastian sat in the front row, on her left, in a gray suit that fit him like a borrowed life. He had shaved. Someone — Maren's old housekeeper, Ulyana guessed — had cut his hair. He looked like a photograph of a man rather than a man, and his hands were folded in his lap with the deliberate stillness of someone holding himself together at the seams. The poison was out of him, mostly. The antidote had come from a steel cabinet in the mill's sub-basement, from a protocol written in 1961 for workers who fell into the preservation vats, and it had pulled him back from the gray edge with forty minutes to spare. But his fingertips were still faintly yellowed, and when he thought no one was looking, he pressed his tongue to the roof of his mouth and winced, tasting the compound's copper ghost.

Ulyana's folder sat on her knees. Inside it: forty-one pages of reconstruction, three photographs, one torn ledger page, and a copy of a death certificate signed by a doctor two years dead. She had arranged the pages in the order she would need them, though she did not need the order. She had them all, every line, every date, every signature, filed in the perfect cold archive behind her eyes. Her memory was the one inheritance she had never questioned.

At the front of the room, behind the estate's long walnut table, sat Merritt Wren.

He was eighty-one years old and he wore his age the way some men wear a uniform — correctly, without apology. Silver hair combed straight back. A charcoal vest over a white shirt with no wrinkle in it. Reading glasses pushed up onto his forehead like a second pair of eyes. He had been the estate's executor for thirty years, and before that he had been the brother who stayed, the brother who ran the mill's books while Aldous ran the mill's town, and if you looked at the two portraits hanging on the wall behind him — Aldous at forty, all jaw and ambition; Merritt at thirty-five, thinner, watchful, standing half a step behind — you could read the whole tragedy in the composition of the paint.

Merritt was speaking. He had been speaking, in a dry unhurried voice, for eleven minutes, walking the assembled town through the formalities of probate with the patience of a man reading a grocery list. Ulyana had stopped listening to the content. She was watching his hands.

They were perfectly still on the walnut. That was the thing. Every other person in this story — Colter with his guilty boots, Harlan Pike with his trembling pen, Maren with her riddles — had betrayed themselves in the hands first. Merritt's hands were resting on the table like objects placed there, and Ulyana thought: *a man who is about to lose everything fidgets. A man who has already won does not.*

"—and with the matter of the confession," Merritt was saying, "we come to the question of the estate's true heir."

He lifted his glasses down from his forehead and put them on. He looked out over the folding chairs with mild, patient eyes, the eyes of a man who had spent half a century being underestimated by people who thought slowness meant softness.

"Before I proceed," he said, "I wonder if Miss Underhill would be good enough to come forward. She has, I understand, prepared remarks of her own."

Every head turned. Ulyana felt the attention land on her like a hand on her shoulder. Beside her, Sebastian's stillness changed quality — from composure to readiness — and she touched his wrist once, lightly. *Wait.*

She stood. She walked to the front of the room with her folder, and she did not stop at the table. She stopped three feet from Merritt, close enough to see that his shirt collar had been laundered so many times it had gone translucent at the fold, close enough to see that behind the mildness his pupils were small and dry and absolutely focused.

"Mr. Wren," she said, "before you proceed, I'd like to enter some documents into the record."

"Of course," he said pleasantly.

She opened the folder. She laid out the pages one by one on the walnut, squaring their edges, and she spoke to the room without turning around, because her voice carried and she knew it did.

"The death certificate of Aldous Wren was signed by Dr. Emmett Voss. Dr. Voss died on March 4th, two years before the date on that signature. I verified the handwriting. It is a tracing — a good one, done over a lightbox, but a tracing. The pen lifts at the terminal stroke of the *s*, which the real Dr. Voss never did in forty years of practice." She laid down the next page. "The coffin interred on the fourteenth of October was empty except for a single child's shoe, size nine, matching a pair catalogued in the 1974 drowning investigation as belonging to Sebastian Wren, age nine, presumed drowned in the Gray River. No body was ever recovered. The investigating deputy, Dane Colter, has provided a sworn statement regarding the suppression of that file."

She laid down the ledger page. "Payments. Six names. Monthly, for twenty years. Harlan Pike. Corliss Finch. Otis Brandt. Dane Colter. Maren Wren. And a sixth name torn from the record — but I recovered it." She let the pause sit. "M.W."

The room had gone very quiet. Somewhere in the back a chair creaked, and someone's breath came out audibly, the sound of a town exhaling a thing it had been holding for weeks.

"Merritt Wren," Ulyana said, and turned to face him at last. "Executor of the estate. Aldous's brother. Cut from the will in 1969 after the quarrel over the river rights. The one name on that ledger who never received a payment — because he wasn't being paid to stay quiet. He was the reason the others had to."

Merritt looked at the pages on his table for a long moment. Then he did something Ulyana had not anticipated, something her memory would replay for years afterward in perfect detail: he took off his reading glasses, folded them, set them down beside the ledger page with a small precise click, and smiled.

It was not a cruel smile. That was the terrible part. It was the smile of a man setting down a heavy load he had carried a very long way.

"Well," Merritt Wren said. "You are everything they said you were."

* * *

He did not deny it. That was the first shock. Ulyana had prepared for denial, for lawyers, for the dry procedural fog a man like this could generate for months. She had not prepared for the calm.

"Miss Underhill has the shape of it," Merritt said to the room, in the same voice he had used for the probate formalities, as if the confession were simply another item on the agenda. "Though she has the order wrong, and the order matters. It usually does, in stories about money. May I?" He gestured at the pages. "I'll want to correct a few details while I still can."

"Mr. Wren—" said Harlan's successor, a pale young attorney named Delacroix who had inherited the estate's business along with its dangers, and whose voice now climbed half an octave. "Mr. Wren, I don't think—"

"You don't think," Merritt agreed gently. "That has been the estate's great good fortune for thirty years. Sit down, Thomas."

Delacroix sat.

Merritt rose from behind the table. He was not a tall man — age had folded him down toward the earth — but the room's attention came with him, all of it, thirty pairs of eyes and the portraits of the dead Wrens watching over his shoulder. He straightened his vest. He clasped his hands behind his back, and he began, in the tone of a man delivering a eulogy he had written himself long ago.

"Aldous Wren built this town, and everyone in this room has spent their life being grateful for it, because gratitude was cheaper than the alternative. He built it on a river, and he owned the river, and he owned the debt of every man who ever took a paycheck from the mill, and he called that ownership *providence*." His voice did not rise. It lowered, if anything, the way a blade lies down. "In 1969 I told him the financing was rotten. The expansion — the great expansion, the one with his name on the water tower — was built on paper that would not survive an audit. I told him I would not sign it. He removed me from the will that week. He told the board I had suffered a nervous exhaustion. My own brother diagnosed me out of my inheritance over lunch."

Ulyana watched the room. She saw old Otis Brandt's widow press her hand to her mouth. She saw Colter, seated near the door in his sheriff's uniform, go the color of paper. She saw Maren Wren, who had insisted on attending in a wheelchair, her hands knotted in a gray blanket, watching her brother-in-law with an expression Ulyana could not at first name — and then could: it was the expression of a woman watching a wound she had always known was there finally open.

"So when Aldous came to me, twenty years ago, with a problem," Merritt continued, "I was in a unique position to help him. The problem was a boy. His own grandson. A boy whose existence — whose *legal* existence — would, upon Aldous's death, transfer control of the Wren fortune to a child, and through the child to whoever held the child's trust. And Aldous had learned, too late, that the person he had most reason to fear was inside his own house." Merritt's eyes moved, briefly, to Maren, and something flickered across his face that was almost tenderness, or its counterfeit. "He wanted the boy gone. Permanently. He asked me to arrange it, because arranging things was what I did, and because he knew I hated him, and he thought hatred was the same as usefulness."

"He asked you to kill a child," Ulyana said quietly.

"He asked me to solve a child," Merritt corrected, without heat. "I did not kill anyone that night, Miss Underhill. That is the detail you have wrong. I want it on the record, before the end, that I never intended the boat to go out. I went to Maren. Maren had already gone to the boy's mother. Between the three of us we built the drowning — the boat, the current, the witness paid to look away and then paid again to leave town. Odette Vale. A frightened girl of nineteen who saw a man put a child in a boat and was given two hundred dollars and a bus ticket to forget it." He shook his head slowly. "Aldous believed his grandson was dead. He mourned him — genuinely, I think, in the small cold room where he kept the things he genuinely felt. And the fortune stayed exactly where Aldous could reach it, and the boy grew up alive, and for twenty years I told myself that was the one good thing I had ever done with my hatred."

"And then?" Ulyana said. "Then you killed him anyway. The conspirators. Harlan Pike. Corliss Finch. Otis Brandt."

"Then Aldous decided to come back," Merritt said.

The words dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Ulyana heard it land in thirty separate minds at once.

"Two years ago, Aldous Wren learned that his grandson was alive. I don't know how — a photograph, a rumor, some small resemblance he caught in a stranger's face. It doesn't matter. What matters is what he did with it. He came to me, and he told me he was going to unmake the drowning. Publicly. He was going to bring the boy back into the light, restore him, rewrite the will — and then, when the boy stood in front of this town as the heir, Aldous was going to destroy him. The debts. The old evidence. The story of a mother who let her son be carried off in a boat. He was going to give Sebastian everything and then make him beg to give it back, and the town would watch, because the town always watches." Merritt's hands unclasped. "I could not allow it. Not for the fortune — though I will not insult you by pretending the fortune was nothing. But because I had spent twenty years keeping one person alive in this valley, and I was not going to watch my brother spend that life as entertainment."

"So you faked his death," Ulyana said. "Aldous's. The empty coffin."

"I faked his death years ago, yes," Merritt said, and now, finally, something moved under the calm — a tide coming in under ice. "The tracing, the doctor's name, the closed casket. Aldous is not dead, Miss Underhill. He is old, and he is hidden, and he is exactly where he can hurt no one — a room I pay for, in a place I will not name, with a window that looks at nothing. I took his death from him the way he took everything from everyone in this room. And I took control of the estate quietly, legally, through the instruments I had spent thirty years positioning, and for two years the machine ran smoothly." He looked down at the ledger page on the table. "Except that the machine had parts. Six people who knew the shape of the drowning. Six people who had been paid to be quiet, and who grew old, and who grew careless, and who began — one of them, at least — to talk in his sleep. Harlan kept a copy of the will he was never supposed to keep. Corliss wrote letters he was never supposed to write. Otis drank. And when Miss Underhill arrived with her insurance forms and her perfect memory and started pulling threads, I understood that the machine could not be repaired. It could only be dismantled."

"You poisoned them," Sebastian said.

It was the first time he had spoken. His voice came out low and rough, and it changed the room's temperature. He rose from the front row, and Ulyana did not stop him this time. He walked forward and stood beside her, and the resemblance to the portrait on the wall — to the young Aldous — was suddenly so plain that Ulyana heard someone in the third row gasp.

"The preservation compound," Sebastian said. "From the vats. The same stuff that nearly killed me last week, in the ink of my own confession." His yellowed fingertips curled at his sides. "You dosed the pen. You handed it to me yourself. You *watched* me sign it."

"Yes," Merritt said.

No elaboration. No justification. Just *yes*, delivered with the terrible tidiness of a man balancing a column of figures, and Ulyana understood in that instant that she had been wrong about the shape of him. She had thought, in the way investigators think, that monsters were made of appetite — greed, rage, lust, the loud fuels. But Merritt Wren was made of something quieter and far more durable. He was made of *bookkeeping*. Every death had been an entry. Every entry balanced. He had killed three men, tried to kill a fourth, and faked the death of his own brother, and he stood before all of them in a pressed vest feeling — she could see it in his face, in the small settling of his shoulders — *relieved*. The ledger was finally going to close.

"Why the others, then?" Ulyana asked. She kept her voice level, and she kept her eyes on his hands. "If Aldous is your prisoner and the boy was your project — why kill Harlan, Corliss, Otis? They were your allies. Your machine."

"Because a machine that has been discovered is not a machine," Merritt said. "It is evidence. And because—" and here, for the first time, his voice acquired something almost like grief, so thin you could have threaded a needle with it, "—because they had made a bargain with silence, and silence is a currency, and they kept spending it. Harlan was going to sell his copy of the will to a journalist in the spring. I know this because I read his mail, as I have read this town's mail for thirty years. Corliss had begun to believe Aldous's ghost was walking — he was right, in his way, and terror makes men talk. And Otis—" he paused. "Otis simply knew too much and remembered too well. I am told the poison was painless. I chose it for that reason. I would like that entered into the record as well."

"You'd like it entered," Ulyana repeated. Something in her chest had gone tight and hot. "Three men are dead, Mr. Wren. A woman is in a wheelchair because of what your brother did to her life. A boy spent twenty years buried alive in a cemetery, cutting grass over his own empty grave. And you'd like the *painlessness* entered into the record."

"Yes," Merritt said. "Because I am not a monster, Miss Underhill. I am an accountant. The difference is that monsters enjoy it."

He reached into his vest.

Ulyana's memory did what it always did in the half-second before catastrophe — it slowed the world and catalogued it. The motion of his right hand, inward, toward the left breast pocket, not the waistband. The weight shift onto his back foot. The way his eyes went not to her but past her, to Sebastian, and stayed there with an expression she could only call *protective*, which was the most horrifying thing in the room. The creak of Colter rising from his chair by the door. Maren's knuckles going white in the blanket. The high window light falling across the walnut table and the forty-one pages of truth laid out on it like a dealt hand.

"Merritt," Maren Wren said, and her voice cracked across the room like a whip. "Don't."

"There is a document in my pocket," Merritt went on, in that same dry, unhurried voice, as though the wheelchair-bound woman had not spoken, as though the sheriff were not on his feet, as though the entire town were not rising inch by inch out of its folding chairs like a tide coming in. "It is the true will. The one Aldous signed in 1987, before I took his death from him — the one that leaves everything to the trust, the one that ends the Wren ownership of this town forever. I have carried it for two years, waiting for a day when it could be read safely. That day is not today. That day may not exist." His hand came out of the vest, and in it was not a document. It was a revolver, old, well-kept, small in his large dry hand, and he held it the way he held everything — correctly, without apology, pointed at the floor but *present*, and the room's breath stopped as one body.

"Everyone in this room spent their lives being paid to look away," Merritt Wren said. "I am asking for thirty more seconds of it. Sebastian — my boy. Come stand by your uncle. What happens next is for you, and no one else, and I will not have it watched."

Sebastian did not move. His hand found Ulyana's sleeve — not to pull her behind him, she realized, but to keep himself standing.

"I'm not going anywhere with you," Sebastian said.

Merritt sighed, and the sigh was the saddest sound Ulyana had heard in all of Grayhollow, and the revolver came up off the floor.
