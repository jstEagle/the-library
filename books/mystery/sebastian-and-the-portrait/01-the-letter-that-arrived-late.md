## 1. The Letter That Arrived Late

Rosalind Dunmore had a theory about confessions: they were like umbrellas. Everyone owned one, most people never used them, and the ones that finally turned up always arrived at the worst possible moment — dripping, slightly bent, and impossible to ignore.

This one arrived on a Tuesday in October, in an envelope the color of weak tea, postmarked from a coastal village she'd have to look up twice before believing it was real.

She was halfway through her second cup of coffee and her first argument with the office radiator when the morning post was dropped on her desk. The radiator lost. It always did. She'd complained to building management four times, and each time a man named Gerry had come up, laid his palm against the metal like a doctor taking a pulse, and pronounced it "fine." Rosalind suspected Gerry of being on the radiator's payroll.

The envelope sat on top of a stack of circulars and one ominous-looking parcel from the actuarial department. It was addressed to *Meridian Assurance Group, Claims Department, Attn: R. Dunmore* — which meant someone had gone to the trouble of finding out not just her firm but her name. People who wrote to firms wrote to firms. People who wrote to *her* wanted something.

She slit it open with the bone-handled letter opener her father had given her when she passed her certification exams, along with the advice that "the truth is usually folded in thirds."

It was.

*To whom it may concern,* the letter began, in a hand that was old-fashioned without being fussy — a slanted, confident script with the kind of flourishes you only saw now in wedding invitations and forgery exhibits.

*I am writing to confess to the death of Edmund Dunmore of Wrenmoor House, on the night of the ninth of June last. He did not drown by misadventure. I held him under the water at the ebb tide, and I held him until he stopped.*

Rosalind set down her coffee. Not because her hands were shaking — they weren't; her hands never shook, a fact her ex-husband had once described as "deeply unsettling" during an argument about nothing in particular — but because she had learned, years ago, that the first read of a document was the only clean one. Every subsequent reading was contaminated by what you already believed. So she gave this one her full, undivided attention, the way she might study a face across a crowded room before deciding whether to approach.

The letter ran to three pages. It was thorough. Almost lovingly so.

It described the night of Edmund Dunmore's death: the fog coming off the water in slow grey ropes, the mudflats exposed by the ebb, the way the victim's torch had swung as he walked out onto the flats alone. It described where Edmund had been struck — not struck, the writer corrected itself, *held*, there had been no blow, she should check if she doubted it, there would be no bruising above the shoulders — and how long, in the writer's estimation, it had taken. It described the satchel Edmund carried, and what was in it, and where it had gone afterward.

Rosalind read that paragraph twice despite herself.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that made her sit very still in her draughty little office while the radiator clicked its idiot sonata: none of those details were public.

The coroner's report listed drowning by misadventure. The local paper, in its single paragraph on the matter, had managed to get both the victim's age and the location wrong. The insurance file — *her* file, Meridian's file, opened because Edmund Dunmore had been insured for a sum that made the actuaries hum thoughtfully to themselves — contained the rest. The satchel. The absence of bruising. The tide tables. The detail about the torch, which had washed up two days later, cracked, eleven feet from where the body was found, a discrepancy so small that only someone who had read the scene report line by line would ever have noticed it.

Someone had read the scene report line by line.

Or someone had *written* it.

She turned to the signature page. There was no name. Just a date — the eleventh of June, the day after Edmund Dunmore's funeral, though of course the postmark could lie; postmarks lied all the time, they were the alibis of the postal world — and beneath the date, a single line:

*You will know whose hand this is. That is why I am sending it to you.*

"Well," Rosalind said to the empty office. "That's not ominous at all."

---

Here is what you need to know about Rosalind Dunmore, and it is the reason her firm kept her on despite her habit of describing their marketing materials as "grief-adjacent stationery": she remembered everything she read. Not in the poetic way people claim to, with a fond glow and a shrug — *oh, I've got a good memory for faces* — but actually, mechanically, completely. A page went into her head the way a coin goes into a slot, and it did not come back out. Doctors had asked her questions about it. She had answered with citations.

Which is why, at 11:40 that morning, standing in front of the archive cabinet on the third floor with her coat still on, she felt the cold arrive in her chest before her brain had finished forming the sentence to explain it.

The Meridian archive held copies of everything the firm had ever touched, going back decades, because the firm's founders had believed — correctly, as it turned out — that paper was cheaper than lawsuits. And eleven years ago, Meridian had handled an estate claim involving a Wrenmoor document: a family trust instrument, signed by one Aldous Dunmore, twin brother of the late patriarch, dated 1989.

Rosalind pulled the folder. She laid the trust instrument on the table beside the confession letter, which she had thoughtfully brought with her in a plastic sleeve, because she was the sort of person who brought things in plastic sleeves.

The handwriting matched.

Not similar. Not *in the same family*. Matched. The same peculiar loop on the descender of the *y*. The same impatient crossbar on the *t*, struck left to right instead of right to left, a habit so unusual that graphologists wrote papers about it. The same tiny hesitation-mark before every capital letter, as though the writer paused at the top of each hill to admire the view.

Aldous Dunmore had died eleven years ago. Drowned, as it happened — the family seemed to have a relationship with water that Rosalind was beginning to find thematically insistent. His death had been ruled accidental too. His body had been recovered, buried, mourned, and filed.

And yet here was his hand, writing a confession to a murder committed last June.

She stood in the archive for a long time. Somewhere below her, Gerry was presumably telling another radiator it was fine. Outside, London went about its business, honking and steaming and generally refusing to be mysterious.

"Okay," she said quietly, to the folder, to the letter, to the dead man whose penmanship was behaving badly. "Let's say you're a forgery. Who forges a dead man's hand to confess to a murder nobody thinks is a murder?"

There were answers, of course. She could think of four off the top of her head, and three of them involved money, and the fourth involved something worse than money. Insurance fraud was the obvious one — a confession, even an anonymous and impossible one, was enough to freeze a payout, and freezing a payout benefited exactly one category of person: whoever was *supposed* to receive it and couldn't afford the scrutiny that came with it. Or perhaps someone wanted the case reopened for reasons of their own — guilt, revenge, a private war conducted through the postal system. Or perhaps —

Or perhaps the confession was true, and the question wasn't *who wrote it* but *how a dead man's hand had come to write it*, and that answer had a shape she didn't like at all, because it meant someone alive had learned to write like the dead, which meant someone alive had studied the dead closely enough to steal their hand off the page, and people who did that rarely stopped at handwriting.

Rosalind looked out the archive window at the grey sky and thought about the phrase *you will know whose hand this is.*

She did know. That was the problem. Knowing was her entire profession, her entire affliction, and the letter writer had addressed her by it — not by name, but by faculty. Whoever they were, they hadn't just found her firm. They had found *her*, the woman who remembered, and they had chosen her deliberately, the way you choose a lock you intend to open.

"That's flattering," she told the letter, "and I resent it."

Her supervisor, a kind, overworked man named Peter whose desk resembled a paper avalanche in suspended animation, took some convincing. This was standard. Peter's job was to resist spending money, and Rosalind's job was to make resisting more expensive than complying.

"A confession letter," Peter said, holding it at arm's length as though it might bite. "Anonymous. Postmarked months ago. And you want travel expenses to — " he squinted at the postmark — "somewhere with no train station."

"Wrenmoor. There's a branch line. I checked."

"You checked."

"I always check. It's the checking that makes me expensive." She tapped the plastic sleeve. "Peter. If this is fraud, we're sitting on a six-figure payout we shouldn't make, and the fraudster knows we know, which means they'll escalate. If it isn't fraud — " she let that hang.

"If it isn't fraud, then a dead man confessed to murder."

"Then a dead man confessed to murder," Rosalind agreed pleasantly, "and I'd very much like to ask him how."

Peter sighed the sigh of a man who had learned that Rosalind's pleasantness was load-bearing. "Two weeks," he said. "Expense cap. And Rosalind — don't do the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you come back with a whole other case nobody asked you to solve."

"I would never."

"You did it in Harwich."

"Harwich was *asking* for it."

He waved her off, already drowning again in his avalanche, and Rosalind went home to pack, which for her meant laying out three identical dark sweaters and a notebook she wouldn't need, because the notebook was a comfort object and she knew it.

That night, before bed, she read the confession one final time, and this time she noticed what she had been too disciplined to notice on the first pass — the small thing, the almost-nothing, tucked into the third page like a stone in a shoe.

The writer described the fog that night. *Fog off the water,* the letter said, *thick as wool, the kind that comes in ahead of the autumn gales.*

But Edmund Dunmore had died on the ninth of June. Early summer. Rosalind had read the weather records herself; the night in question had been clear and mild, the stars out, the tide ebbing under a half moon. There had been no fog. No fog at all.

Unless the writer wasn't describing that night.

Unless the writer was describing a different night entirely — a night years ago, a night with a body and an ebb tide and a missing satchel of its own — and had simply reached into the wrong drawer of memory.

Rosalind turned off the lamp and lay in the dark, cold-fascinated, obligation settling over her like a coat she hadn't chosen but would wear anyway.

Eleven years ago, Aldous Dunmore had drowned wearing a coat.

In the morning, she booked the branch line to Wrenmoor.
