## 7. The Second Funeral

The parish records office in the village of Lower Wrencombe smelled of dust, damp stone, and — incongruously — someone's microwaved fish pie. Rosalind Dunmore decided within minutes of arriving that she liked it here enormously. It was the anti-Wrenmoor: honest about its decay, unbothered by its own history.

"Insurance, you said?" The verger, a round woman named Mrs. Pettigrew with reading glasses on a chain and the air of someone who had catalogued every sin in the parish and forgiven most of them, led her down a narrow stair to the record room. "We don't get many inquiries about the Dunmores these days. Used to, mind. Before."

"Before what?"

Mrs. Pettigrew considered this. "Before everyone who remembered stopped remembering," she said pleasantly, and unlocked a cabinet that looked older than the church itself.

Rosalind had come armed with a specific question. Her photographic memory was not a party trick, whatever her colleagues at Meridian Mutual believed when they made her recite policy numbers at Christmas parties; it was a filing system, and last night it had flagged something she should have caught days ago. The confession letter referred to Aldous Dunmore's drowning in 1989 as fact — *I did what I did to Aldous, and God has kept the sea's silence for me* — but the phrasing was odd. Not *the death*. Not *the body*. *The silence*.

People confess to murders. They do not usually confess to silences.

She wanted the burial register for 1989.

The volume came out wrapped in acid-free tissue, and Mrs. Pettigrew watched with proprietary tenderness as Rosalind turned to the autumn pages. There it was, in faded iron-gall ink:

*Aldous Edmund Dunmore, aged 34. Drowned. Interred 14 November 1989.*

And beneath it, smaller, in different ink, added later like an afterthought or an apology:

*Body recovered by fisherman R. Tull. Identified by T. Fenwick, estate archivist.*

Rosalind read it three times. The letters didn't change. That was the trouble with a memory like hers; it never gave her the mercy of misreading something.

T. Fenwick. Tobias Fenwick, who had greeted her at Wrenmoor's door with tea already brewed and her name already known, who had shown her the archive with the pride of a man presenting his own organs for inspection.

"Well," she said aloud, because the alternative was letting the vertigo win. "That's convenient."

"What's convenient, dear?"

"Nothing." She smiled. "May I see the coroner's correspondence? If there is any. Sometimes parishes keep copies."

There was. A thin folder, thinner than it should have been, containing a single letter from the county coroner's office dated November 1989, acknowledging receipt of a burial certificate and noting — in a marginal hand that Rosalind would think about for the rest of her life — *no post-mortem performed; identification by next of kin's representative; remains commended to earth without examination per family request.*

No post-mortem. No examination. A drowned man buried on the word of one identifier, thirty-six years old at the time, an *assistant* archivist, and the file was thinner than her grocery lists.

"Mrs. Pettigrew," she said carefully, "in 1989, how were bodies identified? Formally, I mean."

"Police usually. Or family." The verger frowned. "Why, was there trouble over the Dunmore one? There was talk, you know. Village talk." She lowered her voice as though the records themselves might be scandalized. "They say the sea gave back only the coat. Mr. Aldous's coat, with his initials inside the collar. And then a week later there was a body, and Mr. Fenwick — young man then, very serious, always carrying papers — he came down and looked at it and said yes, that's him, that's Mr. Aldous, and that was that. My late husband was a sexton then. He always said it didn't sit right with him. Said the face wasn't right. But you couldn't argue with a man holding a folder full of documents, could you? Not in those days."

"No," Rosalind said quietly. "You still can't."

Her husband had been dead eleven years, this husband of Mrs. Pettigrew's, and yet here was his doubt preserved like a fly in amber, passed along secondhand across decades: *the face wasn't right.*

Someone had been buried as Aldous Dunmore. Someone whose face wasn't right. And the man who had certified the identity — the sole pillar on which the entire burial rested — was now the archivist of Wrenmoor, keeper of every record, smoother of every gap.

Rosalind stood very still in the cold little room and let the architecture of it assemble itself in her mind, floor by floor.

If Aldous Dunmore's coat had washed ashore but no body —

If a body had been found a week later, a stranger's body, any body —

If a young assistant archivist had identified it, wrongly, deliberately —

Then Aldous Dunmore had never died at all. He had walked out of the sea, out of his own name, into — what? Into the archive. Into the house. Into thirty-five years of meticulous, suffocating, gapless record-keeping.

Into Tobias Fenwick.

Or Fenwick into him. At this point Rosalind honestly couldn't say which direction the theft ran, and that, she thought, was rather the point. Identities, like tides, went both ways.

* * *

She bought Mrs. Pettigrew a sherry at the Anchor Inn — ostensibly as thanks, actually because village pubs were the finest intelligence agencies in England and worked for the price of two drinks — and by the bottom of her glass she had learned three things.

One: the fisherman R. Tull had died in 1994, so he couldn't contradict anything ever again, which was either luck or tidiness depending on your temperament.

Two: the body buried in 1989 had been found near the old groyne, half a mile from where Aldous's coat came ashore, which even a landlubber like Rosalind knew was the wrong direction for a drowning carried on the same tide.

Three — and this was delivered by a retired postman named Cyril with the relish of a man who had waited decades for someone to ask — that in the summer of 1989, before the drowning, there had been *two* Dunmore brothers seen about the village. Identical. Old Mr. Sebastian's boys. And after the funeral, well. One brother gone under the ground, and the other brother, Mr. Edmund, he'd gone strange. Quiet. Started poking about in the churchyard himself, years later, taking notes. Asking Cyril whether he remembered the funeral. Whether he remembered who'd identified the body.

"And did you tell him?" Rosalind asked.

"Told him what I've just told you, love." Cyril shrugged. "He wrote it all down in a little book. Then he went off home and a month later he was dead in the mud, and everyone said accident, and I said to my wife, I said, funny sort of accident, a man who can swim like an otter." He drained his pint. "But nobody asks a postman anything, do they. We only carry other people's secrets."

Rosalind paid for his next pint too. It was the least she could do for a man who had just handed her the motive, the method, and the timeline, and asked nothing but company in return.

Edmund had known. Edmund had been digging into the 1989 burial exactly as she was — and Edmund had drowned in the same mudflats, ruled accidental, insured, closed. And then, a day after his funeral, a confession letter had arrived written in the hand of a man who had officially been dead for thirty-four years.

The case file wasn't a case file anymore. She understood that now, standing at the pub window watching the grey water work at the flats below Wrenmoor's cliff. The case file was the crime scene. Every document she touched had been arranged. The archive wasn't a record of the family's history; it was the murder weapon, used twice, cleaned meticulously between uses.

* * *

She drove back up the cliff road as the light went coppery, and instead of going to her room she went to the long gallery, where the great portrait of the patriarch hung in its frame of carved laurels. The recent cleaning had been done beautifully — Fenwick's doing, presumably, everything at Wrenmoor was Fenwick's doing eventually — and in the low evening light the paint seemed almost wet, the old man almost breathing.

Sebastian Dunmore, painted in 1958, stern and prosperous, one hand on a globe of the world he owned most of.

Rosalind took out her phone, turned on the flashlight, and held it at a raking angle against the canvas, the way conservators did, the way she'd read about at two in the morning unable to sleep.

And there it was. Beneath the patriarch's jaw, faint as breath on glass: the ghost of another contour. A second chin line, slightly softer. An ear placed a hair's width differently. A mouth that, if you let your eyes unfocus, was not quite the mouth above it — younger around the corners, or perhaps sadder.

A pentimento. A face painted beneath a face.

She had assumed, days ago, that it was an artist's revision — the painter changing his mind about the pose, the way painters did. Everyone assumed that. That was what pentimenti were *for*, cosmetically, historically: happy accidents of revision, charming to scholars.

But artists revised compositions. They did not paint an entirely separate *person* underneath, with a separate ear, a separate mouth, a separate expression — and then bury that person so completely that only a raking light could find him.

Unless the second face had needed hiding.

She thought of the twin brothers. Two identical boys, painted together once, perhaps — a double portrait somewhere, lost or destroyed — and then one of them grown into the patriarch's seat while the other was painted out, covered over, converted into raw material for the official version of events.

Aldous. The hidden face was Aldous. Painted out of the portrait in 1958 and out of existence in 1989, resurrected only in ink, in a forged hand, in a confession designed to close a case that was itself the crime.

"You have a good eye, Miss Dunmore."

She didn't jump, though it cost her. Fenwick stood at the gallery door with a candle in one hand — an actual candle, because of course he did, because Wrenmoor's archivist understood better than anyone alive the value of atmosphere — and his expression was its usual courteous patience, a mask worn so long it had grown into the face beneath.

Which face, she wondered, was that?

"The cleaning," she said lightly. "You can see the artist changed his mind about the pose. Happens all the time."

"It does," Fenwick agreed, drifting closer, candlelight making his shadow climb the wall until it loomed over the painted patriarch like a second, darker heir. "Painters are such uncertain men. Unlike archivists. We know exactly what happened, and we write it down, and then it is true forever." He smiled at the portrait with something horribly like affection. "Records are the only immortality, Miss Dunmore. Flesh is so easily misplaced. A coat here, a body there. The sea keeps what it likes and gives back what suits it."

"That's almost poetry," Rosalind said.

"I was quoting," said Fenwick, "from the parish register. You've been busy today. Mrs. Pettigrew does love a sherry." He turned to her, and his eyes in the candlelight were pleasant and bottomless. "Do be careful on the mudflats, won't you. The tide comes in faster than people expect. Faster than investigators, certainly."

He wished her goodnight and went, his footsteps fading down the stone corridor, unhurried as a man closing a ledger.

Rosalind stood alone in the gallery with her heart going like a hammer and the hidden face swimming beneath the paint, and she understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: Fenwick knew exactly what she had found.

Second: tomorrow morning, if she wanted to catch the tide — both kinds — she would need Callum, Edmund's notebook, and the tide tables, before the only man who could rewrite history decided to rewrite her.
