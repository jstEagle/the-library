## 4. Sebastian's Inheritance

The water came for the archive the way old debts come: slowly, then all at once.

Emrys stood in the flooded lower gallery at half past two in the morning, boots submerged to the ankle, and watched Canon Aldous Wren hold a lantern over a shelf of parish registers as though he were choosing which of his children to drown first.

"You forged an entry," Wren said. His voice was calm, which was somehow worse than shouting. "In my hand, no less. *Pages 114 through 131, original diary leaves, retained pending conservation.* You knew I'd come."

"I hoped," Emrys said. "Hoping is what we do instead of knowing. I'm told it's undignified."

Idris Vane stepped out from behind the map cabinets, rain still dripping off his coat, and Emrys had never been so pleased to see a man look so tired. "Canon Wren," Vane said, "I'd ask you to put down the lantern, but frankly the lantern's the least of our problems."

The tide was in the crypt now. It had found the crack in the sea wall and widened it with the patience of something that had twenty years to wait — the same twenty years, Emrys thought, that a diary had waited on their shelf, its last entries written in a hand that wasn't Maren Quill's.

* * *

It went like this:

Wren did not run. That was the first surprise. He set the lantern on a dry corner of shelf, sat down on a crate of hymnals like a man settling into a pew, and began to talk.

"She came to me on a Tuesday," he said. "Maren. She'd found the restoration accounts — the ones Father Hale moved through the roof fund, through the bell fund, through a fund for a chapel that was never going to be built. Two hundred thousand pounds, gone into pockets I will not name tonight." He looked up. "You'll want me to name them."

"Eventually," Vane said.

"She wanted to go to the police. I asked her to wait one week. Just one week, so the cathedral wouldn't be destroyed by scandal before its four-hundredth anniversary. She laughed at me. Do you know what she said?" Wren's voice cracked on it, twenty years late. "She said, *Aldous, you love this building more than the people in it.* And she was right. God forgive me, she was right, and she turned to leave, and there was a crowbar on the workbench because we were repairing the vestry steps—"

He stopped. The water lapped against the shelving. Somewhere above them, the cathedral clock struck the quarter hour, indifferent as ever.

Emrys found they could not sit. They stood among the drowning files with their arms crossed tight, listening to the man who had taught them everything — how to read marginalia, how to smell forgery in the ink, how to love paper because paper does not flatter you — explain how he had loved a building more than a woman, and then lied about her for two decades with the same meticulous care he'd taught them to bring to every record.

"The entries," Emrys said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "In her diary. After she vanished. You wrote them so it would look like flight."

"I wrote them so her mother would have somewhere to direct her grief. Anger travels better than mystery." Wren's mouth twisted. "I used her own phrases. I'd read the whole diary by then. It felt almost like a collaboration." He paused. "That's a terrible thing to say. I'm aware it's a terrible thing to say."

"It is," Emrys agreed. "For what it's worth, your penmanship slipped in the third week. The descenders on your g's shortened when you were tired. Hers didn't."

Something moved across Wren's face — not anger. Recognition. Pride, even, horrible as that was. "You always were my best student."

"No," Emrys said quietly. "I was your most obedient one. There's a difference, and I've only just learned it."

* * *

They brought him up through the rising water, Vane's hand firm on his elbow, and out into a night rinsed clean by storm. The constables took him at the west door, beneath the carved saints who had watched everything and said nothing, as saints do.

The remains came up three days later.

The tide, having finished its long argument with the sea wall, gave back what it had kept: not much, the coroner said gently, but enough. Enough for certainty. Enough for Maren Quill's sister, gray-haired now, to stand at the graveside and finally stop setting a place at the table.

Emrys attended the funeral in their good coat and did not cry until the very end, when the choir — badly, warmly, off-key in the way that only a small-town choir can be — sang the hymn Maren had once complained about in her diary's margins. *"Four verses. Nobody has ever needed four verses of anything."*

Emrys laughed into their handkerchief, and the laughing and crying got mixed together, and they decided that was probably honest, and honesty was the theme of the season.

* * *

The trial lasted nine days.

Emrys testified for six hours of them. They brought the diary, the ledgers, the lock receipt dated eight days after Maren's disappearance, and the forged archive entry that had sprung the trap — entered into evidence, to their private delight, as Exhibit F.

"Exhibit F," murmured Idris beside them. "Fitting."

"Hush. This is a court of law."

"It's a courtroom in Cornwall, Emrys. The barrister's brought a flask."

The defense argued diminished capacity, then duress, then — briefly and desperately — that the diary entries proved Maren had left town alive. Emrys dismantled that one with fifteen minutes on ink oxidation and a slide projector, and watched the jury's faces change from doubt to something colder and more certain. When Wren was led away, he passed Emrys in the aisle and stopped.

"The archives are in good hands," he said.

"They're in mine," Emrys said. "Which is not the same thing, but it's going to have to do."

* * *

Afterward, there was the work.

Emrys spent the autumn reopening every sealed file in the collection — all forty-one of them, each one a small locked door, each one now standing open under new lamps in the reading room. Some held nothing worse than embarrassment. One held a second set of restoration accounts, which Emrys delivered to Idris personally, wrapped in brown paper like a gift.

"You could have posted it," Idris said.

"Some things you deliver by hand."

He opened it, read the first page, and whistled low. "This goes further up than Wren."

"I know."

"You'll testify again?"

"I'll testify until they build a statue of me doing it, and then I'll testify at the unveiling."

Idris grinned — the first real grin Emrys had seen from him since the case began — and poured two cups of tea from the pot he'd inexplicably brought to a crime scene, because apparently that was simply who Idris Vane was.

"To Sebastian," Emrys said, raising the cup.

"To the cat?"

"To the archive cat, who slept on the false entry for three days and thereby lent it credibility. He's earned a toast. He's earned a pension."

They drank. Outside, the sea wall repairs had begun, and the sound of the masons' hammers carried up through the floor like a slow heartbeat — the town, rebuilding itself around its own truth.

On Emrys's desk, under glass now, lay the diary, open to the last true page. Beneath it, a fresh card in Emrys's own careful hand:

*Maren Quill, 1979–2004 (in the records). Found 2024 (in fact). Kept, always.*

There were forty-one doors open now, and light coming through every one of them, and somewhere in the farthest shelf a file waited with Emrys's own name on the spine — filed, years ago, by a teacher who trusted his best student never to look.

Emrys reached for it.
