## 8. Grandmother Knows Best

The taxi turned off the coast road at half past two, and the moment the sea disappeared behind the hedgerows, Rosalind felt the change in her own body—the slight unclenching, the way her shoulders remembered they were at Ravensworth Hall, where nothing had ever been allowed to go wrong in public.

The estate announced itself the way it always had: a pair of granite pillars, a stretch of rhododendron gone feral and dark, and then the house itself, grey and symmetrical, its windows catching the afternoon light like a face turning toward you. It was smaller than it had been in childhood, or she had grown larger than its architecture. Probably both. The lawns were still mowed into obedience, the gravel still raked into lines so precise they looked printed. Everything at Ravensworth Hall was tended the way a wound is tended—regularly, carefully, and never once discussed.

Vivienne Ravensworth was waiting on the front steps.

She was eighty-one and stood like a woman of sixty, spine straight as a mast, dressed in dove-grey wool with a single string of pearls that Rosalind had never once seen her without. Her hair was white now, cut short and severe, and her eyes—pale, quick, the exact grey of the sea before weather—found her granddaughter's face and performed delight.

"There she is," Vivienne said, opening her arms. "My wandering girl. Come here."

Rosalind let herself be embraced. Her grandmother smelled of bergamot and cold air and, beneath it, something older—beeswax, maybe, or stone. The hug lasted exactly as long as it was meant to and no longer.

"You look thin," Vivienne said, holding her at arm's length. "Are they feeding you in that cottage, or are you living on coffee and other people's misery?"

"Mostly misery. It's low-calorie."

Vivienne laughed, the real laugh, the one that startled people who only knew her public face. "Come in. The tea's been ready since two. I told Mrs. Trelawny you'd be punctual, and she didn't believe me."

* * *

The drawing room had not changed in thirty years, and Rosalind suspected it had not changed in eighty. The same faded roses on the wallpaper. The same landscape above the mantel, a moor at dusk by some painter nobody had ever identified. The same low afternoon light through the long windows, falling in amber bars across a carpet worn pale in the shape of a conversation—two chairs angled toward the fire, a third pushed back against the wall, exiled.

Vivienne poured with her own hands, which was itself a message. In this house, tea poured by staff was hospitality; tea poured by Vivienne was an occasion, and occasions had purposes.

"You've lost weight and gained shadows," her grandmother said, handing her the cup. "Tell me about this podcast of yours. Your mother says you've gone peculiar. She says you've gone quiet, which for you is worse."

"I've found a story."

"You always find stories. It's your curse. Your grandfather used to say you'd find a story in an empty room." Vivienne smiled at the memory, and Rosalind watched her do it—the smooth deployment of the past, the way she could summon Charles Ravensworth's ghost like a servant. "What is it this time? A murder? A missing heiress? Tell me it's not another drowning. This coast has given the world enough drowned girls."

The words landed with a precision that Rosalind would think about later, replay it, turn it over in the dark. But in the moment it was only Vivienne being Vivienne—fluent, barbed, moving three steps ahead of every conversation the way she moved through the garden, knowing which paths flooded.

"It's an old one," Rosalind said, testing the weight of it. "1974. A girl called Elowen Marsh."

The teapot was in Vivienne's hand, returning to its trivet, and it made no sound at all when she set it down. That was the first thing. Not a clink, not a tremor of china—a silence so complete it was louder than a crash. Rosalind had spent two years listening to recordings, learning the difference between what people said and what their bodies said while they said it. She knew what a pause cost. She knew the price of a steady hand.

"Marsh," Vivienne repeated, tasting the name like an unfamiliar wine. "The lighthouse family. That was a sad business. She went off the cliff path in a storm, didn't she? Before your time, darling, before mine really."

"You were thirty-one."

"Was I?" A small laugh, a flutter of dismissal. "One's thirties are all a blur of children and accounts. Why on earth would you want that story? It's fifty years old. It's been told. The sea told it, if I remember."

"The sea's version has some gaps."

"Then write to the sea and complain." Vivienne's smile was flawless, and Rosalind felt, for the first time, the strange vertigo of recognizing a technique because she used it herself. The deflection dressed as wit. The refusal dressed as charm. She was looking at the original manuscript of every trick she owned.

"I've got her diary," Rosalind said.

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock did its patient work. Vivienne lifted her cup and drank, and her eyes over the rim were pleasant and absolutely still, the stillness of a heron at the water's edge.

"How extraordinary," she said. "And where does a podcaster find a fifty-year-old diary?"

"It came to me. Through my aunt's solicitor."

"Margaret's solicitor. How odd. Margaret never mentioned—" Vivienne stopped, and Rosalind watched the sentence die and be replaced. "Margaret kept everything. She was the magpie of the family. I expect it's a fake, you know. These things surface. Grief makes people creative."

"It's not a fake."

"You've had it authenticated?"

"I've had it authenticated."

Vivienne set down her cup and turned her full attention to the window, to the light, to the moor-painting above the mantel, anywhere but her granddaughter's face. "Well," she said lightly. "Then you've got a curiosity. A sad girl's sad book. Rosalind, listen to me." She turned back, and now her voice had changed registers, dropped into the warm, reasonable key she used for persuading people of things they already half-believed. "That story is a stone. You've found a stone at the bottom of a pond, and it looks interesting, and you want to bring it up. But when you lift it, everything that's grown around it comes up too. The pond goes to mud. Is that what you want? For a story?"

"Grandmother," Rosalind said, "that's the most honest thing you've ever said to me about anything."

Vivienne's laugh came again, but thinner this time. "I'm honest about ponds."

* * *

They walked in the garden afterward, because Vivienne always walked in the garden after tea, and because the house had begun to feel to Rosalind like a held breath. The borders were past their best, the roses gone to hips, the dahlias blackened by the first frosts, but the paths were immaculate and the yew hedges stood like a jury. A pheasant exploded out of the vegetable garden and made them both startle, and for a moment they were just two women laughing at a bird, and Rosalind hated how much she loved her grandmother, how the love sat in her chest like a stone of its own.

"You know," Vivienne said, taking her arm as they walked, proprietary and fond, "your grandfather loved this garden. He was hopeless at it, of course. He'd deadhead the wrong roses entirely. But he loved it." A pause, measured to the inch. "You'd have liked him. Everyone says you're like him. The digging. The not letting go."

"Was I like Sebastian too?"

She said it watching the path, watching the gravel, watching nothing—and felt her grandmother's arm stiffen under her hand. Not a flinch. A stiffening, a single degree of temperature dropping in the muscle, there and gone, so fast that anyone who didn't know what they were looking for would have missed it entirely.

"Sebastian?" Vivienne said, and her voice was light as thistledown. "Goodness. That's a name out of the ark. Charles's poor brother. Another one the sea took. This family and its drownings—we ought to have a crest with a life ring on it."

"He was drowned in '75. A year after Elowen Marsh."

"So he was. What a grim pair of anniversaries. You should put them in your programme, perhaps. Tragedy loves company." The arm in hers had gone from stiff to deliberately relaxed, overcorrected, a performance of ease that was itself a confession to anyone fluent in the language.

They came around the yew walk to the walled garden's far corner, and Rosalind stopped, because she had meant to stop here, had planned the whole walk around this corner, the way she planned every interview now—approach, angle, the question placed where it couldn't be dodged.

The ivy-covered wall ended at a buttress of old granite, and set into it, half-swallowed by a century of growth, was a door. Green once. Now grey-green, the paint flaking like fish scales, the iron hinges rusted to sculpture. It was a cellar door, a nothing door, the kind of door a great house grows a dozen of and forgets. Rosalind had run past it a thousand times as a child. It had been locked her whole life. It was locked now; she'd tried the handle on her way up the drive, casually, the way you try a door.

"The green door," she said. "It's been shut as long as I can remember. Why does nobody ever open it?"

And there—there it was.

Vivienne Ravensworth did not go pale. Vivienne Ravensworth did not gasp or drop anything or give any of the theatrics that guilt performs in films. What happened was smaller and far more damning: her hands, folded at her waist in their cashmere gloves, began to shake. A fine tremor, quickly mastered, pressed flat against each other as though she were crushing something between them. Three seconds, maybe four. Then stillness again, and the smile reassembled itself piece by piece like a face being put back on.

"What a strange question," Vivienne said. "It's a cellar door, darling. Cellar doors are shut. That's the whole of their career." She turned away, toward the roses gone to hip, and her voice came out with the brightness of a struck match. "Mrs. Trelawny will have the fire lit. Come inside before you freeze. You always did forget your coat."

She walked on without waiting for an answer, straight-backed, unhurried, a woman in absolute command of her garden and her body and her story.

Rosalind stood a moment longer at the green door. Up close she could see that the flaking paint had been disturbed—recently, she thought, though she couldn't have said how she knew. A scuff at the threshold. A hinge that looked oiled beneath its rust.

She thought about the diary's coded entries. *The green door. S. The tide keeps what it is given.*

She thought about a girl who had walked toward a cliff and never arrived at the edge, and a young man drowned a year later in water too shallow to drown in, and a pond, and stones, and everything that grows around them.

Then she followed her grandmother back up the path, into the warm, into the light, into the smell of bergamot and beeswax, and let Vivienne pour her a second cup of tea and tell her, in loving detail, about the rose that had won a prize in 1968.

She listened beautifully. She laughed in the right places. And all the while, in the notebook of her mind, she wrote down the only testimony that had mattered all afternoon: *Subject's hands shook at the words "green door." Duration: four seconds. She knew. She has always known.*

The drive back down toward the coast was golden with late light, the hedges burning, the sea appearing between gaps in the stone walls like a rumor. Rosalind pulled over at the top of the cliff road and sat with the engine ticking, looking down at Saltburn Point's chimneys and its single crooked street.

Her family's estate bordered the Marsh land. Her grandfather had been twenty-six in 1974. His brother had drowned a year after the girl did. And her grandmother—the woman who had taught her to ride, who sent birthday cards in her own handwriting until the arthritis, who had held her face in her cool hands when Rosalind's father left and said *we don't crumble in this family, we compost*—that woman's hands had shaken at a cellar door.

Rosalind took out her phone and opened the parish records she'd photographed, the betrothal announcements, the faded ink of 1974, and read the name she had been avoiding reading all week, the name that had been waiting for her at the bottom of the page like a stone at the bottom of a pond.

*Elowen Mary Marsh, of the Lighthouse, to Mr. Charles Ravensworth, of Ravensworth Hall.*

Her grandfather.

The pond went to mud.
