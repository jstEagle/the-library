## 14. Someone Else Is Listening

The grey Rover was there when she came out of the archive, and it was not there when she looked for it again.

That was the trick of it—Rosalind understood this later, replaying the morning the way she replayed every piece of tape before an episode went to air. The mind wants patterns; the eye obliges. She had stepped out of the post office annex at half past eleven with a folder of Edith Voss's pension records pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and the street had been empty in that particular Cornish way: gulls wheeling over the harbour wall, a trawler coughing diesel somewhere out past the point, the whole village holding its breath around her as it had since the day she arrived. And parked at the far end of Fore Street, nose angled toward the sea, sat a grey Range Rover. Clean. Recent plates. Not a fisherman's car, not a farmer's, not one of the salt-bitten relics that made up ninety percent of Saltburn Point's vehicular population. It had the look of a vehicle that had been driven down from London and washed recently, and often.

She noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song—without knowing why, only that something had gone flat.

Then Mrs. Tregowan called her name from the bakery doorway, pressing a paper bag of saffron buns into her hands with the fierce, complicated kindness the village had begun to extend her—the kindness of people who suspected what she was doing and hadn't decided whether to forgive it—and when Rosalind turned back to the street, the Rover was gone.

*You're imagining things,* she told herself, walking up the hill with the buns warm through the paper. *You've spent three weeks reading other people's secrets into everything. Of course you're seeing watchers now.*

But she had been a true-crime podcaster for six years, and there was a muscle in her that never fully relaxed. It was the same muscle that had made her pause outside the archive door and check, without quite deciding to, whether anyone had followed her in. The same muscle that had her photographing the pension ledger pages twice, once for content and once because some instinct whispered *keep a copy where no one can find both*. She'd learned that instinct in her third season, covering a wrongful conviction in Yorkshire, when a man she'd interviewed had shown up at her flat at two in the morning wanting to know exactly which of his words she intended to use. Stories about real people did things to real people. She knew that better than most.

The trouble was that until now, she had always been the one doing the watching.

* * *

It was Thomas Penhale who first said it aloud, though he didn't say much.

She'd gone to see him on Thursday, partly for the pleasure of his company—he had become, without either of them announcing it, her ally—and partly because she wanted to ask him about the winter of 1975, about Sebastian Ravensworth's drowning, about whether a sixteen-year-old boy who watched one woman walk toward a cliff might also have watched a boat leave the harbour a year later. But when she reached his cottage above the slipway, he was standing in his garden looking down at the road, and he didn't turn around when she came through the gate.

"You've got company," he said.

"Sorry?"

"Grey Rover." He nodded toward the lane below, where the car sat at the angle of the coast road, engine off, windows reflecting nothing but sky. "Been there since before you came up the hill. Been here yesterday evening too, if it's the same one. I don't sleep much these days. I notice cars."

Rosalind felt the temperature of the afternoon change. "Do you know whose it is?"

"I know whose it isn't." Thomas turned then, and his face had the weathered stillness of a man who had spent forty years reading horizons. "Nobody in this village drives like that. Nobody in this village washes their car." He came down the garden path and stood beside her, and they looked at the Rover together the way you might look at a seal carcass washed up on the sand—something that belonged to another world, arrived without explanation. "Who have you been writing to?"

"No one. I've told no one what I've found."

"Not meaning any offence, girl, but you've been asking questions in a village that remembers questions. You were in the archive Tuesday. Wednesday you were at the parish office. Yesterday you walked the cliff path with a recorder in your hand." He said it without accusation, simply laying out the facts the way he might lay out charts. "A village keeps its own records too. Anyone who wanted to know what you know could know it by now."

"Thomas—" She hesitated. "When Elowen disappeared. When Sebastian drowned. Who kept the secret? Not just your family. Who *organized* it? Someone had to manage fifty years of silence."

He was quiet long enough that a gull cried twice overhead. "That," he said finally, "is a question you should be careful asking. Because whoever's sitting in that car has already asked it. And they're further along than you think."

* * *

She tested him that night, the way you test a hypothesis you're afraid of confirming.

Her rented cottage stood at the end of a lane above the harbour, its back window facing the dark shoulder of the headland. After supper she turned off every light except the kitchen lamp, set it low, and sat in the dark with a glass of wine she didn't drink, watching the road. The cottage had no curtains worth the name—she'd hung a sheet over the bedroom window her first week and never bothered with the rest—and so she sat in shadow and gave the night her full attention, the way she'd once given it to surveillance footage frame by frame.

At twenty past ten, headlights swept the bend below. Slow. Slower than the lane required. The grey Rover crawled past the mouth of her lane, paused—there was no other word for it, it *paused*, a held breath of brake lights—and continued on toward the point. She counted to four hundred. It came back. Crawled past again. Gone.

She sat very still in the dark, and what she felt was not fear, not yet. It was recognition. Somewhere in the last three weeks, without her marking the moment, the story had turned around and begun to read *her*. She had spent her career standing outside other people's tragedies with a microphone, believing herself transparent, weightless, a lens rather than a participant. Now someone was studying her comings and goings, timing her movements, learning the shape of her days—and the knowledge settled into her body with a cold, intimate horror, because she knew exactly how it worked. She had done it to enough people. She knew the patience of it, the methodical accumulation of pattern. Whoever was in that car wasn't following her the way a thug follows. They were following her the way *she* followed.

Which meant they weren't guessing. They were building a file.

* * *

Friday she went back to the archive, deliberately, at the same hour, taking the same route—a controlled experiment, and she hated how naturally the tradecraft came to her, hated that her pulse lifted with something adjacent to excitement even as her stomach knotted. Let them watch. Let them learn that Rosalind Ravensworth noticed being noticed.

The archive was quiet. Mrs. Pascoe, the volunteer archivist, was dozing over her knitting by the radiator, and Rosalind moved through the stacks on sock-soft feet, pulling the box she'd requested under a false pretense two days earlier—Edith Voss's correspondence, officially, though what she actually wanted was the delivery ledgers, the record of who had come and gone from the post office across five decades. If Edith Voss had been Elowen Marsh, then the post office had been more than a job. It had been a listening post. A woman who sorted every letter in the village for forty years knew every secret in it, and someone had kept those secrets *with* her. S had stayed nearby all along. But S was dead—drowned in 1975, or so the stone in the Ravensworth chapel yard claimed. So who else?

She found what she was looking for at noon: a single line in the 1981 delivery ledger, in Edith Voss's small upright hand, noting a registered letter received for "E.V., hold for collection," sender listed only as *V.R.* Vivienne Ravensworth. 1981—the year Sebastian died in the tunnel chamber, the year Elowen buried him alone and walked back into the village wearing a borrowed name. Her grandmother had written to her. Had known. Had corresponded with a dead woman for forty years while sitting across Christmas tables from Rosalind, passing the gravy, complimenting her school reports.

Rosalind photographed the page with shaking hands and was re-shelving the box when she heard the front door open.

She didn't look up. She finished sliding the box home, straightened its label, and only then turned, casually, toward the reading room—and through the archway she saw him. A man she didn't know, mid-forties, expensive coat folded over his arm, standing at the front desk speaking to Mrs. Pascoe in a low, courteous voice. He had the kind of face designed to be forgotten: pleasant, symmetrical, unremarkable. But his shoes were London shoes, and when Mrs. Pascoe gestured vaguely toward the stacks, his eyes tracked across the room with a quickness that didn't match his voice—cataloguing exits, cataloguing her.

Rosalind turned back to the shelf and became intensely interested in a box of harbour trust minutes from the 1960s. Her heart was going like a bird in a box. *Be a researcher,* she told herself. *Be boring. Be furniture.*

After a moment, footsteps crossed the room behind her—unhurried, deliberate—and stopped at the next aisle. She heard him pull a box, riffle papers he clearly had no interest in. Then, pleasantly, from beyond the shelving:

"Lovely part of the world. First time visiting?"

"Every summer since I was a child," Rosalind said, to the harbour trust minutes. Her voice came out steady. Six years of interviews had taught her that.

"Family connection?"

"Distant." She let a silence sit, then added, lightly, "And yours?"

"Business interests." A pause, and then, with a mildness that was somehow worse than menace: "They say the coast here hasn't changed in fifty years. Funny, that. Most places can't hold still that long. Somebody usually has to work at it."

Footsteps retreating. The front door. Through the dusty window she watched him cross the street and fold himself into the grey Rover, and she wrote down the plate number on the inside of her wrist with a biro, pressing hard enough to sting.

* * *

She didn't go straight home. She took the long way, the cliff path, because she refused to be herded and because she needed the wind. Below her the sea worked at the rocks with its old patient violence, and she walked fast with her collar up, running the arithmetic.

Someone had connected Edith Voss to Elowen Marsh. That was the fact underneath all the others, and it changed the shape of everything. Until now she had believed herself the sole inheritor of the truth—the diary hers, the tunnel hers, the revelation hers to time and shape and release. But the man in the Rover hadn't stumbled on her by accident. He'd known which boxes to pretend to browse. He'd known to ask about family connections. Which meant somewhere out there, someone else had followed the same thread she had—perhaps from the other end, perhaps for years—and they had arrived at Saltburn Point with money and a car and a mandate, and their interest was not archival.

*They want the story silenced before broadcast.*

The thought arrived whole, and once it arrived she couldn't unthink it. Her season was scheduled. Her producer had the outline. Three weeks of tapes sat on a encrypted drive in the cottage, and the diary itself lay wrapped in a scarf in the bottom of her rucksack like a sleeping animal. If someone wanted the story dead, they didn't need to kill her. They needed only the diary, the tapes, the drive. Or failing that, they needed her frightened enough to hand them over.

She stopped at the top of the cliff path where it bent inland toward the village, and she looked back the way she'd come, at the grey line of the coast road. Empty. Of course it was empty. That was the whole grammar of it now—absence as presence, the watcher implied by the space where a watcher would be. She thought of her producer's voice on the phone last month, rough with worry: *the podcast survives on strangers' tragedies, not your own bloodline.* She had argued. She had been arrogant. She had believed that knowing the truth entitled her to it, that the story belonged to her because she'd earned it with her grandmother's blood.

Now the story had teeth, and they were pointed at her, and she understood—with the clarity that comes only when the hunter feels breath on the back of her neck—that she had never been outside this story at all. She was born into it. The arranged marriage, the faked drownings, the forty years of letters: it was all load-bearing architecture in the house she'd grown up in, and she had gone digging in the foundations thinking herself an archaeologist, when all along she was a tenant.

* * *

The Rover was outside the cottage when she got home.

Not passing this time. Parked. Engine ticking as it cooled, driver's door just closing—a tall figure moving up her lane with unhurried purpose, and Rosalind stood thirty yards away in the failing light with her keys in her fist and understood that the experiment was over, that the observation phase had ended, that whatever came next would happen tonight, on her doorstep, in the village that closed ranks around its dead.

She did not run. Running was surrender, and besides, she'd learned from the best—from every cornered woman in every case she'd ever covered—that the moment you flee is the moment you confirm you have something to lose. Instead she walked forward, keys between her fingers, heart slamming, and called out into the dusk with a steadiness she did not feel:

"If you've come about the diary, you're wasting your trip. I've already sent copies to three separate solicitors."

The figure stopped. Turned. And in the last grey light, as he stepped beneath the cottage lamp, Rosalind saw his face properly for the first time—and the ground tilted under her, because it was not a stranger's face at all.

It was a face she'd known since childhood. A face from christenings and funerals, from the long polished table at Ravensworth Hall. Her cousin Julian smiled at her, warm and terrible, and said:

"Hello, Roz. I think we need to talk about what you're going to do with Grandma's secret."
