## 11. Silas Did Not Drown

The county records office smelled of dust and radiator heat and the particular despair of institutions that had been asked to do more with less every year since 1980. Bram signed the visitor ledger with a name that was almost her own — B. Quintana, no title, no affiliation — because she had learned in the past two weeks that the moment anyone at the cathedral heard the word *coroner*, a telephone would ring somewhere in the close, and whatever she was reaching for would be moved before her hand arrived.

She had stopped asking permission on day thirty-one of fifty-one. This was day thirty-three.

"Coroner's files, 1984," she said to the man at the desk, who wore a cardigan over his lanyard and had the eyes of someone who had heard stranger requests before lunch.

He looked at her over his glasses. "Inquest records are closed for a hundred years."

"I'm not asking for the inquest. I'm asking for the register of deaths and the burial record. Those are public."

"They are," he admitted, with the reluctance of a man conceding a technicality he'd hoped she wouldn't know.

"Silas Merridew. Drowned, June 1984. Body recovered from the river below the mill weir at Fenstanton."

He typed. The keyboard clattered like small bones. "Merridew... Merridew." He frowned at the screen. "There's a death registered. Silas Merridew, aged fourteen, June the ninth, 1984. Cause of death: drowning. Inquest opened and adjourned, then—huh."

"Huh what?"

"The inquest was never resumed. It just says 'adjourned' and then there's a note from 1987 saying the file was transferred to—" he squinted, "—the diocesan registrar. That's odd. Coroners don't transfer files to dioceses. Dioceses don't keep coroner's files. That's not a thing."

Bram kept her voice level. "And the burial?"

He typed again, slower now, as if the keys themselves had grown suspicious. "Buried the twenty-first of June, Saint Aldhelm's own ground, consecrated section. Plot number..." He turned the screen toward her, half as an afterthought, and Bram read the entry twice because the first time her eyes refused to assemble it into meaning.

*Interred: SILAS MERIDEW.*

Not *Merridew*. *Meridew.* One letter different, transposed, the kind of error a grieving clerk makes at the end of a long week — except that everything else in the row was typed with bureaucratic perfection, dates aligned, plot numbers sequential, the officiant's name spelled out in full.

"That's a typo," the man said, following her gaze. "Happens all the time."

"Does it?" Bram said. "Change a surname by one letter, in the one record that's public, while the death certificate keeps the correct spelling?"

He blinked at her. She thanked him, asked for a photocopy of both entries, and stood at the machine watching the green light crawl across the page, thinking about how a lie is built. Not out of grand gestures. Out of clerical details. A letter moved. A file misfiled. An inquest adjourned and never resumed because everyone who might have insisted on resuming it had been persuaded, paid, or frightened into silence, and the paperwork itself had been quietly walked across town to a building where no one would ever think to look for it.

Someone inside had done this. Someone with access to the registers, the burial ground, the coroner's office. Someone who understood that the best place to hide a boy who didn't drown was under the name of a boy who had never lived.

*

She found that last part three days later, in the parish register of baptisms, 1969–1971, which she pulled from her own archive at six in the morning before the vergers arrived, working by lamplight because she no longer trusted the overhead lights — they buzzed differently when the intercom was live, she'd decided, though she couldn't have proven it, and the paranoia embarrassed her even as she obeyed it.

The baptismal register was written in a firm copperplate hand, each entry complete: child's name, parents, date, godparents, the celebrant's signature. And there, on a page dated March 1970:

*SILAS JOHN MERIDEW, son of Walter and Anne Meridew, born the second of January 1970, baptized the fifteenth of March 1970.*

Beside it, in a later ink, a marginal note in smaller script: *died in infancy, see burial register.*

Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like a bell swinging in a tower she couldn't see. She pulled the burial register for 1970 and found him: *Silas John Meridew, infant, buried April the third, 1970, unconsecrated ground, plot 411.* A baby. A baby who had lived nine weeks and been buried in the cheap ground beyond the yew hedge, and whose name — whose exact, correct, properly-spelled name — had been waiting under the earth for fourteen years until someone needed it.

They hadn't misspelled *Merridew* in the 1984 burial record. They had used the dead infant's spelling deliberately, because the infant's grave was real, the infant's registration was real, and if anyone ever came looking — as she was looking now — they would find a paper trail that folded back on itself like a corridor in a dream. The drowned boy was buried under the name of a boy already buried. Two graves, one name, neither holding what it claimed.

Except the infant's name wasn't quite the same either, was it? *Meridew*, not *Merridew*. Which meant somewhere between 1970 and 1984, the family name had changed by a single letter — or the family in the 1984 records had never existed at all, and *Merridew* was invented whole, a surname chosen to echo a real dead child closely enough to pass, loosely enough to deny.

Bram sat back in the chair and pressed her palms flat against the table, against the cold wood, because the room had begun to tilt in a way that had nothing to do with her inner ear.

She thought she had understood the shape of this thing. A murder in 1984. A confession forty years late. A witness who died — or was said to have died — and a cathedral that sealed its crypt and changed its locks and taught its staff to recite the same three sentences about a boating accident. She had thought the lie was a lid over a well. What she saw now, in the lamplight, with two registers open side by side, was that the lie was load-bearing. It wasn't hiding the crime. It was *holding up the cathedral*. The endowment, the restoration trust, the Harrowgate name carved into the donor wall in letters taller than her hand — all of it rested on the assumption that Silas Merridew was at the bottom of the river, that the witness was gone, that the only people who knew what happened in the crypt in the spring of 1984 were people who had agreed, permanently, to forget.

Take away that assumption and you weren't opening a cold case. You were pulling a stone out of an arch.

*

She took the photocopied pages home, which was against archive policy and which she did anyway, folding them into the lining of her coat like contraband, because that was what they were now. Her flat above the chandlery on Cutwater Lane was cold; she didn't put the heating on. She spread the documents across the kitchen table and weighted their corners with mugs and a salt cellar and the brass paperknife her mother had left her, and she looked at them for a long time without touching anything.

A baby's baptism. A baby's grave. A teenager's drowning. A teenager's grave with the wrong-right name. Four records, forty years apart at the extremes, and threaded through all of them the same invisible hand.

She got out her mother's photograph album — the one she'd inherited along with the paperknife and a box of unsorted papers she still hadn't finished going through, four years after the funeral. Chapter four of this affair had shown her the verger's assistant roster, her mother's name in the 1984 list, and she had spent the weeks since telling herself it meant nothing, that half the town had passed through the cathedral's employ, that a girl of nineteen carrying candles proved nothing except that girls of nineteen carry candles.

But her mother hadn't been a girl of nineteen in 1984. Her mother had been thirty-one. A verger's *assistant* at thirty-one — a demotion, or a return, or a cover. Bram had checked the earlier rosters. From 1978 to 1983 her mother had been listed as *assistant sacristan*, a position with keys. Then, in January 1984, five months before the killing, she had been moved to verger's assistant. Moved *down*. Or moved *closer to the doors* — closer to the crypt stair, closer to the comings and goings, closer to whoever needed a trustworthy pair of hands inside the building who could be relied upon absolutely.

Because her mother was reliable. That was the whole of her, the axis her character turned on. Reliable to the point of silence, loyal to the point of complicity. Bram had loved her for it and been exasperated by it and never once, in thirty-odd years, had imagined it might have a price tag attached.

She turned the pages of the album slowly. Here was her mother at the seaside, squinting, laughing. Here was her mother outside the cathedral in her verger's cassock, 1985, the year after, standing slightly apart from the other staff the way people stand when they are new to grief or guilt. Here was—

Here was a photograph she had looked at a hundred times and never seen.

A group shot, undated, on the tower steps. Ringers and choir, maybe a dozen faces, sun-flared, some of them mid-laugh. Her mother stood at the edge in civvies. And beside her, one row down, a boy of perhaps thirteen or fourteen, thin, dark-haired, his face turned up toward the camera with an expression Bram recognized with a jolt that went through her like a struck bell — the expression of someone listening to something no one else can hear. His eyes were open and aimed at nothing. Aimed, she realized, at the sound. At the bells above them, or at whatever the bells were saying.

Under the photo, in her mother's handwriting: *Ringers & choristers, feast of St Aldhelm. S. says the tenor speaks first if you listen.*

*S. says.*

Bram set the album down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Sebastian had told her, that night in the tower, that he'd come to the cathedral as a boy, that the bells were the first thing that ever made sense to him, that he'd lost his sight at fourteen — *it went out over a season, like a lamp running down*, he'd said, and she had thought then, distantly, that grief made metaphors of everyone. He had quoted the order of the 1984 funeral bells. He had known the crypt grille was loose in the northwest corner. He had watched her, she was sure of it, watched her with an attention no blind man should possess, tracking her through the tower by breath and floorboard and the particular way she held her silence.

She had filed all of it under *character*. Under *charm*. Under the warm amber light of a man who loved his bells.

Now she picked the photograph up again and looked at the boy's face, really looked, and let herself think the unthinkable thing, the thing that had been circling her since the records office like a bird around a spire.

If Silas Merridew didn't drown — if the drowning was staged, if someone inside the cathedral had erased a fourteen-year-old witness so thoroughly that even his grave was a forgery — then the boy hadn't gone far. Children don't vanish into air. They get renamed, relocated, absorbed. They need feeding, housing, protecting. They need someone inside, someone with keys and loyalty and a talent for silence.

And what better hiding place for a boy the world believed dead than a tower the world never entered? What better disguise for a witness than blindness — because no one deposes a blind man, no one suspects him, no one imagines that the man who cannot see has *seen more than anyone alive*?

Her hands were shaking. She put the photograph down.

"No," she said aloud, to the cold kitchen, and heard how unconvincing it sounded.

Because it fit. It fit with a horrible, seamless click, the way a key fits a lock that was built for it. Sebastian, who appeared in the cathedral's records — she would check tomorrow, she would check tonight — appearing from nowhere around 1985, apprenticed to the ringing chamber, given ropes instead of questions. Sebastian, who had guided her through the tower and quoted the funeral and warned her, in his gentle oblique way, that some stones are better left unturned. Sebastian, who had known exactly where she hid the second letter — the letter that vanished from her desk while she was at the Harrowgate house being offered a fellowship with a smile behind it.

Or. Or he had warned whoever came. Or he was theirs, always had been, a sleeper planted in the belfry for forty years, and every warm evening among the ropes had been reconnaissance, and the tenderness—

The tenderness was the part she couldn't make fit either way. That was the vertigo, she realized. Not the forged graves, not the load-bearing lie. The vertigo was that the same evidence pointed in two directions at once, and the direction it pointed depended entirely on whether Sebastian's kindness was real, and there was no document in any archive in England that could tell her that.

She looked at the clock. Ten past eleven. The anniversary service was eleven days away. The statute ran out in eighteen.

She should sleep. She didn't sleep. She sat at the table with the photocopied graves of two boys who shared a name and neither shared a resting place, and somewhere across the dark city, in a tower above the sleeping cathedral, the quarter-chimes rang out — and she caught herself counting them, automatically, the way he had taught her without teaching her, and hating how much she trusted the sound.

At midnight she made the decision, or admitted she had made it days ago. Tomorrow she would pull the tower records, the apprenticeship rolls, the 1985 admissions. Tomorrow she would lay the two lives side by side — the boy who drowned and the man who rang — and whichever way the evidence fell, she would take it to him and watch his face, blind or not, and she would know.

She gathered the papers into their folder. As she did, a page slipped free and landed face-up on the table: the photocopy of the 1984 burial entry, the transposed name, the officiant's signature in the margin.

She had glanced at it a dozen times. Now she actually read it.

The officiating minister's signature was small, hurried, unmistakable once seen. She knew the hand. She had seen it that very week, signing the fellowship offer that sat unopened in her drawer like a bribe in an envelope.

The Reverend A. Vasse.

Edmund Vasse — the dying man, the double letters, the confession and its counter-confession — had personally buried the boy who didn't drown. Had stood at the graveside of a living child and committed him to the ground with words of committal, in front of God and a handful of mourners who must have known, every one of them, that the coffin was light.

Which meant the letters hadn't been a dying man's conscience splitting in two. They had been a plan. Vasse hadn't confessed and recanted. He had *left instructions* — one document to force the crypt open, one to point at the witness, timed to arrive only when he could no longer be questioned, threatened, or stopped.

Bram stood in her cold kitchen holding a piece of photocopied paper, and the floor of the world tilted again, and this time she let it.

The bells rang the hour. Twelve strokes, unhurried, certain, falling away over the rooftops toward the river.

Tomorrow, she thought. But her feet were already moving for her coat.
