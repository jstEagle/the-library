## 11. The Cannery Floor

The cannery had been dead for eleven years, and like most dead things in Grayhaven, it had not been allowed to rest.

Ulyana stood at the edge of Cannery Row at dusk, in the blue hour when the fog came off the water in long gray ribbons and the streetlights buzzed on one by one, half-hearted, as if even they had doubts about illuminating this particular street. The building ran a full block — corrugated steel gone the color of old pewter, windows boarded or blown out, a water tower slumped against the sky like a drunk leaning on a lamppost. A sign above the loading dock still read PACIFIC GLORY PACKING CO., though the O had rusted through and the G hung by one bolt, so that from where she stood it said PACIFIC LORY PACKING, which sounded, absurdly, like a company that shipped nothing but regret.

She had memorized the file photographs of this building before she ever saw it. She had memorized the fire marshal's condemnation order, the tax liens, the sale record showing the property had transferred — quietly, in a private transaction, no broker, no listing — to a holding company called Meridian Bay Holdings LLC. She had memorized the fact that Meridian Bay Holdings shared a registered agent with a shell firm that appeared, in the hymnal ledger, forty-one times.

She had not memorized what the building smelled like. That was new information, and it arrived all at once: brine and rust and diesel and, underneath it, the sweet-rot smell of fish that had soaked into the concrete so deeply that no decade would ever wash it out. The whole structure breathed it. It was the smell of the harbor's honest past, and it made the dishonest present feel like a violation.

Ulyana checked her watch. 7:42. Low tide at 8:15, which mattered because the seaward side of the cannery had a loading tunnel — she'd seen it in the old survey maps tucked in the back of the condemnation file — that flooded at high tide and stood dry and walkable at low. The maps were fourteen years old. She was betting the tide hadn't changed its habits.

She was betting a lot of things tonight. She catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, a habit since childhood, a mind that kept every receipt: *One, the night watchman — if there is one — does his rounds on the landward side, because the seaward side is fenced with chain-link that has been rusted open for years and nobody bothers. Two, Brannock does not yet know her face well enough to post a man specifically for her, though after the fish market, after the gray sedan that had followed her through the stalls until she'd doubled back through the ice room and out the loading door, she could no longer assume it. Three, whatever was inside the cannery, it was worth protecting, which meant it was worth seeing.*

She crossed the lot in the shadow of the water tower, her boots silent on gravel, and crouched at the base of the seaward fence. The rusted gap was exactly where she'd calculated — a curl of chain-link peeled back like the lid of a sardine tin, which felt like an omen of some kind, and she almost smiled. Almost. She slid through, felt the wire snag her coat, freed it with a patience she did not feel, and stood on the cannery's seaward apron, where the concrete ended and the mudflats began, black and glistening under a sky the color of a bruise healing.

The loading tunnel was a dark rectangle in the foundation wall, maybe four feet high, its mouth framed in poured concrete furred with barnacles. The tide was already sighing out, and the smell coming from the tunnel was cold and mineral and old. She clicked on her flashlight, cupped her hand around the beam to kill the spill, and looked in.

The tunnel ran perhaps thirty feet into the building's guts. Dry, at the moment, except for a shallow ribbon of standing water down the center that reflected her light back at her like a second, dimmer flashlight held by someone following.

*You could still turn around,* said the reasonable voice in her head, the one that sounded like DeWitt, all efficiency and no soul. *You have the ledger memorized. You have forty-one payments to Meridian Bay. You could write the report from a motel room and let other people do the crawling.*

But the report would need the crawlers, and the crawlers were compromised, and she had learned — these last two weeks, in this town where every door opened an inch and no farther — that Grayhaven did not give up its truths to people who waited politely outside. The town closed ranks around Silas Marchetti's name the way an oyster closes around a grain of sand: not to protect the sand, but to make a pearl of it, something smooth and beautiful and useless for cutting.

Ulyana got down on her knees in the mud and crawled into the cannery.

* * *

The tunnel was colder than the night, which she hadn't thought possible. The concrete scraped her shoulders through her coat. Her flashlight beam bounced ahead of her, and the standing water threw her reflection up at the ceiling in wavering fragments — a face, a light, a face — so that she kept glancing up to make sure the face was only hers.

Halfway through, she stopped. Not because of a sound. Because of a *lack* of one. The harbor's ambient noise — water, wind, the far-off groan of a container ship — cut off at the tunnel mouth behind her, and inside there was only her own breathing and the drip of water somewhere ahead, at intervals so regular it might have been a clock. *Drip. Drip. Drip.* She counted eleven drips before she made herself move again. She did not like what the counting told her, which was that her heart rate had climbed and her mouth had gone dry and her body, whatever her mind claimed, understood perfectly well that she was entering the throat of something.

The tunnel opened into the cannery's old receiving floor, and Ulyana rose out of it slowly, flashlight first, like a diver surfacing.

The main floor was a cathedral of absence. Steel trusses marched overhead into the dark, hung with the skeletons of conveyor lines. Canning machinery sat under tarps like furniture in a house where the family had died. Pigeons startled somewhere high up — a soft thunder of wings — and then the silence reassembled itself around her.

But the receiving floor was not empty. That was the first thing. That was the thing her flashlight found and her memory took and her stomach slowly understood.

Along the east wall, where the condemnation photos showed nothing but collapsed racking and pigeon droppings, stood a staging area.

It was neat. That was what made it terrible. Everything else in the building was decay — rust and rot and ruin, entropy doing its patient work — but this corner had been *kept*. Wooden pallets, stacked and squared. A row of marine crates, plywood and steel-banded, unmarked, each one the size of a dishwasher. A workbench with a tarp folded on it in a clean rectangle. A shelf holding coils of new rope, shrink-wrapped. A clipboard hanging from a nail. And overhead, a tarp rigged on a line, ready to be pulled across the whole arrangement like a curtain — camouflage for anyone who looked in through the broken clerestory windows from the street.

Cargo that never appeared on manifests. She had written that phrase in her notes two days ago, speculating, and now the speculation had a floor plan and a workbench and shrink-wrapped rope. The smuggling line hadn't died with the cannery. The cannery had died *around* it, like a tree growing around a nail. Brannock's operation used the condemned building precisely because no one was supposed to be here — no inspectors, no lights, no records. A dead building was the best warehouse in the world.

Ulyana moved along the wall, keeping her light low and narrow, and let her memory do what it did. The clipboard: a manifest, handwritten, and she read it in four seconds and held it — six line items, dates, initials, a notation in the margin that read *M.B.H. — hold for R.* The workbench: a utility knife, a box of nitrile gloves, a coffee can full of screws. The crates: she counted seven, and noted that three of them had fresh scrape marks on their bottom edges, the kind made by dragging across concrete, and that the drag marks on the floor led from the tunnel mouth to the crates and back. *In by water. Out by water.* The tide was the truck. The tide left no tire tracks.

She was crouched at the nearest crate, memorizing the staple pattern of its steel banding, when she saw the boot prints.

They were in the dust of the main floor, in the wide lane between the staging area and the old canning lines — a corridor of gray dust maybe six feet wide, undisturbed except for them. Two sets. One larger, one smaller, both fresh — the edges of the prints still crisp, no grit blown into the treads, no spider silk spun across them. The dust in a sealed building doesn't get wind. Fresh prints in sealed dust were hours old at most.

*Someone uses it still.* She had known that, intellectually, since the moment she'd seen the shrink-wrapped rope. But knowing a thing and standing in its footprint were different orders of knowledge. The larger prints were a work boot, size eleven or twelve, deep heel. The smaller set was — she crouched, her flashlight cupped, her breath held — the smaller set was a woman's boot, or a light hiker, size eight, and the stride was long, purposeful, someone who knew this floor in the dark.

Silas? The thought arrived with a physical jolt, a cold hand on the back of her neck. Was this where the dead woman walked? Or was it something else — a courier, a checker, someone whose job it was to count crates in the dark and leave no record except in a hymnal?

She photographed nothing. There was no camera; cameras were liabilities, and besides, she didn't need one. She had never needed one. She stood in the dust lane and looked at the prints the way other people looked at photographs, and her mind fixed them — angle, depth, tread pattern, the slight drag of the right heel on the larger boot, a hitch in the gait of whoever wore it, some old injury or heavy load — and filed them away in the endless, merciless archive behind her eyes.

That was when she heard the boat.

It was very far off at first, a low diesel mutter somewhere out on the black water, and she told herself it could be any boat, any fisherman, the harbor was full of them. But the sound grew, and it didn't pass. It came *in*. She knew the sound of an engine slowing to idle, and this was that sound, and it was coming from the direction of the tunnel — her tunnel — and the tide was low and the water in the loading channel was deep enough, she realized, for a skiff.

Ulyana killed her flashlight.

The dark came down on her like a lid. For a moment she was blind in the absolute sense, and her memory became her only eyes: she rebuilt the floor from what she'd seen — staging wall on her left, dust lane ahead, the tarped canning machinery to the right, a doorway in the far wall, she'd noted it, she'd *noted it*, a fire door hanging open on its hinges, leading deeper into the building, toward the old cook room. Twenty feet. Maybe twenty-five. She could reach it in the dark if she moved now, quiet, along the machinery, out of the sightline of the tunnel mouth.

The engine died. Then the small sounds: a hull kissing concrete, a line thrown, a voice — low, one word, indistinct — and a second voice answering. Two men. Boots on the tunnel's wet concrete, the slap-slap of rubber soles.

Ulyana moved.

She went the way water goes, finding the path of least resistance, one hand on the cold tarp of the canning line, each foot placed with a deliberateness that felt absurdly slow and was, she prayed, silent. Behind her, light bloomed — a flashlight beam, wide and white, sweeping the receiving floor. It passed over the place where she had been standing four seconds earlier and kept going. The beam caught the dust lane and the boot prints in it, and one of the men made a sound, a short exhale through the nose, not quite a word.

The beam swung toward the fire door.

Ulyana was through it. She pulled the door to behind her, easing it until the hinge stopped, leaving it open the width of a hand — closing it would click, and a click in a dead building is a gunshot. The cook room beyond was pitch black and smelled of old steam and rusted steel, and she stood in it with her hand pressed flat against a wall that was cold as a grave marker, and listened to the men enter the receiving floor.

They weren't quiet. That was the interesting thing. They weren't quiet because they didn't think they needed to be. One of them whistled, two notes, tuneless. The other said, "Leave the lights off, we'll be out in ten." A zipper. The scrape of a crate. They were here to work — to load or unload or check — and the building was theirs the way a kitchen is yours at midnight, no ceremony required.

Ulyana stood in the dark and did what she was built to do: she memorized them. The whistle's pitch — a man who whistled flat. The voice that gave orders — mid-forties, harbor vowels, a faint wet rattle at the bottom of the breath that she'd heard in a hundred dockworkers and a few smokers. The tread of the boots, heavier than the size-twelve prints in the dust, steel-shanked. The interval between their footsteps, which told her the second man was favoring his left side.

And then, through the hand-width gap in the fire door, the flashlight beam swung past, and in its spill she saw them — a half-second, a slice of light across a slice of room — and she took them in whole. The tall one in a canvas jacket, watch cap, a face like a closed fist. The shorter one holding the light, and on the shorter one's jacket, over the heart, a small stitched patch she recognized from the harbor district the way you recognize a scar: a blue anchor inside a white circle. The mark of Brannock's stevedore crew, the men who loaded the boats nobody counted.

The beam swung away. The men went on working, unhurried, and Ulyana stood in the cook room of a dead cannery with her heart slamming and her mind cold and clear, and she understood the shape of the night with terrible simplicity: she had come looking for the machinery of a crime, and she had found it running. It ran on tides and silence and men who whistled flat, and it had been running for years, through her firm's client, through her firm's ledgers, through the hymn numbers of a soup-kitchen piano bench, and it had killed at least one man whose grave she intended to visit, and it had drowned — or failed to drown — a woman whose diary was still being written in her brother's hand.

The men finished. She heard the crates drag — three of them, matching the scrape marks — heard the hull take weight, heard the engine catch and fade out through the tunnel toward the open water. The tide would carry them out the way it had carried them in, unmanifested, uncounted, unrecorded anywhere except in the archive behind her eyes.

She waited. She was good at waiting; it was the loneliest of her skills. She waited until the diesel mutter was gone, until the drip resumed its clockwork, until the silence had reassembled itself so completely that she could believe the men had never existed. Then she went back out onto the receiving floor, stood over the boot prints one last time, and let her memory take the whole room in a single long swallow — the staging area, the clipboard, the tarp on its line, the dust, the prints, the fire door, the tunnel — every detail filed where no fire could reach it, no rival could steal it, no one could ever take it from her.

Then she crawled back out through the throat of the building, into the cold and the fog and the honest smell of the mudflats, and stood up under the ruined water tower with her knees aching and her hands shaking in a way she refused to acknowledge.

She was halfway across the gravel lot when the headlights came on.

They were maybe forty yards away, low beams, engine off — a car that had been sitting dark in the lot since before she entered the cannery, a car she had walked right past in the fog without seeing. The driver's door opened. A shape got out, unhurried, and stood beside the car with its hands in its pockets, and did not approach, and did not speak, and did not need to.

Because Ulyana knew the shape. She had seen it in the fish market, behind the gray sedan's windshield, through the ice-room glass. Broad shoulders, still posture, the calm of a man who has never once needed to hurry.

Brannock stood in the fog at the edge of his dead cannery, watching her the way a man watches a tide he has already scheduled, and Ulyana Vane, who remembered everything, understood that she had just been remembered too.
