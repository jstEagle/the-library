## 13. An Alibi Made of Love

The night Silas Marchetti came back from the dead, Tomas Vane was elbow-deep in a bilge pump that had swallowed something whole, and the harbor was doing its November trick — fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like the town holding its breath.

He heard her before he saw her. That was the strange part, the part he would return to for months afterward, turning it over like a coin worn smooth. The slip behind the bait shop was the dead end of the marina, the place where derelicts went to rot and where Tomas went to work on engines no one else would touch, and nobody came back there by accident. The sound she made was small. A scrape of shoe on planking. Then a cough that sounded like the sea giving her back one lungful at a time.

He came up out of the engine hatch with a wrench in his hand and the fog peeled just enough to show him a woman on her knees at the edge of the slip, soaked through, gray-lipped, her hair plastered to her skull in ropes. She was trying to crawl and not managing it. Her coat had gone heavy with water and was dragging her backward down into the black water with the patient insistence of something that had been promised a body.

Tomas did not think. That was the mercy of it, and the curse. Thinking would have told him every reason to walk away. Instead he dropped the wrench, crossed the planks in four strides, and hauled her up by the collar the way you'd haul a net, and she weighed almost nothing, and she looked at him with eyes that had already made their peace and were furious about it.

"Don't," she rasped. "Don't tell anyone."

"I wasn't going to tell anyone," Tomas said, and it was true, and it was the truest thing he'd said aloud in nine years.

* * *

He got her into the room above the bait shop because it was the only door he had a key to that wasn't his own name on it. He'd been renting it under the name Delacroix for three years — a name off a stencil on a crate, a name that belonged to no one — and the room held a cot, a hot plate, a window that looked out on the water and nothing else. He built a fire in the little stove with split kindling and told himself it was for the pipes, for the damp, for any reason at all.

She shook for two hours. He sat across from her with his back against the wall and watched the fog press against the window and waited, because he had learned in the boatyards that the fastest way to get a story was to stop asking for it.

She gave it to him in pieces, between the shivering, in a voice like gravel washed smooth. Her name — not the name in the paper, the other one, the one she'd been born with and buried. Her husband's name, said once, flat, the way you say a word you've had to stop crying over. The cannery. The crates that never went on manifests. The night Emilio told her what he'd found in the pay ledger, and the way he'd laughed, and the way he'd stopped laughing. The dock. The rope. The water going over her head before she understood that the argument on the pier had been the last argument Emilio would ever have, that the man who'd been helping her look for her husband had been the man who'd made sure the looking was over.

"They think I'm dead," she said. "The whole town thinks I'm dead. The paper says so." She laughed then, a terrible small sound. "I read my own obituary off a newsstand. It got my age wrong."

Tomas said nothing. He was looking at her hands — dockworker's hands, rope-scarred, the nails cut short — and thinking about hands, and about what hands were for, and about a night nine years ago when his own hands had held a truth too heavy to set down anywhere.

"Say it back to me," she said suddenly, gripping his wrist with a strength that surprised them both. "Say what I told you. I need to know it's in someone else's head besides mine. In case."

So he said it back. All of it. Every name, every date, every crate. He said it back the way he'd learned to say things as a boy, when his sister could recite a page after reading it once and he'd learned to do the same just to keep up with her, to keep a place at the table where she sat. Silas listened with her eyes closed, and when he finished she opened them and looked at him for a long time.

"You didn't miss a word," she said.

"I don't miss words," Tomas said. "It's a family disease."

* * *

The town grieved her the way small towns grieve — loudly for a week, then in lowered voices at the checkout counter, then not at all. The paper ran her picture twice. *WIDOW OF DOCKWORKER LOST AT SEA FEARED DROWNED IN ROGUE WAVE.* Tomas read the article standing in the bait shop doorway and felt the shape of the lie settle over the town like the fog itself, and understood that the fog was on his side now. Rogue wave. The sea takes who it takes. Nobody in Grayhaven wanted to look too hard at the water, because everyone who worked it knew what else it carried.

Silas stayed in the room above the bait shop and did not exist. That was the discipline of it, and Tomas came to admire it the way you admire a rope bridge — the terrible faith required to walk it. She learned the hours of the fog. She learned which floorboards in the stairwell spoke and stepped around them. She learned to be a woman who had never been born, and she did it so thoroughly that some nights Tomas would climb the stairs and find her sitting by the stove with her hands folded and feel a chill walk up his spine, because the room looked empty, because she had made absence into a craft.

But a person cannot be an absence forever. That was the problem he lay awake with, staring at the ceiling, listening to her breathe on the other side of the wall. A person leaves traces. A person buys bread. A person, seen once at the wrong hour by the wrong eyes, becomes a rumor, and a rumor in a town like Grayhaven travels faster than a fire.

And there was the other problem, the one he didn't let himself name for weeks: the diary.

He'd gone back to the slip the morning after, once she was sleeping, and worked the shoreline in the gray light with a boat hook, because he was a practical man and a drowned woman leaves a practical wake. He found the coat, waterlogged, one button torn. He found a shoe. And snagged in the pilings under the slip, wrapped around a cleat like it had been flung there in a struggle, he found a canvas satchel, and inside the satchel, dry because the flap had held, a diary.

Silas Marchetti's diary. Fourteen months of it. A widow's handwriting, looping and careful, recording grocery prices and grief and the small unbearable domestic facts of a life that had ended on a pier. The last entry was dated the day of the drowning. *Emilio has been gone eight months today. I lit a candle. I am going to be all right. I think I am going to be all right.*

Tomas sat on the planks of the dead-end slip with the diary in his lap and the fog coming in off the water, and he understood — the way you understand a current, in your body before your head — that this book was the most dangerous object in Grayhaven.

Because it stopped. Because a diary that stops is a closed door, and closed doors get sealed, and sealed things get buried, and buried things stop being evidence and start being legend. But a diary that *continued* — a diary with entries dated after the drowning, entries in that same looping hand, written somewhere else, by a woman who was somewhere else, alive —

That was not evidence. That was a door left open.

That was an alibi.

* * *

He did it badly at first. He wants, in the years that follow — in whatever years he is given — to be honest about that. The first entry he wrote took him four hours and he hated every line of it. He sat in the bait shop after closing with the diary open under a lamp and a page of Silas's real handwriting propped in front of him like scripture, and he copied her loops, her slant, the way she crossed her t's with a little downward flick like a bird settling, and what came out of his pen looked like a schoolboy's forgery and felt like a sin.

But Tomas Vane had been forging things his whole life without knowing it. He had forged a smile through his father's funeral. He had forged nine years of small talk with strangers in boatyards, answering to a name that wasn't his, telling no one where he came from or who he grieved. He was a man made entirely of careful handwriting. He simply had never put it to paper before.

By the fourth entry his hand had stopped fighting him. By the tenth he could write as Silas the way an actor walks in borrowed boots — aware of the fit, then forgetting it. He gave her a journey. That was the trick, he realized, the thing that made it true: you couldn't just place her somewhere, you had to *move* her, give her a road, give her a reason. So Silas Marchetti, in the pages of her own diary, grieved, and packed, and took a bus south, and grieved some more, and found a room in a town two hundred miles away where nobody knew her name, and took work cleaning rooms at a motel, and wrote about the ocean from the wrong side of it, and slowly, entry by entry, in ink Tomas bought with cash, the dead woman came back to life in a place where no one could ever check.

He dated the entries forward. He kept a calendar and wrote ahead, a week at a time, then hid the diary — moved it, always moved it, because a hiding place is only safe until it's a habit — and told himself the lie that made the whole architecture stand: *she is alive somewhere else, and this is her record of it.* He was not forging a document. He was keeping a promise on behalf of a woman who could not keep it herself.

He never let himself think about what would happen if someone compared the handwriting to something real. He never let himself think about who might be good at comparing handwriting. He never, in all those months, let the thought of his sister so much as surface in his mind, because he knew — the way you know a hole in a hull — that if he looked at it he would sink.

* * *

Winter became spring. Silas planted things in coffee cans on the windowsill — basil, some kind of hardy mint — and called it her garden, and Tomas understood that she was not growing herbs, she was growing proof. Gardens are what living people have. Dead women don't thin seedlings.

She started doing the shopping herself, some weeks, in a headscarf and glasses and a coat that made her shapeless, at the far market, at the hour when the fog was thickest, and every time she came back Tomas's heart performed a small violent maneuver that he pretended was indigestion. She was careful. She was so careful. But he had learned from the diary — from the real entries, the ones written by the real hand — that Silas Marchetti's carefulness had a seam of stubbornness running through it, and stubbornness is the thing that gets careful people killed.

"You could stay in," he told her once, and heard his own voice come out wrong, too much like someone who cared, and she looked at him with those eyes that had made their peace once and had unmade it since.

"I spent fourteen months being a wife who waited," she said. "I'm not going to spend the rest of my life being a ghost who hides. I go out. I buy my own bread. That's the whole point of being alive, Tomas. Otherwise Brannock already won."

He didn't argue. He went out after she slept and walked the perimeter of the block in the fog like a man pacing a cell, counting the lit windows, hating every one of them for seeing nothing.

* * *

The trouble came in the shape of a letter.

Not a letter — a newspaper clipping, left in his toolbox at the boatyard, which meant someone at the yard was friendly with someone at the cannery, which meant the fog had eyes. The clipping was three weeks old. *INSURANCE FIRM RETAINS INVESTIGATOR IN MARCHETTI CLAIM.* And beneath the headline, a photograph of a woman getting out of a car in front of the Grayhaven municipal building, a woman with a file under her arm and a face that Tomas had last seen across a kitchen table nine years ago, younger by a decade and by a funeral.

Ulyana.

He stood in the boatyard with the clipping in his hand and the wrenches hanging silent around him, and the world did the thing it does when it narrows — sound draining out, color going to gray at the edges — and he read the article three times, and his memory, the family disease, held every word of it without being asked.

An investigator. Retained by the insurer. On the Marchetti claim.

He thought: *they'll send someone competent.* He thought: *they'll send someone who doesn't know the town, who'll be gone in a week.* He thought, with a coldness that frightened him: *whoever they send, Silas is a photograph and a paper trail, and paper trails end in rooms above bait shops.*

He did not think, for almost a full minute: *she'll see through me in a day, she's been reading my handwriting since we were children, she taught me the downward flick on the t's, she — *

And then he thought it, and the boatyard came back, the smell of diesel and brine, and Tomas Vane stood among the dead engines and understood that the one person in the world fluent in his hand was coming to Grayhaven to read it.

He burned the clipping in the shop's waste can and watched the photograph curl. Her face went last. It always did, in fires; the ink held where the paper gave.

* * *

That night he climbed the stairs to the room above the bait shop and found Silas at the window with her seedlings, and she took one look at his face and set down the watering can.

"Someone's asking questions," she said. Not a question. She had spent fourteen months married to a man who found things in ledgers, and she had learned the weather of it.

"An insurance investigator," Tomas said. "On the claim. The policy hasn't paid out. They want to close the file."

"Then close it." She said it lightly, and her hands, he noticed, had gone still in a way that stillness is only ever achieved by people practicing.

"They sent someone good," he said. And then, because he was so tired, and because the fog was pressing at the window, and because some part of him had been carrying this alone for so long that the weight had become a kind of loneliness with a shape: "They sent someone I know."

Silas looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the harbor lights came up one by one along the breakwater, small and yellow and useless against all that water.

"Someone from before?" she asked. "Someone who could — " she touched the diary, there on the shelf where she thought he kept it, and he watched her hand and said nothing — "recognize something?"

"Everyone's from before," Tomas said. "That's what before is for."

She almost smiled. He watched her decide not to press, and he loved her a little for it, in the way you love the people who let you keep your one locked room — and he hated himself for it too, because love was exactly the thing he had no right to be spending. Not here. Not on her. Love was how alibis got sloppy. Love was a door left open in a house you'd sworn to seal.

He went down the stairs, stepping over the board that spoke, and stood in the dark of the bait shop among the rods and the tackle and the smell of brine, and he let himself think it, finally, the thought he'd been holding underwater for fourteen months, and it came up like a body:

*She'll find the diary. She's the best there is at finding the thing that doesn't fit. And when she opens it, she'll know my hand the way she knows her own.*

And beneath that, quieter, in the voice of a boy at a funeral who had never stopped speaking:

*And maybe — God forgive me — maybe that's why I wrote it so well. Maybe some part of me has been writing to her all along.*

The fog rolled in off the water and took the harbor lights one at a time, like a ledger being closed.
