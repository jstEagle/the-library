## 16. The Widow Who Wasn't

The fog came in off the water the way it always did in Grayhaven, not arriving so much as remembering itself — the same gray shawl it had wrapped around the town for a hundred years, drawn close over the rooftops, the fish stalls, the faces of people who had learned to look through one another. Silas Marchetti stood at the mouth of Cooper's Alley with her hood up and her heart going like a gaff hook in her side, and she told herself, as she had told herself every morning for fourteen months, that no one was looking.

That was the arithmetic of her life now. Not money — she'd had money once, real money, the kind that came in an envelope with a policy number on it, though she'd never touched a cent of it. Not safety, exactly; there was no such thing, she'd learned, only intervals between dangers. The arithmetic was simpler than either. It was this: *how long since anyone looked at your face?*

Fourteen months. Fourteen months of being nobody, of being a woman named Ruth Ellery who rented a room above the bait shop and kept to herself and paid cash and smiled the small, forgettable smile of women who want nothing. Fourteen months of practicing a walk that wasn't hers — Silas had walked like a woman who worked the cannery floor, shoulders forward, hands ready; Ruth walked like a woman who was tired of waiting for something. It had taken weeks to learn the difference. It had taken longer to stop grieving the first one.

But Tomas had missed his Sunday at the mission kitchen. That was the thing. That was the splinter working under her skin all week.

Tomas Vane did not miss Sundays. In fourteen months he had missed one Sunday, and that was when the ice storm took out the coast road and even Sister Bernadette had said God would understand. He came every week with engine grease still under his nails from Saturday's work, and he sat at the end of the long table where the drifters ate, and he ate slowly and listened to everything, because listening was how Tomas loved things — quietly, thoroughly, without asking them to be anything other than what they were. He was the one who'd found her half-drowned on the mudflats with the tide going out of her lungs, and he had never once asked her why. He'd just started writing in her diary, in his careful hand, keeping her alive on paper while she learned to be dead in person.

And now he'd missed two Sundays running, and Sister Bernadette's face when Silas asked after him had done a thing Silas didn't like — a small tightening around the eyes, a glance toward the door, the look of a woman carrying information heavier than her habit allowed her to set down.

*Someone is asking questions,* Bernadette had said, which was already more than she should have. *A woman. From the city.*

Silas had known then. You know, in the body, before the mind consents to know. The way you know a storm is coming not from the sky but from the ache in old breaks, the way her husband's shoulder used to ache before rain, God rest him, God forgive her for what she'd let them do to his memory. Someone was asking questions, and the questions had found their way to Grayhaven, and Grayhaven closed around its secrets like a fist around a coin — but fists can be pried open, especially by people who are paid to pry.

So today she broke her own law. Today she went out in daylight, into the market, where the whole town might see her face.

Not her face. That was the point. Ruth Ellery's face, under the hood, behind the fog, wearing the tired-waiting walk. But underneath the practice and the performance, Silas Marchetti was still there, and Silas Marchetti knew that the only thing more dangerous than staying hidden was letting Tomas face whatever was coming alone. He had carried her for fourteen months. She could carry one afternoon of terror for him.

The market on Harbor Street ran the length of the fish sheds, trestle tables under canvas awnings gone green with salt rot, and the smell of it was the smell of the town's whole soul: brine and diesel and cedar smoke and the sweet-rot sweetness of apples past their prime that the fruit man sold cheap rather than haul back up the hill. Gulls screamed over the gutting tables. Somewhere a radio played something tinny and religious. Women in wool coats haggled over clams with the seriousness of diplomats.

Silas moved through it like a ghost through a wedding. She bought nothing. Buying left receipts, and receipts left trails, and trails were the one currency she could not afford. She kept her chin down and her eyes moving, cataloguing the way she'd taught herself to — not faces, never faces, but shapes, postures, the geometry of who stood where. A woman alone draws less notice if she seems to be looking for someone. So Silas looked for someone. She looked for Tomas's broad back and rolled sleeves among the crowd, and she did not find him, and each table she passed without finding him tightened the wire another turn.

She was nearly at the end of the row, near the flower stall where old Mrs. Petraglia sold carnations that smelled like funerals, when she saw the other woman.

It happened the way recognition always happens — sideways, below thought. Not the face first but the *stance*: a woman standing too still amid the market's motion, the way a heron stands in shallow water, everything about her arranged around the act of watching. She stood at the edge of the fish shed with a notebook in one hand, unopened, and she wasn't reading it. She was reading the market. Her gaze moved in sweeps, methodical, left to right, table to table, and Silas watched her watch three separate people, and saw each of them look away a beat too quickly.

City coat. City shoes, wrong for the cobblestones, heels worn on the outside edge from pavement instead of dock planking. Hair pinned with the kind of efficiency that says the mirror time was budgeted, not spent. And the notebook — everyone in Grayhaven carried notebooks, tide tables, parts lists, hymn numbers — but this woman held hers like an instrument, not a habit.

*From the city.* Bernadette's words. *A woman.*

Silas's mouth went dry as ash. She turned slightly, angling her body behind a rack of crab pots, and made herself breathe. Fourteen months of discipline said: *walk away now, hood up, tired-waiting walk, don't run, running is a confession.* And she would have. She was reaching for the alley mouth behind the flower stall when the woman turned her head to follow a gull's arc, and the fog thinned for just a moment, and the light off the water caught her full in the face —

— and Silas stopped breathing entirely.

Because she knew that face. Not from the market. Not from the city. Not from any wanted poster or newspaper or the terrible gallery of faces you memorize when you're hiding. She knew it from a photograph, a small creased photograph that lived in the breast pocket of a wallet that lived in the jacket of a man who fixed engines under a name that wasn't his, and she knew it because she had seen that photograph a hundred times without meaning to, the way you see the icon on a wall every day until it's printed on the inside of your eyelids.

Tomas kept a photograph of his sister. He had never said her name to Silas. He had never spoken of home at all, except once, drunk on communion wine Bernadette pretended not to count, when he'd said: *My sister remembers everything. Every word, every face. I used to think it was a gift. Then I understood it's a sentence.* And he had laughed, and it hadn't been a laugh at all.

Ulyana. That was the name he hadn't said. Ulyana Vane, who remembered everything, standing forty feet away in the Grayhaven fish market with an unopened notebook and a watcher's stillness, sweeping the crowd table by table, and Silas understood with a cold that started in her teeth and spread downward like frost across a window:

*Tomas didn't miss two Sundays. Tomas is hiding. From her.*

And if Ulyana Vane was here — here, in this town, walking these tables — then it wasn't chance. Cities are big. Towns like Grayhaven are small. An investigator doesn't wash up on a harbor's rocks by accident; she follows currents, and the current that led her here led through the diary, through the mission, through the trail of a dead woman who refused to stay dead on paper. Through Tomas's handwriting. Through everything he had built to keep Silas breathing.

Love had left a trail. That was the truth of it, the truth that hit her harder than fear, harder even than the sight of that remembered face: every kindness Tomas had done her had been a footprint. The diary entries dated after her drowning — footprints. The room above the bait shop paid in cash — a footprint. The false names, the practiced walk, the fourteen months of careful nothing — all of it, all of it, a line of prints leading straight back to the one man foolish enough, decent enough, stubborn enough to love a stranger off a mudflat. And now someone had come along reading the ground.

Silas pressed her palm flat against the cold slats of the crab-pot rack and made herself think. Panic was a luxury; panic spent attention like money, and attention was all she had. *What does she know? What can she know?* The diary had been recovered — that much Silas had assumed for months, ever since the entries changed tone, grew cautious, grew *watchful*, as if the hand writing them feared its own ink. If the sister had the diary, the sister had the handwriting. And if the sister had the handwriting, and the sister remembered everything —

Then she had already recognized it. Whatever brought her to Grayhaven, it wasn't suspicion. It was certainty, worn like a coat, buttoned to the throat.

Across the market, Ulyana Vane closed her unopened notebook and began, unhurriedly, to walk the row. Toward the flower stall. Toward the alley mouth. Toward the one gap in the canvas wall through which Silas would have to pass to get out.

There was no way past her. There was the church, though — the church with its side door that Bernadette left unlatched for the drifters who couldn't bear the front steps, the church with the confessional corridor that ran behind the sacristy like a vein behind skin. Twenty yards of open market between here and there. Twenty yards of being seen.

Silas pulled the hood lower, gathered the tired-waiting walk around her shoulders like the fog itself, and stepped out from behind the crab pots.

She did not look at the investigator. Looking was a hook; she'd learned that from watching gulls take bait. She walked, and the market flowed around her, and somewhere behind her — she felt it rather than heard it, the way you feel a shadow cross a window — the heron-stillness shifted, and footsteps began to move, and a voice, low and pleasant and terribly awake, asked the flower woman a question Silas couldn't quite catch.

Mrs. Petraglia's answer carried anyway, thin and clear across the stalls, in the flat tone of a woman deciding, in real time, how much of the truth a stranger deserved:

*"Her? Nobody, dear. Just somebody passing through."*

Silas reached the church door. It was unlatched, as promised. She slipped inside into candle-smell and cool stone, and leaned against the door with her whole body, and let herself shake — one long, silent tremor, head to heel, like a dog shedding water.

Then she pushed off the door and moved down the confessional corridor, fast and quiet, toward the boatyard, toward Tomas, toward the man whose love had built her a life out of lies and whose own blood was now walking the streets of Grayhaven with a notebook and a perfect memory, reading every footprint they'd left.

She had to warn him. She had to warn him tonight, before the sister's sweeps of the market narrowed, before the fog lifted, before the trail of prints became a road.

Behind her, faint through the church walls, she thought she heard the market bell, and beneath it — though it may have been only the sea, only her own pulse — footsteps following, patient as tide, all the way to the door.
