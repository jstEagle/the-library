## 19. The Husband's Grave

The cemetery sat on the bluff above Grayhaven like a congregation that had outlived its church.

Ulyana Vane had read about it before she ever saw it — *Hillcrest Memorial, est. 1897*, three paragraphs in a county history she'd memorized along with everything else, because her memory did not distinguish between the useful and the merely true. It kept all of it. That was the bargain she'd been born with: nothing left her, not the file numbers and freight manifests, not the smell of her father's coat, not the shape of Tomas's shoulders as he walked out of the funeral parlor nine years ago without looking back.

She climbed the gravel drive at seven in the morning, when the fog was still peeling off the water below and the headstones emerged from it one by one, pale and patient, like people waiting to be introduced.

She had come for Marco Marchetti.

The plot was easy to find. The dead of Grayhaven were arranged by parish, and the Marchettis had been Our Lady of the Harbor people for four generations; the stonecutter's work told her the rest even before she read it. Fresh-cut granite, less than two years old, the letters still sharp-edged:

**MARCO ANTONIO MARCHETTI**
**BELOVED HUSBAND. FAITHFUL SON.**
**1979 – 2023**
***THE SEA GIVES AND THE SEA TAKES***

*The sea gives and the sea takes.* Ulyana stood over it with her hands in her coat pockets and let the words sit in her mind the way an appraiser lets a lamp sit under light, turning it slowly, looking for the flaw.

The sea hadn't taken Marco Marchetti. She was nearly certain of that now, though certainty was a currency she spent carefully. He had gone into the water off Pier Nine on a Tuesday night in October with a blood-alcohol level the coroner's report called *consistent with recreational drinking* — a phrase Ulyana had underlined twice, because coroners did not write like poets unless someone asked them to. He had drowned in four feet of calm water beside his own boat, a man who'd grown up on these docks, who could swim before he could write his name. His widow had vanished fourteen months later, and somewhere between those two facts lay half a million dollars of insurance money and a diary written by her brother's hand.

But this morning she wasn't here for Silas. She was here for the man under the granite, because she had finally understood something in the small hours of the motel room, lying awake while the foghorn counted its slow intervals: everyone in this story was looking at what happened *after* the ledger. Nobody had asked who found it first.

Someone had to have found it first. Ledgers do not hide themselves. Codes do not get penciled into hymnals by ghosts. And if Brannock's smuggling line had been running payoffs through fake claims for years — if DeWitt's firm had been laundering its biggest client's dirty money through grieving widows and drowned fishermen — then the most dangerous man in Grayhaven wasn't the fixer or the claims manager or even the nun who lied beautifully from behind a soup pot.

It was whoever noticed first. Whoever did the arithmetic nobody wanted done.

And Marco Marchetti, according to every record Ulyana had memorized, had done the cannery's books for six years. Part-time, off-season, cash under the table. A dockworker with a knack for columns of figures. The kind of man nobody watches, because he is supposed to be exactly where he is.

*The sea gives and the sea takes.* She crouched, brushed a film of lichen from the base of the stone with her thumb, and said quietly, to no one, "You didn't fall, did you."

The foghorn answered. It always answered. That was the thing about this town — it never gave you silence, only noise shaped like an answer.

* * *

The maintenance shed sat at the cemetery's eastern edge, a lean-to of gray cedar with a rusted padlock that surrendered to Ulyana's multitool in eleven seconds. She noted the time — 7:42 — and filed it away with everything else, because filing things away was the only prayer she knew.

Inside smelled of gasoline, cut grass, and decades. A riding mower under a tarp. Coil after coil of hose. A wall of hand tools hanging on painted outlines, each silhouette labeled in a careful, old-fashioned hand — *spade, rake, hoe, post-driver* — the kind of order that belonged to a man who had worked the same ground for forty years and intended to keep working it after he was dead.

And on the workbench, a logbook.

Green canvas cover, spine cracked white. *HILLCREST MEMORIAL — GROUNDS LOG* stenciled across the front in the same careful hand. Ulyana put on gloves — she'd bought nitrile gloves at the pharmacy on her second day in town, along with a flashlight and a rain shell, the whole kit of a woman who expected to touch things other people didn't want touched — and opened it.

The entries ran back eleven years. Weather, mowing schedules, interments, repairs. The caretaker — a W. Ostrander, judging by the initials — wrote in a blunt carpenter's pencil, all business: *Rain 2 days. Fixed north gate hinge. Mrs. Calloway buried, plot 114, good turnout.*

Ulyana turned pages with the flat of her gloved finger, letting her eyes drink each line. This was how her memory worked best: not searching, but flooding. She didn't look for anything. She let everything in, and trusted the current to carry what mattered to the surface.

October 2023. The month Marco died.

*Oct 3 — Rain. Mowed around new section, wet clumps.*
*Oct 5 — Fair. Strimmed fence line.*
*Oct 8 — Overcast. Interment Marchetti, plot 87, 10 a.m. Family small. Widow very quiet.*

Ulyana stopped.

*Widow very quiet.*

Three words. Nothing. And yet her mind snagged on them the way a sleeve snags on a nail, and she made herself keep reading, made herself stay flooded, because the worst thing her gift could do was let her want something so badly that she started seeing it where it wasn't.

*Oct 11 — Fair. Repaired vase at plot 87. Someone left cigarettes.*
*Oct 14 — Wind. Branch down near chapel.*
*Oct 17 — Fair. Someone left cigarettes again at 87. Moved them. They came back.*

Cigarettes. Ulyana closed her eyes and let the file play behind them: Marco Marchetti's autopsy toxicology, memorized in the rival firm's conference room off a fax so blurry she'd had to reconstruct half the numbers from context. Nicotine metabolites present. Cotinine levels consistent with regular smoking — *regular*, daily, a pack-a-week at minimum. But Silas Marchetti, in every photograph, in every neighbor statement, in the diary itself, was emphatically not a smoker. *He quit for me,* she'd written once, early, in an entry about their third anniversary. *He smells like coffee and engine oil now and I love him more than I can say.*

So who smoked? Who came back, again and again, to leave cigarettes on a dead man's grave?

A friend. A brother. Or a wife who couldn't stop buying his brand, standing at plot 87 with a lit cigarette she set down burning on the granite because she couldn't bear to smoke it herself and couldn't bear not to light it.

Or — and here the cold thrill moved through her chest like a fish turning under ice — a wife who came back *after* her own disappearance. After October of last year. After the sea supposedly took her too.

Ulyana turned forward through the log, faster now, hunting dates. November. December. January of this year. The entries thinned in winter — *Snow. Salted walk. Pipe burst in shed* — and then, on a raw gray Thursday in February, a single line that stopped her breath:

*Feb 6 — Fair. Found flowers at 87 wrapped in paper from the mission. Left them.*

The mission. Sister Bernadette's kitchen, where a quiet woman had arrived the night Silas drowned, according to a lie told so smoothly Ulyana had admired it even while seeing through it. Flowers from the mission, left on the grave of a man whose widow was officially dead. Which meant either the mission was haunted, or Silas Marchetti walked out of that soup kitchen sometimes, in some borrowed face, and came up this hill to stand at plot 87 with flowers in her arms.

Ulyana stood very still in the dim shed and felt the case rearrange itself around her, the way a room rearranges when someone opens a curtain. Not a woman hiding from grief. A woman keeping a vigil. Fourteen months of vigil, carried on in secret, in cigarettes and flowers, in a handwriting that wasn't hers because her own hand would have signed her death warrant.

Tomas wrote the diary so the world would believe she was gone. But she came here so *she* would believe Marco wasn't.

Ulyana pressed her gloved palm flat against the workbench and breathed once, slowly, the way she'd taught herself to breathe in interrogation rooms when the person across from her was lying and she needed to wait them out. Except there was no one across from her. There was only a green logbook and a dead man's grave forty yards away and the growing, terrible understanding that she was not investigating a fraud at all.

She was investigating a eulogy that had gotten out of hand.

* * *

She went back to the logbook because she hadn't finished flooding. That was the discipline: never leave a document half-read, because the half you skip is always the half that matters. She turned pages backward now, toward the summer before Marco died, toward the season when — if her theory held — a part-time bookkeeper with a knack for columns had started doing arithmetic somebody didn't want done.

June 2023. July. August.

*Aug 12 — Hot. Man came asking about plots. Said he was planning ahead. Didn't buy.*

Ulyana read it three times. *Man came asking about plots. Said he was planning ahead. Didn't buy.*

Planning ahead. In August. Two months before he went into the water. Men don't shop for their own graves in August and then drown by accident in October — not men who grew up swimming these waters, not men whose widows describe them, in diary entries Ulyana could recite verbatim, as *"careful, always careful, he checks the stove twice."*

Marco Marchetti had known he might die. He had come up this hill, looked at the ground, and gone back down to finish whatever he'd started. And then he had died anyway, which meant whatever he'd started had a shorter fuse than he thought.

Ulyana's hands were steady. Her hands were always steady. But her mouth had gone dry, and she took the flask of coffee from her coat and drank it lukewarm, standing among the hoses, and made herself think like the bookkeeper instead of the investigator. If you were Marco Marchetti, and you found a ledger of payoffs hidden in your employer's books — payments routed through a shell company, laundered through fake insurance claims, including maybe, someday, a claim on *you* — what would you do? You couldn't go to the police; Brannock owned half the harbor and rented the other half. You couldn't go to the insurer; the insurer was inside the machine. You couldn't run; Silas was here, and running meant taking her into the dark with you.

So you'd build a dead drop. Something innocuous. Something no smuggler, no fixer, no claims man would ever think to search. Something you could get to every Sunday, in plain sight, in a building full of witnesses who saw nothing because they weren't looking.

Our Lady of the Harbor. The hymnals. The penciled numbers in the pews — page references, dates, dollar amounts, a ledger disguised as devotion. Ulyana had assumed the code was Brannock's, or his paymaster's, a system for moving figures between hands. But what if it was older than that? What if the hymnals had been a *channel* first and a weapon second — Marco's channel, his way of passing what he'd found to someone he trusted, and Brannock's people had simply co-opted a system already in place?

Or the reverse. Either way, the same question: was there a master copy? A key? Bookkeepers keep keys. Bookkeepers are keys.

She looked at the logbook again, really looked, and this time she didn't flood — she focused, the way she focused on a witness's face, cataloging micro-expressions. The cover. The spine. The way the canvas was worn smooth at the corners from years of the same hands. And there, on the inside front cover, in the same carpenter's pencil but a different pressure — lighter, faster, written by someone who didn't own the book but had borrowed it —

a column of numbers.

Small. Unobtrusive. Eight lines, arranged like a shopping list, like nothing:

**417**
**231**
**417**
**88**
**112**
**305**
**417**
**56**

Ulyana's heart began to beat hard and slow, the way it did when tumblers started turning. Because she knew these numbers. She had copied every penciled figure from every hymnal in Our Lady of the Harbor into the perfect archive of her mind, and she knew that 417 appeared more than any other number — recurring, anchoring, the way a name recurs in a letter. She knew 231 corresponded to a date in March. She knew 88 mapped to eight hundred dollars, 112 to eleven hundred, 305 to a shipment logged on the fifth of May.

And she knew — because she had stood in that pew for an hour on Wednesday evening, pretending to pray, committing every smudged digit to memory — that these exact eight numbers, in this exact order, appeared on the flyleaf of the hymnal in the third pew from the back. The pew where, according to Sister Bernadette's own recollection, a quiet woman used to sit.

Marco Marchetti had written his key here, in the grounds log of his own future grave, where only a caretaker would ever read it. And someone — Silas, almost certainly, coming up the hill with her flowers and her cigarettes — had found it, copied it, and carried it back down to the mission, where it lived now in pencil on a hymnal flyleaf, hidden in plain sight for fourteen months.

He discovered the ledger first. He built the code. And they killed him for it, and the code outlived him, and his widow had been guarding it ever since — not just her own secret, but his. The whole time. Every entry Tomas forged, every flower left at plot 87, every lie Bernadette told with God watching — all of it bent around protecting a dead bookkeeper's column of numbers.

Ulyana photographed nothing. There was nothing to photograph that a court would take seriously, and everything to photograph that Brannock would kill for. Instead she stood in the doorway of the maintenance shed with the morning sun finally breaking through the fog, and she recited the eight numbers aloud, once, softly, the way you'd recite a psalm, and felt them settle into permanent residence in her mind alongside everything else she had never been able to forget.

Then she closed the logbook, returned it precisely to its angle on the bench, wiped her single set of fingerprints from the doorframe with her sleeve, and walked up the hill to plot 87.

* * *

The grave was neat. Someone had weeded it recently — the soil dark and freshly turned at the edges, no dandelions, the granite base scrubbed. On the marker's ledge, weighted against the wind under a smooth gray stone, lay a cigarette.

Unlit. Fresh. The brand was a cheap local one, the kind sold at the bait shop.

Ulyana stood over the grave for a long time. Below her, Grayhaven was waking — gulls arguing over the fish market, a tug groaning out past the breakwater, the mission's kitchen chimney putting up its first thin thread of smoke. Somewhere down there, a woman who was legally dead was tending a garden of false names, and a man who shared her face-shaped hole in the world was repairing engines under a stranger's name, and both of them believed the danger was behind them.

It wasn't. She had seen the boot prints in the cannery. She had heard DeWitt's voice tighten around words he'd been told to use. Brannock was counting crates again, and when men like Brannock count, they eventually arrive at the number that matters: one investigator, one nun, one boatyard mechanic, one ghost. Small numbers. Manageable numbers.

Ulyana crouched and, with two gloved fingers, moved the cigarette aside. Beneath it, scratched faintly into the granite ledge — so faint she understood why no one had ever reported it, why even she would have missed it in any light less slanted than this morning's — was a single mark:

**417**

Page 417. The anchor number. *Be Thou My Vision*, the old hymn — she'd checked, of course she'd checked, she checked everything — the hymn about seeing beyond what eyes can see.

Marco's signature. His way of saying *I was here, and I knew, and if you're reading this, you know too.*

"Okay," Ulyana said aloud, to the granite, to the man under it, to the fog lifting off the water where he'd supposedly drowned. Her voice came out low and even and nothing like bored. "I see it. All of it. Every number, every name, every payment — I've got it, and it doesn't burn, and it doesn't drown, and it doesn't forget."

She straightened, and the sorrow that had been sitting in her chest since the mudflats — sorrow for Tomas, for Silas, for the nine wasted years, for the father whose funeral had split her family open — did something she hadn't felt it do in years.

It hardened. Set like concrete. Became structure.

Purpose.

Because this was no longer a claim file. It was no longer a career or a bonus or a statute clock ticking toward midnight. It was a murdered bookkeeper's message, fourteen months undelivered, and she was the only courier alive who could carry it intact.

She turned down the hill, gravel crunching, and was halfway to the gate when she saw the car.

Black, unmarked, parked on the shoulder below the cemetery drive with its engine running and its wipers clearing a fog that had already lifted. Behind the wheel, a broad silhouette she recognized from the cannery floor, from the fish market, from the shape of every predator she'd ever watched pretend to be furniture.

Brannock's man. Sitting there. Watching the graves.

Watching *her*.

Ulyana did not hurry. Hurrying was a confession. She walked down the drive at the pace of a woman visiting family, passed within thirty feet of the idling car without turning her head, and memorized the plate in a single glance — she would hold that plate forever, along with everything else — and kept walking toward the harbor road, toward the mission, toward the one person in Grayhaven who had lied to her face and prayed about it after.

Because the key was in a hymnal, and the hymnal was in a pew, and the pew was in a building full of people Brannock could reach before noon.

And Sister Bernadette had a girl hiding upstairs.
