## 26. Midnight at the Mission

The rain came in off the harbor around eleven, a fine gray needle-rain that turned the mission's windows to frosted glass and made the whole of Grayhaven sound like a room full of whispering. Sister Bernadette had counted the cars twice from the kitchen window. Three of them now, parked with too much space between them, the way men park when they don't want to be seen together. Engines idling. Wipers going. Nobody getting out.

She dried her hands on her apron and looked at the clock above the stove. Eleven-forty. The night men would come for their coffee at midnight, the ones who couldn't sleep indoors, and the mission would smell of burnt toast and dish soap, and everything would be ordinary. That was what she told herself. That was the prayer she kept repeating like a rosary with the beads worn smooth: *everything will be ordinary, everything will be ordinary.*

Upstairs, behind the door she'd painted the same color as the wall, a woman who did not exist was folding a blanket that belonged to no one, and Bernadette had put her there, and God would have to sort out the arithmetic of that later.

The first car door shut. Then the second. Then, softly, like a held breath released, the third.

Bernadette turned off the kitchen light.

* * *

Brannock's men came through the front the way water comes through a cracked hull — patient at first, then all at once. There were six of them, and the one in front was called Merce, a broad man with a boxer's flattened nose and the unhurried manner of someone who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered. He tried the mission door, found it unlocked, because mission doors were unlocked, because that was the whole point of them, and stepped inside into the smell of coffee and candle wax.

The hall was empty. The dining room was empty. Forty folding chairs sat in their neat rows like a congregation dismissed.

"Kitchen," Merce said, and two men peeled off.

Bernadette stood at the stove with a dish towel over her arm as if she'd been there all along, as if the light being off were some quirk of frugality. She was sixty-one years old and had spent thirty of those years feeding men who frightened other men, and she had learned that fear is mostly a performance, and that the way to beat a performance is not to attend it.

"Sister," Merce said.

"You'll want to wipe your feet," she said. "I just did this floor."

"We're looking for a woman."

"There are always women looking for this mission. It's the door with the cross."

"A particular woman." He took off his cap, which she supposed was meant as respect, and turned it in his hands. "Dark hair. About forty. Been here before, maybe. Came in the night a woman named Silas Marchetti drowned."

"Half the coast came in that night," Bernadette said. "It was a bad storm. We fed sixty."

"You remember sixty?"

"I remember the ones who needed second helpings." She turned back to the stove and lit the burner under the coffee pot with a hand that did not shake, and she was proud of that, and ashamed of being proud, and she offered both feelings up together like a bundled bouquet. "Sit down if you're sitting. Otherwise the door's behind you."

Merce looked at her for a long moment. She could feel him deciding whether she was a wall or a window. She made herself be a wall. Behind her, above the stove, the little window with the painted-over latch looked out onto the alley and the coal chute, and she did not look at it, because not looking was a skill too, and she had practiced it for two weeks.

"Father Olem said you keep to yourself," Merce said.

"Father Olem is a wise man."

"Yeah." He put his cap back on. "We'll have a look around anyway. Insurance people got a warrant or something. Paperwork." He said *paperwork* the way other men said *forgive me*, without meaning.

"There's the chapel, the dining hall, the washrooms, and my kitchen," Bernadette said. "You'll find God in the first and dirty dishes in the last. Take your pick."

They took both. She heard their boots in the chapel, heard one of them laugh at something — she'd learn later it was the plaster saint with the chipped foot that the children always laughed at too — and she stood at her stove and stirred coffee nobody had asked for and prayed the way she'd learned to pray in hard years: not with words, which were too slow, but with attention. *Attention* was her word for it. She attended to the sound of the boots. She attended to the coffee. She attended, most of all, to the silence upstairs, which was the loudest thing in the building, and which had to stay silent for maybe four more minutes, and she counted those minutes the way she'd once counted contractions, with her whole body.

Then the bell rope moved.

She hadn't touched it. Nobody had touched it. But the mission bell hung in the tower above the chapel on a rope that ran down through a closet behind the sacristy, and that closet had a second door, and that second door opened onto the stairwell, and the woman upstairs had been in this building for eleven days and had noticed everything, because women who are hiding notice everything.

The bell went off like the end of the world.

* * *

Silas had heard the cars too. She'd heard them before Bernadette had, because she'd been lying awake in the narrow bed listening to the rain and doing what she did every night now, which was rehearsing. She rehearsed the routes out of the building. She rehearsed the names she'd have to abandon if tonight was the night. She had a whole liturgy of departures and she ran through it like the offices of the hours, and when the third engine went quiet she came off the bed without a sound and stood in the dark with her shoes in her hand.

The boots came in the front. She heard Bernadette's voice, steady as a lighthouse, and she pressed her palm flat against the door — the door painted the color of the wall — and she felt the vibration of the building through her hand like a pulse.

*They're inside. They're inside and the stairs are the only way down and the stairs are where they'll be.*

The window. The painted latch. She'd asked Bernadette about it on her second day, casually, the way you ask about the weather, and Bernadette had said, "Painted shut since the flood," and Silas had said, "Since the flood," in a tone that meant *I'll be the judge of that*, and two nights ago, with a butter knife and forty minutes and her heart going like a bird in a box, she had freed it. The paint had come away in curls like the petals of some diseased flower. The latch had turned. The window opened six inches, enough, onto the sloped lead roof of the sacristy, and beyond the roof the alley, and beyond the alley the coal chute.

She'd looked at that chute every day from the kitchen. A steel throat angling down from the alley into the old coal cellar, its hatch long rusted open, its sides slick with forty years of rain and soot. It was a terrible way out. It was the only way out.

Now the bell went off above her, huge and bronze and insane, and Silas flinched so hard she bit her tongue, and then she understood — *she's ringing it, the mad old saint is ringing it* — and every plan she had rehearsed became worthless and every plan she hadn't rehearsed became the plan.

Boots on the stairs. Two men, maybe three, taking them fast, and Merce's voice below, sharp now, all the patience gone out of it: *"The tower. Check the tower."*

Silas got the window up. The rain came in and hit her face like a handful of coins. She went over the sill onto the lead roof, which was slick and cold and tilted toward the alley, and she lay flat for one second, one full second, letting the rain pin her there while the bell above her swung and groaned and the whole night rang with it. Lights were coming on in the houses across the street. Doors opening. That was the point of a bell, she thought, that was the whole ancient point — you rang it and the town came, and even if the town only came to complain, it came, and men who worked in the dark hated witnesses more than anything on earth.

She went down the roof on her heels and her palms, slow, terrible, the lead burning cold under her hands, and dropped the last three feet into the alley mud. Her ankle turned. She heard it — a small wet click — and felt it bloom, and she made a sound through her teeth and kept moving, because the chute was ten feet away and the boots inside were coming down the stairs two at a time and the bell was still ringing, ringing, ringing, and she understood suddenly that Bernadette would ring it until she couldn't, that the bell was the sister's whole argument, that some women argued in bronze.

The hatch of the coal chute was a black mouth at the base of the wall. She went in feet first.

* * *

Tomas had been in the mission when they came. That was the whole reason he'd come — he'd driven out from the boatyard through the rain because he didn't like the way the week had gone, didn't like the silence on the phone from Brannock's office, didn't like the feeling in his hands, which had been wrong all day, the way they got wrong before weather or trouble. He'd come to sit in the back of the dining hall with coffee he didn't drink, near the stairs, near the painted door, the way he'd sat near it a dozen nights, a paid-for nothing, a man whose whole job was to be present if the present was needed.

He was in the hall when the boots came in the front, and he was on the stairs before the bell rang, because he'd counted the voices and there were six and the stairs were the throat of the building and everything went through the throat.

He met the first two on the landing.

He was a boatyard man with forearms like dock line and he'd been in fights before, dock fights, drunk fights, but never anything like this, because these men fought the way they did everything — professionally, without anger, without hurry. The first one he put down hard, an elbow and a shove and the man went backward down six steps and didn't get up fast. The second was better. The second was very good. Tomas got one arm free and used it, and for a moment, one bright ringing moment while the bell crashed above them and the rain hammered the roof, he thought he might actually hold the stairs.

Then the third man came up behind him and something hit the back of his head and the world went white and sideways.

He went down on his hands and knees. He could hear, from very far away, the bell. He thought: *she's still ringing it. Good. Keep ringing it.* He thought: *the chute. She knows about the chute. I showed her the alley map, she knows.* He thought about his sister, which was strange, because he thought about her most when he was least able to do anything about anything, and the thought was simple and had been simple for nine years: *I should have written back.*

Hands took his collar. A voice said something about the room upstairs, about the painted door, and another voice said the room was empty, bed cold, window open, and the first voice said a word that Tomas didn't catch because he was busy being sick, quietly, with great dignity, onto the mission floorboards.

"Who else is in the building?" the voice asked him, close to his ear.

Tomas looked up. Through the blood coming down over his eye he could see the stairwell, the chapel door, the bell rope swaying in its closet through the open second door, and Bernadette standing at the bottom of the stairs with her hands folded and her face like a locked church.

"Nobody," he said. "You got here too late. Everybody's gone. It's just us and the coffee."

They hit him again. He decided, in the floating interval that followed, that he had said the right thing.

* * *

Bernadette watched them work him over at the top of the stairs and she did not cry out and she did not intervene and she would spend the rest of her life deciding whether that was discipline or cowardice, and she would never settle it, and maybe that was what faith was — living inside a question you couldn't close. What she did was stand at the bottom of the stairs with her hands folded, and when the man with the flat nose came down and said, "You saw nothing, Sister," she looked at him with the full mild terrible weight of thirty years of other people's confessions and said:

"I see everything. That's the job. Whether I say it is between me and someone with better credentials than you."

Merce looked at her. Outside, the rain was easing, and the neighbors were on their porches, and the bell was still going, because Bernadette had rigged the rope to the rail with a loop of clothesline before she came down, and it swung and clanged in the wind like a heart that refused to be talked out of beating.

"Your bell's broken," Merce said.

"Then you'd best leave before it calls someone who can fix it."

He went. They all went, the way bad weather goes, unhurried, promising to return, and the last thing the flat-nosed man did at the door was look back up the street toward the harbor, toward the cannery, and Bernadette saw him look and understood that this had not been a search. It had been a message. *We know she was here. We know you know. We are patient.*

When the cars had gone she cut the clothesline and the bell fell silent, and the silence was enormous. She went up the stairs. Tomas was sitting against the wall with his head back and his eye already swelling shut, and he was laughing — a low, cracked, disbelieving laugh, blood on his teeth.

"She went out the chute," he said. "Tell me she went out the chute."

"The coal cellar door opens into the fish market," Bernadette said. "I oiled the hinges Tuesday."

He closed his one good eye. "Sister," he said, "you are the finest criminal I have ever met."

"I'm going to need you to not be bleeding on my floor," she said, and then, because her hands were finally shaking and she could not stop them and did not try, she knelt down beside him in the wreckage of the stairwell, in the rain-smell and the bell-smell and the coffee-smell, and she prayed out loud, fast and ungrammatical and grateful, while somewhere across the dark town a woman with a ruined ankle limped out of a coal cellar into the fish market, and the clock over the harbor read eleven minutes to midnight, and eleven days remained on the statute, and in a rented room across from the cannery an insurance investigator sat with a ledger rebuilt from memory, hearing the bell die away and knowing — the way you know a knock at your own door — that everything had just changed.

The phone in her motel room was ringing. It rang four times before she picked it up, and the voice on the other end was smooth and unhurried and belonged to a man she had never met and already knew by heart.

"Miss Vane," Brannock said. "I think it's time we talked about your brother."
