## 32. Brannock Closes In

The trouble with a town like Grayhaven was that it remembered everything and said nothing, which meant the saying had to be bought one mouth at a time. Brannock had been buying mouths for twenty-two years — since before the cannery closed, since before the Marchetti boy went into the water with his skull cracked like a dropped jar of pickled herring — and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth knowing.

First: everyone talks eventually. Not to the police, not to priests, but to someone. A wife. A bartender. A dog, if there's nothing else breathing. The trick was never to stop the talking. The trick was to be standing close enough to hear it.

Second: money is only leverage until it isn't. A man who owes you can be forgiven. A man you've paid can be exposed. The best position in any arrangement is the one where the other party can't tell whether they're your partner or your hostage.

Third — and this was the one he'd learned hardest, back when his hands still smelled of fish meal and his name was still on a union card — fear doesn't keep. Fear spoils like chum in the sun. You have to renew it daily or it turns on you.

He was thinking about all three of these things at seven minutes past nine on a Tuesday evening, sitting in the back booth of the Anchor Line with a plate of fried clams going cold in front of him, when Petey Kowalczyk slid onto the bench across from him and said, "She's meeting them. Tomorrow night. At the cannery."

Brannock did not look up from his plate. He picked up a clam, considered it, ate it. He chewed the way he did everything — thoroughly, without hurry, as though the world were a thing to be processed rather than survived.

"Which of them," he said.

"The investigator. The brother. And her." Petey's voice had the wet brightness of a man who'd been running for six hours and had finally found somewhere to put what he was carrying. "The Marchetti woman. My cousin's kid works the bait shop on Harbor Street, and she saw the woman come down the stairs after the fire — the night of the fire — and she didn't say nothing then because she figured the woman was a squatter, but tonight she saw her again, walking up Cannery Row with the Vane woman and the mechanic, and they went in through the loading tunnel, and my cousin's kid knows the Marchetti woman's face because—"

"Because she sold her bait for two years and never once looked at her hands." Brannock brushed salt off his fingers, one at a time. "Yes."

"You're not surprised."

"I'm never surprised, Petey. Surprise is for people who haven't been paying attention." He pushed the plate an inch toward the center of the table, an offering, and watched Petey's eyes drop to it the way they always did, hungry and ashamed of being hungry. "Eat. You've earned it. Now tell me about tomorrow. Time?"

"After ten. Kid says the investigator told the mechanic midnight matters. Statute runs out at midnight Thursday, so I figure tomorrow's their last chance to get their story straight before—"

"Before nothing." Brannock unfolded his napkin, smoothed it flat against the table with the edge of his hand, and began folding it again along its original creases, over and over, a small patient violence done to paper. "There's no *before* for them anymore. There's just what happens in that building, and what gets written down about it afterward, and which of those two things anyone ends up believing."

Petey stopped chewing. "You're not thinking of doing something stupid."

"I'm thinking of doing something efficient. There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork." Brannock smiled, and it was the smile that had made two aldermen retire early and one harbor master leave the state. "The cannery's condemned. Has been for eleven years. The east wing came down in the storm season before last; the rest of it is held up by rust and prayer. It's a firetrap, Petey. Everyone in this town knows it's a firetrap. It's been waiting to burn since the day they padlocked the doors. All I'd be doing is letting it."

"And them inside it."

"People shouldn't squat in condemned buildings. It's dangerous." He let the napkin lie still. "Three bodies in a fire nobody will be able to investigate properly because the building collapses before the department can get a ladder up. The widow's already legally dead — a body that matches her description won't even raise a flag, they'll just amend a file. The brother's got a record of obstruction coming; a man facing charges burns himself up sometimes, happens every year, sad business. And the investigator—" he paused, savoring it, the way another man might savor the last line of a good novel, "—the investigator is a problem the insurance industry has wanted solved for weeks. Nobody will look too hard at that particular ash."

"That's murder, Walt."

"That's arithmetic." He signaled the waitress for the check with two lifted fingers. "Murder is when it's personal. This is inventory management. The Marchetti woman is a liability that's been accruing interest for fourteen months. Her husband was the same kind of liability, and we handled him, and look how much quieter the harbor's been ever since. She's the last entry in a ledger that should have closed when he hit the water. Tomorrow night I close it."

* * *

He walked the Row himself that night, because he had never trusted a job to men whose feet hadn't touched the ground it would happen on.

Cannery Row after dark was a spine of broken streetlights and salt-eaten brick, the buildings hunched shoulder to shoulder like old men waiting out bad weather. Fog came off the water in slow white ropes and wound itself around the pilings. Somewhere out past the breakwater a bell buoy counted the seconds — five, five, five — patient as a metronome in an empty room. Brannock walked the length of it with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, and the fog took his footfalls and gave nothing back, and he thought: *this town has always kept my secrets better than I've kept its.*

The cannery rose out of the mist at the end of the Row, black against blacker water, its remaining wing a long gabled silhouette with the windows knocked out, so that in certain lights it looked like a skull seen edge-on. He stood across the street from it for a long while, longer than a careful man should stand anywhere, and let himself remember.

He'd been young when the place ran full shifts. He remembered the smell — brine and diesel and the sweet rot of fish left too long in the sun — and the sound, the endless industrial heartbeat of the lines, and the women on the cleaning floor with their rubber aprons and their quick red hands, and Silas Marchetti among them, younger then, laughing at something, her hair tied back with a strip of blue cloth. He remembered her husband, Emile, tall and serious, counting crates with a pencil behind his ear, counting them wrong on purpose, skimming a little off every manifest because Emile had a sick mother and a pride problem and had convinced himself that what Brannock didn't itemize, Brannock wouldn't miss.

Emile had been wrong about that. Emile had been wrong about a great many things, right up until the night he found the second ledger — the real one, the one with the hymn numbers, the one that mapped every dirty dollar from the cannery floor to the shell company to the client firm that insured half the waterfront — and instead of coming to Brannock to negotiate, like a sensible man, he'd gone quiet and started copying pages.

Quiet men were the dangerous ones. Loud men you could buy. Quiet men you had to drown.

Brannock looked up at the ruined wing, at the dark sockets of its windows, and felt nothing he would have called guilt. What he felt was closer to housekeeping. Fourteen months ago Silas Marchetti had walked into the harbor wearing her husband's coat, and the plan had been clean: a drowning, a grief-stricken widow, a half-million-dollar policy funneled through the claim machinery DeWitt's people controlled, the last loose thread of Emile's discovery tied off and paid for. Except the widow hadn't drowned. She'd crawled out somewhere down the coast, or been pulled out by some Good Samaritan with a boat and a soft heart, and she'd vanished into the town's blind spots, and the claim had sat unpaid and unclaimed while the statute bled down toward midnight like a clock in a locked room.

And now she was coming back. To the cannery. With the one investigator in three states who could reconstruct a ledger from a glance and the one mechanic in Grayhaven who'd spent nine years being nobody on purpose.

It was almost flattering. It was certainly inconvenient.

He crossed the street. The loading tunnel breathed cold air at him, smelling of iron and old oil and the ghost of a million fish. Inside, his flashlight found the staging area where the unmanifested cargo used to wait for the night boats — the pallet scars still printed in the concrete like fossilized footprints — and higher up, where the investigators had poked around, the scuff marks of fresh boots in the dust. Their dust. Tomorrow's dust.

He walked the wing end to end, reading the building the way Ulyana Vane read documents. Here, the old varnish room, dry as tinder for a decade. Here, the solvent drums the demolition crew had abandoned when the money dried up — three of them still sealed, still legible, still flammable enough to make the point emphatically. Here, the stairwell that would funnel smoke upward and cut off the loading tunnel exit within four minutes of ignition, assuming the wind came off the water the way it always came off the water. Here, the section of roof that the storm had already half-removed, guaranteeing collapse, guaranteeing that whatever the fire department found afterward would be found with shovels.

He didn't need accelerant, though he'd bring it anyway. The building was the accelerant. Eleven years of neglect, of deferred maintenance, of insurance lapsed and permits denied — he had arranged all of it himself, years ago, quietly, the way a farmer arranges a field to lie fallow. The cannery had been dying on his schedule since before any of them knew they'd need it to.

That was the whole secret, he thought, standing in the dark with his breath smoking faintly in the cold. People believed violence was the work. Violence was the receipt. The work was everything you did years earlier, so that when the moment came, the universe itself seemed to conspire — a condemned building, a storm-damaged roof, a foggy night, a fire-prone district, a dead woman walking. You didn't push events. You built a slope, and waited, and gave things a small shove, and gravity did the rest, and gravity had never once talked to the police.

He checked his watch. Ten forty-one. Above him, faint through the broken roof, the bell buoy counted on. Five. Five. Five.

Tomorrow, he thought, the count stops.

* * *

DeWitt answered on the second ring, which meant he'd been sitting by the phone, which meant the termination order had already gone out and the man was nervous about whether it would hold.

"It's late," DeWitt said. No greeting. There never was, between them; greetings were for relationships, and what they had was a contract.

"It's necessary." Brannock stood at his office window above the harbor, watching the fog eat the navigation lights one by one. On the desk beside him lay a single sheet of paper, and he touched it as he spoke, the way a gambler touches his hole card. "I'm calling about your investigator. Vane."

"What about her? Her termination's processing. As of Friday she's—"

"As of tomorrow night she's irrelevant." He let that sit. He could hear DeWitt breathing, hear the small wet sounds of a man rearranging his face around new information. "But before she becomes irrelevant, I want to make sure we understand each other about the Marchetti file. The claim. The statute."

"The statute expires at midnight Thursday. After that the claim lapses, the money stays in reserve, and the whole thing goes to archives. That's the law, Walter. Nothing I can do about—"

"No," Brannock agreed pleasantly. "Nothing you can do about the law. But laws are one thing and claims are another, and claims are decided by companies, and companies are decided by men, and you, Gerald, are the man. So here's what's going to happen. Tomorrow night there may be an incident at the old cannery property. Fire, most likely. Condemned structure, no sprinklers, no alarms, no night watch — you can verify all of that yourself, it's all in the underwriting history your own firm wrote when they declined the demolition bond. If anyone connected to that incident files anything — a claim, a suit, a complaint, a letter — your firm will find no basis to honor it. No coverage. No goodwill payment. No ex gratia anything. The file closes and stays closed. Am I understood?"

A silence on the line, long enough that the buoy's count seemed to fill it. Then DeWitt, carefully: "If there's a death involved, there'll be inquiries. Wrongful death, negligence — the estate could—"

"There's no estate, Gerald. The widow's been dead fourteen months. You denied the inference yourself in your own memo — I have a copy, page two, your phrasing, 'no credible evidence of survival.' Very tidy writing. It'll hold up beautifully." Brannock turned the sheet of paper over on the desk. It was DeWitt's memo, and beneath it, in a folder, were photographs, and beneath those, a file on Tomas Vane thick enough to stop a door. "As for the others — a career criminal's brother and a fired investigator trespassing in a condemned building? Your legal department will bury that correspondence in a drawer so deep it comes out the other side of the earth. You'll make sure of it. Because here's what I'm holding, Gerald, in case your memory needs refreshing: the original ledger page in my handwriting and yours. The authorization memos on the Marchetti claim, initialed. The photographs of the payout routing. Everything, indexed, duplicated, and stored somewhere neither of us can burn."

"You wouldn't—"

"I've never once had to. That's the point of me." He said it without heat, almost gently, the way you'd explain tides to a child. "You'll send the instruction down tomorrow morning. Claims has blanket authority to deny anything touching the cannery incident. Use the phrase 'pre-existing structural condition' — it's in the policy exclusions, subsection eleven, and it will make everything that happens in that building tomorrow night legally invisible. No claim will ever honor it. No court will ever reopen it. By Friday it will be as if none of those people were ever in that building at all."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Friday you'll be explaining subsection eleven to a federal grand jury instead of to me, and I promise you the grand jury asks harder questions." He paused, and for the first time all night something almost like warmth entered his voice, which was the most frightening thing he'd said yet. "You've been a good partner, Gerald. Twenty-two years. Do this last thing and you'll retire with your pension and your reputation and your hands as clean as they were ever going to get. Which is cleaner than mine, and cleaner than hers, and cleaner than anyone who ever signed a hymn book in that mission. We're past clean, all of us. We're just deciding how loud the ending is."

He hung up before DeWitt could answer. Some conversations were better left unfinished; an unfinished conversation stayed obedient.

For a while he stood at the window. Below, the harbor lay black and smooth, and the fog moved through it like something alive and unhurried, and the bell buoy counted its five beats into the dark — five, five, five — marking time for a town that had never once heard it.

Tomorrow night, he thought. They would gather in the dark of the cannery where it all began, the widow and the witness and the brother, trading their little truths, building their little alliance, believing that midnight was a wall that would protect them. Believing the law was on their side because the calendar was.

They'd forgotten the oldest rule of the waterfront. The law was what got written down. And by tomorrow night, there would be nothing left to write on.

He picked up his coat. In the pocket, his fingers found the small tin of lighter fluid he'd carried for thirty years and never once needed — a superstition, a talisman, a joke he told himself. He took the stairs down two at a time, and outside, the fog closed over him like water, and far out past the breakwater the buoy went on counting, patient, patient, patient, as if it already knew how few counts were left.
