## 33. Fire on Cannery Row

The smoke found us before the flames did.

It came down the cannery's central corridor like a living thing — gray at first, almost apologetic, curling along the ceiling beams the way fog curls into a harbor at dusk. I had been standing at the old sorting table with Silas's hand on my sleeve, both of us listening to Tomas count the seconds aloud in the dark, when I smelled it: hot pitch, burning tar paper, and beneath that something sweeter and far worse — creosote-soaked wood, forty years of it, the whole building a wick waiting for its match.

"That's not accidental," Tomas said.

No. Accidental fires announce themselves. They crackle, they pop, they give you the courtesy of noise. This one breathed. It moved with intention, low and patient, the way Brannock did everything — no wasted motion, no drama until drama was useful. He had learned of the meeting, and he had answered not with men but with chemistry. Why risk witnesses when you could simply erase the room?

"Loading tunnel," I said. "South wall. We mapped it."

"We mapped it an hour ago," Silas said. Her voice was steady in a way I would come to understand was her native language — fourteen months of being dead will teach a woman to speak calmly inside a catastrophe. "By daylight. With a flashlight. It's different now."

She was right, and I hated her for being right, because what she meant was: your memory is about to be tested, investigator, and we are all going to bet our lives on it.

"Then let's not waste the test," I said, and took her wrist, and ran.

* * *

You should understand something about my memory, since this is the story of how it saved us and ruined me in the same hour. People imagine a photographic memory as a filing cabinet — pull the drawer, find the folder, read the page. It isn't. It's a room you can never leave. Every face, every figure, every hymn number penciled in a donated hymnal lives in there permanently, lit whether I want them lit or not. For most of my life I treated it as a professional instrument, a scalpel. DeWitt used to say I could recite a policyholder's dental records from a two-second glance at a claim form, and he meant it as praise.

But a room you can never leave becomes a prison when the things stored in it start screaming.

That night in the cannery, as the smoke thickened and the first orange light began to pulse through the corridor behind us like a heartbeat accelerating, my memory gave me everything at once. The floor plan I'd memorized from the county assessor's microfiche — 1971 renovation, south wing converted to flash-freeze storage, loading tunnel running ninety feet under Harbor Road to the water stairs. The route Tomas and I had walked three nights ago, counting paces: forty-two from the sorting floor to the junction where the tunnel forked, left branch bricked over in '89, right branch open but flooded at high tide. The tide table I'd read that morning without thinking much of it — low tide at 11:40 p.m., which meant the tunnel floor would be wet but passable, which meant—

Which meant the tunnel was also the reason Brannock had chosen fire instead of men. He knew about the tunnel. He'd used it himself, once, for cargo that never appeared on manifests. And a man who knows a building's secret exits seals them first.

"The hatch," I gasped, skidding around the corner where the conveyor gantry crossed overhead. "He'll have sealed the tunnel."

Silas stumbled beside me, coughing. Behind us, Tomas had stopped.

"I'm going back," he said. "They'll come in behind the fire. Someone has to slow them down so you two get clear—"

"There is no *them*, not yet — the fire *is* them—" But he was already turning, and I grabbed his collar the way I hadn't grabbed him since we were children fighting over the last of Mother's plum jam, and for one suspended second we were nine and twelve again in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere except in the room inside my head.

"Tomas." I made myself say it slowly, the way you speak to someone standing on ice. "If you go back there, I lose you twice. I won't survive it a second time."

Something moved across his face — the tell I'd been reading since chapter one of our shared history, the small downward flick of his left eyebrow that always preceded surrender. He'd worn it the night our father died. He'd worn it the day he left. He wore it now.

"Junction," he said hoarsely. "Forty-two paces. Then you lead."

* * *

We ran.

I will not pretend I experienced those minutes heroically. What I remember — what I will always remember, filed irretrievably in the permanent collection — is the sound. Fire in an old cannery does not roar; it *chews*. It ate the tar paper roof above us in long, wet-sounding bites, and every bite sent down a rain of black flakes that landed on my arms and stayed there, little constellations of our own destruction. The smoke dropped lower with each corridor. Somewhere a window burst inward and the fire inhaled, and the whole building seemed to lean toward us like a curious animal.

And through all of it, I counted.

Thirty-one. Thirty-two. My memory laid the assessor's blueprint over the darkness like tracing paper — support column here, rusted scale platform there, the gap in the floor rail where a worker had fallen in 1983 and prompted the lawsuit that had, in its small way, started everything, because the settlement had been paid by a shell company, and shell companies were Brannock's native script. I stepped over the gap without looking. Silas didn't. She went down hard, knee striking steel, and I hauled her up by both forearms and felt her weight — real, warm, stubbornly alive — and understood in one white-hot instant why Tomas had spent nine years learning to lie beautifully.

You don't build an alibi out of fiction. You build it out of the refusal to feel this exact feeling ever again.

"Left at the junction," I said. "Bricked side. Don't take the bricked side."

"You just said left—"

"*Right* branch. Left is brick." Even now, even burning, some pedantic clerk in my head insisted on correcting the record. God help me, I loved that clerk. She was the only part of me that had never lied.

Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. The junction materialized out of the smoke exactly as the microfiche promised — the fork, the dead-end arch sealed with cinder block gone soft with damp, and to the right, descending, the tunnel mouth, its steel hatch hanging open.

Hanging open.

Tomas saw it at the same moment I did. "He didn't seal it," he whispered.

"No," I said, and the cold that went through me had nothing to do with the sea air breathing up from below. "He wants us to use it."

Because of course he did. A locked building full of bodies was a massacre, and massacres brought state police, and state police asked questions that even a bought claims manager couldn't un-ask. But a fire with one survivor stumbling out of the water at the harbor stairs — one survivor who'd been seen entering the cannery illegally, trespassing, arson-adjacent, half-mad with smoke — that wasn't a massacre. That was a narrative. Brannock wasn't trying to kill us. He was trying to *author* us.

"He'll have someone at the water stairs," Silas said. Flat. Certain. A woman who had spent fourteen months thinking like prey.

"Then we don't go to the stairs." I closed my eyes — absurd, in a burning building, but the room inside my head needed no light — and walked the tunnel in memory. Ninety feet under Harbor Road. The assessor's plan showed the main run, but the 1971 renovation file had included a plumbing addendum, and the addendum showed a maintenance lateral, eighteen inches of galvanized duct, branching off twenty feet in, running parallel under the road to a storm grate behind the fish market. Too small for a man. Not too small for the plans rolled in oilcloth that Tomas carried strapped to his back, or for the ledger pages sewn into Silas's coat lining, or —

"Not too small for me," I said, opening my eyes. "Maybe. If I'm wrong, I die stuck in a pipe like a cork, and honestly after tonight I've made peace with undignified endings."

"You're not going in any pipe," Tomas started.

"She is," Silas said. "And then I'm going in after her, because I'm smaller than both of you and I've spent a year learning to fit through the world's cracks." She looked at me, and in the pulsing orange light her face was stripped of everything but the essential architecture — cheekbone, jaw, the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic with her own life. "How sure are you?"

My memory presented the addendum page. Galvanized duct, eighteen-inch bore, forty-foot run, installed 1971, serving the flash-freeze condensate line, access grate at market rear. I turned the page over in my mind. I checked the welds. I checked the date against the county permit log I had once skimmed for eleven seconds while waiting for a records clerk.

"Ninety percent," I said.

"And the other ten?"

"The other ten percent is why I'm going first."

* * *

The tunnel took us down into cold and dark and the smell of the sea, and behind us the hatch glowed like a furnace door as the fire reached the junction and found, to its disappointment, nothing left to chew but memories.

I went into the duct feet-first, on my back, arms raised, worming upward through a throat of rusted metal while the world narrowed to the circle of gray light ahead of me and the rasp of my own breath. There is a particular loneliness in a pipe. No one can follow you closely enough to touch. You are alone with the one companion you can never dismiss — the archive, the endless room — and so, to keep panic at bay, I did what I always do.

I recited.

Hymn numbers. Page 217, October 4th, four thousand dollars. Page 88, June 19th, twelve hundred. The whole false liturgy of it, Brannock's laundering scheme dressed in the Lord's songbook, every figure marching past me in the dark like pallbearers. Above me, muffled through forty feet of earth and asphalt, the cannery groaned and settled, and somewhere in that settling the original ledgers burned — the physical copies, the paper truth, the evidence that would have convicted a dozen men in a fair courtroom. I had carried copies out. Silas carried copies. But the masters, the ones with the signatures, were ash by now, and I felt their loss the way you feel a tooth go — not pain exactly, but a new shape in the mouth, a vacancy the tongue keeps returning to.

Except.

Except that I had read them. All of them. Twice. Under a bad bulb in a motel room, my lips moving, my mind photographing each page the way it photographs everything, indiscriminately, forever. Brannock thought he was destroying evidence. He was destroying the *duplicate*. The original had been sitting behind my eyes for six days, catalogued, cross-referenced, indexed against tide tables and shipping manifests and the 1983 settlement and DeWitt's carefully phrased warnings, every word of which I could reproduce verbatim, including the pause before "*quietly resolved*" that told me exactly who had written his script for him.

Paper burns. That was the whole genius of Brannock's method, the whole architecture of his quiet empire — nothing written that couldn't be unwritten, nothing kept that couldn't be fed to a stove. He had built a criminal enterprise out of the assumption that the world forgets.

He had never met anyone like me.

I laughed, there in the pipe, a small unhinged sound that echoed off the galvanized walls, and behind me Silas's voice came floating up the duct: "Keep moving, investigator. Whatever joke you just told yourself, tell it faster."

* * *

The storm grate gave up on the third kick. I spilled out into the alley behind the fish market on my elbows and knees, into air that tasted like salt and diesel and the most beautiful thing I had ever breathed, and then I turned and reached back into the dark and pulled Silas through after her, and then Tomas, and the three of us lay in a heap of oilskin and soot among the crates while above the rooftops Cannery Row turned orange.

From the alley we watched it burn. There was nothing else to do. The fire brigade would come — Grayhaven was small but it had pride — and by then the south wing would be a skeleton and the north wing conveniently untouched, and the fire marshal would write *electrical, aged structure, no indication of foul play*, because that was what fire marshals wrote in towns where the harbormaster owed Brannock money. The system would close over the night like water over a stone.

But we had the diary's true final pages sewn in Silas's coat. We had the oilcloth plans. We had Tomas, bleeding from a gash on his scalp he refused to discuss, alive, which was more than I'd dared to hope at the junction. And we had — I touched my own temple, gently, the way you touch a wound that is also a treasure — we had everything else. Every figure. Every name. Every hymn number. The entire ledger, intact, incombustible, stored in the one vault in Grayhaven that Brannock couldn't reach, couldn't buy, and couldn't burn.

Silas sat up, soot-striped, watching her fourteen months of hiding go up in the smoke. "Everything I built," she said quietly. "Every identity. All of it was in that room."

"I know," I said.

"It doesn't matter." She said it like a woman testing whether the words would hold her weight, and finding that they did. "Do you know why? Because none of it was ever real. The names weren't real. The diary wasn't real — you taught me that, Ulyana, you and your brother, you taught me that a life written down by someone else isn't a life at all." She looked at me, and there was something fierce and newborn in her face. "Tomorrow I sign away the policy. Tomorrow I walk into the commission under my own name and I say it all out loud. Let him burn the paper. I'm done being paper."

Above us, the cannery's roof beam folded with a sound like a struck bell, and sparks went up into the fog, hundreds of them, rising and dying, rising and dying.

Tomas put his hand on the back of my neck — the first time he'd touched me voluntarily in nine years — and said, "It's almost midnight."

Not midnight tonight. Midnight of the statute date. Two days. The clock that had been ticking in every scene of this whole affair suddenly audible even through the fire's roar, even through the bells that were beginning, far off, to ring the volunteers awake.

Two days. One witness who remembered everything. One woman done with dying. One brother who had finally, finally run toward me instead of away.

Behind the fish market, unnoticed in the chaos, a man in a yellow slicker turned from the mouth of the alley and walked unhurried toward the pay phone on the corner — and I memorized his face, all of it, every pore, because whatever Brannock paid him to report, I intended to know precisely what version of tonight reached its author, and to be ready, word for word, when the lie arrived.
