## 1. The Alphabet of Absence

The cathedral woke before Cyrus did, the way it always did, its stones ticking as the night's cold loosened its grip. He had learned to read those sounds the way other men read weather: the groan of the south transept meant frost; a certain hollow tick in the tower stair meant rain coming off the moor by noon. St. Aldhelm's spoke in a language of settling and strain, and after nine years Cyrus Oyelaran was fluent.

He arrived at half past six, as he did every morning but Sunday, letting himself in through the cloister door with the key that lived on a cord beneath his shirt. The key was warm from his skin. That small fact pleased him more than it should have—the idea that the building and he exchanged heat, that they were, in some marginal way, the same temperature.

The archive occupied the undercroft beneath the north aisle: three vaulted chambers that had once stored wine for monks who were dust before Blackmere had streetlamps. Now they held the town instead. Deeds and diocesan returns, parish registers going back to 1741, coroners' inquests, school board minutes, the ledgers of businesses long dissolved—every paper life Blackmere had ever shed ended up here, boxed and foldered and alphabetized under Cyrus's hand. The town thought of him, when it thought of him at all, as a kind of filing clerk with a Latin degree. It did not occur to anyone that an archivist is a confessor of a different order, or that the boxes on his shelves contained more sins than any booth upstairs.

He put the kettle on in the annexe—a lean-to of corrugated glass bolted to the undercroft's outer wall, his one concession to the modern world—and while it heated, he made his first circuit. This too was ritual. He checked the dehumidifier's gauge, ran a finger along the top shelf of Bay 4 where damp liked to gather, straightened the two boxes that always leaned, no matter how often he corrected them, as if they were tired of standing. Then he stood a moment in the middle of Chamber Two, in the smell of acid-free card and old vellum and the faint mineral breath of stone, and let the silence settle over him like a vestment.

It was, he would have said if anyone had asked—and no one asked—the best hour of his day. Perhaps of his life.

* * *

By eight the volunteers began to arrive, and the archive changed character the way a church changes when the congregation files in. Mrs. Tallow, seventy-three and sharp as a paper cut, took up her post at the reading table with her magnifying glass and her opinions. Dev Achebe, a retired teacher who came Tuesdays and Thursdays, started on the map drawers he'd been flattening since spring. They were good people, competent people, and Cyrus was fond of them in the way one is fond of furniture that has proven reliable.

They talked to him about their grandchildren, about the price of heating oil, about the flower show. They never talked to him about themselves—not really—and he had stopped, years ago, offering himself in exchange. There had been a period, early on, when he'd tried. He'd mentioned Lagos once, and then the university, and then the reason he'd come to England with credentials that required no further discussion, and he had watched the polite glaze come down over Mrs. Tallow's eyes like a shopkeeper pulling a shutter. After that he'd learned the local grammar: you gave weather, you gave flowers, you gave the cathedral's history, which everyone already knew, and you kept the rest in a box with a finding aid no one else could read.

"Post," said Mrs. Tallow, setting three items on his desk. "And there's a woman downstairs asking after burial records. 1980s. I told her you'd be along."

He dealt with the woman, found her grandmother's plot in the register, walked her out through the nave because she wanted to see the stone itself. She wept a little, touching the carved name, and thanked him as though he had done something personal, and he felt the familiar strange ache of that—that strangers trusted him with their dead, that he was keeper of the town's griefs, and that not one of them could have picked him out of a police lineup as anything more than *the archivist fellow*. He belonged to the building. The building belonged to the diocese. And Cyrus belonged, in the way of all custodians, nowhere at all.

Back downstairs, he opened the post. A conservation supplier's catalogue. A letter from the Greer Family Trust regarding the annual maintenance grant, signed with a flourish he recognized without looking—Aldous Greer had endowed half of Blackmere and signed everything personally, as though generosity were a signature worth collecting. And a plain brown envelope with no return address, containing a single sheet: a photocopied page from some self-published local history, *Blackmere: A Town Remembers*, with a paragraph highlighted in yellow. It concerned the Voss woman.

Marion Voss. Bookkeeper, forty-one years old at the time of her disappearance. Last seen on the fourteenth of October, eleven years ago this autumn, walking toward the river path in a green coat. The file sat in Cyrus's archive—of course it did; everything sat in Cyrus's archive—in Box 212, in a folder labeled MISSING PERSONS: VOSS, M., and it had not been touched by another human hand in eleven years. He knew this because he was the one who touched it. Once a year, in October, he took the box down, checked the papers against the inventory, and reshelved it. It was the only ritual he performed that wasn't in his job description, and he had never examined why he did it. Some part of him understood that an unsolved thing left in the dark begins to rot, and that someone ought to keep it clean.

There wasn't much in the folder. A statement from her landlady. A note from the constabulary closing the inquiry—*no evidence of foul play; presumed voluntary absence or misadventure*—signed by a superintendent whose name Cyrus had seen on enough charitable gala programmes to know it belonged to the Greer orbit. A photograph: a narrow-faced woman with dark hair going grey early, squinting slightly, as if the camera had interrupted a thought. And one oddity, catalogued in Cyrus's own neat hand on the inventory sheet: *Item 5 — personal diary (empty), recovered from subject's lodgings.* Empty. The police had flipped through it, found nothing, and filed it. Cyrus had done the same, every October, for nine Octobers.

He looked at the highlighted paragraph now. It said nothing he didn't know. *Mrs. Voss was well-liked and meticulous, and her disappearance remains one of the town's quiet sorrows.* Quiet sorrow. Blackmere collected those the way he collected documents—filed them somewhere out of sight, consulted them rarely, and called the arrangement peace.

He put the photocopy in the recycling, then, after a moment, retrieved it and filed it in Box 212. Habit. Or something that only looked like habit.

* * *

The afternoon brought the donation.

Donations came through the side door on Thursdays: attics emptied, estates cleared, widows bringing shoeboxes of letters they couldn't bear to burn and couldn't bear to keep. Cyrus logged each one with the same care he gave everything, because provenance was the spine of his trade—an object without a story of how it came to you was just clutter with ambitions.

This one was a crate, wooden, unmarked, left on the step sometime between his lunch and two o'clock. No note. No donor form. Inside, wrapped in a tea towel gone stiff with age: a brass desk clock, stopped at 4:17; a bundle of letters tied with ribbon; and a diary bound in blue cloth, its corners worn round.

He logged the clock, skimmed the letters—parish business, nothing sensitive—and came last to the diary. He opened it to check condition, as he always did, flipping to the middle to test the binding.

The handwriting stopped him.

Not because it was beautiful—it was, in a cramped, slanted, hurried way—but because the ink changed halfway down the page. The earlier entries were written in a brown-black fountain pen, even and unhurried. The later ones in ballpoint, pressed hard, the letters narrower, the slant sharper, as though the writer had become a different person between one entry and the next. People did become different people. That was not the strange part.

The strange part was the dates.

Cyrus turned back to the first page. *Marion Voss*, it said, in the fountain-pen hand, followed by a date eleven years and some months past. He turned forward. Entry after entry, weekly, then daily, the handwriting evolving the way living hands evolve—until the final dated entry, near the end of the book, which was written three weeks ago.

Three weeks ago, Marion Voss had been dead or vanished for nearly eleven years. Her file lay forty feet from where he sat, in Box 212, gathering its annual inch of dust.

He sat very still. The dehumidifier hummed. Somewhere above him, the verger's vacuum droned across the nave floor, indifferent as weather.

He did what he was trained to do, which was nothing dramatic. He checked the binding for inserted pages—none. He compared the later handwriting against the earlier, stroke by stroke, and found it continuous, evolved, *grown*. He noted the paper stock matched throughout, aged evenly, no substitutions. Whatever this book was, it had been written across eleven years by one hand—or by someone so devoted to imitation that they had reproduced the natural drift of a living script.

Then he closed it, wrapped it back in the tea towel, and did not log it into the collection.

This was, he understood dimly, a violation of everything he was. An archivist who withheld an item from the record was an archivist who had decided he was bigger than the record. But his hands, wrapping the cloth, were steady, and his heart was going like a boy's, and some door in him that had been shut for nine years—the door behind which he kept the reason he'd come to England, the falsified credential, the man he'd been before Blackmere—swung open an inch and breathed out.

He locked the diary in the bottom drawer of his desk, under the false bottom he'd installed for valuables awaiting the safe, and went home to his flat above the ironmonger's, where he ate standing up and slept badly.

* * *

He returned at dawn, before the volunteers, and took the diary out again.

In daylight—or rather, in the undercroft's amber lamplight—he read it properly, from the beginning, and it was at first exactly what it appeared: the commonplace book of a careful woman. Weather. Choir rehearsals. The cost of boiler repairs. Marion Voss had been the sort of person who recorded the price of things, and Cyrus felt an immediate, unearned kinship with her, this woman who believed that what was written down stayed true.

Then, a year before the end of the fountain-pen entries, the tone shifted. She wrote of discrepancies. Of figures that wouldn't reconcile. Of a ledger she was keeping *in parallel*, because the official one lied. She never named the firm outright, but she named initials, and one initial appeared again and again, in entries that grew shorter and colder:

*A.G. has asked me to adjust the third quarter again. I smiled and said I'd see to it.*

*I have made a copy. I don't know yet who to trust with it. Perhaps no one. Perhaps I will simply keep it, the way one keeps a stone in the mouth.*

*If anything happens to me, it will look ordinary. Remember that. Ordinary is what they do best.*

The fountain-pen entries ended on the twelfth of October, two days before she disappeared. The ballpoint began eleven days later.

*They think I am in the river,* the new hand wrote. *Let them.*

Cyrus set the book down. His hands were not steady now.

He read the rest of that first sitting—could not have stopped if the building had caught fire around him. The entries that followed were sparse, coded almost, full of precautions and patience. She wrote of moving. Of watching. Of learning that the man she feared owned not merely a firm but a town—its bank loans, its council votes, its constabulary promotions, its charities, its silences. She wrote of a witness to something older and worse than fraud, a witness everyone believed dead, referred to only ever as *T.* And she wrote, in the final weeks, in a hand that had grown cramped and hurried, of a cipher keyed to the carved saints of a cathedral, and of research hidden *where the dead keep secrets*, and of a deadline approaching that had nothing to do with her and everything to do with the law's short memory.

The last entry, three weeks old, read in full:

*The archivist will find me soon. They always do, eventually—the ones who keep the records. I have watched him for a year now. He touches my file every October. He is careful, and lonely, and honest in the way of men who have something to hide. That is the right kind. That is the only kind who ever opens the box.*

Cyrus sat for a long time in the lamplight with the diary open on his knees, listening to the cathedral tick and settle above him, fluent as ever in its language of stone.

Someone had chosen him. Someone had known his rituals better than he did—known the October touch, the loneliness, the drawer with the false bottom, perhaps, or at least guessed at the shape of the man who kept it. The archive he had trusted for nine years, the one system in his life that had never lied to him, had just been used to lie to him beautifully: a donation with no donor, a book that should not exist, addressed to no one and to him.

Outside, far off, the eight o'clock bell began, and beneath it, faintly, he heard footsteps on the cloister gravel, stopping just short of the undercroft door.
