## 6. A Dinner with the Devil

The invitation arrived on cream cardstock, hand-addressed in a copperplate hand that had clearly been hired rather than inherited.

*Mr. Cyrus Oyelaran requests the pleasure of—* no. *Aldous Greer requests the pleasure of Mr. Oyelaran's company at dinner, Thursday next, seven o'clock, Wrenfield House.*

Cyrus read it three times standing at the archive's front desk, the card balanced on his palm like something that might bite. In eleven years at St. Aldhelm's, he had received perhaps a dozen personal invitations, and most of those had been for christenings and funerals, where his role was furniture with a filing system. No one invited the archivist to dinner. The archivist was the man you sent things to—boxes of dead aunts' letters, water-stained parish registers, the sediment of respectable lives—and then you went back to your own.

He turned the card over. On the reverse, in smaller script, almost an afterthought: *I understand you've been cataloguing the Voss bequest. I knew Marion, years ago. It would be good to speak of her with someone who knew her work.*

The lie was elegant, Cyrus had to admit. He had told no one about the diary. He had told no one he was cataloguing anything of Marion Voss's at all. Which meant Aldous Greer knew what was in the archive without asking the archive, and knew what Cyrus had touched without asking Cyrus, and had chosen, out of all the possible responses to that knowledge, to send cream cardstock and a dinner hour.

He thought about Priya Iyer's voice on the telephone two nights before, low and flat as a stone skipped across still water: *The Greers own the town's institutions, Cyrus. The bank, the school governors, the paper, and half the constabulary. If Aldous Greer invites you to dinner, it's because he's already decided what you're worth.*

He should have declined. He knew that even then, standing in the smell of vellum and dust, the afternoon light falling in dusty columns through the south windows. He should have written back some polite regret, some archivist's apology about pressing duties among the folios. Instead he found himself composing an acceptance in his head before he'd even decided to, and the speed of it frightened him more than the invitation had.

Because here was the thing he would not say aloud, the thing he kept in the same sealed room of himself where he kept the diary and the crypt and the name *T.*: some part of him had been waiting his whole life for a card like this. Cream stock. Copperplate. His name, spelled correctly, treated as though it mattered. Forty-one years of being the man who knew where everything was filed and was invited nowhere, and here, at last, was the most powerful man in Blackmere asking him to sit at his table.

He wrote his acceptance in his best hand, and he hated himself a little as the nib scratched, and he posted the reply anyway.

* * *

Wrenfield House sat on the high ground above the town, behind iron gates and a quarter mile of beech, and Cyrus had lived in Blackmere for eleven years without once seeing it from closer than the road. The taxi—Greer had sent one, which felt less like courtesy than like ensuring the mouse arrived—crunched up the drive at seven o'clock precisely, and the house rose out of the dusk the way old money always did: not grand so much as inevitable, Georgian brick gone the color of dried blood, windows lit warm against the coming dark.

Aldous Greer opened the door himself.

Cyrus had seen him a hundred times across the nave at St. Aldhelm's, in the family pew on the north side, third row, the pew with the brass plate worn smooth by a century of Greer hands. From a distance he was simply an old man, silver-haired, upright, dressed with the kind of expensive restraint that never announced itself. Up close, the distance collapsed and something else remained. His eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and they did the thing that Cyrus would remember afterward as the true beginning of his fear: they took inventory. Face, hands, coat, shoes, the set of the shoulders. Not rudely. Not even quickly. The way Cyrus himself looked at an uncatalogued box.

"Mr. Oyelaran." The handshake was dry and exact. "You're punctual. I find that rarer than intelligence, and worth more."

"Thank you for the invitation, Mr. Greer."

"Aldous, please. Everyone who dines with me is Aldous." He stepped back to admit him into a hall paneled in dark oak, hung with portraits whose faces had gone soft and brown with age. "Marion always said the archive was the best-kept room in this county. She meant it as a compliment. She meant most things as compliments, even when they weren't."

The name, dropped so casually into the first minute, landed exactly where it was aimed. Cyrus felt it like a pin slipped under the skin.

"She was generous with her opinions," Cyrus said.

"Was," Greer repeated, and his face did something remarkable. Grief moved across it—Cyrus could see the machinery working, the slight droop at the mouth, the pause before the next breath—and it was *good*, it was nearly flawless, and it was the practiced grief of a man who had performed this same sorrow so many times that the sorrow had worn a groove in him, like a stair worn hollow by feet. "Eleven years. You'd think a body would learn to stop expecting the phone to ring. Come through. The study's warmer."

The study was not warmer; it was exactly temperature-controlled, like everything else in the house. It was a room built to be photographed for a newspaper profile: shelves of leather-bound books that Cyrus's professional eye assessed in one sweep as mostly unread, a desk the size of an altar, a fire laid and lit with geometric precision. On the mantel, among the silver frames, stood a photograph of a younger Greer with his arm around a young man—Miles, Cyrus realized, the estranged son, the one who never appeared at the family pew—and beside it, smaller, a woman Cyrus did not recognize, and then, at the end of the row, a frame turned face-down.

He catalogued that. He catalogued everything. It was the only weapon he had brought to dinner.

"Whisky?" Greer asked, already pouring. "You strike me as a man who drinks what he's given and doesn't complain."

"I strike people accurately, then."

The old man laughed—a real laugh, or a real-sounding one—and handed him a glass of something amber and expensive. They sat by the fire like two men who had done this a hundred times, and for twenty minutes Aldous Greer talked about nothing with the effortless fluency of a man who had spent sixty years making nothing sound like something. The flood defenses. The school's new roof. The scandal of the county council's parking scheme. Cyrus answered when answering was required and let the talk wash over him, and the terrible thing—the thing he would lie awake examining later—was how *pleasant* it was. The fire. The whisky. The attention. Being spoken to as though his replies were worth hearing. He had not known how starved he was until someone set a meal in front of the hunger.

Then, in the pause between the parking scheme and the price of barley, Greer said, without any change of tone at all:

"You've found her diary."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the patience of a thing that had outlived everyone who wound it.

"I'm not at liberty to discuss the contents of the collection," Cyrus said.

"Of course not. Discretion is the whole of your profession; it's why I'm speaking to you and not to the police." Greer turned his glass slowly on its coaster, one full rotation, the way a man turns a thought. "Marion Voss was a troubled woman, Mr. Oyelaran. Brilliant with figures—she kept books for me for six years, and there was never a penny out of place—but troubled. Her family had a history of it. When she went, it broke something in this town. We all loved her. I loved her. And I have watched for eleven years while strangers picked at the wound."

"Strangers," Cyrus said. "You mean the constabulary."

"I mean anyone who couldn't let a grieving thing lie." The pale eyes came up and held him, and there was nothing warm in them now, though the voice stayed kind as a hand on the shoulder. "I understand the diary continues past her disappearance. I understand there are entries in it that have caused you—concern."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when you realize the deck is moving. Cyrus kept his face still through long practice; he had spent a lifetime being the man no one watched, and the skill ran both directions. But his heart had begun to count.

"I couldn't speak to the condition of any item in the collection," he said.

"No. You couldn't." Greer smiled, and the grief-groove in his face smoothed out, and for a moment Cyrus saw the thing underneath the performance, the thing that had signed ledgers and buried partners and owned a town: a patience so complete it looked like benevolence. "Here's what I'd like instead. I'd like to endow the archive. A new wing—climate control, a proper conservation studio, a salary for you that doesn't require you to count the candles, if you'll forgive the expression. A hundred and eighty thousand a year, in perpetuity. St. Aldhelm's keeps its archivist, Blackmere keeps its memory, and you, Mr. Oyelaran, finally become what you should have been all along: indispensable."

He let it sit there between them, glowing like the fire.

"And in return?" Cyrus asked.

"In return, nothing so crude as conditions. I'm not a crude man." Greer rose, went to the desk, and drew out an envelope—not cream cardstock this time but a bank's letterhead—and laid it on the low table between them. "A first tranche. Consider it a gesture of faith. The Voss matter has caused this town enough pain. If the diary is what people say it is, then it belongs in a locked room, in a controlled environment, in the care of a professional. Not in gossip. Not in courtrooms. Not in the hands of journalists who'll make a circus of a dead woman's private thoughts." He paused, and the kindness in his voice turned, very gently, into something else. "You're a man who understands that some things are preserved precisely by being kept from the light. Paper rots in the sun, Mr. Oyelaran. It lasts for centuries in the dark. That's not a metaphor. It's simply what you do."

* * *

Dinner was served in a room that could have seated thirty and held two, at one end of a table polished to a black mirror. They ate turbot and then grouse and Greer talked about Marion the whole time, and it was the most frightening part of the evening, because the grief was *better* now, loosened by wine, and Cyrus could study it at leisure. The way Greer's voice softened on her name. The anecdote about her correcting him over a decimal error in 2012—"I let her win the argument, of course; she was always right about the numbers"—delivered with a rueful, self-deprecating smile. The detail about her liking her coffee black, about her walking the river path on Sunday mornings.

Every detail was true. That was what made Cyrus's skin crawl. Greer was not inventing a Marion; he was *curating* one, selecting from the real woman the parts that made a harmless, beloved ghost, and Cyrus understood that this was how the man had done everything else. Not with lies. With selections. A town was just an archive, and Aldous Greer had been its archivist for forty years, deciding what was catalogued and what was quietly deaccessioned.

"Tell me about yourself," Greer said, over the cheese. "Before Blackmere. You arrived eleven years ago—almost to the week of Marion, isn't that a coincidence. Where were you before?"

"Nowhere interesting. A cathedral archive in the north. Before that, university."

"Which university?"

The question was light as a leaf and heavy as a stone. Cyrus reached for his glass and found it empty and did not let the hand shake as he set it down.

"A small one," he said. "You wouldn't know it."

"Try me. I collect small knowledge."

"Durham," Cyrus lied, smoothly, instantly, and hated the smoothness, hated how fast the machine in him produced it, the same machine that had produced that lie once before, years ago, laminated into a credential and never since examined. Greer looked at him for a moment longer than the answer required, and Cyrus understood that the old man did not believe him, and did not care, because a man's old lie was not a problem. It was a *handle*. Everything was a handle. The dinner itself was a handle.

"Durham," Greer repeated, pleased. "A fine city. They know how to keep things there." He refilled the glass himself, waving off the housekeeper. "To the archive, then. To its future."

Cyrus raised the glass. "To its future."

The crystal rang, one small clear note, like something locking.

* * *

The taxi waited in the turning circle, headlights breathing in the cold. Greer walked him to the door, and in the hall, under the brown faces of the portraits, he stopped and said, quietly, without performance now, which was somehow worse:

"You'll find what you're looking for eventually. You're the type; I was the type, once. And when you find it, you'll have a choice, and the choice will look obvious from one side and impossible from the other." He put out his hand. "I'd rather be your patron than your problem, Mr. Oyelaran. Think about which you'd prefer. Most men, when they're honest, prefer the patron."

Cyrus shook the hand. It was dry and exact and it did not let go a half-second longer than a handshake should, and then it did, and the door closed, and the taxi took him down through the beech trees with the envelope in his inside pocket like a coal against his chest.

He did not open it until he was back in his rooms above the cloister, with the door bolted and the diary—his diary now, though he could not yet say so—wrapped in acid-free tissue inside the false bottom of the map chest. The envelope held a cheque. Eighty thousand pounds. Drawn on the Greer Family Trust. In the memo line, in that hired copperplate, a single word: *Preservation.*

He sat for a long time with it on the desk, the lamp making a small island of light in the cold room, and he made himself look at the thing honestly, because he was an archivist and honesty about provenance was the whole of his trade. He had taken the money. He had shaken the hand and drunk the whisky and lied about Durham with a straight face, and tomorrow he would deposit the cheque, and the cheque would sit in his account like a hook sitting in a fish.

But he had not given them the diary. He had not given them the crypt, or the ledger fragments, or the name *T.* He had bought something with their money, and the something was time—days, perhaps a week, before Greer concluded that patronage had failed and moved to the other thing he'd promised to be.

He fed the cheque into the back of a ledger he kept of his own, an old habit, a record of debts, and wrote beneath it the date and one word of his own: *Bought.*

Then, because his hands were not quite steady, he took out the diary and read the last entry again, the one dated three weeks ago, in the hand that was becoming horribly familiar, the hand that looked like his own future:

*The saints know the order. The saints have always known the order. Count them as she counted them and the way opens. He thinks the archive is his. He has never understood that archives keep what men bury.*

Cyrus closed the book. Outside, the cathedral rose against the stars, its carved saints ranked along the south porch, patient, numbered, waiting—and for the first time he understood that Marion had not hidden her cipher in the stones.

She had hidden it in the catalogue. And he was going to need Priya Iyer, and a ladder, and a very long night among the saints.
