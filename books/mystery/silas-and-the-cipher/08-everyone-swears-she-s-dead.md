## 8. Everyone Swears She's Dead

The list had forty-one names on it, and Cyrus Oyelaran had written every one of them in his own careful hand, because a list made in one's own hand feels like a promise to oneself. Forty-one people who might have known Marion Voss well enough to know her friends, and among her friends, perhaps, the person she had trusted with the name *T.*

He had organized them alphabetically, of course. It was the only honest way to approach strangers: in order, without favoritism, so that no name could accuse him later of having wanted a particular answer more than the truth.

He began on a Tuesday morning, when Blackmere's high street still smelled of bread from the ovens behind Halloran's Bakery, and he ended—nine days later, hoarse, humiliated, and certain of something far worse than failure—on a Thursday afternoon in the churchyard, talking to a man who dug graves for a living and who, Cyrus would come to understand, had spent eleven years digging one grave in particular over and over again in his mind.

But that was Thursday. First came the wall.

* * *

The first three names were easy. Margaret Ashcombe, retired schoolmistress, received him in a sitting room that smelled of lavender and dust, poured tea he did not want, and answered every question with a warmth that told him nothing at all.

"Marion Voss? Lovely girl. Quiet. Kept herself to herself, mostly." Mrs. Ashcombe stirred her tea though there was nothing in it to stir. "Such a tragedy. The river, they said."

"The river," Cyrus repeated. "You said 'they said,' Mrs. Ashcombe. Who is *they*?"

She smiled at him the way one smiles at a child who has asked why the sky is blue—as if the answer were obvious and also not worth giving. "Everyone, dear. Everyone always knows everything here. That's rather the point of a town."

"And did everyone know why she left?"

"She didn't leave, dear. She drowned." The smile did not move. "That's what everyone knows."

He wrote it down anyway, because an archivist writes down even lies; especially lies. Lies have provenance too.

* * *

By the fourth day he had developed a taxonomy of refusal, which he recorded in the back of his notebook with the grim satisfaction of a man cataloguing a collection no one else will ever want to see.

There was the **Ashcombe Refusal**: warm, complete, and empty—the answer that contained no information but offered endless hospitality. There was the **Halloran Refusal**, named for the baker, who simply stopped hearing him mid-sentence, his eyes going pleasant and distant as a window fogging over. There was the **Refusal by Appointment**, practiced by Dr. Fenwick at the surgery, who checked his watch twice and explained that he really couldn't discuss former patients, though Cyrus had never mentioned patients, or Marion's health, or anything at all before the doctor interrupted him—which meant, Cyrus noted, that Dr. Fenwick knew exactly what Cyrus had come to ask, which meant someone had called ahead.

That was the detail that turned the exercise from frustration into fear. By the sixth day, Cyrus had stopped introducing himself as a curious archivist and started paying attention to what happened *before* each conversation. At the greengrocer's, the owner glanced at the door twice while they spoke. At the post office, the woman behind the counter gave him his stamps and said, with terrible gentleness, "You seem like a nice young man, Mr. Oyelaran. Blackmere looks after its nice young men." He could not have said whether it was a kindness or a warning, and that was precisely the craftsmanship of it.

Someone was keeping a list too. And his name was on it.

* * *

On the seventh day he tried a different door entirely: the Voss house itself, or what remained of it. Marion's cottage on Chandler's Lane had been sold after her disappearance—bought, the land registry showed, by a holding company whose registered address was a Greer Properties office—and let to tenants ever since. The current tenant, a young couple named Bright, invited him in with the unguarded friendliness of people who had no idea their home was a question mark.

"We found things, actually," the wife said, when he explained—vaguely—that he was researching the property's history. "Under the floorboards in the back bedroom. When we pulled them up for the pipes." She fetched a biscuit tin and opened it with the reverence of people handling relics: a hair ribbon, a bus timetable from eleven years ago, and a photograph of four people on the cathedral steps, squinting into sun.

Cyrus recognized Marion instantly—he had studied her missing-person file until he could have drawn her face from memory—but it was the man beside her who stopped his breath. Tall, fair, half-laughing, with his arm slung around Marion's shoulders in the easy way of old friendship. On the back, in pencil: *M., T., Odile & me — St. Aldhelm's steps, June.*

*T.*

"Tomas," the husband supplied helpfully, following his gaze. "Tomas Ellery. Nice fellow. Worked at the marina, I think, before—" He paused, and Cyrus watched the pleasantness come down over his face like a shopkeeper pulling down a blind. "Before the accident. He died, you know. Years back. Car went off the Millbrook road."

"I hadn't heard," Cyrus said, which was true, and which was also the most useful lie he had ever told.

"Closed casket, I heard," the wife added, then caught her husband's look—a flicker, fast as a fish turning—and laughed lightly. "Well. Terrible business, car accidents."

They saw him out with real warmth. Neither of them looked at the other until the door was closed, and Cyrus, walking away, understood that he had just watched two decent people perform a script they had been handed, and hate themselves slightly for the performance. That was how the wall worked, he realized. It wasn't built of malice. It was built of small mercies, agreed upon in advance, by everyone, about everyone, forever.

* * *

The eighth day produced the strangest interview of all: Miles Greer.

Cyrus had not sought him out. He had gone to the public library to check the newspaper archive for the Ellery obituary, and found the microfilm reader occupied by a tall man in an expensive coat that had been worn past the point of fashion into something like defiance. Miles Greer had his father's height and none of his father's stillness; he moved as if apologizing for the space he took up.

"You're the archivist," he said, without looking up. "The one asking about Marion Voss."

The library was nearly empty. Cyrus weighed denial and found he was tired of it. "I am."

"My father says you're ambitious." Now he looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed, and there was something in them that Cyrus had never seen directed at him in Blackmere before: pity. "He says it like it's a compliment. Be careful what you're ambitious about, Mr. Oyelaran. In this town, ambition is just a word for how far you'll walk toward something before someone shows you the edge of the map."

"Do you know a Tomas Ellery?"

Something crossed Miles's face—grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Everyone swears he's dead," Miles said quietly. "That's the phrase, isn't it? That's what you've been hearing all week. *Everyone swears.*" He closed the microfilm reader with a click. "Ask yourself why a whole town needs to swear the same thing. Swearing is for courtrooms and churches, Mr. Oyelaran. Ordinary facts don't need witnesses. Only buried ones do."

Then he left, coat collar up, and Cyrus sat alone with the machine and fed in the reel for October, eleven years ago, and found the obituary at last.

**ELLERY, Tomas James,** beloved son, taken suddenly. Service private. *No flowers.*

Service private. No flowers. Closed casket, according to the Brights. A body in a box no one was allowed to see, buried—or not buried—in ground no one was allowed to visit, mourned by a town that had all agreed, in the same week, in the same gentle voices, to stop asking questions.

Cyrus sat in the humming dark of the library and felt the horror arrive not as a shock but as a slow cold, the way floodwater finds the seams of a cellar. It was not that Blackmere had lied to him. Lies he could catalogue, cross-reference, expose. It was that Blackmere had *agreed*. Forty-one conversations, and beneath every warm refusal ran the same load-bearing silence, maintained for eleven years by hundreds of people who went to each other's dinners and christenings and funerals and never once, never once, said aloud the thing they all must have suspected. A town does not keep a secret that size by force. It keeps it by love—by the thousand daily acts of loyalty that make complicity feel like community. He had spent nine days knocking on doors, and every door had been held shut by someone's genuine affection for their neighbor.

And the worst of it—the part he would not write down, the part that sat in him like a swallowed stone—was that he understood. He understood it completely. He too had chosen the building over the truth, the routine over the ache, for years. If they had asked him, eleven years ago, to swear Marion Voss was dead in exchange for belonging to this town—to Sunday pews and Christmas markets and being greeted by name—he did not know, honestly did not know, what he would have sworn.

* * *

Thursday. The churchyard.

Wren was where Wren always was at that hour, kneeling by the new plot with a trowel and a patience that seemed geological. He was seventy-something and shaped by his work—shoulders set low and permanent, hands like tree roots—and he had tended St. Aldhelm's grounds longer than Cyrus had been alive. In nine days of interviews, Cyrus had saved him for last, partly out of respect, mostly out of cowardice. The sexton saw everything. The sexton buried everything. If anyone in Blackmere knew what the town had agreed not to know, it was the man who dug its holes.

"Mr. Wren," Cyrus said. "Do you have a moment?"

"For you? Always." Wren didn't rise. He kept his eyes on the earth. "You've been busy, lad. Nine days, forty-odd doors. Old Mrs. Petchey counted."

Cyrus crouched beside him, an act of deference that felt right. "Then you know what I'm asking."

"I know what you're asking." The trowel paused. "I don't know that I know any answers."

"Marion Voss. And a man called Tomas Ellery."

For a long moment the only sound was rooks arguing in the yew. Then Wren set down the trowel, wiped his hands slowly on his knees, and looked at Cyrus with eyes that were old in a way that had nothing to do with age—eyes that had watched something for eleven years and never blinked.

"You're the first one to say his name out loud since the funeral," Wren said. "Eleven years, and you're the first. Do you know what that tells me?" He shook his head, almost gently. "It tells me you're either the bravest man in Blackmere or the biggest fool, and in my experience the two get buried side by side more often than anyone likes to admit."

"Were you at the funeral?"

"I dug the grave." Wren said it flatly, a fact of trade. "Course I was there."

"Was it closed-casket?"

The pause lasted three heartbeats. Cyrus counted them. And in those three heartbeats he watched something happen in the old man's face that he had never seen in nine days of refusals—not the smooth blankness of agreement, not the warm fog of loyalty, but a crack, fine as a hairline fracture in porcelain, running through eleven years of careful masonry.

"Aye," Wren said quietly. "Closed casket."

"Why?"

Another silence. The rooks went quiet, as if they too were waiting. Wren picked up the trowel and looked at it as though he'd forgotten what it was for, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing, pitched for Cyrus alone beneath the yew and the stones and the listening tower of St. Aldhelm's.

"Because, lad," he said, "a closed casket means somebody didn't want anybody seeing what was inside. And in eleven years of keeping this ground, I've learned there's only two kinds of people who feel that way. Them hiding a crime—" the trowel turned over once in his root-hard fingers, "—and them hiding a living man."

He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and gathered his tools, and Cyrus rose with him, heart hammering against his ribs like something trying to get out of a locked drawer.

"Go home, Mr. Oyelaran," Wren said, not unkindly, shouldering his spade. "Lock your door. And whatever you do with that notebook of yours—" he nodded at Cyrus's pocket, "—don't write that down."

But Cyrus already had. He had written it in the place where he kept the things that mattered more than paper: *the witness is alive, and the sexton knows.*

And Wren, walking away across his churchyard between the rows of stones he had set and tended for forty years, stopped once—only once—without turning around, and said to the air:

"If you truly want to find what's left of Tomas Ellery, come back tomorrow. Come at dusk. And come alone."
