## 2. The Hand I Knew

The confession sat on the counter between the register and a stack of unsold paperbacks, and Rafael Galloway could not make themselves touch it again.

Outside, the rain had settled into its afternoon rhythm — the patient, gray drumming that made the bookshop feel like the inside of a bottle. The final notice from the landlord was still propped against the till where they'd left it, face down, as if shame worked by osmosis and could be absorbed through the paper. *Ninety days.* Ninety days to find money that didn't exist, or ninety days to decide which shelf of their life to dismantle first.

But none of that mattered now, because there were two letters on the counter instead of one, and one of them was signed in handwriting Rafael would have known blindfolded, drunk, at the bottom of the lake itself.

*Tomas.*

Rafael had read it four times. Each time, the words rearranged themselves into something worse.

*I killed my sister. On the night of June 14th, 1995, I drowned Marisol Vega in Lake Verity, and for thirty years I have let an accident stand in her place. I am old enough now to be ashamed properly. Come to me before the month is out, Rafael, or don't come at all. But come knowing what I did.*

It was precise in the way lies are precise — every date correct, every detail placed like furniture in a room no one lived in. And it was wrong in the way only love is wrong: too clean, too complete, too eager to be believed. Rafael had spent twenty-two years taking confessions apart for a living. They knew the architecture of guilt, and this wasn't it. This was a confession built like a gift box — designed to be opened, designed to hold something else inside.

The second letter lay beside it, unsigned, written in a different hand entirely — cramped, furious, pressed so hard into the page the pen had nearly torn through.

*You have thirty days to expose the truth about Marisol Vega's death. If you fail, there will be another name in thirty days. You know how these things end, Detective. You've ended them yourself.*

*Detective.* Nobody had called them that in six years. Not since the pension, not since the shop, not since the day Rafael had hung up the badge and discovered that the person underneath it was mostly scar tissue and inventory lists.

They picked up the second letter again. Held it to the window light, the way they used to hold photographs. The ink was blue-black, fountain pen, old-fashioned. The pressure uneven — anger, yes, but also age. A hand that shook between strokes and steadied itself with effort.

And beneath the fury, under the loops and slashes, something familiar. Something Rafael couldn't name yet, the way you can't name a face across a crowded street until it turns toward you.

The bell over the door didn't ring. The phone did.

Rafael flinched hard enough to knock a bookmark off the counter. They stared at the landline — the ancient beige thing they kept because the cell signal died three blocks in every direction — and let it ring four times before answering.

"Galloway Books."

Silence. Then breathing. Then, faintly, water sounds — rain on a roof, maybe, or something else — and a click.

Rafael stood holding the receiver long after the line went dead, listening to the dial tone like it might translate.

"All right," they said aloud, to the empty shop, to the letters, to thirty years of accumulated cowardice. "All right."

They set both letters carefully in the drawer beneath the register — the one that stuck, the one no thief would bother with — locked it, and went to find Tomas Vega.

* * *

The number rang eleven times before the recorded voice told them, in a pleasant automated calm, that it was no longer in service.

Rafael tried it twice more anyway, because grief makes you stupid in predictable ways, and then stood in the back room surrounded by boxes of books nobody wanted, holding a phone that had become a small black tombstone.

Tomas Vega. Sixty-one years old now, if he was alive. Last Rafael knew, he'd been in Portland — a machinist, then a foreman, then, after the layoffs, something vaguer. Christmas cards every year until eight years ago, when they'd stopped coming mid-sentence, the way Tomas did everything: completely, and then all at once gone.

Rafael dialed directory assistance. Nothing. Dialed the union hall in Portland, got a voicemail tree, left a message they immediately regretted — *this is Rafael Galloway, it's about the Vega family* — because if anyone was listening, and someone clearly was, they'd just announced themselves on a stage they hadn't finished building.

They lowered themselves onto the packing crate by the window and let the memory come, because it was going to come anyway, and it was better to choose the door than have it kicked in.

* * *

The Vega house had been yellow. That was the first thing, always the first thing — the yellow house on Delmore Street with the porch swing that sang on humid nights, and Marisol in the doorway with flour on her hands because she was always baking something she'd give away before her own family ate it.

Rafael had been twenty-six, two years on the force, walking a beat that included Delmore Street, and the Vegas had adopted them the way poor neighborhoods adopt lonely cops — gradually, then permanently. Elena Vega fed them until they were ashamed. Arturo Sr. taught them dominoes and cheated magnificently. And the children —

Marisol was nineteen that summer, sharp-tongued and sunlit, saving money for nursing school by working the concession stand at the lake pavilion. She called Rafael *Galloway* like it was a joke with a punchline only she knew. She was going to be the first Vega with a profession. She had a laugh that carried across water.

And Tomas — Tomas was fourteen, all elbows and silence, a boy who read voraciously and spoke rarely, who'd decided early that Rafael was worth trusting and never explained why. Rafael helped him with algebra on the porch swing. Taught him chess badly. Sat with him the summer his father died and said nothing, because Tomas had taught Rafael that silence, done right, was a kind of shelter.

Then June 14th, 1995. The lake. The dock. Marisol, found at dawn by two fishermen, thirty feet from shore, her lungs full of lake water and her shoes still on the dock above her.

Accidental drowning, the coroner said. Slipped, perhaps, in the dark. Alone, certainly — everyone agreed she'd been alone.

Everyone but the family, who knew Marisol didn't go to the dock alone after dark, ever, because she was afraid of the water and always had been. Everyone but fourteen-year-old Tomas, who stopped speaking entirely for five weeks and started again only to say, once, to Rafael, in a voice like a crack in ice:

*She wouldn't have been there. Somebody put her there.*

And everyone but young Officer Galloway, who stood on that dock in the week after the drowning and saw something they would spend thirty years learning to forget.

Rafael had stayed. Through the funeral, through the months after, through Elena's slow collapse into a grief that eventually took her like a tide takes a sandcastle. When Elena died — six years later, quietly, in her sleep, the doctors said, though everyone who loved her knew better — it was Rafael who handled the paperwork, and Rafael who became, without ceremony or declaration, the last standing wall of Tomas's childhood. Seventeen years old and orphaned twice over, and Rafael drove him to school, signed his forms, sat across from him at dinner in the yellow house until the bank took it, and told themselves this was friendship, this was duty, this was what neighbors did.

It was family. They had both known it and neither had ever said it, because saying it would have required explaining why the Vega family needed replacing in the first place, and that explanation began and ended on a dock in June of 1995.

Idris Kane had been there too. Kane had been Rafael's training officer then, twenty years senior, a big deliberate man with a fisherman's patience and a cop's economy of motion. It was Kane who'd caught the case, Kane who'd walked the shore, Kane who'd interviewed the four witnesses and closed the file in nineteen days flat — fast, even then, even for an accidental drowning, though nobody thought to wonder why speed looked like efficiency instead of evasion.

*Kid, sometimes the lake just takes people,* Kane had said, hand heavy on Rafael's shoulder, the night Rafael had come to him — come to him! — with something they'd seen. *Sometimes there's nothing under it. You want there to be a monster because monsters can be caught. Water doesn't leave fingerprints.*

Rafael had been twenty-six and tired and grateful to be let off the hook, and they had let themselves be let off, and that was the whole story, the entire shameful architecture of the next thirty years: a young officer handed an excuse and building a life on top of it.

Kane retired to a fishing lodge upstate twelve years ago. Sent a card every Christmas. *Still fishing,* the cards said. *Still catching nothing. Write back, Rafe.*

Rafael had never written back.

* * *

By evening the rain had thickened and the shop had filled with that blue-gray light that makes every book look like it's mourning. Rafael unlocked the register drawer and laid the confession out flat, and this time forced themselves to do the work — not read it as a wound, but read it as a document.

Paper: heavy stock, cream, expensive. Postmark: local, dated four days ago. Handwriting: Rafael spread it under the desk lamp and studied the letterforms the way they'd once studied ransom notes.

The *M*s broke in the middle, a small hesitation stroke. The *g*s looped low and closed tight. The capital *T* of the signature crossed with a single decisive slash, slightly left-leaning — a left-handed writer, or a right-handed writer imitating slant.

Rafael knew Tomas's hand. Had signed school forms next to it for years, had watched it evolve from careful adolescent print to a swift, confident cursive. The *M*s had broken in the middle then, too. The resemblance was real.

But.

Rafal fetched the Christmas cards from the shoebox in the back room — the last one, eight years old, *Dear Rafe, work is work, the mountains are good, tell the shop hello* — and laid it beside the confession.

The old Tomas wrote fast, loose, generous, his lines drifting uphill across the page like a man walking while thinking. The confession's lines marched straight as ruled paper. Controlled. Practiced. Written slowly, or written many times, or written by someone whose hand had changed — stiffened, the way hands do with age, or illness, or tremor.

Or the way a hand changes when it's copying.

Rafael sat back. The lamp hummed. Rain ticked against the glass like fingers testing a lock.

There was a phrase in the confession that had snagged Rafael on the very first read and hadn't let go since: *I am old enough now to be ashamed properly.* Old enough. Tomas was sixty-one; sixty-one-year-olds don't describe themselves as *old enough now*, not about shame, not in that cadence. That was the phrasing of someone older. Someone for whom sixty-one sounded young. Someone seventy-five, eighty, looking down the slope of their remaining time and wanting, needing, to put something right before the ground closed.

And the second letter — the threat, the deadline, the *you know how these things end* — was written by a different hand again, but Rafael kept hearing something in it. Under the fury. A rhythm. A particular way of ending sentences with a small downward hook, like a signature being practiced.

Someone who wrote letters often. Someone of a generation that still did.

Rafael pulled a sheet of the shop's own stationery across the counter and wrote, in block capitals, the names of everyone who had stood near that case in 1995. Four witnesses. Two fishermen. The coroner. Kane. Themselves. Elena. Tomas.

Then, because the pen was already moving and some part of them had known since morning, they added a fifth category and stared at it:

*Who loved her enough to lie?*

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the street a car hissed through standing water.

Because here was the thing Rafael had turned over all afternoon like a stone in the pocket: the confession was false. Rafael was almost certain of it — certain in the marrow, in the place where twenty-two years of interviews lived. Guilty people confess in fragments, in bargains, in self-excuse. This confession had no self in it at all. It was a coat someone else had sewn and Tomas had agreed to wear.

Which meant Tomas was protecting someone. Which meant the real truth was still underwater. Which meant the second letter-writer — angry, aging, patient enough to wait thirty years and precise enough to count thirty days — knew it too, and had chosen Rafael as the instrument.

*You know how these things end, Detective. You've ended them yourself.*

Rafael looked at that sentence a long time. Then they took out the phone book — the actual paper phone book, the shop's, older than most of its stock — and ran a finger down to *Kane, I.*

A lodge upstate. A number that probably still worked, because Idris Kane was a man who paid his bills and answered his door and never, in forty years, had refused to pick up when Rafael called.

Until now.

Rafael dialed. It rang. It rang with the vast empty ringing of a line connected to a house where nobody was picking up, and on the ninth ring a machine clicked on — Kane's voice, older, slower, but unmistakably him: *You've reached the lodge. Leave it after the tone, and if this is Rafe, I'm fine, the fish are fine, call your mother.*

The machine cut off before Rafael could speak. They sat in the blue dark of the closing shop, listening to the rain, and understood with sudden total clarity that the past had not merely reached through paper today.

It had reached through everything — the postmark, the dead number, the unanswered lodge, the handwriting that was and wasn't Tomas's — and it was not reaching *toward* Rafael.

It was reaching *around* them. Closing.

On the counter, the second letter caught the lamplight, and for the first time Rafael noticed what exhaustion had hidden all day: a faint indentation on the back of the page, writing from a sheet that had lain beneath it, pressed through by a heavy pen.

Three words, backwards, ghostly, legible only if you tilted the page against the light and already suspected what you'd find.

Rafael tilted the page.

And felt thirty years fall away like a dropped coat.
