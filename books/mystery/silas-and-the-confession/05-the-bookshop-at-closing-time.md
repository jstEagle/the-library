## 5. The Bookshop at Closing Time

The rain had followed Rafael home from Odette's, a thin gray insistence that turned the shop windows into running glass and the street outside into a smear of headlights. By four o'clock the light in the store had gone the color of old newsprint, and Rafael had spent most of the afternoon doing what they did best these days: nothing, expertly. Dusting shelves that didn't need it. Straightening the poetry section for the third time. Avoiding the two letters, which sat where they'd sat all week, in the locked drawer of the register, wrapped now in a cloth napkin like something that might bleed.

The final notice from the bank was tucked under the register's cash tray. Ninety days. It felt almost companionable at this point, another piece of correspondence demanding things of them.

At 4:47 — eleven minutes before closing, because the universe had a sense of humor and it was cruel — the bell over the door rang.

Rafael looked up expecting the usual: a lost tourist, a student with a syllabus, old Mrs. Halloran returning the biography she'd sworn she'd finished and hadn't. Instead there was a woman standing just inside the door, shaking rain off a black umbrella with the crisp economy of someone who'd grown up somewhere rain was an event, not a season. Mid-thirties. Tall, close-cropped hair, a travel coat that had seen three continents. She had Marisol Vega's jawline.

Rafael knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a photograph you've studied too long — not the living face but its architecture, rebuilt by memory into something younger and drowned. The recognition landed in their chest like a dropped book.

"Are you still open?" the woman asked. Her accent was transatlantic, unplaceable — Lagos or London or somewhere between, the accent of a childhood assembled in pieces.

"Eleven minutes," Rafael said. Their voice came out steady. They were grateful for that. "What are you looking for?"

"Something specific." She moved down the center aisle, reading spines without really seeing them, and Rafael watched her the way they'd once watched suspects work a room — cataloguing, noting the way her eyes kept coming back to the window, to the street, as if she expected to be followed. "I've been to three shops today. Nobody stocks anything useful."

"Useful how?"

She stopped at the true crime shelf — thin pickings; Rafael ordered it out of some old professional guilt rather than any customer demand. "Lake drownings." She said it flatly, no apology in it. "Forensics, ideally. How bodies behave in cold water. How long before someone floating at dawn was actually in the water. That kind of thing."

The bell over the door might as well have rung again. Rafael's hands, resting on the counter, went very still.

"That's an unusual request," they said.

"I'm told I'm an unusual person." She pulled a volume off the shelf — some remaindered thing about forensic pathology, waterlogged and macabre — and turned it over in her hands. "Most people find it morbid. My aunt found it obscene. *Your mother has been dead thirty years, Lena, let her rest.*" She said the word *thirty* with a small, bitter precision, as if tasting it. "But she wasn't resting. She was filed away. Accidental drowning, case closed, everyone very sorry, everyone very quick. And nobody — *nobody* — has ever been able to tell me why my mother went out on that dock alone at night when she was afraid of deep water."

The shop was very quiet except for the rain. Rafael became aware of their own heartbeat, slow and enormous, and of the drawer beneath the counter where Tomas's confession lay folded in a napkin, six feet from this woman, closer than anyone had been to it in weeks.

"You knew her," Lena said. It wasn't quite a question. She was watching Rafael's face with an attention that felt like being read by a polygraph. "You went still when I said *dock*. You know which dock."

"This is a town on a lake," Rafael said. "Everyone knows the dock."

"Everyone doesn't flinch at it."

*Careful,* said the detective who still lived somewhere in Rafael's chest, thirty years retired and apparently not retired at all. *She's interviewing you.*

"And you are?" Rafael asked, buying time the way they'd once taught younger detectives to buy time — with a question that sounded like procedure.

"Lena Okafor." She set the forensics book on the counter. "My mother was Marisol Vega."

There it was, then. Said aloud, in Rafael's shop, under the flickering lamp over the register. The name Rafael had carried since 1995 like a stone sewn into a coat lining — spoken by the daughter, who had Marisol's cheekbones and a stranger's name and eyes that did not blink enough.

"I'm sorry," Rafael said, and meant it so completely it frightened them. "I knew her. A little. Everyone did. She worked summers at the marina café."

"She waited tables. She wanted to be a nurse." Lena recited it like liturgy learned late, secondhand, in another language. "I got all of it in fragments. I was raised by my father's family in Accra after — after. He couldn't stay in this country. Too much grief, he said. Or too much suspicion, depending on which version of him you got." She opened the forensics book, riffled pages without reading them. "I came back with questions. Everyone here keeps giving me answers instead. Do you understand the difference? Answers end conversations. Questions start them. This town is full of people desperate to end mine."

Rafael understood the difference perfectly. They had spent thirty years ending conversations.

"What kind of questions?" they asked, and heard their own voice do the thing it used to do in interview rooms — gentle, open, lethal.

Lena leaned on the counter. Close now. Rain still beaded in her cropped hair. "Why did the coroner rule accidental drowning when the autopsy notes — I paid a records service, don't look surprised, I can afford surprises — mention bruising on both upper arms consistent with *grip pressure*? Why did every witness that night have an alibi so clean it could've been printed on stationery? Why does my uncle Tomas —" and here her voice caught, actually caught, the first crack in the composure, "— why does my uncle Tomas sell his house, empty his accounts, and vanish three weeks ago, leaving me a voicemail that says only, *Don't trust the paper trail, Lennie. Trust the lake.*"

The clock above the door ticked past five. Neither of them moved to turn the sign.

"Tomas," Rafael said carefully. "He was a boy when his sister died."

"He was fourteen. He raised himself after that, far as I can tell. Raised me too, summers, when Dad sent me back." Something moved across her face — tenderness and anger braided together. "He's the only one who ever talked to me like she was real. Like she laughed and burned rice and sang off-key. Everyone else talks about her like she's a headline." She straightened. "So. You're the last name in her diary I haven't tracked down. *Rafael G., if anything ever happens, R. will know.* That's all it says. No explanation. Just that. What would you know, Mr. Galloway?"

The bell over the door chose that moment to shudder in its fitting as a gust hit the glass, and in the small violence of that sound Rafael made their decision — or rather, the decision made itself, the way it always had, in the half-second before conscious thought arrived.

They lied.

"I was a patrol officer back then," they said. "I took statements. I stood in the rain. I don't remember much more than the rain." They slid the forensics book across the counter and rang it up, though Lena hadn't asked them to. "Cold water changes everything. Time of death becomes a guess dressed as science. If your mother went in late, the bruising could be post-mortem — rocks, pilings, recovery efforts. It happens more than people want to hear."

Lena took the bag slowly. She was looking at Rafael the way Rafael had once looked at witnesses who were telling the truth badly — with patience, and with the certainty that patience would win.

"That's an answer," she said quietly. "Not a question. Thank you for confirming which one you are."

She left a twenty on the counter for a nine-fifty book and didn't wait for change. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, rain behind her like a curtain.

"My uncle trusted you," she said. "*If anything ever happens.* He wrote that when he was nineteen, Mr. Galloway. People write things like that about maybe five other people in a lifetime. Whatever you're carrying —" her eyes went, briefly, precisely, to the register drawer, and Rafael's blood went cold, though she could not possibly have known, could not possibly — "I hope it's lighter than it looks."

Then the bell rang, and she was gone into the rain, and Rafael stood alone in the dying light of the shop with their heart going like a trapped bird.

* * *

They didn't sleep that night. Or rather, they slept the way they'd slept during stakeouts decades ago — in shallow installments, surfacing at every sound, the radiator ticking, a car passing, the building settling. Around two in the morning they gave up, went down to the shop in the dark, and unlocked the register drawer.

The napkin. The letters. Tomas's handwriting, looping and careful, the same hand that had written *thank you for staying* on a card when he was fifteen, that had addressed Rafael's birthday envelopes for twenty years until the envelopes stopped coming. *I did a terrible thing a long time ago. I am the reason my sister went into the water.* And beneath it, unsigned, in a hand Rafael had recognized with a horror they still hadn't fully metabolized: *Thirty days. Expose the truth, or there will be another name.*

Two letters. One family. One girl in the rain tonight asking questions that the letters answered, or seemed to answer, or answered wrongly — Rafael no longer knew. That was the poison of it. Every hour they held the letters, they were choosing. Every hour they withheld them, they were doing exactly what Kane had done in 1995, exactly what the chain of command had done, exactly what the whole rotten machinery of a small town did with grief: filing it, deferring it, deciding on someone else's behalf what the family could bear.

And yet.

Tomas's voice came back to them across three decades — fourteen years old, hollow-eyed on the Vega porch the week after the funeral, gripping Rafael's sleeve with both hands: *Promise me you won't let them take apart our family to find out how she died. Promise me, Rafe. We've lost enough.* And Rafael, twenty-six years old and certain that promises were load-bearing structures, had promised. Had built a life on top of that promise the way you build on a fault line, telling yourself the ground would hold.

Lena Okafor was the ground moving.

Rafael sat behind the counter in the dark until nearly four, the letters in their lap, the rain easing off against the windows. Twice they picked up the phone to call the number Tomas had left in Christmas cards years ago — dead, dead, disconnected, the operator's voice flat as a headstone. Once they drafted, mentally, the opening sentence of a letter to Lena: *Your mother was afraid of deep water, and she was right to be.* They deleted it from themselves before it could become ink.

Because here was the arithmetic they couldn't escape, the equation that had kept them up: if the confession was true — if Tomas had done it, had somehow, impossibly, at fourteen, done it — then giving Lena the truth would hand her a life sentence shaped like her own blood. And if the confession was false — and Rafael's gut, that unreliable, indispensable organ, insisted it was false, insisted Tomas was covering for someone the way he'd covered for Wren's alibi all those years ago — then the letters weren't a confession at all. They were a flare. Someone was signaling from inside a burning building, and Rafael was the only one who could see the fire, and the fire had a deadline.

Thirty days. Twenty-three left now.

At dawn the light came up gray over the lake end of the street, and Rafael locked the letters back in the drawer, and washed their face, and put the kettle on, and admitted the thing they'd been circling all night:

They hadn't hidden the letters from Lena to protect Tomas.

They'd hidden them because the second letter's handwriting had shaken them more than the first — because whoever wrote *another name in thirty days* wrote with a hand Rafael had trusted with their life, and until Rafael understood that, every person who walked through the door asking questions was either a lifeline or a fuse, and Rafael no longer had the skill to tell which.

The bell over the door rang at 9:02.

Rafael looked up, kettle forgotten, and Lena Okafor stood dripping in the doorway — no umbrella this time, hair plastered dark against her skull, breathing hard as if she'd run the last block. In her hand she held a wet envelope, and her eyes had the particular brightness of someone who had not slept either.

"You lied to me," she said. Not angry. Worse than angry. Certain. "Last night you said you barely remembered the rain." She crossed the shop and laid the envelope on the counter between them, square and white and damning. "This was in my mother's diary. A spare page, folded into the back cover, postmarked 1995 and never sent. It's your handwriting, Mr. Galloway. And it says: *Marisol saw something on the dock. I'll handle it.*"

The kettle began, faintly, in the back room, to scream.
