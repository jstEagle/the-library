## 9. Lena's Questions

The bell above the bookshop door had a voice Rafael knew the way they knew their own pulse — a small brass complaint that announced every customer, every draft, every ghost. On Thursday morning it rang, and Rafael looked up from a ledger of losses to find Lena Okafor standing in the doorway with rain on her shoulders and a paperback clutched against her chest like a shield.

She was wearing the same green coat as before. Her hair was damp at the temples. She looked, Rafael thought, like someone who had not slept well and had decided to be furious about it.

"You're open," she said.

"Barely."

"Good." She crossed the shop in six strides and set the paperback on the counter between them. It was a battered copy of *Lakeside: A History of Harrow Bay and Its Waters*, water-stained, spine cracked, price sticker long gone. "I bought this at the estate sale on Corliss Street last weekend. My mother's name is written inside the front cover."

Rafael didn't touch it. "Lots of people owned books about the lake."

"Not lots of people." Lena opened the cover and turned it around so Rafael could see the flyleaf. Marisol Vega's handwriting — round, quick, slanted forward like a woman always about to move — filled the margins of the title page. And beneath it, in the same hand but darker ink, older ink, a single line:

*Ask R. Galloway. He'll know what really happened.*

The rain ticked against the front window. Somewhere in the back room, the old radiator knocked twice and went quiet, as if it too were holding its breath.

"She wrote that in 1994," Lena said. "A year before she died. I've been reading her diaries for three weeks, Mr. Galloway. Do you know what's strange about them? Everything else is ordinary. Groceries. My fevers. The price of flour. She was the most ordinary diarist who ever lived — except for this one line, and except for the fact that the diary stops eight months before she drowned." She looked up. "You'll know what really happened. That's what she thought. So tell me. What did you know?"

Rafael had spent thirty years learning how to keep their face still. It had served them through interrogations, through funerals, through the slow public death of their career. They deployed it now, all of it, and watched it fail completely — because Lena wasn't a suspect or a reporter or a superior officer. She was looking at them the way Tomas used to look at them across the dinner table, waiting for the truth he already deserved.

"I knew your mother," Rafael said finally. "That's the whole of it. She came into this shop when it was new. She liked poetry. Neruda, mostly. She'd read a poem and argue with it like it owed her money."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have that won't hurt you."

Lena laughed — a short, hard sound with no humor in it. "You think I can be hurt? I found out two months ago that the woman who raised me wasn't my mother, that my mother died in a lake in a town I'd never heard of, and that everyone who might have told me chose not to. I have been hurt, Mr. Galloway. I'm past the part where it hurts. Now I'm just the part where I don't stop."

* * *

Rafael made coffee because their hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. The machine in the back room was older than the shop itself, and while it groaned and hissed, Rafael stood with both palms flat on the counter and tried to do arithmetic with the truth — how much could be given away without giving away everything.

The letters were upstairs in a tin box behind the accounting ledgers. Two letters. One confession signed by a dead man's living brother, one threat promising another name in thirty days. Twenty-one days now. If Lena saw them, she would either run to the police — and the police were the reason none of this had ever been solved — or she would run toward the lake, alone, and become the second name.

So: omission. Rafael had built a career on knowing which facts to withhold and calling it procedure. It turned out the skill didn't rust.

They carried two cups into the front and found Lena standing at the poetry shelf, running her finger along the spines. She stopped at the N's.

"He's still here," she said quietly. "Neruda. You kept him."

"Nobody buys poetry anymore."

"You kept him." She took down a slim volume, opened it, and went very still. Tucked inside was a receipt from 1996, and pressed flat beside it a sprig of some dried flower, brown and weightless. "This is hers."

Rafael set the cups down. "I couldn't sort her things after the funeral. It felt like — deciding she was finished. So I didn't. Thirty years, and I never finished sorting."

Lena held the flower in her palm like a bird that might startle. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register — lower, careful, the sharpness set aside like a knife laid on a table rather than dropped. "My father told me she ran off. That's the story I got at seven years old. Then he died, and my aunt told me she drowned, an accident, sad, let it lie. Then I find her diaries, and the diaries say *ask R. Galloway*, and I drive four hours to a dying bookshop in a town that smells like rain and regret, and the man behind the counter tells me she liked poetry." She closed the book gently, so gently. "I'm going to sit down now, and you're going to decide whether you're the kind of person who lets a woman drink bad coffee while lying to her face, or the kind who tells her something true even if it costs you. Those are the options. I've thought about it a lot on the drive over."

* * *

She sat in the reading chair by the window, the one with the torn armrest, and Rafael sat across from her, and for a long moment neither of them drank anything.

"What I can tell you," Rafael said, "is what the record says. Your mother drowned on the night of June 9th, 1995, at the public dock on the east shore. The ruling was accidental drowning. Blood alcohol under the limit. No signs of struggle. Case closed in eleven days."

"And what the record doesn't say?"

Rafael held their cup and watched the steam rise and thought about Odette Brandt's face when she'd slid the file across her kitchen table — *there's a page here that doesn't belong, Rafe, and I think you know why.* A witness statement, unfiled, unsigned by any investigating officer. Rafael had gone home that night and stared at the ceiling until dawn.

"The record doesn't say much of anything," Rafael said. "That's the problem. Eleven days is fast for a drowning case. Fast enough that I've spent thirty years telling myself it was efficiency, and lately telling myself it was something else."

"Lately? What changed lately?"

There it was — the sharpness again, precise as a scalpel. Rafael set down the cup. "I got old," they said. "Old men reread their files. It's a disease."

Lena studied them for a long time. Rain moved across the window glass in slow silver ropes. Outside, a delivery truck ground past, and the whole shop shuddered faintly, the way old buildings do, as if remembering something.

"My mother was afraid of the water," Lena said suddenly. "Did you know that?"

"No."

"She was. There's a diary entry from 1993 — she writes about taking me to a pool when I was small, before we left, and how she wouldn't go in past her knees. She jokes about it. But she jokes about everything in those diaries, and underneath the joke she's terrified. So explain to me how a woman afraid of the water ends up drunk at a dock at night, alone, in June, wading in far enough to drown." Lena leaned forward. "People don't accidentally do the thing they're most afraid of. Not alone. Not at night. Something put her there, Mr. Galloway. Something or someone."

Rafael felt the floor tilt — the same sensation from Odette's kitchen, the ground shifting under thirty years of settled fact. Because Rafael had asked that exact question once, in 1995, in a room with fluorescent lights and two men who had explained, patiently, why the question didn't matter.

"You've done homework," Rafael said.

"I've done nothing else since March." Lena's eyes were wet, but her voice stayed level, and somehow that was worse than tears. "I requested the coroner's report. Did you know you can just — request it? It took nine weeks and forty dollars. Cause of death: asphyxiation due to submersion. Toxicology: 0.06. There's a note about a contusion on her left shin, consistent with striking a dock ladder. Consistent with. That phrase appears eleven times in four pages. Consistent with accident. Consistent with intoxication. Consistent with nothing anyone bothered to disprove." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, efficient about it. "I'm not crazy. Tell me I'm not crazy."

"You're not crazy."

"Then say the rest of it. Say there's something wrong with the case. Say it out loud, in this room, where she used to buy her poems, and then maybe I'll believe I haven't driven my whole life into a wall."

The radiator knocked. The rain fell. Rafael heard their own voice come out quieter than intended.

"There's something wrong with the case."

Lena exhaled — a long, shuddering breath, thirty years compressed into one. She nodded slowly, twice, as if sealing something. "Okay," she said. "Okay. Now we're somewhere."

"We're nowhere near anywhere," Rafael said. "And I need you to hear me on this. Whatever happened in 1995, the people involved are not strangers. Some of them are still here. Some of them are respected. One of them—" Rafael stopped, hearing the shape of the sentence they were about to finish, and rerouted. "One of them has spent thirty years making sure no one asks your questions. If you go asking them loudly, you will not be safe. I mean that literally. I'm not being dramatic. I've seen what this town does with inconvenient women."

"Like my mother."

The words landed in the room and stayed there.

"Yes," Rafael said. "Like your mother."

* * *

They talked for two hours. Or rather, Lena talked and Rafael rationed — a fact here, a confirmation there, each disclosure measured against the letters hidden upstairs like contraband. Rafael told her about the speed of the investigation. About the alibis, four of them, all corroborated — though Rafael said nothing yet about who had corroborated them, because saying that name aloud would lead straight to the cabin, to the wall of research, to Tomas, and Tomas was alive, and alive was a secret that could get a man killed faster than guilt ever could.

Lena told Rafael about herself instead. About Lagos, and the aunt who raised her, and the box of photographs that arrived after her father's death with one picture of a young woman laughing on a porch swing — Marisol, twenty-six, radiant, a stranger wearing Lena's own smile. About how she'd learned Spanish from tapes before she learned the town's name. About how she'd dreamed, as a child, of drowning, over and over, and woken gasping, and been told it was nothing.

"Dreams are nothing," she said. "But I want to know why nobody in this town would look me in the eye yesterday. I walked into the diner and the room went quiet. I asked about the Vega family and the waitress suddenly had orders to fill. Somebody knows something, and everybody knows everybody knows."

"That's Harrow Bay," Rafael said. "It's a town that keeps its secrets the way the lake keeps its dead. Not maliciously. Just — deep, and cold, and unwilling to give anything back."

"But you gave something back. Just now." Lena stood, gathered her mother's book, held it against her chest again. At the door she paused, and the sharpness returned, but gentler now, aimed with care. "You know more than you said today. I've interviewed enough evasive people to recognize the architecture of a partial answer. But you gave me real pieces, and you told me the case was wrong, and you looked sick doing it, which means it costs you something. So I'm going to trust you — provisionally. Conditionally. The way you trust a bridge that creaks." She pulled up her hood. "But I'm coming back, Mr. Galloway. And next time, I want the parts you didn't say."

When the door closed behind her, the bell rang its small brass complaint, and Rafael stood alone in the shop with two cooling cups of coffee and the weight of what hadn't been said pressing down like weather.

Upstairs, the tin box waited. Rafael climbed the stairs, took it down, and read the second letter again — *another name in thirty days* — twenty days now — and then the first, the confession, page after page of Tomas's careful hand describing a night Rafael had lived through and half-remembered and wholly failed to understand.

Except.

Rafael stopped on the third page. A detail. Small, easy to miss — the kind of detail a confessor includes to prove authenticity. *She was wearing her yellow scarf. The one with the blue thread. I remember because it caught on the ladder rail.*

Rafael had never told anyone about the scarf. It appeared nowhere in the file. It appeared nowhere in the coroner's report. It existed in exactly two places in the world: the evidence bag Rafael had personally sealed in June of 1995 — and the memory of the figure Rafael had seen standing at the end of the dock, holding something yellow that fluttered like a struck match in the dark.

Tomas had been fourteen that summer. He had not been at the lake.

Which meant the confession in Rafael's hands had been written by someone who knew what the killer carried — or by the killer themselves.

Rafael sat down slowly on the stairs, the letter trembling in their hands, and outside the rain kept falling on Harrow Bay like it had something left to hide.
