## 13. Nine Days

The rain had stopped by the time Rafael locked the shop, but the gutters still ran loud along Corvane Street, carrying the morning's water toward the lake like the town itself wanted to be rid of it. Rafael stood under the awning with a manila folder tucked inside their coat and watched the postmark on the third letter blur in the damp.

*Twelve days. You were there, Rafael. You know what you saw.*

Twelve days had become eleven overnight. Eleven now, counting today.

The postmark read Halloway Station — a sorting annex upstate, the kind of place that processed mail for a dozen small towns without anyone noticing which one it came from. Rafael had called the annex twice that morning. The first time they'd been transferred to a recording. The second time a woman named Doris had told them, kindly and firmly, that postmark information was not something she could discuss over the phone, and was there anything else, hon?

There was something else. There were nine days of something else.

Rafael walked back inside, flipped the sign to CLOSED even though it wasn't yet noon, and laid the three letters out on the counter beneath the good lamp — the brass one with the green shade that Marisol's husband had once used for reading contracts. Three envelopes. The confession. The threat. And now this third one, the one that had reached into Rafael's chest and closed its hand around everything they'd kept buried for thirty years.

Same paper stock. Rafael had learned enough about paper in thirty years of selling books to know it when they held it: heavy cream laid stock, a faint tooth to it, the kind sold in reams at office supply stores but also — Rafael turned a sheet toward the light — the kind stocked by exactly two shops within fifty miles of Halloway. The stationer on Delmore Street, and the gift shop at the Lakeside Inn.

The Lakeside Inn sat four hundred yards from the dock where Marisol Vega had drowned.

Rafael stood very still for a moment, listening to the gutters, feeling the shape of the coincidence settle over them like wet wool. Then they took out a notebook — an actual paper notebook, because some habits from the job never died — and wrote: *Delmore Stationery. Lakeside Inn gift shop. Ask Odette who delivers to each.*

They were still writing when the bell above the door chimed.

Lena Okafor came in shaking rain off her umbrella, and Rafael's hand moved before their mind did — sliding the notebook shut, sweeping the letters into the drawer beneath the register with a motion so practiced it frightened them. Thirty years ago they had learned to hide evidence smoothly. Apparently the skill kept.

"You're closed," Lena said, looking at the sign, then at Rafael, then at the lamp still burning over the counter.

"Taking inventory."

"At noon on a Tuesday."

"Inventory doesn't keep business hours." Rafael smiled, and hated how easily the smile came, how much it cost. "Looking for anything particular?"

Lena set her umbrella in the stand and didn't answer right away. She was wearing a dark green coat Rafael hadn't seen before, and her hair was pushed back like someone who'd been driving with the window down. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"I found my mother's diary," she said. "The real one. Not the one my grandmother kept for show. It was in a box my aunt sent me last month, and I only just went through it." She paused. "Your name is in it. A lot."

The gutters roared outside. Rafael kept their hands flat on the counter, because if they didn't give them a job they might shake.

"She was generous with names," Rafael said. "Half this town is in Marisol Vega's diary, I'd imagine."

"Not half this town. You." Lena crossed to the counter slowly, the way you'd approach a dog you weren't sure of. "She wrote about you teaching Tomas to swim. She wrote about you bringing books over every Sunday. She wrote—" Lena stopped, and her voice did something complicated. "She wrote that you were the only person who made her feel like she belonged here. That's not a small thing to write about someone. That's not a passing acquaintance."

"No," Rafael said quietly. "It isn't."

"So help me understand why every time I come in here, you look like you're hiding something."

The question landed clean, no anger in it, just precision — and precision was worse. Anger could be deflected. Precision had to be answered or dodged, and Rafael had been dodging for nine days and could feel the dodge itself becoming a wall between them, brick by brick.

"Lena—"

"You got a letter," Lena said. "I know you did. I've seen you reading the same envelope twice. I saw you sweep your desk clear yesterday when I walked in. I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you I grew up with a mother-shaped hole in my life and I have spent eight months learning to investigate things, and I am *good* at it now, and you are bad at hiding things from someone who loved you once through paper."

Rafael looked at her. Marisol's jaw. Marisol's way of standing with her weight forward, ready. The girl had her mother's stubbornness and her father's patience, and together they made a person who would not leave this shop until she had what she came for.

"If I tell you something," Rafael said, "you'll act on it. You'll go somewhere you shouldn't, ask someone you shouldn't, and whoever wrote these letters will know the moment you do. These letters are addressed to *me*, Lena. Whatever game this is, I'm the piece on the board. I won't put you next to me."

"That's not your decision."

"It's the only part of this that is."

Lena stared at them for a long moment. Then she nodded, slow, and Rafael thought — foolishly, hopefully — that she'd accepted it.

"Okay," she said. "Then answer me one thing. Just one. Yes or no." She leaned in. "Do the letters have to do with my mother?"

The truth rose in Rafael's throat like floodwater. They swallowed it.

"No," they lied.

Lena held their eyes for three full seconds. Rafael had interrogated killers who blinked sooner.

"All right," she said softly, and picked up her umbrella. "When you're ready to stop lying to me, Rafe, I'll be at the Lakeview Motel. Room 6. I'm not going anywhere. My mother waited her whole life for people in this town to tell her the truth. I can wait too. But I want you to remember, while you're deciding — she drowned waiting."

The bell chimed behind her. Rafael stood in the empty shop with the lie sitting in their mouth like a stone, and outside, through the wet glass, they watched her cross the street without hurrying, a woman who knew exactly where she was going.

* * *

Odette Brandt's apartment smelled of tea and old carbon paper, and she received Rafael's question about stationery suppliers with the particular delight of a woman who had spent forty years in archives and considered all information a debt to be tracked.

"Lakeside Inn gift shop gets theirs from a wholesaler in Verrone," she said, tapping her temple. "Bulk orders, quarterly. Delmore Stationery buys retail, off the shelf, cash customers mostly." She peered at Rafael over her glasses. "Why? Somebody writing you love letters?"

"Somebody writing me letters."

"Mm." Odette poured tea without asking, which was her way of saying the conversation wasn't over until she decided it was. "You look terrible, Galloway. You looked terrible at Kane's lodge too, and that was before whatever's been eating you since. Sit down. Drink. Tell me about the letters or don't, but sit down either way, because you're swaying."

Rafael sat. The tea was too hot and too sweet, the way Odette had made it since 1995, since the weeks after the drowning when she'd kept Rafael fed and functional through the case that wouldn't close. Rafael drank it and felt the warmth do nothing against the cold underneath.

"Odette. The unfiled witness statement. The one from the Vega file. Who else has access to the archive?"

"Access?" She snorted. "Me, and God, and God hasn't visited lately. Why?"

"Because someone knows things. Things from that night. Things that were never in any report." Rafael turned the cup slowly. "And I need to know if the file's been touched."

Odette set down her own cup. When she spoke again, the archivist's playfulness was gone, replaced by something older and more careful. "The file hasn't left my custody. But Rafael — listen to me. If someone knows details that were never filed, then either they were there..." She let it hang. "...or someone who was there told them. And you've been asking yourself which, haven't you. Every night since the first letter."

Rafael didn't answer. Outside Odette's window, the town lay gray and dripping, the lake invisible beyond the rooftops but present anyway, the way it had been present for thirty years — a silence at the edge of everything.

"There's one more possibility," Odette said gently. "One you haven't let yourself say out loud. That the letter-writer knows what you saw because they know *you*. Because they've known you a long time, and they're not threatening you, Rafael. They're inviting you. There's a difference. Threats push you away." She refilled the cup. "Invitations pull you in."

Rafael walked back to the shop through the gathering dusk, and the whole way, Odette's words turned over like a page refusing to lie flat.

* * *

The break-in happened on the ninth day.

Rafael knew it before opening the door — knew it in the animal way, the way that had never left, the way that reads a street and finds it wrong. The deadbolt was intact. The window latch untouched. But the smell inside was wrong: cold air and disturbed dust and something faintly chemical, like a cleaner used to erase fingerprints from wood.

The front of the shop was perfect. Untouched. Every book squared, every display intact — which was itself a message, Rafael understood as they moved through the dim aisles. Whoever had come had known exactly what they wanted and where it lived.

The back room door stood open six inches.

Rafael pushed it with two fingers and turned on the light.

The filing cabinet hung open like a wound. Boxes dragged off shelves, their contents sifted through with a thoroughness that was almost professional — not ransacked, *searched*. Papers lifted and examined and replaced in slightly wrong stacks, the way a card player cuts a deck to find a mark. The box where Rafael kept the old Vega clippings had been emptied onto the table and reassembled with two articles missing. The photograph of the 1994 town picnic — Rafael, Marisol, Tomas squinting into the sun — was gone from its frame. Only the frame remained, glass wiped clean.

And the letters. All three. The confession, the threat, the third one with its terrible intimate knowledge — gone from the register drawer, which now stood open and empty, its lock showing fine scratches around the keyhole. Picked, not forced. Patient hands.

Rafael stood in the middle of the room for a long time, doing the arithmetic of violation. Someone had been here within the last twelve hours. Someone had known about the drawer — which meant they'd watched the shop, watched Rafael hide the letters, watched long enough to learn the habit. Someone had taken the confession signed by Tomas Vega and the note promising another name, and left everything else, including the till, including the rare shelved upstairs, including the cash box with four hundred dollars sitting in plain sight.

This wasn't theft. This was collection. Someone was gathering the story back into their own hands — and leaving Rafael holding nothing but the memory of it.

Their phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, and Rafael's blood went thin and cold before they even read it, because the timing was not a coincidence, the timing was a *signature*:

**You read fast. Now we both know what you know. Nine days, Rafael. Say it yourself, or I will.**

Rafael lowered the phone. In the doorway behind them, a shadow shifted — and Lena Okafor stood in the broken threshold of the back room, her face white in the bare bulb's light, her eyes going from the emptied frame to the open drawer to Rafael's phone still glowing in their hand.

"Room 6," she said quietly. "You told me to wait. So I waited, and then I followed the only lead I had, which was you." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Somebody took the letters, didn't they. The ones about my mother. The ones you swore had nothing to do with her."

Rafael opened their mouth. Closed it. Thirty years of silence, and it had finally run out of room.

"Lena," they said. "Sit down. There's something I have to tell you — and something I have to show you. And after that, neither of us is going to sleep tonight."
