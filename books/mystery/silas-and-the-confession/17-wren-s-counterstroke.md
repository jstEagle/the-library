## 17. Wren's Counterstroke

The phone rang at 5:47 in the morning, and Rafael Galloway knew before answering that the world had tilted.

Nobody called at 5:47 with good news. Good news waited for business hours. Good news had manners. What came at 5:47 came wet and cold and smelling of lake water.

"Galloway." The voice belonged to a woman Rafael didn't know — Detective Ansel, Halden County Sheriff's Office, the flat professional tone of someone reading from a card. "You're listed as an emergency contact for Idris Kane. I'm sorry to inform you—"

"No." The word came out of Rafael before they could stop it, small and stupid and thirty years too late for all the other things they should have refused.

"—that Mr. Kane was found this morning by a neighbor on Lake Verity. His boat was adrift near the north shore. The medical examiner's preliminary finding is accidental drowning. There was alcohol involved."

Rafael sat down on the edge of the bed, in the gray dark, and listened to the rest of it the way you listen to weather. Kane's heart, possibly. The alcohol, certainly. A man his age, alone on the water at night — the detective's voice was gentle now, almost apologetic, as if she knew how routine it all sounded. *Happens every summer, Mr. Galloway. I'm sorry for your loss.*

They said the right things. They agreed to come in. They hung up and sat in the dark of the apartment above the bookshop, listening to the rain start against the window, and thought: *He told me to stop. He was afraid, and I left him alone with it.*

* * *

The lodge was sealed with yellow tape that flapped in the wind off the lake. Rafael stood outside it for a long time, hands in their coat pockets, watching two deputies carry Kane's boat up onto the trailer. The boat looked smaller than Rafael remembered. Everything about Kane had always been oversized — the laugh, the hands, the appetite for bad coffee and worse jokes — and now he'd been reduced to a boat and a tape line and a detective's card in Rafael's pocket.

Ansel found them there. She was younger than Rafael expected, sharp-eyed, with the particular patience of someone who'd learned that grief makes people stupid and witnesses make people stranger.

"You were close," she said. Not a question.

"Thirty years. We were partners."

"And recently?"

"Recently we argued." Rafael watched her face take that in. "About an old case. The Vega drowning, 1995. I'd been asking questions he didn't want asked."

Ansel didn't write it down, which was its own kind of message. She already had it written down somewhere else.

"Mr. Galloway, I'm going to be straight with you, because I think you'll appreciate it. Kane's blood alcohol was point-one-nine. His liver was a disaster — we found the medical records. The ME is comfortable calling it accidental." She paused, and the wind pushed a strand of hair across her face, and when she tucked it back Rafael saw that her eyes hadn't softened at all. "But the reopened Vega case has your name all over it. You visited Kane eight days ago. Two witnesses saw the argument — it got loud. And three days ago someone filed a complaint naming you as harassing a sitting councilman."

"Aldous Wren."

"I didn't say the name."

"You didn't have to."

She let that sit. Out on the lake, a loon called, that long unraveling cry that had scored every summer of Rafael's life, and Rafael thought: *he died on the same water she did. Thirty years apart. And I'm standing on the shore of both.*

"Am I a suspect?" Rafael asked.

"You're a person of interest. There's a difference, and right now the difference is me." Ansel tucked her notebook away. "I read the old file. All of it, including the parts that don't quite fit. I'm not the first person to notice they don't fit, am I?"

"No," Rafael said. "You're not."

"Then maybe we want the same thing. And maybe you should be careful, because the last detective who wanted the same thing is in that boat." She said it without drama, without accusation — a fact laid on the table like a knife. Then she walked back through the tape line and left Rafael alone with the loon and the water and the growing certainty that Kane hadn't drowned by accident, and that Rafael's own grief was about to be used as a weapon against them.

* * *

It happened faster than Rafael thought possible.

By evening, the Halden Courier's website carried the story: *REOPENED 1995 DROWNING CASE CLAIMS SECOND VICTIM — RETIRED DETECTIVE QUESTIONED.* The article was careful, legally bulletproof, and devastating in the way careful things are. It named Rafael. It named the argument at Kane's lodge — sourced to "neighbors." It noted, in the fourth paragraph, that Rafael Galloway had "recently made unsubstantiated allegations against Councilman Aldous Wren," and in the fifth that Wren had "declined to comment beyond expressing sorrow for the Vega family's renewed pain and hope that the truth, whatever it is, finally comes to light."

*Whatever it is.* Rafael read that phrase four times. It was beautiful. It was a man standing in front of a crowd with his hands open, saying *I have nothing to hide* so loudly that no one heard him saying *and neither will you.*

Lena arrived at the bookshop at closing time, rain still dripping off her jacket, her face pale and set. She'd seen the article. Of course she'd seen it. She put her phone on the counter between them, the article glowing on it like something diseased.

"They're building a case against you," she said.

"They're building a story. The case is optional."

"You don't understand how it works now. I do — I've spent two years watching how it works in the city. You don't need to be guilty. You need to be *legible*. A grieving ex-cop with a grudge, an old man who drank himself into a lake, a councilman who's sad about it all. That's a story people can hold. That's a story that closes." She was shaking, Rafael realized. Not with fear — with the specific fury of someone who had lost a mother to a story just like it. *Accidental drowning. Case closed. So sorry for your loss.*

"The letters," Rafael said quietly. "The break-in. Someone took them."

"I know. I've been thinking about that. Whoever broke in knew what to look for — the back room, the specific drawer. That's not a burglar. That's someone who's been reading over our shoulders." Lena pulled out a chair and sat, uninvited, the way family does. "Which means Wren knows about Ruth. Or he knows about the letters, at least. Which means Ruth isn't safe."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Ruth Ibarra, ninety-one years old, in a care home on the east side, lucid and failing and dying on her own schedule — unless someone else's schedule intervened.

"She's fine," Rafael said. "I called this morning. She's—"

"When? Before the article or after?"

Rafael opened their mouth. Closed it. The article had gone up at eleven a.m. Rafael had called at eight.

"After," Lena said. "Call again."

Rafel called. The care home's evening nurse answered on the fourth ring, and her voice had that particular careful brightness that staff use when something has gone wrong and the families haven't all been told.

"Mrs. Ibarra? She's — yes, she's resting. There was a visitor this afternoon, a family friend, and she's been a little agitated since. Nothing to worry about. Is this family?"

"I'm a friend," Rafael said. "What was the visitor's name?"

A pause, the sound of pages. "It just says 'associate of the family.' No name logged. I'm sorry, is there a problem?"

"No," Rafael said, and heard themselves lie smoothly, the old detective's muscle memory taking over. "No problem at all. Thank you."

They hung up and looked at Lena, and Lena looked back, and neither of them said *Wren* because they didn't have to. The word was already in the room, sitting down, taking off its coat.

* * *

The person-of-interest interview was scheduled for Thursday, which gave Wren's machinery two days to work, and it worked beautifully. Rafael watched it the way you watch a tide come in — nothing you can point to, everything rising.

The county clerk "discovered" that the bookshop's lease had a zoning irregularity. The bank called about the business loan, suddenly attentive, suddenly concerned. A former colleague from Rafael's detective years gave a quote to the Courier: *Rafe was a good cop once, but he's been off the job a long time, and he's had some losses. I hope everyone takes that into account.* Losses. As in judgment. As in grief. As in *unstable.*

"They're not trying to convict you," Lena said, spreading the clippings across the back-room table like tarot cards. "They're trying to make you unbelievable in advance. So when the evidence comes out — Faulk, the letters, whatever you've got — it arrives pre-poisoned. A bitter ex-cop with a financial motive and a dead friend he argued with. You're not a witness anymore. You're a theory."

Rafel stood at the window, watching the rain write its endless illegible draft on the glass. Thirty years ago, they had learned that evidence could be buried. They were learning now that a person could be buried the same way — not in the ground, but in the narrative, under so much reasonable doubt that no one dug.

"Then we stop being the story," Rafael said. "We become the source."

"What?"

"Thursday, they'll take my statement. I'll give it. All of it — the altered report, the suppressed witness statement, what I saw on the dock. On the record, with a stenographer, in a room I can describe." Rafael turned from the window. "You can't bury a person who insists on being specific. Specific is the one thing they can't spin."

"And if they arrest you anyway? If Kane's case goes from accident to homicide and you're the one holding the shovel?"

Rafael thought about Kane's boat on the trailer, small and empty. Thought about the loon crying over the water. Thought about a detail from the second letter — *You were there, Rafael. You know what you saw* — and about how long they had carried that seeing like a stone in the chest, and how the stone had finally started to move, and how the only thing worse than confessing was dying with it still inside.

"Then I'll answer their questions," Rafael said, "the same way I'm finally answering everyone else's. Completely."

Lena studied them for a long moment. Whatever she saw — and she had gotten very good, these past weeks, at reading the archaeology of Rafael's face — made her nod slowly.

"Okay," she said. "But we do one thing first. Tonight. Before Thursday."

"What thing?"

She slid her phone across the table. On the screen was a photograph, grainy, taken through a windshield at a distance: a car Rafael didn't recognize, parked across from the care home, engine off, a man in the driver's seat who was not reading, not eating, not waiting for anyone a normal person waits for.

"I've been watching the home since the call," Lena said. "That car's been there three nights. And Rafael — Ruth's window is the second one on the left. And last night, around two a.m., someone stood under it for eleven minutes. I timed it."

Rafel looked at the photograph, and the cold that moved through them wasn't fear exactly. It was older than fear. It was the 1995 feeling, the dock feeling, the feeling of watching something happen that you were going to be unable to stop.

"He's not going to kill her," Rafael said slowly. "She's dying anyway. A death in a care home is a gift to him — no investigation, no questions. He doesn't need her dead. He needs her *silent*."

"Which he can get other ways. Medication. A 'confused elderly woman's' statement that she never wrote any letters. Or—" Lena stopped.

"Or a guardianship petition," Rafael finished. "A concerned community member, petitioning the court: the poor woman lacks capacity, she's being manipulated by an obsessed ex-detective. One hearing. One friendly judge. And Ruth Ibarra becomes a non-person — everything she says after that is the rambling of the incompetent, inadmissible, dismissed." Rafael heard their own voice go flat and cold, the old cop's voice, the one that had learned thirty years ago how power actually worked in Halden County. "He's not building a defense. He's building a machine that turns anything she says into noise."

The rain kept on. Somewhere below, a car passed, headlights sweeping the ceiling, and for half a second the room went white and then dark again, like a lighthouse, like a warning.

"Thursday," Lena said. "Your statement. But Rafael — if we go in there and give them everything, and Wren's machine is already running, what happens to Ruth between now and the hearing? What happens to Tomas, wherever he's hiding? What happens to us?"

Rafael picked up the photograph of the car, the man, the window with the light on behind the glass — a small yellow square of it, someone's whole life burning quietly in the dark — and felt the deadline letter's promise settle over the room like the lake fog coming in.

*Another name in thirty days.*

Kane had been the first name. Rafael had assumed the letters meant a second murder.

Standing there, watching the light in the old woman's window, Rafael understood, finally, that the promise had never been about killing at all. It was about silence. And Wren was keeping his deadline perfectly.

"We get to her first," Rafael said. "Tonight. Before his machine finishes building itself."

Lena was already pulling on her jacket. And outside, the rain went on falling on the bookshop, on the lake, on the yellow tape still flapping around an empty boat — falling on everything, indifferent, like a truth no one had agreed to hear.
