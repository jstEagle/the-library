## 19. Faulk Turns

The marina at Halloway Point smelled of diesel and dead minnows, and Denny Faulk's boat sat at the end of the pier like a confession nobody had gotten around to reading. *Miss Behavin'*, the transom said, in letters that had once been gold and were now the color of old teeth. Rafael stood at the top of the ramp for a long moment, hands in their coat pockets, feeling the weight of the recorder against their sternum like a second heart.

Lena had wanted to come. Rafael had told her no — not because they didn't trust her, but because what they were about to do required a man to feel cornered, and cornered men did not talk when there was a witness in the room. Especially not a witness with Marisol Vega's jaw and her way of looking through you.

The morning fog hadn't burned off. It hung over the water in gray rags, and somewhere out on the lake a loon called, that sound that always seemed less like a bird than a memory of one.

Faulk was on his deck, hosing down the cockpit. He was sixty now, thick through the shoulders in the way of men who'd spent decades hauling lines and lies alike, with a sun-cured face and a mustache that had gone from brown to iron without passing through gray. He saw Rafael coming and didn't stop hosing. That was its own answer.

"Marina's private," he said.

"It is." Rafael stopped at the rail of the next slip over, close enough to be heard, far enough not to crowd. "I'm not here about the marina."

"I figured." He shut off the hose. Water dripped from the gunwale into the black water below, each drop a small silver coin sinking. "Kane's dead, Wren's got the whole town singing his praises, and you're walking the docks at seven in the morning. I can do arithmetic, Galloway."

"Then do this sum," Rafael said. "Wren killed Kane."

Faulk's hand paused on the coiled hose. Just a hitch, half a second, the same tell Rafael had watched flicker across Aldous Wren's face at the gala when the word *lake* crossed the air between them. Men who worked for Wren learned the same stillness. It was almost touching, how loyal the training was.

"That's a hell of a thing to say," Faulk said.

"It's a hell of a thing to have done." Rafael stepped onto Faulk's deck before he could object, crouching near the transom bench but not sitting — staying mobile, staying level. "State attorney has Kane's autopsy flagged. The ruling came down in eleven hours, Denny. Eleven hours for an accidental drowning of a retired detective on a calm night in his own boat. You know how fast that is? I closed cases slower than that, and I was good."

"Wren's got friends—"

"Wren *had* friends. Friends are only useful until they're liabilities." Rafael let that sit. Out on the water, the loon called again, closer. "You were his alibi in 1995. You've been his alibi ever since. And three days ago you met him on the dock where Marisol Vega died, and he talked, and you recorded it or he thinks you might have, and either way you've been sleeping badly since. Am I warm?"

Faulk set the hose down. Very carefully, the way a man sets down something he'd rather throw. "You don't know what you're walking into."

"I know exactly what I'm walking into. I walked into it thirty years ago and I've been living in it since." Rafael pulled out the photograph — the dock, 1995, the police tape, and at the edge of frame, blurred, a second figure in a pale jacket. Their own buried testimony made flesh. "This is me. This is what I saw. This is what got cut out of my report by people above me, people whose chain ran through Aldous Wren's campaign donors. I have spent thirty years being the man who stayed quiet, Denny. I am telling you as a professional: it does not get lighter. It gets heavier. Every year it weighs more."

Faulk looked at the photograph for a long time. When he looked up, his eyes were wet, which surprised Rafael — not the tears themselves but how fast they came, like a dam that had been holding for so long it had forgotten how to hold anything else.

"You think I don't know heavy?" His voice cracked on it. "You want to know what I did? You want the inventory?"

* * *

He told it standing up, facing the water, because he couldn't say it to a face. Rafael sat on the cooler and listened and let the recorder run and did not interrupt, because after thirty years of silence they knew better than anyone what interruption cost.

"I was twenty-six," Faulk said. "Working security for Wren's development company, nights mostly, weekends at his parties keeping drunks off the boats. He paid triple what the county did. My mom was sick. There was a house payment. You know the speech — everybody knows the speech, that's what makes it work, everybody's got one.

"The night Marisol died, I wasn't even supposed to be there. Wren called me at nine, said come down to the lake house, said there'd been an accident. I got there and she was already in the boathouse, under the tarp, and Wren was sitting on the dock with his shoes off like he was going swimming, and he was crying, actually crying, and part of me thought — okay, accident, drunk guy, girl hit her head, terrible thing. That's the story he gave me and I took it, because taking it was easier and because he was crying.

"He said the kids had been drinking down by the water. Tomas, Marisol, a couple others. Said there'd been a scuffle, said Marisol slipped. Said if it came out he'd been there — a grown man, drinking, alone with a teenage girl — it would ruin him before he'd even started. He said his wife was pregnant. He said *please*, Denny. First time he'd ever said please to me.

"So I helped him move her. Into the water, off the end of the dock. We made it look like she went in swimming. And then I did the alibis — that was my real job, that was what he kept me for all these years. I was the corroborator. I put myself at four places that night vouching for four people, including Wren, including Tomas Vega, who I swear to God I believed was guilty for about six years and then just — stopped asking. Stopping asking is a skill. I got good at it."

"And the truth?" Rafael asked quietly. "When did you learn the truth?"

Faulk's shoulders dropped. "Ten years ago. Wren was drunk at his fiftieth, sentimental, and he put his arm around me and said, 'You know what happened out there, don't you, Den?' And he told me. She wasn't slipping. She was running. She'd found out about the land deal — the eminent domain scam he ran through the council in '94, the one that gutted the east shore. Her family's property was in it. She'd confronted him at the lake house and he panicked and pushed her and she went off the dock and hit the ladder bracket on the way down." He swallowed. "And then he cried again. Same crying. I realized the crying was the most honest thing about him. He cries about everything and it means nothing."

The fog was lifting off the lake in slow sheets. Faulk finally turned around, and his face was wrecked and relieved at once, like a man setting down luggage he'd carried so long his arms had grown around it.

"Kane called me two weeks ago," he said. "Old man, dying of boredom at that fishing lodge, and he calls me and says, 'Denny, somebody's pulling the Vega file.' Then Kane turns up drowned, ruled accidental before his coffee's cold, and I know — I *know* — Wren did it, and I know I'm next, because I'm the last living piece of the alibi that isn't him." He laughed, a horrible small sound. "You know what I did yesterday? I updated my will. That's where I am."

Rafael stood. "Then here's the offer. The one thing Wren can't give you."

"Away out of state? A lawyer?"

"Immunity. Cooperating-witness status. Testimony on the record, protected, before Wren's people can reach you." Rafael held up the recorder. "This conversation, your statement, everything you give the state attorney today — you go in first, Denny. First man through the door gets the deal. Wren can offer you money and silence and eventually a boat accident of your own. I'm offering you the only thing he never has: a version of your life where this ends."

Faulk stared at them. "They'll bury it. They buried it before."

"They buried it before because it was one scared sergeant's report against a machine." Rafael thought of Ruth Ibarra in her care-home bed, refusing to name what Rafael saw. *You have to say it yourself.* "Now it's a machine turning on itself. Faulk's testimony, my testimony, the reopened file, a journalist holding copies of everything — it can't be buried twice. But it needs you. Justice doesn't come clean, Denny. It comes assembled out of compromised pieces, and right now you're the piece that makes the whole thing hold."

* * *

He went back to his truck — a dented F-150 with a tackle box in the bed and, Rafael would later learn, a fireproof document box bolted under the rear seat, because Denny Faulk had been preparing for this day for ten years whether he admitted it or not.

He came back carrying a rubber-banded bundle of envelopes, soft with age, and dropped them into Rafael's hands like something hot.

"Insurance," he said. "Wren wrote to me. Ninety-four, ninety-five, ninety-six — before he got careful, before everything went through burners and intermediaries. He was young and arrogant and he liked the sound of his own pen. Dates, instructions, the land deal, the cover-up. He references 'the girl problem' twice. In his own handwriting." Faulk's jaw worked. "I told myself I kept them to protect myself. Truth is I kept them because some part of me was waiting for somebody to come ask. Thirty years, Galloway. What took you so long?"

Rafael weighed the bundle. Through the top envelope, in a slanted, confident hand — the same hand Rafael had seen signing legislation photographs in the *Gazette* — the salutation was visible: *Den —*

"There's one more thing," Faulk said. He was looking past Rafael now, at the water, at the gray line where the fog was finally tearing open. "The night we moved her. Off the end of the dock. There was someone watching from the shore. Kid, maybe. Pale jacket. Wren never saw them. I did." His eyes came up and met Rafael's, and Rafael felt the ground tilt the way it had tilted in Odette Brandt's archive, the way it kept tilting, thirty years late. "That was you, wasn't it? That's why you're really here. Not for Wren. For the report they cut."

Rafael closed their hand around the letters. "Get in the truck, Denny. We're going downtown before Wren hears you're breathing."

Faulk nodded — and then froze, his gaze fixing on something behind Rafael's shoulder, at the top of the ramp, where a black sedan had just pulled in and stopped, engine idling, windows dark.

"That's Wren's driver," Faulk whispered.
