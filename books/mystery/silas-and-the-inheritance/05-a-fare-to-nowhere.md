## 5. A Fare to Nowhere

The call came in at 11:47 on a Tuesday night, and Edmund Galloway knew the voice before he knew the words.

"Marlow Bend Taxi. This is Edmund."

A pause on the line—the particular pause of a woman deciding how much of herself to spend on a sentence. Then: "I need a cab tomorrow night. Nine o'clock. The Starlite Motel, out past the quarry road."

Edmund's pen hovered over the log book. He wrote addresses for a living; he had written ten thousand of them into that ledger, and he could not remember the last time his hand had shaken over one.

"Destination?" he asked.

"The crossroads at Miller's Bend. Where Route 9 meets the old county spur."

He wrote it down because his hand was already moving, already committing the crime. *Starlite Motel to Miller's Bend crossroads.* And beneath it, in the column where he logged account names: *Vera.*

Vera. Truth, in the old language. Someone had a sense of humor, or a death wish, or both.

"I'll send a driver," he said.

"No." The word came flat and immediate, a door closing. "You drive me yourself. I asked for you specifically."

"We have six drivers—"

"And I asked for the dispatcher." A breath, static-soft. "Nine o'clock. Don't be late, Mr. Galloway."

The line went dead before he could ask how she knew his name. But of course he knew why she hadn't waited. She'd said everything she intended to say, and every extra second on an open line was a second someone else might hear it. He recognized the discipline of it. He practiced a version of it himself, had practiced it for fifteen years—say less, hold still, be no one.

He sat looking at the entry in the log book. The Starlite Motel had been closed since 2011, its neon sign lying face-down in the weeds behind a chain-link fence like a man who'd been knocked out and never helped up. Nobody booked a cab from the Starlite unless they were already hiding there.

And Miller's Bend crossroads—he knew that address the way you know a scar. It was in his logs, fifteen years deep, in the entries he'd been paid to lose. The night Ruth Hollis disappeared, three calls had come through this dispatch box, and all three had been routed to a cab heading north on Route 9 toward the county spur, toward the crossroads where a woman could stand under one flickering light and wait for a car that would take her anywhere but Marlow Bend.

Edmund closed the ledger. His hands were steady now, which frightened him more than shaking would have. Fifteen years of practice at being steady.

*Don't go,* said the part of him that had kept him alive this long. *Send Ruiz. Send anyone. Log it, forget it, file it under the things that never happened.*

But the dead man at the reservoir had been wearing Edmund's coat, and in the pocket had been a note naming a witness who was supposed to be at the bottom of the reservoir herself. The past had already reached up through the water and taken hold of his ankle. He could either walk toward it or be dragged.

At 8:52 the next evening, Edmund pulled the cab into the Starlite lot. Room 14's curtain moved—a small twitch, the kind of movement designed to be invisible to anyone not looking for it—and then went still.

He got out. Leaned against the fender. Waited.

At 9:00 exactly, the door of room 14 opened, and a woman came out carrying a single canvas bag, and Edmund Galloway felt the world tilt on its axis like a cab taking a corner too fast.

* * *

She had planned this the way she planned everything now: in fragments, in advance, leaving nothing whole enough to follow.

Ruth Hollis—who had been Ruth Delacroix once, then Ruth Hollis, then Marianne Webb in Ohio, then a dozen names written in pencil—had watched the Starlite parking lot for two days before making the call. She'd watched Edmund Galloway too, though he didn't know it. She knew his schedule better than he did. She knew which nights he drank one beer instead of two, which mornings he bought the paper, which route he walked home along the canal with his collar up and his head down, a man practicing invisibility so long he'd gotten good at it.

She'd seen the news, of course. Small towns print their dead on cheap paper, but they print them. *Body recovered from Ashcombe Reservoir.* And then, days later, nothing—which was itself a message. When a stranger drowns wearing a local man's coat and the story dies quietly inside a week, somebody with money has decided the story should die.

She'd known the investigator's name. Marcus Bell. He'd found her twice before—once in 2014, once in 2019—and both times she'd made him look her in the eye and explain why finding her would get her killed, and both times he'd driven away heavy-hearted and left her alone. He was a decent man. Decent men were dangerous, in their way. They kept coming back.

So when the third letter came—no signature, just a page torn from a legal pad saying *They know. Bell is missing. Move or be moved*—she'd understood that the clock she'd been outrunning for fifteen years had finally caught up.

She needed a driver. Not just any driver. The town had eyes everywhere, and most of them belonged to Charles Ashcombe in one way or another—on his payroll, in his debt, or simply afraid of him in the boneless way small-town people learn to be afraid. But there was one man in Marlow Bend who had helped her disappear once already, whether he admitted it or not. Three calls routed through his dispatch box on the worst night of her life, each one a thread in the net that caught her when she fell. Somebody had arranged that. Somebody had been paid to arrange it, probably—but whoever it was had done it, and done it cleanly, and never once in fifteen years given her up.

That kind of silence wasn't loyalty. She knew that. It was probably cowardice, or complicity, or both. But it was the only currency of trust she had left, and she meant to spend it.

She came out of room 14 at nine o'clock precisely, bag over her shoulder, hair cut short and gray at the temples where she'd bleached the color out years ago. She did not look at the driver as she approached. She looked at the lot, the fence line, the dark mouth of the quarry road.

"Miller's Bend," she said, opening the rear door. "Crossroads."

"Yes, ma'am." His voice. God, his voice—fifteen years older but unchanged underneath, the same careful flatness, a voice built like a locked drawer.

She slid in. Pulled the door shut. And there in the dome light's weak yellow circle she saw him turn around to confirm the fare, and their eyes met in the mirror, and Ruth Hollis watched Edmund Galloway's face do something it clearly had not done in years: come apart.

* * *

Edmund had prepared himself for a stranger. He had prepared himself for a trap, for Ashcombe's men, for a gun. He had prepared himself for anything except the specific arithmetic of her face—the face from the taxi window in 2009, twenty-six years old and bleeding at the hairline, pressed to the glass as the cab carried her away from the only town that wanted her dead—translated forward fifteen years. The same deep-set eyes, now ringed with sleeplessness. The same stubborn jaw, softened by time into something almost gentle. Gray in her hair. Lines around her mouth that looked carved rather than grown.

"Ruth," he said. Just the name. It came out of him like something expelled.

"Drive," she said.

He drove. Out of the Starlite lot, left onto the quarry road, the headlights carving a tunnel through the dark. His hands were on the wheel and his heart was somewhere up around his teeth.

"You're supposed to be dead," he managed.

"So are you, sort of," she said. "Look at you. Fifteen years and nobody in this town can pick your face out of a lineup. That's a kind of dying, Mr. Galloway. I'd know. I invented it."

"You know my name."

"I've always known your name." She shifted in the seat; he heard the canvas bag rustle. "You think I got out of Marlow Bend by accident? Three calls came through dispatch that night. Three drivers got rerouted to cover for a woman who officially didn't exist anymore. That doesn't happen by itself. Somebody upstairs made it happen, and then never talked about it. For fifteen years. Do you understand what that's worth to me? What it cost you?"

Edmund said nothing. The road unspooled ahead of them, black and empty, the reservoir glinting off to the right like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"There's a man dead in your coat," Ruth said quietly. "Marcus Bell. His name was Marcus Bell. He spent eleven years trying to find me—not to hurt me. To get my statement on record before the trust settles this winter and every document connected to the Hollis case gets sealed forever. He was the last person alive who thought the truth was worth more than the money." Her voice didn't break, but it bent, the way metal bends before it snaps. "And they put him in the water wearing your coat to make sure that when the body surfaced, it surfaced pointing at you."

"I know," Edmund said. "There was a note in the pocket."

"Then you know what I know." She leaned forward; he could feel her eyes on the back of his neck. "Charles Ashcombe is going to kill me, Edmund. And then he's going to kill you. And then the trust settles, and the boy who killed Danny Ferro gets his inheritance, and everyone who ever whispered about it gets to keep whispering until we're all dead and it never happened."

The crossroads rose up ahead—the old county spur branching off Route 9, the single sodium light overhead buzzing its lonely buzz, moths spiraling around it like debris in orbit. Edmund slowed the cab. Stopped it. Put it in park, and did not move.

"Forget me again," Ruth said. "That's what you're going to say. Or what I'm supposed to tell you. Forget me again, Vera isn't real, the woman in your cab was nobody, and maybe you live another fifteen years." She opened the door. Cold air poured in. "But here's what I learned out there, dispatcher. You don't live those years. You just serve them. One after another, like sentences."

She stepped out onto the gravel. Stood in the cone of the sodium light, a woman with a canvas bag and nowhere in the world to be.

"A car's coming," she said. "North. It'll take me to the state line, and then I start over again with a new name, and in five years someone finds me again, and in seven years someone kills me. Unless—" She stopped. Looked back at him through the open door, and her eyes in the yellow light were the exact eyes from the window of a cab fifteen years ago. "Unless the dispatcher decides to stop losing phone calls and start making them."

Down the county spur, far off, headlights swung onto the road.

Edmund looked at them. Then at her. Then at the ledger on the front seat, fifteen years of other people's secrets alphabetized and safe.

The approaching car sped up.

*It wasn't the pickup,* he thought, with a cold that started in his spine and spread outward. *The car Ruth was expecting wouldn't speed up.*
