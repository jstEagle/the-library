## 7. The Intermediary

The name came to Edmund the way names always did in Marlow Bend—sideways, through the static of other people's conversations. He was restocking the coffee machine at the depot at six in the morning, dead shift, when two county road workers came in arguing about the fire that had taken the old Hollis place years back, and one of them said, offhand, that the only man who'd ever made money off that whole mess was old Pell, the lawyer who used to keep an office above the pharmacy before his hands started shaking.

Pell. The name landed in Edmund's chest like a coin dropped down a well. He'd spent fifteen years not saying it aloud, not even in the privacy of his own skull, because a name spoken is a door opened, and Edmund Galloway had built his entire life on keeping doors shut. But there it was now, dragged up by two men who didn't know what they were carrying: *Aldous Pell*, attorney at law, retired, dying—everyone said dying, though no one could agree on what of—and living out his last stretch in the gray clapboard house on Delaney Road with its gutters full of leaves and its windows dark by eight o'clock.

Edmund had only met him twice. Both times at night. Both times in the depot parking lot, when the lot lights buzzed like trapped flies and a silver sedan idled at the edge of the frame with its parking lights on, patient as a hearse. The first time, Pell had handed him an envelope through the window of Edmund's cab—cash, banded, smelling faintly of another man's cologne—and had said, in a voice like dry paper folding, "You were never here, and neither was I." The second time, three months later, same envelope, same words, and then never again. Fifteen years of silence purchased in two transactions. Edmund had told himself it was easy money for easy work. He had told himself a great many things.

Now a man was dead at the reservoir wearing Edmund's coat, and a note in his pocket named a witness who was supposed to be drowned, and every road Edmund might take away from all of it ran straight through the house on Delaney Road.

He waited until dark. Of course he waited until dark. Fifteen years of night shifts teach a man to move the way water moves—only where nothing resists him.

* * *

Delaney Road was four blocks of houses that had stopped trying sometime in the nineties, porches sagging into their own shadows, hedges gone feral. Pell's house sat at the end like the last tooth in a bad jaw. One window glowed amber behind a curtain—the kitchen, probably, where old men sit because sitting anywhere else requires stairs. A sedan Edmund didn't recognize was parked at the curb, clean, recent, the kind of car that doesn't belong on a street like this. He noted it the way he noted everything: automatically, without letting himself feel what the noting meant. Someone else had come to see the dying man today. Or was coming. Or was watching.

He went around the back, past a birdbath filled with black rainwater, and knocked on the kitchen door—three soft raps, then two, a rhythm from a life he'd buried. Through the curtain's gap he watched the shape inside go very still. Then the shape moved, slowly, the way bodies move when they've stopped believing speed will save them, and the door opened on a chain.

"Dispatcher," Pell said. Not a question. His voice had gone thin as onion skin, but the eyes behind it were still a lawyer's eyes—weighing, filing, pricing everything they saw. "I wondered which way you'd run. Toward or away."

"Open the door, Mr. Pell."

"Mr. Pell." A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be coughed up in pieces. "Fifteen years and you still give me the title. That's either respect or a threat." The chain rattled loose.

Inside, the kitchen smelled of camphor and burnt coffee and something underneath both—something sweetish and wrong that Edmund recognized from the depot, from the time a driver's brother had come in to wait for him during his last week of cancer. The smell of a body eating itself alive. Pell wore a cardigan over pajamas and moved with a walker's careful geometry, one hand trailing the counter. On the table: a glass of water, a spread of pill bottles arranged with terrible neatness, and a legal pad covered in handwriting that deteriorated line by line, top to bottom, like a seismograph recording a collapse.

"You sent him," Edmund said. "The man in the reservoir."

Pell lowered himself into a chair by degrees, like a building being demolished politely. "Marcus Webb. Investigator, missing persons, out of Corvett County. Good at his work. Too good, as it turned out." He looked at his own hands, which trembled against the tabletop like small trapped birds. "I hired him eleven months ago. Officially to close out cold files. Unofficially—" the dry-paper voice caught, smoothed itself, continued, "—unofficially I wanted to know if Ruth Hollis was still alive before I died, so I would know whether I'd spent thirty years guarding a grave or a secret."

"And?"

"And Marcus found her." Pell's eyes came up, and in them Edmund saw something he hadn't expected and had no shelf to file it on: grief. Real grief, unperformed. "And then he grew a conscience. Do you know how rare that is, Galloway? I've bought silence my whole career. Judges, deputies, editors, dispatchers." A pause, deliberate, a scalpel laid gently on the table between them. "It's the cheapest commodity in any county. And this young man takes my retainer, finds my witness, and calls me to say he won't bury it. Says the mechanic's mother deserves to know her son's killer signed a trust instead of a confession. Says he's going to the state attorney general after the settlement clause triggers." Pell's mouth twisted. "I told Charles Ashcombe none of it. I swear that on whatever's left of me. But someone was listening to my calls. Someone always was."

"The reservoir," Edmund said. "They put your number in his pocket? Your name?"

"They put a message in his pocket meant for whoever found him. A warning dressed as a clue. *Ask the dispatcher.*" Pell smiled without any humor in it at all. "They know about you, son. They've known since the coat went missing from your closet—which means someone has been inside your home, in your life, close enough to touch your things. You weren't warned tonight because they want you frightened. Frightened men run to their holes. And they think your hole is the same one it was fifteen years ago."

Edmund stood very still. Outside, the sedan's headlights swept once across the curtain and moved on—or pretended to move on. His heart was doing something quiet and enormous, a tide going out.

"Why tell me any of this?" he asked. "Why burn yourself?"

"Because I'm already burning." Pell gestured at the pill bottles, the legal pad, the sweetish smell, all of it. "Thirty years ago I told myself I was protecting a family from scandal. Then I told myself I was protecting a woman from reprisal—that disappearing her was mercy. Then I just told myself stories, the way we all do, until the stories were load-bearing walls." He reached into the cardigan's pocket and set something on the table between the water glass and the pills. Small. Brass. Cold-looking under the kitchen's single bulb. "First National Bank, Marlow Bend branch. Box 214. In it is Ruth Hollis's original statement—signed, dated, witnessed by me, describing exactly what she saw on the Corbett Mill Road the night Danny Ferro died. Describing the car. Describing the driver. It has been in that box since 2009, and every year I paid the rental fee like a man paying rent on his own conscience."

Edmund didn't reach for the key. Reaching for it would make it real; not reaching kept the last door shut a few minutes longer. "Why not take it to the state yourself?"

"Because a dying fixer's word is worth less than the paper it's not written on. They'd have me discredited before my obituary ran—I have thirty years of enemies and no friends left alive to defend me." His voice dropped, and the trembling in it wasn't the illness anymore. "But a dispatcher. An invisible man. A citizen with no history, no debts, no loyalties anyone can name. You're the only witness to my witness, Galloway. Isn't that a joke? Fifteen years of erasing yourself, and it turns out you're the one thing they couldn't erase: the man who drove her away and took the money and remembers every mile of it."

"I don't want to be anything," Edmund heard himself say, and hated how true it sounded, how small.

"Nobody does," Pell said. "That's how men like Charles Ashcombe stay kings. Not because people are evil. Because they're tired." He pushed the key an inch closer across the table. "Settlement clause triggers December twenty-first. After that, the trust locks permanently and the fortune is untouchable—every dollar of it built on a boy crushed on a county road and a girl drowned on paper. Take the key. Take the statement. Find her again, if you can stomach knowing where she's been all these years. And do it fast, because I can feel the clock in my chest winding down, and I promise you theirs is running faster."

* * *

Edmund walked back the way he'd come, past the birdbath and its black water, and did not look at the sedan, which was no longer at the curb. That absence felt louder than any presence. At the corner of Delaney and Vine he stopped, once, and looked back at the amber kitchen window, and saw Pell's silhouette seated at the table, unmoving, a man alone with the arithmetic of his life. Edmund raised a hand—not a wave, nothing so warm—just an acknowledgment, one silhouette to another. The silhouette did not raise its hand back. Perhaps it hadn't seen. Perhaps it had decided there was nothing left worth acknowledging.

At the depot he sat in his cab in the dark lot and held the key on his open palm for a long time. It weighed almost nothing. Keys always weigh almost nothing; it's the doors that are heavy. Box 214. First National. He could drive there right now, at two in the morning, and stand in the fluorescent hum of the lobby, and hold fifteen years of a dead girl's testimony in his hands. Or he could drop the key down a storm drain and go back to being nobody, and maybe live, the way he'd lived—half-alive, unlisted, a voice on a radio belonging to no face.

He was still holding it when his radio crackled with the overnight traffic, and he listened the way he always listened, half-attentive, until the scanner carried the address and his blood went cold all at once, uniformly, like frost crossing a windshield.

*Structure fire, 400 block Delaney Road. Fully involved on arrival.*

He drove with the light bar off and the pedal down, and he knew before he rounded the corner, knew from the orange bloom against the low clouds, that the amber kitchen window was gone—that the last man alive who could testify beside him was burning the way paper burns, quick and total, and that somewhere in the crowd of neighbors in bathrobes on the lawn, among the firemen dragging hose, among the ordinary gawkers of an ordinary catastrophe, there would be faces he'd seen before. A clean sedan. Patient headlights. Men whose employer had just closed the last account in a thirty-year ledger.

The house groaned and folded inward like a breath let go. Edmund stood at the tape's edge with the brass key biting into his palm, and understood, finally and completely, that there were no more doors left to keep shut. Only one left to open—and men coming to make sure it stayed locked forever.

Box 214 opened at nine a.m. He would be first in line.
