## 2. Hollowmere

The bus let me off at the bottom of a hill that seemed personally offended by my arrival, and I climbed it in a rain that had the patience of a debt collector. Not the dramatic sort of rain that arrives with thunder and an excuse—this was the kind that simply decided, hours ago, that you were going to be wet, and saw no reason to rush the matter.

Hollowmere announced itself in stages. First the church spire, gray against gray, like a pencil line someone had drawn and then regretted. Then a scatter of slate roofs hunched around a green. Then the green itself, drowned and gleaming, with a duck standing in the middle of it looking as though it owned the place and was disappointed in the tenants.

I had been to a hundred parishes like it. That was the problem. Parishes like this one ran on a currency I knew how to counterfeit: the nod, the half-smile, the assumption of belonging. I had spent eleven years practicing the art of being nobody in particular, and I was very good at it. Nobody looks twice at a man with a wet coat and a lawyer's case, because every village has six of him by Tuesday.

Except that Hollowmere, it turned out, looked twice.

* * *

The first face belonged to a woman sweeping water off her doorstep, which is a job with the long-term prospects of bailing the sea. She looked up as I passed, and her broom paused—not long, not rudely, just a half-beat, the way a clock hesitates before it strikes.

"Evening," I said.

"Evening," she said. "You'll be wanting the Lamb."

It wasn't a question. It was delivered with the mild certainty of a woman who had been told I would come, told where I would go, and found the whole arrangement satisfactory.

"I might," I said. "Or I might be wanting a bed first."

"The Lamb has beds," she said, and went back to her ocean.

I stood in the rain a moment longer than dignity required. There was nothing sinister in what she'd said. That was precisely what bothered me. A stranger arriving in a village gets directions, yes, but they come with curiosity attached—*where are you from, what's your business, is it true about the price of feed in the eastern counties*. Curiosity is the tax villages charge outsiders, and I had paid it so many times I barely noticed the transaction anymore.

This woman hadn't asked me a single thing. She had simply *known*.

I filed it where I file everything: in the drawer marked *probably nothing*, which over the years had grown so full it no longer closed properly.

* * *

The Lamb & Whistle was warm in the way that only old pubs manage—warmth that has soaked into the beams over centuries and is lent back out at a fair rate. A fire, a low ceiling, a landlord with forearms like a man who had once arm-wrestled a tree and won on points.

"Mr. Vane," he said.

I had not told him my name.

"Your room's ready," he continued, before I could decide how to feel about it. "End of the corridor, the one that doesn't rattle. Mrs. Petch said you'd want the quiet one."

"Did she," I said.

"She's very thorough, Mrs. Petch." He slid a brass key across the bar. "Supper's at seven. Lamb. It's always lamb on Thursdays. Nobody remembers why."

I took the key. It was warm, which meant someone had been holding it recently, which meant someone had been waiting.

"Landlord," I said, "I don't suppose you could tell me who arranged all this? The room, the lamb, the general air of expectation?"

He smiled with genuine pleasure, the smile of a man who has been asked the one question he is equipped to answer. "The vicar, sir. Reverend Loxley. He said you'd come today, on the four o'clock bus, wet through, and that we were to look after you proper." He leaned in, confiding, delighted. "He even got the weather right. Said it'd be rain. Said you'd need the fire lit in your room before you got here."

"And was it?"

"It was," he said proudly. "Toasty as a toast."

I carried my case upstairs thinking about the fire. Anyone can predict a bus. Buses in the country keep schedules the way rivers keep banks—loosely, and only when it suits them, but predictably enough. Rain in October is not prophecy; it's arithmetic. But a lit fire is a different order of knowledge. A lit fire says: *we know not only that you are coming, but when you will be cold.*

Someone in Hollowmere had done arithmetic on me. I did not yet know what the sum was, but I resented being a figure in it.

* * *

I ate the lamb, which was excellent, and listened, which is the profession within the profession. Pubs in small parishes are confessional booths with better lighting, and by the second pint I had assembled a rough census of Hollowmere. The Marsh estate up past the mill, shut up seven years and spoken of in the past tense. The bakery on the corner, run by a widow named Hartley whose bread, I was assured with religious intensity, was the only reason God had not yet given up on the village entirely. And the church, of course. The church above all. In a parish like this, the church is not a building; it is the operating system.

The vicar found me before I found him, which I suspect is his general method with everything.

"Mr. Vane!" He materialized at my elbow with the suddenness of a man who has spent thirty years appearing at the elbows of the grieving. He was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, with a face built for sympathy the way a barn is built for hay—vast capacity, weathered, fundamentally sound. "Reverend Loxley. I do hope the room is adequate. Mrs. Petch at the shop said the fire—"

"The fire was lit before I arrived," I said. "Which was a kindness I haven't yet earned."

"Kindness doesn't need earning, Mr. Vane. That's rather the point of it." He beamed and steered me toward a bench by the window, sitting me down with the gentle authority of a man who has moved furniture all his life and sees people as a category of furniture. "You must forgive the fuss. We are so very grateful you came."

"Grateful," I said. "That's an interesting word for it. I haven't done anything yet."

"You came," he said, as though this were the entire job description. "In my experience, Mr. Vane, that is the difficult part. Everything else is paperwork and prayer, and I'm adequate at one of them."

I studied him. He was good—very good—the warmth reaching all the way to his eyes, or at least to the lobby of his eyes. But there was a precision underneath it. He had known my name, my bus, my preference in rooms. Somewhere in the last week, someone had written a brief on Sable Vane, and this man had read it cover to cover.

"Reverend," I said, "I was engaged by a solicitor. A Mr. Fenwick, of Fenwick and—"

"Ah." The beam flickered. Just for an instant—like the fire in my room when a door opens somewhere else in the house. "Yes. About Mr. Fenwick."

"Is he here? I'd like to review the instructions before tomorrow. I'm meant to read a will to the trustees, and I prefer to know what I'm reading before I read it. It's a professional quirk."

The vicar folded his hands and looked at them with the tenderness of a man examining a difficult scripture. "Mr. Fenwick resigned this morning."

I let the silence sit there, because silences are where people put the things they'd rather not say, and if you're patient they come and fetch them back.

"Resigned," I repeated.

"Effective immediately. He's gone to his sister's in Corbury. His health, I understand." Loxley said *his health* the way men say *the weather turned*, a phrase that explains everything and investigates nothing.

"His health deteriorated rather suddenly, then. He wrote to me nine days ago with the crispness of a man in perfect command of his fountain pen."

"Resignations are like that," said the vicar. "They look sudden from outside." He rose, smoothing his cassock, and the warmth returned at full wattage, banked and glowing. "You'll want to rest. The reading is at eleven. The trustees are—well. They're the trustees. God gives every parish the trustees it deserves, and I've never been certain what we did."

He paused at the door of the pub and turned back, and for one unguarded half-second I saw the calculation running under all that wool, quick and cool as a fish under ice.

"Mr. Vane. Whatever you find in that document tomorrow—remember that Hollowmere is a small place. Small places survive on what they agree not to say. I'd hate for you to mistake our silence for ignorance. It's the opposite. It's *load-bearing*."

Then he smiled, blessed me with a fluency that suggested long practice, and went out into the rain, which received him like an old friend.

* * *

My room was warm and the bed was good and I did not sleep.

I lay listening to the rain work the slate above me and took inventory, because inventory is what I do instead of counting sheep. Sheep count themselves, in my experience, and come out wrong.

Item: a woman who knew my destination before I did. Item: a landlord with a lit fire and a schedule. Item: a vicar who had read my file and quoted its warmth back at me with the accuracy of a trained actor and the precision of a trained something else. Item: a solicitor who had hired me and then evaporated nine days later, leaving behind a will I had been instructed to read *only at Hollowmere parish*—an instruction I had thought quaint at the time and now thought of as a leash.

And underneath all of it, the thing that had been itching since the doorstep: nobody had asked me anything. Not the woman, not the landlord, not the vicar. In every village I had ever entered, I had been interrogated within an inch of my coat. Here, nothing. Here, my arrival had been *expected*, and expectation is the death of curiosity.

Which meant someone had told them who I was.

Not the name—anyone can have a name. The *substance*. Sable Vane, estate lawyer, discreet, thorough, the man who untangles things. Someone had distributed a portrait of me with the efficiency of a parish newsletter, and the parish had read it and nodded and said, *ah, yes, that explains the fire.*

There are two explanations for that. The comfortable one is efficiency: a competent solicitor prepares a village for an awkward reading, and a competent vicar executes the preparations, and everyone is warm and lamb is served.

The uncomfortable one is that in Hollowmere, information doesn't travel. It's *issued*. And I had just been issued to them.

I got up and looked out the window. Across the green, the church stood black against the rain, and as I watched, a light moved inside it—low, steady, moving from the vestry toward the tower stairs. A candle, or a lamp carried low. A verger making his rounds, most likely. Parishes keep vergers the way ships keep rats: anciently, and with grudging affection.

The light stopped at the tower door. It stayed there a long moment, motionless, as though the man holding it were looking out—not at the rain, not at the green, but at my window. At me.

Then it went out, all at once, like a held breath released.

I stood in the dark of the warm little room, in the parish that had lit my fire before I arrived, and I thought about the will in my case, and the diary it mentioned, and a woman seven years dead who had apparently kept writing.

Tomorrow I would read it aloud to the trustees. Tonight, someone in the church had watched me watch them, and neither of us had turned on a light to do it.

I decided, lying back down in the dark, that I would get very little sleep in Hollowmere.

I was right about that. It is one of the few things, in the weeks that followed, I was ever entirely right about from the beginning.
