## 4. Diary Entries After Death

The diary arrived the way most dangerous things arrive in my experience: politely, in brown paper, tied with string.

It came on the third morning of my stay in Hollowmere, left at the door of my lodgings above the bakery by someone who had not knocked. I know because I was awake. I am always awake at six; a man who has spent years being nobody learns to sleep like a cat with one ear open. By the time I got down the stairs — past the smell of warm bread that Mrs. Fenwick produced each dawn as if by alchemy rather than labor — the lane outside was empty except for a heron standing in the millstream with the smug patience of a creditor.

The parcel sat on the step, dry despite the drizzle, which meant it had been placed there minutes before.

Inside: a ledger-bound book, its spine cracked, its corners soft as old cheese rind. And a card, written in a hand I recognized from the will's codicil — the vicar's, though he'd have denied the flourish:

*You asked what continued after she died. Read for yourself. — T.*

I made tea. This is a habit of mine when the ground begins to shift: boil water, warm the pot, perform the small liturgies of ordinary life while the extraordinary thing sits on the table daring you to touch it. Mrs. Fenwick, delivering a second pot unasked and studying me with the frank appraisal of a woman who has fed curates, farmers, and one memorable tax inspector, said only, "You've gone grey about the mouth, Mr. Vane. Eat something."

"Thank you," I said. "What is it?"

"Bread pudding." She set the plate down with the finality of a judge. "You'll eat it whether you want it or not."

I ate it. It was excellent. Hollowmere, I was learning, was a parish of contradictions: a place where kindness arrived unbidden and menace arrived equally so, often wearing the same coat.

Then I opened the diary.

* * *

The first half was exactly what you would expect from Edith Marsh in the year before her disappearance. Entries about weather. About the choir. About her father's health, described with the tender exasperation daughters reserve for fathers who insist they are fine while visibly dying. She wrote about the bells — Quill's bells — with real affection. She wrote about her uncle-by-marriage, Aldous Crane, in sentences so carefully neutral they rang like alarm bells to anyone who has ever read a hostile witness statement.

*"Mr. C. called again today. Papa says he is family and family looks after its own. I have noticed that when Mr. C. looks after something, it tends to become his."*

That entry was dated the eleventh of March, seven years ago. Her disappearance was logged the following June.

I turned pages. April. May. The handwriting loosening slightly, the entries shortening — a woman running out of time or out of paper or both. The last entry before the gap read:

*"Papa slept through the whole afternoon. Dr. Lyle says the heart is tired. I sat with him and did the accounts, and I thought: if anything happens to him, I shall have to be braver than I have ever been. I don't feel equal to it. But then I remember Grandmother Marsh, who buried two husbands and a bankruptcy and still kept bees into her nineties. Bravery, she said, is mostly just refusing to be hurried."*

*Refusing to be hurried.* I wrote the phrase down. I don't fully know why. Some sentences you keep the way you keep a key that fits no lock yet.

Then came the gap. Pages torn out — not removed neatly but ripped, the stubs still there like broken teeth. Roughly fourteen months missing. When the entries resumed, everything had changed.

The handwriting was smaller. Tighter. Written, I judged, by someone conserving ink, or strength, or hope.

And the date was eighteen months *after* the coroner had signed off on Edith Marsh's death.

* * *

*"14th October.*

*I watched the funeral procession from the loft window today. Not mine — old Mr. Tice, the saddler. Eleven years I knew him and he never once overcharged me, which in this parish counts as sainthood. They buried him under the yew, next to the plot everyone thinks holds me.*

*I could see my own stone from up there. Someone puts fresh flowers on it. I don't know who. I have theories, but theories are dangerous things in a small place, so I let the flowers be anonymous and I say thank you anyway, silently, the way you'd thank God."*

I set the book down and pressed both palms flat against the table until the wood stopped seeming to move.

Someone alive wrote this. Someone alive wrote this *in the parish*, watching funerals from a loft window, watching their own memorial stone, counting flowers. My heart was doing something undignified — the exhilaration of the hunt, that hot wire down the spine that I had sworn off years ago along with my name and my former life. This was the feeling that had cost me everything once. I knew its face. I knew how it lied.

I poured more tea and kept reading, because knowing a feeling lies has never once stopped me from listening to it.

* * *

*"2nd November.*

*Rang the six o'clock bell myself tonight. Mr. Quill's hands were bad again — I saw him flexing them after practice, the way a man does when he won't admit the cold has gotten into the joints. So I offered to take the hour and he looked at me for a long moment and said, 'You ring like your grandmother.' Which is true, and which means he knows exactly who I am, and which means either he is the safest man in Hollowmere or the most dangerous, and after seven years I still cannot decide which.*

*I rang the hour. Nobody died. Small victories."*

My tea went cold in the cup.

Quill. The verger. The ancient, stooped, apologetic man who had shuffled me around the churchyard two days ago pointing out drainage problems with the pride of a general reviewing fortifications. If this entry was true — if Quill had let Edith Marsh, declared dead seven years, pull the ropes of his beloved bells —

But wait. The dates. These entries were three years old. Whatever arrangement existed between Quill and the dead woman might have ended long ago. Might have ended badly. Might explain why the man's hands shook now, why he watched me across the churchyard with eyes that measured rather than greeted.

Or — and here the colder thought arrived, the one I didn't want — these entries might be forged. A clever fabrication, planted where I'd find it, dated to lead me by the nose. I had been played before. Being played once teaches you humility; being played twice teaches you paranoia, and paranoia at least keeps you alive.

I needed evidence beyond ink. Paper stock, watermarks, provenance. Things I could hold up against the light.

I kept reading.

* * *

*"19th December.*

*Snow. The kind that makes the parish go quiet in a way I love and fear, because quiet in Hollowmere is never empty, it is attentive.*

*I saw the Squire's car come up the lane at dusk, going toward the Hall. Mr. C. He visits the widow Petch now, twice a month, always after dark. The widow Petch whose farm borders ours — mine — and whose signature appears on more documents than a woman who cannot write her own name should reasonably produce. I copy everything I can from the loft. I have begun keeping lists. Lists are a form of prayer, I think, when you cannot pray properly."*

There it was. The angle no outsider could ever have. A dead woman watching land transactions from a hayloft, cataloguing the slow theft of an estate, unable to go to a solicitor or a magistrate because to surface was to die — or so she believed. Every entry had that quality: knowledge available only to someone *inside*, folded small, breathing flour dust and candle smoke, watching the parish through gaps in floorboards.

*"3rd February.*

*The new constable walked past the bakery four times today, looking up. Mrs. F. told him we'd sold out of seed cake and he went away looking disappointed, which is the correct response to seed cake. Still. Four times. I have moved my writing box behind the flour sacks and I have started burning yesterday's drafts. If I stop writing suddenly, whoever finds this book should know: I did not leave. I was never able to leave. Look at the flour sacks. Look at the bells. Look at who profits."*

I read that last line three times.

*Look at who profits.*

She had known, three years ago, that the diary might be found. She had written it partly as testimony — a message in a bottle, addressed to whoever was stubborn enough to pick it up. And someone *had*. Someone had taken this book, kept it safe for three years, and arranged for it to reach me via a resigned solicitor and a well-meaning vicar, timed to the delivery of a will that legally could not exist.

Which raised a question I sat with while the bakery below filled with the smell of the afternoon's second bake: who had held this diary? Who had read it and done nothing for three years? Or worse — who had read it and *waited*, letting the trail grow cold, until a stranger with a reputation for untangling deaths wandered into the parish and became useful?

The exhilaration was curdling now, exactly as I'd feared it would. Because there were only two kinds of people who keep a dead woman's diary for three years: protectors, and bait-keepers. And in my limited but vivid experience, the second kind far outnumbered the first.

* * *

The final entries were the worst. Or the best, depending on how much you enjoy having your assumptions dismantled.

*"28th March.*

*He is selling the water meadows. MY water meadows. I watched the surveyors from the churchyard wall this morning, wearing Mrs. F.'s spare shawl and carrying a basket so I'd look like nobody. One of them stepped on my grave to drive his stake. I confess I laughed. It startled a crow.*

*If I am ever restored to myself, I am putting a bench on that exact spot. Let future surveyors contemplate their sins."*

Gentle humor, even now. Even hiding. That did something to me that the fear hadn't — some knot behind the sternum loosening. You can forge grief. You can forge terror. But you cannot easily forge the specific voice of a woman laughing at a surveyor standing on her own grave. Fabrication smooths people out; life leaves these rough, human edges.

*"9th April.*

*Quill brought me communion today, privately, in the vestry. Seven years and he has never once said my name aloud — he calls me 'the lady' as if there were only one. Perhaps in his mind there is. He prayed for my father. He did not pray for justice, which surprised me, until he explained: 'Justice comes round on its own, miss, like the seasons. My job is to keep ringing till it does.'*

*I am tired tonight. Tired in the bones, where sleep doesn't reach. Sometimes I think: let it end, one way or the other. Either let me live or let me be properly dead. This in-between is its own kind of grave, just roomier."*

The last entry was dated the seventeenth of April, three years ago. Three years of silence since. No explanation for the stopping. Either she had given up writing, or given up something larger, or the diary had been taken from her.

I closed the book and sat for a long time in the lamplight, listening to the rain start up again against the window, and to Mrs. Fenwick below, singing to herself as she scraped baking trays — a tuneless, cheerful sound that I found, unexpectedly, enormously comforting.

Here is what I knew, laid out like evidence on a mortuary slab:

Edith Marsh had been alive three years after her death, hidden in this parish, likely above this very bakery — the flour dust, the loft, Mrs. F. herself, perhaps. She had watched Aldous Crane dismantle her inheritance. She had trusted the verger. And then the diary stopped, and sometime between then and now, a will had been drafted citing a codicil, and a solicitor had summoned me and immediately resigned, and everyone in Hollowmere smiled at me with slightly too many teeth.

Someone alive wrote these pages. That much I believed down to my boots.

Whether she was still alive — and whether handing me this book was an act of rescue or of baiting — remained gloriously, dangerously unclear.

I banked the lamp, tucked the diary inside my coat, and did what any sensible man does at nine o'clock in a suspicious parish: I went out into the rain for a walk, because there was one more angle I wanted to check. From the churchyard wall, in the dark, you can see the loft window above the bakery. And I wanted to know whether it showed lamplight — or whether it had been dark for three years.

It was dark.

But as I stood there, dripping, a shape crossed the church porch behind me — quick, low, deliberate — and by the time I turned, the lane held nothing but rain and the heron, still standing in the stream, watching me with the patient disapproval of a creature that knows exactly how many bodies a river can hide.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would pull the records on every death in this parish for the last decade. Because a woman who writes *look at who profits* has just handed me a map — and I intended to follow it to whatever graveyard lay at the end.
