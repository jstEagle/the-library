## 10. The Fourth Accident

The knock came at half past six in the morning, which in my experience is the hour reserved for two kinds of news: the kind that can wait for breakfast, and the kind that makes you wish you'd never learned to read.

I opened the door of my room at the Lamb and Flag to find a boy of perhaps twelve standing in the drizzle, holding a note as though it might bite him. He had the particular pallor of someone who had been sent on an errand no adult wanted.

"You're the lawyer," he said. Not a question. In Hollowmere, apparently, I was already a fixture—*the lawyer*, definite article, like the church or the millpond itself.

"I'm a lawyer," I said. "There's a difference, though I grant it's subtle."

He thrust the note at me and fled, which struck me as sound judgment.

The note was from Mrs. Petch, the baker's widow, written in a hand so small and precise it looked typeset: *They've pulled a man out of the millpond. The one who resigned. Thought you'd want to know before it's in the paper. —P.*

I stood there a moment with the rain coming off the eaves in ropes and thought, with a clarity that felt almost physical, *No.*

Not him. Not yet. He'd resigned—he'd *run*, which was the one sensible thing anyone in this parish had done since I arrived—and running was supposed to buy a man time. That was the whole arithmetic of flight. You gave up your home, your practice, your name in the parish register, and in exchange you got to keep breathing.

I dressed fast, skipped the landlady's offer of kippers with a regret I genuinely meant, and went out into a morning the color of wet slate.

* * *

The millpond sat at the bottom of Hollow Lane, a broad brown mirror fringed with alders, and by seven o'clock it had acquired an audience. Parish folk have a genius for assembling at disasters the way iron filings assemble at a magnet; there were already forty of them behind a sagging rope, murmuring in that low collective tone that is neither speech nor silence.

Two constables from the district stood at the water's edge looking unhappy. A police surgeon—a different one from the coroner's man, I noted, younger, with the defensive set of a junior officer handed a job above his pay—crouched over something under a tarpaulin near the bank.

And standing slightly apart from everyone, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring the scenery, was Aldous Crane.

Of course he was. Crane had the gift—I'd begun to think of it as a gift, the way you'd call a spider's patience a gift—of being present at every significant moment in Hollowmere without ever appearing to seek them out. He nodded to me across the crowd, pleasant as Sunday.

"Sable Vane," he called. "Terrible business. Do come and look, if you've the stomach. You of all people will appreciate the particulars."

*I of all people.* I filed that away and went and looked.

Under the tarpaulin lay Mr. Fenwick Hobb, formerly solicitor to the parish trustees, lately resigned, currently very dead. He was sixty-odd, slight, dressed in his good coat, and his face had that terrible smoothness the water gives. There was mud on his knees.

"Mud on the bank," the young surgeon said, catching me looking. "Slipped, most likely. Been raining a fortnight. The stones down there are green with slime." He said it quickly, all in one breath, like a boy reciting a lesson he hadn't fully understood but had been made to memorize.

"When?" I asked.

"Coroner will fix the hour. Last night, between ten and midnight, we think."

"Who found him?"

"Mrs. Tallow, walking her dog." He gestured vaguely at the crowd, where a small ferocious woman held a small equally ferocious terrier, both of them surveying the scene with professional satisfaction.

"And when did Mrs. Tallow find him?"

"Half five this morning."

"So he lay here—" I did the arithmetic aloud, because I am constitutionally incapable of not doing arithmetic aloud, "—for better than five hours, in plain view of the lane, before anyone noticed."

The surgeon's ears went pink. "It's dark. And foggy off the water."

"It was light by four."

"Look," he said, lowering his voice, and here his eyes did something complicated—flicked toward Crane, flicked back, settled somewhere in the middle distance, "the man resigned his position under a cloud. Everyone knows he'd been drinking. There'll be an inquest, of course there will, but—" He straightened up and tugged the tarpaulin back over Fenwick Hobb's face with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him. "Accidents happen in Hollowmere, Mr. Vane. More than their share, some say. But nobody says it loudly."

He walked away before I could answer, which was its own answer.

* * *

I spent the rest of the morning doing what I do, which is the unglamorous work of asking boring questions until they stop being boring.

Fenwick Hobb had resigned nine days ago—abruptly, by letter, citing his health. His clerk, a mournful young man named Tibbs, told me Hobb had been cheerful that last week. "Cheerful like a man who's paid off a debt," Tibbs said, polishing glasses that didn't need polishing. "He said to me, 'Tibbs, there's a weight coming off my shoulders, and when it does, I may tell you everything.' Then he laughed. I'd never heard him laugh before. It frightened me a little, if you want the truth."

"What debt? Who owed whom?"

"That's just it, sir. He wouldn't say. Only that he'd been keeping something for someone, and keeping it had got expensive."

Expensive. I turned that word over all afternoon. Keeping things in Hollowmere was expensive—the widow Petch had been keeping something for seven years, if my suspicions ran true, and it had cost her a husband's name on nothing and a lifetime of small careful lies. What had Hobb been keeping? Papers, most likely. Solicitors keep papers the way magpies keep silver. And what does a man do with dangerous papers when he decides to stop keeping them?

He either returns them or he sells them. And either way, somebody notices.

By evening I had assembled the following: Hobb had drawn five hundred pounds from his own account three days before his death—a sum his clerk described as "more than he'd saved in a decade." He had told his landlady he was going away for a fortnight and asked her to mind his cat. His bag was packed. His ticket, purchased that afternoon from the station master, was for the morning boat train.

A man does not pack a bag, buy a ticket, and entrust his cat to a neighbor, and then go for a midnight stroll along a slime-green millpond. Even in Hollowmere, even drunk—and Tibbs swore Hobb hadn't touched a drop in years, having lost a brother to drink and sworn it off at the graveside—even granting every charitable assumption, the facts refused to lie down in the shape of *accident*.

But the parish machinery had already lain them down there, and patted them flat, and was now arranging flowers.

* * *

The inquest—if you could call it that—was held the next day in the vestry room, lasting rather less time than the funeral tea that followed. The coroner was a brisk, elderly gentleman who had driven over from the county seat and clearly wished to drive back. The young surgeon repeated his lesson about the slippery stones. Mrs. Tallow described her dog's discovery with real dramatic flair. Aldous Crane testified briefly, sadly, beautifully, to the deceased's long service and recent melancholy, and produced—God help us—a letter from Hobb's own files, dated the week of his resignation, in which Hobb wrote of feeling "unwell in mind and spirit" and "a burden I cannot continue to carry."

Suicide would have been tidier, I suppose, but drowning doesn't always cooperate, and *accident while in a state of mental distress* let everyone have their verdict and their sympathy too.

The coroner asked if anyone wished to add anything.

Every instinct I possess—which is to say, every scar—screamed at me to keep my mouth shut. I have built a life, such as it is, on the principle that the man who asks questions in public gets answered in private, usually at inconvenient hours and with unpleasant instruments. Three times now I had untangled a death the authorities had waved through, and three times I had done it quietly, leaving no name, taking no credit, becoming no one. Anonymity wasn't vanity. It was armor. I had lost everything once—name, practice, future—to a case I took too publicly, and I had sworn, in the specific way one swears after learning what a vow costs, never again.

But Fenwick Hobb had packed a bag. He had bought a ticket. He had laughed, once, like a man setting down a weight.

"Mr. Coroner," I heard myself say, "I'd like to know why a man bound for the morning boat train, with luggage packed and fare paid, went walking on a millpond bank at midnight."

The vestry room went very quiet. Somewhere a clock ticked with theatrical insolence.

The coroner peered at me over his spectacles. "And you are?"

"Sable Vane. Retained by the parish trustees regarding the Marsh estate."

"Ah." Something passed across his face—not recognition exactly, more the wariness of a man who has heard a name mentioned in certain rooms. "Your point, Mr. Vane?"

"My point is only that the stones are slippery, yes, but the lane runs past the front of Hobb's lodgings and the station lies the other direction entirely. A man doesn't walk to the millpond by mistake. The millpond isn't on the way to anywhere."

"Except," said a voice from the back, mild and reasonable, "to the mill."

Laughter, relieved, grateful for any exit from discomfort. Aldous Crane stepped forward into the pause the way other men step into doorways, naturally, owning it.

"Mr. Vane raises a fair question," he said, "and I hope he'll pursue it—with discretion. Fenwick was my friend these thirty years. If there's anything untoward, I want it found. But I'd ask us all to remember that grief makes suspicious men of the best of us." He put his hand briefly, warmly, on my shoulder. "You're new among us, Mr. Vane. You'll find Hollowmere takes care of its dead. We've had practice."

*We've had practice.*

Everyone laughed again. I laughed too. It was that or scream, and screaming in front of the coroner rarely advances an inquiry.

* * *

That night I sat in my room at the Lamb and Flag with the diary open on my knee and the lamp turned low, and I made myself look at the thing I'd been circling for days.

Three deaths. The ones I'd solved, scattered across three counties, three years apart—each ruled accidental, each quietly reopened by me, each closed without trial because the principals kept dying or vanishing or both. I had thought them separate. I had prided myself, God forgive me, on thinking of them as *my* cases, discrete puzzles, each with its own neat solution.

Now I laid them side by side on the bedspread like photographs of the same criminal at different ages, and the family resemblance was unmistakable.

The first: a gamekeeper in Northumberland, thrown from a horse on flat ground, whose testimony in a land dispute had been scheduled for the Monday after he died. The dispute concerned land that had passed, through two intermediate trusts, into the hands of a company whose registered director was a name I hadn't known to look for then.

The second: a housekeeper in Yorkshire, scalded in a kitchen she'd worked in safely for twenty years, who had written—so her sister told me, years too late—a letter to a magistrate about documents she'd found while cleaning a study.

The third: a coachman, a runaway horse, a stone wall. He had driven for a family connection of the Cranes. He had talked, in his cups, about a night he'd driven someone to the coast and waited with the lamps doused.

All three had stood to inherit, or testify, or simply *know*. All three had died within weeks of becoming useful. And threading through the land records of each, if you dug deep enough and knew which names to fear, ran the same slow river of trust deeds and nominee directors, pooling at last—though I hadn't known it then—in the estate of a missing woman named Edith Marsh.

I had solved three accidents. I had never once asked why the same accident kept happening, wearing different clothes, in different counties, like a murderer who changes his coat but not his walk.

Hollowmere wasn't a new case. It was the spine. Everything I'd undone had been a rib off this bone.

And the machinery here—the coroner who drove in and drove out, the surgeon who recited his lesson with pink ears and guilty eyes, the constables who arrived with conclusions pre-installed—this machinery killed faster than I could investigate. Hobb had lasted nine days from resignation to millpond. At that rate, whoever else in this parish was carrying a weight on their shoulders had perhaps a fortnight, and I had not yet identified a single person I could protect, arrest, or even warn without painting a target on them.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the rain, and I admitted the thing I'd been refusing to admit since the boy knocked at half past six.

Quiet wasn't working. Quiet was how Fenwick Hobb ended up under a tarpaulin with mud on his knees while the whole parish nodded along. My invisibility, my beautiful careful anonymity—it wasn't armor anymore. It was camouflage, and I was wearing it in the wrong war.

There was a soft scrape at the window.

I froze. Rain ticked against the glass. Then again—scrape, deliberate, patient. I crossed the room and opened the sash onto the wet night, and there, wedged in the frame where the leaded pane met the sill, was a folded card, weighted with a pebble, dry beneath the dripping eaves.

I unfolded it by the light of the rekindled lamp.

Heavy cream stock. A crest I recognized from the gates of Crane's estate. Inside, in a hand as confident as a signature:

*Mr. Vane — You ask interesting questions in public. Come dine with me Thursday and ask them in private. I find curiosity is best fed than starved. — A.C.*

And beneath, smaller, almost an afterthought, the sentence that turned my blood to well-water:

*I understand you prefer to travel under a borrowed name. No matter. All names are welcome at my table.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the third reading changed nothing and I needed it to change something.

He knew. Not suspected—*knew*. Knew about the old identity, the buried name, the life I had dismantled brick by brick and carried away in a carpet bag. Which meant someone had told him, or sold it to him, or he had dug it up years ago and kept it, the way Fenwick Hobb kept things, expensively, waiting for the moment it became useful.

The fire crackled. Down in the street, the rain went on erasing the lane, the millpond filling silently in the dark, patient as everything else in this parish.

I had come to Hollowmere believing I was investigating a trap.

Standing there with Crane's card in my hand, I understood, with the calm horror of a man watching the floor open, that I might be standing in one—that the will, the codicil, the impossible diary, the whole lovely puzzle might have been laid out for me like a dinner, and I the guest of honor, and the seat at Crane's table already carved with my name on it.

Thursday, then. I would go. A man who refuses a predator's invitation only confirms he's prey.

But I would go knowing one thing Crane did not know I knew: somewhere in this parish, a woman who had been declared dead seven years ago was writing a diary in ink bought from the vestry supplies.

And whatever Aldous Crane thought he was serving at that table—

I intended to bring my own knife.
