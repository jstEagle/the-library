## 12. The Diary's Missing Year

The diary had a wound in it.

Sable noticed it the first night at the vicarage, when he'd turned past the entry dated the fourteenth of March and found the stub of pages — a ragged seam where something had been cut away with a blade, not torn in haste. Tearing was passion. This was surgery. Someone had sat down with a straight edge and removed a year of Edith Marsh's life from between two dates that were meant to look like neighbors.

He had thought about that seam for three days now, mostly while pretending to read the parish registers the vicar kept pressing on him, and partly while eating Mrs. Tallow's seed cake, which she delivered to his room each afternoon with the cheerful insistence of a woman who believed no problem could survive butter and caraway seeds.

"You're frowning at that book again," she said today, setting down the plate. "It's a diary, isn't it? I shouldn't say anything, but the whole parish knows you've got it. The whole parish knows everything. That's half our trouble."

"Then you'll know this already," Sable said. "What happened in Hollowmere between April of one year and April of the next? Seven years back."

Mrs. Tallow considered. "Rain," she said finally. "And the harvest festival was rained off. And old Mr. Petchy's cow got into the churchyard." She frowned. "Why?"

"No reason. Thank you, Mrs. Tallow."

"Well." She gathered her tin with dignity. "If it's about Edith Marsh, I'll say what everyone says: she ran off with a man from up north and drowned herself out of shame, or drowned herself out of shame and then ran off, depending who's telling it. Neither story makes much sense if you think about it longer than a minute."

"And how long have people been thinking about it?"

"Oh, seven years," said Mrs. Tallow. "Nobody thinks longer than a minute, though. Thinking is discouraged here." And she left him alone with the cake and the wounded book.

* * *

Sable cleared the little table by the window and laid out his instruments like a surgeon: the diary; a jeweler's loupe he'd bought in another life under another name; a ruler; a pencil sharpened to a point cruel enough to qualify as a weapon in most parishes; and three envelopes of paper samples he'd collected over the past two days with the furtive grace of a man shoplifting nothing worth having.

One envelope held a sheet from the vicarage stationery cupboard — cream laid paper, watermarked with a fleur-de-lis. One held a page from the vestry's supply of sermon paper, which lived in a locked cabinet Mr. Quill kept the key to. And one held a leaf he'd lifted, with apologies to posterity, from the parish's own record of burials, because if you wanted to know what paper a place used, you went to the paper it trusted with its dead.

Then he began the slow work.

The missing section ran from mid-March of one year to the following spring — precisely the span during which, according to every official document in three counties, Edith Marsh had fled Hollowmere, been seen once in a railway town two hundred miles east, and vanished. The entries before the cut were a woman's ordinary life rendered in a small, upright hand: eggs, sermons, her father's cough, the price of coal. The entries after the cut were different. Not just in content — in *angle*. He'd felt it on his first reading without knowing why, the way you feel someone watching you from a doorway before you turn your head.

Now, under the loupe, he began to understand the feeling.

The post-cut entries described the parish from below and behind. They knew which flagstone in the chancel rocked. They knew the vicar cleared his throat twice before lying and only once before telling the truth. They knew that light came through the tower's east slit at four in the afternoon in October and made a gold coin on the floor that Quill — the entries called him *the old man*, always *the old man* — stood in for exactly as long as it took to wind the clock. These were not the observations of a woman passing through. They were the observations of a woman *stationed*. A garrison's-eye view of Hollowmere, written by someone who had learned the parish the way a spider learns a web: from inside it, patiently, thread by thread.

But observation wasn't proof. Sable had watched clever men hang on observation and cleverer men walk free on its absence. He needed the paper to testify. Paper, unlike people, could not be charmed, threatened, or promoted into silence.

He flattened the first surviving page after the cut and angled it toward the window.

Chain lines first. Every sheet of paper carries the ghost of the mould it was made on — fine vertical lines, wider horizontal ones, spaced by the papermaker's habit rather than any rule. Under the loupe, the diary's chain lines ran at intervals of twenty-eight millimeters, with a double line — a maker's tic, a signature in wire — every fourth interval. He measured the vicarage stationery: thirty-one millimeters, single lines. Different mould, different mill, different story.

His heart did something small and unprofessional.

He measured the burial register. Twenty-eight millimeters. Double line, every fourth interval. He measured the sermon paper from the vestry cabinet, the paper locked behind Mr. Quill's careful key. Twenty-eight. Double line. Every fourth.

Sable sat back and let himself feel it for exactly four seconds, which was three seconds more than he usually allowed. Then he got back to work, because a coincidence of moulds proves a supplier, not a hand, and the hand was the thing.

Ink next. The pre-cut entries were written in a blue-black iron gall ink, the kind sold in stationers in three counties, the kind that faded to a rusty brown as it aged — and these had aged properly, rusting evenly across seven years, honest ink doing honest time. The post-cut entries were also iron gall. Also faded. But under the loupe the fading was wrong — or rather, right in a way that told a story. Iron gall fades fastest where the ink sits thickest, so old writing goes brown at the downstrokes first. The post-cut entries browned unevenly, blotched, as though the writer had gone back over certain passages with fresh ink years after the fact — touching them up, keeping them alive, the way you'd keep a fire banked in a room you couldn't afford to let go cold.

And there was something else. On six pages, in the margins, almost invisible unless the light came low across the paper: pinpricks. Tiny punctures, regular as stitching. Sable counted them, mapped them, and then went very still, because they weren't random. Pricked through and held to a window, the pages resolved into letters — a second text hidden inside the first, written in holes, legible only to someone holding the diary up against the sun.

*HE COUNTS THE PLATES,* said one margin.
*I AM NOT AFRAID ON THURSDAYS,* said another, and Sable read it four times and could not decide whether it was brave or heartbreaking, and suspected it was both.
*THE OLD MAN KNOWS. HE LEAVES THE FLOUR BAG ON THE THIRD STEP.*

Flour bag. Third step. He wrote it in his notebook and underlined it twice and hated himself a little for how much hope he invested in an underline.

* * *

By evening he had assembled the case, and it was beautiful in the way that only quiet things are beautiful.

The paper stock matched the vestry supplies exactly — same mill, same mould, same watermark, including a batch flaw, a faint crease in the wire that printed itself into every fourth sheet, present in both the burial register and eleven pages of the diary. You could not buy that paper in a shop. It did not travel. It lived in one locked cabinet in one stone building in one parish, and it had spent a year recording the thoughts of a woman the parish had buried.

The ink matched too — not just in type but in provenance. The vestry kept its own ink, mixed by Mr. Quill from a recipe older than the current vicar, and Quill's mix carried a peculiarity Sable had confirmed that morning by the simple method of asking to see the ink horn and then, while admiring it, dipping the corner of a test slip. Quill's ink contained a trace of oak gall from the particular oaks on Crane land — identifiable, a chemist in the county town had confirmed by post, by its unusual tannin profile. The diary's later entries carried the same profile. The woman had written in ink brewed from trees standing on the estate of the man who swore she was dead.

And the dates. Here was the crown of it. The final surviving entry after the cut was dated three years after the disappearance — long past the date of the memorial stone, long past the coroner's finding, long past the moment the law had declared Edith Marsh a matter for probate rather than for search. Three years dead, and writing in vestry ink on vestry paper, describing the view from inside the church tower.

Sable spread the pages across the table and looked at the whole argument at once, and it said, in the flat neutral voice of evidence, the sentence he had been circling for days:

*She never left.*

Not fled. Not drowned. Not run off with a man from up north. She had walked perhaps forty yards from wherever she'd last been seen, and stopped, and stayed — hidden in the one place a parish never looks, which is inside its own daily round. Hidden the way a note hides in a hymn. And someone had helped her. Someone with keys to the vestry cabinet, someone who mixed the ink, someone who left flour bags on third steps.

Someone whose hands shook when they talked about her.

Sable thought of Mr. Quill standing in the porch with his rake, correcting him — *she wore the grey coat with the horn buttons, sir, not the blue; everyone remembers it wrong* — and then denying, with those same trembling hands folded tight, that he had ever met her at all. You don't remember the buttons of a stranger. You don't remember the buttons of a dead woman. You remember the buttons of a living woman you have spent seven years keeping alive, one flour bag at a time.

He laughed — a short, startled sound in the empty room — and pressed his hand flat against the diary pages as if he could feel warmth coming up through them.

"You're here," he said aloud to a woman seven years missing. "You've been here the whole time. In the tower. In the loft. Behind the wainscoting of this entire parish, watching us all fail to see you."

It was triumph, and it tasted extraordinary, and it lasted until he reached the end of the table and looked again at the cut edge of the missing year.

Because there was a question he had been declining to ask, and now it stood in front of him with its hat on. If Edith had cut the pages herself — and the blade-work was careful, loving even, the work of protecting something — then what was in the missing year that needed protecting? Not evidence of her survival; the surviving pages proved that well enough. Something else. Something she'd judged more dangerous than silence.

Or — and the thought arrived cold, wearing its own coat — the cut hadn't been hers at all. The will's codicil had named the diary and sent it to him. Someone had wanted him to find everything except that year. Someone had curated his discovery down to the inch.

He looked at the window. Rain again, moving across the churchyard in grey curtains, and beyond it the tower, black against the last light, its bell mouth open like a patient throat.

There was a knock at the vicarage door — two knocks, soft, apologetic, the knock of a man who hoped nobody was home. Sable opened it to find Mr. Quill on the step, collar up against the rain, holding a lantern that lit his face from below and made the shaking of his hands unmistakable.

"Beg pardon, sir," the verger said. "I saw your light. I — that is—" He glanced past Sable's shoulder at the table, at the pages laid out like an autopsy, and something in his face went through several weathers in a single second. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "You'll want to know about the bells, sir. What they heard, seven years ago. I can't say it standing in a doorway. Midnight tomorrow, the tower. Come alone, and come before the clock strikes, because after the strike I may lose my nerve, and I've only got the one nerve left."

He turned to go, then stopped, rain running off his hat brim.

"She liked the four o'clock light," he said, not looking at Sable. "In October. The gold coin on the floor. I used to stand in it so she'd know I was there." His hands trembled around the lantern. "Midnight, sir. Don't be late."

Sable watched the old man's lantern dwindle down the churchyard path, and behind him on the table the diary lay open to its wound, and somewhere above the wet dark tower the bell hung silent, waiting to tell what it had heard.
