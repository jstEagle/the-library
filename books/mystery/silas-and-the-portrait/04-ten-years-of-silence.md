## 4. Ten Years of Silence

The county records annex smelled of cold paper and older dust, a smell Beatrix had always found reassuring—the smell of things that could not change. They had built a career on that smell. Paper did not lie; it only waited. People lied, and paper remembered the lie forever, which was, in Beatrix's professional opinion, the closest thing to justice the world offered.

Tonight the smell did not reassure. Tonight the annex felt like a throat.

They had come after hours, using the after-hours key every estate lawyer in the county kept in a desk drawer and never mentioned. The fluorescent lights buzzed on in banks, one row at a time, illuminating the long steel shelves of probate archives like a morgue for the deceased affairs of the living. Beatrix walked the aisles with the file number written on their palm in ink gone slightly smeared: 2014-P-0417. *Merrick, Silas. Deceased.*

Except he wasn't. Except he had signed a will three days ago in ink that still smelled of the bottle.

Beatrix had spent the drive from the Merrick house telling themselves a dozen sensible stories. A clerical error—a death certificate filed for the wrong man. A bureaucratic resurrection. Names repeated in families; Silas Merrick the younger, say, named for a grandfather. Sensible stories, all of them, and all of them thin as vellum, because none of them explained the portrait. None of them explained the gray coat hanging in the dead man's studio, the coat Beatrix had worn to a dozen depositions, the coat that had hung in their own closet on Harrow Street until two days ago, when Beatrix had reached for it and found the hanger bare and thought nothing of it, because coats went to the cleaner, coats were lent, coats were lost.

Nobody steals a coat to paint it. Somebody steals a coat to *become* the person who wears it.

The 2014 box sat on the shelf where the index said it would, gray and unremarkable, its string-tie still knotted in the flat bureaucratic bow of a clerk with thirty years of routine. Beatrix carried it to the reading table under the one lamp that worked, set it down, and for a moment simply looked at it. Ten years old. Sealed and archived and untouched, if the dust was honest.

The dust was honest. The contents, Beatrix already suspected, would not be.

They untied the string.

* * *

The file opened the way files do: petition, certificate, inventory. The death certificate first—Beatrix had learned to read these documents the way doctors read X-rays, the eye going straight to the fracture. *Name of deceased: Silas Ambrose Merrick. Date of death: March 9, 2014. Cause: cardiac event, unwitnessed. Body viewed by affiant: yes.*

Body viewed. By whom? Beatrix turned the page and found the affidavit of identification, the document in which some living soul swears before the county that the thing in the casket or on the slab or in the photograph is the person named above. And there, in the signature line, in a hand Beatrix knew the way one knows one's own reflection—

*Beatrix J. Galloway.*

The lamp hummed. The radiators ticked. Beatrix did not move for a long moment, because moving would make it real, and not moving was a kind of shelter.

It was their signature. Not similar. Not a decent forgery. It was *theirs*—the slight upward kick on the terminal y of Galloway, the way the B leaned too far forward as if in a hurry, the small ink blot after the final stroke where a left-handed writer's hand drags through wet ink. Beatrix had signed ten thousand documents in twenty years of practice and their hand had evolved like a face evolves, the hurry increasing, the flourish dying off. This signature was the 2014 version of it, the version from the year Beatrix had hung out their own shingle, the version with the confident kick and the drag-blot. Dated March 11, 2014.

Beatrix had not signed it.

They knew that the way one knows one's own name. In March of 2014 Beatrix had been—Beatrix did the arithmetic with a cold, detached part of the mind, the part that kept working while the rest of the body went quiet—Beatrix had been in their first year of solo practice, drowning in traffic citations and a landlord dispute, and had never once heard the name Silas Merrick. The first Merrick document to cross their desk had crossed it eleven days ago, in a manila envelope with no return address, and had smelled of yesterday's ink.

But the signature was not merely good. It was *informed*. The forger had known the drag-blot. The drag-blot was the kind of detail only the hand itself knew, the private stammer of a signature, the thing no copyist studying documents would ever think to reproduce because no copyist would ever notice it.

Unless the copyist had studied the hand for years. Unless the copyist had watched Beatrix sign things, hundreds of times, at close range, with a painter's eye for the mechanics of a gesture.

Beatrix sat down. The metal chair was cold through their trousers. They turned the affidavit over, looking for anything—initials, a notary stamp, a flaw. The notary was one Delia Marsh, whose stamp Beatrix recognized; Marsh had retired in 2019 and died two years after, which meant the one living witness to this page was beyond questioning. Convenient. Everything about this file was going to be convenient, Beatrix thought. That was how you could tell it had been arranged.

They kept reading.

* * *

The petition for probate named an executor: one Ambrose Merrick, brother of the deceased, of Gull Point Road. Beatrix turned to the letters of administration and found the same name, the same road, and a signature that was Silas's own hand mirrored—Ambrose's writing was the twin of Silas's, or rather Silas's was the twin of Ambrose's, the two hands indistinguishable except that Ambrose pressed harder, the pen biting through to the next page. Beatrix could feel the grooves with a fingertip. Ambrose Merrick had signed his brother's probate papers like a man engraving a headstone.

There was no obituary copy in the file, which was odd; clerks loved obituaries, pasted them in like flowers. There was no funeral home record, no certificate of disposition of remains. The file said Silas Merrick had died, had been identified, and had been probated, and nowhere in the entire gray box did it say what had been done with the body. Beatrix went through the folder twice, then a third time, tilting pages under the lamp. Nothing. A man had died and been buried in a gap in the paperwork.

Or a man had died on paper only, and gone on living somewhere with no address, no death, and no name.

Beatrix leaned back and let the vertigo come, because fighting it was worse. It rose the way floodwater rises, silent and total, and it was not fear of the dead man or even of the living one—it was the vertigo of a person who looks down at the floor of their own life and sees that a board is missing. The past was supposed to be the one place no one could get into. It had no windows, no doors, no coat rack where a stranger could hang himself while you slept. And yet someone had been inside it. Someone had taken a year from Beatrix's own history—2014, the founding year, the year of the shingle and the first client and the cheap champagne drunk alone in a rented office—and had written themselves into it. Had written *Beatrix* into it. Had needed Beatrix in it, had needed an estate lawyer's signature on a dead man's identification affidavit ten years before Beatrix ever heard of the estate, because—

Because the will named Beatrix personally. Because the will required Beatrix to authenticate it. Because the whole architecture, the entire ten-year edifice, had been built with a load-bearing wall that ran straight through Beatrix Galloway's signature.

You don't forge a signature you might someday need to deny. You forge it so that when the moment comes, the signature's owner cannot deny it without denying themselves.

Beatrix stood, walked to the window at the end of the aisle, and looked out at the parking lot, at the sodium light and the rain starting to stipple the asphalt. Their reflection hovered faintly in the glass, a pale ghost superimposed on the dark, and Beatrix caught themself studying it the way one studies a stranger—the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head—trying to see what he had seen. What had he been looking at, all those years? What had he catalogued? The drag-blot. The kick of the y. How many other details of Beatrix's life had been entered into the record without Beatrix's knowledge, annotated in a hand that could reproduce their own?

*He's not framing you,* Beatrix thought. *Framing is crude. He's drafting you. He's been drafting you for a decade, the way you draft a will—revising, restating, adding codicils—until the final document is airtight and your name is on every page.*

The phone buzzed in their pocket. Vann. Beatrix looked at the name on the screen for a long moment and did not answer. Not yet. Not until they knew what else was in the file. A detective could be an ally or a noose, and right now Beatrix's neck was not the only thing at stake; the truth was, and the truth was still in the box.

They went back to the table.

* * *

The inventory of estate assets, filed April 2014, listed the contents of the Merrick house on Corvair Lane with a clerk's flat thoroughness. Furniture. Tools. One automobile. And then, under the heading *Artwork and Studio Contents*, a list that stopped Beatrix's breath.

*Paintings, oil on canvas, approx. forty (40), various subjects. Sketches, unframed, approx. two hundred (200). Easel, one. Pigments and solvents, assorted. One (1) portrait, unfinished, subject unidentified.*

Forty paintings. Beatrix thought of the shuttered studio, of the canvases stacked face-in like dealt cards, of the one portrait they had seen—the one of Beatrix, in the gray coat—and felt the pattern begin to assemble itself behind their eyes with the slow, horrible click of a lock turning. Forty paintings in 2014. The studio had held more than forty now; Beatrix had felt it, the sheer crowded mass of the place, canvases leaning in ranks like a congregation. He had kept painting. And the portrait of Beatrix had not been the first of its kind. It had been the latest.

The file's last documents were the closing papers: the estate settled in November of 2014, assets distributed to the executor, Ambrose Merrick, in trust. Signed, sealed, approved. A judge's stamp. A clerk's stamp. And at the bottom of the final receipt, in the line reserved for *attorney for petitioner*, a signature Beatrix had already seen once tonight and would see, they understood suddenly, for the rest of their life:

*Beatrix J. Galloway.*

Twice. He had needed them twice. The affidavit and the closing. The identification of the dead man and the distribution of his estate. The beginning of the lie and the end of it, both notarized by Beatrix's own hand, both performed by someone who knew that hand better than Beatrix did.

Beatrix sat very still and did the only thing a lawyer can do when the record turns against them: they read it again, looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the forgery would split under pressure. And they found it. Not in the signatures—those were perfect, perfect enough to be a kind of taunt—but in the dates.

The affidavit was dated March 11, 2014. The death certificate, March 9. But the certificate's filing stamp—the county's own ink, the one thing in the box the forger could not produce—was dated March 14. Three days after Beatrix's signature. Which meant the affidavit had been sworn before the certificate it identified had even been filed. Which meant the identification had been made not of a body but of a *document*. Someone had signed Beatrix's name to a paper identifying a death that had not yet entered the record—had built the identification backward from the paperwork, the way a painter builds a portrait not from the model but from the idea of the model.

He hadn't forged the past. He had *composed* it. And he had composed Beatrix into it with the loving, patient attention of a man who had all the time in the world, because the man he was composing it for was dead, and the man he was composing it as was dead too, and the only living person in the entire painting was the one whose eyes would open last.

Beatrix closed the box, retied the string in the same flat bureaucratic bow, and sat in the humming lamplight with their hands flat on the gray cardboard, feeling the ten-year silence of it pressing up against their palms.

Then they took out their phone, photographed every page, and dialed Vann.

She answered on the second ring, no greeting, as if she had been holding the phone. "Galloway."

"You need to pull the 2014 Merrick probate file before anyone else touches it," Beatrix said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt, which was the one professional skill they had never had to practice. "And Vann—the man you pulled out of the harbor. Before you tell me anything else, I need you to check something for me. His hands. Whether there's ink on them, or paint. Whether he was left-handed."

A pause, long enough for the rain to fill it. "Why left-handed?"

"Because the man who forged my signature," Beatrix said, "dragged his hand through the ink. And so do I. He learned it from watching me." They looked down at the box, at the flat bow of the string, at ten years of composed silence. "He's been watching me since before I knew his name. And I think he's been signing my name to his work the whole time."

The line was quiet. Then Vann said, "Stay by the phone," and hung up, and Beatrix sat alone in the archive of the dead, in the smell of cold paper that could no longer be trusted, and understood with perfect clarity that somewhere out in the rain, in a room with good light, a canvas was already stretched and waiting—and that the next face on it had already been chosen, and the face was theirs.
