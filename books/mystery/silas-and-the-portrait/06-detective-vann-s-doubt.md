## 6. Detective Vann's Doubt

The morgue smelled of bleach and old cold, and Ilsa Vann had learned over nineteen years that the smell never quite left her coat. She stood at the observation window above the autopsy suite and watched Dr. Ferreira work the drowned man's shoulders with the patience of a woman defusing something.

"Again," Vann said into the intercom.

Ferreira didn't look up. "You're going to keep asking until you hear it a third time?"

"I'm going to keep asking until it makes sense."

"Nothing about this one makes sense." Ferreira set down the retractor and peeled her glove back to point, though she knew Vann couldn't see the gesture through the glass. She said it anyway; they'd developed the habit years ago, two women talking across a barrier that neither could entirely see through. "Time of death, based on core temp, lividity, potassium in the vitreous — he went into the water between eleven p.m. and two a.m., four nights ago."

"And the coat?"

"The coat was dry when he was pulled out." Ferreira let that sit. "Not damp. Dry. Wool holds water like a grudge, Detective. If that man had been wearing that coat when he hit the harbor, it would have been soaked through, and it would have stayed soaked. Somebody put it on him after he died. After he was *found*, maybe. There's no saltwater penetration in the fibers at all."

Vann wrote nothing down. She didn't need to. The sentence was already carved somewhere behind her sternum, where the bad facts went.

*Somebody dressed him.*

She thanked Ferreira, signed out, and stood in the corridor for a full minute under the fluorescent hum before she moved. Because there were two ways to read what the medical examiner had just told her, and one of them made this case strange, and the other made it worse than anything she'd worked in nineteen years.

If the killer had put the coat on the body after death, then the coat wasn't evidence of contact between victim and owner. It was a costume. A prop. Which meant the drowning wasn't a crime of passion or panic or profit. It was a *staging*. Someone had looked at a dead man and seen an empty frame.

And the only other place that gray wool coat existed — the only place anyone had ever photographed it, catalogued it, seen it move through the world — was hanging in Beatrix Galloway's closet, and painted on a canvas in a dead painter's house, worn by a lawyer who hadn't known she was being watched.

Vann pushed through the exit doors into the rain.

* * *

Galloway's office sat above a shuttered bookstore on Corvane Street, up a staircase that groaned like a ship. Vann had been there twice — once officially, once not — and both times she'd noticed the same thing: the office was too clean. Not tidy. *Curated.* Files squared to the desk's edge. Pens aligned. A single photograph of a coastline, face-down since the second visit, as if Galloway had turned it over mid-conversation and never turned it back.

Tonight the lights were still on at nine-forty. Vann climbed the stairs without announcing herself, which was its own kind of test, and found the door unlocked.

"You should lock this," she said from the threshold.

Beatrix Galloway didn't startle. That bothered Vann more than screaming would have. The lawyer sat behind the desk with the original 2014 case file open — Vann's case file, the one that had gone missing from archives three weeks ago and reappeared in Galloway's possession by routes nobody would explain — and looked up with the flat, exhausted calm of someone who had stopped expecting the world to behave.

"Detective Vann." Galloway closed the file, but slowly, deliberately, so Vann could see there was nothing being hidden. Or so it seemed. "Come to arrest me? You've been circling for days."

"I've been waiting," Vann said, "for you to be worth arresting."

She came in anyway. Sat across the desk without being asked. Put the autopsy summary on the blotter between them, face-up, because tonight she wanted Galloway reading the same page she was.

"The drowned man," Vann said. "John Doe, pulled from the harbor off Pier Nine. Wearing your coat."

"My stolen coat," Galloway said. "Let's keep the distinction precise. It matters legally, and apparently it matters to whoever took it."

"It matters more than you know." Vann tapped the summary. "He died between eleven and two. He was in the water four hours before we pulled him out. And the coat — your coat — was dry. Bone dry. No salt saturation. No postmortem immersion staining on the inner lining. You know what that means?"

Galloway's hands, folded on the closed file, went very still. "Tell me."

"It means he wasn't wearing it when he died. It means somebody killed him naked, or near enough, and then *dressed* him. Dressed him in clothes lifted from your closet, to match—" Vann stopped herself. She'd rehearsed this part on the drive over and hated every version of it. "To match a painting."

The silence that followed had texture. Rain against the window. The building settling. Somewhere below, a cat arguing with a dumpster lid.

"The portrait," Galloway said finally. Her voice had dropped half a register, gone careful, the way voices go when people are walking on ice they can hear cracking. "In the studio. Me, in the gray coat."

"In the gray coat, standing at a railing. Harbor light behind you." Vann had seen the crime scene photographs of the Merrick house twice now and the painting had followed her home both times, the way certain images do. "I pulled the harbor cam footage from the night our John Doe died. Pier Nine has a railing. Harbor light behind it. Our man was found floating thirty feet off that railing, wearing your coat, positioned—"

"Positioned how?"

"Face-up. Arms at his sides. Like a figure lying on a table for a sitting." Vann leaned forward. "Here's my theory, Galloway, and it stays in this room, because if I say it upstairs in a briefing room, I get reassigned to traffic fatalities by Friday. The drowning wasn't the crime. The drowning was *rehearsal*. Somebody is staging scenes from those paintings. Using bodies. Using dead men as models."

Galloway didn't flinch. But Vann watched something move behind her eyes — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. The look of a person fitting a new piece into a puzzle they'd already started alone.

"He died before the coat went on," Galloway said quietly. "So the coat isn't linking him to me. It's linking him to the *painting*. To the series." She unfolded her hands and pressed them flat against the file, as if holding it down against wind. "Which means whoever did this doesn't care about the victims as people. They're stand-ins. They're being cast."

"That's the part I can't make fit," Vann admitted, and saying it cost her something; she did not share the parts that didn't fit, not with suspects, not with partners, not with anyone. "A killer who works from paintings is a killer who plans in months, maybe years. Those portraits go back two decades. Dozens of them. Every one of them dressed in clothes that later showed up on a corpse or a missing person. That's not compulsion. That's a *project*. And projects have a final image."

"The last canvas," Galloway said. "Me."

"You're the only living person in the whole series." Vann held her gaze. "Everyone else in those frames is already dead or disappeared. So either you're the finale, or you're the witness, and I genuinely don't know which scares me more."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the rain had thickened and the office lamp had become the only light left on Corvane Street — Vann said the thing she had come to say and had been circling for three hours like a bird over open water.

They were standing at the window now, both of them, looking down at the wet street, because it was easier than looking at each other.

"I pulled your history," Vann said. "Before I met you. Standard. Everyone connected to a case gets pulled." She watched a taxi slide past below, tires hissing. "Clean record. Twenty years practicing estate law. No debts worth mentioning, no enemies worth naming, no vices worth blackmailing. You donate to the maritime museum. You take the same vacation every September. Your life is so orderly it reads like it was designed."

"It was," Galloway said. "Order is what I built after everything else burned. You'd know that if you read the file you're accusing me of stealing."

"I read it."

"Then you know why I don't sleep well in October."

Vann turned from the window. This was the moment. She'd been carrying the question since the morgue, since before the morgue, since the first time she'd stood in that too-clean office and felt the shape of something wrong that wasn't guilt.

"Off the record," Vann said. "Truly off. No recorder, no notes, no report. If I write any of this down, I have to act on it, and I'm not ready to act, because acting on a half-theory gets innocent people destroyed. So: off the record. Do you understand me?"

"I'm a lawyer, Detective. I understand evidentiary privilege better than you do."

"Then answer me straight." Vann stepped closer. "The night John Doe died, where were you?"

"At home. Alone. Asleep, mostly badly." Galloway met her eyes without dropping them. "No witnesses. No cameras on my building's rear entrance — it's been broken since March. I have no alibi, and we both know it, and we also both know that if I were your killer, I'd have manufactured a better one. I forge documents for a living, Detective. Alibis are just documents with better lighting."

It was, infuriatingly, a good answer. Vann had interviewed four hundred liars and the good ones always gave answers that were slightly too clever, and the honest ones always gave answers that were slightly too angry, and Galloway was giving both at once, which meant either extraordinary honesty or extraordinary craft, and Vann had run out of instruments for telling them apart.

"Here's my problem," Vann said. "Every fact in this case points away from you and toward you simultaneously. Your coat on the body. Your signature on documents from 2014 you swear you never signed. Your face in a painting by a man who died before you ever took his file. Either you're the most elaborate killer I've ever encountered, or somebody has spent years constructing a version of you that fits inside a murder, and I cannot tell which woman is standing in front of me."

"No," Galloway said. "Neither can I. Some nights I'm not sure the original is still in here to check against." She said it lightly, but her hand had drifted to the desk edge, gripping it, and Vann understood suddenly that the joke was load-bearing — that underneath the composure was a person who had opened her own closet three nights ago and counted her coats twice, and stood there wondering which of her own memories might have been edited while she slept.

Vann exhaled. Made the decision the way she made all her worst decisions: quickly, on instinct, with the full knowledge that she'd spend months explaining it to herself.

"Here's what I've got that isn't in any report," she said. "The archive clerk who processed the Merrick estate inventory in 2014 — Marcus Ellery. He went missing eight days ago. Reported by his sister. The department logged it as a probable walk-away; he had debts, a divorce pending. Standard." She paused. "Our John Doe is five-eleven, brown hair, early forties, surgical scar on the right knee."

Galloway had gone white. "Ellery catalogued the paintings."

"Ellery catalogued the paintings, which means Ellery knew what every canvas depicted, which means Ellery knew the series was a kill list before anyone else did." Vann watched the understanding land, blow by blow, on the other woman's face. "And eight days after he disappears, a man matching his description floats up at Pier Nine wearing a costume from one of those canvases. I requested dental records this morning. I'll have confirmation tomorrow."

"If it's him—"

"If it's him, then the killer isn't hiding the pattern anymore. He's *performing* it. He took the one man who could read the paintings as evidence, and he turned him into a painting himself." Vann picked up her coat from the chair. "That's not a murderer covering tracks, Galloway. That's an artist approaching his deadline."

At the door, she stopped. Didn't turn around. Said it to the wood.

"I'm supposed to bring you in. My lieutenant wants it, the DA wants it, and half the guys in the squad room have a pool going on your arrest date." Her hand rested on the handle. "But the ME's findings don't fit you. A forger dresses a scene. A planner builds an alibi. Nobody who constructs a frame this elaborate drowns their model in their own stolen coat and leaves it floating for the tide to give back. Whoever did this wanted us to find it. Wanted it loud. You're too quiet for this job, Galloway. Whatever else you are — you're too quiet."

"And if I'm not?" Galloway's voice came steady from behind her. "Quiet, I mean. What then?"

Vann opened the door onto the dark stairwell, rain sound rising from the street below.

"Then I've just handed my case file to the prime suspect," she said, "and I'll find out the hard way."

She was halfway down the stairs when Galloway called after her — not loudly, but with an urgency that stopped Vann's boot on the third step.

"Detective. The file. The 2014 file." A pause, and when the voice came again it had changed, gone thin and strange. "There's a page missing. Page forty-one. It was in the archive copy — I checked it against the microfilm index myself, three weeks ago. Someone removed it after the file reached my office. And Detective — the removal slip is dated *tomorrow*."

Vann stood in the stairwell darkness with the rain coming down outside and felt the case tilt beneath her, the way a floor tilts when you realize the house is built on something hollow.

Page forty-one. She didn't know yet what was on it. But she knew, with the cold certainty of nineteen years of dead ends, that whatever it was, someone had already decided it belonged to tomorrow — and that tomorrow was coming whether she was ready for it or not.
