## 9. Gull Point

The bus stopped running to Gull Point in 2009, so Beatrix rented a car from a man named Oskar who asked no questions and charged too much for a sedan with a smell of salt and old cigarettes baked into the upholstery. The road north out of the city unspooled between hedgerows gone feral with autumn, and by the time the sea appeared—flat pewter under a sky the color of wet slate—the light was already failing.

She had brought the letters.

They rode on the passenger seat in a leather portfolio she'd taken from the estate safe, wrapped now in a cotton cloth like relics. Eleven unsent letters in Silas Merrick's hand, each addressed to *Ambrose*, each never posted because there had been nowhere to post them—to a dead man, to a brother who existed only as a death certificate dated fourteen months before Silas's own. She had read them four times since finding the safe. The fifth reading happened in her head the whole drive, sentences surfacing like drowned things: *I saw what I saw at the Point and no amount of paint will cover it.* And: *You were always the one who could walk through walls. Teach me how.* And the last one, undated, the ink feathered as if written with a shaking hand or in damp air:

*If you come back, come at night. Come the way we used to. I'll leave the lamp lit.*

The lamp. She'd checked the harbor authority's records before leaving. Gull Point Light had been automated in 1998 and decommissioned entirely in 2016, replaced by a steel skeletal beacon that blinked its idiot red eye without human attendance. But the old tower still stood at the end of the spit, white paint peeling down to gray bones, and someone—according to the same records—had leased the keeper's cottage continuously since 2011. A name appeared on the lease: **E. Halloway, Keeper (ret.)**.

Beatrix drove the last three miles with the windows down despite the cold, letting the sea air scour her. She needed her head clean. Since the break-in at her office—since that page of the 2014 file annotated in handwriting that mimicked hers so well she'd stood in her own doorway doubting which signature was real—she had stopped trusting anything that arrived without weather on it. Documents could be forged. Portraits could be staged. But a retired lighthouse keeper living alone on a wind-scoured spit had no reason to lie to her yet, and more importantly, probably no talent for it.

The road ended at a gravel lot where two gulls argued over something unidentifiable. Beyond it, a footpath crossed the spine of the spit—shingle and coarse grass, rabbit-browsed—and the tower rose at its end like a held breath.

The cottage crouched beside it, low-roofed, chimney smoking.

Beatrix knocked.

* * *

Edith Halloway answered the door the way people answer doors when they've been expecting someone for years and have simply forgotten who: without surprise, only assessment. She was small and square-built, seventy if she was a day, with a face like driftwood—weathered smooth, all soft edges long gone—and eyes the pale washed blue of sea glass. She looked at Beatrix for a long moment.

"You're not police," she said. "Police wear their shoes too new."

"Beatrix Galloway. Estate lawyer." She offered the word *estate* like a key and watched whether it turned.

Halloway's gaze flickered—just once—toward the tower. "Silas Merrick," she said. Not a question. "Come in before the wind decides it owns you."

Inside, the cottage smelled of peat smoke and paraffin and, faintly, of oil paint—a ghost of turpentine under everything, so faint Beatrix might have imagined it. The front room was small and fiercely tidy: a stove, a table, two chairs, shelves of logbooks going back decades, their spines labeled in a precise hand. On the wall above the hearth hung a single painting.

Beatrix stopped.

It was the lighthouse, of course—but painted from the water side, at night, in a storm. The tower leaned into the wind like a man walking against it, and the beam cut the canvas diagonally, and beneath the beam, small and dark, were two figures on the rocks. Boys, by their size. One pointing up at the light. One looking back over his shoulder at the sea, as if he'd heard something in it.

"Silas painted that," Halloway said, watching her. "Nineteen ninety-one. He was eleven. He gave it to me the day his father signed the papers to move the family inland. Said—" her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile, "—said I should keep it where the weather couldn't get at it."

"You knew him as a boy."

"I knew both of them as boys. Silas and Ambrose. Twins, but you'd never have known it to look at them after they turned ten. Silas went pale and quiet, all inside himself. Ambrose went loud. Loud boys are easy to love and hard to see." She poured tea without asking, set the cup in Beatrix's hands. Her grip was strong; the cup didn't tremble. "You've come about the night."

"The night," Beatrix repeated.

"October 2013. You'll have read about it in your files, or you wouldn't be here." Halloway sat across from her, hands folded, a woman settling in for a story she had told herself many times and almost never aloud. "There was a party up at the Vane house. The Vanes owned half this coast then—shipping money, older than sin and twice as respectable. Their son Rowan threw parties the whole county pretended not to know about. That October night there was a storm coming in off the Atlantic, a bad one, and the party spilled out onto the point anyway. Young people do that. They think weather is a rumor."

Beatrix set down the portfolio. "What did you see?"

"From the gallery. I was on watch—not required anymore, but forty years of habit doesn't retire just because the lamp does. Storm came in around eleven. By midnight the wind was screaming off the water and I saw headlights on the coast road, coming fast, faster than anyone should drive in that. Then nothing. Lights out. And then—" Halloway's voice flattened, became the voice of reportage, of logbook entries, of a woman trained to record exactly what she saw and nothing she hadn't. "Then I saw a figure come down the path from the road end. Running. Fell twice on the shingle. Got up. Came to the tower door and hammered on it like the sea itself was after him."

"Silas."

"That's what he said his name was." The pale eyes fixed on her. "But I'd known those boys twenty years, Mrs. Galloway. I'd watched them climb my stairs a hundred times. And the young man dripping on my floor that night looked at me with Ambrose's eyes and said he was Silas, and I let him say it, because whatever he was running from, he needed to not be himself for a while. You understand? There are nights when a person gets to choose which twin survives."

Beatrix's tea had gone cold in her hands. She set it down. "What was he running from?"

"A crash. Up on the coast road. Two cars—I heard it, or heard the aftermath, metal and then that terrible quiet storms make. He came down the path saying *there's been an accident, there's been an accident, I saw it, I saw who—*" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. "And then he stopped saying it. Mid-sentence. Like a man catching himself falling. After that he said only that he'd found a body. Only ever a body. Never *who*. Never *whose fault*."

"And the body?"

"Rowan Vane's passenger. A girl from the city, nineteen, working a summer at the Vane house. The official finding was that she'd run onto the road. The second car was never identified. Hit and run, they called it, though everyone knew whose son had been driving, and everyone knew the Vanes' lawyers would make sure knowing stayed useless." Halloway looked into the fire. "The girl's family got a settlement and silence. The file closed. And the boy who knocked on my door stayed in the keeper's cottage for six weeks, sleeping in that chair, painting nothing—which for Silas Merrick was like a heart not beating—and then one morning he was gone, and a week later the news said the painter Silas Merrick had died abroad, quietly, and there was a funeral with a closed coffin, and I stood at the back of it and did not cry, because I did not believe a single word of it."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, a gull cried once and gave up.

"Why?" Beatrix asked. It came out rougher than she intended. "Why fake his own death? If he witnessed the accident—if he was the victim of something, why erase himself instead of testifying?"

Halloway turned the pale eyes on her again, and for a moment Beatrix saw the watcher behind them, the forty years of watching water and weather and the small boats of human intention go out and not all come back.

"Because of what he said he saw," she said quietly. "Who was driving the second car. Because the name he said that night was a name that would have gotten him killed, or worse, disbelieved into ruin, and he knew it even at nineteen, soaked and shaking on my floor. Because the Vanes weren't just rich, Mrs. Galloway. They were owed. Half this coast felt it in its bones—that family collected debts the way other families collect photographs." She paused. "And because the person driving that second car was not a stranger to the Merrick twins. That's what broke him. Not fear. Recognition."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship's cabin does when the swell changes. Beatrix gripped the arm of the chair.

"Who," she said. "Who was driving?"

Halloway was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had the flatness of a woman setting down something heavy she has carried too far already.

"He never told me the name. He told me something better and worse. He told me, the last morning, standing at that door with his bag packed, that he'd finally understood why his father had taught them both to forge signatures. Old Merrick was a sign painter—did you know that? Shopfronts, ledgers, legal notices. Taught his sons lettering like other fathers teach fishing. And Silas said to me, standing right there—" she nodded at the door, at the gray light beyond it, "—he said, *'Edie, my father didn't teach us to write. He taught us to disappear into other people's hands.'* And then he walked out into the weather and became his brother, and his brother had died eight months before, and nobody, nowhere, ever checked."

Beatrix sat very still. The letters in the portfolio seemed to warm against her knee like something alive.

"There's one more thing," Halloway said. "You should have it, because I'm eighty-one next spring and the sea keeps what it's given long enough." She rose, went to the shelf of logbooks, and drew out a slim volume that did not match the others—no printed spine label, just a date range in pencil: *Oct–Dec 2013*. She opened it to a page near the middle and laid it on the table in front of Beatrix.

It was a log entry in Halloway's precise hand, recording wind speed, visibility, barometric pressure for the night of the accident. But at the bottom of the page, below the formal entry, was a single line in a different hand—loose, hurried, the letters slanting as if written by someone leaning away from the page:

*I saw the driver's face. It was like looking in a mirror. —A.M.*

Beatrix stared at it. *Like looking in a mirror.* A twin. The driver had been—

No. Not the twins themselves. Someone else who shared a face with someone. Or—

Her mind snagged, slipped, caught. The third sibling. The name that had surfaced in the buried settlement papers, the child struck from every will, erased from every record—the gap in the family tree shaped exactly like a person. If the hidden sibling was also a Merrick, also carried the bloodline, also carried the face—

Then *like looking in a mirror* meant the driver had worn the twins' face. Meant the crash that destroyed everything had been caused by the invisible brother. Meant Silas hadn't fled a stranger's crime.

He had fled his own family's.

"Mrs. Galloway." Halloway's voice came from far away. "You've gone the color of the horizon. Sit back down."

Beatrix didn't sit. She was looking at the logbook line again, and beneath her fear another feeling was assembling itself, cold and structural, like a keel being laid: the killer had spent years dressing bodies in stolen clothes, staging scenes, completing a series. Rehearsing. And rehearsals are for a performance that hasn't happened yet. A recreation. Of a crash. With a specific driver. A specific debt.

The killer wasn't finishing Silas's paintings.

He was finishing Silas's testimony.

"Did he ever come back?" Beatrix asked. "After 2013. Did either of them ever come back here?"

Halloway's face did something complicated—grief and guilt sharing the same small space. "Once," she said. "Two years ago. Winter. A man came up the path at dusk wearing a caretaker's coat, said he was doing an inventory of the property for the estate. He stood where you're standing. He looked at the painting over the hearth for a long time." Her voice dropped. "And when he left, I noticed the logbook was out of place. I found that line added that same night. In a hand I'd last seen on a storm-lit floor in 2013."

Beatrix's mouth had gone dry. "Describe him."

"Big shoulders, careful hands. Soft-spoken. Kept his cap low." Halloway met her eyes. "And Mrs. Galloway—he thanked me for the tea I never offered him. Knew how I took mine. Knew the cottage. Knew the *tower*." She glanced toward the blackening window, where the first spit of rain had begun ticking against the glass. "Whatever you're carrying in that portfolio, you should know: whoever wore that coat didn't come here to remember. He came here to check whether the past was still where he'd left it."

Outside, the storm that had chased Beatrix up the coast finally arrived, slamming rain against the cottage in sheets, and in the sudden roar she heard—or thought she heard, or would swear forever after that she heard—the gravel of the lot shift under tires that were not hers.

She moved to the window. Down the darkening spit, at the edge of the lot, headlights swept once across the tower's peeling flank and went out.

An engine ticked over in the rain. Idling. Waiting to see if the light in the keeper's cottage would go out too.

* * *

Beatrix killed her own lamp and stood in the dark with Edith Halloway's hand steady on her shoulder, watching the shape of a car resolve and not resolve in the rain, and understood with total clarity that the case file had led her here not because she had followed it well—

but because she had been allowed to.
