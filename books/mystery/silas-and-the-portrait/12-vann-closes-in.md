## 12. Vann Closes In

The morgue smelled of bleach and something older, something that no amount of chemical scrubbing would ever reach—the smell of the harbor itself, of everything the water gave back when it was done keeping secrets.

Detective Ilsa Vann stood at the viewing window with a paper cup of vending machine coffee going cold in her hand and watched the pathologist work on the man from the pier. The body lay under harsh light, and even after nine days in cold saltwater, even with what the crabs and the current had done to the face, there was something about him that nagged at her. Not recognition exactly. Familiarity. The way you know you've seen a face before but can't place where, and it sits in the back of your skull like a stone in a shoe.

"Run his prints again," she said through the intercom.

Dr. Okafor looked up, scalpel poised. "We ran them twice. No hits. He's not in AFIS, not in IAFIS, not in any database we subscribe to."

"Then run them again."

"You know what they say about doing the same thing—"

"I don't care what they say." Vann set down the coffee. "Nobody drowns in this harbor without leaving a shadow somewhere. Rent records. Utility bills. A library card. Somebody misses this man."

Okafor considered her for a moment, then shrugged and turned back to the table. "There is one thing," he said, almost as an afterthought, and Vann felt the familiar tightening behind her sternum that meant the case had just moved. "The hands."

He lifted the dead man's right hand into the light. The fingers were long, ink-stained at the cuticles in a way that survived decomposition, and the fingertips were calloused in a pattern Vann had seen described in old case files but never on a living body: the pads worn smooth and slightly flattened, the way they get when someone spends years turning pages, thousands of pages, tens of thousands.

"A reader?" she asked.

"An archivist," Okafor said. "Or a scholar. Someone who handled paper professionally. And look here—" he rotated the wrist gently, exposing the inner forearm, where the skin had sloughed but the dermis beneath held its record. A faded tattoo, mostly destroyed by the water, but legible: a small compass rose, and beneath it, in letters gone blurry, what might have been *G.P.*

Vann stared at it for a long moment.

Gull Point.

* * *

She spent the afternoon pulling missing persons reports, and by four o'clock she had him: Daniel Reyes-Kim, forty-one, last seen eleven days ago leaving the county records annex at 6:40 p.m., security footage showing him carrying two archival boxes toward the parking structure he never reached. Reported missing by his sister three days later. The report had been flagged, cross-referenced against the harbor John Doe, and dismissed—wrong height, wrong age on first glance, face too far gone for family ID.

But the tattoo matched. Gull Point Lighthouse Historical Society, volunteer docent, 2016–2019. And the hands matched. And the teeth, once Okafor pulled the records from a dentist in Ashford, matched beyond any reasonable doubt.

Daniel Reyes-Kim had spent the last six years of his life as a contract archivist, and one of his contracts, listed in his résumé with the bland pride of a man who loved his work, was *Estate of S. Merrick — cataloguing and preservation survey, private commission, 2019–2021.*

Vann sat in her car outside the annex for a long time after reading that line, watching the rain start, not moving.

Two years. Two years inside the Merrick house, handling every document, every canvas, every scrap of paper Silas Merrick—or whoever Silas Merrick had been—had ever touched. Two years of learning where everything was kept, what everything meant, which paintings hung over which secrets.

And then he'd died wearing Beatrix Galloway's coat.

She called the estate lawyer's office. It rang through to voicemail. She tried the cell. Voicemail again. She sat with the phone in her lap and the rain ticking on the roof and thought about the portrait she'd seen photographs of—a canvas of Beatrix Galloway in a gray coat, painted with the kind of attention that took years—and she understood, with the cold certainty that sometimes arrives all at once, fully formed, like ice across a pond, that the drowned man in the morgue was not the target.

He was the rehearsal.

* * *

She found Beatrix Galloway at the harbor, of all places, standing at the rail near the pier where the body had been pulled from the water, watching the fishing boats come in with their lights doubled on the wet surface. Rain beaded on the lawyer's shoulders. Vann had learned enough about Beatrix Galloway in the past week to know the visits weren't morbid. They were investigative. The lawyer came to the place where the story had surfaced and stood there until it told her something.

"You're a hard woman to reach," Vann said, coming up beside her.

"My office was broken into Tuesday night." Beatrix didn't look at her. "I've been working from my apartment. And before you ask—no, nothing was stolen. Something was left. The original 2014 file on Silas Merrick's death, opened to page forty-seven, annotated in handwriting that looks like mine."

"It isn't yours."

"It isn't mine."

Vann nodded slowly. She'd read the incident report; she'd seen the photographs of the page. The annotations were good. Disturbingly good. The kind of forgery that required study, patience, affection, almost. You didn't learn to write like another person overnight. You learned it the way you learned a language—by immersion, by love, by copying them until your own hand forgot how to exist.

"We identified your drowning victim," Vann said.

Beatrix turned then. In the sodium light her face was pale and set, and Vann saw that the fear she'd glimpsed in the interview room a week ago had been burned down to something harder underneath. Char, not ash.

"His name was Daniel Reyes-Kim," Vann continued. "Archivist. He catalogued the Merrick estate between 2019 and 2021. Private commission. He vanished eleven days ago, and he's been in the water most of that time."

"The coat—"

"He went into the harbor without it. The medical examiner found lividity patterns inconsistent with the garment—he was dressed after death. Someone put your coat on a dead man and arranged him at the end of Pier Four like a figure in a tableau." Vann paused. "Like a painting."

The rain filled the silence between them. Down the pier, a trawler bumped against its mooring, and somewhere a gull screamed once and quit.

"There are more," Beatrix said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"In the studio, you said. Dozens of portraits. Strangers in specific clothing." Vann had spent two nights since Beatrix's call cross-referencing, running names and dates through databases that groaned and coughed up fragments. "I've confirmed eleven so far. Eleven bodies over twenty-two years, recovered within weeks of portraits depicting them in clothing later found on or near the remains. Drownings, mostly. All accidental-looking. All ruled accidents, because nobody was looking for a pattern, because the victims didn't know each other, because the deaths were scattered across three counties and nobody thinks to connect a retired teacher in Ashford to a deckhand in Port Ellery to a—" she stopped herself.

"To an archivist," Beatrix finished.

"To an archivist who knew where everything in that house was kept." Vann turned to face her fully. "Here's what I think happened. Reyes-Kim catalogued that studio. He saw the portraits. Maybe he understood what they were—maybe he was the first person in twenty years to see the series whole, to stand back far enough to see the pattern. And instead of going to the police, he started making calls. Quiet ones. Records requests. He pulled the deed history on the Merrick property. He requested the 2013 marine incident report from Gull Point. He was building a file."

"And whoever killed him knew he was building it."

"Whoever killed him watched him build it." Vann let that land. "The same way someone has been watching you, Ms. Galloway. The same way someone painted you in a coat from your closet and then stole the coat and put it on a corpse. This isn't random. This isn't opportunistic. This is a man completing something, and you—" she hesitated, hating the sentence even as she built it, hating more that it was true, "—you're the final panel."

Beatrix's jaw tightened. "Because I'm authenticating the will."

"Because you're the only person alive whose signature can make the will real. Whoever holds that estate legally holds the studio, the paintings, the name—all of it, laundered through probate, made clean. Or if you void the will, the whole architecture collapses and the name dies for good. Either way, everything runs through you." Vann shook her head. "You're not a witness anymore. You're the decision. And the man we're looking for needs you to make it—or needs you gone before you can."

* * *

They walked back along the promenade together, an unlikely pair—the detective and her prime suspect, side by side in the rain, both knowing how it looked to anyone who saw them. Vann had already taken heat for it. Her lieutenant had asked, twice, why she was spending so much time with the lawyer who'd reported a break-in at her own office and couldn't produce a clean alibi for the night Reyes-Kim disappeared.

Because Beatrix Galloway had no alibi. That was the ugly arithmetic of it. Eleven days ago, on the night the archivist vanished, Beatrix had been alone, working late, phone off, door locked, exactly as she claimed—and exactly as a killer would claim.

And yet.

Vann had been a cop for nineteen years. She had developed, over those years, a sense she couldn't defend in court and never tried to: a tuning fork in the chest that hummed when a suspect's story was built and went quiet when it was true. Sitting across from Beatrix Galloway in three interviews now, the fork had never hummed. The woman's fear was real. Worse—it was precise. She wasn't afraid of being caught. She was afraid of being *written*, of being edited into someone else's narrative, and Vann recognized that fear because she'd seen it before, in witnesses of crimes so intimate that the violation outlasted the violence.

Someone was authoring Beatrix Galloway. Page by page. Coat by coat. Signature by signature.

"I need to show you something," Vann said, stopping under the awning of the closed chowder house. She took out her phone and opened the file she'd assembled that afternoon—the spreadsheet, the map, the photographs laid in sequence. Twenty-two years of strangers in borrowed clothes. "Reyes-Kim figured this out before we did. That's why he's dead. But he left a trail, and I've been following it since this morning. His sister says he was obsessive about backups. Cloud storage, encrypted drives, the works. We served a warrant on his accounts at noon."

"And?"

"And there's a drive we haven't found. His calendar shows a meeting scheduled for the day after he disappeared—location entered as just an address." She turned the screen toward Beatrix. "Twelve Gull Point Road. The lighthouse keeper's cottage."

Beatrix went very still. "That's where they grew up. The twins."

"That's where it started, whatever *it* is. And our killer knows Reyes-Kim was asking questions about it, which means the killer either followed him there or—" Vann exhaled. "Or the killer *is* connected to it. Family. History. Someone who grew up in that weather."

She pocketed the phone. The rain had thickened, drumming on the awning, running off the edge in a beaded curtain between them and the dark water.

"Here's what keeps me up tonight," Vann said. "Eleven bodies in twenty-two years. That's a pace. Roughly one every two years. Disciplined. Patient. A man who can wait two years between... performances doesn't rush. But in the last month, he's killed an archivist, forged a will, broken into your office, planted evidence, and delivered a painting of you asleep in your own bed. He's accelerating. Something changed. Something scared him or excited him, and either way, his timeline collapsed."

"The will," Beatrix said. "Silas resurfacing. Me."

"You. You're the variable. And men like this don't tolerate variables." Vann met her eyes. "So I'm going to say the thing I've been circling for ten minutes, and I want you to hear it without flinching. Based on the acceleration curve, based on the staging, based on the fact that he's already rehearsed your death once with a stand-in body and your actual coat—I give it days. Not weeks. Days before he moves on you for real. Either to kill you, or to destroy your credibility so thoroughly that killing you becomes unnecessary. And I can't protect you around the clock, because half my chain of command still thinks you're my best suspect."

"What do you need from me?"

"The truth, fast, and all of it. Every document, every letter, every name. The twins, the lighthouse, the family that paid the hush money—everything you've dug up, I want it tonight, because tomorrow morning I'm driving to Gull Point, and I'd rather walk into that cottage knowing what's buried there than find out the way Reyes-Kim did."

Beatrix was quiet for a moment. Rain ran down her face and she didn't wipe it away.

"There's something else," she said finally. "Something I haven't told anyone. The night the painting arrived—the one of me asleep—I checked my building's security footage. My hallway camera was disabled for ninety minutes. But the camera in the stairwell, the one angled at the fire door—" she pulled out her own phone, thumbed through to a photograph, and handed it over. "I got one frame."

Vann looked down at the image: grainy, high-contrast, a figure caught mid-turn on the landing, face averted, tall and narrow in a dark coat. Ordinary. Forgettable. Except for the hands—one raised, braced against the wall, and on the ring finger, catching the stairwell light, a signet ring with a crest Vann had seen before, rendered in oil paint, hanging in a photograph of the Merrick studio: a gull above crossed keys.

The Merrick family crest.

"Get me everything tonight," Vann said, handing the phone back, her voice low and flat. "And Ms. Galloway—until this is over, don't sleep alone, don't answer your door, and don't go anywhere near that studio."

Too late, she saw, by the look on Beatrix Galloway's face. Too late by weeks. The lawyer was already halfway inside the painting, and the painter knew it, and somewhere across the rain-dark city he was mixing colors for the final panel—waiting, patient as varnish, for the sitting to begin.
