## 18. Night in the Studio

The studio smelled of linseed and dust and ten years of held breath.

Beatrix had arrived at four in the afternoon, while the light was still honest, so that anyone watching from the tree line would see exactly what they were meant to see: a lawyer with a document case, walking up the gravel drive of the Merrick house as if summoned by routine. They let themselves in with the executor's key, moved through the rooms turning on lamps one by one, and stood at the foot of the stairs for a long moment before climbing to the sealed door at the end of the hall.

The false claim had gone out that morning through three channels, each one leaky on purpose. The original 2013 case file—the real one, the one that named names—had been found. It would be authenticated publicly, tomorrow, here, in Silas Merrick's studio, before witnesses and cameras. Beatrix had spoken the lie aloud twice on the phone, and both times their voice had held steady, which frightened them more than shaking would have. Somewhere in the last month they had learned to lie like a person who had been lied to for years: fluently, and without pleasure.

Now they waited for dark.

* * *

They had prepared the room the way one prepares a stage, because that was what it was—a stage built inside a killer's own theater. The case file lay open on the worktable beneath a single lamp, its pages weighted with a jar of brushes, looking found rather than placed. Vann's people had wanted to fill the room with officers; Beatrix had argued them down to two, tucked into the deep alcove behind the stacked canvases where the shadows pooled like standing water. Vann herself would be outside, close enough to move in ninety seconds, far enough not to spook him. "He'll smell a crowd," Beatrix had said. "He's spent his whole life smelling crowds." Vann hadn't liked it. She'd agreed anyway, which was its own kind of confession—that the theory only worked if the bait was genuine, and the bait could only be genuine if it was Beatrix, alone, in the dark, in a house owned by a dead man who had never existed.

Beatrix turned off the lamp over the worktable and sat down on an old wooden stool in the corner, half behind an easel, and let the darkness come up around them like water.

The portraits watched from every wall.

In daylight they were horrors arranged in sequence—strangers in coats and scarves and boots, each canvas a rehearsal for a corpse. In the dark they became something else: a congregation. Faces softened into suggestion. Beatrix found their eyes moving from canvas to canvas, counting, cataloguing, doing the detective's work because the detective's work was all that kept the fear in its cage. Forty-one figures. Vann had matched nineteen to bodies. The rest were still waiting somewhere in the record to be recognized, or were waiting still to happen.

"You're not going to be forty-two," Beatrix said quietly, to no one, to themselves. Their breath made a small ghost in the cold air.

The house ticked and settled. Somewhere below, a shutter knocked against its frame in the wind coming off the harbor. Beatrix had turned off their phone at the gate; the only light now was the gray seep under the door and, after a while, the moon finding the tall window and laying a pale lane across the floorboards like a path only one person would walk.

They thought about the caretaker.

It had taken the matriarch's trembling admission and two days of records to find him, and once found he was everywhere and nowhere—the quiet man in the estate invoices, paid monthly for twenty-two years, *groundskeeping and maintenance*, signature reduced to initials. E. Merrick. He had signed himself into the margins of his brothers' erasure and stayed there, sweeping leaves off a grave that wasn't his, tending the house of the man whose name he would one day need. He had been in the drive when Beatrix first arrived. He had carried the ladder. He had said, *Mind the third step, it's loose,* and smiled with such plain kindness that Beatrix had thanked him.

That smile was the thing Beatrix kept returning to, sitting in the dark among the portraits. Not the drownings, not the forged hand, not the second portrait of themselves asleep—but the smile, and the loose step, and the way he'd said *mind* as though minding things were his whole vocation. A life spent as a copy teaches you care, maybe. Teaches you that the world is full of steps that will betray people who aren't watching.

Beatrix watched.

* * *

At 11:40, the gravel spoke.

Not footsteps—a single shift of weight, out near the gate, the sound a patient man makes when he has been patient for twenty-two years and can be patient for eleven more minutes. Beatrix's heart did not race; it slowed, strangely, the way water goes still before it freezes. In the alcove behind the canvases, nothing moved, which meant the two hidden officers had heard it too and were holding their discipline.

Then the front door.

He didn't force it. Of course he didn't—he had a key older than Beatrix's career. The hinges breathed. Footsteps crossed the hall below, unhurried, and paused at the bottom of the stairs, and Beatrix understood that he was listening the way they had listened, letting the house tell him what it held. The third step creaked under his weight when he climbed, right where he'd warned them it would, and Beatrix felt something almost like laughter move through their chest and die there.

The light under the studio door went dark as his body blocked it.

A long pause. Then the key—the other key, the one for the seal—and the lock turning with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door opening inward, and the moonlight widening across the floor as though the room were inhaling.

He came in carrying a flashlight with the lens hooded, so that only a coin of dim amber touched the boards ahead of him. He was smaller than Beatrix remembered from the drive, or perhaps the memory had inflated him; grief and terror do that to a figure. He wore a workman's coat, dark, unremarkable, the coat of a man whose profession is not being noticed. He closed the door behind him. He stood in the middle of his brothers' studio, in the middle of the congregation of faces he had painted—or some of them, some of them were his, Beatrix was nearly sure now, the later ones, cruder, more furious—and he looked at the case file glowing under its lamp like an altar.

"Hello, Edmund," Beatrix said.

The flashlight came up fast, but the beam swung past them, searching the corner, finding the easel, the stool, the shape of a person sitting very still with hands visible and empty. The coin of amber steadied on Beatrix's face.

For a moment neither of them moved. The wind worried the shutters downstairs. One of the hidden officers shifted by a fraction—canvas whispering against canvas—and the caretaker's head turned toward the sound, then back, and Beatrix saw him decide, with terrible economy, that whatever else was in this room, the file came first.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. His voice was mild. That was the worst of it—it was the voice from the drive, the voice that had said *mind the third step*. "This isn't your part."

"I'm afraid it is." Beatrix rose slowly from the stool, keeping their hands where he could see them. "You wrote me into it. Ten years ago, at least. My signature's all over the 2014 file, and I never signed anything. You taught my hand to yourself before you ever used it. Why?"

"Because the record needed a witness," he said simply, "and you were the only honest one I could find."

He began to move—not toward Beatrix, toward the table, toward the file, sidelong, the way men move around animals they don't trust. The flashlight stayed on Beatrix's face, pinning them the way he pinned strangers to canvas: with attention, with that awful patient study.

"The file isn't real," Beatrix said. "You know that. I leaked it. There is no recovered 2013 file. There's only what I put on that table tonight, and none of it matters unless someone says it does."

"I know." He reached the table. His free hand hovered over the pages without touching them, the way a man hovers over a flame. "I've known since Tuesday. But you'd say it publicly tomorrow. You'd authenticate it. And an authenticated lie becomes the truth forever—that's the whole lesson of my family, counselor. My brothers proved it. My father proved it, painting signs for people who needed the truth repainted." His thumb finally came down on the paper, and something in his shoulders loosened, as if touching it completed him. "So I only had to stop you from saying it. Or make sure that when you said it, no one believed you anymore. Either way, the family stays buried, and Gull Point keeps its clean white face."

"And the drowned man? The archivist?" Beatrix took one step forward, into the edge of the lamplight, making themselves seen. "He catalogued your brothers' estate. He knew the inventory. Was he a rehearsal too, or just housekeeping?"

The flashlight trembled—once, barely—and Beatrix saw the crack run through the mildness, quick as a flaw in glaze.

"He was a rehearsal," Edmund said. "They're all rehearsals. Don't you understand yet? Every one of them is the night at the lighthouse, done again until I get it right." His voice didn't rise; it condensed, dropping lower, denser. "Two men went out onto the gallery in the storm. Only one came back down. My brother Ambrose pushed him—or he fell, or Ambrose let him fall, I was thirty feet away in the rain and I will never know which, and that *not knowing* is the thing I've been painting for twenty-two years. The Wexford family paid to bury it. Silas paid with his name—he erased himself so the money would flow and the questions would stop. And I got the part nobody wants. I got to remember."

"So you dress bodies in stolen coats," Beatrix said, "and float them in the harbor, and paint them first."

"I *fit* them first." For the first time his voice cracked open and something howled in the space behind it. "Do you think I want to? Each one is closer. Each one gets the rain right, the angle right, the weight of a falling man right. When it's perfect—" He stopped. The flashlight lowered, and in the sudden dimness Beatrix saw his eyes go to the far wall, to the newest canvas there, draped in cloth, the one Beatrix had refused to uncover. "When it's perfect, it won't be a stranger anymore. It'll be one of them. Ambrose or Silas, falling the way I saw it. And then I can stop."

"But instead you painted me."

"You weren't supposed to be in the series." He said it like an accusation, like Beatrix had broken a rule of composition. "You were supposed to authenticate the will. That's all. The will was the ending—Silas's name restored, the estate acknowledged, the record corrected at last. You were the honest one. You were the *witness*." The word came out raw. "And then you started asking questions. You started pulling threads. And I realized the only way to stop a witness is to discredit one, or bury one, and I couldn't—I found I couldn't do either one to you. So I painted you instead. Asleep. Safe. The only portrait in the room I ever made out of—"

He caught himself. Too late. The word had already left him, hanging in the cold air between them, and Beatrix heard it land: *care.*

"That's why the second portrait came from inside my apartment," Beatrix said quietly. "You didn't break in to threaten me. You broke in because you couldn't stop watching the one person in this whole rotten story who was telling the truth."

Edmund Merrick set the flashlight down on the worktable, beside the file, and Beatrix saw his hands in the lamplight—painter's hands, caretaker's hands, scarred and steady—and saw them begin, very slightly, to shake.

"They're going to take me," he said. It wasn't a question. He had known since the gravel spoke; he had walked in anyway, because the file was here, and a man who has spent his life completing a series cannot leave a page unfinished. "Vann's outside. She's been outside since dusk—I heard her car cooling when I came up the drive. You planned this like one of my paintings, counselor. Every object in its place. Even the light." He looked around the studio then, really looked—at the forty-one faces, at the draped canvas, at Beatrix standing in the moonlane with their hands open—and something moved across his face that Beatrix would spend months trying to name and would finally, in a courtroom transcript, call relief.

"Before they come in," he said, "answer me one thing. You're the honest one. Everyone says it. Your whole life is the record—you keep it, you defend it, you'd die before you falsified a line of it." He stepped back from the table, away from the file, and folded his shaking hands like a man at a graveside. "So tell me what happens to my brothers' names now. Silas, who died to save a lie. Ambrose, who killed—or didn't—to keep one. If you void the will, they stay erased. If you honor it, a murderer inherits a museum of his victims. You're the author of the ending, Beatrix Galloway. Tell me how it goes."

The door behind him opened without a sound. Vann filled it, weapon low, two officers fanning wide behind her, and the alcove emptied its shadows into the lamplight, and Edmund Merrick did not turn around. He kept his eyes on Beatrix, waiting, the way a sitter waits for the painter to decide.

Beatrix looked at the forty-one faces. At the draped canvas. At the man who had spent twenty-two years invisible, asking to be seen at last—even if seeing meant the end.

"It goes honestly," Beatrix said. "Every name. Every night. Starting with yours."

Edmund Merrick closed his eyes. And in the stillness before Vann's officers reached him, he began, softly, to describe the night at Gull Point lighthouse—as if, having finally been asked, he could no longer bear to keep it.
