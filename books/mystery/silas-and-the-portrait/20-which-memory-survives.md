## 20. Which Memory Survives

The holding cell at Harrow Point had no windows, but the man inside had painted windows on the wall of his mind for so long that he sat facing the blank cinderblock as if it overlooked the sea. They had taken his name in three registers. He had given three different answers, and all of them were true, and that was the problem Beatrix Galloway had been hired—by fate, by a dead man's ink, by whatever force arranged such things—to solve.

She watched him through the glass with Vann at her shoulder, and the detective said nothing for a long time, which was Vann's way of saying everything.

"He hasn't asked for a lawyer," Beatrix said.

"He's asked for a brush. Twice." Vann's voice was gravel smoothed by exhaustion. "Judge said no. Said a man who paints corpses doesn't get to keep his hands busy." She shifted her weight. "You don't have to go in there, Galloway. You've given your statement. The recorder caught all of it. Forty minutes of him explaining how he drowned a man in a stolen coat because the composition required it."

"He wasn't explaining to me. He was *correcting* me." Beatrix remembered it still—the studio at midnight, the smell of turpentine and old varnish, the caretaker's coveralls traded for a shirt that fit him the way Silas Merrick's memory fit him: imperfectly, deliberately, like a coat cut for a twin. "Every time I got a detail wrong, he flinched. Like I was defacing something."

"Because he spent thirty years getting the details right." Vann turned from the glass. "The D.A. wants him as the Merrick killer. Clean, simple, one name on the indictment. But he's given us three names, and the paperwork on two of them says they're dead." She looked at Beatrix then, and there was something in her face Beatrix had learned to read as respect, though Vann would have denied it under oath. "And there's one document in this whole mess that decides which name he dies under. And you're the only person in this state legally empowered to touch it."

Beatrix had known this moment was coming since the recorder clicked off in the studio. She had felt it waiting for her like a signature waiting for a page. But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different professions entirely.

"I need to see the will," she said.

* * *

They brought it to her in the conference room, in an evidence sleeve, though it was her own handling of it that had first made it evidence. Silas Merrick's last will and testament. Dated three days before the drowned man surfaced in her stolen coat. Signed in a hand that the document examiner, a patient woman named Okonkwo with ink-stained cuffs, had authenticated as *genuine*—which was the trap inside the gift, because the hand was genuine the way a forgery can be genuine: trained, perfected, inherited from a sign painter father who taught his sons that a name was just a shape, and a shape could be owned by anyone steady enough to hold a brush.

Beatrix laid the page flat and read it the way she had read ten thousand wills, and for the first time in her career the words refused to stay words.

*I, Silas Ambrose Merrick, being of sound mind—*

There it was. The crack in the stone. Not Silas. Not Ambrose. Both. A name that had never existed on any birth certificate, any census, any gravestone—a name assembled from the two brothers the way a forger assembles a signature, one stroke from here, one stroke from there, until the whole reads true to an untrained eye.

*—do revoke all prior testamentary instruments—*

There had been no prior instruments. Silas had died intestate in 2014, a death that had never happened, witnessed by a signature that Beatrix now knew was her own—or close enough to her own that the younger Beatrix, twenty-six and drowning in her first probate file, had signed documents she could not remember signing. The case file knew. The case file had always known. He had been writing her into the record for years the way he wrote figures into canvases: patiently, in layers, waiting for the varnish to set.

*—and do leave the whole of my estate, real and personal, including the house at Verrin Lane, the studio and its contents, to my executor and sole witness, Beatrix Galloway, in trust—*

In trust. For what? The will ran to eleven pages and she had read them all before, but she read the trust provisions again now, and they were the strangest part, stranger than the name, stranger than the date. The estate was not left to her. It was left *through* her—to the memory of Silas Merrick, painter, drowned in the harbor in 2013 at the age of thirty-one, whose death certificate was a forgery and whose life had been a forgery and whose grave at Gull Point, which Beatrix had visited in the rain, held a coffin filled with river stones.

*—that my name may be restored to the record, and my work entered into it, and the truth of what was witnessed at Gull Point on the fourteenth of October entered with it.*

The fourteenth of October. The night of the storm. The night the Aldous family's car had gone off the lighthouse road with two teenagers inside, and one of the Merrick twins had seen it happen and seen who was driving, and had understood, at nineteen years old, that the truth would cost more than his life—it would cost his *name*, and so he had paid with the name instead.

Except the confession had untangled even that. It hadn't been Silas at the lighthouse road. It had been Ambrose. Silas had taken the guilt the way he took everything—quietly, into the studio, onto canvas—and Ambrose had taken the escape, and the third twin, the erased one, the one no will had ever mentioned, had taken the debt. Thirty years of debt. Thirty years of painting strangers in clothes that would later dress their corpses, rehearsing the accident over and over, each drowned body a study for the final composition, until the final composition required a witness who could make the record itself testify.

Beatrix had been that witness. She was that witness still.

She sat in the conference room with the will under the fluorescent hum and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she was not deciding a case. She was deciding a *man*. Which man. Which memory. Whether the record would say that Silas Merrick had lived and painted and died, or that Ambrose Merrick had lived and lied and killed, or that some third creature—the name on page one, the name that belonged to no grave and no certificate—had done all of it and none of it.

Vann came in with two coffees and set one down without asking.

"Okonkwo's final report is on your desk," she said. "The signature on the will matches the signature on the 2014 death certificate to within a two percent deviation. Same hand. Both forgeries, both by the same hand, thirty years of practice between them." She sat down across from Beatrix. "The D.A. says authenticate it. If the will is valid, the estate has an executor, and the executor can be compelled to open the studio, inventory the paintings, and hand over everything as estate records. Cleanest path to the victims' names."

"And if I void it?"

"Then the estate passes under the old intestacy rules, the state takes it, and the studio gets sealed by a court order nobody will fight, because nobody will have standing to fight it." Vann wrapped her hands around her coffee. "The victims' families get nothing but a closed door and a press conference."

Beatrix looked at the will. *In trust.* Not to her. Through her.

"He wrote it so I'd have no choice," she said. "That's the part I keep coming back to. He didn't leave me the estate. He left me the *decision*. He knew I'd have to choose between the law and the truth, and he built the will so the truth could only enter the record through the law's own door." She laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bitter. "Thirty years of studying probate files, Vann. He knew exactly which instrument to forge. Not a confession. A will. Because a will is the one document the law will accept from a dead man without question—as long as I say it's real."

"Is it real?"

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment.

"That's not the question," she said. "That's the trap. Okonkwo proved the hand is genuine. The question is whose. And the answer is: nobody's. A man who never existed signed it, in a name that never existed, witnessed by a version of me that never signed the 2014 papers." She turned the page. The trust provisions caught the light. "And it's still the truest document in this entire file."

* * *

She went to see him on the third day, because she had promised herself she would not decide until she had looked at him once without the glass between them.

The interview room was gray and small and smelled of floor wax. He sat with his hands flat on the table, and Beatrix noticed—she could not help noticing—that his fingers were stained to the second knuckle with pigment that three days of custody had not removed. Cadmium red, she thought. The red of the harbor at dusk in the last portrait. The red he had used for the water in every painting of the drowning.

"You read it," he said. Not a question. He had always known what she would do; that had been the horror of him, and it was, she realized now, the horror of herself too.

"I read it."

"And?"

"And I'd like to know which name you want on your headstone," Beatrix said. "Because the state will ask, eventually. And I'll be the one they ask."

Something moved in his face—not a flinch this time, not the correction-reflex, but something older and more tired. He looked at his stained hands as if they belonged to the brothers he had spent a lifetime being.

"Silas painted the truth," he said. "Every canvas in that studio is a true thing wearing a lie. Ambrose lied to survive, and the lie ate the truth, and I—" He stopped. Started again, in the flat voice of a man reading his own obituary. "I was the one nobody wrote down. No will mentioned me. No portrait showed me. I learned to paint by copying them both, and then I learned to live by copying them both, and then one day there was no me under the copies at all." He looked up. "The name on the will. Silas Ambrose. That's the only name that was ever mine, because I made it, and everything I made is true."

"The drownings were true too," Beatrix said quietly. "Seven men. An archivist who catalogued your family's estate and never knew why it mattered."

The flinch came, at last. The correction he could not make.

"They were rehearsals," he said. "I never said they were innocent. I said they were *true*. The accident was true. The cover-up was true. The hush money was true. I put the truth into the world the only way it would fit—body by body, canvas by canvas—and if the world needed seven of them before it would look, then that is a fact about the world, not about me." His voice did not rise. That was the worst of it. It never rose. "You of all people understand, Ms. Galloway. You authenticate documents for a living. You decide what the record says a life was. I just did it with a brush instead of a stamp."

Beatrix stood. She had come for one answer and found she had brought it with her.

"When I decide," she said, "one of your brothers stops existing. The record will erase him the way this family erased you. Silas's paintings become the work of a murderer, or Ambrose's guilt becomes the fiction of a dead man, and either way the record closes over it like water." She gathered her coat. "I want you to know that I understand exactly what I'm about to do. That's the difference between us. I'm going to do it with my eyes open."

He said nothing as she reached the door. Then, as her hand touched it:

"He's in the studio," the killer said. "Under the floor. Where I kept the file. The original file—the one from 2013, the one the Aldouses paid to make disappear. I never destroyed it. I couldn't. It's the only thing in the world that proves I was ever real." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere Beatrix had not heard it go before—not flat, not rehearsed, but small, the voice of the erased twin, the one no will mentioned. "You're the executor. Whatever you decide. Just don't let it be the version where none of us was ever there."

* * *

The studio at Verrin Lane had been sealed with police tape and a court's indifference, but Beatrix had keys now—keys that had arrived in her mail the week the will was signed, keys she had never reported, keys that had terrified her more than the portrait of herself asleep. She let herself in at dusk, when the harbor light came through the north windows the way it must have come for thirty years, and stood among the canvases with the killer's words in her ears.

Under the floor. Where he kept the file.

She found the loose board without difficulty; he had wanted it findable, wanted *her* to find it, had built his whole architecture of guilt around the certainty that she would one day stand exactly here. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a metal document box, and inside the box was the 2013 file—police reports, witness statements, the Aldouse name redacted and re-redacted and finally, on the last page, written in a young hand in the margin, the words that had started everything:

*He was drunk. I saw his face. It was the son.*

And beneath that, a signature. Not Silas's. Not Ambrose's. A third hand—younger, shakier, trained by the same father but not yet perfected—the hand of a nineteen-year-old who had written down the truth and then spent thirty years learning to forge every name but his own.

Beatrix held the file and understood the shape of the decision, at last, completely. She could authenticate the will. She could void it. She could burn this file or enter it. Every path led through her, and every path erased someone, and the law—which she had served for twenty years, which had made her, which had been edited into her own past by a forger's patient hand—offered no guidance at all, because the law had never been built for a man who was three men, all of them dead, one of them still breathing.

She thought of Silas, who had painted the truth and been erased for it. Of Ambrose, who had lied to live and died in the lie. Of the third twin, who had copied them both until nothing remained.

Then she thought of the seven drowned men, and the archivist, and the families who would sit in a courtroom and hear a name read into the record, and she knew that whatever she decided, it would not be mercy. It would be authorship. And authorship, she had learned from the best forger on the coast, was just another word for choosing who gets to have existed.

She closed the box. Outside, the harbor was turning red.

In her coat pocket, her phone buzzed. A message from Vann, four words, and Beatrix read them twice before the meaning settled like sediment:

*The Aldouses know you're there.*
