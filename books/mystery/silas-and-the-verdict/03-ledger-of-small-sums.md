## 3. Ledger of Small Sums

Ulyana had broken into exactly one building in her life before tonight, and that had been her own apartment in Portland when she'd locked herself out in a bathrobe during a heat wave. The groundskeeper's cottage required marginally more skill and considerably more dignity.

The padlock on the back door was older than she was. She'd bought a bolt cutter at the hardware store in Ashfall — the clerk hadn't blinked, which told her something about Ashfall — but in the end she didn't need it. The hasp screws were rusted to lace. One firm shoulder later, the door swung open into air that tasted like dust and dried lavender and fifty years of nobody.

"Sorry, Mr. Brandt," she murmured, clicking on her headlamp. "If you're dead. Which is apparently negotiable."

Her phone's flashlight swept a small room: a cot with a army blanket folded at its foot, a wood stove, a shelf of mason jars labeled in a careful hand — *nails, short*; *nails, long*; *string, good*. A man who sorted his string by quality was not a man who confessed to murder casually. That thought settled over her like the dust.

She'd spent the drive from the funeral home rehearsing her skepticism. *No body on record*, the director had admitted, wringing his hands like a man being asked to explain weather. *Paperwork says cremation, but I can't find the paperwork.* And the grave itself — she'd stood over it that morning, freshly turned earth, a granite marker reading SILAS BRANDT, 1929–2021, and felt the specific vertigo of a story refusing to hold still. You don't dig a grave for a man you can't produce. Unless someone wanted him erased twice: once by paper, once by earth.

But the cottage argued against the confession too. Everything here said *caretaker*, not *killer*. On the wall above the stove hung a photograph of a young Black man in coveralls standing beside an elderly white woman in a garden hat, both squinting into sun, both half-smiling like they shared a joke the camera couldn't hear. Marguerite Voss, 1970, one year before she vanished. Silas looked at her in the photo the way people look at people they'd follow anywhere.

Ulyana sat down on the cot, which exhaled a small cloud of history, and let herself feel it: the crack running through her professional skepticism, widening. Three years of podcast episodes built on public records and secondhand memory, and now she was trespassing in a dead-or-not-dead man's house because of a letter postmarked the day after his funeral. Her producer would have called this a liability. Ulyana called it Tuesday.

Then she saw the ledgers.

They were under the cot — no, beside it, tucked into a wooden crate that also held a hand trowel and a tin of seeds. Twenty-odd cloth-bound books, spines cracked, dates inked along each: 1972 through 2019. Fifty years of garden accounts. She pulled out the earliest and opened it to a page of entries in that same careful hand:

*Mar. 14 — roses, 6, terrace bed*
*Mar. 14 — lime, 40 lb.*
*Apr. 2 — lime, 40 lb.*
*Apr. 30 — deep trench, November prep*

She frowned. November prep, written in April? She flipped forward. The entries repeated like a heartbeat: roses, lime, trench. Some years the amounts changed. Some years there were extra lines — *lime, 80 lb., west walk* in 1988; *deep trench, north* in 1994. No gardener on earth needed forty pounds of lime twice a month, and no trench needed six months' preparation unless it wasn't for planting.

Unless it was for keeping something down.

Ulyana sat very still in the dusty dark, heart tapping out a rhythm she recognized from three years of chasing this story. *He didn't confess to killing her,* she thought. *He confessed to knowing where she is.*

She photographed every page until her phone overheated, then wrapped two ledgers — 1971's neighbor years, 1970 and 1972 — inside her jacket, feeling like the world's most apologetic thief.

That was when the floorboard creaked behind her.

* * *

Wren Voss had been watching the cottage for three nights, and she wanted it noted — by someone, anyone, the universe at large — that stakeouts were nothing like the movies. There was no dramatic music. There was cold, and there was her thermos of coffee going lukewarm, and there was the particular humiliation of crouching in juniper brush while a stranger with a headlamp rummaged through your family's garbage.

Well. Not garbage. Silas's things. Which Wren supposed made them sacred, or as close to sacred as anything got on Voss land.

She'd found the cottage at fifteen, exploring the estate the way lonely kids do, mapping every room of the great rotting house and every outbuilding until she knew the property better than the family that still technically owned it. The cottage had been the only place on the grounds that felt warm. Someone had loved it once. Someone had kept *string, good* in labeled jars, and fifteen-year-old Wren, whose own household ran on chaos and her grandmother's bitter anecdotes, had understood immediately that this was what care looked like when nobody was watching.

So when the letter came — slipped under the gatehouse door last week, unsigned, four words in a shaking hand: *Don't let them dig* — Wren had known exactly where "them" would start digging.

And here they were. A woman, mid-thirties maybe, city jacket, sitting on Silas's cot reading his ledgers by flashlight like her life depended on it.

Wren stepped inside. "Those are numbered," she said. "If you're taking them, take them in order."

The woman startled so hard she nearly dropped 1972. Then she did something unexpected: she laughed. Short, startled, real.

"Okay," she said. "In my defense, I was going to put everything back."

"You were going to put the padlock back too?"

"I was going to *feel bad* about the padlock. There's a difference." The woman stood, extended a hand. "Ulyana Nakamura. I do a podcast. About Marguerite Voss."

"I know." Wren didn't take the hand yet. "I've listened to all eleven episodes. Your episode seven, where you made Aunt Marguerite sound like a person instead of a headline — that was the first time anyone ever got her right." She paused. "It's also how I knew you'd come. You don't leave stories alone. Neither do I. It's our worst quality."

Ulyana tilted her head. "You're a Voss."

"Wren. Grandniece. Resident ghost, depending who you ask." She finally shook the offered hand. Her grandmother had been Marguerite's first cousin, which made Wren family the way mildew was family to a bathroom — present, tolerated, quietly ruining things. The Vosses who still lived in town, the ones with money and opinions, considered her the wrong kind of relative: the poor branch, the loud branch, the branch that asked questions at funerals. "Why are you reading garden ledgers at midnight?"

"Because your groundskeeper may have faked his death, and he left me a confession that isn't a confession." Ulyana held up the ledger, open to the April page. "Tell me if this means anything to you. Roses, lime, a deep trench prepped in spring for November. Every year. Like clockwork."

Wren took the book. Read. Frowned. Something cold walked up her spine wearing familiar shoes.

"Silas planted the terrace roses every March," she said slowly. "My grandmother used to complain about it. Said he babied those roses like they were—" She stopped.

"Like they were what?"

"Like they were *memorial*." The word came out smaller than Wren intended. She sat down on the cot herself, abruptly, the ledger heavy in her lap. "There's a spot on the rose terrace where the flowers always grew best. My grandmother hated it. Wouldn't sit near it. I used to think it was because the view faced the empty house." She looked up at Ulyana. "You think he buried something under my aunt's roses."

"I think he wrote it down in code for fifty years because he couldn't say it out loud. And I think somebody tried to bury him last week for the same reason."

Outside, wind moved through the junipers, and somewhere far off a dog started up and quit again. Wren realized she wasn't afraid of this stranger. That was new. That was — she searched for the word and landed, embarrassingly, on *relief*.

"The family will fight you," she said. "Not all of them. But Judge Edmund still lives at the top of the hill, and he's beloved the way judges are beloved — politely, and from a distance. He'll offer you tea and archives and tell you my aunt's story attracts unstable people." She smiled without warmth. "He told me that at my grandmother's funeral. While holding my elbow. Very firmly."

"Charming."

"He's worse than charming. Charming is just menace with better lighting." Wren stood, decision arriving in her chest whole, the way true things did. "Here's what happens now. You give me back 1972 — I'm not letting it out of my sight — and we read these together. All of them. Because whatever Silas hid, he hid it in arithmetic, and arithmetic takes two."

"Why two?"

"Because one of us needs to keep watch," Wren said, "and because I've spent thirty years investigating this family alone, and honestly? I could use a colleague."

Ulyana studied her for a long moment — the flashlight catching dust motes between them like slow snow — and then she grinned, tired and bright.

"Fair warning," she said. "Colleagues of mine tend to end up in episodes."

"Fair warning back." Wren hugged the ledger to her chest. "Vosses tend to end up in graves. Let's try to break the pattern."

They worked until nearly three, cross-referencing dates against rainfall records and estate maps Ulyana had pulled from the county archive. The pattern sharpened with every page: clusters of entries — lime, lime, trench — that mapped onto locations. The rose terrace. The west walk. Once, chillingly, the greenhouse.

It was Ulyana who spotted the anomaly, tapping 1994 with one finger. "This year's different. Look — the entries stop in October. Mid-month. And then nothing until January."

Wren leaned in. In the margin beside the final October entry, in Silas's careful hand, almost invisible among the figures, someone had written a single line:

*She would be 68 today.*

Below it, smaller, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper:

*Ask the third grandchild.*

Wren went cold. "Marguerite only had two," she whispered. "Edmund and my grandmother. There was never a third."

Ulyana looked at her across the little lamplit circle of their work, and neither of them said what they were both thinking:

Somebody had been lying for fifty years. And Silas Brandt — alive, somewhere, terrified — had just told them exactly who to ask.
