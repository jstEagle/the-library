## 10. Marguerite's Trust

The charge sheet was read aloud on a Tuesday in March, and Ulyana Nakamura cried in the back row of the Ashfall County courthouse, which she would later insist — on the record, in episode forty-one — was allergies.

*Edmund Voss, seventy-eight years of age, charged with the murder of Marguerite Voss (1971), the murder of Deputy Corliss Ames (1988), and conspiracy to obstruct justice.* The judge's voice didn't waver. Edmund stood in his good coat with his hands folded, and when the clerk finished reading, he turned his head — just slightly, just enough — to find Ulyana in the gallery. He did not glare. He did not sneer. He gave her a small, courteous nod, the nod of a man acknowledging an opponent across a chessboard, and it was somehow worse than rage would have been.

She nodded back. She couldn't help it. Fifty years of politeness had been the architecture of everything he'd built; it seemed only right that politeness should be the last thing he saw.

Outside, Wren was waiting on the courthouse steps with two coffees and the expression of someone who had slept four hours and intended to be smug about surviving anyway.

"Well," Wren said, handing over a cup. "The Honorable Edmund Voss. Charged."

"Charged," Ulyana agreed.

"Convicted pending."

"Convicted pending."

They clinked their paper cups like champagne flutes, because neither of them owned champagne glasses, and because it felt correct that the toast should be cheap and warm and held in cold hands.

Theo came down the steps three minutes later, having spent the intervening time explaining to a local reporter — for the fourth time — that no, he had not been the informant, yes, he understood why they'd thought so, and no, cutting the power to a greenhouse during a live broadcast was not, legally speaking, tampering, thank you for asking. He looked lighter than Ulyana had ever seen him. Suspicion, once lifted, turned out to have been heavier than anyone realized while carrying it.

"Theo," she said, "I owe you an apology."

"You owe me nothing." He shrugged. "You suspected me for eleven days. I've suspected my own grandfather of murder since I was nine. You were actually right about *him*, which puts you ahead of my entire childhood."

It was, Ulyana reflected, the closest thing to absolution the Voss family produced, and she took it gladly.

* * *

The codicil changed everything, as codicils written in secret by murdered heiresses tend to do.

Marguerite Voss, it emerged, had been pregnant in 1971. She had left the estate not to her nephews but to "my child, born or soon to be born" — and when the child never appeared in any record, everyone had assumed the pregnancy ended with her. It hadn't. Silas Brandt, twenty-six years old and terrified out of his mind, had driven a screaming nineteen-year-old girl named Delia Marsh — Marguerite's maid, her only confidante — to a bus station in the rain, with an envelope of cash and instructions never to come back. Delia had raised the baby alone in Ohio under her own name. The baby grew up, married, had a daughter of her own, and died believing her mother's stories about "the beautiful house in Ashfall County" were just stories.

Her daughter's name was Iris Brandt.

Silas had known. Of course he had known — he'd paid for Iris's braces through a post office box, sent birthday cards signed "an old friend of your grandmother," and told no one, because telling anyone meant telling everyone what Marguerite's child had needed protecting *from*. When Ulyana put the ledger down in front of him and showed him the entry from June 1971 — *envelope, postage, $4.20* appearing every month for fifty years like a heartbeat — the old man had wept into his hands for a long time.

"I kept her alive in ink," he said finally. "That's all I could do. Ink and lime and roses."

Iris Brandt, thirty-four, a veterinary technician from Dayton with Marguerite's same stubborn chin, arrived in Ashfall in April and stood in the ruined rose terrace for an hour without saying anything at all. Then she said, "So this is where the money goes," and laughed until she cried, and Wren put an arm around her shoulders like they'd known each other for years instead of hours, because that was simply what Wren did now. The found family was expanding whether anyone planned it or not.

The trust documents took four months. Wren handled the lawyers with a ferocity that made Ulyana glad they were on the same side. The estate — grounds, greenhouse, cottage, and the great sad house itself — passed into the Marguerite Voss Trust, chartered for one purpose: a community archive for Ashfall County, free to anyone whose family history had ever been misfiled, misremembered, or buried under roses.

"There's a certain poetry," Wren observed, signing the final page, "in a house built on secrets becoming a building where nobody's allowed to keep them."

* * *

Silas Brandt lived until the first week of June.

The doctors had given him less. He treated their estimate as a personal insult and set about disproving it with the same methodical patience he'd once applied to rose beds. Every afternoon that the weather permitted, Ulyana drove up to the nurse's cabin in the hills — his cabin now, officially, though he still called it "Mrs. Halloran's place" out of fifty years of habit — and they worked.

Because there were more secrets in the ledger. Of course there were.

"The thread was the key," he reminded her, tapping the binding with one crooked finger. "But a key opens more than one door, girl. Pay attention."

He taught her the second cipher: entries that repeated in threes marked places where he'd hidden things — not evidence, not anymore, those were gone or burned, but *keepsakes*. Marguerite's letters to Delia, sewn into oilcloth inside the well housing. A photograph of Marguerite at nineteen, laughing, holding a trowel like a sword. Her handwriting on the back: *S. teaches me the roses. E. disapproves. Good.*

"She'd have liked you," Silas said, watching Ulyana read it. "She asked too many questions too."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said about me in years."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment."

"No," Ulyana agreed, grinning. "But it was entirely true."

Some afternoons he didn't teach at all. Some afternoons he sat on the porch with a blanket over his knees and told her about 1971 — not the horror, he'd given the horror to the microphone already, in the last ten minutes of episode thirty-nine, delivered in a voice like dry leaves — but the before. Marguerite racing him across the south lawn on a bet. Delia hiding apples in the linen closet. The whole estate briefly, improbably, alive.

"People think a mystery is about the ending," he said one evening, as the light went gold over the hills. "It isn't. It's about who gets to tell it. For fifty years, the only person telling it was the man who ended it. That's what I couldn't forgive myself for. Not the silence. Giving him the pen."

Ulyana wrote that down. She wrote most things down. But some sentences she kept in a separate notebook, the small one, the one that wasn't for episodes.

* * *

He died on a Thursday morning, in his sleep, in a bed with clean sheets, which after everything felt like a verdict in itself.

The funeral was nothing like the fake one. There was a body this time, and a plot dug properly deep beside Mrs. Halloran's people in the hills cemetery, and a crowd that surprised everyone except, perhaps, Silas himself, who had spent fifty years learning exactly how many lives one quiet man could touch. Half of Ashfall came. The other half listened to the rerun of episode thirty-nine on the drive home.

Wren spoke. Theo spoke. Iris brought her granddaughter, who was four and mostly interested in the worms, which Silas would have loved. Ulyana spoke last, and kept it short, because she knew what he'd say about eulogies: *Don't decorate the man, girl. Plant something.*

So they planted something. Everyone did. Roses, all along the terrace where the old ones had been torn out — hardy varieties, the kind that survive neglect and frost and bad decades. Iris laid a small stone at the end of the row, uncarved, because Wren suggested that some markers work better blank.

* * *

The archive opened in September.

Ulyana had planned to attend quietly, sit in the back, maybe record ambient sound for a bonus episode. Instead she found herself standing at the front with scissors, because Wren had decided — unilaterally, in the manner of Wren — that the person who'd followed a dead man's letter across the country should be the one to cut the ribbon.

"This is your fault," Ulyana muttered, snipping.

"Everything good here is somebody's fault," Wren said serenely. "Get used to it."

Inside, the great hall smelled of fresh paint and old paper. Volunteers shelved county records, church registries, boxes of photographs donated by families who'd driven in from three counties over. Theo ran the oral history booth, gently coaxing stories out of farmers who claimed they had none and then talked for two hours. Iris had insisted the children's corner be built in the old conservatory annex, sun-warmed and full of ferns, "because somebody in this family ought to grow up thinking this house is a good place."

On the wall above the front desk hung the ledger itself, open under glass to the June 1971 page — *envelope, postage, $4.20* — with a placard explaining the cipher, the thread, the fifty years. School groups stopped there first. Ulyana watched a girl of maybe ten lean close to the glass, frowning at the columns of small sums, and felt something in her chest go loose and warm.

"That's how it starts," she told the girl. "One number that doesn't fit."

The girl looked up at her with sudden, total seriousness. "Did you figure it out?"

"Took me three years and a lot of help."

"Oh." The girl considered this, then nodded, satisfied. "My mom says I ask too many questions too."

Ulyana crouched down to her level. "Best thing you can be," she said, and meant it with her whole chest.

That evening, the found family — because that's what it was now, no point pretending otherwise — sat on the terrace steps with takeout containers and cheap wine, watching the light die over the hills. Wren argued with Theo about shelf organization. Iris fell asleep against Wren's shoulder mid-sentence. Somewhere below, the roses were putting down roots in soil that had finally been allowed to tell the truth.

Ulyana opened her phone and glanced at her inbox, out of pure habit. Three hundred new messages. Listener mail, interview requests, a note from a woman in Oregon whose own family had a ledger nobody could explain.

And one message at the top, from an address she didn't recognize, subject line in all caps:

**YOU MISSED AN ENTRY.**

She stared at it. Below the subject line, no body text — just a photograph, grainy, of a ledger page she knew intimately. Page 214. Except in this photograph, there was an entry in the bottom margin that she had never seen, in handwriting that was not Silas's, not Marguerite's, not Edmund's.

Small, careful, unfamiliar: *third trench, spring, ask S. why.*

Ulyana looked up at the darkening hills, at the lit windows of the archive, at the sleeping, arguing, laughing people around her who had become, without her noticing the exact moment it happened, hers.

Then she opened the small notebook — the one that wasn't for episodes — and began to write.
