## 5. What Ada Saw

Dorothy Merrick-Pell answered her door in Kittery Falls with the expression of a woman who had been expecting me for forty years and had decided, somewhere around year thirty-five, to stop being impressed by anything.

"You're the podcast lady," she said. "The one with the dead man's letter."

"That's me. Rosalind Nakamura."

"I know. You introduced yourself. Come in before the cold does." She stepped aside, and I noticed she held a photograph in one hand, face-down against her cardigan, the way you hold a card you're not ready to play.

Her living room was a museum of the ordinary — doilies, a console TV that hadn't worked since the Clinton administration but was too heavy to move, a wall of framed photographs in which the same narrow-faced woman appeared at various ages, growing up and then, abruptly, stopping.

Ada.

I'd seen the one surviving photo of her in the newspaper archive: grainy, cropped from a company picnic, half-smiling at someone outside the frame. Here she was whole. Here she was a person who had once been nineteen and laughed at something, who had once been thirty and squinted into summer sun, who had been thirty-one for exactly as long as it took the Kennebec River to finish with her.

"Mrs. Pell," I began, "I want to be upfront about why I'm—"

"Dorothy." She lowered herself into an armchair with the economy of someone who'd had both hips replaced and resented every minute of it. "And sit down, podcast lady. You're making the room nervous."

I sat. On the coffee table between us sat a teapot, two cups, and a plate of store-bought shortbread arranged with the kind of precision that suggested she'd been waiting a long time and rehearsing longer.

"You said on the phone you had questions about Ada's work," she said. "At Hollis Mill."

"I did. I do. But I have to tell you what I've found first, because you deserve to hear it from a person and not a speaker." I told her about the letter. About Harold Voss, dead four days now, confessing to her sister's drowning from whatever mail route the dead use. About the ledger with its impossible columns. About the payments that began three weeks before the fire that killed six people.

She listened without moving. When I finished, she poured the tea — both cups, hers and mine, though she never asked if I wanted any — and then she turned the photograph over and set it on the table facing me.

Ada Merrick, mid-twenties, standing on the mill office steps in a wool coat, holding a ledger against her hip the way other women in that era held handbags. She wasn't smiling. She had the look of someone who knew exactly what the numbers said and was tired of people asking her to say otherwise.

"She was the night bookkeeper," Dorothy said. "Nineteen seventy-nine to eighty-three. Second shift, ten to six, alone in that office with the steam pipes knocking like a haunted house. She used to say the mill talked to her at night. All those numbers, she said, they add up to a voice if you listen long enough."

"Was she good at her job?"

"She was *exact*." Dorothy said the word the way some people say *saint*. "Ada couldn't let a number be wrong. Couldn't. I've watched her re-add a grocery receipt in the parking lot because the total looked a nickel light. It wasn't a virtue, exactly. It was more like a condition. She saw order the way some people see faces."

* * *

Here is what I have learned in six years of true-crime work, and what no one wants to hear: the great tragedies are rarely committed by monsters. They are committed by bookkeepers. By people who look at a discrepancy and decide the discrepancy is the problem, not the theft.

Ada Merrick had found a discrepancy.

"Tell me about the fire," I said. "The official story."

"The official story was a boiler fault. Grease buildup in the flues, a spark, and poof. Six women dead on the third floor because the stairwell door was locked — company policy, they said, to keep out union organizers." Dorothy's voice was flat as a ledger line. "The state investigated for four months and concluded nobody had done anything wrong, which is a remarkable trick when six people are dead."

"And Ada? Where was she that night?"

"Home. She'd been moved off nights two weeks before. Transferred to days, payroll department. A promotion, they called it." Dorothy smiled without any warmth arriving to support it. "Ada didn't want the promotion. She liked nights. She liked being alone with the numbers. But you didn't refuse Mr. Voss a promotion. Mr. Voss didn't offer things; he *assigned* them."

Harold Voss. The name landed in my stomach like a stone dropped down a well.

"Why move her off nights?"

Dorothy looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached under the doily on the side table and produced a manila envelope, soft with age, and slid it across the coffee table.

"I've been waiting for somebody to ask me that question for forty years," she said. "Forty years, podcast lady. You're the first one who came with the right question instead of the easy one."

* * *

Inside the envelope were photocopies — Ada's own copies, made on the mill's copier, which was a small act of rebellion in itself — of ledger pages from the fall of 1983. Not the hardware store ledger. The mill's books. But the numbers had the same architecture: entries that balanced on the surface and rotted underneath. Payments to a vendor that didn't exist. Materials ordered that never arrived. And in the margins, in Ada's small, furious pencil, the annotations of a woman who had done the arithmetic of a crime.

"She found it in October," Dorothy said. "Fire-safety work that was billed and never done. Sprinkler heads on the invoice, no sprinkler heads on the pipes. She traced it back two years. It wasn't just the mill, either. The payments went through a supply company — Voss's hardware, the one in town. He was the middleman. The mill paid his store for safety equipment, his store kicked most of it back to the owners, and everybody's books came out beautiful and the pipes stayed dry."

My mouth had gone dry. "And she threatened to report it."

"She didn't threaten." Dorothy's chin came up. "She *announced*. There's a difference, and Ada was all difference. She told the family at Thanksgiving — November of '83 — that she was going to the state labor board after the holidays. She'd written it all up. She had copies. She said, and I quote, because I have had forty years to memorize it: 'Numbers are the one place poor people get to tell the truth, and somebody's been lying in my numbers.'"

The shortbread sat untouched between us. The radiator ticked. Somewhere outside, a truck ground through slush.

"What happened after Thanksgiving?"

"December fifth, she's moved to days. December ninth, she tells me someone went through her desk at work — nothing taken, but her papers weren't where she'd left them, and Ada knew her papers the way you know your own teeth. December eleventh, she tells me a man she didn't recognize was parked outside our mother's house twice in one week. And December fourteenth—" Dorothy's voice did not break. It simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and then resumed at a different pitch. "December fourteenth, she told me she'd made an appointment with the labor board for January third. She said, 'If anything happens to me, Dot, check the books. The books will say what my mouth can't.'"

"Did you check the books?"

"I was twenty-three and terrified and I didn't know what I was looking at." She met my eyes, and I saw that the flatness was a load-bearing wall, holding up something enormous. "By the time I understood, she'd been dead two weeks. Drowned. Car in the river at Miller's Bend, no skid marks, no witnesses, and a state trooper who wrote 'accident' before his coffee got cold. And I was twenty-three, and the men who ran this town were the men who ran everything, and my mother told me to let Ada rest. So I let her rest." She picked up her tea and set it down again without drinking. "Forty years of letting her rest, and it turns out I was sitting on her the whole time."

* * *

I want to tell you what I felt in that room, and I want to be honest about it, because honesty is the only currency I have that doesn't devalue.

I felt the old familiar cold, the one that comes when a case stops being a puzzle and becomes a person. I've interviewed hundreds of people about their dead. I've learned to hear the difference between grief that has been processed and grief that has been *stored*, and Dorothy Merrick-Pell's grief had been in storage so long it had fossilized into furniture. She'd been living with it so long it had become the doilies, the console TV, the arrangement of the shortbread. Her sister's murder wasn't a wound. It was the architecture.

And underneath the cold, I felt something I like less: recognition. Ada Merrick had been killed for bookkeeping. Not for passion, not for jealousy, not for any of the operatic reasons we like our murders to have. She had been killed because she was *exact*. Because she couldn't let a number be wrong. Because she believed — genuinely, constitutionally believed — that columns of figures were a place where the truth could hide and also a place where it could be found.

I make my living finding things in numbers. Dates, amounts, patterns. Every episode I've ever produced is, at bottom, a woman with Ada's condition, armed with better software and no river nearby.

"Mrs. Pell," I said. "Dorothy. The ledger I told you about — Voss's ledger. It records payments. And some of the entries are in code. Names, hidden in the numbers. We've started decoding them."

"We," she said, catching it the way hawks catch movement.

"Harold Voss's grandson. Silas. He's helping me."

Something moved behind her face — not approval exactly, but the recognition of a strange symmetry. "The grandson is helping you hang the grandfather."

"The grandson is helping me find out who else was in the room."

She nodded slowly. Then she stood, went to the writing desk in the corner, and came back with one more thing: a Christmas card from December 1983, unsigned, the handwriting blocky and careful.

*Ada — Some doors are better left unopened. Think of your family. Think of Dot. — A friend*

"Arrived four days before she drowned," Dorothy said. "She laughed at it. She said, 'A friend who won't sign his name is a confession that won't sign its name.' She pinned it above her desk." Her voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture across forty years of flat. "She thought she was safe, podcast lady. She thought the truth was armor. That's the part I can't — she thought the numbers would protect her."

I looked at the card. *A friend.* The handwriting was careful, controlled, the penmanship of a man who'd been taught to write properly and had never once written anything he hadn't meant.

"May I photograph this?" I asked.

"You may take it," Dorothy said. "I've kept it long enough. It was never mine to keep. It was evidence, and I was too young and too scared to know the difference." She pressed it into my hands and closed my fingers over it with hers, and her grip was stronger than a woman her age had any right to. "You find out who 'a friend' was. And you find out who signed the checks. And when you do—" she released me and straightened her cardigan, and the wall went back up, flat and load-bearing, "—you say her name right. Not 'the victim.' Not 'the drowning.' Ada. Ada Merrick. She was exact, and she was brave, and she was twenty-six years old, and she was my sister."

* * *

I drove back through the dark with the card on the passenger seat, and I'll admit I talked to it. "Who were you, friend?" The streetlights strobed past. Somewhere behind me, Kittery Falls slept on top of its forty-year secret.

Silas was waiting at the Voss house when I pulled in, porch light on, ledger open on the hood of his truck. He'd decoded three more entries that afternoon, he said, and his face in the porch light told me the afternoon had not gone well for anyone's peace of mind.

I held up the Christmas card. "I think I've got a handwriting sample."

He looked at it, then at me, and his jaw did something complicated.

"Rosalind," he said. "The entry I decoded tonight. It's dated December sixteenth, 1983. Two days after Ada died." He turned the ledger toward me, and there in the cold light of the porch, in my own careful transcription of his grandfather's cipher, was a line that made the river feel suddenly very close:

*Payment rendered — silence secured — retainer continues — M. Ashford.*

The judge. The *retainer continues*.

Which meant that in 1983, a young lawyer named Marcus Ashford hadn't just been paid once. He'd been put on a schedule.

"Silas," I said, "how many more of these entries are there?"

He turned the page. And turned it again.
