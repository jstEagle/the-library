## 5. Room 214, Booked Forever

The Meridian's reservation system was built in 2009 by a man named Denny Kowalczyk, who charged Edmund Voss forty thousand dollars for it and then, as far as anyone could tell, moved to Portugal. It had a beige interface. It crashed if you opened two windows at once. It required a password that Voss had changed every quarter since installation and written on the inside of his desk drawer, because Edmund Voss believed in security the way some men believe in horoscopes — ritually, loudly, and without any actual effect on events.

I knew about the drawer because I dusted around it.

This is the thing people never understand about concierges: we are not invisible. We are *furniture*. And furniture sees everything. A man can hide his affairs from his wife, his debts from his accountant, his sins from his priest, but he cannot hide them from the woman who holds his umbrella, remembers his mistress's anniversary, and knows which drawer sticks.

So at eleven p.m. on Tuesday, with the lobby empty except for a sleeping bellhop and a fern that had seen better decades, I sat down behind the front desk, opened the drawer, typed in the current password — *TIDEFALL7*, because Edmund Voss had all the subtlety of a foghorn — and began to look for a book that wasn't a book.

* * *

Let me explain what Tamsin had given me, because she'd given it to me the way you hand someone a live wire: quickly, and while looking away.

"The ledger isn't paper," she'd said, three nights ago, in the blue hour between the last checkout and the first arrival. "It's the reservations themselves. Every bribe my father ever took or paid is recorded as a booking. The dates mean something. The room numbers mean something. The guest initials — they're real people, but they've never stayed here. Not one of them."

"And you know this how?"

"I grew up watching him do it." Her hands were wrapped around a teacup she hadn't sipped from in twenty minutes. "He'd sit right there, at your desk, after close, and enter bookings for guests who didn't exist. I thought it was a game when I was small. Then I thought it was madness. Then I understood it was neither. It was memory. He didn't trust paper. Paper burns."

"Computers crash," I said.

"Computers get backed up," she said, and looked at me like she was handing me something heavier than information. "You're the only person in this town who'd know where to look. That's why I'm here. That's why I paid you."

I hadn't agreed yet. I want that on the record. I had said *I'll consider it*, which in concierge is a full contract with notarized signatures, but in my own private accounting still counted as consideration.

Now I was considering.

I started where any sensible person starts: with the archive. Denny Kowalczyk's system kept seven years of reservations, including cancelled ones, no-shows, and bookings made and voided within the same minute — the digital equivalent of nervous tics. Seven years of the Meridian's life, every night of it, laid out in beige rows.

I pulled the history for Room 214.

Room 214 was a corner room on the second floor, east-facing, with a view of the harbor that our brochure called *sweeping* and our maintenance log called *leaks in heavy rain*. It was a perfectly ordinary room. It had been occupied, according to the system, every single night for six years.

That's not unusual for a hotel's best suite. It is extremely unusual for Room 214, which was not our best suite, was not even our best corner room (that honor went to 218, which had the bathtub people wrote poems about), and which, I happened to know from six years of walking past its door, had smelled faintly of mildew and disuse the entire time.

I checked the occupancy reports. Housekeeping logged 214 as serviced weekly — linens changed, surfaces wiped, the ritual maintenance of a room nobody slept in. The chambermaids had a name for it. They called it *the ghost room*, and they drew straws over who got it, because servicing an empty room for an hour pays the same as servicing a wrecked one, and everyone wanted the easy money, and everyone also felt vaguely guilty about it, which is the most human thing I know.

Six years. Every night booked. Let me show you what that looks like.

*March 4, six years ago: R. Calloway, 3 nights.* Cancelled after one. Rebooked same day: *R. Calloway, extended stay.*

*June: M. Ostrowski, 2 nights.*

*September: H. Brandt, 1 night, repeated 14 times across the month.*

Different names. Different lengths. Sometimes overlapping — two names booked into 214 on the same night, which the system permitted because Denny Kowalczyk had built exactly one validation rule into his forty-thousand-dollar masterpiece, and it concerned credit card formatting.

I ran the names. R. Calloway did not exist — no ID on file, no folio charges beyond the room rate, no phone number, no email. Neither did M. Ostrowski. Neither did H. Brandt, or T. Quist, or L. Ferreira, or the other thirty-odd identities that had rotated through 214 like a very slow, very expensive revolving door. In six years, Room 214 had hosted more than forty guests, and not one of them had ordered room service, made a local call, complained about the mildew, or existed.

But here's the detail that made me put down my tea and sit very still in the dark lobby:

Every single booking — all of them, six years, forty-plus ghosts — was paid from the same account.

Not the same *card*. The same account. A corporate account under the name Meridian Harbor Development LLC, billed monthly, never late, never disputed, never once flagged. I stared at that account number until the digits swam. I knew it. Of course I knew it. It was the account that paid for the hotel's renovation five years ago, the one that had turned the ground floor into the marble-and-brass temple of respectability the town was so proud of. It was the account Edmund Voss controlled jointly with his business partner.

Aldous Crane.

* * *

I want to be precise about what I felt, because precision is the only integrity I have left some mornings.

It wasn't shock. Shock is for people who believe the world is decent until proven otherwise. I stopped believing that somewhere around the year I found a state senator's luggage receipt tucked inside a Bible he'd left in the chapel of Suite Nine, and the receipt was for a second suitcase, and the suitcase was never checked out, and neither was the senator's conscience.

No. What I felt was the specific vertigo of a pattern snapping into focus. You know it if you've ever done a jigsaw puzzle and suddenly the sky is done, the whole sky, two thousand pieces of nothing becoming everything at once. For six years, someone had been renting silence. Paying for it monthly. Booking it under fake names the way other men book tee times.

And the payments came from an account shared by the two most powerful men in Saltcrest Bay.

I did what I always do with dangerous knowledge: I filed it. Mentally. Under V, cross-referenced under C, with a sub-entry under 2 for room numbers. My memory is a card catalog; I've told you this. What I haven't told you is that the catalog has a locked drawer, and things go in that drawer when knowing them out loud would get someone hurt, usually me.

Then I did something I almost never do. I went looking for more.

* * *

Here is how you read a ledger disguised as a hotel, in case you ever need to. You don't start with the big numbers. Big numbers are for amateurs and auditors. You start with the anomalies too small to notice — the bookings that are *slightly wrong* the way a forged painting is slightly wrong, off in ways no one can name but everyone can feel.

Rule one: real guests have friction. They change their dates. They argue about the rate. They request rooms away from the ice machine. They leave stains, complaints, tips, and occasionally lawsuits. A real guest leaves a wake.

Rule two: fictional guests are smooth. They book, they pay, they vanish. No wake. No wake means no wave. No wave means the surface stays calm, and keeping the surface calm is the entire point.

So I filtered. Out went every booking with a folio charge beyond the room rate. Out went every booking with a phone call, a meal, a spa appointment, a lost-and-found entry. Out went every guest whose name matched an ID scan — and yes, we scanned IDs, because the town council passed an ordinance about it four years ago, sponsored, I now noted with interest, by a councilman whose initials appeared in the 214 log twice.

What remained was beautiful. Horrible, but beautiful. A clean thread running through six years of the Meridian's history: roughly four hundred bookings, all in a rotating cast of perhaps fifty names, all paid from one account, all in a handful of rooms — 214 above all, but also Suite Nine, and, during one fevered autumn, Room 108, the one nearest the service stairs.

Four hundred transactions. If Tamsin was right, four hundred bribes. Or payments. Or silences. Dates that meant court dates, maybe. Room numbers that meant amounts — 214, was that two hundred fourteen thousand? Initials that meant judges, commissioners, chiefs of police.

I cross-checked one. Just one, as a test. *H. Brandt, September, six years ago, fourteen separate one-night bookings.* Fourteen nights in a row of a man who didn't exist, renting a room nobody slept in, paid from an account belonging to the harbor development.

Six years ago, September, there had been a zoning hearing. The harbor expansion — the one that turned the old fishing flats into condominiums and made certain men extremely rich — was tied up in court. The judge who could have killed it was named Hollis Brandt.

The hearing concluded in eleven days. The expansion was approved on day twelve.

H. Brandt had checked out on day thirteen.

I closed the file. I sat in the dark and listened to the lobby clock tick and thought about the word *forever*, which is what the booking history of Room 214 amounted to. Someone had built a machine for buying people and hidden it inside a machine for hosting them, and both machines ran on the same quiet principle: make the guest comfortable, ask no questions, remember everything, tell no one.

I had been the front desk of a bribery operation for six years. I had handed keys — metaphorical keys, but still — to judges and commissioners and God knew who else, and smiled, and said *welcome to the Meridian*, and meant it, because hospitality is sincere even when everything around it is rotten. That's the trick of it. That's the genius. You don't need to corrupt the staff. You just need the staff to be good at their jobs.

I was excellent at my job.

* * *

At 1:40 a.m., the elevator hummed.

I closed the archive window with the calm of a woman who has been closing windows her whole life, and by the time the doors opened I was doing something blameless with a stack of tomorrow's arrival cards, arranging them alphabetically, because order is my rosary.

Edmund Voss crossed the lobby in his dressing gown.

He was seventy-one years old and moved like a man being reeled in slowly by something patient. He didn't see me — or rather, he performed not seeing me, which is different, and which I recognized, because I perform invisibility the same way. He went to the front desk — *my* desk, the desk where I had just spent three hours dismantling his life's secret architecture — and stood there a moment, looking at the arrival cards, at the fern, at the little brass bell.

"Can't sleep," he said, to no one, to the fern, to the night.

"No, sir," I said, to no one, to the night.

He looked at the desk drawer. Just for a second. The drawer with the password inside it. And then he looked at me, and for one unguarded instant — the first in the eleven years I'd worked for him — Edmund Voss looked *afraid*. Not of me. Of the arithmetic of me. Of the sum of what I might have noticed over eleven years of noticing everything.

"Paloma," he said. "You're here late."

"There was filing, sir."

"Filing," he said, tasting the word. "Yes. There's always filing." He stood there another moment, a man in a dressing gown at the edge of his own empire, and then he said, quietly, almost tenderly: "Some files are better left alphabetical, Miss Sterling. Not cross-referenced. Alphabetical. Do you understand?"

"Perfectly, sir," I said, which was a lie, because what I understood was that he had just confirmed everything, confessed everything, and threatened everything, using fewer than twenty words and the tone of a man discussing linen inventory.

He nodded, satisfied, and shuffled back toward the elevator. The doors closed on him. I watched the floor indicator climb to three, where his apartment was, and stop.

Then I reopened the archive, and I pulled the log for tonight, and I found what I already knew I'd find, because of course he'd come down to check, because of course the dying man audits his own ledger:

A booking. Made this evening, at 11:52 p.m., while I was elbow-deep in six years of ghosts.

*Room 214. Guest: E.V. One night. Ten days from now.*

The night of the spring tide.

Edmund Voss had finally booked himself into the room where nobody existed — and whatever he intended to do there, he had given himself ten days to prepare, and he had just looked me in the eye and asked me, in the language of filing, to look the other way.

I keyed the reservation. Confirmed it. Printed the card and set it neatly in the stack, between *Faber* and *Ferreira*, alphabetical, orderly, innocent.

Then I sat back down at the terminal, and I started pulling every booking Aldous Crane's account had ever touched — because if Voss was planning to end something in ten days, I intended to know exactly what, and exactly whom, before the tide came in.

The first result loaded. The account had made one other reservation recently. Not for 214.

For Suite Nine. Tonight. Under a name I'd never seen.

Guest: *T. Marsh.*

I knew that name. Everyone in Saltcrest Bay knew that name. Thomas Marsh had drowned five years ago, out past the pier, in the spring tide — along with his daughter, a girl named Tamsin, whose body they never found.

The elevator hummed again, upstairs. Someone was awake on the third floor. And someone, apparently, had just checked into a room reserved for a dead man.
