## 10. Alibi

The Meridian has three clocks, and I trust none of them. The one behind the desk runs four minutes fast because a slow clock makes guests anxious and an anxious guest tips badly. The one in the kitchen is set by whoever last boiled an egg. And the one in my head — the one that has never once been wrong about how long a lie takes to set — said it was eleven minutes past eleven when Detective Ruiz asked me where I was at 10:40 p.m., and I said, without a flicker, "In the lobby. Watching the desk."

Both statements were true. Neither was the truth. That is the difference between a lie and an alibi, and I have had five years to learn it.

* * *

They took Tamsin through the service corridor, past the laundry, out to the cruiser idling by the loading dock, because the front door would have meant the lobby would have meant witnesses, and the police, whatever else they were, understood theater. I watched from the desk. She didn't struggle. She didn't cry out. She looked back at me exactly once, over the shoulder of the officer guiding her, and her face did something I have spent a career cataloguing on other people and had never once seen in a mirror: it asked me for nothing.

That is the most dangerous request there is. A person who begs you for a lie, you can refuse. A person who asks for nothing leaves you alone in a room with your own conscience, and that room has no checkout time.

Edmund Voss was dead. Not staged-dead, not planned-dead, not the tidy theatrical death he had spent ten days arranging with the care of a man setting a dinner table for his own funeral. Dead-dead. The prop gun in Tamsin's hand had been a prop gun at some point in its life, the way I was at some point in my life an honest woman. Somewhere between the plan and the moment, the machinery had been swapped, and the only two people in the world who knew the plan were standing on opposite sides of a crime scene tape — one of them dead, one of them holding the murder weapon, and me in the middle with a ledger in my head and no one to testify for.

I did what any professional would do. I called the police, and then I called the night porter to bring me coffee, because whatever was coming, it was going to be a long night, and I refuse to conduct history on an empty pot.

* * *

They set up in the Meridian's own conference room, which was either laziness or arrogance or both, and I suppose it was both, because men who have been bought stop worrying about appearances the way men who have stopped bleeding stop worrying about the wound. Detective Ruiz ran the interview. He was a careful man, Ruiz — I'd watched him work two cases in my lobby, and he had the habit of asking questions he already knew the answers to, then writing down your answer anyway, so that the record showed you were a fool in your own handwriting. The third man at the table was from the county. He said little. He had the stillness of a man being paid by the hour to be a wall.

They asked me to walk them through the evening.

"Certainly," I said. "Though I should tell you, I notice things. It's the job."

"We know," Ruiz said. "That's why you're here."

I gave them the evening in the shape of an evening. The lobby at nine. The storm coming in off the water, the bar doing modest business, a honeymooning couple in 305 who had ordered champagne and then argued about it, which I mention because I mentioned it to Ruiz, because a concierge's testimony is only as good as its clutter. Details are the ballast of a lie. A clean story sinks. A story with a champagne argument and a broken umbrella and a delivery of linens at 9:40 floats forever.

"And Mr. Voss?" Ruiz said. "When did you last see him?"

"Ten o'clock, perhaps. He came down, asked for the key to the boathouse. I gave it to him."

That part was true. I had given him the key at ten o'clock, and he had looked at me the way a man looks at the last person he intends to see, and he had said, "Paloma. If anything happens tonight, remember the reservations." I had thought he meant the ledger. I had thought he meant everything.

"He said that?" Ruiz leaned forward. "Remember the reservations?"

"He's an old man who trusts his staff with his hotel. I assumed he meant the morning bookings."

"You assumed."

"I'm paid to assume. Assumption is hospitality's native language."

The county man wrote something. Ruiz watched me the way Ruiz watches everyone — not for the truth, but for the seam of it, the place where the stitching shows. I have been stitched by professionals. I did not show him a seam. What I did not say: that at 10:40 I had been not in the lobby but in the stairwell between the second and third floors, because Tamsin had asked me — no, Tamsin had not asked me anything, Tamsin had said, "He's doing it tonight. I can feel it. I'm going up." And I had said, "Then I'm going up with you," and she had said, "No," and I, who have never once in my life taken no from a guest, had taken it from her, and I had stood in that stairwell listening to a hotel breathe while somewhere above me a plan became a murder.

I gave Tamsin an alibi instead. I told Ruiz that from 10:30 to 10:55, Tamsin Cole — the name on her reservation, the name on her thin paper life — had been in the lobby with me, going over folio discrepancies, that we had argued amiably about a double charge from the bar, that she had signed the adjustment slip at 10:52.

There was no slip. There was no discrepancy. There was a woman in a stairwell and a dead man in a suite and me, at the desk of my own ruin, inventing a signature.

Ruiz asked to see the slip. I produced it. I had produced it while he was asking, my hands doing the work my conscience had already agreed to, because that is the trick of a good lie — you don't tell it, you file it. I had been forging folio adjustments since before Ruiz made detective. The penmanship of a hotel desk is the most trusted handwriting in any town. Nobody audits kindness.

"Convenient," Ruiz said.

"Hotels are convenient. That's the entire industry."

* * *

They kept me forty minutes more, walking me through it again, looking for the place where my story would fatigue. Stories fatigue like people. Mine had been trained. I told them about the champagne couple again, slightly differently, because a witness who repeats herself perfectly has rehearsed, and a witness who varies in the details but holds the spine has simply lived. The spine held: Tamsin, lobby, 10:30 to 10:55. The slip at 10:52. The boathouse key at ten. Edmund Voss alive at ten, and I the last to see him, and I said that plainly, gladly, because a witness who hides a small true thing looks like she's hiding a large false one, and I was done hiding anything that didn't matter.

When they finally stood, Ruiz paused at the door.

"The woman. Cole. You've known her a month."

"A guest is a guest."

"She paid you to prevent a murder. That's an unusual service."

"She paid for a month of rooms and my discretion. The rest was her phrasing. People say dramatic things at desks. I once had a man ask me to hide him from his wife, the FBI, and the moon. I gave him a room facing the courtyard."

Ruiz almost smiled. "And what did you give her?"

"A quiet floor," I said. "And everything I had."

That last part was true, and I let him hear it as sentiment, because the truth makes excellent camouflage when nobody expects it.

* * *

After they left, I sat at the desk in the empty lobby with the storm easing off the glass and did the arithmetic that would keep me up for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turned out to be.

Here is the arithmetic. If Tamsin fired that gun — if the prop had been swapped before she ever picked it up, or after, or if she had picked it up in grief and panic over a father already dying — then my alibi put her somewhere she wasn't, and made me the author of a fiction with a body in it. And if she didn't fire it — if Crane moved faster than Voss planned, if the old man's theater was hijacked by a man who preferred a real corpse to a useful one — then my lie was the only wall standing between an innocent woman and a police force that had already chosen its verdict before it chose its questions.

I didn't know which. That was the thing. I had built her alibi on a foundation of maybe, and I had done it in eleven seconds, without hesitation, and the speed of it frightened me more than the content. Five years ago I had learned to lie for strangers — for the senator and the woman who wasn't his wife, for the judge and the boy, for all the alphabetized secrets in my head. I had told myself those lies were merchandise. Sold, not told. But this one I had given away, freely, at cost, to a woman who had asked me for nothing, and I understood, sitting there while the tide came in under the pier with its slow criminal patience, that the line I had spent five years not crossing was not a line at all. It was a door, and I had been standing in it with my coat on.

I locked the folio adjustment slip in the desk drawer with the boathouse key and the strongbox photograph and the rest of my small archive of dangerous paper, and I thought: Crane will come for the slip. Crane will come for the slip the way water comes for a low room. And when he does, I will be standing in the lobby, watching the desk, and I will smile at him the way I smile at every guest, and I will know something he doesn't.

Because Ruiz had made one mistake, and I had caught it the way I catch everything — late, but completely. When he asked me about the boathouse key, he had said, "the boathouse," and I had never said boathouse. I had said Voss asked for a key. The boathouse was in no report. The boathouse was in no statement. The boathouse was under the pier, bolted beneath the pilings, where the spring tide was already on its way to rising.

Somebody had told Ruiz where to look.

And that somebody wasn't the police.
