## 17. The Trap in Suite Nine

There is a rule of hospitality older than any hotel I've worked in: a guest will tell you everything if you make him feel like the smartest man in the room. Drunks confess to bartenders for the same reason. Priests get the whole catalog because they nod and never compete. The trick is not to extract the secret — it's to arrange the room so the secret feels like his idea to share.

I had spent five years filing other people's confessions. It was time to file one of my own making.

* * *

The morning after Chief Marrero gave me forty-eight hours, I stood in front of the hotel safe in my office and did something I had never once done in eleven years at the Meridian: I opened it in public.

Not all the way open. That would be amateur. I opened it the way you open a door you don't realize is ajar — three inches, a pause, a glance over my shoulder that was just slow enough to catch. Inside, visible to anyone with patience and a vantage point, sat a leather-bound book with a cracked spine and a rubber band around its middle. I had bought it that morning from the stationery shop on Delancey, aged the cover with tea, and cracked the spine myself by bending it back until it complained. The pages inside were the Meridian's old banquet menus from 2019, which no one would ever miss, but which looked, at a glance and in bad light, exactly like what a guilty man expects a ledger to look like: dense, handwritten, numbered.

Because that was the thing about Aldous Crane — and I'd had two days to think about nothing else — he believed the ledger was a book. He had said *book* twice in our conversation, that velvet-voiced lunch where he'd offered me a promotion and a warning in the same breath. *"Whatever Edmund left behind — papers, a book, anything —"* and I had watched his eyes when he said it, and his eyes had gone to my hands, as if he could already see me holding it.

A man who thinks the evidence is a book will look for a book. A man who thinks it's in a safe will come to the safe. And a man who comes to your safe has already told you everything you need to hang him — you only have to get him to say it somewhere other than inside his own head.

Which meant I needed a recording. And to get a recording, I needed Crane relaxed, clever-feeling, and talking.

I needed Suite Nine.

* * *

Suite Nine was the Meridian's best room and its best lie. It sat on the top floor with windows facing the harbor, and over the years it had hosted two governors, one disgraced senator, and a wedding party that cost more than my annual salary. It was also the room where Crane conducted his private business, back when Voss still pretended not to know what his partner did after dinner. I knew this because Room 214 told me — six years of rotating fake names, all paid from an account whose quarterly statements listed, among other disbursements, "Suite Nine accommodations, M.H." The Meridian Hotel billing itself for its own corruption. Even fraud appreciates symmetry.

I called down to housekeeping at seven a.m. "Prep Suite Nine for an arrival tonight. Mr. Crane's preference list."

"Mr. Crane isn't checking in," Marta said.

"He might as well be," I said, and hung up before she could ask the obvious question. Marta had worked here nineteen years. She knew when not to ask. She also happened to be the cousin of Rosa Ibáñez, the chambermaid who had vanished two days ago, leaving her car at the pier and her daughter asking questions at the police station that nobody answered. Marta didn't know yet that I knew about Rosa. She would. Everyone would, eventually. But tonight, all I needed from her was pressed linens and the good decanter.

Then I made the second call, the one that mattered.

"Aldous," I said, when his assistant put me through. "Paloma Sterling. I have something I think you'll want to see before anyone else does."

A pause. I could hear him deciding how surprised to sound. "Paloma. This is a pleasure. What kind of something?"

"The kind Edmund left behind." I let the silence do a lap of the room. "He was more thorough than either of us gave him credit for. I found it yesterday, going through the reservation archives. I've been sitting with it ever since, trying to decide who to call first." Another silence, shorter this time. "You were his partner. It seemed right that it should be you."

"You're a professional, Paloma. I always said so." Warmth poured through the line like brandy. "Where are you keeping it?"

"Where Edmund kept everything important," I said. "At the hotel. Under lock." And then, because the concierge's art is knowing when to stop selling: "Come by this evening. Eight o'clock. The hotel empties out after the dinner service, and I'd rather not do this in front of an audience."

"I appreciate the discretion."

"Oh, I'm very discreet," I said. "It's practically my job description."

It was. For years it had been. I hung up and realized my hands were shaking, and that the shaking wasn't fear, or not only fear. It was the tremor of a woman stepping off a ledge she'd been standing on for eleven years.

* * *

The recorder came from an electronics shop two towns over, paid in cash, bought by a girl I hired through the classifieds — a college student named Devi who asked no questions because I paid double not to ask them. She wired it into the office phone jack and showed me the playback in four minutes flat. "Is this legal?" she asked, zipping her bag.

"It's legal enough for a town where nothing else is," I said.

She nodded like that was a reasonable answer, which in Saltcrest Bay, it was.

But a phone recording alone wouldn't do it. Crane was careful on phones; careful men are careful everywhere except in rooms built to flatter them. So I built him a room.

Suite Nine, by eight o'clock, was a stage set dressed for a confession. The lights were low and amber — Crane liked amber; he'd once sent back a note complaining that the suite's bulbs were "clinical." The good scotch was out, the bottle from the locked cabinet, the one we saved for people whose approval had a price. On the coffee table, positioned with the precision of a florist, lay the fake ledger — closed, rubber-banded, spine up, looking exactly like the most important object in the world. And beside it, a single sheet of paper with three lines typed on it:

**Rm 214 — recurring — acct. verified**
**Initials cross-ref: see index**
**Index removed from premises — copy held**

That last line was the hook. You don't show a guilty man the treasure; you show him the map with one corner torn off. Crane would read *copy held* and understand instantly that even if he walked out with the book, there was a second version somewhere with his name in it, and that the only way to find out where — or whether — was to talk to the woman across the table. Guilty men hate loose ends more than they love money. Loose ends are the only things that can still surprise them.

I put Tamsin in the connecting room, 9B, with instructions I hated giving and she hated receiving.

"If it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," she said. Then, quieter: "If it goes wrong, I come in anyway. You don't get to die for me twice in one month."

"I haven't died for you once."

"Give it time." She was wearing dark clothes and her hair pinned back, and she looked, for the first time since I'd known her, like someone capable of the crime she hadn't committed. "Paloma. He killed my father. Whatever story the staging tells, whatever Voss planned — Crane moved faster, and Crane made it real. Don't forget that when he smiles at you."

"I never forget anything," I said. "It's practically my job description too."

She almost smiled. Then she went into 9B and closed the door, and I stood alone in Suite Nine with a decanter and a dead man's prop and waited for eight o'clock.

* * *

Crane arrived at 8:04, which told me he'd wanted to arrive early and had made himself wait, the way you make yourself wait before opening a gift. He wore gray, impeccably, and the cufflinks — small, silver, monogrammed — caught the amber light. A.C. I had seen those initials in the decoded reservations forty times. He'd been signing his bribes in jewelry for six years.

"Paloma." He took my hand in both of his, warm, lingering half a second past friendly. "You look tired."

"Grief does that," I said. "Even borrowed grief."

He laughed, delighted, and I thought: *there it is.* Men like Crane collect wit the way I collect secrets — proof of their own superiority, freely given. Pour him a drink and let him feel clever, and he'd build his own gallows and compliment the carpentry.

I poured. He settled into the chair angled toward the coffee table — I had placed the single guest chair precisely so that the ledger sat in his natural sightline — and he lasted almost ninety seconds.

"So." His eyes flicked to the book, away, back. Casual. Terrible at it. "Edmund. You said he left something."

"He left a mess," I said, and sat down across from him, and picked up the ledger, and turned it over in my hands without opening it. "Six years of it. You know what the funny thing is? It was in the reservation system the whole time. Dates, rooms, initials. He hid it in the one place nobody looks because it's full of ordinary noise. Ten thousand bookings, and thirty of them were payments." I shook my head with genuine admiration, because the admiration was real. Voss had been a better criminal than he was a father, and that was saying something. "I only found it because I started wondering why Room 214 was occupied every single night by guests who never existed."

Something moved behind Crane's face — quick, quickly buried. Not surprise. *Confirmation.* He'd known about 214. Of course he had. He'd probably suggested it.

"And now you have it all written out," he said pleasantly. "In a book."

"In a book," I agreed. "Old habits. I'm a paper person. Screens can be hacked. Paper has to be stolen." I slid it an inch toward him and watched his pupils dilate. "Of course, an index like this is only useful while it's current. Which raises an awkward question about copies."

"Does it." He reached for the book.

I lifted it away, gently, like taking a toy from a child you actually like. "Before you touch it, Aldous, I need us to understand each other. Because I've been thinking about what happens after tonight, and there are versions of after that I like and versions I don't."

"Name your terms." All the warmth still in his voice, but the temperature underneath had changed. The brandy was gone from it.

"Rosa Ibáñez," I said.

The pause was beautiful. Two seconds of a man deciding whether to know who I meant, and failing, because I'd already watched his face when I said the name and there was nothing there — no question mark, no search. He knew. He'd known before I finished the sentence.

"I don't follow."

"She saw you outside Suite Seven the night Edmund died. She told the police, or tried to, and then her car turned up at the pier and she didn't. I'd like her to turn up again. Alive. That's term one."

"You're upset," he said, with magnificent condescension, "and you're misreading circumstances. Rosa took personal leave. People do."

"On foot, leaving her car and her purse?" I leaned forward. "Aldous. I'm offering you a trade, and trades require honesty, so let me save us both the theater. You went to Suite Seven that night. You knew about the staging — maybe Edmund told you himself, maybe you read it in his calendar, I genuinely don't care — and instead of letting a dying man have his last performance, you swapped the prop gun for a real one and let everyone believe the plan ran on schedule. You murdered a man and used his own suicide note as your alibi." I sat back. "Say I'm wrong. Say it plainly, and I'll hand you the book and we'll both sleep well."

And there it was — the moment I'd built the whole evening around. The trap in Suite Nine wasn't the recorder or the fake ledger or the torn-corner index. The trap was the invitation. Guilty men cannot bear being doubted by someone who knows too much. They correct the record. They *explain*. Especially to a woman holding a drink and a book and no weapon at all, especially when they believe they're the smartest man in the room and the smartest move is to charm her into partnership.

Crane swirled his scotch. Looked at the ledger. Looked at me. And smiled the smile of a man about to do something he thought was clever.

"Paloma. Sweet, meticulous Paloma." He set down the glass. "Do you know why Edmund's little theater was going to work? Because everyone wants it to have worked. A sick man stages his death to expose corruption — it's operatic, it's noble, the town gets its scandal and its martyr in one package. All I did was keep the schedule. If the gun was real—" a small shrug, elegant, damning, forever— "then the play simply had a different author. And the author, unlike Edmund, doesn't leave ledgers lying around." He extended his hand for the book. "Now. The index. Where's the copy?"

"Under the pilings," I said, "at low tide. Where Edmund hid the strongbox. Where you've been looking for it for two weeks — which is why Rosa's car is at the pier, isn't it? You thought she'd seen you dig."

He was on his feet before I finished the sentence, and the charm was gone entirely, and what was underneath was worse than anger: it was arithmetic. I watched him calculate — the book, the copy, the witness, the woman — and I watched him decide I was a problem with a simple solution, and I felt the fear arrive late and cold, exactly on schedule, because I had planned for it to arrive *after* the words I needed were already spoken and already recorded on a line wired to a device in a drawer four feet from his elbow.

"You should have taken the promotion," Aldous Crane said.

"Yes," I agreed. "Everyone says that."

He lunged for the ledger, and I let him have it — let the rubber band snap, let the banquet menus fan across the carpet, let two full seconds of his confusion bloom — and in those two seconds the door of 9B opened and Tamsin stepped out with the poker from the fireplace in 9A held like she'd been born holding it.

"Hello, Aldous," she said. "You remember my father's suite."

Crane turned, and I saw him understand, all at once, the shape of the room he'd been sitting in — and I pressed the button in my pocket that stopped the recording, and said, in my best concierge voice, the one I use for guests who have broken something expensive:

"Sir. I'm afraid checkout was at noon."
