## 1. The Retainer

The rain had been falling on Harrow's Bend for three days without conviction, a thin gray drizzle that seemed less like weather than like the town forgetting to stop something. Felix Zhou heard it through the floorboards of his basement office, a sound he'd long ago stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Above him, the county morgue kept its regular hours. Below, in this room with its single high window and its smell of old paper and colder coffee, Felix did the work the county didn't know it was paying him to do.

Officially, he was the coroner. Unofficially — in the ledger he kept locked in his desk drawer, written in his own private shorthand — he was something else. Twenty-two deaths certified as accident, suicide, or misadventure over eleven years, each one carrying a small marginal note in his hand: *staged*. He had never once said so aloud. Saying so aloud would mean testimony, and testimony meant attention, and attention was the one thing Felix Zhou had spent his whole adult life learning to live without. Invisibility wasn't cowardice. It was craft. A man who saw what others missed could only survive by being the kind of man others never looked at twice.

The knock came at four in the afternoon, when the light through the high window had gone the color of dishwater.

He almost didn't answer it. Nobody knocked on that door. The stairs down from the street were narrow and unlit, and the townspeople who needed their dead explained went around to the front entrance upstairs, where there was a receptionist and a sign-in sheet. Whoever was knocking had found him anyway.

"Come in."

She descended the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and Felix understood within three seconds that he was looking at a dying woman. Not sick — dying. There is a difference, and coroners learn it early: the sick are fighting something, but the dying have already negotiated terms. Tamsin Voss was perhaps forty, thin in the way of someone whose body had begun spending itself faster than it could earn, dressed expensively but carelessly, as though clothes had become a language she no longer spoke fluently. Her hair was dark going gray at the temples, and her eyes —

Her eyes were the problem. They were too awake. They moved across his office the way a person reads a will, cataloguing, assessing, deciding what she could afford to lose.

"Mr. Zhou." Her voice was low and even, an instrument tuned past trembling. "I'm told you're the man who notices things."

"I'm the county coroner."

"You're the county coroner," she agreed, "who has quietly disagreed with the county eleven times." She let that sit between them. Rain ticked against the window glass. "Don't worry how I know. Worry instead about why."

Felix set down his pen. Every instinct he owned — and he owned good ones, honed on the bodies of people whose stories had been rewritten after they stopped — told him to end this now. *Ma'am, if you have a complaint about a ruling, take it to the district attorney.* Send her up the stairs into the daylight where she belonged, back into the world of people who wanted things loudly.

Instead he said, "Sit down, Ms.—"

"Voss. Tamsin Voss."

The name landed somewhere in him and rang faintly, like a bell struck in another room. He filed it away and watched her lower herself into the chair across from his desk, folding her hands in her lap with the composure of someone who had rehearsed this conversation in front of a mirror until the mirror gave up.

"I want to hire you," she said, "to investigate my murder."

"Your—"

"My death," she corrected herself, with a small, terrible precision. "Which has not yet occurred. And which, when it does occur, will be ruled an accident. I want you to prove otherwise. Afterward."

The rain filled the silence. Felix looked at her hands — the knuckles prominent, the skin translucent, one thin gold ring loose on a finger that had lost weight since it was fitted — and then at her face, waiting for the tell. The flicker. The performance. People who came to him with conspiracies always had a tell somewhere, some seam where the story showed its stitching.

He couldn't find hers. That frightened him more than anything else she'd said.

"Ms. Voss," he said carefully, "I don't investigate crimes that haven't happened. I certify the ones that have."

"And you do it beautifully," she said. "That's rather the point. You're very good at making death look tidy. So is whoever is going to kill me. I need someone equally fluent in the language." She reached into her bag — worn leather, expensive once — and drew out an envelope, setting it on the desk between them. It was heavy. It made a sound money doesn't usually make. "Triple your standard rate. Half now, half on delivery of your findings."

"Findings about a crime that hasn't happened yet."

"About a crime that is scheduled," she said. "There's a difference, Mr. Zhou, and I suspect you're one of perhaps five people in this state who'd understand it. Sometime in the next ten days, I am going to die. It will look like an accident. A fall, most likely — I'm frail, everyone knows it, frailty is the perfect accomplice. And the person arranging it will walk out of the funeral with everything they've wanted for thirty years, and no one will ever ask a single question." She paused. "Except you. Because I'm asking you to."

Felix looked at the envelope. Triple rate. Enough to fix the leaking pipe in the corner, enough to buy years of quiet, and quiet was the only currency he'd ever valued. Taking this retainer meant stepping into the light. It meant a name in someone's files, a face in someone's memory, a thread leading back to this basement room.

Everything in him said refuse.

"Who?" he asked. Damn him. "Who arranges it?"

"That's what I'm paying you to find out." She smiled then, briefly, and it transformed her — not into beauty exactly, but into evidence of what she'd been before the dying started. "Though I'll give you a starting point, because I'm fair. My father died thirty years ago. Fell from the quarry overlook on the Voss property. Ruled accidental. I was fourteen." She drew a folded paper from her bag next, yellowed and soft at the creases, and laid it beside the envelope. "This is his death certificate. I've carried it for thirty years because I've never believed it, and because I can't read it anymore — not the part that matters."

Felix unfolded it with the automatic gentleness of his trade. The document was standard county stock, 1994, the typewriter font slightly uneven, the ink faded to the brown of old tea. Cause of death: blunt force trauma consistent with fall. Manner: accident. Signed and certified by the coroner of record, a name he knew from history, a man two decades dead himself.

But it was the handwriting that stopped him.

Not the typed fields — the annotation in the margin, a small notation in blue-black ink, slanted leftward, with that peculiar hooked terminal on the final letter that he had seen a thousand times. Had seen it in archived files. Had seen it in his own training materials, in the case studies used to teach certification, held up as a model of thoroughness. A hand he knew the way you know a voice on the telephone before the caller identifies themselves.

His own mentor's hand. The man who had taught Felix to read a body like a sentence, who had retired to the coast and died respectable — writing margin notes on the 1994 death certificate of Edmund Voss.

The room seemed to tilt a degree, the way a photograph does when you notice something wrong in the background.

"Where did you get this?" His voice came out lower than he intended.

"It was my father's copy. Why?" She leaned forward, and her too-awake eyes fixed on his face, reading him now the way he read the dead. "You recognize something."

Felix said nothing. The rain kept its gray time above them. Thirty years of paper sat in the county archive two blocks away, and somewhere in those boxes, he suddenly understood, was a story that had been waiting all this time for someone fluent enough to read it — and a woman sitting across from him who had maybe ten days left to hear it.

"How much of the half do you want tonight?" Tamsin Voss asked.

* * *

He should have refused. He told himself that later, walking her up the narrow stairs into the drizzle, watching her get into a black car driven by a man who never once looked at Felix directly — a man built like furniture, patient and upholstered. He told himself it all the way back down the stairs, all the way through unlocking the drawer and adding a twenty-third line to his private ledger:

*Voss, Tamsin. Hired: to prove a death not yet committed.*

And beneath it, after a long moment, the thing he hadn't said aloud:

*Certificate annotated by R. Calloway. Why?*

His mentor. Dead four years, buried with honors, eulogized as the finest coroner the county ever produced. And thirty years ago, standing over the body of Edmund Voss at the bottom of a quarry, writing notes in the margin that a fourteen-year-old girl had been carrying ever since like a stone in her pocket.

Felix turned off the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the rain, feeling reluctant curiosity curdle slowly into dread. Ten days, she'd said. A fall, she'd guessed.

He reached for his coat. The archive closed at six, and he intended to be the last one inside it.
