## 14. The Poisoner's Arithmetic

The garden did its work at dawn.

I had walked past the foxgloves a hundred times that summer without seeing them—purple spires nodding along the south wall of the Marlowe Grand's kitchen garden, where the chef sent the sous-boys to cut herbs and where, I now understood, anyone with a pair of scissors and five unsupervised minutes could cut something else entirely. Digitalis purpurea. The common foxglove. Beautiful, ordinary, and in sufficient quantity, quietly lethal to an aging heart.

I stood in the wet grass at six in the morning with my coat collar up and my notebook open, counting plants. Forty-one spires. Three of them freshly cut, the stems snapped low where a careful hand had taken only what it needed and left the rest to keep growing. Not stripped bare, the way panic strips things. Pruned. Curated. The work of someone who intended to come back.

I wrote down *41* and underlined it twice, because numbers were the one language I had never once mistrusted.

Then I went inside, made myself a pot of black tea I would not drink, and sat down in the back office with every record the Marlowe Grand owned about what Sir Aldous Vane ate in the last two weeks of his life.

* * *

You would be astonished how much a grand hotel remembers if you ask it politely.

Sir Aldous had dined in his suite for eleven consecutive nights before his collapse—a habit of his final years, after Ysolde's mother died and he stopped trusting himself to sit in the dining room without weeping into the soup. Every tray was logged. Every course itemized by the kitchen, initialed by the floor staff, countersigned by the manager on duty. Forty years of Odile Ferrant's discipline ran through those pages like a spine through a body: dates, times, dishes, deliveries, signatures. A complete arithmetic of an old man's appetite.

I laid the pages out across two pushed-together desks and read them the way I read ledgers—backwards, from the end toward the cause.

The night he collapsed, Sir Aldous had eaten sole meunière, potatoes fondantes, a small salad, and taken his usual glass of Burgundy. The toxicology report—which Detective Inspector Havelock had shared with me under protest, on the strength of my testimony being "materially relevant," a phrase she used the way one uses a leash—had found digoxin in quantities consistent with a large single dose. That was the prosecution's story. One poisoned dinner. One poisoned dish. One person who delivered it.

Mirabel had carried the tray up at 7:42 that evening. Her signature was on the log. Her fingerprints were on the wine glass. Case closed, said the Crown, with the tidy satisfaction of people who believe arithmetic is finished once you've found a number you like.

But arithmetic is never finished when you stop early. That is not arithmetic; that is wishful bookkeeping.

Because here is what the ledger told me, and what no one at the trial had thought to add up: digoxin does not behave like arsenic. It does not sit in a dish waiting patiently to be eaten all at once. It accumulates. It builds in the blood over days, nudging a heart slower and slower until either it recovers or it doesn't. And the concentration found in Sir Aldous's blood was too high—*significantly* too high—for a single dose administered at dinner, even a generous one. Havelock's own expert witness had fudged this on the stand, calling the levels "consistent with" acute poisoning, which is lawyer-language for *we have decided not to do the division*.

So I did the division.

Working backward from the post-mortem concentration, accounting for clearance rates in a seventy-eight-year-old man with the mild cardiac insufficiency his own physician had noted years earlier, the total dose in Sir Aldous's body could not have arrived in one evening. It had to have been building. Three days, at minimum. Possibly four.

Which meant the poisoning had not happened on the night Mirabel carried the tray.

It had happened on three nights. Or four. Quietly, cumulatively, each dose calibrated so that no single dinner would kill him—so that no single tray, no single server, no single signature would ever point anywhere. The fatal night was simply the night the sum came due.

I sat back and looked at the pages spread before me and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from admiration. It was elegant. Whoever had done this had understood the drug, understood the old man's routines, and understood the ledger—understood that a hotel's perfect records would become its perfect alibi, because every entry looked innocent. Sole meunière. Potatoes fondante. A glass of Burgundy. Nothing on any single page worth a second glance.

The murder weapon was not poison. The murder weapon was repetition.

* * *

Three dinners. I pulled the logs for the four nights preceding the collapse and laid them side by side.

Night one: pheasant, logged as prepared by the kitchen, delivered by the floor steward Pellam, signed for by Sir Aldous himself. Night two: lamb, same delivery chain. Night three—the night before the collapse—sole meunière again, and here the log showed a small irregularity I had marked in green ink weeks ago and not yet understood: the delivery time was recorded as 7:38, but the kitchen's own chit timed the dish leaving the pass at 7:51. Thirteen minutes unaccounted for. A tray sitting somewhere between the kitchen and the fourth floor with no one watching it.

And on night three, the signature at the bottom of the delivery line was not Pellam's.

It was initialled *O.F.*

Odile Ferrant had personally delivered Sir Aldous's dinner exactly once in the entire two-week window. Once, in forty years of delegating everything, the hotel's manager had carried a tray herself—and it was the thirteenth-minute night, the night with the gap, the last night before the sum came due.

I told myself there were innocent explanations. Of course I did; I am paid to believe in innocent explanations the way priests are paid to believe in forgiveness. Perhaps Pellam was ill. Perhaps she was passing. I checked the staffing roster: Pellam was on shift, hale, and logged as having served two other trays that same hour. No illness. No emergency. Just a manager who, for thirteen minutes on one specific evening, wanted her hands on a specific plate.

But here is where the arithmetic turned cruel, and where I want to be precise, because precision is the only inheritance I will ever leave anything.

If the doses were spread across three nights, then whoever administered them needed access to Sir Aldous's food on *all three* nights—not just the last one. And access to a tray in the Marlowe Grand is not a family privilege. It is a staff function. Casper Vane had not set foot in the service corridors since the funeral arrangements began; the corridor cameras—he insisted on them after a burglary scare in the spring—showed him entering through the front doors on each of the three evenings, dining downstairs with investors, and leaving by midnight. Rhys Vane had been in Cardiff until two days before the collapse; his train tickets were in the confession file Havelock let me copy. Ysolde had taken her meals in her room, yes—but her room was serviced, her trays logged, and her movements on the relevant evenings placed her, by her own maid's testimony and the lift register, on the second floor visiting a friend's suite until nearly ten.

Mirabel had carried trays, true. On night one and night two among them. But—and I checked this three times, because I wanted desperately to be wrong—on both of those nights, the kitchen chits matched the delivery times to the minute. No gaps. No unwatched minutes. Whatever entered Sir Aldous's food on the first two nights had not entered it through any window Mirabel's hands had passed through.

The gaps—the unaccounted minutes, the trays alone in corridors, the substitutions of hands—all clustered around one pattern. Staff. Floor stewards, kitchen passes, service stairs. And one name, appearing at the edges of the irregularities with the quiet persistence of damp appearing at the edges of a wall.

I am not ready to write that name in ink yet. In pencil, though—in pencil, heavily—I wrote *O.F.* and drew a box around it, and then, because I am who I am, I drew a second box around the first, the way one quarantines a number one does not yet trust oneself to believe.

* * *

Bex found me at half past nine, still at the pushed-together desks, surrounded by paper like a man building a raft out of receipts.

"You haven't gone home," Bex said. It wasn't a question. Night porters have a genius for statements shaped like observations.

"I'm doing sums."

"At this hour?" Bex came closer, squinting at the spread of logs, and I watched something move behind their eyes—not curiosity exactly. Recognition. "That's his dinners. The last week."

"It is."

Bex stood very still. Then they pulled the third chair around backwards and sat on it the wrong way, arms folded on the backrest, which is what Bex does instead of deciding to trust someone. "Can I say a thing I've been not-saying for two months?"

"You may."

"The night he collapsed. I told the police about the teacup and they wrote it down and nothing happened, and I told myself that was fine, that maybe it didn't matter." Bex's fingers picked at the chair's upholstery seam. "But I've been doing my own sums, sort of. Porter-style. And there's a thing about the service stairs."

"Go on."

"That night—before the collapse, maybe twenty minutes before—I saw someone coming down. Service stairs, fourth-floor landing. They had the wall light off on the landing above, which nobody does by accident; that switch sticks, you have to hold it. Someone held it off so they could come down dark." Bex swallowed. "I didn't see a face. But I saw the shoes. Black, polished, low heel. And—" they hesitated, and I watched them decide, visibly, whether the next sentence would cost them something, "—and I smelled bergamot. That hand cream the manager uses. The whole stairwell smelled of it after they passed."

Bergamot. I kept my face still, which is the hardest work I do. "Anyone might wear such a cream."

"Sure," Bex said. "Anyone. But how many people take the service stairs from the fourth floor at 7:30 at night with the lights held off? Guests don't know that switch sticks. Family doesn't use those stairs at all—they'd sooner be seen dead than seen carrying." Bex looked at the logs, at my green ink, at the double-boxed initials, and I saw them arrive at the same sum I had, independently, the way two auditors opening the same fraudulent book will sometimes flinch at the same line. "You've got a name in a box there."

"I have a hypothesis in a box," I said. "Hypotheses are not convictions."

"No," Bex agreed. "But mine's getting heavier." They stood, turned the chair back around, and paused at the door. "Emrys. If your sums are right—if it took three nights—then whoever did it wasn't hiding from the cameras or the logs. They were *using* them. Using the fact that everyone looks at the family and nobody looks at us." A pause. "Nobody looks at us, Emrys. That's supposed to protect us. It's the only thing we've got."

They left. I sat with that for a long while, because Bex had said something true enough to bruise: the invisibility of staff is our shelter, and someone had turned it into a weapon, and every one of us who had ever been grateful not to be noticed had, in some small way, held the door for her.

* * *

By midnight I had the whole construction pinned to the corkboard behind the office door, and I will reproduce it here plainly, because a case should be able to survive being stated simply:

One. Foxglove grows in the hotel garden; three stems recently harvested by a careful hand.

Two. Digoxin accumulates; the fatal concentration required three or more administrations, not one.

Three. Each administration required unobserved access to Sir Aldous's tray. The logs show such windows on precisely three nights, and on none of them did the accused have opportunity without also having company.

Four. The person with reliable daily access—who could reach the tray on any night without arousing comment, whose presence near a dinner tray would never be recorded as remarkable because it never was remarkable—was not an heir. Was not family at all.

Five. The family had motive, perhaps. Money always gives the family motive. But the family lacked the *means*, and means are the load-bearing wall of any case. Take it out and the whole prosecution comes down.

The suspect pool did not narrow so much as it drained—family names running out through the cracks of trains and cameras and lift registers, until the pool held only the people who had always been there, invisible, indispensable, trusted. The people with keys. The people with routines. The people whose signatures appeared on every page of Sir Aldous's last two weeks because they appeared on every page of his last forty years.

I looked at the double-boxed initials for a long time. Then I did what I had been avoiding since morning: I pulled the personnel file, and beneath forty years of commendations and Christmas bonuses and handwritten notes in Sir Aldous's own hand—*Odile is the Marlowe Grand*, he had written, *more than the bricks*—I found the thing I had been looking for without knowing its shape.

A gap. Thirty-one years ago, eight months of employment history that simply did not exist. Before the gap: a junior housekeeper hired at nineteen. After it: a manager, appointed suddenly, promoted over three senior candidates, with a note from Sir Aldous authorizing the appointment that gave no reason at all.

People are promoted for competence. People are promoted *instantly*, against precedent, with reasons withheld, for secrets.

I closed the file and sat in the dark office with the rain starting against the window, and I understood that I was no longer asking who killed Sir Aldous Vane. I was asking what Sir Aldous Vane had known, thirty-one years ago, that had bought a lifetime of loyalty—and what he had learned, in the last week of his life, that had ended it.

The answer, whatever it was, lived in the ledgers. The ones still standing in the archive.

And the ones, I remembered with a cold turn of the stomach, that Odile Ferrant had asked to borrow "for the memorial inventory"—three days ago, and not yet returned.
