## 18. Offered a Place at the Table

The summons came the way all dangerous things come to a concierge: on cream cardstock, hand-delivered, with no return address.

*Mr. E. Thornbury. The Vane Suite, 8 o'clock. Dress is irrelevant.*

I read it twice at the front desk, then set it face-down beside the bell. Around me the Marlowe Grand went about its evening as if nothing had changed—as if its owner weren't eleven days dead and one of his heirs weren't sitting in a cell across town waiting for a jury to decide whether she'd put foxglove in his tea. The lobby chandelier threw its usual amber light over the marble. A porter wheeled luggage toward the lift. Somewhere in the kitchen, someone was reducing a sauce.

Only I knew that under my arm, in a folio disguised as a stack of guest correspondence, sat thirty years of ciphered ledgers that could hang a murderer or unmake a family. And only I knew which of those two outcomes the Vanes preferred.

I took the service stairs up to the fourth floor. Old habit. The main staircase has eyes.

* * *

The Vane Suite had been closed since the funeral—sheets over the furniture, windows cracked against the smell of lilies left too long. Someone had opened it for tonight. Two lamps burned low. Casper Vane stood at the mantel with a glass of whisky he wasn't drinking, and Ysolde sat rigid on the settee in charcoal silk, her hands folded like a woman posing for a portrait she didn't consent to. Rhys was by the window, half-turned away, watching the rain come down over the square.

Three heirs. The fourth was in Hollowbrook Prison, and the fifth—the true heir, though none of them said the word aloud—was downstairs signing invoices in an office she'd kept for forty years.

"Thornbury." Casper turned. He had his father's height and none of his father's warmth; grief had carved him into something angular. "Thank you for coming."

"You rang the front desk," I said. "It's rather what I'm for."

Ysolde's laugh came out brittle and died fast. "Always the professional. Papa adored that about you. He used to say you were the only person in this building who never wanted anything from him."

"He was generous with flattery," I said. "He paid for it, after all."

Rhys shifted at the window but said nothing. He'd been avoiding my eyes since the boiler room, since I'd caught Odile feeding decades of ledgers into the incinerator and she'd smiled at me with a face gone smooth as porcelain. Since Rhys had confessed, in a stairwell smelling of ash, that he'd written the letter—or believed he had—and that his interest in justice began and ended at the estate's edge.

Casper set down the untouched whisky. "We'll be direct. You've been asking questions. Staff schedules, room bookings, dates going back thirty years. Bexley the night porter has been seen talking to you. Mirabel's solicitor received an anonymous note last week referencing—" he paused, choosing the word carefully, "—*irregularities* in the registry."

"Irregularities," I repeated. "Is that what we're calling them?"

"We're calling them what they are," Ysolde snapped. "A problem. One that a trial is currently solving for us, if certain people would stop interfering."

The rain ticked against the glass. I let the silence do its work; silence always does. It was Ysolde who broke first, because Ysolde had never learned that the person who speaks second owns the room.

"Miri didn't do it," she said. "You know she didn't. We know she didn't. But the jury doesn't need to know anything except that someone did, and she's the one they've already decided on. She was the ward. She had access. She inherits nothing and loses everything either way, so the arithmetic is simple—"

"The arithmetic," I said, "is murder."

"No." Casper's voice dropped, quiet and terrible in its calm. "The arithmetic is survival. Sit down, Thornbury. Please. What we have to offer you will take longer than your pride will."

I remained standing. He took it as agreement enough.

* * *

"There is a version of events," Casper began, pacing now, "in which Sir Aldous Vane died of natural causes. An old man, a weak heart, a lifetime of brandy and grievance. The inquest noted cardiac failure. The prosecution built its case on opportunity, not certainty, and juries convict on stories, not chemistry. If the ledger disappears—if these *irregularities* stay buried—Mirabel is convicted of a crime she may or may not have committed, the family endures a scandal it can survive, and the Marlowe Grand keeps its name off the front pages."

"And the version where the truth comes out?"

"Then the truth comes out." He stopped pacing. "All of it. Not just the poisoning. The swap. Thirty years of it. My mother's shame, my father's rage, the fact that the woman who ran this hotel for four decades is—"

"Don't," Ysolde said sharply.

"—is *family*," Casper finished, "and none of us knew, and every inheritance, every title, every legal document in this family rests on a lie older than Mirabel. Do you understand what that means? It means years of litigation. It means the estate frozen. It means reporters digging through my mother's grave and Rhys's parentage and God knows what else. It means the Marlowe Grand becomes a *story*, and hotels die of stories, Thornbury. They die of them the way people die of fevers."

I thought of Odile Ferrant in her office, straightening papers that were already straight. Forty years of belonging, purchased with a secret, defended with foxglove. And here were her nephews and niece proposing to buy their own belonging with the same currency: a girl in a cell who loved them.

"What exactly," I asked, "are you offering me?"

Ysolde leaned forward. This was her part; I could see she'd rehearsed it. "A position. Not concierge—*estates manager*. Full authority over the Marlowe Grand's operations, a seat on the trust board, salary commensurate with—well, it won't matter to you, but it's substantial. And more than that." Her voice softened into something almost tender, and that was the most frightening thing in the room. "A place. Papa liked you, Emrys. May I call you Emrys? Papa trusted you, and so do we. You've spent twenty years keeping this hotel's secrets. We're asking you to make it official. To be *ours*. Family confidence. Permanently."

"Family," I said. "I'm not a Vane."

"You could be the next best thing," Rhys said quietly, from the window. "The keeper of it. Every great house has one—a person outside the blood who holds it together. You'd never have to wonder again whose side you're on. You'd be on the house's side. Forever."

There it was. Laid out on the settee between the lamps like something precious and rotten: belonging. Twenty years of standing behind a desk while other people arrived and departed, other people inherited and mourned and belonged—and here was the door, held open, with a price tag written in a convicted girl's name.

I looked at each of them in turn. Casper, who wanted the scandal buried because scandal would freeze the estate he'd already begun spending in his mind. Ysolde, who wanted her mother's shame kept underground where she'd buried it herself long ago. Rhys, who had sent the confession letter believing himself the swapped child, and who now stood silent because the offer on the table served him too—if the ledger vanished, his claim died quietly, and so did the question of what he truly was.

And beneath it all, the missing voice. Mirabel, who had refused my help once already, in a prison visiting room, because exposing the birthright secret would destroy the family she loved. She had chosen their names over her own neck. And they were choosing theirs over her life.

"You rehearsed this," I said. "All three of you. Who spoke first—her?" I nodded toward the floor, toward the offices below us, where a woman who never left was surely working late. "Or did Odile simply approve the terms?"

The silence answered me. Casper's jaw tightened. Ysolde looked at her hands.

"She suggested we make you comfortable," Casper admitted finally. "She said you were reasonable. She said everyone has a price, and yours was obvious—you've been alone in that lobby for twenty years, watching everyone else go home to lives. She said you'd take it."

I felt something cold settle in my chest, and recognized it, after a moment, as clarity.

"Tell her," I said, "that she's right. Everyone does have a price. Mine is a teacup, a key log, and a night porter's testimony—and I already own all three."

Ysolde stood. "Emrys—"

"The ledger stays. Mirabel walks. And when the jury asks who poisoned Sir Aldous Vane, this family is going to say the name out loud before I do, because the difference between a conspiracy and a family is that a family protects its children—all of them, even the ones who came in through the wrong door." I picked up my folio. "You have until closing arguments. That's not a threat. It's a reservation. And you know how seriously we take reservations here."

I was at the door when Casper spoke, and there was something new in his voice—not anger, not quite. Fear.

"Thornbury. If you do this—if you burn it all down—do you know what happens to *you*? You'll have testified against the family that gave you everything. No reference in England will touch you. You'll be finished in this profession."

I considered it honestly, the way I consider everything: the cost, the alternative, the arithmetic.

"I've spent twenty years being indispensable to people who never learned my first name," I said. "I think I can survive being free of you."

I opened the door—and stopped.

Odile Ferrant was standing in the corridor, one hand raised to knock. She was in her evening black, immaculate as ever, and she was looking past me into the suite at her three nieces and nephews with an expression I had never seen on her face in twenty years of service.

Not guilt. Not fear.

Possession.

"Mr. Thornbury," she said pleasantly, stepping aside to let me pass. "Do walk me down. I believe we have a few matters of hotel business to discuss—you and I. Privately."

And as I fell into step beside her toward the service stairs, she said, without turning her head, in the mild tone of a woman discussing linen orders:

"I hear you've been reading my ledgers, Emrys. All the way back to 412. Tell me—how much of the ending have you worked out? Because I should very much like to know how close you are, before I decide what happens next."

The stairwell lights clicked off behind us, one floor at a time, all the way down.
