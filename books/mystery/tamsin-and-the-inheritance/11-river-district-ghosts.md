## 11. River District Ghosts

The river district smelled of diesel and old rope, and by three in the morning it smelled mostly of Quentin's coffee going cold in the cupholder of his own cab, parked nose-out between a shuttered chandlery and a wall of stacked crab traps that had been there so long the traps were growing their own ecosystem. He'd been on stakeout for six hours. His back had opinions about it. His mind, for once, was quiet — narrowed down to a single point, the way it always went when a case stopped being a job and started being a hunger.

Ferry Terminal Four. The last stop on the fare.

He'd spent two days pulling the thread. The taxi log from the night Tamsin Vey was supposed to have died — four years ago now, though the trial paperwork treated it like yesterday — showed a pickup at 11:47 p.m., eleven minutes after her declared time of death. Female passenger, thirties, dark coat, paid cash, asked to be taken to the river district and no further. The driver, a man named Petrosian who'd since retired to a sister's house upstate, remembered her only as "the quiet one who watched the mirrors." Quentin had driven out to ask him in person, and Petrosian had said something that hadn't left Quentin since: *She kept checking behind us like she expected the road itself to be following.*

Now Quentin sat watching Terminal Four do what Terminal Four did at 3 a.m., which was nothing. A security light buzzed over the ticket booth. Rain came down in that fine, patient drizzle that didn't fall so much as accumulate, beading on the windshield until the wipers dragged them off with a sound like a slow inhale. Across the water, the city's lights smeared themselves into long orange wounds on the black surface.

He'd told himself he'd give it one night. He knew better. He'd already called the dispatch office and traded his next two shifts for a favor he'd owe double, and he'd brought a thermos, a change of socks, and the particular grim equipment of a man who has decided that sleep is a problem other people have.

*Obsession,* Odile would say, if she could see him. She'd say it in that careful lawyer voice, the one that dressed worry up as procedure. *Mr. Okonkwo, we are attempting to establish reasonable doubt, not write a biography.* But Odile hadn't sat in Petrosian's kitchen watching an old man's hands shake around his tea while he described a dead woman checking her mirrors. Odile wasn't the one who'd started noticing the shape of the hole where Tamsin Vey should have been — a hole too clean, too well-cut, the records around it smoothed like river stones. Holes like that weren't made by accidents. They were made by tools.

And Quentin had begun to suspect, in the small hours, that he knew the feeling of those tools from the inside.

He pushed that thought back down where it lived and drank the cold coffee instead.

*

At 4:12, the dockworker arrived.

Quentin had done his homework on Terminal Four's skeleton crew: two men on the overnight maintenance rotation, one of whom — a broad, gray-bearded man named Hollis, according to the port authority roster — had worked the terminal for nineteen years. Nineteen years meant memory. Memory was the whole currency of this case, and everyone else's had been debased. Quentin wanted to see if Hollis's had held its value.

He gave the man twenty minutes to settle in, then got out of the cab and walked over through the rain, badge-less, hands visible, the posture of a man with nothing to hide and nowhere urgent to be. In his experience, the trick with working people at four in the morning was not to seem like work.

"Cold one," Quentin said.

Hollis looked up from a clipboard he was holding more for company than use. He had a face like weathered pilings and eyes that did a quick, professional inventory — cab, stranger, hour — before settling into wary neutrality. "Cold every one," he said. "Terminal's closed till five-forty."

"Not here for the ferry." Quentin leaned against a bollard, close enough to talk, far enough not to crowd. "Here about a passenger. Four years back, late October. Woman, thirties, dark coat, cash fare. Would've come off the last boat or near it."

Something moved behind Hollis's eyes. Not recognition exactly. More like a man hearing a floorboard creak in a house he thought he knew.

"Don't keep four years of faces," Hollis said.

"No," Quentin agreed. "But you remember this one. Because she did something nobody does."

Hollis set the clipboard down. Slowly. Like a man buying time. "What's that?"

"She paid you to forget her."

The rain filled the silence. Out on the water, a buoy clanked its idiot bell somewhere in the dark.

Hollis's jaw worked. "I don't know what you—"

"You're doing great," Quentin said, and meant it, because he'd watched enough people lie to know the difference between a liar and a man fighting his own head. Hollis wasn't constructing a story. He was standing in front of a door he couldn't account for, and it scared him. "Let me guess how it went. End of your shift, she comes up to you. Friendly, ordinary, asks a couple of questions — did anyone come looking for a woman matching her description, had anyone asked about the ferry schedule. Then she pays you. Cash, more than it's worth, and she says something like—" Quentin paused, letting the words arrive rather than forcing them, "—something like *you never saw me*, or *you won't remember my face*. And the strange thing, the thing that's been sitting wrong in you for four years, is that you believed her. You still believe her. You can't hold onto her face no matter how hard you try, and part of you thinks that's your fault."

Hollis had gone very still. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped, gone rough. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've met three other people she talked to," Quentin said, which was true in spirit if not in letter, "and they all have the same scar behind the ear and the same polite nothing where a memory should be. You don't, though. Do you. No scar. Which means whatever she did to you, she did it differently. Gently. Or maybe just cheaply, because she was in a hurry and she liked you."

"I never—" Hollis stopped. His hand drifted up toward his right ear, an unconscious gesture, and found nothing there but rain-wet skin, and something in his face cracked open with relief and loss at once. "There was a woman," he said. "I can't tell you what she looked like. I've tried. It's like — you know when you walk into a room and forget why? It's like that, except the room is a person, and I've been walking into it for four years." He stared out at the water. "She asked me if the morning boat kept a manifest. Passenger lists. I told her the foot ferry doesn't, cash rides don't get logged. She seemed glad about that. Then she gave me two hundred dollars and told me she was going to be dead soon, and that the kindest thing I could ever do for anybody was to let it be true."

Quentin felt the words go through him like cold water finding a seam.

"And you took the money," he said quietly.

"I took the money." Hollis's laugh had no humor in it, only exhaustion. "And then I stood here and watched her walk down the pier, and I swear to God, by the time she reached the end of it I couldn't have picked her out of a lineup of one. I've hated myself for it ever since. Figured I was getting old. Figured I'd had too many beers." He turned to look at Quentin directly, and there was something almost pleading in it. "You're telling me it wasn't me. That somebody *did* that to me."

"Somebody taught her how," Quentin said. "That's the part I'm trying to run down."

*

Hollis, once the dam broke, turned out to hold more than the woman. He held the terminal's rhythms, its blind spots, its paper trail — such as it was. The foot ferry ran unmanifested after midnight; the pilot, a taciturn man named Greer, kept his own logbook out of personal stubbornness, and Greer, Hollis confided, was "particular about his book" in the way of men who trusted ink more than institutions.

Greer wouldn't be in until five-forty. Quentin bought the wait with another coffee from the vending machine in the maintenance shed — a machine that dispensed something technically legal in most states — and stood under the shed's corrugated awning watching the sky consider lightening and decide against it.

He laid it out in his head, the way he laid out all his cases, except this one refused to sit flat. A woman walks out of a taxi eleven minutes after her official death. She boards a foot ferry that keeps no list. She interrogates a dockworker about manifests, pays him to participate in her erasure, and vanishes down a pier into a fog of deliberately broken memory. Every step of it was the opposite of a murder. It was an anti-crime. A negative space with a pulse.

And yet the file said she was dead. The neighbors swore to it in identical sentences. The prosecution had built a case on a body that had never surfaced, and the Harrow machine had ground forward for four years as if the absence itself were evidence. Somebody had written her death into the world's ledger and gotten the world to initial it.

Which raised the question that had been keeping Quentin's coffee cold: if Tamsin Vey could erase herself that thoroughly, why leave a taxi record? Why leave a boarding pass?

Unless she hadn't left them. Unless she'd left *him* a trail — or left whoever might someday come looking one, the way a drowning man leaves bubbles.

Or unless someone else had arranged the clues, and he was being led by the nose down a pier toward something with teeth.

Both possibilities, he noted, ended with him wet and alone at a ferry terminal at dawn. He drank the terrible coffee anyway.

*

Greer arrived at five-thirty-eight, a compact man in his sixties with a white beard trimmed like topiary and the unhurried gait of someone whose entire career had consisted of arriving on time while the world failed to. Hollis made the introduction with surprising gentleness — "This is the fella asking about the quiet one" — and Quentin watched Greer absorb that phrase and understand all of it.

"My book's my book," Greer said, before Quentin had finished his first sentence. "You want to look, you look. You want to take pictures, you ask first."

The logbook lived in the pilot's cabin, a space the size of a generous closet smelling of diesel and decades. Greer pulled it from a shelf of predecessors — a spine of identical notebooks going back years, each labeled in block capitals — and opened to late October, four years ago, running a thick finger down the columns.

"Last crossing that night, ten forty. Eleven souls." The finger stopped. "Twelve."

Quentin leaned in. There it was, in Greer's square, unsentimental hand: a tally mark, and beside it, in the margin, a notation. Not a name — Greer didn't take names — but a note: *WOMAN, PAID DOUBLE, ASKED TO RIDE PAST STOP. DISEMBARKED OLD QUAY. SIGNED THE BOOK HERSELF.*

"Signed the book herself?" Quentin said.

"She asked to. Said she was paying for the extra stop and wanted it witnessed proper." Greer turned the book around.

And there, below the tally, in handwriting that was small and precise and deliberate — the handwriting of someone signing a document she intended to be found — was a signature:

*T. Vey.*

Quentin stared at it for a long moment. The rain ticked on the cabin roof. Somewhere in his chest, the hunger that had been driving him for days turned over and became something sharper, less comfortable. Hope, yes — real hope, the first clean hope of the case, proof in ink that the dead woman had been alive and moving and thinking four years ago. But underneath the hope, colder: *she signed it.* A woman this careful, this ruthless about her own invisibility, had put her name in a stranger's notebook. That wasn't carelessness. That was a message in a bottle, thrown with aim.

She had wanted someone to find this. Someone patient. Someone who checked taxi logs against death certificates and drove out to ferry terminals in the rain.

Someone like the person she'd expected to come looking. And the thought arrived then, unwelcome and fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do: *what if she's been expecting me specifically? What if I've been here before?*

He filed it with the other thoughts he wasn't ready for, in the drawer that was getting crowded lately, and thanked Greer, and asked one more question.

"The old quay. Anything out there?"

"Nothing but gulls and the ruin of the cannery offices," Greer said. "And the lockers."

"What lockers?"

Greer shrugged. "Harbormaster storage. Old ones, from when the quay worked. Numbered doors, padlocks. Half of them haven't opened since the flood." He squinted at Quentin. "You want to go out there, wait for low tide. The path floods otherwise. Tide turns around seven."

Quentin looked at his watch. Six-eleven.

He had fifty minutes, a rain-soaked city at his back, and a signature in a logbook burning a hole in his memory. He went back to the cab, traded his soaked jacket for the spare sweater he'd brought — the change of socks proving prophetic — and drove the coast road north with the heater full on and his heart going like a fare meter with a stuck lever.

*

The old quay was a graveyard of industry: rusted cranes frozen mid-reach, the cannery's brick bones furred with moss, gulls screaming ownership claims over all of it. The tide had pulled back to expose a causeway of wet stone and stranded kelp, and Quentin walked it fast, breath steaming, scanning the harbormaster's crumbling annex until he found them — a row of steel lockers set into the seawall, numbered in flaking paint, most hanging open and empty as eye sockets.

Except one.

Locker 31 was shut. Its padlock was new — bright brass against the corroded door, no more than a few years old, and beneath it, taped inside the frame where the rain couldn't reach, a plastic sleeve held a laminated card. Quentin pried it free.

Printed on it, in the same small precise hand as the logbook signature, was a single line:

*If you found the taxi, the book, and the nerve — prove it. Combination is the date she died. All of them.*

Quentin stood in the wind coming off the water and laughed once, short and disbelieving, because it was a test and a taunt and an invitation all at once, and because he already knew the answer. The file said October 23rd. The neighbors' rehearsed litany said October 23rd. But the taxi had rolled at 11:47 on the *24th*, and Greer's book confirmed the crossing, and the date she died — the date she *actually* died, the fiction she'd built with her own hands — was the one the world had been given.

Four digits. Two-three-two-four.

The padlock clicked open on the second try.

Inside the locker, dry and wrapped in oilcloth, lay a thin folder, a disposable phone, and a note card bearing three words in that same deliberate hand:

*Ask me about Meridian.*

Quentin stood there with the sea wind tearing at his collar, the folder heavy in his hands, and understood that he had just crossed some line — that the investigation had stopped being something he was doing and become something he was *inside of*, that a woman he'd never met had been waiting four years for precisely this fool, this rain-soaked, obsessive, gap-riddled fool, to stand exactly here.

He opened the folder. The first page was a photograph of himself.

Younger. Standing outside the Meridian Rest clinic, four years ago, mid-stride, caught by a long lens. On the back, one line: *You've been here before. Come find out why.*

The gulls screamed. The tide, far below, had already begun its slow turn back in.
