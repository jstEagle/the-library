## 13. Tamsin

The room she rented had one window and the window faced a brick wall, which was exactly why she'd chosen it. A woman who wanted to be seen would have picked the harbor view. Tamsin Vey paid cash for anonymity and a wall of old mortar, and every morning she drank her coffee looking at nothing, practicing being no one.

It was harder than people imagined. Not the hiding — hiding was simple, a matter of routines and false names and never walking the same street twice in a week. What was hard was the forgetting. She had spent six years at Meridian Rest learning to take memories apart the way a watchmaker takes apart movements, lifting out the mainspring of a man's certainty and replacing it with something quieter, something that served. She could do it to anyone. She had done it to herself more thoroughly than to any of them, and still the past came back at night like water through a bad seal.

Tonight it came back as the smell of lilies.

* * *

Meridian Rest had smelled of lilies because Dr. Ansel Harrow — no relation by blood, but cousin enough in ambition to the construction Harrows that no one ever asked — believed patients associated hospitals with antiseptic, and antiseptic with fear. So the clinic on Wren Hill smelled instead of white lilies and lemon polish, and the corridors were carpeted in green, and the intake rooms looked like studies in a gentleman's club, all leather and brass lamps, so that when they sat you down and asked you what you remembered, you felt you were confiding in a friend.

Tamsin had been nineteen when she arrived, a scholarship girl from the river district with a gift for neurochemistry and no family worth mentioning, which was the real qualification. They didn't advertise for technicians who could edit minds. They advertised for lab assistants, sleep-study monitors, data entry. Then they watched how you handled the first small thing — a patient's shame about an affair, say, softened into vagueness — and if your hands didn't shake, they moved you upstairs.

Her hands hadn't shaken. That was the confession she could never make aloud: not that she'd been forced, though she had been, not that she'd been afraid, though she had been, but that the work had come easily to her, the way music comes easily to some children, and there had been mornings when she'd felt the deep unlovely pleasure of competence.

Corwin Ashe had been the one who brought her the files. He was younger then, already elegant, already smiling the smile that made you feel he knew a joke about you that you hadn't heard yet. He'd sit on the edge of her workbench while she prepped a session, turning a folder over in his long fingers.

"Simple one," he'd say. "A witness saw something inconvenient. We're going to help him remember it differently."

"Inconvenient to whom?" she'd asked, the first time, because she was nineteen and still asked questions.

"To everyone," Ashe said, "if you think about it. Certainty is such a heavy thing to carry. We lighten loads."

She thought about it for years afterward. It was true, in its way, the way most lies are true in their way. The witnesses did seem lighter afterward. The dockworker who'd seen a Harrow foreman drop a pallet of rebar onto a man who owed him money — after three sessions he remembered the pallet slipping on its own, a tragic accident, no one's fault, and he wept with relief at his own forgiveness of the world. The clerk who'd copied ledgers showing the shell companies — after her sessions she remembered copying weather reports, and she smiled at Tamsin in the corridor, grateful for reasons she couldn't name.

That was the part that hollowed her out. Not the fear. The gratitude.

Because coercion was only the frame around it. Yes, there was the contract she'd signed at twenty-one, the one with clauses she hadn't understood until she understood them, the debt to the clinic that grew no matter how many hours she worked, the photographs of her mother — alive then, in a care home upstate, her dementia managed by doctors whose salaries came, Tamsin eventually realized, from the same well. One word from Ashe and her mother's care would become a different kind of care. That was the coercion, plain as a gun.

But the frame wasn't why she stayed compliant. She stayed because the work was beautiful, in the way a scalpel is beautiful, and because every session taught her more about the architecture of a self than any university could have, and because — this was the truth under all the other truths — she told herself she was gentler with them than anyone else would be. Other technicians broke things. Tamsin left seams so fine even she couldn't find them later. When she edited a memory, she kept its emotional grain, its texture, so the person wouldn't feel amputated, just... redirected. She told herself that was mercy.

She told herself that right up until the night she realized she was the best they had, and that being the best they had meant she was the reason the Harrows' city stayed quiet.

* * *

There had been forty-one names in her own ledger before she burned it. She remembered thirty-eight of them without trying. The other three she had chosen to lose, and losing them was the hardest editing she ever did, because she'd done it to herself, alone, with the equipment humming and her hands — finally, finally shaking.

One of the three had been a taxi dispatcher.

She didn't know his name anymore. That was the point. But she remembered the shape of the loss: a fortnight, a hospital bed at Meridian Rest, a young man who'd seen something on a night route he shouldn't have seen — a transfer of a body, or something near enough to a body, between a Harrow site and the river. He'd been curious, the way night workers get curious, and curiosity in this city was a condition requiring treatment.

They'd sent him to her because she was gentle. She'd sat across from him in the green-carpeted room with the brass lamp, and he'd talked about his mother's letters, and how he read them on slow shifts, and she had taken the fortnight out of him with hands that didn't shake, and she had left the love for his mother intact, the loneliness intact, everything except the fourteen days, and she had built over the gap a smooth story of flu and overtime that his own mind would defend against inspection forever, because minds defend their edits better than any editor can.

And then, because she was the best they had, she had done one thing more. She'd written a note in his handwriting — she was good at hands, at the small tremors and loops that make writing a fingerprint — and tucked it into his coat pocket where future-him might someday find it. *The witness lives.* Three words planted like a seed in fallow ground. She hadn't known why she did it. She'd told herself it was insurance, a thread left loose in case she ever needed to unpick the garment. Later, alone, she admitted what it really was: penance. A crack left in the wall so that someday, if he ever came looking, he could find his way through.

She had thought about him sometimes over the years. Not often. A face without a name, a voice on the edge of recall. When she'd doctored her own death, one of the last things she'd checked — obsessively, compulsively, the way you check a lock twice — was whether any of the records connecting her to that dispatcher survived. They didn't. She'd made sure.

* * *

The death had been her masterpiece, and she could admit that now, here, with coffee going cold and a brick wall for a view.

By twenty-six she had learned everything Meridian Rest could teach and several things it couldn't. She knew the clinic's license was a fiction maintained by payments to two inspectors. She knew Dr. Harrow kept a second set of ledgers in the flooded basement because he believed water damage was the best security system ever devised. And she knew that Corwin Ashe had begun to look at her the way he looked at assets nearing the end of their useful life — with appreciation, and arithmetic.

So she planned her exit the way she planned a session: backward, from the desired result.

The desired result was that Tamsin Vey be dead. Not missing — dead, definitively, boringly dead, because missing persons got searched for and dead persons got filed. She gave herself a cause of death (drowning, the river, unglamorous), a date (a Tuesday in late autumn), a witness ecosystem (three neighbors whose evening routines she knew down to the minute), and a chain of custody for the story that ran entirely through people whose memories she had already touched, gently, over months, planting the sight of a woman matching her description near the rail bridge, planting the sound of an argument, planting the sad little details that make a death feel witnessed.

She did it in installments, each one small enough to pass unnoticed, each neighbor receiving only a sliver — a coat here, a silhouette there — so that no single mind held the whole fabrication and no single mind could unravel it. By the time she walked into the river district carrying a bag of her own clothes weighted with stones, three separate people half-remembered watching her die, and none of them had seen anything at all.

The scar behind the ear was her signature, her failsafe. A tiny keloid line, administered during "routine dermatological screening," that marked every mind she had edited — hers included; she'd given herself one, symmetry being a form of honesty. If anyone ever traced the pattern, they'd find the clinic. She'd assumed no one ever would. She'd assumed the city's fear of the Harrows ran deeper than any investigator's curiosity.

Then, six years later, a lawyer named Odile Ferran started asking questions about a murder that hadn't happened, and somewhere across the city a man with a perfect closure rate began pulling threads.

* * *

Tamsin learned about Quentin Okonkwo the way she learned about most threats: obliquely, through the small tremors in the world. A notice in the legal pages. A neighbor of the rail bridge suddenly unavailable, then oddly serene, repeating phrases about "the poor Vey woman" in words too polished for grief. And then — this was the tremor that made her stand up from her coffee — a dispatch log surfacing. Her fare. The one she'd taken to the ferry terminal four hours after her own declared time of death, paid in cash, hood up, trusting a stranger's discretion because she'd had no choice.

She'd thought that log was buried. She'd buried it herself, or believed she had.

Now someone had dug it up, and the digging had a shape she recognized. Methodical. Night-shift patient. Cross-referencing routes. The kind of mind that doesn't stop at the first contradiction because contradictions are the job.

And beneath the professional alarm, something else moved in her, unwelcome and warm: the note. *The witness lives.* Had he found it? Was that what had woken him, some seed she'd planted six years ago pushing up through the smooth surface she'd laid over his mind? She sat with that possibility for a long time, guilt and hope tangled past separating. If she had used him once as a canvas, perhaps she had also, without meaning to, left him a door.

She should run. Every instinct said run. She had a go-bag packed under the bed, three identities in rotation, a ferry schedule memorized. Running was what she was good at; she had turned self-erasure into a discipline, a craft, almost a virtue. Each year she shed another layer of Tamsin Vey — the laugh, the way she signed her name, the taste for apricots — until what remained was a function wearing a coat.

But running now meant the trial proceeded. It meant Odile Ferran's client — whoever he was, some scapegoat harvested from the Harrows' margins — went to prison for a murder Tamsin had staged, and the machinery she'd fed for six years ground on, and the forty-one names stayed edited, and the city stayed quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

She went to the window and looked at the brick wall, at the mortar softening in the rain, and understood that the wall was the point. You could stare at nothing only so long before you became nothing.

* * *

That night she took out the one object she'd allowed herself to keep: a photograph of her mother, taken before the care home, before the disease, before the well-paid doctors whose kindness had been a leash around Tamsin's throat. Her mother had died eighteen months ago. Tamsin had not gone to the funeral. Going would have required being someone, and being someone was how people found you. She had read about the service in an obituary and edited her own grief down to a manageable size, the way she'd been taught, the way she was best in the world at doing.

It hadn't worked. Grief, unlike memory, resisted editing. She'd left the seam rough on purpose, maybe. Or maybe her hands had shaken after all.

She put the photograph away and opened the notebook where she kept the only record of her real life — not names, never names, but the ledger of debts. Thirty-eight entries. Tonight she added a thirty-ninth line, and her pen hesitated over it, because the ink made it real:

*The dispatcher. Whatever I took, help him take it back.*

Below it, after a moment, she wrote the practical truth, the one that turned her stomach and steadied her hand at the same time:

*If he's pulling my threads, Ashe will let him get close. Close enough to use. Which means close enough for me to reach — if I'm willing to be seen.*

Being seen. Six years of practice undone by a single decision. She could feel the old fear rising, the animal knowledge that visibility is mortality, that the woman in the river had died so that the woman at the window could live. To walk out of this room as Tamsin Vey — even once, even briefly — was to invite every ghost she'd manufactured to recognize her.

But the ghosts were already recognizing her. That was what the investigation meant. The past she'd buried was excavating itself, thread by thread, and she could either flee ahead of it forever, shedding selves until there was no one left to save, or she could turn and meet it — meet *him*, the man whose fortnight she'd stolen, whose note she'd written, whose perfect record was probably, if she knew her own craftsmanship, a scar shaped like her.

She packed the go-bag properly this time, not for flight but for contact. Ferry schedules. Cash. The burner phone she'd never activated. And at the bottom, wrapped in a sock, a driver's license in the name Tamsin Vey, expired six years, kept against every rule she lived by — the one document that proved the drowned woman had once existed above water.

Outside, rain began again, the same patient rain that fell on the dispatch office across town, on the flooded archive at Meridian Rest, on the rail bridge where three neighbors still rehearsed a death that had never happened. Tamsin stood at the window a final time, watching the brick wall darken, and spoke aloud to the empty room — the first time in months she'd used her real voice.

"Okay," she said. "I'm coming in."

Somewhere across the city, she was certain, a man was reading a note in his own handwriting and not knowing who had written it. Soon, she thought, he would know. And God help her, she needed him to know before Corwin Ashe decided the cleanest solution to a living witness was to arrange a second funeral — this one permanent, and this one, this time, attended.
