## 15. Trial Date Moved Up

The notice came at 7:42 on a Tuesday morning, while Odile Ferran was still standing in her kitchen in one sock, waiting for the kettle to make up its mind. Her clerk had emailed it from the courthouse steps — she could hear, through his phone's tinny speaker, the gulls and the traffic and the particular echo of marble colonnade that meant the Hall of Justice — and he read it to her twice because neither of them believed it the first time.

*In re: State v. Adeyemi. The Court, on motion of the prosecution citing scheduling exigencies, has advanced the trial date. New date of trial: nineteen days hence.*

Three weeks gone. Just like that.

Odile set her phone face-down on the counter and looked out the window at the rain beginning to stitch itself across the glass, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm: *Ashe knows.*

She had been telling herself for days that Quentin Okonkwo's investigation was moving quietly enough — a man who drove a taxi for cover, who asked questions the way other people asked about the weather. But quiet had never been Ashe's fear. Momentum was Ashe's fear. A case crawling toward its date was a case he controlled; a case gathering speed, gathering witnesses, gathering *recantations*, was a case he needed to end before it finished becoming something else. Moving the trial up wasn't panic. It was surgery. Cut the timeline short enough and the infection of doubt wouldn't have time to spread.

The kettle clicked off. She didn't pour the water. She stood there instead, in her one sock on the cold tile, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for a week.

Nineteen days. She had a client who couldn't tell her where he'd been on the night of June eleventh because — he insisted, weeping once, politely, into his hands — he genuinely did not remember, only that he'd woken two mornings later with river mud on his shoes and no memory of how it got there. She had a prosecution witness who had recanted on the stand and then sat there blinking like a woman waking from anesthesia, unable to explain why she'd ever accused Marcus Adeyemi of anything. And she had a hearing transcript sealed by judicial order, which meant somewhere in this building there existed an official record of the state's star witness saying *I don't know why I said it, I don't remember saying it,* and that record was locked in a drawer where no jury would ever read it.

And she had Quentin Okonkwo, off somewhere in the wet dark, chasing a dead woman through taxi manifests.

Odile poured the tea without drinking it and got dressed.

* * *

Her office occupied the third floor of a building that had been a bank before the Harrow developments pushed the financial district east, and it still had the bones of grandeur — coffered ceiling, a vault door she used as a coat rack, tall windows that looked out onto a street where the rain ran gold under the sodium lamps even at midday. It was too much space for a practice her size, and she kept it anyway, out of a stubbornness she'd never fully examined. Rooms like this made clients believe you could win. That belief was half the job. The other half, lately, was lying to herself about the same thing.

Marcus Adeyemi arrived at ten, escorted by his sister, who waited outside in the hall as though the office itself might contaminate her. He was thirty-one, a hospital orderly with soft hands and a habit of sitting very straight, as if posture were a form of prayer. He'd lost weight since the arraignment; his collar stood away from his neck.

"Ms. Ferran." He folded himself into the chair across from her desk. "I saw the news. The date. Is that — is it bad?"

"It's a tactic," Odile said. "They want less time for preparation. For both sides."

"But mostly us."

"Mostly us," she admitted.

He nodded slowly, absorbing it the way he absorbed everything — without outrage, which frightened her more than screaming would have. In four months he had never once raged. He sat in her office like a man waiting for a verdict he already understood, and every time she watched him do it, a small cold voice in her reminded her that innocence did not look like this. Innocence fought. Innocence demanded. Guilt sometimes fought too, but resignation — resignation was what guilt wore when it had run out of lies.

Except when it wasn't. Except when it was a man whose memory had been handled by professionals.

"Marcus," she said, "I need to ask you again about the night of the eleventh."

He closed his eyes briefly. "I've told you everything I—"

"You've told me what you remember. I'm asking about what you don't." She leaned forward. "Think about the edges. Not the middle — the edges. The last thing you're certain of before the gap. The first thing after."

He was quiet for so long she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "There was a smell." His voice had changed — smaller. "I never mentioned it because it sounds insane. Like a dentist's office. That clean, sharp — eucalyptus, maybe? And something underneath it, like pennies." He opened his eyes and looked at her with an expression of dawning, terrible politeness. "Why would I remember a smell if I can't remember anything else?"

Odile kept her face still. Eucalyptus and copper. She had read enough of Okonkwo's notes by now — the man wrote his reports like dispatch tickets, terse and numbered — to know what Meridian Rest's former patients described when they described anything at all. The clinic had kept its sedation suites smelling of eucalyptus, according to a fire inspector's report from nine years ago. Something about masking the antiseptic. Making the place feel like a spa.

"I don't know yet," she said, which was true. "But I'm going to find out."

When Marcus had gone, his sister collecting him from the hallway with a hand on his elbow like he might blow away, Odile stood at the window and let herself feel the thing she'd been refusing to feel, which was fear — not for the outcome, she'd lost cases before, everyone had — but for the shape of the trap. Because here was what she knew, and here was what she could prove, and the distance between those two things was where Marcus Adeyemi was going to die of a life sentence.

She knew the Harrow Construction Group employed Assistant District Attorney Renata Voss's husband. She knew Voss had personally taken over a case that should have been a routine matter for a line deputy. She knew Corwin Ashe had offered Okonkwo triple his fee within a week of the retainer, which meant Ashe had known about the retainer almost immediately, which meant someone inside her own orbit had reported it.

Someone inside her own orbit.

That thought had been sitting in her chest for days like a swallowed stone. She turned it now, deliberately, feeling all its edges.

* * *

At noon she walked down to the Hall of Justice through the rain without an umbrella, because she wanted to arrive looking like someone with nothing left to protect, and filed a motion to compel discovery of the prosecution's expert list. The clerk — a heavyset woman named Doreen who had worked the criminal counter since Odile was a law student and who remembered everything and repeated nothing — stamped it without comment, then leaned across the counter.

"Heard something this morning," Doreen said, voice pitched low beneath the fluorescent hum. "Voss's office requested the accelerated date ten days ago. Before your witness recanted." She tapped the counter twice with a lacquered nail. "Ten days ago, honey. They moved the date *before* they knew they had a problem. You want to think about what that tells you."

It told her that Ashe hadn't been reacting to the recantation at all. The recantation had been the reaction — someone else's move on a board he was already clearing. Or worse: the recantation itself had been scheduled, a pressure test, an experiment to see whether the memory work held or failed under oath, with the trial date moved first so that whatever the experiment revealed, the clock would be too short to exploit it.

Everything was a session note. Everything was a rehearsal. She was beginning to understand that she wasn't practicing law in this city anymore; she was performing a ritual that the people who actually ran things permitted because it kept the rest of the population believing in rooms like hers.

Back at the office, she pulled the file on her own retainer for the hundredth time and stared at it.

The money had come in cash, in a lawyer's trust envelope, delivered by courier. The instruction letter was typed, unsigned. The stated client was anonymous — an interested party to the Adeyemi matter, seeking to establish that Tamsin Vey had not been murdered on the night of June eleventh, and further that she had not been murdered at all, and further that the charge against Adeyemi was unsupportable. The fee had been generous. The instructions had been precise. And the return address on the courier slip was a mail drop three blocks from Harrow Tower.

She had known that from the first week. She had told herself it meant nothing — that rich people used mail drops, that anonymity was a service like any other, that a lawyer who took cash took the shadow that came with it. She had told herself that whoever hired her wanted the truth, and the truth happened to be dangerous, and danger was simply the market price.

But ten days ago, someone had moved a trial date. And someone had known about her retainer before Corwin Ashe ever picked up a phone.

Odile sat down at her desk, unlocked the bottom drawer, and took out the letter again. She had it photographed, enlarged, analyzed by a document examiner who charged her a month's fees and told her only this: the typing matched machines manufactured between twenty and forty years ago, maintained meticulously, and the paper stock was custom-ordered in lots too small to trace. Someone careful. Someone old-fashioned. Someone who wanted the case investigated *and* wanted the investigation doomed, and had built a machine that would do both at once.

Unless—

Unless there were two hands. Unless someone inside the Harrow orbit had grown a conscience, or a grudge, or simply a terror of what Ashe was building, and had hired her the only way that person could — anonymously, deniably, with money that couldn't be traced back to a name. Hiring an outsider because everyone inside was watched. Hiring her precisely because she was nobody's.

Two explanations. Two hands on the same envelope. And no way to know which one until she decided whom to trust with the question.

There was exactly one candidate.

* * *

Quentin Okonkwo answered on the fourth ring, and she could hear immediately that he was driving — the tick of the turn signal, radio murmur, rain against glass.

"Ferran." No greeting. He never bothered.

"They moved the trial. Nineteen days. Voss requested it ten days ago, before the recantation."

Silence, except for the road. Then, quietly: "Before."

"Before. Which means Ashe isn't scrambling. He's executing. And it means—" she stopped herself, but the stone in her chest had been sitting there too long, and nineteen days was not enough time for discretion anymore. "Okonkwo. I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you say anything."

The signal clicked off. When he spoke again his voice had the flat attention she was learning to recognize, the dispatcher's voice, the voice of a man taking down an address he intended to drive to.

"I'm listening."

"The retainer. The cash that started this. It came anonymously, through a courier, with a drop address near Harrow Tower. I've known since week one and I didn't tell you, because I told myself it was just a rich person's caution." She heard her own voice go dry, procedural — the tone lawyers use when the confession is worse than the crime. "But there are two readings. Either Ashe hired me to run an investigation designed to fail, so that when it failed, every thread of doubt about the state's case would be discredited along with it. Poisoned fruit. Or someone inside the Harrow machine hired me in earnest, because they couldn't hire anyone inside without being seen doing it. I don't know which. I have never known which. And I am telling you now because in nineteen days it won't matter what I know — only what we can prove."

The road sounds went on for a moment. Rain. A horn, distant.

"You could be lying to me right now," Okonkwo said. Not cruelly. Almost gently.

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because if I'm Ashe's instrument, then everything I've given you is contaminated and you need to know it before you stake anything on me. And if I'm not — if somebody inside hired me honestly — then that person is still inside, still watching, and may be the only reason any of us survives the next nineteen days." She pressed her palm flat against the cool glass of the window, against the gold-running rain. "I'm tired of not knowing which side of my own case I'm standing on, Okonkwo. That's the confession. Do what you want with it."

Another long silence. She imagined him at a red light, wipers going, that broad unreadable face lit green then red then green.

"For what it's worth," he said finally, "the note in my own handwriting — the one from my missing fortnight — says *the witness lives.* Three words, block capitals, no period. Whoever taught me to write like that also taught me to be careful." A pause. "Careful people don't hire loud lawyers. They hire ones nobody's watching. If someone inside hired you, they chose well."

"That's either reassurance or a warning."

"Both. Most true things are." The light must have changed; she heard the engine gather itself. "Nineteen days. I need the passenger manifest from the ferry company, the Meridian ledgers copied before Ashe burns them, and a way to get a recorder into a room with Tamsin Vey. Can you get me a court order for any of that?"

"A judge will sign nothing for an unlicensed investigator and a lawyer with an anonymous client."

"Then we do it the other way." And she heard it in his voice then — not hope exactly, but its skeleton, the structure hope stands up inside when hope itself is too exhausted. "Ferran. If someone inside the Harrow orbit hired you — find out who. Not for the case. For you. You can't stand on a floor you can't see."

He hung up. Odile sat for a long time in the grand empty room with the vault-door coat rack and the coffered ceiling, listening to the rain finish its argument with the windows.

Then she opened the drawer again and took out the courier slip, and did the thing she should have done four months ago: she called the courier company, gave them the tracking number, and asked, in her flattest courtroom voice, for the origin log.

The clerk put her on hold. Elevator music, tinny and obscene. Odile watched the rain and counted seconds, and at second forty-seven the clerk came back, and her voice had changed — careful now, the way voices go careful around things with weight.

"Ma'am? This package wasn't couriered from the drop address. The drop address is where it was *sent*. Origin was logged as a private residence." Paper rustling. "An old house, out past the river district. Name on the account is..." more rustling, longer, "...Harrow. Elowen Harrow. The widow."

Odile set the phone down very carefully, as though it were something sleeping.

The old matriarch. The one the family kept at arm's length in the river district house, the one whose son built the empire and whose grandson was going to inherit whatever survived the next nineteen days. Someone had hired her from inside the Harrow orbit after all.

From the very top of it.

Outside, the rain was easing. Nineteen days, and the whole case had just tilted on its axis — and somewhere out there, in an old house past the ferry terminal, a dying woman had set all of this in motion, and Odile Ferran had exactly one decision left worth making: whether to drive into the lion's den and knock on its door.
