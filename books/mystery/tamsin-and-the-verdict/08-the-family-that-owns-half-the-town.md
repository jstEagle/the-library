## 8. The Family That Owns Half the Town

There are three ways to get into a Verlaine party, and I had credentials for none of them. You could be born into it, marry into it, or own the building where the caterers kept their coats. I owned nothing but a key ring with forty-seven keys and the particular talent of looking like I belonged wherever I was standing — which is its own kind of property, and in Hollowmere it appreciates.

The invitation came on cream cardstock, embossed with a V whose tail curled like something drowning. *The Verlaine Family requests the pleasure of your company at a memorial gathering in honor of Marguerite Verlaine, seven years passed.* Seven years passed, like weather. Like a kidney stone. The Grand Meridian was listed as a sponsor of the evening's flowers, which meant that when Mrs. Okafor's florist called in sick two days before the event, I was able to volunteer myself as delivery personnel with the smooth sincerity of a woman who has never once wanted anything for herself.

I carried peonies. Forty pounds of them, white as drowned things, up the gravel drive of Verlaine House while a man in livery watched me the way customs officers watch luggage.

"Delivery," I said. "Grand Meridian."

He consulted a clipboard as though peonies might be contraband, then stepped aside, and I walked into the house where Marguerite Verlaine had grown up and stopped growing at twenty-six.

* * *

You hear "the family that owns half the town" and you think it's a figure of speech. It isn't. The Verlaines held the mortgage on my hotel until 2011. They held the mill lease, the marina, the parcel on Cordwainer Street where the new pharmacy went up, and — this is the part people forget when they're being charmed — they held paper on eleven private residences, including, I happen to know from a file I once closed very carefully, the home of Constable Ewan Pike.

When you owe a family money, your memory develops a certain softness around the edges. Seven years ago, when a girl drowned and questions were asked, half of Hollowmere discovered their recollections were beautifully vague, like paintings viewed through steam. I include myself in that number. I have included myself in it every day since.

Verlaine House itself was the kind of old money doesn't buy anymore: grey stone, deep sills, windows tall enough to stand in. Inside, someone had spent a fortune making grief look effortless. Candles everywhere. A string quartet playing something minor-keyed and expensive. Photographs of Marguerite arranged along the mantel like evidence she'd been loved, which perhaps she had, in the way you love a thing that also costs you something.

She'd been beautiful in the way of people who don't know it yet — the photographs caught her mid-laugh more often than posed, one shoulder always higher than the other, as if perpetually about to say something improper. In the largest portrait she stood at the edge of the water with her shoes off, and even printed and framed and hung by mourners, she looked like she was keeping a secret.

I set down the peonies. I picked up a champagne flute I hadn't been offered. And I began to work.

* * *

A concierge learns to read rooms the way priests read scripture — fluently, and with an eye for what's been edited. This room had been edited. There were clusters of guests who laughed too readily, and clusters who stood too still, and between them moved Julian Verlaine like a current nobody could see but everybody felt.

He was smaller than I expected. That sounds absurd — I'd seen him at funerals and council meetings for years, a grey-suited figure at a distance — but proximity changed him. Up close he was compact, silvering at the temples, with his sister's dark eyes and none of her stillness. He shook hands like a man paying out rope: generous, measured, yours to hang yourself with.

"Ashgrove." He said my name before anyone introduced us, and smiled as if we shared a joke about it. "The Grand Meridian. My sister tells me you run the finest front desk between here and Geneva."

"I run the only front desk between here and Geneva," I said. "Quality follows scarcity."

He laughed — a real laugh, or a flawless reproduction; after seven years of filing this town's secrets I can no longer always tell — and touched my elbow briefly, a gesture calibrated to the millimeter. "Beatrix, isn't it? Beatrix Ashgrove. My mother stayed with you once, years ago. She said you remembered how she took her tea without being told."

"Earl Grey, milk last, no sugar, and the newspaper folded to the obituaries first."

Something flickered across his face — not quite surprise, not quite pleasure. Recognition, maybe, of a fellow professional. "She did love the obituaries," he said. "She liked knowing who'd outlived whom."

"And now you know who's outlived Marguerite."

The words left me before I'd authorized them. For one full second the room's temperature dropped, and Julian Verlaine looked at me with an attention so complete it felt like being x-rayed. Then the warmth returned, seamless, as if a page had turned.

"Yes," he said softly. "Now I do." And he moved on to charm someone else, leaving me holding my flute and the distinct sense that I'd just been weighed, measured, and found worth a second appointment.

* * *

Camille Verlaine did not circulate. She stationed herself near the long window overlooking the lake — the lake, the actual lake, because this family apparently saw no reason to look away from the scene of the thing — and received visitors like a monarch holding court over her own grief. She wore black so severe it looked structural. Her hair was pinned with a violence that suggested the pins were doing emotional labor.

I'd filed Camille for years under *harmless*. The spinster sister, the one who ran the charitable trust and signed the checks for the church roof. But harmless people don't stand like fortresses, and harmless people don't do what she did when our eyes met across the room: nothing. No polite nod, no glance away. Just a long, level look, the kind you give a word you've looked up twice to check the spelling.

I gave her back the same look, because I am nothing if not consistent, and crossed toward the photographs on the mantel as if that had been my destination all along.

"You're the hotel woman." She'd come up beside me without sound. Up close she smelled of vetiver and something underneath it, older, like a cellar. "Beatrix Ashgrove."

"That's me. I brought the peonies."

"They're excessive." She studied the flowers rather than me. "Marguerite hated white flowers. She said they looked like they'd given up."

"I'll remember that."

"No," Camille said, "you won't. People never remember what the dead actually wanted. They remember what's convenient." She turned those dark eyes on me at last, and there was something in them I recognized from the mirror on my worst nights — a tiredness that had given up trying to be anything else. "Why are you here, Miss Ashgrove? The flowers were delivered an hour ago. Your work was finished."

"The hotel sponsors the arrangements. I like to see a job through."

"Mm." She let the syllable sit between us like a stone dropped in a well, listening for depth. "My brother says you notice everything."

"Your brother flatters me."

"My brother does many things," Camille said. "Flattery is the least of them." And then, before I could decide whether that was a warning or an invitation, a man in a grey suit touched her arm — some cousin, some creditor — and she turned away, and the audience was over.

But not before I saw it: the way her gaze went past my shoulder, quick and involuntary, toward the study door. Toward the room where, presumably, the family kept its papers.

I filed that. I file everything. It's the only inheritance I've got.

* * *

The fragment came an hour later, and it came cheaply, because rich houses are built for eavesdropping. The service corridor behind the ballroom — where I'd gone ostensibly to check on the second wave of peonies and actually to breathe air that didn't smell of candles and performance — ran behind the study, separated from it by a door that had never in its life fit its frame. Old house. Old gaps. Old habits on both sides of the wall.

Two voices inside. Julian's, unhurried, warm even now, the voice of a man reading a bedtime story to himself. And another voice, younger, tight with the particular anxiety of a man whose signature is on something.

"—told you, it's handled. The trust instruments were re-executed years ago—"

"Instruments." Julian, amused. "You make forgery sound like an orchestra."

"It wasn't—" A pause you could have parked a car in. "It was legal review. Clean-up. If anyone ever pulled the original file—"

"Who would pull the file, Marcus?" Gentle. Almost kind. "Pike is retired. The solicitor is dead. The registry clerk owes me his daughter's tuition. And the town—" here he paused, and I heard him smile, I swear I heard it, the way you can hear rain coming, "—the town signed its verdict seven years ago. Everyone did. That's the beauty of this place. Nobody wants to know badly enough to find out."

Silence. Then, quieter, the young one — Marcus — almost whispering: "And if someone does?"

Another pause. Longer. When Julian answered, all the warmth had drained out of the voice, and what remained was worse than anger. It was bookkeeping.

"Then we'll know exactly who, and exactly when, and we'll handle it the way this family has always handled things. Quietly. At the source."

I stood in that corridor with my back against cold plaster and my heart going like a trapped bird, and I thought: *at the source.* And I thought: *the one who watched.* And I thought about a drawer in my office containing two letters in handwriting I still couldn't place, and about a boy named Theo Verlaine who had checked into my hotel asking questions with edges on them.

Somewhere beyond the wall, ice rang against crystal. The quartet began something new, something in a major key, as if the house itself had decided the moment for honesty was over.

* * *

I gave myself three minutes of panic, timed against the mantel clock on my way back through the ballroom, because unmanaged panic is just noise and I have never tolerated noise. Three minutes, then back to work. That's the discipline. That's the whole trick of surviving Hollowmere: you feel the thing fully, briefly, and then you file it.

Julian was saying goodbye to guests at the foot of the staircase, pressing shoulders, kissing hands, a patriarch in miniature. As I passed, he caught my eye across the crowd — deliberately, unmistakably — and raised his glass a fraction of an inch. Not a toast. An acknowledgment. *I see you, Miss Ashgrove. I've always seen you.*

Camille stood at the window again, watching the lake go dark, and did not look at me at all, which from her I understood to be the louder message.

Outside, the gravel crunched under my sensible shoes and the cold off the water hit my face like a slap from an old friend. Behind me, Verlaine House glowed gold at every window, beautiful and lit and lying.

Halfway down the drive, my phone buzzed. A message from the Grand Meridian's night desk, forwarded automatically, routine as breathing:

*Message for B. Ashgrove, hand-delivered, no name. Envelope marked personal.*

I stopped walking. The lake lay flat and black to my left, holding the whole sky and giving nothing back.

I should have waited until morning. I want that on the record — the version of me that files things, that times her panics, that knows better — she would have waited.

I opened it under the streetlamp at the end of the drive, where the light was thin and moths were already gathering, and the handwriting on the single line inside was the same hand as the first confession, the same hand as the second promise, slanting neat and patient as a schoolmaster's:

*You heard the papers mentioned. Tomorrow, ask Camille what she signed. Ask her what she got for signing.*

*More soon.*
