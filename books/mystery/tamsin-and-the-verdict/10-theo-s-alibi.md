## 10. Theo's Alibi

The trouble with insomnia at the Grand Meridian is that it's never really yours. The hotel keeps its own hours, and I keep them with it — the boiler's midnight cough, the elevator's three a.m. pilgrimage to the top floor and back, empty, like a ghost commuting. By four, the lobby belongs to me and the rain, and I've made my peace with that arrangement for eleven years.

Then Theo Verlaine started coming down.

It began innocently enough, which is how everything dangerous begins. He'd appear at the desk around one or two in the morning with a book he wasn't reading and ask if the kitchen was still open. It wasn't; it never is. I'd make him tea anyway, because the kettle lives behind the desk and because I wanted to, and wanting to was already a problem I chose not to examine.

"You're always awake," he said on the third night, accepting the cup like it was a ritual we'd been performing for years instead of days.

"Hotels don't sleep. Someone has to stand guard over the linens."

"And you? Do you sleep?"

"I sleep the way the hotel does," I said. "Lightly, and with one ear on the doors."

He laughed, and I catalogued the laugh the way I catalogue everything — pitch, duration, sincerity index — and filed it under *dangerous* without quite believing my own filing system. That's the thing about my habit of noticing: it works beautifully until the thing I'm noticing is my own heartbeat.

We talked about nothing, those first nights. His years in Lisbon. The correct way to fold a map. Whether the rain in Hollowmere was worse than the rain anywhere else (it isn't, but we pretend, the way towns pretend). He had his father's ease and none of Julian's polish — Theo wore his charm slightly rumpled, like a good coat from a leaner year. When he smiled, I noticed, the left corner came up a fraction higher than the right, as though the smile were slightly skeptical of itself.

I should have been suspicious of a man who asked questions about letters. Instead I found myself leaving the kettle filled.

* * *

On the fifth night, the conversation stopped being nothing.

The rain had settled into its long autumn argument with the roof, and Theo sat across the desk from me in the wing chair guests aren't supposed to use, his feet up on the luggage bench guests are definitely not supposed to touch. He'd abandoned the pretense of the book entirely.

"My father wants me to stay through the winter," he said. "Indefinitely."

"That must be nice. Having somewhere to be indefinitely."

"It would be, if I knew why." He turned the teacup in his hands. "Beatrix. Can I ask you something strange?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the strangeness."

"The letters. The ones that came to the desk. Did they say anything about—" He stopped himself, recalibrated. "Did they name anyone?"

And there it was again, that oddly specific curiosity, surfacing like a hand breaking water. I could have deflected. Eleven years of concierge work teaches you deflection the way seminaries teach Latin. But the hour was wrong for deflection, and the man was wrong for it too, sitting there with his rumpled charm and his skeptical smile, looking at me like I was the only honest thing in Hollowmere.

"They didn't name anyone," I said. Which was true. What I didn't say was that the handwriting had looked familiar, that I'd spent two nights comparing it against seven years of mental files, that the second letter had promised *more soon* the way a predator promises prey it will be quick.

"But someone wrote them," Theo said. "Someone who knows this town."

"This town knows itself," I said. "That's the problem with it."

He studied me for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and the lobby lamps did their warm, complicit work of making everything look gentler than it was.

"When Marguerite died," he said quietly, "I was fourteen. They sent me to my aunt in Geneva before the funeral even finished. Nobody let me ask questions. Nobody answered the ones I asked anyway. Seven years, Beatrix. And now someone is writing letters, and my father won't talk about it, and my aunt Camille has started locking her study door, which she never used to do." He set down the cup. "I came back because I want to know what happened to my sister. Whatever it costs."

*I came back because I want to know what happened to my sister.* It was a beautiful sentence. I have a professional appreciation for beautiful sentences; I also have a professional suspicion of them. Beautiful sentences are what people build when the truth needs somewhere tidy to live.

But God help me, I believed him. Or I wanted to believe him, which in my experience is the same feeling wearing a better coat.

"There's something else," he said. "Something I probably shouldn't tell you, since you're clearly the kind of woman who files things away." A glance at my drawer — how did he know about the drawer? — and then back to my face. "You'll want an alibi eventually. Everyone does, in this town, once the questions start again. So here's mine."

* * *

His alibi, delivered in the small hours with rain against the glass, went like this:

The night Marguerite drowned, Theo had been at the Verlaine house, in bed with a fever of a hundred and two degrees. Dr. Anselm had come out twice — once at nine, once near midnight — and had left a prescription record, and the household staff had taken turns sitting with him because Camille had been at a benefit dinner in the city and Julian had been, in Theo's words, "at the estate office with the solicitors, going over trust papers, which is what he was always doing in those days."

"The doctor's records," I said. "From seven years ago."

"Anselm kept carbon copies. Old-fashioned man. He showed me when I got back — I asked to see them, and he had them in a box in his cellar, along with every patient file since 1981." Theo's voice was steady, reasonable, arranged. "Two visits. Times noted. My temperature noted. He remembers the night because Marguerite's death made it memorable — everyone remembers where they were that night, but Anselm has it in ink."

It was airtight. That was the first thing wrong with it.

An alibi that arrives pre-assembled, with documentation, corroborated by a dead man's filing habits, offered voluntarily at one in the morning to a woman who hadn't yet asked — that isn't an alibi. That's a story, and stories get built in advance by people who expect to need them. I've read enough confessions folded into envelopes to know that innocence is rarely so well organized.

The second thing wrong with it was smaller and worse: Dr. Anselm had died in March. Quietly, in his sleep, in a house that had recently changed hands. There was no box in any cellar anymore. There was no cellar. I knew this because I file things, including obituaries, including probate notices, including the sale of a dead doctor's property to a buyer whose name I'd flagged months ago and never followed up on.

Theo's alibi rested entirely on a witness who no longer existed and documents that had very likely been burned or boxed away by strangers. It was unfalsifiable. It was perfect.

It was almost too neat, and neatness, in my long experience of other people's secrets, is just guilt with good posture.

"So," Theo said. "Do I pass?"

"You're asking me to investigate you."

"I'm asking you to do what you do anyway. You've been doing it since I walked in — don't look surprised, Beatrix, you have a face like a ledger. Every time I speak, I can hear the little pen scratching." He leaned forward. "I'd rather you check than wonder. Wondering is how this town rotted in the first place. Everybody wondering, nobody checking, everybody agreeing not to know."

*Everybody agreeing not to know.* He couldn't have known how precisely that landed, how it described me standing at my window seven years ago, watching a figure move along the boathouse dock, choosing the warm dark of my room over the cold truth of the shore.

"Your alibi has a flaw," I heard myself say.

"Tell me."

"Dr. Anselm died in March. His practice records went with the estate sale. Whatever carbons existed are gone or locked in someone's attic, and the someone who bought that house bought it through a shell company registered in Wilmington." I watched his face while I said it, hunting for the flicker. "So your alibi is a memory. Yours, and a dead man's."

For a moment — half a moment, less — something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man realizing the examiner knows more of the answers than he does.

"Then I'll find another way to prove it," he said. "Staff rosters. The benefit dinner guest list. My aunt's calendar, if she still has it. Fever leaves traces, Beatrix — neighbors heard me being sick, for God's sake, Mrs. Hollis next door brought soup. Ask her. Ask everyone. I'm not afraid of being checked."

Mrs. Hollis. Soup. Neighbors. He was producing corroboration faster than I could discount it, and each new piece was plausible, and plausibility stacked high enough starts to look exactly like truth.

That's the trap of it. That's the whole architecture of the trap. A liar gives you one fact and dares you to disbelieve. An honest man gives you twenty and drowns you in them. And I sat there at my desk with the rain coming down and the kettle ticking as it cooled, unable to determine which one was sitting in my lobby offering me his life for inspection.

* * *

He left around three. I watched him cross the lobby, and at the stairs he paused and looked back, and the left corner of his smile came up, and he said, "Thank you for the tea. Same time tomorrow?" as if we were arranging something ordinary. As if either of us were ordinary.

I locked the front door behind him out of habit, though there was nothing outside but rain.

Then I sat down at the desk and did the thing I hate most in the world, which is turning my attention inward. I took out the mental file marked THEO VERLAINE and tried to review it objectively, the way I'd review any guest, any stranger, any suspect.

Subject returned after seven years abroad. Arrival coincides with the letters. Asks specific questions about correspondence addressed to the desk. Offers unsolicited alibi, extensively documented, resting on deceased witnesses. Watches my window — I had seen him do it, once, from Room 312's angle, before I knew what 312 meant. Charming. Insomniac. Left-handed — I'd noticed that the first night, the way he held his cup, the way the pen dragged when he signed the register.

Left-handed.

The handwriting on both letters had slanted leftward. I'd told myself it was a common enough trait, that thousands of people in the world slant leftward, that pattern-matching at four in the morning is how innocent men hang.

But the file wouldn't close. That was the horror of it. I am very good at closing files; it's practically my religion. And this one stayed open, gaping, because every fact inside it pointed two directions at once. Either Theo Verlaine had come home to find his sister's killer, or he had come home because he was his sister's killer's son and the letters had flushed him out of Geneva like a bird off a hunted field. Either he loved the truth or he needed to control who found it. Either he watched my window because he was protecting me or because he was studying me, the way you study a lock before you pick it.

And underneath all of it, in the margin of the file, in handwriting I refused to examine too closely, the real entry, the one that mattered:

*I want him to be innocent.*

There it was. Five nights of tea and rain and low-voiced talk, and I had committed the cardinal sin of my profession: I had started caring about the outcome. A good investigator holds the scales level. I was standing on one side of them, leaning, pretending the floor was flat.

I thought about Marguerite, whose diary pages I'd been reading in stolen hours — sharp, funny Marguerite, who had written *someone is moving my money and everyone smiles at me like I'm already gone*. Marguerite had trusted the people around her too. She'd trusted her brother. Look where trusting got her: at the bottom of the reservoir with the whole town signing the verdict that kept it comfortable.

If I let myself believe Theo because believing him felt like sunlight, then I was no better than Hollowmere. I was the whole town in miniature — agreeing not to know, because knowing might cost me something I'd only just discovered I wanted.

I hated myself for it. Thoroughly, specifically, with the precision I usually reserved for other people.

* * *

At five, the rain thinned. At six, the kitchen staff arrived and the world resumed its daytime costume. I brewed coffee I didn't drink and stood at the window watching the light come up gray over the reservoir road, and I made myself a promise, there in the glass's faint reflection:

I would check. All of it. Mrs. Hollis and the soup, the benefit dinner lists, Camille's calendar, the fever, the neighbors, the shell company in Wilmington. I would investigate the one person in Hollowmere I didn't want to investigate, and I would follow the evidence wherever it went, even if it led to the wing chair in my lobby, even if it led to the left-corner smile, even if it broke something in me that had been sealed shut for eleven years and longer.

Because duty was the only inheritance I had ever trusted. Because Marguerite deserved a witness who didn't flinch. Because I had been silent once, at a window, and silence was the debt I'd been paying interest on for seven years.

I opened the drawer where the letters lived and looked at them a moment — the confession, the promise of *more soon* — and I noticed something I should have noticed weeks ago.

The third letter hadn't come yet. The second one had promised more, and more had not arrived, and in my experience promises like that are never forgotten. They're scheduled.

As if the thought had summoned it, as if the town itself had been waiting for me to finish my vow, the mail slot clacked below the door.

One envelope slid across the marble, pale in the early light, and lay there face-up, addressed in that left-slanting hand:

*To the one who watched.*

I hadn't touched it yet, and already I knew: whatever was inside had been written for me alone.
