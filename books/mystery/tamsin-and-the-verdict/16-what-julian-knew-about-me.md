## 16. What Julian Knew About Me

The invitation came the way all of Julian Verlaine's invitations come: as though the world had already agreed and was merely being polite enough to inform you.

A card, cream stock, his initials embossed in a corner. *Dinner, Thursday, eight o'clock, the Verlaine house. Wear whatever you like; you'll be the best-dressed person there regardless.* No reason given. No RSVP requested. The man didn't request things. He scheduled them.

I stood at my desk at the Grand Meridian turning the card over twice, looking for a second meaning in the blank back, because that's what I do — I read the backs of things, the margins, the silences between words. There was nothing. Just good paper and a man's confidence that I would come.

He was right, of course. That was the trouble with Julian Verlaine. He was always right about what people would do, because he'd spent fifty years studying them the way I studied them — except where I filed secrets away like pressed flowers, he'd learned to spend them.

"You're making a face," Marta said, passing with a stack of linens. "The face you make when you're deciding whether to tell someone bad news or let them find out naturally."

"I've been invited to dinner."

"By who?"

"Julian Verlaine."

Marta stopped. The linens settled against her hip like a sleeping cat. "Beatrix Ashgrove," she said slowly, "you are not going to dinner with Julian Verlaine."

"I'm absolutely going to dinner with Julian Verlaine."

"Why?"

Because Ewan Pike was dead in the reservoir with water in his lungs and his shoes tied too neatly. Because Camille had gripped my wrist in the gala's shadowed corridor and said *I never touched her* like a woman setting down something heavy before it crushed her. Because Room 312 had been papered with seven years of me, and somewhere in that gallery of my own life was a photograph of Theo watching my window, and I still hadn't decided which version of him to believe. Because a man who kills when silence fails him had finally run out of reasons to leave me silent.

"Curiosity," I said.

"That's what killed the cat."

"The cat also died of old age eventually. Everything does." I put the card in my drawer — the real drawer now, the one with the letters, since the archive fire had taught me that the hotel's memory was flammable but mine apparently wasn't. "Hold the desk Thursday night. If I'm not back by midnight, call—"

"Call who? The police?" Marta's laugh had no humor in it. "Which police, Beatrix? Pike's replacement is Verlaine's golf partner."

"Then call no one," I said. "Just remember I went."

* * *

The Verlaine house sat on its hill above Hollowmere like a portrait hung slightly crooked on purpose, so you'd keep looking at it. Seven years ago I'd seen it lit for a wake. Tonight it was lit for dinner, warm gold in every window, and I thought: *this is how he sees himself. Illuminated. Above the town. Unbothered by weather.*

A housekeeper took my coat. A hallway of ancestors watched me pass — Verlaines in oil, all jaw and certainty, going back two centuries of owning things. At the end of the hall, through double doors, Julian stood with his back to me, pouring wine.

"You're punctual," he said without turning. "I'd have bet on it. Punctuality is just another form of filing, isn't it? Everything in its place, everything on time."

"You wanted to see me."

"I wanted to feed you." He turned, and there it was — the charm, banked low tonight, no audience to perform for except one concierge with a guilty conscience. He looked tired around the eyes in a way the gala lighting hadn't permitted. "Sit. The lamb is from Delacroix's farm, and if you don't eat it, I will have insulted a man whose sheep hold grudges."

I sat. He poured. The dining room held a table built for fourteen and set for two, which is its own kind of message: *look how much room I have. Look how little I need.*

"To honesty," Julian said, raising his glass. "It's so rare in this town that when you find it, you should toast it."

"Hollowmere runs on dishonesty," I said. "It's our chief industry. Well — that and the mill, before the mill closed."

"And yet you kept the books on all of it." He smiled. "Every sin in this town, alphabetized in a steel cabinet behind the front desk of the Grand Meridian. Don't look shocked. I've known about your files for years, Beatrix. I think they're marvelous. You're the only honest institution we have."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the year after Marguerite drowned." He said it the way other men say *since the war*. "You were new at the desk then. Twenty-six. You watched everyone, and everyone assumed you saw nothing, because you were polite. People mistake politeness for blindness. It's their great failing and your great advantage." He tasted his wine, approved of it. "I made a point of learning what you knew. All of it. Consider it professional courtesy between collectors."

The lamb arrived. I let it sit. My appetite had left the room sometime during the word *collectors*.

"If you know what's in my files," I said, "then you know why I'm here."

"I know why you *think* you're here." He cut his lamb with unhurried precision. "You think you're here to catch me saying something. You've got some little recorder in your handbag — don't check, you'll ruin the evening — and you're hoping I'll confess over the salad course, because you've been reading detective novels again. I can tell. Your questions have gotten more structured lately."

My handbag sat on the chair beside me, suddenly very loud in its silence.

"But here's what you haven't considered," Julian went on, "because you're honest, and honesty has a blind spot exactly the size of itself. You assume I'm afraid of the truth. Beatrix. Look around this room. Look at this house. I have lived inside the truth for seven years. It's the most comfortable thing I own."

"Marguerite found out about the money," I said. "The forged papers. Camille told me."

"Camille tells everyone everything these days. Grief makes people sloppy, and Camille has always confused guilt with grief." He set down his fork. "Yes. Marguerite found out. She was cleverer than anyone gave her credit for — including me, which I regret, professionally. She confronted me at the boathouse. She cried. She threatened to go to the solicitor in the morning. And I—" He paused, and for one unguarded half-second something moved behind his face, something old and wet and dark, like a shape under lake ice. Then it sealed over. "I made a decision. One decision. And seven years of quiet followed, because everyone in this town wanted quiet more than they wanted answers. Including you."

The candles moved. Or the air did. Something shifted.

"Including me," I repeated.

"You were at the boathouse window that night." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he's carried for weeks. "You saw a figure on the dock. You never told Constable Pike. You never told anyone. You locked it away in that beautiful cabinet of yours, under V, between the veterinarian's gambling debts and Mrs. Hollis's affair with the choir director." He leaned back. "You want to know the real reason my sister confessed to you by letter? Not remorse. Strategy. But the reason it *worked* — the reason you started digging instead of burning the letters — is that you've been waiting seven years for permission. Someone finally asked you the question you couldn't ask yourself: what did you see, Beatrix, and why did you stay quiet?"

The room was very warm. The ancestors in the hall watched through the open doors with their painted eyes.

"I stayed quiet," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "because I was twenty-six and scared and I told myself it was probably nothing. That's the whole confession. It isn't interesting. It isn't a conspiracy. It's a young woman being a coward for one night and then spending seven years pretending the night didn't happen."

"Yes," Julian said. "That's exactly what it is. Which is why I'm offering you this."

He rose, crossed to a sideboard, and came back with a folder — heavy stock, Verlaine crest, the works. He laid it beside my untouched plate.

"Director of Guest Relations. The Grand Meridian, outright, within two years — I control the lease, Beatrix, I've controlled it since old Meridian died owing me money. Your salary triples. Your files become an asset instead of a liability. Every secret in Hollowmere, formally collected, properly compensated. You'd be the keeper of the town's conscience, with a budget." He sat back down. "And in exchange, the letters stop arriving. The digging stops. Theo goes back to Geneva, heartbroken but alive, and you both live long lives wondering, occasionally, at three in the morning, and then falling asleep."

"That's not an offer," I said. "That's a bribe with furniture."

"It's an inheritance," he corrected. "This family corrupts everything it touches — ask my sister, ask my dead daughter, ask the solicitor who fell down his own stairs. I'm simply offering you the choice to be corrupted comfortably. Most people in this town took the same deal decades ago and got far worse terms. Pike got a pension. The coroner got a boat. You'd get a hotel." He lifted his glass again. "You've already been complicit for seven years, Beatrix. All I'm proposing is that you be paid for it."

There it was. The seduction of power doesn't arrive wearing a mask; it arrives wearing your own face, showing you the version of yourself you might have been if you'd been a little less stubborn and a little more tired. And God help me, I was tired. Seven years of filing other people's sins while sitting on my own. A hotel. A title. Permission to stop carrying it.

I looked at the folder. Cream stock. Good weight. The kind of paper that gets signed by people who've stopped asking questions.

"You said 'my dead daughter,'" I said quietly.

Julian's glass paused halfway to his mouth.

"You said it like a fact. Like something that happened. Not like an accident." I met his eyes. "You've confessed to me tonight, Mr. Verlaine. Over lamb. With a recorder in my handbag, as you so kindly noted. You confessed because you believe it doesn't matter — because you think every witness in this town is either bought, buried, or in love with your son. But you made one error."

"Did I." His voice had cooled several degrees. The charm was gone now, folded up and put away like the good silver after guests leave. What remained underneath was neither angry nor afraid. It was patient. That was the frightening part. Anger burns out; patience renovates.

"You assumed I'd want the hotel," I said. "You studied me for seven years and you learned everything I hid and nothing I wanted. I don't want to keep this town's secrets, Julian. I want to stop needing to."

I stood. My legs held, which surprised me. I picked up the folder and set it on the table between us, squared its edges to the tablecloth, because even now — especially now — everything in its place.

"Keep your directorship," I said. "File it under J."

I walked the length of that absurd table, past the candles, past the ancestors, past the housekeeper who materialized with my coat as though she'd been listening at the door, which she certainly had, and which meant Julian would know every word I said next, so I chose them carefully.

At the door I turned. He hadn't risen. He sat alone at the head of a table for fourteen, and for the first time in seven years of knowing his name, I saw Julian Verlaine look small.

"For what it's worth," I said, "your daughter sounded funny in her diary. Sharp. She'd have hated this dinner."

Something crossed his face — fast, involuntary, human — and was gone.

"Goodnight, Beatrix," he said. "Do give my regards to Theo. And do be careful driving home. The roads by the reservoir are dark this time of year. People drown on them."

Outside, the night smelled of cold water and coming rain. I got halfway down the drive before I heard the car behind me — slow, headlights off, engine too quiet for the hill — and I realized that Julian Verlaine had not offered me a job at all.

He'd offered me a head start.
