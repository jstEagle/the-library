## 18. The Last Letter

The fourth letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays at the Grand Meridian are the flattest day of the week — checkout by eleven, nothing in the book but a bridge club from Innsford and a salesman who haggled over the price of a wake-up call. If you're going to threaten someone's life, I have always maintained, do it on a Friday. Give the threat room to breathe.

This one didn't come through the post. That was the first thing I noticed, and I noticed it the way you notice a missing stair in the dark — too late, already falling.

It was tucked under the desk blotter. My side of the desk. The side no guest ever touches, because guests are trained from birth not to touch a concierge's things; it is one of the few surviving courtesies of civilization. Someone had lifted the corner of the leather, slid the envelope beneath, and pressed it flat again so neatly that the blotter sat without a wrinkle. I had been sitting at that desk for forty minutes before I found it, drinking my second coffee and mentally reorganizing the linen delivery, while my own death warrant waited six inches from my elbow like a patient cat.

The envelope was cream stock, heavy, unmarked. No stamp, no postmark, no return address. Inside, a single card of the same cream stock, and on it, in that handwriting — the handwriting I had now spent two weeks learning like a face — the same slanted, elegant script with its peculiar habit of crossing the *t* like a blade coming down:

*You were promised the truth. It lives where Marguerite died. The boathouse at Verlaine Hollow, midnight tonight. Come alone. Come unarmed. Come willing to listen.*

*If you bring the police, or the boy, or your clever little machine, Theo will pay for your curiosity. He is easier to reach than you think. Ask him about his mother sometime — oh, wait. You can't.*

*I have watched you for seven years, Beatrix. Don't make the eighth year boring.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The handwriting was the signature, and we both knew it.

I read it three times. Then I did what I always do when the world misbehaves: I filed it. Not in the drawer with the others — those letters lived at my apartment now, wrapped in a tea towel at the back of the wardrobe like contraband jam — but in my head, in the cabinet behind my eyes, in the folder marked THREATS, ACTIVE. The folder had been thin for most of my life. Lately it had developed girth.

*Your clever little machine.* So he knew about the recorder. Of course he knew. I had bought it in Innsford with cash like a woman in a spy film, and I had felt so competent doing it, and the whole time he had probably been watching me from whatever room or car or shadow he used these days, amused. Julian Verlaine did not merely know things. He knew the shape of your knowing. He knew what you'd bought and where you'd hidden it and how proud you were of the hiding.

And he knew about Theo. Knew that Theo was the pressure point, which meant he knew what Theo had become to me, which meant —

Which meant everyone in this town had read my heart before I'd finished writing it.

* * *

I found Theo in the staff corridor at half past four, arguing gently with Mrs. Okafor about whether the bridge club required sparkling or still water, and the sight of him stopped me mid-stride in a way that frightened me more than the letter had. Because the letter had told me exactly what I was looking at. It had put a price tag on him. *Theo will pay for your curiosity.* And there he stood, tall and rumpled and earnest, holding a pitcher of water as though it were a matter of international diplomacy, and all I could think was: *he is easier to reach than you think.*

"Beatrix." He saw my face and set the pitcher down. "What is it?"

"We need to talk. Not here."

We talked in the old card room on the third floor, the one nobody used since the hotel had stopped pretending to be the kind of place where people played cards. Dust lay on the green baize table like snow that had given up. Theo closed the door and I handed him the card and watched him read it, and I watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"He wrote this today," Theo said. "He was *here*. Under your blotter."

"Yes."

"That's — Beatrix, that means he walked past the front desk. Past you. While you were sitting there."

"I'm aware. I've been aware of very little else since four o'clock, if you want the truth, and I've been compensating by being extremely rude to the bridge club."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't. "You can't go."

"I have to go."

"You can't go *alone*, unarmed, at midnight, to a man who drowned his own sister—"

"And who will hurt you if I don't." I took the card back from him. My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that; I had expected them to shake, and instead they were the steadiest they'd been in weeks, as though some part of me had been waiting seven years for this appointment and had finally stopped dreading the calendar. "Theo. Listen to me, because I've thought about this since four o'clock and I've thought about very little else, as established. He doesn't want to kill me. If he wanted me dead, I'd be in the reservoir next to Ewan Pike, and we both know it. He wants me at that boathouse. Alone, he says, but he knows I won't come alone — he's counting on it, in fact. This letter isn't an invitation. It's a stage direction. He's casting me in the last scene of his play, and he's written my lines already."

"Then don't take the part."

"The alternative is that he takes it out on you." I said it flatly, because softness would have undone us both. "And I've spent seven years watching what happens to people Julian Verlaine finds inconvenient. Ewan Pike swam in a reservoir with dry shoes on. The solicitor who handled the trust papers died between one signature and the next. I am not adding you to that file."

"So we go to the police. Detective Harrow — she believes us now, after Camille, after the ledger—"

"Harrow is a good woman with a slow department. By the time she gets warrants, it'll be Thursday, and Thursday is past the deadline." I folded the card along its crease. "No. Here's what happens. I go. Alone, visibly alone, because that's what the stage direction requires. But I go *prepared*, and I go having already moved the pieces, and Julian Verlaine has made one mistake in seven years and it's this: he thinks I'm like everyone else in Hollowmere. He thinks I keep secrets the way the town does — buried, out of politeness, out of fear, out of that terrible small-town instinct to let sleeping dogs sign the verdict. He's watched me for seven years and he still doesn't understand that I don't file secrets to bury them. I file them to *keep* them. There's a difference. A grave and an archive are both quiet places, but only one of them is organized."

Theo stared at me. "You've been planning something."

"I've been planning several somethings. One of them involves Detective Harrow receiving a very detailed letter tomorrow morning, whether or not I'm alive to lick the stamp. One of them involves a recording device that Julian believes he knows the location of, and is wrong about. And one of them—" I looked at him, and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized, something quieter, "—involves you staying away from that boathouse. Completely away. Not stowed nearby. Not lurking in the reeds with righteous intentions. Away."

"Beatrix—"

"If you're within a mile of that water, he wins. Do you understand? He wrote *the boy* into the letter because he knows you'll come. Your whole life you've been trying to catch your father being guilty so you could stop loving him, and Julian knows that better than anyone, because he built you that way. If you show up, you give him both witnesses in one place, and then it's Marguerite's night all over again with different names." I stepped closer. The dust hung between us in the grey window light. "I cannot do what I have to do tonight if I'm also doing arithmetic about you. So do the one thing I will ever ask of you that I actually mean: stay away, and let me finish this."

He was silent for a long moment. Outside, the bridge club could be heard concluding that still water was fine, actually, if it was cold.

"And if he kills you?" Theo said finally. His voice cracked on *kills* like a plate hitting tile.

"He won't. He needs me alive — I'm the last witness, and a confession needs a witness the way a lock needs a key. He told me that himself, in his way, in the first letter. *The truth is still unfolding.* He's been unfolding it toward me for seven years. Tonight it finishes unfolding, and tomorrow it goes in a file, and the day after that it goes in front of a judge." I held out my hand, palm up, an absurd formal gesture in a dusty card room, and after a moment he took it. "Go home, Theo. Pack a bag. Be somewhere Harrow can find you and I can't. If everything goes the way it should, I'll see you when it's over, and we'll have the conversation we've been circling for weeks — the real one, the one that starts after the verdict."

"And if it doesn't go the way it should?"

"Then you'll get a letter," I said. "You know how fond this family is of letters."

* * *

I went home and dressed for a drowning.

That sounds theatrical. Everything about that day was theatrical; Julian had seen to that. But I stand by the phrase, because I chose my clothes the way you choose them for a funeral — dark, plain, nothing that catches. Flat shoes, because the path down to Verlaine Hollow was gravel and mud and I refused to die or even stumble on a heel. My hair pinned back. No jewelry. And beneath the dark coat, strapped where a shoulder holster would sit if I were a different kind of woman, the recorder — not the one from Innsford, the obvious one, the one Julian's surveillance had surely catalogued, but the second one, the backup, the one I'd bought the same afternoon and told no one about, not even Theo. *Especially* not Theo. It sat against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I confess I found the company comforting.

Before I left, I did the thing I had promised myself I would do. I sat at my kitchen table with paper and pen — actual pen, actual paper, because digital files burn and clouds belong to other people — and I wrote the letter to Harrow. Everything. Seven years of it. The figure at the boathouse window, the shape of the shoulders, the lantern light on the water, my silence and its long cost. The forged trust papers. Camille's confession. Room 312 and its wallpaper of photographs. Ewan Pike's dry shoes. Julian's dinner-table offer, delivered like a man discussing wine. I named dates, times, account numbers I'd memorized off documents I probably shouldn't have memorized. I sealed it, addressed it, and left it propped against the inside of my door with a note taped to it in my own unmistakable hand: *MARTA — POST THIS FIRST THING. NO EXCUSES. THIS MEANS YOU.* Marta from 2B collected her neighbor's mail when I traveled; she was nosy enough to read the note and obedient enough to obey it, which is the finest combination a landlady can possess.

Then I took out the drawer's contents one last time — the first confession, the promise of *more soon*, the third letter addressed to *the one who watched* — and I read them in sequence, like chapters, because that is what they had always been. Chapters of a book someone else was writing about my town, using my silence as binding glue.

Seven years ago I had stood at my window above the boathouse road and seen a figure carry something — someone — down to the black water, and I had told myself it was a dream, a deer, a trick of lantern light, anything, everything, because the figure wore the build of a man whose family employed half of Hollowmere and I was twenty-four years old and afraid in the specific way the young are afraid: efficiently, permanently, and without honor. I had gone back to bed. I had gone back to work. I had built a career out of noticing everything except the one thing that mattered, and I had called the career vigilance.

Tonight the account came due. Fine. I had never minded paying bills. What I had minded, all these years, was that nobody ever sent them.

At half past eleven I locked my door, walked down the hill past the shuttered bakery and the church whose clock had run four minutes slow since before Marguerite was born, and turned onto the lane that led to Verlaine Hollow. The night was cold and absolutely clear, the kind of autumn night where the stars look close enough to be streetlights for a town slightly better than ours. The reservoir lay below in the dark like a held breath. Somewhere ahead, the boathouse stood on its rotting legs over the water, and a single lantern burned inside it — lit for me, arranged for me, a stage waiting for its last scene.

I checked the recorder against my ribs. I thought of Theo, hopefully far away and furiously awake. I thought of Marguerite, who had been funny and sharp and twenty-six forever.

"All right," I said aloud, to the town, to the water, to whoever was watching — and someone was always watching. "Let's settle the file."

The lantern flickered, and a figure detached itself from the boathouse shadows, unhurried, as though he had all the time in the world and intended to spend mine.
