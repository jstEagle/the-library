## 8. Vivian's Version

Vivian Ashe had been in the Grand Verity Hotel for four days before she admitted to herself that she was hiding.

Not from the police — they had no interest in her. Not from the hotel staff, who were professionally incurious in the way of people who see everything and are paid to forget it. She was hiding from the truth, which was this: she had come to a hotel to grieve a father she had not spoken to in eleven years, and grief, it turned out, did not care whether you'd earned it.

She sat in the Palm Court at nine in the morning with a pot of tea she hadn't touched and a folder of letters she couldn't stop touching. Around her, the hotel performed its morning liturgy: the clink of silver on porcelain, the fountain doing its patient arithmetic, a bellhop wheeling a rack of luggage past with the serene confidence of a man who had never once wondered where any of it was going.

Her father had loved this place. That was the unforgivable part, really. Not the secrets, not the years of silence, not even the letter that had arrived after his death like a hand reaching up out of the ground. No — what she could not forgive was that Aldous Wren had apparently been *happy* here, in this lobby with its tired velvet chairs and its brass everything, while his daughter in Chicago waited for a phone call that came, in the end, only as news of a death.

*If anything happens to me,* the letter said, in handwriting that shook only toward the end, *the ledger will clear our name. Or damn it. Either way, you'll finally know. It's at the Verity. Hidden in plain sight, Vivi. I know how much you hate that phrase.*

She hated it because he'd used it for everything. The good silverware, hidden in plain sight in the kitchen drawer. Her Christmas presents, hidden in plain sight in the trunk of his car. Her whole childhood had been a series of things he refused to simply *give* her, preferring instead to make her hunt.

"More tea?" asked the waiter, a young man with the mournful dignity of an actor between roles.

"No, thank you." She smiled. It was a good smile; she'd practiced it in mirrors during difficult decades. "Actually — could I ask you something? My father stayed here for many years. Aldous Wren. I'm trying to find out about his... arrangements."

The waiter's face did something interesting. It didn't change, exactly. It just became slightly more closed, the way a shop does when the sign flips to CLOSED but the lights are still on.

"I wouldn't know about that, madam. But Mr. Halloran at the concierge desk is wonderful with questions."

"Mr. Halloran," she repeated, filing it away.

Vivian had spent thirty years as a litigation attorney learning to notice what people did with their faces, and so she noticed that the waiter walked away a little too quickly, and that he stopped at the concierge desk, and that a slim person with careful eyes looked over at her — one look, brief and total, like a lighthouse sweep — before returning to whatever they were doing with a stack of mail.

So. The concierge knew who she was. Or knew enough.

Good, she thought, and then, less steadily: *or very bad.*

* * *

Room 412 had been sealed since the death, which meant nothing to a woman who had once talked her way into a deposition room during a court recess by claiming to be the court reporter's niece. But the Grand Verity was not a courthouse full of distracted clerks; it was a machine built for discretion, and machines like that don't have nieces.

So she did it the honest way, which was to say the expensive way. She extended her stay through the end of the month. She tipped the housekeeping staff early and often, learning their names — Marisol, who had three children and a laugh like a struck bell; Dev, who was saving for a culinary course and described omelets the way other men described sports cars. She learned that kindness opened more doors than cash, though she paid both, because her father had taught her never to rely on a single instrument.

"He carried a leather folio everywhere," Marisol told her on the third day, folding towels in the service corridor with the ease of a woman who had decided Vivian was all right. "Every day, under his arm, like a vicar with a Bible. Room 412's inventory doesn't list one, though. I checked."

"You checked?"

Marisol shrugged, unrepentant. "He was kind to me. Eleven years, he remembered my children's names every time. When someone like that dies alone, you want to know why."

*Alone.* The word went into Vivian like a splinter. She had assumed her father died alone because he deserved to — because he had chosen the hotel over the family, the folio over the daughter. But standing in the service corridor smelling of lemon polish and steam, she felt the assumption shift under her weight, the way ice shifts before it decides.

"Did anyone visit him? Before they sealed the room?"

"A woman came. Day after the funeral. Said she was family." Marisol's hands paused on a towel. "She wasn't you."

"No," Vivian said. "She wasn't."

She thought about that woman all afternoon, sitting in the Palm Court with her untouched tea, watching the concierge desk in the lobby's long mirror without appearing to watch it. The slim figure — Halloran — moved through the day like someone reading a book everyone else had returned to the shelf already. They greeted a honeymooning couple by name. They intercepted a package addressed to a guest who was, per the register, still at breakfast, and set it aside with a small frown of protective suspicion. They noticed everything, and what they noticed, they kept.

Vivian recognized the type. She saw it in junior associates sometimes, the ones who'd grown up in houses where love was conditional on vigilance. People who watched because somewhere, once, watching had been the price of safety.

She was fairly sure she was one herself.

* * *

That evening she went down to the bar, because her father's letters kept mentioning the bar. *Victor always held court at the corner table,* one read, dated 1996. *Same table your grandfather used. This hotel has a longer memory than any of us, Vivi. That can be a mercy or a noose.*

The bar at seven was amber and murmuring. The corner table was occupied by a couple celebrating something small — an anniversary, maybe, or a successful biopsy — and Vivian felt an absurd flash of resentment toward them for sitting where history had happened, before she caught herself. History happened everywhere. That was rather the problem.

"Whiskey," she told the bartender. "Neat. Whatever my father would have ordered."

The bartender, a broad calm man named Tomas, poured without comment, then said, "Aldous Wren drank the same thing for eleven years. Never varied. He'd sit right there—" a nod at the corner, "—and read. He wasn't a talker. But he listened like a priest."

"Did people confess to him?"

"People confess to anyone who looks like they'll keep it." Tomas wiped the bar with a slow circular motion. "You've got the same look, if you don't mind my saying."

"I mind," said Vivian, and then, because it was a lie: "No. I don't."

Tomas smiled and moved off, and Vivian sat with her whiskey and her father's ghost and tried to assemble the man from the pieces. Here was what she knew, laid out now like evidence on a conference table:

Her father, Aldous Wren, had spent decades at this hotel documenting something. A ledger. Entries going back to the nineties. A scheme involving inheritances — the word *inheritance* appeared in his letters more than any other word except *Vivi*, and she had chosen not to examine that until now, and examining it now made her throat tight.

Her father, Victor Ashe — solicitor, charmer, the man whose signature she'd forged on school forms so many times she could do it asleep — had run some kind of "estate services" operation, and her father's letters suggested Wren had been *watching* him. Recording him. For thirty years.

And then Wren had died, alone, in Room 412, one week before sending his daughter a letter saying he'd been murdered.

There was a version of this where her father was a victim. There was a version where he was a monster. Vivian had spent her career explaining to juries that most people were both, and that the law's great failure was its insistence on choosing.

"Rough night?" said a voice beside her.

She turned. The concierge — Halloran — stood at the bar's edge with a cup of coffee and the particular weariness of someone halfway through a double shift. Up close, their noticing quality resolved into something almost gentle: dark circles, ink on one finger, a cardigan worn like armor against the hotel's aggressive air conditioning.

"Do you make a habit of approaching guests who look like trouble?" Vivian asked.

"Only the ones drinking my father's whiskey order." They glanced at her glass. "Neat, no variation, corner table. You've been asking about Aldous Wren for three days. The staff talk. I listen."

"And you are?"

"Coral Halloran. Concierge." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "I arranged Mr. Wren's funeral. Nobody claimed the body, so somebody had to say the words over the box. I said them."

Something in Vivian's chest moved — an old door swinging on a rusted hinge. "What did you say?"

"That he noticed things. That he was kind to the staff. That he seemed to be waiting for someone who never came." Coral sipped their coffee, eyes steady on hers. "I didn't know then if the someone existed. Now I'm looking at her, so I suppose I can update the eulogy."

It should have stung. Instead it landed like an absolution, which was worse, and Vivian found she had to look at her whiskey for a moment before she could speak.

"We weren't close," she said finally. "Eleven years. He wrote to me anyway. Every few months, these long letters full of hints. *Hidden in plain sight.* Like grief was a game he wanted me to win."

"And did you hate him for it?"

"Yes."

"Me too," said Coral, "with mine," and Vivian understood that this was the closest thing to intimacy the concierge offered strangers, and that she had just received it, and that she did not entirely trust it.

* * *

She worked the archive angle for two more days, and it went badly, and she knew it went badly because on the second evening she caught Coral watching her do it.

The hotel's records lived in a basement corridor behind the ballroom — she'd mapped it from fire escape diagrams, another thing her father had taught her without meaning to. Getting in required a keycard she didn't have, so she did what she could from the public side: the historical photographs in the mezzanine corridor, the plaque commemorating the hotel's centenary, the framed newspaper clipping about a 1994 charity gala at which, she now noticed, a young Victor Ashe stood half a head taller than everyone else, smiling like a man who has just found the loose thread on the world.

She was photographing the clipping with her phone when she saw them in the frame's edge — not in the old photo, but reflected in the glass covering it. Coral, at the far end of the corridor, holding a stack of guest mail, watching her with an expression she couldn't parse. Not hostile. Not friendly. The expression of a chess player watching an opponent make a move they'd predicted three turns ago.

Vivian lowered her phone. Walked toward them. Coral didn't pretend to be doing anything else, which she respected enormously.

"You're following me," she said.

"You're investigating a murder in my hotel," Coral replied. "We're circling each other. It's not the same thing, but it rhymes."

"My father—"

"Was Victor Ashe. Yes. I know." Something flickered across their face — pity, maybe, or its professional cousin. "Ms. Ashe, I'm going to say something I probably shouldn't, and you can decide what it's worth. Someone got into the archives two nights ago. The 1994 records are damaged. And the security log says it was your keycard, at a time when you were having dinner in the Palm Court in full view of forty people, including me."

The corridor went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the fountain kept its patient count.

"That's not possible," Vivian said.

"No," Coral agreed. "It isn't. Which means either someone copied your card, or someone wants us both looking at you instead of at them." They shifted the mail in their arms, and for a moment they looked very young and very tired. "I don't know yet which side you're on, Ms. Ashe. But I know you're not on the side that's forging security logs. Whoever did that knows this building better than any guest should."

"And you're telling me this because?"

Coral considered the question honestly, which she could tell because they actually paused before answering.

"Because Aldous Wren arranged his own funeral through me, in writing, six months before he died. He knew. Whatever he knew, he knew it was coming for him. And he left a letter saying the proof was hidden in plain sight in this hotel." Their eyes held hers, dark and level. "You came here hunting his ledger. So did I. I think we should compare notes before whoever killed him decides we're both problems."

Vivian felt the ground tilt — not unpleasantly, the way a ship tilts when it finally catches wind. Two investigators, circling the same truth. She had spent her whole life alone with her family's secrets; the idea of handing half of hers to a stranger in a cardigan was either the stupidest thing she'd ever done or the first smart thing in eleven years.

"One condition," she said. "You tell me everything about the funeral. I want to know what you said. All of it."

"It wasn't flattering."

"Neither was he."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Coral's face. "Meet me tonight, after the bar closes. There's a service stairwell behind the kitchen nobody uses after ten." They turned to go, then stopped. "Ms. Ashe — bring the letters. All of them. If we're doing this, we do it with everything on the table."

"They might damn my father."

"They might clear him." Coral's voice was quiet. "But either way, you'll finally know. Isn't that what he promised you?"

The words landed exactly where the letter had landed, in the soft unprotected place beneath her ribs, and Vivian Ashe stood alone in the mezzanine corridor surrounded by eighty years of other people's photographed happiness, and realized she had already decided.

She just hoped, walking back toward the elevators, that she wasn't leading the killer straight to the last person in the building who trusted her.
