## 9. Missing Pages

The Grand Verity's archive lived in the basement, in a room that smelled of dust, lemon polish, and the particular melancholy of paper that no one had loved in decades. Coral had always found it comforting. Down here, every guest who had ever passed through the brass doors was still passing through them — registration cards in long rows of grey boxes, folios bound by year, the whole hotel's memory filed with a tidiness that would have made Coral's grandmother weep with joy.

Coral went down at six in the morning, before the day shift arrived, carrying two coffees and Wren's decoded cipher copied onto the back of a laundry slip. The 1994 entry was the third one they'd cracked, and it sat in their mind now like a splinter: *Room 214 — June 9, 1994 — H. — settled.*

*H.* for Halloran.

They'd barely slept. Every time they closed their eyes they saw their mother's kitchen table, and imagined a lawyer's pen moving across it, and could not stop imagining it.

The archive door was unlocked, which was normal. What was not normal was the smell of fresh air where there should have been none — that faint disturbance, like a room recently exhaled from. Coral noticed it the way they noticed everything: not as a thought but as a prickle along the forearms.

The 1994 box was on the shelf where it belonged. The label was straight. The box itself was square to the shelf's edge, squared *too* carefully, the way a person squares an object after handling it, overcorrecting toward innocence.

Coral set the coffees down on the reading table, pulled on white cotton gloves — the archivist Mrs. Okafor's rule, and Mrs. Okafor's ghost was the sort you did not offend — and lifted the lid.

The folio inside was gutted.

Not gone. Gutted. Someone had taken the time to open the leather binding, ease out the pages with a thin blade or a very patient fingernail, and leave the cover sitting in its place like a shed skin. The registration cards for June 1994 were there, fanned neatly. The folio that should have held the estate correspondence — the wills, the transfers, the letters of administration — held nothing but the ghostly impression of pages once pressed against its lining, and a single dried coffee ring that had not been there when Coral had catalogued this section two years ago.

Two years ago. Coral had touched this folio two years ago, during the great reorganization, and it had been whole. Which meant the theft had happened since. Which meant it had happened while Coral was walking these halls every day, noticing everything, and noticing *nothing*.

"Well," Coral said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud made them true or at least survivable. "Well, that's not good."

They sat down and drank both coffees, one after the other, because decisions required caffeine and this was going to require several.

* * *

By eight, Coral had done what any sensible person does upon discovering a crime: they went to security, smiled the smile that got concierges anything, and asked Devraj at the desk to pull the archive corridor footage for the past week.

"Anything specific?" Devraj asked, already swiveling his monitor. He was twenty-three and treated the camera system like a video game he was extremely good at.

"Anyone near the archives. Especially late."

"There's never anyone near the archives. I watch this feed sometimes when it's slow. It's the most boring thing in the building. Once a moth flew into frame and I got genuinely excited."

"Devraj."

"I'm pulling it, I'm pulling it."

He scrubbed backward through the week. The corridor was exactly as boring as advertised — housekeeping carts, a delivery of toilet paper in truly heroic quantities, Mrs. Okafor herself shuffling past with her keys. And then, Tuesday night, 11:47 p.m., a figure in a dark coat, hood up, moving with purpose. The figure paused at the archive door, glanced up — directly into the camera, though the hood shadowed the face — and let themselves in with a keycard.

"Gotcha," said Devraj.

"Can you get the door log?"

He tapped. The log bloomed on screen. **ARCHIVE — SERVICE DOOR — ACCESS GRANTED — CARD #117 — ASHE, V. — 23:47.**

Coral felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it did on ships in films.

"That's—" Devraj frowned. "That's the woman from 1103, right? Checked in last week? Pretty, scary, asks a lot of questions?"

Vivian Ashe. Of course it was Vivian Ashe. The daughter at the desk, asking pointed questions about Wren's mail. The woman who'd visited Room 412 before it was sealed. Everything fit so neatly that Coral's skin crawled with the wrongness of it, the way a sentence can be grammatically perfect and still be a lie.

"Rewind it," Coral said. "Show me the timestamp again."

**TUESDAY 23:47.**

And here was the thing. Here was the thing that made Coral's tea go cold in their stomach, if Coral had been holding tea, which they were not, but the sensation occurred anyway.

On Tuesday at 11:47 p.m., Coral had been in the staff canteen. Not alone — that was the point, that was the whole point. Tuesday nights were the quarterly fire-safety refresher, mandatory for all front-of-house, run by the interminable Mr. Bhatt from Risk & Compliance, and Coral had sat through ninety minutes of slides about extinguisher classes wedged between Marta from Housekeeping and young Tobias from the bell desk, checking the clock, checking it again, checking it a third time because the clock seemed personally offended by Coral's existence.

Ninety minutes. Twenty-two staff members. A sign-in sheet with Coral's signature on it, in ink, at 10:58 p.m., and again at 12:31 a.m. when the blessed thing finally ended.

Which meant that either Vivian Ashe had walked through a locked service door eleven floors above a meeting Coral attended — or someone had used her keycard to make it look that way.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited, wearing its little hat of horror — someone wanted Coral to find exactly this. Exactly this contradiction. A frame job with a flaw in it is not a failed frame job. It is bait.

"Coral?" Devraj was watching them. "You've gone weird. Weirder than baseline."

"The footage," Coral said slowly. "Who else can access the camera system?"

Devraj's face did something complicated. "Me. Marcus. Security management. And—" he hesitated, and lowered his voice as though the cameras might hear him, "—technically anyone with the master override. Facilities has it. Legal has it. It's not, like, *fortified*, Coral. It's a hotel."

A hotel. Where a solicitor named Penhale had kept an office on the mezzanine for thirty-one years, with a key to everything and a reason to know about keycards and a name that had not yet appeared in the cipher but whose patience, whose immaculate cufflinks, whose habit of remembering everyone's business had always struck Coral as more than professional courtesy.

No. Not yet. One suspicion at a time; that was how you kept your footing.

"Copy the footage," Coral said. "Quietly. Don't tell Marcus, don't tell anyone. Put it somewhere only you can reach."

"This is the part where I ask if we're doing something bad."

"We're doing something careful. There's a difference, and the difference is coffee." Coral slid the second cup across the desk. "I owe you a week of coffees. Possibly a month."

"For a month," Devraj said, accepting the cup, "I'll learn to lie convincingly. Within reason."

* * *

The rest of the morning was the performance of Coral's career. They stood at the concierge desk and recommended restaurants and hailed cars and solved a small crisis involving a guest's lost hearing aid (found, miraculously, in a pot of lobby ferns — Coral chose not to investigate how) — all while their mind ran the same loop beneath the pleasant surface, like a river under ice.

Someone had gutted the 1994 folio. Someone had used Vivian Ashe's keycard during an hour she could not have been in the basement — unless she had cloned it, unless she had an accomplice, unless the impossible timestamp was itself the trick, a double bluff wrapped around a triple bluff. Coral turned it over and over like a stone with something underneath.

If Vivian was guilty, why leave evidence so theatrical? If Vivian was innocent, then whoever framed her knew the archives, knew the camera system, knew the staff schedule well enough to pick the one hour Coral was accounted for — which meant they knew Coral was looking. Which meant the letter, the ledger, the cipher, all of it, might be a trail laid not for Coral's benefit but *through* Coral, using them as a bloodhound with a lamp.

"You're making a face," said Marta, appearing beside the desk with a linen cart like a small sturdy warship coming alongside. Marta missed nothing; it was why Coral loved her and occasionally feared her.

"Just tired."

"Mm." She looked at Coral the way she looked at rooms that claimed to need nothing. "You found something in the basement."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because yesterday you hummed. You never hum. Today you have the face of a woman in a mystery novel, chapter nine." She snapped a folded towel over her arm. "Whatever it is — and I'm not asking — be careful. This hotel eats careful people too."

She rolled away before Coral could ask what that meant, which was very Marta.

* * *

At noon, Coral took their break in the back stairwell, third floor landing, the one spot in the building with a window and no foot traffic, and laid the facts out on the mental table like a hand of cards:

One. The 1994 folio was emptied sometime in the past two years, likely recently, likely by someone unhurried.

Two. The keycard log implicated Vivian Ashe at an hour that alibied her out of existence — or implicated her clumsily, or implicated her deliberately.

Three. Whoever did it either didn't know about the fire-safety meeting, or knew perfectly well and wanted the contradiction found.

Four. Aldous Wren had written, in a letter postmarked after his death: *the proof is in the book you file every day.* And now the book was being eaten, page by page, ahead of Coral.

Which meant Coral was in a race. And races, Coral reflected grimly, were run by two kinds of people: those who knew the course, and those who were being chased along it.

The stairwell door opened below. Footsteps — unhurried, polished-shoe footsteps — ascending. Coral knew those footsteps. Everyone at the Verity knew those footsteps; they preceded Mr. Penhale the way thunder precedes weather.

But it wasn't Penhale. It was Latch — the private investigator who'd been interviewing staff about Wren's death, the man with the notebook and the questions shaped like courtesy. He climbed into view, saw Coral, and stopped with the easy surprise of a man who is never actually surprised.

"Miss Halloran." He consulted his notebook without opening it. "Taking the stairs. Healthy habit."

"It's quiet up here."

"Everything quiet is worth knowing about." He smiled. His eyes stayed level and unreadable, twin locked drawers. "Funny thing. I've been asking about the archives. Who goes down there. When. Whether anything's been... disturbed." He tilted his head. "You'd know, wouldn't you? You practically live in that basement."

The ratchet clicked another notch. Coral smiled back with thirty years of hospitality in it.

"If I hear anything, Mr. Latch, you'll be the first to know."

"I believe you will," he said pleasantly, and continued upward, and Coral stood on the landing listening to his footsteps fade, understanding suddenly and completely that the race had spectators — and that at least one of them was betting against them.

Below, faint through the walls, the grandfather clock in the lobby began to strike noon. Twelve strokes, patient as a verdict.

Coral had a folio to reconstruct, a dead man's cipher to finish, and a growing suspicion that the safest person in the Grand Verity Hotel was the one everyone had already stopped suspecting.

Starting tomorrow, Coral decided, they would watch Mr. Penhale's shoes.

Everyone's shoes told the truth eventually.
