## 13. Latch Makes an Offer

The trouble with working nights at the Grand Verity, Coral had long ago decided, was that the hotel never let you finish a thought. Thoughts at the Verity were like guests: they checked in, made demands, and left without tipping.

Coral was in the middle of one — a good one, about whether Vivian Ashe's charm was a costume or a skin — when Mr. Latch materialized beside the front desk with the quiet inevitability of weather.

"Miss Halloran." He said it the way other people said *checkmate*, though Coral hadn't told him their surname. They'd checked. Twice. The staff directory listed them as C. Halloran; the registration cards said nothing; Latch had simply known, the way he seemed to know everything that passed through the lobby like smoke through a keyhole.

"It's just Halloran," Coral said. "Like Madonna. Or a house fire."

Latch smiled. It was a careful smile, the kind a man practices in a mirror until it stops being a smile and becomes a tool. He was dressed, as always, in his gray investigator's suit, and his shoes — Coral noted automatically, because noticing was the one muscle they'd never been able to relax — were polished to a shine that couldn't quite hide the resoling. Hotel-issue slippers, once upon a time. Resoled by the cobbler on Marlow Street who did half the staff's boots at a discount. Which meant Mr. Latch, private investigator, man of the world, was living somewhere inside the Grand Verity.

Room service trays, Coral thought. He's eating off room service trays and calling himself a detective.

"You've been busy," Latch said, resting two fingers on the desk as if claiming it. "The archives. The old registration cards. 1994, I hear."

"I'm a concierge," Coral said. "I'm interested in hospitality history. Did you know we once hosted a man who trained pigeons for the war effort? Fascinating."

"I did know, actually. Room 208, March of '43. He kept them in the bathtub." Latch leaned in slightly. "You see my problem, Halloran? You're good. Better than good. And good people asking questions about Aldous Wren make my client nervous."

"And who is your client?"

"Anonymous," Latch said, with the satisfaction of a man who'd been waiting all week to say it. "Retainer paid in cash. Instructions delivered through a post office box. All very old-fashioned. Very Wren, if you take my meaning."

"I don't."

"No," Latch agreed pleasantly. "But you will."

* * *

He steered them to the bar, which was empty at four in the afternoon except for Tomas polishing glasses that were already clean, and ordered two coffees with the ease of a man who knew the prices without looking. Coral sat because sitting was information-gathering, and because refusing would have told Latch exactly how rattled they were, and Coral would rather have died than hand him that.

"So," Latch said. "Aldous Wren kept a ledger. Thirty years of entries. Room numbers, dates, names. A record of certain... transactions. Estates that changed hands in ways the courts never quite examined."

"That sounds like gossip."

"That sounds like leverage," Latch corrected. "My client wants it before the estate settles. Once probate closes, the ledger becomes evidence, or it becomes trash, but either way it stops being negotiable. There's a window. We're in it."

"We."

"I'm offering you a share." He slid a napkin across the bar. On it, in tidy accountant's figures, was a number with enough zeroes to make Coral's rent anxiety sit down and be quiet for a while. "For whatever you find. For whatever you already found. For access. Your choice how you earn it."

Coral looked at the number for exactly as long as it took to want it, then looked away, because wanting it was also information, and they weren't going to hand Latch that either.

"And if I say no?"

"Then you keep doing what you're doing, alone, in a building where someone killed a man in his sleep and made it look like his heart gave out." Latch sipped his coffee. "I'd rather have you inside the tent, Halloran. You notice things. It's your whole personality, I think. It's certainly your whole defense system."

That landed closer than Coral liked. They thought of their mother's kitchen, of learning early to read the weather of an adult's face before speaking. *Noticing is just love with nowhere to go,* their mother used to say, and Coral had carried that sentence around like a stone in the pocket for twenty years.

"What does your client actually want with the ledger?" Coral asked. "If it's evidence of crimes, destroying it protects criminals. If it's proof of ownership, why not come forward legally? Anonymous clients don't hire investigators to do the right thing. They hire them to do the quiet thing."

Latch's careful smile flickered — not much, a hairline crack in porcelain, but Coral saw it, and saw that Latch knew they'd seen it.

"You're right, of course," he said. "Which is why I'm telling you this part free of charge: I don't much care for my client. I care for my fee. But a man can only polish his conscience so long before it starts to squeak." He set down the cup. "Here's what I can't figure. My client gives me instructions that could only come from inside this hotel. Timings. Staff rotations. Which cameras work and which are decorative. Whoever hired me has keys, Halloran. Or worse — has friends."

The lobby hummed behind them, indifferent. A bellhop wheeled luggage past. Somewhere upstairs a vacuum droned its one long note. The Grand Verity went on being the Grand Verity, and Coral felt, for the first time since the letter arrived, the true shape of the thing: not a mystery to be solved from the outside, but a net already drawn around them, knot by knot, while they'd been busy decoding.

"The letter," Latch said.

Coral's heart performed a small, traitorous somersault. "What letter?"

"Don't." Latch's voice lost its salesman warmth entirely, and what was underneath was sharper and somehow more honest. "Wren sent it to you. Postmarked after his funeral. I know because my client knows, and my client knows because—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "Because there's very little that happens in this building my client doesn't know. Including, I suspect, that you've been pulling thirty years of registration cards and reading them by flashlight in the archive corridor like a teenager with a torch and a paperback."

"I use the desk lamp," Coral said, mostly to prove they weren't rattled, and immediately regretted confirming anything at all.

Latch almost laughed. Almost. "Here's my last offer, and it's the honest one. Help me find the ledger first. Not for my client — for us. Because whoever killed Wren is still here, still watching, and now they're watching you. My client wanted the ledger recovered quietly. Quietly means no witnesses. You've made yourself a witness, Halloran, every night you've spent in those archives."

"And you're warning me out of professional courtesy?"

"I'm warning you because dead witnesses don't pay fees," Latch said, "and because I read a man's mail for forty years in the army and I know what it looks like when someone's in over their head." He stood, dropped bills on the bar, and left the napkin with the number on it, face up, like a card he expected them to pick up later.

At the door he paused. "One more thing. The woman. Ashe. Whatever she's told you, remember whose daughter she is. Victor Ashe didn't fall out of a window in Lisbon by accident, and daughters inherit more than money." Then he was gone, into the revolving door, around and around and out, leaving Coral alone with two cold coffees and the distinct sensation of having been measured for something.

* * *

Tomas drifted over, collected the cups. "Friend of yours?"

"Occupational hazard."

"He tipped," Tomas said, sounding scandalized. "Nobody tips at four in the afternoon. That's a man trying to be remembered fondly."

Coral almost smiled. Leave it to Tomas to diagnose the entire encounter in nine words. "What else did you notice?"

Tomas shrugged, polishing. "He asked for the coffee *before* he looked at the menu. Knew we switched suppliers in March. Knew Marta's name." He paused. "Knew yours, and you only started days shift Tuesday."

The net, drawing tighter. Coral thanked him, walked back to the desk, and stood there amid the brass bells and the guest mail slots — slot 412 among them, unremarkable, patient — and made themselves think.

An anonymous client with insider knowledge. Instructions timed to staff rotations. Knowledge of the letter — knowledge that lived, so far as Coral knew, in exactly three places: Wren's dead hand, Coral's locked drawer, and wherever the killer kept their secrets.

Unless the letter had been read before it reached them. Unless the mailroom, the front desk, the whole circulatory system of the hotel had eyes in it. Penhale's office handled the estate. Penhale had master-key access to everything, a fact Coral had filed away weeks ago and not yet understood why. Vivian had been asking pointed questions about Wren's mail since the day she checked in. And Latch himself lived somewhere in the building, resoled slippers and all, loyal to a fee rather than a person.

Allies indistinguishable from threats. Threats indistinguishable from allies. Coral thought of Edda Kwan, retired upstate, the only person in thirty years of hotel memory who might tell them the truth for free. Of Vivian Ashe, whose grief might be real or might be the best performance Coral had ever witnessed. Of their mother, who had signed her name once without reading and lost everything, and who would understand — better than anyone — what it cost to trust the wrong charming stranger.

The napkin lay on the bar where Latch had left it. Coral retrieved it, folded it once, and did not throw it away. Not because they intended to take the money. Because the number itself was evidence: whoever wrote it believed the ledger was worth that much, and worth it *now*, before probate closed, which meant the client wasn't protecting the dead.

They were protecting themselves.

Coral slipped the napkin into their pocket, picked up the house phone, and dialed the number Edda Kwan had scrawled on the back of a Christmas card eleven years ago, hoping retirement hadn't taken her phone along with the front desk.

"Edda," they said, when the line clicked open. "It's Coral Halloran, from the Verity. I need to ask you about a young lawyer named Victor Ashe — and I need you to tell me fast, because I think somebody in this building already knows I'm asking."
