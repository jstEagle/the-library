## 3. What the Keeper's Daughter Kept

The Greyhollow Public Library occupied a granite building that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated both books and people. It sat at the top of Harrow Street, three stories of salt-stained stone with windows like slitted eyes, and when I pushed through its heavy door the smell hit me first — old paper, damp wool, and underneath it all, the sea, which in Greyhollow seemed to get into everything the way smoke gets into your clothes after a night by a fire.

"Closed for lunch," said a voice from somewhere behind the stacks.

"It's eleven forty," I said.

A pause. Then: "We take it early here."

I found her at the circulation desk — a small woman in her seventies with reading glasses on a chain and the particular posture of someone who had spent fifty years telling people to be quiet. Her name tag read MRS. PELL, though the letters had faded to the point where it might as well have said anything. She was stamping due-date cards into a ledger with the deliberate rhythm of a woman who considered every transaction a small victory over chaos.

"I'm not here about books," I said. "I'm Lior Brightwater. I do a podcast called *The Long Dark*. I'm looking into Tamsin Vale's disappearance."

I'd expected the usual reactions — the polite blankness, the rehearsed grief, the gentle redirection I'd been getting from every shopkeeper and fisherman since I'd stepped off the ferry. Mrs. Pell gave me none of them. She set down her stamp, removed her glasses, and looked at me with the flat appraisal of someone deciding whether I was worth the trouble of lying to.

"The podcast," she said. "My niece listens to it. She says you have a voice like a man reading his own obituary."

"That's the review from my mother, actually."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"What exactly are you looking for?" she asked.

"Honestly? Anything. The official record is thin. Police report, coroner's finding of death in absentia, a newspaper piece that reads like it was written by the chamber of commerce. There's almost nothing about who Tamsin Vale actually *was*."

Mrs. Pell was quiet for a long moment. Outside, fog pressed against the windows like something trying to get in.

"There's a local history collection," she said finally. "Second floor. It's open to researchers." A beat. "Some of it is open to researchers."

* * *

The local history room smelled like the 1970s — cigarette smoke soaked into cardboard archive boxes decades ago and never quite left. I spent two hours with the town's paper trail: council minutes, ferry schedules, a scrapbook of clippings kept by some long-dead busybody named Agnes Crane whose handwriting got shakier and more indignant as the years went on. Agnes had opinions about everything. The new sea wall. The price of diesel. And, in a clipping from six weeks after the disappearance, a single furious paragraph about "the unseemly haste" with which the Vale estate had been settled.

Settled. Six weeks. For a missing person with no body.

I photographed the clipping twice because my hands weren't steady enough to trust the first attempt. This was the feeling I lived for — the thing I'd built a whole career chasing and a whole marriage losing. That click, deep in the chest, when a fact refuses to fit the story it's been given. Most podcasts die of the opposite problem: too many facts, none of them clicking, an audience drifting away episode by episode while you beg them to care about a cold case nobody cold cares about anymore. But this. This clicked.

And then there was the diary itself.

I'd brought it with me from the rental — irrational, maybe, carrying a priceless artifact around a town full of people who didn't want me here, but I couldn't leave it in that room. It felt wrong, leaving it alone. Like leaving a dog in a hot car.

I set it on the reading table under the window's grey light and opened it again, and this time I did what I should have done on day one: I stopped reading the words and started reading the hand.

Here's what nobody tells you about handwriting: it's a lie detector that works on time. People can fake their signature. They cannot fake the way their letters lean when they're tired, or how their loops tighten over years, or the thousand unconscious habits that make a hand a fingerprint of the self. I'm no forensic examiner. But I've spent four years staring at documents connected to dead people, and I know when a page feels wrong.

The early entries felt right. March 1979 through October 1984 — a girl growing up in ink. The script started round and childish, all enthusiasm and no discipline, and matured entry by entry into something quick and slanted and confident. You could watch her become a person. She wrote about the light. She wrote about her father teaching her the mechanism, the great glass lenses, the way he talked to the lamp like it was a horse he was gentling. She wrote about hating school in Greyhollow, about the mainland being a rumor, about a boy named Colm she liked and then stopped liking in the space of one devastating sentence: *"Colm kissed me behind the fuel store and I have decided we will never speak of it and also I hate him."*

She was funny. That's what got me. Every account of Tamsin Vale — the memorial plaque, the newspaper piece, the townspeople's soft-voiced recitations — described her as a sweet girl, a tragic girl, a girl-shaped absence. Nobody mentioned she was *funny*, that she could be mean in the way sixteen-year-olds are magnificently mean, that she was furious at the world in the ordinary healthy way of someone who hasn't yet learned to be afraid of it.

Then came October 1984. The last entry before the disappearance was dated the 14th. Four lines. Uncharacteristically flat:

*"Father says the weather will turn. He has asked me to stay in tonight. I said I would."*

That was it. No complaint, no joke, no fury. Just obedience, recorded in a hand that pressed so hard into the paper it nearly tore.

And then — nothing. Until 1991.

Seven years later, same diary, same book, physically impossible unless someone had carried it to wherever Tamsin wasn't. The entry began mid-thought, as if the writer had been talking the whole time and we'd only just tuned in:

*"—told me the gulls are the same here. Nothing is the same here. I planted the rosemary anyway. She would say that's sentimental. She would be right."*

Same slant. Same quick confident loops. But older — the strokes less certain, a slight tremor in the descenders, the penmanship of a person who had written this way for years but hadn't written *here* in a while. And the pronouns were doing something strange. No names. Ever. In seven years of entries — because they continued, 1991, 1993, 1996, sparse as desert rain but always arriving — not one name appeared. Not her own. Not her father's. Not the town's. Everyone was *she* or *he* or *they*, as if naming people had been trained out of her, or forbidden.

I turned pages until my fingers ached. And then I found the marginalia.

Down the outer margins of the later entries, in pencil, in a different hand entirely — smaller, neater, ruled like a clerk's — ran little marks. At first I took them for smudges. Under the window light I saw they were deliberate: tiny symbols, repeated. A circle with a line through it. Three short vertical strokes. Something like an anchor, drawn badly. They appeared beside certain entries and not others, and where they appeared, the ink entries themselves seemed to avoid specifics — the writer going vague mid-sentence, circling back, changing subjects.

Someone was editing her. Or she was editing herself. Or —

Or a third possibility, which arrived in my head fully formed and refused to leave: the pencil marks were instructions. *Say this much. No more.* And the ink obeyed.

"You've been up here three hours."

Mrs. Pell stood at the top of the stairs with a thermos and two cups, which in librarian currency is a declaration of alliance. She poured without asking and sat down across from me, and for a while we just drank tea that tasted faintly of tin and watched the fog move past the window like it had somewhere better to be.

"You've noticed," she said. Not a question.

"Noticed what?" I said, because journalists are cowards.

Her eyes went to the diary, then back to me. "That the Vale family left obligations behind."

The word landed strangely. Not debts. Not secrets. *Obligations.*

"What kind of obligations?"

Mrs. Pell wrapped both hands around her cup. When she spoke, she looked out the window rather than at me, the way people do when they're checking whether the walls have ears.

"I catalogued this collection for thirty-one years, Mr. Brightwater. I know what's in these boxes better than the boxes do. And once a year — every year, on the same week in November — a sealed envelope arrives at this library. No return address. Postmarked different places, over the years; I used to look. addressed to the trust, care of the solicitor's office, but delivered here first, always here, with instructions that it go into the Vale file." She paused. "Sealed. I was never to open them. I never did."

"But?"

"But I'm a librarian, not a saint. I held the last one up to a lamp in 2003 and I'll tell you what I saw, because I'm old and it doesn't matter anymore." She leaned forward slightly. "Paper. Folded paper. Handwritten. And along the edge, where it showed — a margin. With pencil marks on it."

The tea went cold in my hands. "Pencil marks like these?"

"Like those." She nodded at the open diary. "Every year, Mr. Brightwater. Forty years. Whatever the Vale family left behind, it isn't money and it isn't a grave. It's a correspondence. Someone out there has been writing to this town once a year since that girl drowned — or didn't — and this town has been filing her letters like minutes of a meeting nobody will admit to holding."

I sat very still. Somewhere below us, the front door opened and closed; footsteps crossed the lobby and left again. Mrs. Pell didn't glance toward the sound, but her shoulders did something small and controlled, and I understood that even in her own library, in her own domain, she had spent decades being careful.

"Why tell me?" I asked. "You've seen what this town does with questions. Half of Greyhollow won't meet my eye, and the other half gives me the same speech word for word, like they rehearsed it."

"They did rehearse it," she said simply. "We all did. Years ago. There was a meeting." She said it the way you'd report the weather, and somehow that was worse. "As for why —" She gathered the cups, stood, straightened her cardigan with a brisk tug. "Because I'm seventy-eight, Mr. Brightwater, and I have kept other people's obligations my whole life, and I find lately that I'd like to put down one or two of them before I go. Consider it overdue processing."

At the top of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail.

"One more thing. The envelopes stopped coming last year. November came and went, and nothing arrived. First time in forty years." She looked back at me, and her faded eyes were sharp as a stamp coming down on paper. "Whatever the arrangement was, Mr. Brightwater — it ended. Right around the time a package containing a certain diary found its way to a podcaster in the city. So you might ask yourself: who wanted you to have it? And what did they want you to find?"

She went downstairs. I stayed a long time in the grey light with the diary open in front of me, listening to the fog scrape against the windows, feeling the thing I hadn't felt in years — not hope exactly, nothing so gentle. More like the moment on a dark road when your headlights catch something standing at the edge of the trees, and you realize it has been watching you pass for miles.

Tamsin Vale had kept writing. Someone had kept delivering. And now the deliveries had stopped — the year I entered the story.

Which meant either the witness was done hiding.

Or done waiting.
