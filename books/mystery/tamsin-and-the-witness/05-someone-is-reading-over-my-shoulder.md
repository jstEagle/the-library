## 5. Someone Is Reading Over My Shoulder

I want to tell you that I noticed something was wrong the moment I opened my door. I want to tell you that a podcaster with four years of dead ends and one impossible diary develops instincts — that I felt the wrongness in the room like a change in air pressure.

What actually happened is this: I came back from the Greyhollow Arms with two fish-and-chip dinners in a cardboard box, kicked the door shut behind me with my heel because my hands were full, and said, out loud, to nobody, "Okay, Tamsin. Let's see what you were hiding."

Then I saw the diary on the bed.

Not where I'd left it. I'm not a tidy person, but I have a system, and my system involves leaving things exactly where I dropped them so I can find them again by process of grief. The diary had been left of the bed. Centered. Squared to the edge of the mattress with a precision I have never once achieved in my life. A bookmark of dried kelp lay across it like a ribbon at a funeral.

And on top of the kelp, a single sheet of paper, folded once.

I stood there holding cold fish and chips and did the math every true-crime listener does at some point in their listening career: *how long would it take someone to kill me, and how loud would they have to be about it?* Greyhollow's answer, judging by the fog pressing against the window, was: quietly, and forever.

I set the box down on the desk without looking away from the bed, which is harder than it sounds. My hands had opinions. They wanted to shake; I overruled them.

The note was written in fountain pen, blue-black ink, a hand so controlled it looked typeset:

*You ask the wrong questions loudly.*

That was all. No signature. No threat. Just five words arranged with the calm of someone who had all the time in the world and knew I didn't.

Underneath the note, the diary was open.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about me: I got into true crime the way most people get into most things — sideways, out of grief, wearing someone else's coat. My sister disappeared when I was nineteen. Not dramatically. No lighthouse, no fog, no town rehearsing its sorrow. She just walked out of a bus station in Leeds and became a missing person, which is to say she became a folder, which is to say she became a question I've been carrying around for fifteen years like a stone in my shoe.

So when people ask why I do this — why I chase other people's vanished daughters for an audience of eleven thousand, nine hundred of whom listen on free tiers — the honest answer is that I know exactly what it sounds like when a town says *she drowned* and means *stop asking.*

But knowing a thing and being ready for it are different animals. When I read that note, my body understood before my brain did. My brain wanted to be flattered. *Someone's reading your work! Someone cares! You've rattled a cage!* My body, meanwhile, had gone very still and very cold, the way bodies do when they remember they're made of soft parts.

Because here's the thing about the note: it wasn't angry. It wasn't scared. It was *editorial.* Whoever wrote it hadn't written *leave town* or *drop it* or even the classic *we know who you are.* They'd critiqued my technique. Which meant they'd been listening. Which meant they'd been listening closely enough to form opinions.

Which meant, somewhere in this town of four hundred rehearsed grieving faces, my audience included the killer. Or the keeper. Or whatever you call the person who stands between a question and its answer for forty years.

I sat down on the chair by the window — after checking under it, behind it, and inside the wardrobe, yes, I checked the wardrobe, I'm not proud but I am thorough — and I opened the diary to the page the kelp marked.

It was dated three days ago.

* * *

Let me back up, because if you've been with the podcast since episode one you'll remember the diary, and if you haven't, God help you, start at episode one, the production values alone are worth it.

Tamsin Vale disappeared on the ninth of November, forty years ago. Seventeen years old, lighthouse keeper's daughter, last seen walking the cliff path toward the harbour in weather the fishermen still describe the way other people describe war experiences. The official story: she slipped, the sea took her, no body ever recovered, case closed, grief accepted, please respect the family at this difficult time, forever, amen.

Three weeks ago, a package arrived at my PO box in London. No return address. Inside: Tamsin Vale's diary, covering the last six months of her life, ending — as diaries of the disappeared are supposed to end — eight days before she went into the sea.

Except it didn't end there.

Page ninety-one ends on the second of November, 1984. Page ninety-two begins on the fourth of March, 1991. Then 1997. Then 2003. Entries spaced years apart, each one a page or less, each one in handwriting that grew older the way handwriting does — steadier, smaller, more economical, as though the writer had spent decades learning to say more with less ink.

An impossible document. A dead girl keeping a journal from beyond her own disappearance. Either Tamsin Vale was alive, or someone had spent forty years forging her voice, page by page, and mailing them somewhere for safekeeping.

I'd spent three weeks in London photographing every page, running the handwriting past two graphologists (one said same hand throughout, aging naturally; the other said *bring me more samples and don't contact me again*), and cross-referencing dates against tide tables, weather records, and the town's public history. Then I'd come to Greyhollow, because eventually every podcast host has to stop talking about a place and go stand in it.

The entries themselves — the post-disappearance ones — were maddeningly oblique. Fragments. A line about "the light going out" that could mean anything. A line about "the arrangement" that sounded almost administrative. One entry, dated 2009, that consisted entirely of: *They paid again. I signed again. The sea keeps what it takes and so do we.*

I'd read that line maybe fifty times since arriving. It was the reason I'd started asking the questions I'd been asking — at the archive, at the pub, of Mrs. Penhale the retired librarian, of the solicitor Mr. Quill whose face went through four distinct emotions before settling on brick.

*You ask the wrong questions loudly.*

Well. At least I was asking them somewhere.

* * *

The new entry — the one dated three days ago, the one waiting for me on my own bed like a served summons — read:

*You found the ledger's shadow. Good. Now find what it was shadowing.*

*I watched you at the harbour today. You walk like your sister did. Like the answer might be behind you.*

*One correction, Brightwater, since you're taking notes: I didn't disappear. I was disappeared. There's a difference, and the difference has a price, and four families have been paying it since before you were born.*

*Ask about the night the light went dark. Ask why a keeper's lamp fails for exactly two hours and no one is punished. Ask who else was on the cliff path.*

*And stop eating at the Arms. Marta salts everything and talks too much.*

My first reaction, I'm ashamed to report, was professional outrage. *Walk like your sister did?* How did they know about my sister? That detail isn't in any episode. It's in one interview I gave to a small magazine two years ago, buried in paragraph nine. Someone had done homework. On *me.*

My second reaction arrived about four seconds later and was considerably less dignified. I locked the door. I put the chair under the handle. Then I stood in the middle of the room doing the breathing exercise my therapist taught me — four in, seven hold, eight out — while the fog leaned against the windows like something with weight.

Marta. The landlady at the Arms. Salty chips, saltier gossip, the woman who'd told me within an hour of check-in which founding families still "kept to themselves." If the note was right about Marta, then either the writer was watching me closely enough to track my meals, or Marta was reporting on me, or both, and none of those options improved my evening.

I thought about the handwriting. Because here's what kept snagging me, the burr under the saddle blanket: the note matched the diary. Not the young hand, not the aged hand of the later entries — something between, or beside, or underneath. The same economy. The same way of making an *r* that looked like it resented being written.

Two graphologists. One said same hand throughout. The other said bring me more samples and never contact me again.

Maybe the third sample should be a threatening note delivered to my rental room by person or persons unknown. See how they liked that.

* * *

I did what any reasonable investigator would do at nine p.m. in a fog-locked town where someone had just demonstrated total surveillance of my movements: I went out.

Look. Fear makes you stupid in predictable ways, and one of mine is that it makes me want data. Panic is just curiosity with adrenaline, and I had a lot of both. Also, the alternative was sitting in that room with the note, and the note had a presence, the way a gun on a table has a presence. Better to be moving.

Greyhollow at night is a town pretending to be asleep. Fog does that — it gives every place the intimacy of a held breath. The harbour wall was slick and black. The lighthouse stood up on its headland, automated now, sweeping its white blade through the murk every twelve seconds, indifferent as a metronome. Forty years ago it had gone dark for two hours on the night a girl walked the cliff path, and the official record called it equipment fault, and nobody had ever been disciplined, demoted, or even mildly inconvenienced, which in my experience is what accountability looks like when everyone involved owns shares in the silence.

I stood at the base of the seawall and let the fog take my outline. Somewhere behind me, a door closed — softly, the way doors close when someone wants you to hear them closing.

"You shouldn't be out."

I turned. Wren Halloway — the archivist's granddaughter, early twenties, perpetually looking at me like I was a stain on a document she otherwise respected — stood by the fish shed with her collar up and a torch she hadn't switched on.

"You followed me," I said.

"I live here. This is my street. You're the one trespassing on my evening." She came closer, boots quiet on wet stone. "There's talk about you. At the pub. Aldous Crane asked after you tonight. Asked what you'd found, who you'd spoken to, how long you were staying." She paused. "Aldous Crane doesn't ask after anyone. He's eighty-one. He asks *at* people."

"That's not ominous at all."

"No," she agreed. "It's extremely ominous. That's why I'm telling you." She glanced up at the lighthouse, its beam stroking the fog. "Did you get it yet?"

"Get what?"

"The note." She said it flatly, watching my face, and whatever she saw there made her exhale. "Right. So you did. Everyone who pokes this town gets one, eventually. My gran got hers in 1979. Said it was the politest threat she ever received."

"Wren." I stepped closer. "Your grandmother. The envelopes. The ones she mailed every year—"

"I told you what I know, which is nothing, which is the point." But her voice cracked slightly on *nothing,* and I filed that away the way I file everything: ruthlessly, and with guilt. "Go home, Brightwater. Lock your door. And tomorrow, when you go asking about the night the light went out—" she stopped herself.

"When I go asking about what?"

She was already backing into the fog. "Ask old Tam Renshaw. He was fishing off the point that night. He's the only one who'll talk, and he'll only talk drunk, and he's only drunk on Thursdays."

"It's Wednesday," I said.

"I know," said Wren Halloway, and the fog closed over her like water.

* * *

Back in the room, I moved the chair from under the door handle — then put it back, then took it down again, then compromised by wedging it at an angle that would neither hold the door nor look completely insane to a future coroner.

I laid the note beside the open diary and photographed both from six angles, because evidence is comfort and documentation is a love language. Then I sat with the whole impossible spread of it: a dead girl's living handwriting, a stranger's critique of my investigative style, a warning about salted chips, and the growing certainty that I was not investigating this town so much as being permitted to walk through it while something enormous watched from the treeline.

*You ask the wrong questions loudly.*

Fine. Then I'd ask the right ones. Quietly.

The night the light went dark. Two hours, unexplained, unpunished. A keeper's lamp doesn't fail politely on schedule — and Edmund Vale, by every account I'd gathered, was religious about that lamp. Neighbours described him checking the mechanism the way other men check on sleeping children. A man like that doesn't lose two hours of light to a fault he doesn't report, doesn't rage about, doesn't spend the rest of his life explaining.

Unless the fault wasn't a fault. Unless the darkness was the point.

I wrote it in my notebook, in my own hand, small and mean: *Who benefits from a dark lighthouse? Who needs the coast blind for two hours on a storm night?*

And beneath it, because I couldn't help myself, because wry is my resting state and dread is just wry with the lights off: *Note to self: stop narrating your theories out loud in pubs, you absolute amateur.*

The fog pressed at the glass. Twelve seconds between sweeps of the light. Eleven. Ten.

Tomorrow was Thursday. Old Tam Renshaw fished off the point, drank at the Shipwright, and was, according to Wren, the only mouth in Greyhollow that had ever opened on its own.

I turned off the lamp and lay down on top of the covers, dressed, with the diary on the nightstand and my phone in my fist and the recorder running, because if someone was going to come through that door, the least they deserved was to become content.

Just before sleep took me — that shallow, listening sleep of the recently threatened — I heard it. Faint, from the direction of the harbour. Footsteps on wet stone, unhurried, passing below my window and fading toward the headland.

Walking, I realized, the way you'd walk if you had all the time in the world.

Walking, I thought, the way you'd walk if the tide was on your side.

Thursday couldn't come fast enough.
