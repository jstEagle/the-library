## 10. They Bury Their Own

I want to tell you that I sensed it before I opened the door — that some animal part of me smelled the violation through the wood, the way dogs know an earthquake is coming. But the truth is less flattering and more instructive: I noticed nothing at all until my key turned and I stepped into a room that had been turned inside out like a pocket by a pickpocket in a hurry.

The mattress stood on end against the wall. My clothes had been unfolded — not searched so much as *considered* — and left in soft grey drifts across the floorboards. The lining of my suitcase gaped where someone had slit it with more patience than skill. My laptop was there, untouched, which told me something important right away: this wasn't burglary. A burglar takes the laptop. This was archaeology. Someone had been looking for paper.

And they'd found it.

The diary was gone.

I stood in the doorway for a long moment, doing the arithmetic of violation. The window latch was still locked from the inside — no, wait. It wasn't. It hung open an inch, and below it, the flowerbed under the sill bore two neat heel-prints pressed into the rain-softened soil, pointing away from the house toward the lane. Whoever it was hadn't even bothered to be clever about it. They hadn't needed to be. In Greyhollow, cleverness is a luxury reserved for people who expect consequences.

Here's what nobody tells you about being robbed: it isn't the loss. It's the recalibration. Every object you own gets re-sorted into *mine* and *theirs*, and you discover how little was ever really yours. My notebooks were gone too — three months of interviews, timestamps, the fisherman's grudging account of the dark lighthouse, the solicitor's stonewall rendered verbatim in my terrible shorthand. All of it, lifted. Someone had done their homework on me. They knew exactly which pieces of the past I'd been carrying around, and they took only those.

They left the coffee. Small mercies.

I did what any professional does when a story bites back: I made tea, sat down amid the wreckage, and got angry in an organized fashion. Anger, I've found, is just grief wearing work boots, and I had plenty of both to get through. Because here's the thing — the diary wasn't mine. I'd known that from the moment I opened the package. It belonged to Tamsin Vale, or to whatever ghost had been writing in her name for forty years, and now it belonged to whoever had pried up my floorboards looking for God knows what else.

But it also belonged to the truth, and I had become, without quite meaning to, its custodian. Somebody in this town had decided that was a job opening they intended to fill themselves.

I was cataloguing the damage for the second time — because the first time I'd been too furious to be accurate — when I heard the scrape of a foot on the gravel path outside. Not retreating. Approaching. Bold as brass, in broad afternoon light, while the fog sat on the harbour like a lid on a pot.

I opened the door on a girl of maybe nineteen, standing very straight with her chin up in the way people do when they've rehearsed courage in front of a mirror and are hoping the performance holds. She wore a fisherman's jumper two sizes too big and an expression that was trying very hard to be apologetic and mostly achieving *defiant*.

"You're the podcaster," she said.

"Lior."

"Wren Halloway." She didn't offer a hand. Her eyes went past my shoulder into the ransacked room, and something in her face flickered — not surprise. Confirmation. "So it's started, then."

"It started forty years ago," I said. "This is just the bit where it notices me."

That earned me half a smile, quickly suppressed, like she'd caught herself enjoying the wrong thing. "Can I come in? There's a bench out here and Mrs. Tregowan waters her geraniums at four and she sees everything."

"Mrs. Tregowan sounds like my kind of source."

"She's everybody's kind of source. That's the problem with this town." She came inside, picked her way through my scattered shirts with the delicacy of someone walking through a church, and sat on the one chair I owned that hadn't been dismantled. Then she looked at her hands for a while, and I let her, because I've learned that the silence before a confession is load-bearing. Lean on it wrong and the whole thing collapses.

"I know who took your book," she said finally.

* * *

Here's what Wren Halloway told me, sitting in my wrecked rental with fog pressing against the windows like something trying to get in, and I'm going to give it to you the way she gave it to me — in pieces, with pauses, because that's how a person dismantles a wall they were born inside of.

Her grandmother, Edith Halloway, had been the postmistress of Greyhollow for thirty-one years. Retired now, sharp as a tack, hands going papery but mind intact. And every year — every single year, on the same week in October, the anniversary week — a sealed envelope would arrive at the post office addressed to the Halloway Trust, care of Crane & Sons, Solicitors. No return address. Postmarked from towns along the coast, different ones each time: Penzance one year, Falmouth the next, once, memorably, from a place called Mousehole that Wren's grandmother had laughed at for a full minute, the only time Wren ever heard her laugh about any of it.

Inside each envelope: a single page. Handwritten. Old-fashioned hand, looped and careful.

Edith never opened them. She said she didn't want to know, and Wren believed her, mostly. But Edith kept them safe, carried them up the hill to the solicitors' office herself, watched Aldous Crane sign a receipt for each one, and then came home and poured herself a whisky and didn't speak for the rest of the evening. Once a year. For thirty-one years.

"And she was paid for it," Wren said. "That's the part that—" She stopped. Started again. "Every Christmas, an envelope comes to our house. Cash. Not a cheque, nothing traceable. Just notes in an envelope with no note inside, if you see what I mean. Gran calls it 'the pension.' She won't call it anything else. When I asked her once — I was sixteen, I thought I was being brave — she looked at me like I'd asked her to spit on her own grave, and she said, *'It's not a bribe if you never ask what it's for.'*"

"And did you ask what it was for?"

"Forty times." Wren's jaw tightened. "She never answered. But last month she got sick — nothing fatal, don't worry, the women in my family are unkillable — and she was on the good painkillers, and she talked. Not a lot. Just enough." Wren looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious in equal measure, which is a combination I recognized, because I'd seen it in my own bathroom mirror often enough. "She said the envelopes were pages from a diary. Tamsin Vale's diary. And that the first year, when the first envelope came, Aldous Crane read the page and went white as the foam on the harbour, and then he set up the trust disbursement the same week. The 'maintenance' line. Your maintenance line."

My skin did something cold and complicated. "Your grandmother has been the delivery mechanism. Forty years of diary pages, posted home by—"

"By whoever writes them." Wren said it flatly, but her hands weren't flat; they were fists in her lap. "Alive. Whoever writes them is alive. Everyone in this town says Tamsin Vale drowned, and everyone in this town has been paid, one way or another, to keep saying it. My gran took the money because she was poor and it was there and asking questions in Greyhollow gets you answers you don't want. I get it. I even forgive it. But I'm sick of it." She stood up, and the oversized jumper made the gesture look almost comically small, a child declaiming in adult's clothing, except nothing about her voice was small. "I'm sick of walking down the high street knowing every shopkeeper who smiles at me has an envelope somewhere with their name on it. I'm sick of my gran flinching every October. And I'm sick—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, I could see her hating it, "—I'm sick of a woman maybe being alive out there somewhere while this whole town agrees, out loud, every day, that she's dead. That's not grief. That's a script. We're all just reciting our lines."

I gave her a moment. Then I asked the question I already dreaded. "Who took the diary, Wren?"

She sat back down. "I don't know for certain. But the night before last, I saw Mr. Pentreath's truck parked down your lane at eleven o'clock. Gideon Pentreath. He does odd jobs for the Cranes — always has, his father did before him. And this morning he bought a padlock and a new hasp for his shed, which he's never locked in his life, because according to my dad, Gideon can't keep a secret longer than it takes him to tell it." She met my eyes. "He'll have put it somewhere obvious. Men like Gideon always do. They hide things the way children do — somewhere they can check on them."

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "You live here. I'm leaving eventually. Whatever happens after I go, you'll still be standing in Greyhollow when the fog closes back over."

For the first time since she'd walked in, she looked uncertain — and younger, suddenly, nineteen again instead of however old righteous anger had been making her. "Because my gran's dying," she said quietly. "Not this month, maybe not this year. But dying. And when she goes, the pension stops, and the town will find some other poor family to pay, and it'll go on and on until everyone who remembers why is dead, and then it'll just be a thing the town does, like the regatta. Like lighting the lamp at Christmas. I don't want to inherit it. I don't want my kids to inherit it." She swallowed. "You're the first person in my whole life who came here to break the thing instead of live inside it. So. I'm choosing sides."

There are moments in this work — rare, and I've learned not to advertise them — when the loneliness lifts. Fourteen years of dead ends and closed doors and polite men telling me to respect the families' privacy, and here was a teenager in a borrowed jumper handing me a crowbar and asking, in effect, *where do we start?*

"We start with Gideon's shed," I said. "And we start tonight, because whoever sent him will be checking whether the job was done clean, and the longer that diary sits in a padlocked shed, the better the odds it ends up in a stove."

"There's a problem with tonight," Wren said, and nodded at the window, where the fog had thickened from soup to cement. "Storm coming in off the Atlantic. Ferry's already cancelled — notice went up at the harbour office at noon. By dark, the phones will be down too. They always are. Old lines, salt air." She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile; it was the smile of someone who has finally understood the machinery well enough to describe it. "That's how they do it, you know. Bad weather. Town closes ranks best in a storm. Nobody in, nobody out, no signal, no witnesses passing through. Whatever happens in Greyhollow during a storm stays in Greyhollow."

"So we're sealed in with them."

"With them," she agreed, "and with Gideon's shed, and with whatever else they're planning to bury before the sky clears." She pulled a torch out of her coat pocket — she'd come prepared, this girl, prepared and furious — and set it on the table between us like a signature on a contract. "Low tide's at eleven. The lane behind the fish quay floods otherwise. You in?"

Outside, the first gust hit the cottage hard enough to rattle the loose pane, and somewhere down the hill a shutter banged once, twice, like the town clearing its throat.

I picked up the torch.

"I'm in," I said. "Let's go rob the man who robbed me."

Wren grinned — properly this time, no suppression — and for one bright, stupid moment, standing in the ruins of my rented room with a storm rolling in and a stolen ghost for a co-conspirator, I felt something I hadn't felt since the package arrived: not fear, not obsession, but hope. Foolish, tide-dependent, felony-adjacent hope.

The fog took the rest of the afternoon. At eleven, we went to get the diary back.
