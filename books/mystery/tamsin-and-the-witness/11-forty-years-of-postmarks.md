## 11. Forty Years of Postmarks

My grandmother kept her secrets the way she kept her buttons — in a tin that rattled when you shook it, sorted by nothing you could ever guess at.

I'd known about the tin since I was six. It lived on the top shelf of her wardrobe, behind a box of Christmas cards so old they still had tuppence stamps on them, and I was told once, in the tone Grandmother reserved for matters of state, that it was not a toy and not my business and not to be touched unless she was dead or dying. She said it the way other people said *I love you*, which in Greyhollow was close enough to the same thing.

She died on a Tuesday in March, four months before Lior Brightwater came to town with their recorder and their questions and their terrible habit of looking at people like they were already transcripts. By then I had opened the tin. I wish I could tell you I waited until after the funeral out of respect. I didn't. I opened it the night she died, sitting on the floor of her bedroom with the wardrobe door open, because grief makes burglars of us all and I wanted — God help me — I wanted one last secret from her. Something with her handwriting on it. Something that proved she'd been more than the small, stooped woman who smelled of lavender and salt and never once told me what she thought of anything unless I asked twice.

The tin held envelopes. Forty-one of them, give or take, bundled in string gone soft as wool. Every envelope sealed. Every envelope addressed in the same careful hand to *The Trustees, Greyhollow Founding Families Trust, c/o Crane & Mowbray Solicitors*. And every envelope franked — stamped, postmarked, processed through Her Majesty's mail like any birthday card or gas bill — with dates running back further than I'd been alive.

The earliest postmark read October 1984.

Tamsin Vale disappeared in June 1984.

I sat on my grandmother's floor for a long time doing arithmetic that didn't want to be done. Then I put the tin back on the shelf, behind the Christmas cards, and I did not touch it again until the night someone ransacked Brightwater's rental and took the diary, and Wren Halloway — that's me, keep up — decided that forty years of my family licking stamps for the founding families was quite enough.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about my grandmother, Edith Halloway, before you judge her: she was the postmistress of Greyhollow for thirty-eight years, and in a town like Greyhollow, the postmistress is not a civil servant. She's a confessor. Everything comes through her hands — the tax letters, the love letters, the hospital results, the cheques from sons who moved to Australia and couldn't think of anything else to send. She knew who owed money to whom, whose marriage was a performance, which of the founding families drank more than the church approved of. And she told nobody anything, ever, because telling things was not the job. Keeping them was.

I used to think that was integrity. Standing behind the little barred window all those years, saying *good morning, Mrs. Crane* to women she knew were liars, weighing parcels for men she knew were worse than liars, and never once letting her face do anything but the weather report.

Then I found the tin, and I understood that it wasn't integrity at all. It was employment.

* * *

Lior came to my door two days after the break-in, looking like a person who'd been sleeping in their coat. Which, I learned later, they had. The whole town had turned on them by then — after the fire at the Crane house, after Aldous died, after the word *arson* started travelling up and down Fore Street faster than the fire brigade ever managed to travel anywhere. People crossed the street to avoid them. Old Mrs. Pentreath, who'd once fed gulls with the tenderness of a saint, spat near Lior's shoes outside the chippy, and Mrs. Pentreath hadn't spat since the Falklands.

So when I opened my door and said, "You'd better come in before someone sees you," Lior looked at me the way you'd look at a vending machine that started dispensing sympathy.

"You're Wren Halloway," they said. "Your grandmother—"

"Was the postmistress. Yes." I stepped aside. "And before you ask, no, I don't know if the diary is in my house, because someone went through this place last Thursday while I was at work, and whoever it was knew exactly where she kept things. They just didn't find the tin."

"The tin?"

"Kettle's on. Sit down. This is going to take longer than your attention span, podcaster, and I've seen your episodes."

That was unkind. His episodes were fine. But I was nervous, and nervousness in Greyhollow comes out sideways, as insult. It's practically our dialect.

* * *

I set the tin on the kitchen table between us, and I watched Lior Brightwater — a person who had made a career, a failing career, out of other people's secrets — go absolutely still.

"Forty-one envelopes," I said. "Sealed. Addressed to the trust. Postmarked every year, October, like clockwork. 1984 through—" I counted backward on my fingers, hating myself a little for how easily the arithmetic came now, "—2025. Last year. Eight months before she died."

Lior didn't touch them. I'll say that much for their manners. They leaned over them instead, reading postmarks like scripture, and I saw the exact moment the shape of it landed on them, because their face did something complicated — the look of a person watching a machine they'd imagined turn out to be real, and larger, and older than they'd hoped.

"She mailed these," Lior said slowly. "Your grandmother. To the trust. Annually."

"For forty years."

"But they're sealed. You don't know what's in them."

"No," I said. "But I know what my grandmother did at the counter every October, because she trained me on the counter from the time I was twelve. There was always one envelope, first week of October, that she handled different from the rest. She wouldn't let me touch it. She'd weigh it herself, stamp it herself, and here's the part I only understood later — she never put it in the outgoing sack with everything else. She drove it to Crane & Mowbray herself. Twenty miles to Penzance, in the car, once a year, rain or shine. I thought it was legal papers. I thought everyone's gran delivered legal papers to solicitors in Penzance. Doesn't everyone?"

Lior made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed lately.

"And the weight," they said. "You weighed post for a living, effectively. What did the envelope weigh?"

"One sheet," I said. "Maybe two. Paper weight. I know paper weight the way you know audio levels. It was a page, Brightwater. One page, every year, for forty years."

The kitchen got very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed its usual editorial, and the fog pressed against the window the way it does here, like something leaning in to listen.

"A fresh diary page," Lior said. "Every year. Delivered to the trust."

"That's what it looks like."

"Which means—" They stopped. Started again, slower, the way you approach a dog you think might bite. "Which means whoever wrote those pages has been writing them continuously. Since 1984. The diary isn't an artifact. It's a correspondence."

"It's a report," I said. "That's the word that came to me at three in the morning, and I haven't slept properly since. She wasn't hiding, whoever she is. Tamsin Vale, or whoever's been holding that pen. She's been *filing*. Annual returns. Like a company. Like a — God, like a pensioner submitting proof of life."

Lior sat down hard. I poured tea neither of us drank.

* * *

Here is the thing I have not yet confessed, and may as well confess now, to you, whoever you are listening to this in whatever future Lior built out of all our ruin: I opened one of the envelopes.

Not the oldest. Not the newest. Number thirty-something, 2011, the year I left for university and my grandmother gave me a card that said *Mind how you go* and nothing else, which in retrospect was possibly the most emotional sentence she ever produced. I opened the 2011 envelope in February of this year, four days after finding the tin, standing in her dead kitchen with a butter knife, hands shaking, feeling like both a burglar and a priest.

Inside was a single page. Cream paper, slightly foxed with age even then. Handwriting that slanted right and pressed hard enough to emboss the next line through.

I'm not going to reproduce it here. Some of it belongs in Lior's episode, and some of it belongs to no one. But I will tell you the parts that broke me.

It began: *Another year. The doctor says the sea air is good for the lungs, which is the sea's idea of a joke.*

It mentioned the cottage — not by address, but by habit. *The gorse has come in thick along the north wall. I let it. Let them try to walk to me through that.*

And near the bottom, in smaller writing, as if the pen had grown tired of pretending: *Tell Aldous the arrangement stands. Tell him also that I remember June better every year, not less, and that memory is the interest on what they pay. One day the principal comes due.*

Signed with a letter. Just one. A looping, confident **T**.

I reseal it with tape, because I am not a monster and also because I panicked. But I want you to understand what I understood standing there with the tape gun in my hand: my grandmother had spent her entire working life as the postal vein between a living woman and the men who paid for her silence, and she had never once, not across a childhood of asking, given me so much as a hint. Not a look. Not a hesitation over the marmalade. Forty years of complicity worn so lightly it looked like virtue.

I loved her. I want that on the record too. And I have spent every night since trying to make those two facts fit in the same tin.

* * *

"They knew," Lior said eventually. "Crane, Mowbray, the families. They weren't paying a lump sum and hoping. They were receiving *proof*. Annual confirmation that the witness was alive, silent, and — Christ — *grateful enough to write it down.*"

"Not grateful. Contractual." I pushed the tin an inch toward them. "Think about it from their side. You're paying a fortune, generation after generation, into 'maintenance' — I've seen the trust ledger, don't look surprised, my gran kept copies of everything, keeping things was the whole job. You're three patriarchs deep into a bribe your grandfathers started. How do you know the money's working? How do you know the woman's still alive, still quiet, still on the headland and not in a newspaper office? You make her report. In her own hand. On her own paper. Once a year, like a licence fee."

"Handwriting verification," Lior murmured. "Continuity of custody. They turned a witness into a subscription service."

"And the pages went into the archive," I said. "Into the Vale collection, at the library, where anyone doing research could eventually find them — except nobody looks at the recent accessions in a forty-year-old case, do they? Except you. The trust wasn't hiding the diary, Brightwater. They were *filing* it. Somewhere respectable. Somewhere that made the pages look like history instead of evidence."

Lior stared at the tin. When they spoke again, their voice had gone flat in the way I'd heard on their podcast when they'd stopped being excited and started being frightened — the register shift, right before an episode's final act.

"Wren. Your grandmother drove that envelope to Penzance herself. Every year. For decades. Do you understand what that means about the night she died? About whether her death was—"

"Don't." I stood up. My chair went over backward and I let it lie there, because picking it up would have meant admitting my hands weren't steady. "Don't say it. She was eighty-six, she had a bad heart, the doctor said—"

"The doctor said," Lior repeated gently. "Wren. Who drives twenty miles each way to deliver an envelope that could be posted? Why hand-deliver? Because handing it over means *seeing* who takes it. Means confirming the chain. Your grandmother wasn't the postmistress of this arrangement. She was its auditor. And auditors who die eight months before an outsider starts pulling threads—"

"I said *don't*."

The gull outside screamed again. Somewhere down the lane, a door slammed — Greyhollow's native percussion, everyone announcing that they were home and watching.

I made myself sit back down. I made myself look at the tin, at the string, at the soft grey envelopes with their neat annual postmarks, and I made myself say the thing I'd been circling for three sleepless weeks, because Lior had come all this way and the truth deserved a straight road for once.

"When I was fourteen," I said, "she took me to the lighthouse. First and only time. We climbed to the lamp room and she pointed out at the water — at the rocks below the north cliff, where the cave mouth is at low tide — and she said, *'That's where the town keeps its wages, Wren.'* I thought she meant fishing. I thought it was one of her sayings. She had hundreds of sayings and I loved every one of them and I never once asked what they meant, because asking twice was the rule and I was a coward with a boat timetable."

Lior said nothing. Good. They're learning.

"She wasn't speaking in riddles," I said. "She was confessing. The only confession she ever made, and she made it to a fourteen-year-old who thought she was talking about fish. That's the machinery, Brightwater. That's the horror of it — it doesn't need evil men twirling moustaches. It needs a postmistress who keeps her mouth shut, a solicitor who weighs an envelope, three families who pay their dues, and a girl on a headland who writes one page a year to prove she's still bought. It runs on ordinary people doing their jobs. It ran on my grandmother doing hers *perfectly*."

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, furious at the wetness, and pulled the 2011 envelope from under the others and laid it on the table between us.

"There's one more thing," I said. "The last envelope. 2025. It's thinner than the rest — I weighed it against the others, don't look at me like that, weighing is grieving in this family. Half a page at most. And it isn't sealed."

Lior went very still.

"My grandmother died in March," I said. "The envelope's dated October. It was already in the tin when I found it — sealed, stamped, ready. Which means either she wrote ahead for a delivery she expected to make..." I turned it over. Through the paper, faint as breath, the embossing of a single looping letter showed where a pen had pressed hard. "**T**," I said. "Brightwater — the witness wrote to the trust *after* my grandmother died. Someone else carried the mail. Someone else knows the route, the solicitors, the arrangement. And whoever it is has been watching us since the day we started asking."

I slid the unsealed envelope across the table.

"So open it," I said. "Because I can't."
