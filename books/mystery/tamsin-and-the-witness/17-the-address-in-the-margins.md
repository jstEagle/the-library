## 17. The Address in the Margins

The pages came out of the ashes like survivors of a shipwreck — singed at the edges, curled into themselves, smelling of smoke and salt and forty years of secrets that apparently could not be killed by something as simple as fire.

I laid them out on Wren Halloway's kitchen table the morning after the Crane house burned, and I will admit that my hands were not steady. Not because of grief. Aldous Crane had offered me money, then threats, then a version of the truth doled out like communion wafers, and now he was dead and I couldn't summon a single honest tear. No — my hands shook because of what was *on* the pages.

Marginalia. Dozens of entries' worth of it, written in the margins of Tamsin Vale's diary in a hand so small it looked like it was trying to hide from itself. Most of it had been legible before the fire. Now whole lines had been eaten, leaving fragments that read like a ransom note assembled by a drunk:

*...the light keeps...*
*...never on a Sunday...*
*...if you are reading this, then someone finally...*

And one line, half-consumed, that had kept me awake since two in the morning:

*...the house below the old signal station, when the water goes...*

"The water goes," Wren said, standing over the table with her arms crossed and her grandmother's disapproval inherited wholesale. "That's tide talk. That's how everyone here says it. *When the water goes.* My gran used to say we'd walk to the far headland *when the water goes*, like it was a country you could only visit part-time."

"Where's the old signal station?"

Wren didn't answer right away. She picked up one of the scorched pages, held it to the window light, and turned it slightly, the way my mother used to turn earrings to catch the sun. Underneath us, Greyhollow was doing what Greyhollow did best: closing ranks. Since the fire, three people had stopped speaking to Wren in the street. The chippy had refused to serve me. Someone had keyed a word into the side of my rental car that I won't repeat in print, mostly because they'd spelled it wrong.

"North of the lighthouse," she said finally. "Past the shingle spit. There's nothing there now — just foundations and gorse. The coastguard abandoned it in the sixties." She set the page down. "Except."

"Except."

"Except my gran always said there *was* something there. A light that showed sometimes at night, way out past the point, low down near the water. Everyone said it was reflections. Everyone said a lot of things in this town." She looked at me. "You think she's alive."

I thought about the diary entries dated decades after a drowning. I thought about envelopes mailed annually to a trust, each containing a fresh page in a dead girl's handwriting. I thought about Aldous Crane dying in a house fire that had started, according to the fire brigade's preliminary finding, "in suspicious circumstances," and about the surviving patriarchs who had already given statements to the local paper implying that an interfering podcaster had brought violence to their peaceful town.

"I think," I said, "that if she's not alive, then someone has spent forty years committing a very elaborate literary fraud for no profit whatsoever. And if she is alive—" I touched the burned fragment, the words *when the water goes* — "then someone tried to burn this diary yesterday, which means someone thinks she can still hurt them. Dead women don't scare men like the Cranes. Living ones do."

* * *

We spent the day decoding, and I want to be honest about how unglamorous that was. Podcasts have taught people to imagine investigation as a montage: red string, dramatic zooms, a sudden gasp of revelation scored to strings. The reality was Wren and me hunched over a kitchen table littered with toast crumbs, holding charred paper up to a raking lamp and arguing about whether a smudge was an *h* or a *b*.

But the marginalia rewarded patience, because whoever wrote it — and I no longer doubted it was Tamsin herself — had been writing to a future reader. To me, maybe, though that felt too vain to say aloud. More likely to anyone. To the record itself. She had known the diary would survive her; she had simply never expected it to survive *this* fast.

By mid-afternoon we had assembled enough fragments to reconstruct the key passage. Wren wrote it out in pencil on the back of a takeaway menu, filling gaps with brackets the way archaeologists fill gaps with plaster:

*[The] house [below] the old signal [station]. You reach it [when] the water goes — [two hours] either side of low [tide], no more, and [not] in a swell. The path is [under] the cliff path, [where] the iron ring is set [in] the rock. I have lived [there] since [the winter] of [19]84. Nobody looks [at] a place that isn't [there].*

Nobody looks at a place that isn't there.

I read it four times. On the fourth reading, my eyes snagged on the date and refused to move. 1984. Six years after the disappearance. Which meant that for six years after Edmund Vale's daughter walked into the sea — or didn't — she had been somewhere else. Somewhere the payments hadn't reached yet, or hadn't been arranged yet, or —

Or she'd run first, and let them find her later. Let them buy her back. That fit the ledger, the torn page, everything Aldous had almost told me over his beautiful dinner. *Something happened that night the families must never allow published.* He'd said it like a man reciting a company charter. He'd never once said *Tamsin is dead*. I'd noticed that at the time and filed it under grief-evasion. It went in a different drawer now.

"Wren." My voice came out strange. "What time is low tide tomorrow?"

She checked her phone, and I watched her face do the thing faces do when coincidence stops being funny. "Quarter to seven in the morning. And Lior — there's a storm system coming up from the southwest. The harbourmaster's already talking about closing the ferry route again." She hesitated. "Swell's supposed to build through the day."

Of course it was. This town had a genius for weather. It closed its roads and cut its phone lines precisely when it needed darkness, and I no longer believed in its coincidences any more than I believed in its grief.

"Then we go at first light," I said. "Before the swell. Before anyone notices we've gone."

"They'll notice. They notice everything now." Wren was quiet a moment, turning the takeaway menu over as if the answer might be printed on the grease stain. Then: "My gran got an envelope every year for thirty-one years. Sealed. She never opened them — she just took them to the post office. But she told me once, near the end, when the dementia was starting to chew through her discretion, that the woman who sent them *smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke*. That's all she ever said. Beeswax and woodsmoke." Wren looked up. "You don't get that smell from a grave."

* * *

I slept badly, which is to say I lay in the rental room staring at the ceiling while the wind tested the window frames like a burglar checking the locks. Somewhere around three I gave up and read the reconstructed passage again, and then the other fragments, and then — because sleep had clearly resigned from the project — I listened back to my own recordings from the first week in Greyhollow.

It's a habit of mine, reviewing early tape. You hear things in your own voice you missed at the time: the questions you should have asked, the pauses where interviewees decided how much truth they could afford. I played the clip of Mrs. Penhaligon at the bakery, the first person who'd told me Tamsin drowned, with that rehearsed sorrow: *"Sweet girl. The sea took her. Terrible night, the light out, no one could see anything."*

No one could see anything.

I'd taken it as atmosphere. Now I heard it as testimony. The light was dark for two hours that night — officially an equipment fault, unofficially the hinge on which everything swung. A smuggler's boat needed the light dark. Edmund Vale was the keeper. And his daughter had seen what happened in that darkness, and had run, and had spent six years outside the machinery before letting the machinery buy her in.

And then — the thought arrived with the particular cold clarity of four a.m. — she had spent the next thirty-four years filing reports. Annual pages, sealed, delivered home. Not silence. *Correspondence.* The witness wasn't hiding from the town. She was auditing it.

I got up and packed a bag: recorder, spare batteries, the ledger photographs, a torch, and the half-burned pages wrapped in a plastic sleeve against the spray. If the tide was going to race me, I intended to arrive with everything that mattered already in my pocket.

* * *

We left at ten past five, in the blue-black hour when Greyhollow looks least like a stage set and most like what it actually is: a place built on a rock by people who wanted to be difficult to visit. Wren drove us north along the coast road with the headlights off until we were clear of the last houses, which she assured me was normal and which felt, in every nerve I owned, like fleeing a crime scene.

The shingle spit crunched under our boots like breaking crockery. Beyond it, the land rose into gorse and old stone — the signal station's foundations, exactly as promised: roofless walls ankle-high, a stub of chimney, the sea working patiently at everything. And below it, where the cliff path bent inland around a buttress of rock, Wren found the iron ring.

It was set flush into the stone, rusted to the colour of dried blood, and I'd have walked past it a dozen times without seeing it. From the ring, a ledge descended — not a path, exactly, more a suggestion the rock made grudgingly, angling down toward a shelf that ran along the base of the cliff into the murk.

"That's not a walk," Wren said. "That's a dare."

"It's a commute," I said. "Somebody's been doing it for forty years."

"Somebody with better knees than yours."

She wasn't wrong, and I told her so, and we stood there a moment in the grey pre-dawn listening to the sea. The forecast swell hadn't arrived yet; the water moved in long, tired rollers, the kind that look manageable and lie. Below us, at the base of the cliff, I could see the ledge running north into shadow, and beyond the shadow — I had to squint, and then stop breathing, and then squint again — a shape. Low. Square. Roofed in something dark. Half-swallowed by the cliff's overhang so that from the coast road above, from anywhere above, it would read as geology. As nothing.

A place that isn't there.

"There's a light," Wren whispered. "Look. In the window."

There was. Faint, warm, the colour of candle rather than bulb, appearing and disappearing behind what might have been a curtain or might have been my own pulse. Forty years of gran's stories — *a light that shows sometimes at night, way out past the point* — resolved themselves into a single small flame in a single small window, and I felt something in my chest crack along a seam I hadn't known was there.

Because here is the thing I hadn't let myself feel until that moment: if someone was home, then the diary was true, and the drowning was false, and the entire architecture of Greyhollow's grief — the memorial bench, the anniversary flowers, the rehearsed sorrow of forty years — was theatre performed over a living woman's head. Every year they'd mourned her to her face, or within earshot of it. Every year she'd watched them do it.

"Tide's going out," Wren said, checking her phone. "Low water quarter to seven. We've got maybe ninety minutes of safe ledge, less if the swell comes early." She looked at me. "Lior. If she's in there, and she's who we think she is — what do we even say? *Sorry to bother you, sorry about your funeral?*"

"We say we read the diary," I said. "All of it. Even the parts addressed to nobody. Especially those." I clipped the bag across my chest and tested the first step of the ledge with my heel. Rock held. "And then we ask her if she'll tell the truth on the record. Once. Before the tide, before the storm, before whatever the Cranes have left in their arsenal decides we've heard enough."

"And if she says no?"

I looked at the little lit window, and at the sea that had spent forty years being her moat and her gate, depending on the hour.

"Then we knock anyway," I said, "and we hope she's kinder than her neighbours."

The ledge was wet and narrow and tilted outward over water the colour of gunmetal, and every step of it I made while thinking about beeswax and woodsmoke, and about a girl who had run from a lighthouse in the dark and never stopped running, only gotten very good at standing still. Behind us the sky was beginning to bruise with the storm that was coming to seal the town shut. Ahead of us, the flame in the window steadied — and then, unmistakably, deliberately, went out.

Someone had seen us coming.

Someone had been watching the tide, too.
