## 1. The Will That Shouldn't Exist

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which Ivo Kavanagh would later consider the last ordinary Tuesday of his life. It came the way all his work came — by post, in a buff envelope, with the firm's franking stamp slightly crooked the way it always was, as though the machine itself had grown tired of the job.

He was eating toast at his kitchen table in Clapham, reading a letter from a client whose late husband had left the family cottage to a woman neither of them had ever heard of, when he slit the envelope open with a butter knife and a folded will slid out onto the table like something trying to escape.

He almost didn't notice the date. That was the thing he would remember afterward — that he almost didn't notice. He'd read four hundred wills in his career and the dates had long since stopped registering, the way a lighthouse keeper stops seeing the light. He read the opening clauses, the standard recitals, the appointment of executor, and it was only when his eyes snagged on a phrase — *Edith Marlowe, of Ashcombe, deceased, late of the same* — that something ticked over in the back of his mind, some small clerk of a thought putting up its hand.

*Marlowe. Ashcombe. Why do those go together?*

He put down the toast. He got the file.

The firm's records were kept in the basement, in a room that smelled of cold paper and other people's unfinished business, and it took him eleven minutes to find the Marlowe file, which was thick and grey and had not been touched, judging by the dust, since the last century. He carried it upstairs and read it standing at the kitchen counter, and the toast went cold, and the tea went cold, and outside a delivery van reversed beepingly into the morning, entirely unaware that anything in the world had changed.

Edith Marlowe had vanished from the seaside town of Ashcombe on the fourth of October, 1994. Ivo knew this because the file told him so, in the flat language of missing-person reports and coroner's correspondence. She had been declared dead in absentia seven years later — the presumption of death, the standard seven years, the whole machinery of the law grinding out its patient conclusion that a woman who had not been seen in seven years was, for all legal purposes, no longer among the living. A grant of probate had been issued on her estate in 2001. Her daughter, Nell Marlowe, had inherited. The file had been closed with a full stop.

And yet here, on his kitchen table, sat a will signed by Edith Marlowe and dated the nineteenth of March, 2005.

Eleven years after she vanished. Four years after the law had declared her dead.

Ivo read the date four times, the way you might tap a wall four times to be sure the hollow sound wasn't your imagination. Then he read the signature. Then he read the attestation clause, where two witnesses had sworn they saw her sign it, both witnesses named, both with Ashcombe addresses, both with signatures that looked — he tilted the page toward the window light — entirely ordinary. No tremor. No hesitation. Just two people signing their names on a day in March 2005, watching a dead woman write hers.

"Well," Ivo said, to the kitchen, to the toast, to no one. "Well, well."

He was fifty-one years old, a probate solicitor of the unglamorous and dependable kind, the sort of man other solicitors sent their strange cases to because he was thorough and because he didn't gossip and because, as one colleague had once put it with more affection than tact, Ivo had the imagination of a filing cabinet. He took this as a compliment. Filing cabinets, in his experience, were the only things that never lost anything.

But a filing cabinet, he now thought, looking at the impossible date, is only as good as what people put into it. And somebody had put this into his.

* * *

He telephoned the firm at ten o'clock and asked for the senior partner, Gerald Fenwick, who had signed the covering letter. The covering letter itself was a marvel of evasion. *Dear Mr Kavanagh, We enclose the original will of the above-named deceased for your attention. The circumstances are, we believe, best discussed in person. We would be grateful if you could travel to Ashcombe at your earliest convenience. The executor, Mrs Ruth Adeyemi, solicitor, will receive you. Yours sincerely.* Not one word about the date. Not one word about why a London firm was sending a will to Ivo Kavanagh, a probate specialist, rather than handling it themselves — or, more to the point, rather than simply refusing it, which is what any sensible firm would have done. A will executed by a legally dead person was not a document you probated. It was a document you sent back with a polite letter explaining that the law, whatever its faults, did not permit the dead to make wills.

Gerald Fenwick was not available. Gerald Fenwick was, the receptionist said, in a meeting. Gerald Fenwick was in a meeting at eleven, and at half eleven, and at twelve, and when Ivo called back at two he was told that Mr Fenwick had left early and could perhaps be reached tomorrow.

"Gerald," Ivo said to the dead line, "you have handled three of my referrals in fifteen years and you have never once left early."

He tried Fenwick's mobile. It rang four times and went to voicemail, and the voicemail message was Fenwick's usual — brisk, faintly irritated, the voice of a man perpetually late for something better — and Ivo left a message anyway, because he was a thorough man and thorough men leave messages.

"Fenwick, it's Ivo Kavanagh. About the Marlowe will. Ring me, would you. There's a date on this document that I'd very much like you to explain."

He did not hear from Gerald Fenwick again for a very long time.

* * *

That evening he read the will itself, properly, with a ruler under the lines the way his father had taught him, and the will was — this was the unsettling part — *good*. Not the work of an amateur. The clauses were clean, the residuary estate was properly defined, the executor's powers were drafted with a competence that made Ivo's professional hackles rise, because whoever had drawn this document had known exactly what they were doing, and had done it for a woman who had officially not existed for four years.

The will was short. It left a sum of money — a modest sum, a few thousand pounds — "to the parish church of Ashcombe, for the upkeep of the churchyard, and in particular for the tending of a grave that is empty." Ivo read that clause three times. *A grave that is empty.* Somebody had a sense of humour. It was not, he suspected, a kind one.

Everything else — the residuary estate, whatever it amounted to — went to "my daughter, Eleanor Marlowe, whom I ask to forgive me, though I do not ask her to believe me."

*Though I do not ask her to believe me.*

He set the will down and sat for a while in the lamplight, and the house was quiet around him in the particular way of a house belonging to a man who lived alone and had stopped minding, mostly, and he found himself thinking not like a lawyer now but like something older and less useful — like a boy reading a story with the torch under the blanket. Because there was a voice in that clause. A real voice. You didn't get *though I do not ask her to believe me* out of a precedent book. Somebody had been sitting somewhere — a kitchen, a rented room, a life — and had thought about her daughter, and had written that.

The diary came with the will. He'd registered it only as an afterthought that morning: a second, smaller package, a notebook bound in faded green cloth, wrapped in tissue, with a note in Fenwick's handwriting: *Recovered property. To be read in connection with the enclosed.* He opened it now, carefully, the way you open something you suspect might be a trap, and read the first entry.

*3rd January 1995. Three months to the day. The gulls here sound different than at Ashcombe — hungrier, or maybe that's me. I have decided to write things down again. If I am ever found, let them find the truth in my own hand. M. says a diary is a dangerous thing. M. is right. I am keeping it anyway.*

The entry was dated three months after Edith Marlowe had vanished from the face of the earth.

Ivo closed the notebook. He opened it again. He checked the handwriting against the will — the firm, upright letters, the particular way the writer crossed her t's with a single decisive stroke, like someone closing a gate. The same hand. He was not a handwriting expert, but he was a solicitor of twenty-five years' standing and he had seen enough signatures to know when two hands were one hand.

Either the diary was a forgery — a very good one, made by someone who knew Edith Marlowe's writing intimately, made for a purpose Ivo could not begin to guess — or Edith Marlowe had been alive on the third of January, 1995, writing about gulls and about someone called M., three months after the town of Ashcombe had buried her empty coffin and agreed, all together, that she was gone.

He went to bed at one in the morning and lay in the dark doing what lawyers do when the law runs out: thinking.

* * *

The train took him down the coast the next day, and the last hour of the journey was the kind of journey that makes you understand why people in old novels go mad in seaside towns. The line ran along a grey sea under a grey sky, and the fog came in off the water in slow, deliberate drifts, like something being poured, and the stations got smaller and smaller — a halt, a platform, a nameboard — until the train sighed into Ashcombe itself, which turned out to be a single platform, a single lamp, and a town folded into the hillside above it like laundry someone had dropped.

He had looked it up, of course. Ashcombe: population 2,300, declining. One church, one pub, one parade of shops, a harbour that had once been busy and now wasn't. A town that had made its living from the sea and then, when the sea stopped paying, had made it from whatever was left. He knew the type. Half the coastline of England was strung with towns like it, places where the twentieth century had arrived late, looked around, and decided to keep going.

But knowing a type is not the same as knowing a place, and there was something about Ashcombe — standing on the platform with his overnight bag, breathing air that tasted of salt and coal smoke and something older — that made the back of his neck prickle. It was not menace, exactly. It was more like the feeling of walking into a room where people had been talking about you and had stopped.

The taxi driver — there was one taxi, a man named Tregose, according to the card in the station window — took him up the hill to the Grey Gull, the town's only lodging, and made conversation the way country drivers do, in a low running commentary about the weather and the road and the state of the sea wall, and Ivo let it wash over him until the man said, turning onto the front, "Here for the Marlowe business, then?"

Ivo kept his voice mild. "What makes you say that?"

"Only thing that brings outsiders down here lately." Tregose's eyes in the mirror were pale and unreadable. "That and the developers. Everyone's got a theory about the Marlowe woman. Thirty years of theories." He paused. "Town got tired of theories, mind. Best thing anyone can say about Edith Marlowe now is nothing at all."

"Is that so."

"That's so." The taxi stopped. "Grey Gull. Mrs Penhaligon does a good breakfast. Tell her Tregose sent you, she'll give you the room at the back, away from the gulls." And then, as Ivo paid him, the driver added, in a voice so quiet it might have been meant for himself: "Though I'd say the gulls are the least of what's away from that room."

Ivo stood on the pavement and watched the taxi go, and decided he had probably imagined the last part. He decided several things that evening. He was a thorough man, and thorough men are very good at deciding things, right up until the moment the things decide them instead.

* * *

Ruth Adeyemi's office was above the chemist's on Fore Street, up a staircase that smelled of lavender polish, and Ruth Adeyemi herself was a small, exact, silver-haired woman of about sixty who looked at Ivo across her desk with the expression of someone who has been expecting a particular visitor for a long time and has already grown tired of him.

"Mr Kavanagh." She did not offer to shake hands. "You came."

"Your firm asked me to."

"My firm asked you to." She almost smiled. "Yes. Sit down, Mr Kavanagh. Would you like tea? It's going to be that kind of conversation."

It was that kind of conversation. She made the tea herself on a tray with a proper cloth, and poured it, and said nothing for a full minute, which Ivo recognised as a technique because he used it himself, and which he nevertheless found himself falling for completely.

"I've been the executor of that will for eleven years," she said at last. "Eleven years, and in all that time the condition of the estate has not been met, and so the estate has sat where it sat, and I have sat on the document, and the document has sat on me. And now the firm in London — your firm — has decided it's time. So they've sent you." She looked at him over the rim of her cup. "Did they tell you why you, specifically?"

"They said my experience."

"Your experience." She set down the cup. "Mr Kavanagh, I'm going to say something to you, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you respond, because you strike me as a man who responds quickly and thinks afterwards, and in Ashcombe that order will get you hurt."

Ivo, who responded slowly and thought before, said nothing at all, which seemed to please her.

"Thirty years ago, a woman walked out of this town and did not come back, and this town decided what that meant, and it agreed on it, all of it, together, in the way small towns agree — not with a meeting, not with a vote, but with a thousand small decisions made over cups of tea. She left. She was unhappy. Her husband had died the year before. She was grieving, or she was selfish, or she was both, and she went, and we buried an empty coffin because it was kinder to the daughter, and we closed the book." Ruth Adeyemi's voice was level and unhurried. "That is the story. It is not necessarily the truth. But it is the story, and everyone in this town has been living inside it for thirty years, and some of them have built houses on it, Mr Kavanagh. Real houses. With real foundations. And when you start pulling at a story that people have built on, they do not thank you. They do not thank you at all."

"And you?" Ivo asked. "What have you built on it?"

She considered him for a long moment, and something in her face — a tiredness, a kind of guarded grief — moved like a fish under ice.

"I was Edith Marlowe's friend," she said. "That's what I built on it. And I have kept her will for eleven years because she asked me to, and I have waited eleven years for someone to come and do what she asked done. And now you're here." She stood, and began gathering the tea things, and her hands were perfectly steady, which somehow told Ivo more than trembling would have. "The pub is the Anchor, if you want dinner. Order the fish. And Mr Kavanagh —"

"Yes?"

"When you ask your questions in this town — and you will, you're a lawyer, it's in the marrow — ask them quietly. And notice who stops talking. That's how you'll know you've asked the right one."

* * *

The Anchor was low-beamed and warm and full of exactly the number of people a pub should hold on a Thursday, and Ivo ate his fish at a corner table and listened, in the way of a man who has spent his life listening to what is not said. The talk was football, and boats, and the price of crab. Nobody looked at him, which was itself a kind of looking. And when, near closing, he asked the landlord — a broad, red-faced man with forearms like mooring rope — whether he might have known Edith Marlowe, the room did not go silent, exactly. It went *attentive*. Conversations continued, but at a lower volume, the way a sea continues under a wind that has changed.

"Marlowe," the landlord said, polishing a glass that did not need polishing. "Before my time, mostly. Sad business. She left, you know."

"So I've heard."

"Aye. Well." The glass squeaked under the cloth. "There's nothing much to tell, sir. That's the thing about Ashcombe. There's never much to tell."

Ivo walked back to the Grey Gull through fog that had come up off the harbour like a held breath, and the town was quiet around him, and the lamp over the front burned in a small yellow island, and he found himself thinking about the clause in the will. *For the tending of a grave that is empty.* And the line in the diary, in that firm upright hand: *If I am ever found, let them find the truth in my own hand.*

In his room, he set the green notebook on the bedside table, and before he turned out the light he opened it at random, somewhere in the middle, to a page dated the summer of 1996 — nearly two years after a dead woman had stopped existing — and read a single line.

*I saw his face today. He was in the crowd at the market. I don't think he saw me. But he is looking. He is always looking.*

Ivo turned out the lamp and lay in the dark, and outside, the gulls — the ones he was supposedly sheltered from — cried over the black harbour like something asking a question that no one in Ashcombe, apparently, would answer.
