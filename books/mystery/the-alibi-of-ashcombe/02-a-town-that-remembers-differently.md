## 2. A Town That Remembers Differently

The bus from the nearest railway station — a place called Penlow Cross, which consisted of a platform, a vending machine that ate coins with genuine enthusiasm, and a view of a sheep field — took forty minutes to reach Ashcombe, and for thirty-nine of those minutes Ivo Kavanagh had it entirely to himself.

He used them to read the will again.

It was a short document, as wills went. Edith Marlowe's estate was not large: a house on Harbour Row long since sold, some savings, a life insurance policy that had lapsed decades ago and been resurrected by clerical accident in a way Ivo intended to investigate purely for the pleasure of complaining about it. The oddity remained the date at the top of the page, typed in a font that hadn't existed when Edith Marlowe walked out of Ashcombe and out of recorded history.

*Eleven years after she vanished.* He'd checked the date three times on the train, as though the paper might blush and correct itself. It hadn't. Paper rarely did. That was rather the point of paper.

And then there was the letter from his own firm, which he'd read so many times he could recite it. *Mr Kavanagh — given the unusual circumstances of this matter, the partners feel it should be handled personally, and discreetly, by someone senior.* Which was firm-speak for *we don't fully understand this either, and we'd like it to be your problem.*

The bus dropped him at the top of Fore Street, and Ashcombe assembled itself around him the way towns do in coastal fog: in pieces, reluctantly, as if it would prefer not to be seen all at once.

It was the kind of town that postcards described as "unspoiled" and estate agents described as "a project." A single street ran downhill toward a harbour that was more mud than water at low tide, flanked by buildings whose paint had surrendered in stages over the years, each stage a different shade of hopeful. Gulls supervised everything from the rooftops with the air of landlords between tenants. At the far end of the street, half-swallowed by mist, stood a church tower, and beyond it, if you knew where to look — and Ivo didn't yet — the cliffs.

He walked down Fore Street with his overnight bag and his briefcase, and he became aware, gradually, of being watched.

Not aggressively. Nothing so useful as aggression. It was subtler than that: curtains shifting a quarter-inch, a man repainting a gate who somehow managed to watch Ivo without his eyes ever moving, two women outside the bakery conducting a conversation that paused — just barely, just politely — as he passed, then resumed at a slightly lower volume.

*They know why you're here,* said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like his mother. *Small towns always know.*

He found the offices of Adeyemi & Co. above the old fishmonger's, up a staircase that creaked in a musical key, past a window filled with dried seaweed arrangements that someone clearly considered decorative. The door bore the firm's name in gold leaf gone the colour of weak tea, and beneath it, smaller: *Wills · Conveyancing · Probate · Notary*. Beneath *that*, in biro on a card taped to the glass: *Back in 20 mins. Help yourself to biscuits. R.A.*

Ivo helped himself to a biscuit. It was a custard cream, slightly stale, and he ate it standing in the little waiting room with its four chairs, its rubber plant, and its framed certificate of incorporation from 1987, and he thought: *any solicitor who leaves biscuits out for strangers is either very confident or very clever, and in my experience the two qualities travel together.*

She was back in nineteen minutes.

Ruth Adeyemi was perhaps fifty-five, tall, grey-haired close to her skull, dressed in the uniform of rural solicitors everywhere — good coat, better shoes, an expression calibrated somewhere between welcome and assessment. She carried a bag of shopping in one hand and a folder in the other, and she looked at Ivo the way a doctor looks at a symptom.

"You'll be Mr Kavanagh," she said. It wasn't a question. "You're early."

"The tide table on the station wall suggested I shouldn't be late."

"It's usually right." She unlocked the office, held the door. "Come through. Tea?"

"Please."

"Milk?"

"Yes."

"Sugar?"

"One."

"Good," she said, as though he'd passed something. "People who take no sugar can't be trusted. Sit down."

Her office was small and ferociously organised. Files stood in ranks; the desk surface held a blotter, a lamp, a telephone, and nothing else, which told Ivo more about Ruth Adeyemi than any reference letter could have. She made tea in a side room with the ease of long habit, came back, poured two cups, sat, folded her hands, and looked at him.

"So," she said. "You've read the will."

"I've read it several times. The date—"

"—is eleven years after Edith Marlowe was declared dead in absentia. Yes. I executed it. I witnessed her signature myself." She said it flatly, without drama, the way one reports the weather or a delayed train. "Before you ask: yes, I'm certain. Yes, I verified her identity. And no, before you ask that too, I won't tell you where she was living, because she asked me not to, and she's dead now, and the dead get to keep their appointments."

Ivo set down his cup. "Mrs Adeyemi—"

"Ms."

"Ms Adeyemi. You must see that the document is legally impossible. A person declared dead cannot execute a valid will eleven years later. If she was alive, the declaration was procured falsely. If she wasn't alive—" he gestured at the folder, "—then someone forged this, and you're either complicit or deceived, and I genuinely don't know which possibility you'd prefer."

Something moved behind her eyes — not anger. Something wearier and older than anger.

"I've had thirty years to think about which possibilities I prefer," she said. "Do you know what I've learned? That in a town like this one, the truth isn't hidden. It's *agreed upon*. There's a difference. Hidden things want finding. Agreed things just sit there, being agreed, generation after generation, until nobody remembers they were ever anything else."

"And Edith Marlowe's story has been agreed upon."

"Oh, beautifully." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of someone watching weather come in off the sea. "You'll hear it today, probably within the hour. She was unhappy after her husband died. She walked into the sea one night in October, or she caught the morning bus and never wrote, depending on who tells it. Either way: sad, simple, closed. A woman who left. The town held a memorial service. Lovely flowers. Her daughter laid a wreath on an empty grave because there was nothing else to lay it on." She picked up her own tea. "That's the version. Everyone signed it, in the way towns sign things. Nobody signed anything, obviously. That's how agreements work here."

"You're telling me this because—"

"Because you strike me as a competent man, Mr Kavanagh, and competent men waste enormous amounts of time discovering things they could simply be told." She opened the folder and slid a photocopied sheet across the desk. "Probate requires certain local verifications. Signatures, confirmations, that sort of thing. You'll need to talk to people. So talk to them. Be polite. Be patient. And when they give you the agreed version — and they will, every single one of them, with the sincerity of parishioners — remember that you were warned."

Ivo looked at the sheet. It was a list of names in her handwriting: neat, upright, unhurried. A harbourmaster. A retired doctor. A developer named Fenn. A publican. Half a dozen others.

"These are witnesses?"

"These are *Ashcombe*," Ruth said. "Which in probate terms comes to the same thing." She tapped one name near the bottom. "Start with Tom Pellowe, the Anchor's landlord. Publicans know everything and repeat almost none of it, which makes them excellent barometers. Watch what happens to the room when her name comes up. That will tell you more than any witness statement I could draft for you."

"When *whose* name comes up?"

"Edith's." She met his eyes. "You'll see."

---

He saw it that same evening.

The Anchor sat halfway down Fore Street, low-beamed and salt-damp, smelling of woodsmoke and twenty years of spilled beer. Ivo arrived at seven, bought a pint of something local and dark, and established himself at the end of the bar with the studied harmlessness of a man on holiday. He'd been a lawyer long enough to know that the fastest way to make people talk was to give them absolutely no reason to.

For twenty minutes, it worked beautifully. He learned that the fishing had been poor since the nineties, that the new development up on the headland — Fenn's development, though the name arrived attached to a shrug — had brought jobs and taken views, that the chip shop changed hands every eighteen months like a cursed object. The landlord, Tom Pellowe, was a broad, slow-moving man with forearms like mooring rope and the gift of appearing to listen while actually conducting surveillance. He refilled Ivo's glass once without being asked, which Ivo recognised as an opening move.

"Pellowe," Ivo said, when the room had thinned to a dozen regulars and a dog. "I'm handling an estate. Marlowe. Edith Marlowe. I understand she was known here."

The effect was not dramatic. That was what made it remarkable.

It was the sound a room makes when it decides, collectively and instantly, to become furniture. Conversation didn't stop — it *lowered*, the way a fire settles when a door opens. Three men at the dartboard discovered a sudden fascination with their scores. A woman by the window turned her face to the fogged glass as though the harbour at night, invisible, were riveting. The dog looked up, sensed the change in atmospheric pressure, and put its head back down.

Tom Pellowe polished a glass that was already clean.

"Marlowe," he said. "Edith Marlowe. Aye. Sad business, that."

"Was it?"

"What?"

"Sad. Was it sad?" Ivo kept his tone mild, curious, faintly dim. "I only have the file. Woman walks out one October, never heard from again. Declared dead seven years later. It reads sad enough, but files are thin things."

"That's about the size of it." Pellowe set the glass down with great care, as though it might go off. "Unhappy woman. Husband lost at sea some years before. One October night she went, and that was that. Town did right by her. Memorial service. Flowers. Nell — the daughter — she's away nursing now, downcountry. Good girl." He said the last part quickly, the way a man steps over a loose floorboard he knows is there.

"And nobody heard from her again. Thirty years."

"Not a word." Pellowe's eyes came up and met Ivo's, and for one unguarded moment there was something in them that wasn't agreement at all — something quick and sorry and frightened, there and gone like a fish under a boat. Then it was packed away, and the landlord's face was a landlord's face again. "You'll be wanting signatures for the estate, I expect. Happy to help. Anything for the family."

*Anything for the family,* Ivo thought, walking back up Fore Street an hour later through fog that had thickened into something you could lean against. *Not anything for the truth. For the family. As if the family and the truth were different parties with opposed interests.*

And weren't they, possibly? Wasn't that exactly what Ruth Adeyemi had tried to tell him?

At the corner by the church, he stopped. The fog moved in slow banks, and through a gap in it he saw the graveyard wall, and beyond it the pale suggestion of headstones, and he thought about a memorial service for a woman who might not have been dead, and a wreath laid on emptiness, and a daughter who had knelt there in front of the whole agreed-upon town.

His phone buzzed. A message from Ruth Adeyemi, sent at ten minutes to eleven:

*One more thing I should have said this afternoon. The diary arrived with the will. You'll have it in the morning. Read it slowly. And Mr Kavanagh — whatever you conclude tonight about this town and its silence, be prepared to conclude something worse tomorrow.*

Ivo stood in the fog with the phone glowing in his hand, and around him Ashcombe slept the sleep of a town that had made its peace — or believed it had — and the gulls cried out over the harbour like witnesses who couldn't be sworn.

He looked at the message a long time.

Then he looked up the street, toward the dark shape of the churchyard, and found himself wondering — with the first true vertigo of the whole affair — not whether the town had lied about Edith Marlowe.

But what, precisely, the diary would say she had seen.
