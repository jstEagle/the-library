## 4. The Daughter Who Buried an Empty Coffin

Nell Marlowe had learned to read death the way sailors read weather. It came in through the ward in layers — first the smell of lavender and disinfectant fighting a losing war, then the particular hush of families who had run out of small talk, and finally the sound itself: that slow, tidal breathing from Room 6 that everyone on the night shift pretended not to count.

She was good at her job. She knew this because people told her so at funerals, gripping her hand too hard and saying *you were wonderful with him* or *she never once complained about you*, as though kindness were a service one could render without cost. Nell had stopped explaining, years ago, that the cost was simply paid somewhere else, privately, in a car park with the engine running.

So when the man from London rang the hospice on a grey Tuesday asking after a *Nell Marlowe, daughter of Edith Marlowe of Ashcombe*, she did what she always did with weather she didn't like.

She said, "I'm afraid you have the wrong number," and hung up.

He rang back within the hour. That was the first thing she noticed about Ivo Kavanagh: he did not accept the forecast he was given.

* * *

She'd known he was coming before he arrived. Word travelled up the coast faster than the buses — Ruth Adeyemi had telephoned on Sunday, careful and lawyerly, saying only that a probate solicitor would be in touch regarding her mother's estate, and Nell had stood in her kitchen holding the phone against her shoulder like something hot, listening to her own silence stretch.

"There is no estate," Nell had said finally. "There's a grave with nothing in it and a headstone with my mother's name misspelled. *Marlow*. One L. They couldn't even be bothered."

"I'm aware the circumstances are unusual," Ruth had said, which was the sort of sentence lawyers used the way other people used anaesthetic.

Everything about her mother was unusual. That was rather the point.

Now it was Thursday, and the man was sitting in the hospice family room with his coat folded over his knees like a well-behaved dog, waiting for her shift to end. She watched him through the glass for a full ten minutes before going in, because watching people unawares was half her training. He sat very still. He looked at the wilting chrysanthemums on the sideboard with an expression she recognised — not pity exactly, but the professional assessment of a man who dealt in endings and wanted to know how these ones were managed.

He looked tired, actually. Tired in a way that wasn't just the train from London. She'd seen that look on relatives who'd been keeping vigil for weeks.

Against her better judgement, she went in.

"You're the solicitor." Not a question. "Ruth said you'd come. I'm telling you now, so we can both save the tea: there's nothing to discuss."

"Miss Marlowe." He rose, offered a hand, and had the decency not to over-grip. "Ivo Kavanagh. Thank you for seeing me at all."

"I haven't. I'm walking past you to the kettle." She filled it anyway, because twenty years of nursing made refusal of hospitality physically difficult, and because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. "You've come a long way to be told no."

"I have. It's a very scenic no, though. The coast road was extraordinary."

"It rains here sideways nine months of the year."

"So I gathered. My umbrella died heroically outside the chippy."

Despite herself — despite everything — the corner of her mouth moved. She hated him a little for that. People who could make you almost smile when you'd built your whole life into a fortress were dangerous, and she'd had enough danger from one woman to last several lifetimes.

* * *

He didn't push. That was the second thing she noticed. He let the kettle roar and asked about the hospice instead — how long she'd worked here, whether the building had always been a convalescent home, whether the sea view helped or hurt. Safe questions, gentle questions, the questions of a man laying stones across a river one at a time and pretending he wasn't.

"My mother left when I was eleven," Nell said, because she'd decided somewhere between the kettle and the cups that the fastest way to be rid of him was to give him the official version, complete and sealed. "August 1994. She went out to post a letter and didn't come back. There was a search. The police were useless — they decided fairly quickly she'd walked into the sea, because that's what women do here apparently, we're practically amphibious. No body, obviously. They declared her dead seven years later, which is the legal minimum, and we had a memorial service over an empty coffin because the church felt we needed *closure*, whatever that is. I was thirty-one and I still didn't know if I was supposed to mourn or hope. I chose mourn. It's less exhausting."

Kavanagh listened with his whole body, which was disconcerting. Most people listened while composing their reply; he listened like the words were arriving from somewhere far away and valuable.

"That must have been very hard for a child," he said.

"I wasn't a child. I was eleven, which is worse, because children get carried and eleven-year-olds get *explanations*." She wrapped both hands around her tea though she didn't want it. "Everyone in town had a theory. She'd run off with a lover. She'd gone to London to start again. My father — God rest him — spent two years driving to bus stations all along the coast on the anniversary, just looking. Just standing on platforms. Then he stopped, and we didn't talk about her again, and that was the correct decision, and I am begging you professionally and personally to make it too."

"May I ask why you're so certain there's nothing to find?"

"Because I buried her already." It came out harder than she intended, and she heard, underneath the hardness, the thin whistle of something else. "Thirty years ago. I did the grieving. I did the years of jumping every time the phone rang, thinking — maybe. Maybe someone found her. Maybe she lost her memory. Maybe, maybe, maybe. Do you know what hope does to a person over decades? It's not noble. It's a disease. And then one day you're forty-one and you're spooning soup into a dying man who keeps calling you by his daughter's name, and you realise you've spent your whole life waiting for a woman who chose the door, and you put it down. You put it down and you don't pick it up again. Not for a will. Not for a solicitor with kind eyes. Not for anyone."

The room was quiet except for the tick of the wall clock and, distantly, the tide-breathing from Room 6.

"I'm sorry," Kavanagh said quietly. "That was more than I asked."

"Yes, well." She scrubbed a hand over her face. "Night shifts. I say things."

But it wasn't the night shift, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person at the table.

* * *

He stayed another twenty minutes and was scrupulous, maddeningly scrupulous, about not mentioning the diary. She knew about the diary. Of course she knew — Ruth had mentioned it in that same Sunday call, apologetically, the way you mention a tumour on a scan. *Entries continuing past the disappearance.* Nell had hung up on her too.

When Kavanagh finally rose to go, he paused at the door with his terrible dead umbrella under his arm.

"One question, purely administrative, and then I'll leave you in peace. The memorial service. The empty coffin. Was anything placed inside it?"

Nell stared at him. "What kind of question is that?"

"A lawyer's question. We're trained to notice containers."

For a moment — a long, cold moment — she was eleven years old again, standing at the graveside in a dress that scratched, watching four men lower a polished box that weighed nothing, that the pallbearers kept adjusting their grip on because empty coffins behave strangely, they shift, they float on shoulders like a lie. Her father's hand on her shoulder, trembling. Mrs. Penhale from the bakery sobbing theatrically. And underneath the hymns, underneath everything, the thought she had never said aloud to any living soul:

*It should be heavier. If she's really gone, it should weigh something.*

"Flowers," Nell said. "Letters. I wrote her a letter. Children do that. It helps them say goodbye."

"And did it help?"

"No." The word came out flat and true. "Nothing helps. You just get further from it."

He nodded slowly, and she saw him file something away behind those tired eyes, some small fact joining others on a shelf she couldn't see.

"Thank you for the tea, Miss Marlowe. I'm staying at the Anchor until Saturday. If you think of anything — anything at all —"

"I won't."

"— the fish and chips are meant to be decent, if you change your mind about talking."

"I won't change my mind."

"Of course not." And he smiled — a small, rueful, entirely honest smile — and let himself out into the rain.

* * *

She lasted four hours.

It was Mr. Brightwell in Room 3 who broke her, ironically, because dying men have no patience for other people's evasions. He'd been a fisherman for fifty years and faced his own ending the way he'd faced weather: irritably, and with constant commentary. Around ten that night, morphine-loosened, he fixed her with his milky eye and said, "You've got your mother's way of lying, girl. Chin up, voice steady, hands busy. Saw her do it a hundred times at the harbour."

Nell froze with the water jug halfway to the bedside table.

"What did you say?"

"Edith Marlowe." He smiled with half his mouth. "Knew her since she was knee-high. Best liar on this coast, God rest her wherever she is. Which, knowing Edith—" his eyelids sagged, "—is probably nowhere anyone's thought to look."

She stood there with the jug until her arm ached, and then she finished her rounds with mechanical precision, and then she sat in her car in the car park with the engine running, the way she did after the hard deaths, and let the defences come down one storey at a time.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she burned with the letters, the thing she kept below the grief like a stone at the bottom of a well:

Her mother hadn't seemed like a woman leaving. Nell had replayed that August morning ten thousand times and it never changed. Edith had made pancakes. She'd braided Nell's hair — she hadn't done that in years, she'd been *tender*, unhurried, humming — and at the door she'd knelt down and held Nell's face in both hands and looked at her for a long, long moment, the way you look at something you're memorising.

And she'd said: *"Whatever you hear about me, love — however it looks — you remember the pancakes."*

Which meant nothing. Which meant everything. Which was either the maddest thing a abandoning mother ever said or the sanest thing a frightened one ever managed, and Nell had spent thirty years refusing to decide which.

The letters had arrived for two years after. Plain envelopes, no return address, postmarks from towns up and down the coast. *I'm safe. Don't look for me. Burn this.* Each one. Every one. And then the phone call — a man's voice, pleasant as oil, asking whether her father knew how much trouble a girl could get into asking questions — and Nell, nineteen and terrified, had fed every letter into her father's stove and told no one, not even him, and had built her life on top of the ash.

They weren't the letters of a woman who'd run off with a lover. She'd known that even at nineteen. They were the letters of a woman hiding.

And now a solicitor with kind eyes and a dead umbrella was in town, asking about containers, and a dying fisherman was comparing her hands to her mother's, and somewhere in Ashcombe there was a diary with entries written by a woman the whole town had agreed to bury alive.

Nell sat in the dark car park until the rain stopped, which took a while, because it always does here.

Then she drove home, dug the old telephone directory out of the drawer where it had lived untouched for a decade, and looked up the Anchor's number with hands that were not quite steady.

"This is Nell Marlowe," she said, when he answered. "You asked if I'd think of anything." A breath. "Ask me about the letters."

On the other end of the line, very quietly, Ivo Kavanagh said, "What letters?"
