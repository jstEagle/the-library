## 3. Bellwether Lane, Revisited

Bellwether Lane was the kind of street that seemed designed by someone who had heard about streets secondhand. A crooked cobbler's shop with a hand-painted sign gone the color of weak tea. A lamppost that worked on alternating Tuesdays, as far as anyone could tell. Six houses of soot-dark brick leaning companionably against one another like old men at a funeral, and at the end of it all, number nine — Edith Ashgrove's house — shuttered and waiting, its windows fogged with twenty years of nobody.

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, which in this city counts as a miracle, and the cobbles shone like something recently forgiven.

"You grew up here," Bram Hale said. It wasn't a question. He walked like a man who had once been paid to walk places and had never quite shaken the habit — hands clasped behind his back, eyes doing a slow, professional sweep of every doorway and drainpipe.

"Until I was eleven." I kept my hands in my coat pockets because they wanted to do something embarrassing, like tremble. "Then Edith and my mother had their falling-out, and we moved across the river, and after that it was Christmas cards until it wasn't even that."

"What was the falling-out about?"

"I was told it was money. I was eleven; I believed it was money. I've since learned that 'money' is what families say when the truth is worse and they haven't the vocabulary for it yet."

Bram grunted. In the two days since he'd appeared in my archive with his leaked case files and his suspicion worn openly like a warrant card, I had catalogued perhaps four of these grunts. This one, I judged, meant *fair enough*.

Number nine's door was locked, of course. The estate had been in probate limbo since Edith's death — no will anyone could find, no next of kin anyone could be bothered to locate, which is what happens when your only living relative is a cathedral archivist who has spent two decades perfecting the art of being overlooked. But locks, I have found, are less statements of fact than invitations to negotiate, and Bram negotiated this one in under a minute with a strip of shim steel he produced from inside his coat with the unselfconscious ease of a man reaching for a handkerchief.

"That's illegal," I said.

"That's *former* constable," he said, and shouldered the door open.

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of dust and lavender and something underneath both — old paper, old smoke, the particular staleness of rooms where time has been standing still so long it's started to slouch. Furniture under sheets. A hat stand wearing a single felt hat like a mourner who'd stayed too long.

I had been in this house as a child. I remembered a kitchen that always smelled of burnt sugar, a parlor with a piano nobody played well, an aunt who could fold a paper boat from a hymnal page in four moves flat. The rooms were smaller than memory had made them. Memory, I've noticed, is a terrible archivist: it discards the boring documents and embellishes the rest.

Bram went through the downstairs with the methodical patience of a man reading a very dull book very carefully. I drifted toward the parlor, toward the piano, and stood there touching nothing, which for me constitutes a kind of religious experience.

"She came here first, you know."

The voice came from the doorway, and I confess I made a sound I will deny having made if you ever ask me. A woman stood there — small, silver-haired, wrapped in a cardigan the color of wet slate, holding a tin pail as though she'd come to collect something and stayed to collect us instead.

"You're the lockmaker's widow," Bram said. "Fenwick."

"Odile Fenwick." Her eyes were pale gray and missed nothing, which put her immediately in the top tier of people I'd met that week. "And you're the one they sacked. And you're the nephew. Word travels on Bellwether Lane faster than it does by wire, and it's more accurate."

"How did you get in?" I asked.

"The gate's been broken since 1987. I've been keeping an eye on this house for twenty years because nobody else would. That's how I know she came back." She set down her pail. "Edith. Three weeks before she died, she knocked on my door at half past ten at night, soaked through, asking questions."

"What sort of questions?"

Odile looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face seemed to settle something. "About a baby," she said. "A baby swapped at birth. She asked whether my husband — God rest him, he made locks for half this city — had ever been asked to make a key for the crypt at St. Verity's. Whether there was a duplicate. She said—" Odile's voice caught, not dramatically, just a small snag like thread catching on a nail. "She said there was a boy christened in 1962 who wasn't the boy everyone thought he was. And that the proof was in the crypt, and she needed to see it before she died, because she had a feeling someone didn't want her looking."

Bram and I exchanged a glance. In my experience, glances exchanged with ex-constables fall into two categories: *do you believe this* and *we're both in danger*. This one managed both.

"And then?" I asked.

"And then three weeks later she was dead on her own kitchen floor, and the coroner said her heart gave out, and everybody said what a shame, and life went on." Odile picked up her pail again, gripping it like a railing. "Except I never believed it. Twenty years I've not believed it, and I've had nowhere to put the not-believing. So when I saw two strangers picking the lock of her house this morning, I thought: finally. Somewhere to put it."

There was a silence in that dusty parlor that felt less like absence than like attendance — as though the room itself had leaned in.

"Mrs. Fenwick," I said, "would you like some truly terrible tea? There won't be any working kettle, but I know a café two streets over where the tea is also terrible but at least it's hot."

Something happened to her face then — a loosening around the mouth, a brightening at the eyes — that I recognized, absurdly, from mirrors. It was the look of a person who has been alone a long time being offered company.

"I'd like that very much," said Odile Fenwick.

* * *

The café was called The Copper Kettle, which was false advertising twice over, as neither the kettle nor anything else in it was copper. We took the corner table beneath a window fogging with steam, and Odile talked, and Bram took notes in a small battered notebook, and I mostly listened and occasionally remembered how to breathe.

It was while Odile was explaining — in the digressive, circumstantial style of a woman who had stored up twenty years of sentences and intended to spend them all — that I noticed the boy.

He was at the counter, maybe fourteen, choir robes stuffed hastily into a satchel that also contained what looked like a stolen communion wafer tin, hair cut badly by someone who loved him but couldn't see straight. He was pretending, with tremendous commitment, to read a newspaper upside down.

"He's been following us since the house," Bram murmured without looking up. "Since before the house. Since the lane."

"Choirboy," I said. "St. Verity's cassock. Third-from-the-left row, if I know our processional order, which I do, because I alphabetized it."

"Should I go have a word?"

"No." Something in me — some instinct older than caution — said wait. "Let him come to us. People always come to us eventually. It's practically the theme of my week."

We waited. Odile ordered a third pot of terrible tea. The boy read his upside-down newspaper with the focus of a scholar deciphering a dead language. And then, inevitably, the newspaper slipped, or his nerve did, and he sidled over to our table with the studied casualness of a cat approaching a vacuum cleaner.

"You're talking about Edith Ashgrove," he said.

"We might be," Bram said.

"You're talking about the crypt key too. And the register." His voice cracked in the middle, which he covered by crossing his arms. "I want in."

"In on what, exactly?" I asked. "Because at present, 'in' consists of bad tea and increasingly alarming conversation."

"I know things." He sat down without being invited, which I respected enormously. "I'm Pip. I live at St. Verity's — lived. I ran off Tuesday. And I know why you're asking about the registers, because Canon Merridew's been acting strange about them for weeks. Locking the vestry. Burning papers in the grate at odd hours. And last month he asked me—" Pip hesitated, and for the first time the bravado flickered. "He asked me to fetch a box from the crypt archive. Alone. And not tell anyone. And the box had a christening gown in it. Folded up small. Like something somebody couldn't bear to throw away but couldn't stand to keep."

The table went quiet. Outside, the rain started again, gentle now, almost apologetic.

"Why did you run away?" Odile asked gently.

Pip examined his hands. "Because I read the letter that was in the box," he said. "And then I understood why he didn't want anyone knowing. And I got scared. And running away is what I do when I'm scared." He looked up, and his chin came out defiant. "But I'm done running. And you lot clearly need help. You're an archivist who looks like a strong wind could file him away, a sacked policeman, and a widow with a biscuit tin. No offense."

"Some taken," said Odile, who was smiling.

"So here's my bargain." Pip held up one finger. "You let me stay with you — I'm not going back to the cathedral, not tonight, maybe not ever. Two—" another finger "—you feed me, because I've had one sausage roll since Tuesday. And three, when we catch whoever killed Edith Ashgrove, I get to be there when it happens. I need to see it end."

Bram looked at me. Odile looked at me. Apparently, in the space of three days, I had become the sort of person decisions were deferred to, which was either the most flattering or most terrifying development of my adult life.

"All right, Pip," I said. "Sausage rolls first. Then everything you know, in order, slowly, and starting with exactly what that letter said."

His face did something complicated — relief and fear shaking hands awkwardly — and he nodded, and reached into his satchel.

What he drew out was not the letter. It was a photograph, creased twice, faded to sepia at the edges: two infants, side by side in a hospital bassinet, each tagged with a paper wristband. On the back, in handwriting I knew better than my own — because I'd spent two days studying it in a confession I still couldn't stop rereading — someone had written:

*One of these is mine. God forgive me, I no longer know which.*

The rain picked up against the window. Odile reached over and touched my arm, lightly, the way you steady someone on a moving train.

"Well," I said, when I could speak again. "I suppose we'd better find out."
