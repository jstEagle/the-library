## 4. What the Crypt Keeps

There are two kinds of locked doors in this world: the kind that keep people out, and the kind that keep something in. The crypt door beneath St. Verity's was, I had always assumed, the first kind. Standing before it at eleven o'clock on a Thursday night, a stolen lantern in one hand and my own conscience rattling around like a coin in an empty collection plate, I began to suspect it had been the second all along.

"You're certain about the hinges?" Bram whispered. He'd brought his old constable's coat out of whatever shame-drawer he kept it in, and it fit him the way guilt fits everyone — snugly, and too late.

"Odile was certain," I said. "Her husband made the duplicate key for Edith twenty years ago. She says the lock is decorative. The real security is that nobody ever bothers."

"Nobody ever bothers," Pip repeated, delighted, as though this were the wisest proverb he'd ever heard. He'd wedged himself between us wearing his choir cassock under a borrowed overcoat, on the theory — his theory — that if we were caught, a boy in vestments would be forgiven anything up to and including arson. "That's the best hiding place there is. Right where everyone can see it but no one looks."

"That," said Bram, "is also how most murders get done."

Cheerful man, our constable.

The key turned with a sound like a held breath being released. Odile had pressed it into my palm that afternoon without ceremony, closing my fingers over it as if she were handing me something warm and alive. "My husband made two," she'd said. "One for Edith. One he kept, in case she ever needed rescuing from whatever she thought she needed rescuing from." A pause. "I suppose now it's you needing it instead."

The door swung inward onto darkness that smelled of stone and candle grease and time. I went first, because it was my cathedral, my crypt, my aunt, and my crime, in roughly ascending order of weight.

* * *

I have catalogued every shelf of St. Verity's restricted archive twice, but I had never been below. Archivists are not meant to go below; the crypt records are sealed by canon law and by a standing instruction so old its author's name has worn off the ledger. We preserve what's down here without reading it. It occurred to me, as my lantern light crawled across rows of lead-lined boxes, that this was a strange way to live — guarding secrets you've agreed never to know.

"There." Bram pointed his own light at a niche near the eastern wall, half-hidden behind a funeral bier older than the parish itself. "Fresh mortar. See it? Someone patched that wall recently. Within the last few years, maybe less."

Pip crouched beside it, running his fingers along the seam like a blind man reading a face. "It's hollow. Listen." He rapped twice. The stone answered with a sound too thin for rock.

Bram looked at me. I looked at the wall. Somewhere above us, the cathedral settled against the rain with a long wooden groan, like an old woman turning over in bed.

"Well," I said, "we've already broken in. It seems a pity to leave having broken nothing else."

It took a chisel from Bram's coat and perhaps four minutes of work that would have gotten us all excommunicated in a more vigorous century. The stone came away in pieces, and behind it sat a biscuit tin. Not an archive box, not a reliquary, not anything with dignity or provenance — a biscuit tin, the kind sold at Christmas with a picture of the seaside on the lid, rusted at the corners and heavier than it had any right to be.

Edith Ashgrove had hidden her truth behind a wall in a crypt, wrapped in a tin that once held shortbread. My aunt, who signed her letters *yours in haste*, who died on Bellwether Lane with her front door unlocked, had trusted the Church's forgetfulness more than any living soul.

I opened it with hands that did not feel entirely like mine.

Photographs first. A hospital ward, 1962 by the fashion of it. Two bassinets side by side. On the back, in Edith's small fierce handwriting: *Tuesday, both of them, same hour. What are the odds?* Then another photograph — a christening, a squalling infant swaddled in white, held by a woman whose face I didn't know, while a clergyman's hand hovered above the baby's head in blessing. And folded inside that photograph, soft as moth wings, an actual christening gown, sixty years old, smelling of cedar and something faintly medicinal.

"She kept a gown," Pip said quietly. All his delight had gone somewhere else. "You don't keep a gown unless it matters. You don't hide it behind a wall unless it *hurts*."

Beneath everything lay a note, one page, written in the hand I knew best in the world after my own — the hand that had addressed twenty years of Christmas cards to a nephew who never wrote back.

*I have checked the register three times and my memory a hundred. One child went home to Bellwether Lane that night. The other child went home to a lie. The boy who isn't the boy wears his name like a borrowed coat, and God help me, I think he knows it fits.*

I read it aloud because none of us told me not to, and hearing it in my own voice made it worse.

"The boy who isn't the boy," Pip repeated.

"Ashgrove," Bram said slowly. "Yara. If Edith was asking about a swapped baby — if she died for it — then either someone stole an Ashgrove name, or..." He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The sentence finished itself in the cold air of the crypt, in the space between the lantern and the wall.

Or the name I had carried for thirty-one years belonged to someone else, and the inheritance I'd spent my life assuming was mine — the house on Bellwether Lane, the name, the whole dusty inheritance of being Edith's blood — might sit, right now, on the head of a stranger.

The vertigo they describe in books is a lie. It isn't falling. It's the floor quietly announcing it was never floor at all.

"I need to sit down," I said, which was absurd, because I was already sitting, on a funeral bier, in a crypt, holding my possibly-fake life in a biscuit tin.

* * *

We were still sitting there — Pip cross-legged, Bram pacing, me performing composure badly — when the light came down the stairs.

Not torchlight. Lantern light, steady and unhurried, and beneath it the voice I would know anywhere, having heard it deliver four hundred sermons and my own induction oath.

"Yara." Canon Merridew descended into view the way weather descends — gradually, inevitably, with a kind of grand patience. He was in his nightclothes under a greatcoat, which somehow made it worse; he had come down here barefoot-adjacent, in the middle of the night, as naturally as if the crypt were his study. Perhaps it was. "And Constable Hale. And—" his eyes found Pip, and something flickered there, amusement or appetite, "—one of my choristers, out of bed after hours."

"Ex-constable," Bram corrected, chin up, daring him to make something of it.

"Mm." Merridew came closer. His gaze traveled over the breached wall, the scattered stones, the open tin. I watched him take inventory the way I take inventory — completely, instantly, without appearing to move his eyes. "You know," he said mildly, "that wall was sealed under a standing order older than either of our appointments. What you've done tonight is theft from the Church, destruction of Church property, and violation of a sealed record. Any one of those could end a career." A pause, gentle as snowfall. "What remains of yours, Mr. Hale. What hasn't yet begun of yours, young man."

"We found Edith's things," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "She hid them here. Before she died."

"I know what she hid here." He said it simply, without drama, and that simplicity was the most frightening thing in the room. "I have always known. I signed the order sealing this section, Yara. Why do you imagine I did that?"

The question hung there. I reached for an answer and found my hands full of tin and gown and photographs and none of them was an answer.

Merridew crouched, unhurried, and gathered the scattered stones back toward the wall with his own hands, brushing dust from his greatcoat afterward like a man tidying a garden. Then he stood, and looked at each of us in turn, and what was in his face was not anger. It was something sadder and far more carefully maintained.

"Go home," he said. "All of you. Put the tin back where you found it, or don't — it changes nothing now. But hear me, Yara Ashgrove." He stepped close enough that I could smell candle wax and wintergreen on him. "Some records are sealed because the truth in them is heavy, and the people carrying it have carried it long enough. Whatever you think you're digging toward — dig gently. Some foundations, disturbed, bring down more than the building."

He took his lantern and went up the stairs, and the dark closed behind him like water.

None of us spoke until his footsteps had faded entirely.

"He let us go," Pip whispered. "Why would he let us go?"

"Because," Bram said, and his voice had gone flat and cop-like, the voice I'd begun to recognize as his honest one, "he wanted to see exactly what we'd found. And now he knows." He turned to me. "Yara. That note. *The boy who isn't the boy.* Is there any chance — any at all — that Edith meant you?"

I looked down at the photograph in my hand: the hospital ward, the two bassinets, my aunt's handwriting underneath. *What are the odds?*

"Odile said Edith was asking questions before she died," I said. "About a baby swapped at birth. About who went home to which family." I swallowed. "Bram — I don't even know which bassinet I'm supposed to be."

Above us, faint through forty feet of stone, the cathedral clock struck midnight, and the sound fell into the crypt like a stone into a well, and I understood, with terrible clarity, that tomorrow morning I would walk back into the registers — the registers only I could read — and go looking for myself.

And that whoever had razored that page out in 1962 would be waiting to see whether I found them first.
