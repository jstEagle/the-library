## 5. Dispatch Records, 2:14 A.M.

The dispatch office smelled of burnt coffee and old carpet the way it always did at three in the morning, when the fog pressed against the windows like something trying to read over your shoulder. Theo Ashgrove sat alone behind the desk with a radio that had gone quiet for the first time all night and a box of company records he'd pulled from the storage room under pretense of inventory.

Nobody inventoried anything in Greywater Bay Cab Company's basement. Nobody had since his father died.

He'd told himself it was routine — that every dispatcher worth his headset knew the logs backward, that a man who couldn't sleep might as well be useful. But his hands knew better. They had known better since the letter arrived, since he'd traced that looped *g* across the page and felt something in his chest tilt like a boat taking water.

The records room was down eleven concrete steps, lit by a bulb that buzzed at a frequency just below hearing, so that you felt it in your teeth rather than heard it. The shelves ran floor to ceiling, cardboard boxes labeled by year in his father's square, patient handwriting — *1987, 1988, 1989* — and then, after 1994, in Theo's own hand, because that was the year his father's hands had started to shake and Theo had taken over the labeling without either of them ever saying why.

His father, Aldous Ashgrove, had dispatched this office for thirty-one years. He had known every street by its sound on a wet night, every driver by the way they keyed the mic. He had kept the logs the way monks keep scripture — complete, exact, unembellished. When Theo was a boy, his father had told him: *The log is the only honest thing in this town, Teddy. People lie. Paper doesn't.*

Theo had believed him. He was beginning to understand that his father had believed it too — believed it hard enough that maybe, somewhere in these boxes, the paper had caught him in a lie of his own.

* * *

He started with the night Edmund Vail drowned.

It wasn't hard to find. The company kept current-year logs in the front office cabinet, and the pages for that week were still there, still smelling faintly of toner. Theo laid them out on the desk under the desk lamp and read them the way you'd read a stranger's diary — slowly, guiltily, looking for what wasn't meant to be found.

The night of the drowning had been ordinary. That was the first thing that unsettled him. Calls logged at intervals, fares to the cannery, the hospital, the Anchor Tavern at closing. Rain noted in the margin in the day man's hand. At 11:40 p.m., a pickup on Harbor Road — no name, just an address and a fare amount. At 1:15, nothing. At 2:14 a.m. —

Theo stopped.

2:14. The number sat on the line like a stone in a shoe. There was no call logged at 2:14. No pickup, no drop-off, no fare. Just the time itself, written into the log in a column where times went, followed by a dash and three letters in small capitals:

**HLD**

Hold. It was an old code, one his father had taught him before Theo was old enough to work the board. *If a call comes in and you can't take it — if it's wrong somehow, if the voice is wrong or the request is wrong — you log it as held and you never explain it in writing.* His father had called it the courtesy of ambiguity. *Paper doesn't lie, Teddy. But paper can keep quiet.*

Someone had called the cab company at 2:14 in the morning on the night Judge Edmund Vail walked into the water. And someone — his father was dead; the day man wouldn't have used the old code; it had to have been his father, working the overnight as he always did in bad weather — someone had answered that call, written down the hour, and then chosen silence.

Theo sat back. The lamp hummed. Out in the dark, fog moved along the window glass like a slow animal breathing.

He thought about the coat. He'd noticed it at the funeral, or rather he'd noticed its absence from the story everyone told — the judge had gone down to the dock in the rain, everyone said, slipped, gone into the harbor. But Ruthie Callahan at the diner had mentioned, in the way people mention things they've been told not to, that the judge's coat had been dry when they brought him up. Dry. On a night the rain came down like nails. A coat didn't lie either.

And now a phone call at 2:14, held and hidden.

He turned back through the pages, looking for the call itself — maybe it was logged elsewhere, maybe there was a follow-up entry, a return call, anything. There wasn't. The 2:14 entry stood alone, a door in a wall with no house around it.

Then, because his hands needed something to do while his mind went cold, he flipped forward a week. And there it was again.

Not the same night. Not the same handwriting even — but the same shape. A call at 2:09 a.m., logged **HLD**, eight days after the drowning, and beneath it, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, a single word his father had allowed himself: *Vail St.*

Vail Street. The judge's own block. Someone calling from Edmund Vail's street, twice, in the weeks after his death, and both calls swallowed by the courtesy of ambiguity.

Theo closed the book. His heart was doing something strange and slow, like a clock winding down in an empty house.

* * *

He should have stopped there. Any reasonable man would have. But reasonable men were not made in Greywater Bay, and Theo had inherited his father's insomnia along with his father's handwriting, and so at four in the morning he carried the current-year log downstairs into the records room and set it on the table beside the boxes from three years ago.

Because there was a reason the number 2:14 had landed in him like a stone in a shoe. There was a reason it felt less like a discovery than a recognition.

Three years ago, a courthouse clerk named Marisol Quade had gone into the harbor off Pier Nine, on a night much like this one, and the town had done what the town does. There had been a service — small, because Marisol had come from away and kept to herself. There had been a paragraph in the *Greywater Sentinel*, between a church bake sale and a notice about dredging permits. *Apparent accidental drowning. No signs of foul play.* The coroner had signed it. The town had filed it. Even Theo, who had driven her once or twice — a quiet woman with ink-stained fingers who always sat up front and always said thank you — even Theo had let it go. That was the thing about this town he could never explain to outsiders: grief here wasn't loud, it was efficient. The bay took people and the town balanced its books and by spring the pier boards were replaced and nobody remembered which ones had rotted.

But his father had not let it go. Theo hadn't known that then. He knew it now, standing in the buzzing light with the 1999 box open in front of him, because he had spent two days — since the letter, since the looped *g*, since the word *Marisol Quade* risen from the grave of a newspaper paragraph — pulling everything the company had from the autumn Marisol drowned.

The log for that week lay open under his hands. He read it once quickly, the way you'd skim a familiar road, and then again slowly, the way you'd walk a road at night when you think someone is following you.

The night of Marisol Quade's drowning, there was a call logged at 1:52 a.m. Pickup: Courthouse steps. Drop-off: blank. In the notes column, in his father's hand, the old code again — but this time not **HLD**. This time something else, something Theo had never seen his father write in thirty years of logs:

**A.A. drv.**

Aldous Ashgrove drove.

His father had not merely taken the call. His father had left the office — left the board, left the town's late-night callers to ring out into an empty room — and driven the fare himself. And the destination column was empty. Thirty-one years of meticulous logging, and the one destination his father had not written down was the one that mattered most.

Theo stood very still. The bulb buzzed. Somewhere above him, a pipe ticked as the building cooled.

He forced himself to keep reading. The following nights. The calls around the funeral. And there — nine days later, the morning after Marisol Quade was buried in a plot nobody visited — another entry. 2:14 a.m. Pickup: none listed. Notes: **HLD — Vail St.**

2:14. Vail Street.

The same hour. The same street. Three years apart, on opposite sides of a drowning, and the same man sitting at this same desk, answering the same telephone, writing the same small capitals, keeping the same silence.

Two deaths, one pattern. Except one of them wasn't a death at all, was it? Not according to the letter sitting in Theo's jacket pocket, the letter that said *Marisol Quade is alive, I saw her, I know where she is* — the letter that named a witness the whole town had watched sink beneath the gray water and agreed, collectively, to forget.

His father had driven someone from the courthouse steps the night Marisol Quade drowned. His father had held a call from Vail Street the night Edmund Vail drowned. And between those two silences, three years wide, lay whatever his father had known and never said — not to the coroner, not to the police, not to his son, not even to the paper that was supposed to be the only honest thing in town.

*Paper doesn't lie,* his father had told him. *But paper can keep quiet.*

What else had it kept quiet? What else was down here in the dark, in the boxes, waiting?

* * *

Theo worked until the sky outside the high little window went from black to the color of dishwater. He pulled the logs for every month surrounding both drownings. He built, on a sheet of legal pad, a timeline in two columns — *Marisol, 3 yrs ago* and *Vail, last month* — and the columns began to rhyme in ways that made the skin of his arms prickle.

Calls from the courthouse, late, held or driven personally. Calls from Vail Street. One other entry he couldn't place: eighteen months ago, a fare logged simply as **L.V.** — Lena Vail, the judge's daughter, though she'd left town by then, hadn't she? Or was that before she left? He couldn't remember anymore which stories the town told were true and which were just load-bearing walls. His father had driven her somewhere and logged her only by initials, which meant his father had wanted the trip findable — findable by exactly one person, someday, if that person ever went looking.

Findable by him.

Theo set down the pen. His hand was steady, which surprised him; inside, nothing was steady at all. He thought of his father in this chair, in this light, wearing the same expression Theo was wearing now — the look of a man holding a telephone receiver very slightly away from his ear, as if the voice coming out of it might be catching.

Who had called at 2:14? Both times? Had it been the judge himself, calling from his own street, asking for a cab that Aldous Ashgrove refused to send — or refused to record? Or had it been someone watching the judge's house, someone reporting movement, someone his father chose to protect by writing nothing?

And the courthouse steps, three years ago, at ten minutes to two in the morning. Who walks out of a locked courthouse at that hour unless they're running? Unless someone inside has opened a door for them?

*A.A. drv.* His father had put himself in the car. Whatever happened that night, Aldous Ashgrove had decided it was safer to drive it himself than to trust it to anyone else — safer, perhaps, than to let it exist anywhere but behind his own headlights.

Theo gathered the pages together with hands that finally, faintly, trembled. Upstairs, the radio crackled awake — Danny Ruiz out at the Anchor wanting a fare to the cannery, the ordinary machinery of the ordinary town grinding into its gray morning. Theo climbed the eleven steps, took the call, wrote the log entry in his own square, patient handwriting — the handwriting his father had taught him, the handwriting that would someday sit in these same boxes and be read by God knew who.

*People lie,* his father had said. *Paper doesn't.*

But his father had lied. His father had lied with paper, in the only language he trusted, in small capitals and empty columns, and he had lied for three years, right up until his heart quit on him in this chair, and now the lie was Theo's inheritance along with the desk and the headset and the insomnia.

He looked at the legal pad. Two columns. Two drownings. One hour of the night recurring like a signature — 2:14, 2:14 — the way a killer returns to a grave, or a guilty man returns to a window.

There was one more place the pattern might lead, and it was a place Theo had been avoiding since the letter arrived, because going there meant admitting what the looped *g* had already told him. The confession's handwriting belonged to someone who had once written him letters — dozens of them, summer-long, salt-smelling, tied with string and kept in a shoebox under his bed for twenty years. Letters from a girl who grew up on Vail Street, in the judge's house, whose father had just drowned in the harbor.

Lena.

If the confession was hers — if she had written it, sent it, or failed to send it — then she knew things. About her father. About the night. Maybe about the calls at 2:14. Maybe about why a taxi dispatcher had once driven a frightened woman away from the courthouse steps and never told a soul.

The morning shift man clattered in, stamping rain off his boots, complaining about the fog on the bridge road. Theo handed over the board, took his coat, and stepped out into the street, where the fog was lifting just enough to show the courthouse at the top of the hill — its dome gray against gray, its steps empty, its windows dark, keeping its own kind of log.

He turned toward home, toward the shoebox under the bed, toward twenty years of letters he'd promised himself he'd never reread.

The past, he understood now, had never been past. It had been idling at the curb this whole time, meter running, waiting for him to get in.
