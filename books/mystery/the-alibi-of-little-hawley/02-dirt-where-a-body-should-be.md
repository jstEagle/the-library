## 2. Dirt Where a Body Should Be

The call came at 6:40 in the morning, which was the first strange thing about it. Nobody calls the Hotel Meridian's front desk at 6:40 in the morning unless something has gone wrong, and nobody calls *me* directly unless they want it kept off the books.

"Oscar." Mayor Colleen Voss-Drayton's voice, pitched low and fast, the way people talk when they're standing near other people who shouldn't hear. "I need you down at Hillcrest Cemetery. Now. Before the crews start their shift."

"It's not my department," I said, which was true. Nothing is my department. That has always been the arrangement.

"Oscar." She said my name again like she was setting it down on a counter between us. "Please."

Colleen Voss-Drayton had never said please to me in eleven years of mayorship and thirty years of her family staying at the hotel before that. I noted it the way I note everything—quietly, in the ledger behind my eyes—and I put on my coat.

* * *

Hillcrest Cemetery sits on the east slope above town, forty acres of leaning headstones and ambitious oaks, and for a hundred years it has been full in the way old cemeteries are full—not of bodies so much as of names. The relocation had been announced last spring with great ceremony: the state was widening Route 9, the cemetery's oldest section sat squarely in the path, and so the founders' row—the Voss plot at its center—would be moved to fresh ground on the north side, with all appropriate honors, just in time for the centennial gala. It had been framed as an act of reverence. A town carrying its dead to better lodgings.

When I arrived, the work crew stood in a loose half-circle around an open grave, and not one of them was working.

October fog still clung to the hollows between the stones. A backhoe idled with its bucket resting on the turf like a dog told to stay. Six men in hi-vis vests, and every face turned toward the hole as if it might explain itself.

Denny Kowalczyk saw me first. Denny has run excavation crews in this county for twenty-five years; he once pulled a tractor out of the flooded quarry with nothing but chain and stubbornness, and I have never seen him look rattled. He looked rattled now. His face had the color of the fog.

"You Hartwell?" he said. "She said to wait for Hartwell."

"I'm Hartwell."

"Then come look at this," he said, "and tell me I'm crazy."

They'd rigged straps around the coffin and lifted it onto the grave's edge, ready for transfer to the new vault. It was a fine coffin, even after sixty-two years—bronze fittings gone green, mahogany gone black and soft at the seams. The lid had been sealed with solder when Edmund Voss was laid to rest in June of 1962, and the seal had held. Denny's man Ray had cut through it that morning with a reciprocating saw, careful as a surgeon, because the plan was to move the founder intact, unopened, into his new marble home with a plaque and a ribbon and the whole town watching.

Except the lid came off light. That was the first thing Ray told me, later, when he could get words out. Coffins don't come off light. Even empty ones have weight in the hinges, the hardware, the lining. This one came up like a book cover.

I looked inside.

Dirt. Not even much of it—a shallow drift across the bottom, maybe two inches, the kind of sediment that finds its way into any sealed box left sixty years in wet ground. No liner. No remains. No brass nameplate on a lid interior. No skeleton in a suit, no wedding band fused to bone, no founder of Little Hawley.

Edmund Voss's coffin was empty, and it had been empty for a very long time. You could see it in the way the dirt lay—in the way the interior wood had dried and checked without ever bearing weight. Whatever had been buried here in 1962, it had never been a man.

Nobody spoke for what felt like a full minute. Somewhere below us a truck changed gears on Route 9, the road that was eating the cemetery, indifferent as weather.

"Well," said Ray finally, "that's not right."

That's not right. Twenty-five years of digging holes and the man's vocabulary failed him entirely, and I understood why. There are things you find in graves that are sad, and things that are grim, and things that are merely strange. An empty coffin under a statue and a scholarship and a hundred years of civic pride is none of those. It is a door opening where there was only ever wall.

"Cover it," Denny said hoarsely. "Put the lid back on. Nobody says anything until the mayor gets here."

But of course six men had already said everything to each other with their faces, and faces travel faster than phone calls.

* * *

Colleen Voss-Drayton arrived twenty minutes later in a gray coat over her pajamas, hair pinned up without a mirror, driving her own car, which I had never seen her do. She parked badly, walked badly, and stopped at the edge of the grave with the particular stillness of a person looking at their own future tipped sideways.

She was sixty-one years old and built her whole public self out of composure—I'd watched her deliver eulogies, budget cuts, and a concession speech with the same serene jaw—but standing over that open box, she looked briefly like a girl. Like someone's granddaughter.

"Okay," she said. Her voice came out steady, which must have cost her. "Okay. Show me."

Denny lifted the lid again. She looked for a long time. The fog moved through the oaks above us and dripped.

"When did the seal last get inspected?" she asked.

"There's no record of an inspection," I said. "There's no record of anything after the burial. I checked the cemetery board minutes while I waited for you. The plot was transferred to perpetual care in 1963 and then nobody touched it for sixty years except the groundskeeper, and he mowed around it."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've been reading the minutes since Tuesday, when the relocation schedule was posted," I said. "It's what I do. I read things."

She turned and looked at me properly for the first time. Most people look at Oscar Hartwell the way they look at furniture—registering the shape of him without ever seeing inside. Colleen looked at me the way you look at a locked drawer you've suddenly remembered exists.

"Denny," she said, without taking her eyes off me. "Your crew. Can they keep quiet?"

"They're good men."

"That's not what I asked."

Denny shifted his weight. "They'll keep quiet till Friday. After that it's out of my hands. Guys got wives."

"Friday," she said. "Fine. Fine." She pressed her gloved hands together like a woman praying or counting. Then she turned back to me and lowered her voice, though we were alone among the dead and six men pretending to be deaf.

"Oscar. I need a favor, and I need it informal, because if this goes through channels—if the sheriff's office logs it, if it touches a single clipboard—it becomes a story by noon and a scandal by Sunday, and the centennial is in three weeks. Three weeks, Oscar. A hundred years of this town, and my family's name on all of it, and I am *not* going to let some—" She stopped herself. Breathed. Started over, quieter. "Somebody robbed a grave. That's the likely answer. Grave robbers, drifters, kids, God knows. It's ugly but it's contained, and I need time to handle it contained. I need someone discreet to poke around quietly and tell me what happened before anyone else does. Someone who knows everybody and whom nobody notices. Someone—"

"Like a concierge," I said.

Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You've spent thirty years watching everyone in this town. Don't pretend you haven't. Half the time I think you know what I'm going to order for breakfast before I sit down."

"Coffee, black, and the fruit plate you don't eat," I said. "You leave the melon."

Something flickered across her face—annoyance, maybe, or the uncomfortable recognition of being known—and then it settled into resolve. "Name a number. Off the books. Cash."

"I don't want money."

"Everyone wants money."

"Not me," I said, and it was the truth, and the truth surprised both of us. What I wanted was standing in front of me in a hole in the ground: a question with teeth. "I want to know how a man gets buried twice. Or never."

She studied me for a long moment, and I watched her decide—watched the arithmetic run behind her eyes, weighing discretion against risk, the hotel's quiet man against whatever it was she feared this actually was. Because she did fear it. Underneath the mayoral machinery grinding into gear, underneath the talk of drifters and containment, there was a colder thing in her face. She wasn't afraid of a scandal. She was afraid of something specific, something with a shape she recognized, and I filed that away where I file everything.

"Two weeks," she said. "Quiet. If you find grave robbers, we close it quietly and the centennial goes forward. If you find anything else—" She didn't finish. "Just find it first. Find it before anyone else does. Can you do that?"

"Mayor," I said, "noticing things is the only thing I've ever done."

* * *

I stayed after the crew covered the coffin and the backhoe finished refilling the grave, tamping the earth flat with the same careless competence it would use on any utility trench. I stayed until the trucks pulled out and the fog burned off and Hillcrest was just a cemetery again, ordinary and patient.

Then I walked over to the marker.

EDMUND VOSS, 1898–1962. FOUNDER. BENEFACTOR. BELOVED. Below it, the line every schoolchild in Little Hawley can recite: *He gave this town its future.* The granite was clean. Somebody washed it. For sixty-two years somebody had been washing this stone, and I made a note of that too, because in my experience the most honest information in any town lives in the small maintenance nobody admits to—who trims which hedge, who pays which bill, who scrubs which marble.

I stood there a long while, longer than a concierge has any reason to stand at a grave, and I'll be honest about what I felt, because honesty is cheap when no one's listening.

First I felt the shock, cold and total, the way you feel ice water. Edmund Voss was the fixed point of Little Hawley. Everything here pointed at him—the statue on the square, the scholarship, the annual memorial, the founding myth we'd all been raised on like mother's milk. And the fixed point was hollow. The town had been orbiting an empty box for sixty-two years.

And underneath the shock—shamefully, wonderfully, underneath it like a coal under ash—there was something else.

A thrill.

I'm seventy-three years old. I have spent my life being the man nobody sees, and I told myself I preferred it that way, and mostly I did. But there is a difference between choosing invisibility and having nothing worth being visible for, and I had not felt the second feeling in decades. Standing over that refilled grave, I felt my pulse doing something youthful and disreputable. Somewhere in this town, sixty-two years ago, someone had committed a fraud so large that an entire municipality grew up inside it—and the fraud had just cracked open like an egg, three weeks before the party meant to celebrate it.

Someone had put an empty coffin in the ground in June of 1962 and convinced a whole town to mourn it. Which meant someone had needed the town to believe a man was dead.

Which meant someone had needed him *gone.*

I took out my little notebook—the one I keep guest preferences in, room temperatures and pillow firmness and which widow takes her tea at four—and on a fresh page I wrote the date, and under it: *June 14, 1962. Who benefits from a death? Who mourns it hardest? Who keeps the stone clean?*

Three questions. It was a start. In my experience, every large lie is held up by small attentions, and small attentions leave fingerprints.

I closed the notebook and started down the hill toward town, past the older section where the relocation wouldn't reach, and it was there—halfway down, where the oaks thin out—that I passed the Voss plot's neighbor and noticed, without meaning to, the flowers.

Fresh lilies. White, unwilted, stems cut square, laid against the neighboring stone with a care that had nothing casual in it.

On a grave that had no business receiving them.

I bent down and read the marker, and the name on it went into my notebook next to the date, and I walked the rest of the way into town faster than I have walked in years.

Because the stone beside Edmund Voss's empty grave belonged to Daniel Reyes—his business partner, drowned in the Meridian River the same week Voss was buried—and someone, sometime recently, had brought him lilies too.
