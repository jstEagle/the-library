## 16. The Man with the Cane

The lilies came again on Thursday, four days early, and this time Oscar was waiting.

He had taken the long way to the cemetery, past the shuttered feed store and the old water tower, arriving an hour before dawn with a thermos of coffee and thirty years of practice at being furniture. He settled himself on a bench beneath the yew tree at the cemetery's edge—a bench he'd chosen because its broken slat cast a shadow deep enough to swallow a man—and he did what he had always done best.

He waited. And he watched.

The mist lay over Little Hawley Memorial like a sheet pulled up to the chin. The Voss plot sat on the rise at the center, the obelisk catching the first gray light, and beside it the open wound of the empty grave, still fenced in orange plastic from the relocation survey. Oscar had come to think of that hole as the most honest thing in town. Everything else was marble and myth.

At 5:47, a car door closed somewhere near the service gate.

Oscar didn't move. He let his eyes do the work, tracking the sound through the fog: footsteps, uneven, accompanied by a rhythmic tap he knew before he saw it. Tap. Shuffle. Tap. A cane striking stone.

The figure emerged from the mist by degrees—first a shape, then a coat, then a face. An old man, tall even stooped, wearing a wool overcoat too fine for the hour and a flat cap pulled low. In one hand, the cane—dark wood, silver handle worn bright by decades of grip. In the other, wrapped in butcher paper, a bundle of white lilies.

Not Edmund Voss. Oscar had studied the archive photographs until he could have picked the founder's face out of a crowd of strangers. This man carried something of him—the heavy jaw, the deep-set eyes—but softened, weathered, remade by sixty years of living someone else's life.

Which meant only one thing.

*The son.*

Edmund Voss had officially died childless. The town histories said so. The centennial brochures said so. Colleen Voss-Drayton's entire claim to the founding bloodline said otherwise, but the record said childless, and the record, as Oscar now understood, had been written by the same hand that wrote everything else.

The old man reached the grave and stood before it for a long moment, the cane planted like a fence post. Then he knelt—slowly, wincing, one hand braced on the headstone—and laid the lilies at its base.

He spoke. Too low for Oscar to catch the words across forty yards of fog. But he spoke, and then he did something no mourner had ever done at that grave in all the years of anonymous June flowers: he laughed. A short, bitter bark of a sound, swallowed by the mist.

Then he turned to go, and his eyes swept the tree line out of pure habit—an old man's caution, the reflex of someone who had spent a lifetime checking exits—and Oscar felt the moment tip. The gaze passed over the bench, over the shadow, over him.

And stopped.

For three heartbeats neither of them moved. Then the old man said, clearly enough to carry, "You can come out or you can freeze. Either way, I've seen you."

Oscar considered his options, all two of them, and rose from the bench.

* * *

They met halfway, between the yew and the obelisk, two shadows negotiating in the gray light. Up close the man was older than his posture suggested—eighty, maybe more—with a network of broken veins across his cheeks and hands that trembled slightly around the cane.

"You're the hotel man," he said. "Hartwell. I've watched you watch people for twenty years. You're very good." A dry pause. "I'm better."

"Are you," Oscar said.

"I was leaving flowers before you were born, son." The old man's mouth twitched. "Well. Nearly."

"Who are you?"

The question hung there. Somewhere beyond the fence a mourning dove started up, three notes, over and over, like a question itself.

The old man looked down at the lilies, then back at the hole in the earth where a body should have been. When he answered, his voice had changed—stripped of the performance, down to something raw.

"My name is Thomas Reyes."

The ground seemed to shift under Oscar's feet. Not Voss. Never Voss.

"Daniel Reyes was my father," the old man went on. "Edmund Voss was the man who killed him. And I am the reason—" he tapped the headstone with his cane, once, hard, "—that this grave has been lying to your town since 1962."

* * *

They walked. The old man wouldn't stay in the open; he steered them along the cemetery's back path, through a gap in the hedge that only groundskeepers used, toward the derelict caretaker's cottage at the property's edge. Inside it smelled of dust and mouse and decades of disuse, but the stove worked, and Thomas Reyes built a small fire with the competence of a man who had lived rough before.

"I was nine years old in June of '62," he said, crouched by the flames. "My father owned half the Meridian. Everyone forgets that now. They remember Voss built the town, but Voss built it with my father's money and my father's labor, and when the partnership soured—when my father finally opened the books and saw what Edmund had been doing—Edmund decided the books would never see daylight."

"He drowned him," Oscar said.

"In the reservoir. On a Tuesday night, after they'd argued in the boathouse. I know because I was there. Hiding, like children hide. I saw Edmund push him. I heard the splash." His hands were steady now, which frightened Oscar more than trembling would have. "Sixty-three years, Mr. Hartwell, and my hands have been steady ever since. It's what happens when the worst thing has already happened."

"And Voss faked his own death."

"A week later. Heart failure, the papers said. There was a closed casket—my mother wasn't allowed to view the body, which she always found strange, right up until the day she understood why. There was no body, Mr. Hartwell. There was a coffin full of sandbags and a deathbed letter written in Edmund's own hand, and a coroner named Aldous Finch who owed Edmund money and took the payment instead." He looked up. "You've been pulling threads. I can tell. You have the look of a man who's run out of coincidences."

"The coroner's grandson kept a confession," Oscar said carefully. "Unsent. It names Voss alive as late as 1998."

Thomas smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, because it was Edmund Voss's smile wearing another man's grief. "1998 is close. My father's murderer died in March of 2001, in a rented room in Port Ellison, under the name Walter Crane. Pancreatic cancer. Slow, and mean, and deserved." He fed a stick into the stove. "I visited him. Twice. Once to confirm he was dying, and once to hear him say it."

"Say what?"

"Everything." The word dropped into the small room like a stone into water. "In April of 2001, I sat in that rented room with a tape recorder I bought at a Radio Shack that isn't there anymore, and I asked Edmund Voss to account for himself, and he did. Forty-one minutes. He named the accounts he drained. He described the argument in the boathouse. He admitted the drowning, the fake death, the forged letter, the payments to Finch. He even laughed about the scholarship—they give it out every year in his name, don't they? To promising young citizens of Little Hawley?" The laugh again, that bitter bark. "He thought it was funny. Dying men get to be funny."

Oscar realized he had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle of it. He made himself start again.

"Where is the tape?"

"That," said Thomas Reyes, "is the wrong question."

"It's the only question."

"No." The old man rose, using the cane, and stood with his back to the stove fire, and for a moment the light made him seem taller than he was, a silhouette with his father's shoulders. "The right question is: who else knows it exists? Because here is what you don't understand yet, hotel man. I brought those lilies every June for sixty years not for Edmund Voss. For my father. Because that grave sits on top of the truth, and somebody ought to mourn the truth even if nobody buries it. And in all those years, I was careful. I wore disguises. I varied my days. And I was never followed, never caught, never known—until this month, when the town decided to dig him up and found nothing, and suddenly everyone in Little Hawley with something to lose started looking at that grave the way I've been looking at it for six decades."

He limped closer. Up close his eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and absolutely unblinking.

"Somebody followed me last week. A man in a gray sedan, parked across from my boarding house in Corning, two hours from here. He didn't know I made him. But I've been making tails since before he was born." He let that sit. "Colleen Voss-Drayton knows her great-grandfather's coffin is empty. She knows the story doesn't hold. What she doesn't know yet is whether anyone alive can prove what happened. And there is exactly one thing in this world that can."

"The tape," Oscar said quietly.

"The tape." Thomas nodded slowly. "Forty-one minutes of a dead founder confessing to murder. Do you understand what that recording is worth? Not money. More than money. It's worth the statue coming down and the scholarship ending and the Voss name becoming a curse instead of a brand. It's worth prison for whoever helped cover it up, if any of them are still breathing. And it's worth killing for, Mr. Hartwell, because the people who inherit a lie will defend it harder than the people who committed it. The murderer is dead. The heirs are not."

Oscar thought of the note on his pillow. *Some coffins stay shut for a reason.* Thought of his slashed tires. Thought of Ruth Alvarez flinching at the portrait in the lobby, humming a song older than either of them, mopping the same floor she'd been hiding on for forty years.

"There's someone else you should meet," he said.

"I know who you mean." Something moved behind the old man's face—not softness exactly, but its foundation. "Ruth. Danny's little girl. I've known where she was for longer than you'd believe. Why do you think I never came to her? Why do you think I stayed away from this town except for one hour, one day, once a year?" He gripped the cane with both hands. "Because everyone who gets close to that truth ends up in the ground or in the wind. My mother. Her father, the groundskeeper. Half the people who might have spoken. I wasn't going to lead the gray sedan to her door."

"It may already know where her door is."

The words landed, and Oscar watched the old man absorb them—the flinch, the recalculation, the fear arriving late but fully, the same way it had arrived for Oscar himself a week ago. Thomas Reyes crossed to the cottage window and looked out at the brightening morning, at the mist burning off the graves, and when he spoke again his voice was low and level and final.

"Then we're out of time. Listen to me, Hartwell, because I'll only say this once. The tape exists. It has existed for twenty-four years inside a place so obvious that no one has ever thought to look. Edmund told me where, at the end, because he wanted it found eventually—he wanted credit, even posthumous, even infamous. *Let them dig me up twice,* he said. *Once out of the ground and once out of the lie.*" He turned from the window. "But I'm eighty-two years old, I sleep badly, and I drive slowly, and there is a gray sedan that has already found me once. If anything happens to me—if I take a fall down a staircase, if my brakes fail on Route 9—the tape dies with me unless someone else knows where it is."

"So tell me."

"Not here. Not now." He snuffed the stove fire with the toe of his boot, quick and practiced. "Tomorrow. Bring the girl. Both of you, together, so that no one of us holds the whole truth alone—that's how it survives us. There's a place off the old mill road, the fishing cabin on Tannery Pond. Noon." He was already moving toward the back door, cane tapping, and the morning outside had gone fully light, which meant he'd waited too long and knew it. At the threshold he paused, and looked back, and for one moment the performance fell away entirely and Oscar saw just an old orphan, nine years old forever, standing at the edge of the only grave that mattered.

"Hartwell," he said. "One more thing. The man in the gray sedan—he wasn't following the flowers. He was following *me*. Which means someone gave him my name. And there are only three people alive who know it."

He counted them off on trembling fingers: "Me. You. And whoever read the coroner's grandson's letter before you did."

The door closed behind him. Oscar stood alone in the cold cottage, the fire's smell fading, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the mist lifted.

He had found the coroner's grandson eleven days ago. He had told no one. No one except—

His blood went cold, all at once, the way it does when the body understands before the mind consents to.

Someone had gotten to the grandson's house first. Someone had read the confession, learned of Thomas Reyes, and set a tail on an old man with a cane—all while smiling across a desk and offering Oscar a generous consulting fee and a tidy verdict about drifters.

Outside, far off, on the county road, an engine started. Gray, quiet, patient.

Noon tomorrow. Tannery Pond. And between now and then, Oscar had to warn Ruth—because if Colleen Voss-Drayton knew a witness was breathing, then the night cleaner of the Hotel Meridian had just become the most dangerous person in Little Hawley.

He was moving before the thought finished, running through the wet grass, an invisible man suddenly very much afraid of being seen.
