## 3. Heirs and Alibis

The Voss house sat at the top of Lantern Hill like a verdict nobody had ever appealed — grey granite, black shutters, windows tall enough to watch the whole town and judge it. Ulyana Dravenmoor climbed the drive at nine in the morning, the fog still clinging to the hedges in ropes, and thought about how a house like this didn't shelter a family so much as archive one. Generations of Voss faces hung in the entry hall beyond the door, each portrait lit by its own small lamp, each gaze angled slightly downward, as if the painter had been instructed to capture them mid-judgment.

Odile Voss received her in the morning room, standing, because sitting would have conceded something. She was eighty-one years old and built like the town's founding charter — thin, brittle-looking, and utterly load-bearing. Her hands were folded at her waist. Her eyes were the pale grey of a January sky, and they did not flicker when Ulyana introduced herself and set the two letters on the table between them.

"You've come about the letters," Odile said. Not a question. "The sheriff called ahead. He said you'd come poking."

"The sheriff called ahead," Ulyana agreed, "which tells me the family has nothing to hide and wants everyone to know it."

Something moved at the corner of Odile's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been the muscle memory of a smile she'd worn so long at so many funerals and galas that it now operated independently of feeling.

"Sit down, Coroner. You'll want to. This will take a moment, and I'm told standing listeners retain less." She waited until Ulyana sat. "You have a letter from Elias Marsh. I know because his nurse telephoned my granddaughter's husband, who telephoned me, because in this town a secret travels faster by telephone than it ever did by mouth. Elias Marsh is ninety-four years old, his hands shake so badly he can't feed himself, and you expect me to believe he wrote a confession."

"I don't expect you to believe anything," Ulyana said. "I expect you to tell me where you were on the night of December the ninth, 1995."

And there it was — the flicker. Not in the eyes. In the hands. The folded fingers tightened by a single degree, the way a rigging tightens before a storm takes the mast.

"I was at the Crane house," Odile said. "The whole circle of us. We were planning the first Founders' Trust dinner, if you can believe it — thirty years ago this winter, and we were already planning anniversaries. Ada was meant to come. She didn't. We assumed she'd been kept away by the snow."

"The same snow that closed the coast road."

"The same snow."

"Who else was there?"

"Julian Crane. Marguerite and Edmund Bellamy. My late husband, Aldous. And myself." She recited it the way other women recited recipes — smooth, oiled, worn to a groove. "Five of us, playing cards until the power failed, then talking by firelight until dawn. You're welcome to ask any of us. We've all told the story a hundred times. It hasn't changed."

*It hasn't changed.* Ulyana wrote nothing down, and watched Odile notice that, and watched her decide not to be troubled by it. "Mrs. Voss, do you know a woman named Ada Wrenlow?"

"I knew a girl named Ada Wrenlow." The pale eyes finally came up and met Ulyana's, and they were not cruel, exactly. They were worse than cruel. They were *tidy*. "She worked at the cannery. She was clever. She wanted things she hadn't earned. She left town that winter, as I understand it, and no one has seen her since. Is there anything else? I have a dinner to plan. Thirty years, Coroner. Do you know how difficult it is to get good candle wax in this quantity on short notice?"

Ulyana did not know what Odile Voss had done to survive the winter of '95. But she knew what she was looking at now: a woman who had rehearsed a sentence until it had replaced the memory underneath it. The story wasn't a story anymore. It was load-bearing. Pull one brick and the whole house came down on everyone inside it.

"I'll find my own way out," Ulyana said.

"I'm sure you will," said Odile Voss. "Everyone in this town eventually does."

* * *

Julian Crane found Ulyana before she found him — or rather, she found his car first, parked crooked in the gravel lot behind the Anchor & Ale at eleven in the morning, and found him in the last booth, a tumbler of something amber in front of him and a second tumbler's worth already gone somewhere.

He was fifty-eight and had his mother's jaw and none of her discipline. He looked up when she slid into the booth across from him, and his expression performed surprise the way a bad actor performs it — a beat too slow, a degree too wide.

"Coroner Dravenmoor." He lifted the glass in something adjacent to a salute. "To what do I owe the — no. Let me guess. The letters."

"Everyone knows about the letters."

"Everyone knows about everything. That's the disease of this town." He drank. His hand was steady, which told her he'd been practicing for years. "You want to know where I was in December of '95. Everyone wants to know where everyone was in December of '95, now that Elias Marsh has taken up confession-writing from his deathbed."

"Where were you, Mr. Crane?"

"At my father's house. Cards. Firelight. The whole cozy tableau." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, because underneath it was something that looked almost like relief — the relief of a man who has said the words so many times they've lost all taste, like a Communion wafer chewed ten thousand times. "You know what the funny thing is, Coroner? I can't remember a single hand of cards we played. Not one. But I can recite the alibi like scripture. *Five of us, playing cards until the power failed, then talking by firelight until dawn.* Ask me my own daughter's birthday and I have to think. Ask me that night and it comes out smooth as a hymn."

"Who taught you the hymn?"

The glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

For a moment — one long, fog-bound moment — Julian Crane looked at her with something raw and drowning in his face, and Ulyana thought: *there it is, the crack, the one that runs all the way down.* Then he set the glass down, and the rawness closed over like water.

"Nobody taught me anything," he said. "It's just what happened. It's what we all remember." He turned the tumbler in a slow circle on the scarred wood. "You should talk to the Bellamys. The old ones are dead — car accident, years back, both of them at once, very tragic, very convenient, people said. But there's a granddaughter. Ivy. She's the one with the Trust now, or a piece of it." He drained the glass and stood, and his voice dropped to something almost human. "She was a child, you know. In '95. She was six years old. Whatever the rest of us were doing that winter, she was six." He put money on the table, too much of it. "Be gentle with her, Coroner. She's the only one of us who's still scared of the right things."

He walked out into the fog, and Ulyana sat a moment longer, looking at the ring his glass had left on the wood, and thought: *he's waiting to be arrested. He's been waiting thirty years. He drinks at eleven in the morning because the waiting is the punishment and no one will ever pronounce the sentence.*

* * *

Ivy Bellamy lived in a narrow house on Salt Lane with a garden gone wild and a wind chime made of sea glass that spoke in small broken syllables when Ulyana knocked. She was thirty-six and looked younger until you reached her eyes, which had the particular exhausted clarity of someone who has not slept properly in a week. She opened the door only as far as the chain allowed, and when she saw the coroner's badge, the chain came off faster than it had gone on.

"You got one too," she said. "A letter. Don't you? That's why you're here."

"May I come in?"

The house was clean the way anxious houses are clean — everything squared, nothing lived-in-looking, as if the whole rooms had been held breath for days. She made tea and didn't drink hers. She sat with her hands wrapped around the cup as though the cup were the only warm thing in the world.

"I found the letter Tuesday," she said. "In the postbox. No stamp. Someone walked it up the lane." Her voice was level, but her thumb kept moving over the rim of the cup, around and around. "It had words in it. Words I've heard my whole life at every Trust dinner. *Five of us, playing cards until the power failed, then talking by firelight until dawn.* That's the toast. That's the official story. We say it every year, Coroner. We raise a glass and say it, and everyone in town thinks it's charming, this little ritual the founders kept, and it's not charming. It's a *lock*. We're all saying the same sentence into the same lock, every year, turning it."

"Who taught you the toast?"

"My grandmother. When I was nineteen and inherited my parents' share. She sat me down at this table and she made me say it back to her until I got the words exactly right. Exactly. She said, *the words matter, Ivy, the words have always mattered.*" Ivy looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "I was six years old in 1995. I don't remember anything. I don't remember that winter, I don't remember Ada Wrenlow, I don't remember anything except that the grown-ups in my family went quiet every December the way houses go quiet before something falls over. And now someone is sending me the lock in the mail, and I'm the only one in my family who's *frightened*, and I don't know if that makes me the innocent one or just the last one to be told."

Ulyana set the two letters on the table — the forged confession, the anonymous promise. Ivy read them with her lips slightly parted, and when she reached the line about the anniversary dinner, her hand went to her mouth.

"The dinner's in four days," she whispered. "The thirtieth. Everyone will be there. Everyone in town."

"I know."

"Odile won't cancel it. I asked. She said —" Ivy's voice caught, and she imitated the old woman's flint with unnerving precision: "*We have never once cancelled, and we will not begin for the sake of an anonymous crank.*" She pushed the letters back across the table. "Coroner Dravenmoor. I don't know what my family did. I want you to believe that. But I know they did *something*, because I've watched them rehearse being innocent my entire life, and nobody rehearses that hard for something that never happened."

Outside, the sea-glass chime stirred in a wind that hadn't reached the town yet, and said nothing useful.

Ulyana walked back down Salt Lane through the thickening fog with three testimonies in her head and one sentence in all of them, word for word, comma for comma, smooth as a hymn. Five heirs, thirty years, and a single sentence worn into all their mouths like a key cut from the same blank.

Someone had taught them the words. Someone with the authority to make a whole room repeat a sentence until it replaced the truth. Someone whose profession was, above all things, the careful use of exact language.

She had an interview of her own to schedule, she realized. With a retired coroner, in a shuttered cottage at the edge of the marsh, whose doctrine she had built her whole career upon.

*Every grave deserves the truth,* Ambrose Kettle had taught her.

She was beginning to understand which graves he had meant.
