## 10. Edith's Bargain

Edith Vane had dressed for the meeting the way she dressed for funerals: carefully, and in colors that would not be remembered.

Grey wool, pearl buttons, the small garnet brooch her mother had worn to every vestry dinner for forty years. She stood at the window of her house on Chandler's Row and watched the rain come down in the fine, patient way it did in this city, the kind of rain that did not fall so much as settle, as though the sky had decided long ago that Saint Alder's deserved nothing more dramatic than a slow dampening. Across the street, the cathedral's west tower rose into the murk, its stones going black with water, its clock face a pale coin in the grey.

Three minutes past eleven. The archivist was punctual. Of course she was. Edith had watched Mara Sterling for fifteen years the way one watches a clock one cannot stop: with a certain grim respect, and the constant low knowledge that it would, eventually, strike.

She had chosen her own house for the meeting because she refused to be summoned anywhere. Not the cathedral—never the cathedral, not for this. Not the archive with its smelling-shelves and its filing cabinets that clicked shut like small jaws. Edith Vane had spent the better part of her life in rooms where other people set the terms, and she had learned, late and at cost, that the only ground worth standing on was ground you had swept yourself.

The doorbell rang. A clear, small sound, swallowed at once by the rain.

Edith did not hurry. She took one last look at the room—the fire laid but unlit, the two chairs set at an angle that was neither intimate nor adversarial, the tea service on the low table with the good porcelain, the kind with the hairline crack in the saucer that only she knew was there. She had debated the fire. Lit, it suggested warmth she did not feel and could not honestly offer. Unlit, it suggested a chill she very much intended. She left it unlit.

She opened the door herself.

"Miss Vane," Mara Sterling said.

"Miss Sterling." Edith stepped back to let her in. "You came alone. I confess I half expected the verger lurking at my gate like a heron."

"He doesn't know I'm here."

"No," Edith said, closing the door against the wet. "I imagine he doesn't."

* * *

She took the archivist's coat and hung it in the hall, noting—because noting things was the one habit she and Mara Sterling shared, though Edith had never been fool enough to call it a virtue—that the girl's hands were steady and her eyes were not. There was a particular look that came into a person's eyes when they had spent a week turning over stones. Edith had seen it before, fifteen years ago, on faces she had since learned to see in her sleep. The look of someone who had lifted a rock and found not the clean dark they expected but something pale and soft beneath, something with a shape.

"Sit, please. Tea?"

"Thank you."

Edith poured. The tea was Darjeeling, the second flush, from a tin she had bought the morning after Mara's note arrived, and she had bought it deliberately, because Darjeeling was what one served when one wished to signal that this was a conversation between civilized people, and civilized people, Edith had found, could be persuaded of almost anything if the porcelain was thin enough and the light was warm enough. She handed the cup across. Their fingers did not touch. She had arranged that too.

"You wanted to talk," Mara said. "About Thomas Wren."

Edith let the silence sit for a moment, the way one lets a note decay before playing the next. Outside, the rain worked at the glass. Somewhere down the street a delivery van groaned and was gone.

"I wanted," Edith said, "to talk to you before you talked to anyone else. I understand you've been to see the Vane pew's old friends. Mrs. Ashcombe, I hear, was her usual self."

"She was."

"And you've been in the archive. The restoration fund ledgers." Edith watched the girl's face. There it was—the flicker, quickly mastered, the small muscular flinch of a professional caught holding a card she thought was hidden. "Don't trouble yourself denying it. I was on the finance committee for twenty-two years. I know exactly what the ledgers for 1987 look like, and I know exactly what they looked like before they were made to look like that."

Mara set her cup down with a click so small it was almost nothing. "Then you know there are gaps."

"I know there are gaps the way a woman knows her own house has been robbed. Not because she saw it done, but because the silver isn't where the silver lives." Edith folded her hands in her lap. "Forty-one thousand pounds, give or take, over eighteen months. Siphoned out of the crypt restoration in quantities small enough to hide inside legitimate invoices and large enough to matter. Stone that was never laid. Timber that was never treated. A stained-glass commission paid twice to a glazier who only ever existed on paper. It was elegant work. I would say that, even now. Whoever did it understood that the best place to hide theft is inside generosity. No one audits a gift."

"Who?" Mara asked. No preamble. No softening. Edith almost smiled. The girl had the manners of a filing cabinet, and God help her, Edith respected that.

"You know who."

"I want to hear you say it."

"Then you want a great deal more than I've invited you here to give." Edith rose, went to the window, and stood with her back to the room. It was easier to say the next part to the glass, to the rain, to the black tower across the street. "Ambrose Hale took that money, Miss Sterling. I don't say it lightly, and I don't say it for nothing, and I will not say it again outside this room. He took it to cover debts—his brother's debts, if you want the whole ugly genealogy, though the brother was only the spigot and Ambrose was the hand on the tap. He was not yet dean then. He was a rising curate with a talent for making rich old women feel that giving to Saint Alder's was the same as giving to God, and I was one of the rich old women, and I liked him, and that is the part I have had fifteen years to be ashamed of. I liked him. He made giving feel like grace."

The rain filled the pause.

"And Thomas Wren found out," Mara said quietly.

Edith closed her eyes. Behind her lids she saw the cloister as it had been that October: the new stone pale as bone, the smell of lime mortar and wet leaves, and a boy's voice, high and bright and terrible with the particular courage of the twelve-year-old, saying *I saw the papers, Father. I saw them in your office when I was putting the hymnals back. I'm going to tell.*

"He was a choirboy," Edith said. "He sang like something the cathedral had grown out of its own stones, and he was honest the way only children and fools are honest, which is to say completely, and without any idea what honesty costs. He found the duplicate invoices. He went to Hale directly—no parents, no vestry, no one. He went to the man himself, because he trusted him. That is the detail I cannot get past, Miss Sterling. Fifteen years, and I cannot get past it. He trusted him."

"What happened?"

"You know what happened. Everyone knows what happened. The boy drowned. The pond in the deanery garden—no, the millpond, forgive me, the old one below the cloister walk. Slipped in the rain, the coroner said. A tragic accident. The vestry closed the file with a unanimity I have never seen before or since in that building, and believe me, I have watched that vestry argue for forty years about the color of pew cushions." Edith turned from the window. "The file was closed because four people agreed to close it. I was one of the four."

Mara had gone very still. It was the stillness of the archive itself, Edith thought—of a room where nothing is permitted to decay and nothing is permitted to be disturbed, where every truth has a box and every box has a label. She was looking at Edith the way she must look at a document with a forged seal: not with horror, not yet, but with the cold, gathering attention of someone who intends to know.

"Why?" Mara asked. "Why did you agree?"

"Because Ambrose came to each of us, one at a time, in the days after the boy died, and he did not threaten us. That was his genius. He grieved *with* us. He sat in this very room—your chair, as it happens—and wept, and said that if the truth came out, the boy's death would not have been an accident in the public memory but a scandal, and the Wren family would spend the rest of their lives as the family of the boy who caught the cathedral stealing. He said the kindest thing—the *kindest* thing—was to let Thomas be a boy who slipped in the rain. And he was right. That is the unforgivable part, Miss Sterling. He was right. The Wrens were a poor family, a scattered family, a family that came to Saint Alder's for the warmth and the free supper and the choir scholarship. What did we have to offer them instead? The truth. The truth that their son had been killed over forty-one thousand pounds of other people's money. I have told myself for fifteen years that we spared them. I have never once believed it."

The fire remained unlit. Edith's hands were cold. She sat back down, slowly, because her knees had begun to inform her of their age.

"So you know," Mara said. "You've known for fifteen years, and you've said nothing, and now—what? Now you've invited me here to confess to a crime you had no part in, out of the goodness of your heart?"

"No." Edith met her eyes. "I've invited you here because you've been pulling threads, and threads, once pulled, unravel the whole garment, and I am too old and too tired to watch it happen unmanaged. I am here to tell you where the thread ends."

"The crypt."

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well. Edith heard it go down and down without finding bottom.

"You've seen the seal," she said. "Fresh wax over the old lock. I know you've seen it, because I know you, Miss Sterling, better than you think. You are the only person in that cathedral who reads the building the way I read a balance sheet. You noticed within a day, and you have been circling it ever since like a moth around a window."

"The crypt was sealed by vestry order in 1987," Mara said. "At the close of the restoration. Standard practice—tools and materials locked in, inventory to follow. The inventory never followed."

"No. It didn't." Edith drew a breath, and the breath cost her. "Because the crypt does not contain tools and materials. It contains Thomas Wren's effects—his schoolbag, his hymnal, his coat with his name tape sewn in his mother's hand—and it contains the duplicate invoices, and the second ledger, the true one, and a letter. And it contains other things besides, things I was not told about and did not ask about, because asking is how one becomes complicit, and I had already become complicit in every way that mattered."

"Who sealed it?"

"Ambrose. With the vestry's blessing and my silence. He said the crypt should rest undisturbed, out of respect for the dead. Everyone wept at the beauty of it. The beloved curate, sealing the tools of his grief behind wax and iron." Edith's mouth twisted. "He sealed his own crime in the one room in Saint Alder's that no one would ever, ever open. It was the most arrogant thing I have ever witnessed a human being do. And the most secure. For fifteen years it has been the safest secret in this city, because the only people who know what the crypt holds are the people who want it to stay there, and I—" her voice caught, and she was furious at it, and mastered it, "—I have wanted it to stay there. Every day for fifteen years, I have wanted it."

The rain thickened against the glass. Mara had not touched her tea in some minutes; it sat cooling, a thin skin forming at its edge, and Edith thought: *there, that is the conversation, that skin, that slow stillness, that is what happens when a living thing stops moving.*

"Why are you telling me this now?" Mara asked.

"Because you are going to open that crypt whether I help you or not. I have watched you for three weeks. I know the set of your shoulders. You are your archive, Miss Sterling—you cannot leave a record false. It isn't courage. It's something older and less forgivable than courage. It's the need for the file to be *true*." Edith leaned forward. "So I am not going to waste both our time asking you to stop."

*But I am going to ask you something,* she thought. *And God forgive me, I am going to ask it well.*

* * *

She let the silence do its work. She had learned that from Ambrose Hale, and it shamed her, the way all her best instruments shamed her now, every tool in her hand stamped with the maker's name she wished to forget. Silence, poured slowly, was the strongest solvent in the world. People rushed to fill it. People confessed into it. People, given enough silence, would agree to almost anything just to hear a human voice again.

"Miss Sterling," Edith said at last, gently. "May I tell you what is on the other side of that door you want to open?"

"You may tell me anything. Whether I believe it is another matter."

"Fair. That's fair." Edith looked down at her hands, at the rings she had worn for fifty years, her wedding band and her mother's garnet, the small geology of a life. "Here is what happens. You break the seal—you'll do it properly, I have no doubt, some inventory audit, some legal fig leaf, because you are incapable of doing anything improperly except this—and you find the boy's things, and you find the ledger, and you find the invoices, and perhaps you even find the letter. And then what? You take it to the coroner. The case reopens. Ambrose Hale is arrested, or he flees, or he does something desperate in the interval, because a man with fifteen years of sainthood built on a grave is not a man who comes quietly. And Saint Alder's—"

She stopped. Started again, quieter.

"Saint Alder's will not survive it. I don't mean the stones. The stones will outlast us all; they always do. I mean the *thing itself*. The choir. The supper for the poor on Thursdays. The scholarship that put three Wren cousins through school. The two hundred people who come on a wet Sunday because it is the one warm room in their week. All of it is downstream of the founding families' money, and the founding families will not pour another penny into a cathedral that killed a child and called it rain. You will have the truth, Miss Sterling. You will have it in your hands, clean and complete and labeled. And you will watch everything it touches close. The doors. The fund. The Thursday supper. Ilsa's kitchen, Odell's verger's keys, Sef's organ—do you imagine the diocese keeps an organist on staff for an empty nave?"

"You think I should weigh a boy's murder against a soup kitchen."

"I think," Edith said, and her voice did not waver, because she had rehearsed this, she had rehearsed it in the bath and on the walk and in the dark at three in the morning, "that you should weigh it *honestly*, which is more than anyone else in this affair has ever done. I am not offering you comfort, Miss Sterling. I am not telling you the truth will hurt. I am telling you the truth will *cost*, and I am telling you who will pay, and it will not be Ambrose Hale, and it will not be me, and it will not, in the end, even be you. It will be the poorest souls in that building. The boy is dead. He has been dead fifteen years. Nothing in that crypt will make him less dead. But there are people alive today who eat because Saint Alder's feeds them, and they are not dead, and they will be the ones who pay for your clean file."

Mara was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was low and even.

"Is that what you tell yourself? Or is it what Hale told all of you, and you've been keeping it warm ever since?"

Edith laughed—a short, ugly sound that surprised her out of her own chest. "Both. That is the devil's whole trick, Miss Sterling, and I want you to understand that I know it is the devil's trick. The best lie is a true thing told at the wrong weight. The supper *is* real. The scholarship *is* real. And none of it—*none of it*—is worth a murdered child, and I know that, and I have known it for fifteen years, and I have said nothing, because I am a coward who balances books." She set down her own untouched cup. "So no. I am not asking you to believe me. I am asking you to do one thing, and I will ask it plainly, because you deserve plainness even if I have never deserved it."

"Ask."

"Leave the crypt sealed. Not for Hale. Not for the families. For the boy." Edith's voice dropped. "He has lain quiet for fifteen years, Miss Sterling. The whole city has agreed to let him rest. His mother, before she died, told me—told *me*, at the church fete, over the jam stall, with her poor cracked hands around a paper cup—that the one mercy God had given her was that her son's death was an accident. That she could not have borne it being anything else. She is dead now, but her mercy is not. It is sitting in that crypt under wax and iron, and if you open that door, you will take it from her. You will take it from everyone. And the file will be true, and the truth will be a stone around every neck in that building, and Thomas Wren will still be dead."

The rain ran down the window in long, silver threads. Somewhere in the house a clock ticked, patient as water dripping into a cistern.

Edith watched the archivist across the cold tea and the unlit fire, and she saw the temptation land. She saw it land because she had built it to land—had built it from true things, from the supper and the scholarship and the dead mother's cracked hands—and she watched it settle over Mara Sterling's face like frost over glass, and she thought: *perhaps. Perhaps it will hold her. Perhaps the girl loves that cathedral more than she loves the truth, and perhaps love is heavier.*

Mara stood.

"Thank you for the tea, Miss Vane."

"Miss Sterling—"

"You're right about one thing. I can't leave a record false." Mara took her coat from the chair back, and her hands, Edith noticed, were steady now—all of her was steady, all of her had gone quiet and hard and bright, like a blade laid flat on a table. "But you should know something, since you've been so honest with me. I don't need the crypt to be lying to you. I already know it isn't. And I didn't come here today to decide whether to open it."

Edith went cold. "Then why did you come?"

Mara paused at the door, and for a moment she looked very young, and very tired, and very much like a girl who had once hidden in the dark from something terrible—and then it was gone, filed away, labeled, shut.

"To find out," she said, "whether you'd try to stop me. You didn't. You just tried to make me want to stop myself." She pulled the door open onto the rain. "That's the difference between you and him, Miss Vane. I'd think about that, if I were you. You still have time to be on the right side of the file."

The door closed. Edith stood alone in the cold room, in the silence she had built so carefully, and heard, faint through the rain, the cathedral clock begin to strike eleven-thirty—one slow bronze note at a time, counting down toward something.

She looked at the two cups of untouched tea going cold on the table, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the archivist had come here not for the confession at all.

She had come to see whether Edith Vane would warn him.

And Edith, standing in her careful grey dress with her mother's garnet at her throat, realized with dawning horror that by tonight, someone else would already know that she had talked.
