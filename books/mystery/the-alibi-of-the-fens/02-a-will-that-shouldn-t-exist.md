## 2. A Will That Shouldn't Exist

The rain had followed me back from the meeting, a thin gray drizzle that made the city look like something developing slowly in a darkroom. I climbed the three flights to my office — Delacroix & Associates, which was a generous name for one lawyer and an assistant who did everything else — and let the door fall shut behind me with its familiar tired click.

The office was cluttered the way a workshop is cluttered: case files stacked like sedimentary rock, a dead orchid I kept meaning to revive, Marisol's collection of mismatched mugs colonizing every horizontal surface. It smelled of old paper and burnt coffee. It smelled, in other words, like the only place in the world that had ever felt like it belonged to me.

I set the envelope on my desk and looked at it for a long moment before touching it again.

Clients left things behind all the time. Contracts, photographs, jewelry once — a woman had left a funeral urn on my desk for eleven days because she couldn't decide whether her husband counted as settled or pending. But there was something about this envelope, this plain manila rectangle with no return address, that made me treat it like evidence rather than paperwork.

I put on gloves. Not because I expected fingerprints to matter. Because the gesture steadied me. Ritual before revelation.

Inside: the key, brass and heavy, cold as a coin from a riverbed. And the will.

It was typed on paper that had gone the color of weak tea at the edges. The header read:

**LAST WILL AND TESTAMENT OF E. M. ALDERSEY**

Dated the fourteenth of November, 1994.

I read it twice standing up, then sat down and read it a third time, slower, the way you'd re-read a letter from someone you thought had stopped writing to you years ago.

The language was formal, old-fashioned, almost liturgical. *I, Edith Miriam Aldersey, being of sound mind and deliberate purpose, do hereby declare...* Deliberate purpose. Most wills say *sound mind and memory*. This one wanted you to know the mind wasn't just sound. It was aimed.

The provisions were strange in a way I couldn't immediately name. There were bequests to family members — Ambrose Aldersey, Camille Aldersey, others whose names meant nothing to me yet — but each bequest carried a condition like a fishhook buried in bait. Ambrose would receive the east lodge *provided he has spoken truthfully at each anniversary dinner*. Camille would receive the ledgers and accounts *provided she has kept them faithfully and produced them upon demand*. Everything given, everything conditional. A will written like a trap for liars.

And then the final clause. The one the stranger had underlined in his own hand, or paid someone to underline, the ink slightly different, newer:

*To the beneficiary named herein, should she die before the thirtieth anniversary dinner of the Hollow Fen estate, I leave the whole of my estate, unencumbered, together with the truth of what occurred in the year of my departure.*

*Should she live past it, this instrument is void, and the truth dies with me.*

Beneficiary named herein. Except no name appeared anywhere in the body of the document. I checked. I went through it line by line with a magnifying glass I keep in the top drawer for exactly this kind of paranoia, and the beneficiary clause referred only to *she*, only to *the beneficiary named herein*, and the herein was empty. A will that named its beneficiary nowhere except in a witness line I hadn't looked at yet.

Because I was saving it. Some instinct, some lawyer's superstition, told me the end of the document was where the real document lived.

I turned to the last page.

Execution and attestation. Signed, *E. M. Aldersey*, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, urgent, like someone writing against a clock. Below it, two witnesses. The first: *Nell Fairweather*, in careful schoolroom script. The second —

I stopped breathing. Not dramatically. Not the way people gasp in novels. My lungs simply declined the next instruction, the way a machine declines a command it doesn't recognize.

The second witness line read: **V. DELACROIX**.

Not a first name. An initial and a surname. My surname. The surname I had taken at eighteen, off a form, out of a phone book, because the state needed me to have one and nobody else cared enough to give me theirs. Delacroix. A name I had chosen the way you choose a coat off a clearance rack — because it fit, because it was there, because no one was watching.

Except apparently someone had been watching. In 1994. Twenty-six years ago. When I was — I did the arithmetic without wanting to — when I was nine years old.

Nine-year-olds don't witness wills. Nine-year-olds don't have surnames yet, not mine, not this one. The name on that page was either the most impossible coincidence in the history of English probate law, or it was a message, folded flat and filed away, waiting twenty-six years for someone to open the envelope.

My hands were shaking. I noticed it the way you notice weather — remotely, as a fact about the room. I set the will down on the blotter and pressed my palms flat against the desk until the tremor ran out of them like current into ground.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Marisol said from the doorway.

Marisol Reyes had been my assistant for six years, which in assistant years is roughly a marriage. She was thirty-one, chronically over-caffeinated, and possessed of the rare professional gift of knowing when to pretend not to hear things. She also had an unerring radar for my tells. She claimed I had seven. I claimed I had none. We had never resolved it because she could produce the list.

"I'm not doing any face."

"The squinting one. Like the document owes you money." She came in carrying two mugs, set one on my desk — the chipped blue one, mine by treaty — and leaned over my shoulder without asking, which was also by treaty. "What've we got? Dead guy?"

"Dead woman. Maybe."

"Maybe-dead women are the best kind. They bill better." She picked up the will before I could stop her, and I watched her eyes travel down the page with the speed of someone who reads contracts recreationally, which she did, God help her, for fun. "Ooh, conditional bequests. Somebody hated their family." Then she reached the attestation block, and her eyebrows went up. "Vesper Delacroix. Witness. That's you."

"That's not me."

"It's got your name on it."

"It has letters on it. Letters are cheap." I took the will back from her, more gently than I intended. "Look at the notarization stamp."

She looked. Even Marisol, who trusted me the way you trust gravity, went quiet for a second.

The stamp was round, embossed deep enough to catch shadow. *County of Marsh Hollow — Commission No. 4471 — Notary Public.* And beneath the commission number, in small type, the expiration: *Commission expires December 31, 1997.*

"What's wrong with it?" she asked, though I could see her working it out even as she asked, her lips moving through the dates.

"The commission number format didn't exist in 1994. The state switched to alphanumeric commissions in 1996. This says four-four-seven-one, all numerals, in the post-'96 style, stamped on a document dated November '94." I tapped the embossing. "And the seal matrix itself is wrong. Look at the border. Double-line with star breaks. That design entered use in 1998. Whoever stamped this used a seal that wouldn't be manufactured for four years after the date on the page."

"So it's forged."

"So it's *impossible*." I heard my own voice come out sharper than the facts warranted. "A forgery tries to look right. This doesn't try. This is a document dated 1994, stamped with equipment from 1998, witnessed by a woman who may or may not exist and a child who definitely didn't —"

"A child with your name."

"— and notarized by a ghost." I made myself stop. Made myself take the blue mug and drink coffee that had gone lukewarm and bitter. "It's a hoax, Marisol. Someone built this to get a lawyer's attention. It worked. That's all it is."

Marisol looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't file under anything. Then she shrugged, theatrical, and dropped onto the corner of my desk the way she did when she'd decided a conversation wasn't over, merely adjourned.

"Sure. Hoax. Somebody spent real money on period paper, aged it professionally — feel those edges, that's not a toaster oven, that's a specialist — tracked down or fabricated a notary seal, and hand-wrote a witness signature matching the surname of the exact lawyer they wanted to hire." She blew on her coffee. "All to scam you out of — what did the weirdo pay you?"

"Double my retainer. Up front. Cashier's check."

"Right. A con artist's dream: paying extra, in advance, in the most traceable form of payment that isn't cash, for services rendered proving a murder *didn't happen*." She shook her head slowly. "Vesper. If it's a hoax, it's the least profitable hoax ever constructed. If it's not a hoax —"

"Then it's a joke somebody's playing, and jokes don't scare me."

"I didn't say joke." She was quiet now, and quiet Marisol was rarer and worse than loud Marisol. "I said hoax. A hoax is funny. This—" she nodded at the will, at the tea-colored paper, at the impossible stamp gleaming its little metallic smirk — "this isn't trying to be funny. This is trying to be *found*. There's a difference. A forger hides. Whoever made this left the seams showing on purpose. Wrong stamp, wrong year, your name sitting right there in the open. They wanted whoever opened this envelope to start asking questions."

"Then they wanted the wrong lawyer. I don't chase ghosts."

"No?" She stood, collected her mug, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "You've been holding that thing with gloves on for an hour. You re-read it four times. You're doing the face, and you haven't touched the blue mug until just now, and you always touch the blue mug first." She tapped the doorframe twice, her little percussion of farewell. "Whoever wrote that will knew a Delacroix. Think about what that means, and then tell me it's nothing."

The door clicked shut behind her, tired hinge and all, and I was alone with the paper and the rain.

* * *

I worked the rest of the afternoon the way I work everything: methodically, compulsively, building a wall of procedure between myself and the thing on the desk.

I photographed every page. I catalogued the paper stock — laid finish, watermarked, a mill that had closed in 2001, so the stock itself was plausibly period-correct, which meant whoever made this either had genuine old stock or extraordinary patience. I checked the typewriter font against reference books. Elite pica, consistent lift on the lowercase e, a machine from roughly the right era, or a computer font good enough to fool me, which very few were.

Everything was almost right. That was the thing I kept circling back to. Almost right in ways that were too precise to be accident and too visible to be incompetence. The will was a document designed to pass casual inspection and fail expert inspection — and to fail it *specifically*, at specific points, like a trail of breadcrumbs baked into bread.

Wrong stamp. Right era. Empty beneficiary clause. My name in the witnesses.

I am not a sentimental person. Sentiment is a liability in my profession; clients pay you to be the coldest head in the room. And my history — such as it was — had taught me early that the past is a country best visited rarely, if at all. There was nothing there. That was the whole point of my history: there was nothing there. A group home in a county I don't name, a caseworker whose face I've lost, a folder of records I read once at nineteen and never opened again because it contained no answers, only forms. I was born in paperwork and raised by procedure. Before the paperwork, nothing. I had built a career, a life, a self on that foundation: *before the paperwork, nothing.*

So why did my hands shake?

I kept coming back to the question the way your tongue returns to a broken tooth. And around six o'clock, with the rain gone silver in the window light and the office sinking into that blue hour where files turn to shadows, I finally admitted what was actually bothering me.

It wasn't the stamp. Stamps can be stolen, seals can be faked, paper can be aged. It wasn't even the name, not exactly — Delacroix is uncommon but not unique; there are Delacroixes in three states; coincidence is the load-bearing wall of every conspiracy theory.

It was the handwriting.

I spread the last page under the desk lamp and looked at the second witness line again. *V. Delacroix.* The letters were small, cramped, careful — the handwriting of someone young, or someone pretending to be. And the V had a particular habit I recognized because I saw it every day of my life, in my own signature, in my own notes, in the margins of every brief I'd ever drafted: the downstroke started with a tiny backward hook, a hesitation, like the pen had to talk itself into committing.

Nobody copies that. Nobody knows about that. I'd never noticed it myself until this moment, and I have been signing documents professionally for fifteen years.

The hook was there. On a page dated 1994. In ink that had faded the way only old ink fades.

I sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the street noise rose and fell like breathing.

Here is what I knew, and what I knew I knew, underneath the lawyer and the procedure and the wall: someone had written my name — my secret, chosen, invented name — on a legal document twenty-two years before I chose it. Which meant it wasn't invented. Which meant the choice was a memory I didn't have. Which meant the blank page at the start of my life might not be blank at all.

It might be erased. And erasure leaves marks.

I looked at the brass key lying on the blotter beside the will. Heavy, old, unremarkable — the kind of key that opens one specific door and no other. The stranger had said the fen would explain it. The fen, the estate, the anniversary dinner six days away, the murder that hadn't happened yet.

I should have refused the case. Every instinct I trust said refuse it: the client was a cipher, the document was impossible, the fee was a bribe aimed at my curiosity, and curiosity, in my experience, is how lawyers end up as footnotes. I have never lost a case because I have never taken a case I couldn't win, and this one had losing written all over it in invisible ink.

But my hands had shaken. And Marisol had seen it. And somewhere under the adrenaline and the dread was something older and less dignified — the hunger that had signed me up for law school, for every hopeless appeal, for every client nobody else would take. The hunger to be needed by something true.

I pulled a fresh file folder, wrote *ALDERSEY / HOLLOW FEN* across the tab, and slid the will inside. Then I picked up the phone and booked a car to the fen country for the morning.

The last thing I did, before turning off the lamp, was stand at the window and watch the rain erase the city street by street, and think about a woman named Edith Miriam Aldersey who had signed her will in a hurry, in ink, in 1994 — the same year, I realized with a cold slow drop in my stomach, that a nine-year-old child in a group home three hundred miles away had been given new placement papers and a new silence to live in.

Same year. Same autumn, if the date held. November.

Whatever happened at Hollow Fen in November of 1994, it had reached across three hundred miles and thirty years and put its finger on the one page of my life I'd never been able to read.

In the morning, I was going to go stand on the page.
