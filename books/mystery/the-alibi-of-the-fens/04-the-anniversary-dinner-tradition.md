## 4. The Anniversary Dinner Tradition

Nell found me the next morning in the east sitting room, where I'd been pretending to read a book about drainage that Ambrose had left on the table like a warning.

"Tea," she said. It wasn't an offer. It was a diagnosis. "You've got the look of someone who's been running on hotel coffee and suspicion."

"I have been running on hotel coffee and suspicion," I admitted.

"Then come to the kitchen. Nobody eavesdrops properly in a sitting room. Too many doors."

I followed her down the back stairs, through a corridor that smelled of beeswax and old stone, into a kitchen that was the first honest room I'd seen at Hollow Fen. Copper pots hung from hooks worn bright by decades of hands. A bread board bore the grooves of ten thousand loaves. The window over the sink looked out not at the sculpted gardens but at the fen itself — flat, silvered, indifferent — and I understood that Nell had arranged her life so she could watch it while she worked. You keep your eye on the thing that took something from you. Anyone who'd grown up near water knew that.

She set a pot on the trivet, two cups, milk in a jug with a chip on the lip that someone had loved enough not to throw away.

"Now," she said, pouring. "You'll want to ask me questions, Mr. Delacroix, and I'll answer some of them. But first you need to understand the dinner. Because everything in this house bends around the dinner, and if you don't know that, you'll misread every face you see."

"The anniversary dinner," I said. "Ambrose mentioned it. He didn't mention much else."

"Ambrose mentions nothing else because there's nothing else he's allowed." She sat across from me, folded her hands around her own cup, and for a moment she looked less like a housekeeper and more like a woman deciding how much of a wall to take down. "Every ten years. This year makes four."

"Four dinners. Forty years."

"Forty years since the first one. Thirty since the last." Her eyes went to the window, to the fen. "The first was Edith's idea. Her fortieth wedding anniversary — no, don't look like that, it wasn't a happy occasion, whatever the portraits say. Harold Aldersey was dead by then, thank God, and Edith wanted the family in one room under one roof for one night a decade, whether they liked each other or not. She said a family that only meets at funerals forgets how to be a family while anyone's alive."

"That's almost sentimental," I said.

"It was tactical," Nell said, with the ghost of a smile. "Edith was both. She could make a trap feel like a gift. That was her genius and her sin, depending on which side of the trap you stood." She sipped. "So: the dinner. Every ten years, the whole family comes. They dress as if for a funeral, which is what it amounts to. They toast Edith. And then — this is the part that matters to you — they open the letter."

"What letter?"

"There's a sealed letter kept in the library safe. Edith wrote it before she died. One letter per dinner — no, that's wrong. Let me say it properly." She set the cup down, aligning the handle with precision that looked habitual rather than fussy. "There is one sealed letter. After each dinner, it goes back in the safe unopened if the terms aren't met, and a new seal is pressed over the old one. There are three seals on that envelope now, layered like tree rings. This year would be the fourth."

"Terms," I said. "What terms?"

"That's written inside the letter, isn't it? Nobody living knows. Edith told the solicitor — old Mr. Pryce, dead now, his practice went to a nephew — that the letter contained conditions. If the family met them, the letter would be read aloud and burned. If they didn't, it stayed sealed another ten years." Nell's voice flattened. "They've never met them. Whatever she asked of them, thirty years running, they haven't managed it."

I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. "And the will?"

Her eyes came back to mine, and there it was again — that flicker, that half-second of recognition she thought she was hiding. I'd caught it yesterday when she'd taken my coat. I caught it now. She was a better actress than the family gave her credit for, or she simply hadn't decided yet what to do about whatever it was she saw when she looked at me.

"The will is part of it," she said carefully. "Everyone knows there's a will from 1994. Everyone knows it names a beneficiary. Nobody knows who."

"Nobody?"

"Nobody who's talking." She said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. "Ambrose has the estate in trust pending resolution of the will. Camille manages what can be managed. Your client — whoever paid you to come here — knows more than any of them, I'd wager, because your client knows what's *in* the will, and knowing that is worth more than the fen itself."

"You know I can't discuss my client."

"I know you can't," she said, agreeably. "I also know you're a bad liar for a lawyer, which is a compliment in this house."

* * *

She put out bread and butter and a jar of damson jam that tasted of a summer I'd never had, and she talked, and I let her, because Nell talked the way rivers run — around obstacles, toward the low ground, carrying more than you'd guess from the surface.

The dinner, she said, was a production. Sixty covers once; these days fewer, as the cousins thinned out and the distant branches stopped answering invitations. But the core never changed. The long table in the great hall. The portrait of Edith moved from the stairwell to the head of the table, where a living hostess would sit. Candles — real ones, always, because Edith had hated electric light at table, said it made everyone look like they were being interrogated. The toast at seven o'clock precisely. *To Edith, who keeps her appointments.* And then the walk to the library, the opening of the safe, the lifting of the seals, and — three times now — the reading of the first line only, the line that stated the terms had not been met, and the return of the letter to the dark.

"What's the first line?" I asked.

Nell recited it without hesitation, in a cadence that suggested she'd heard it three times and had carried each hearing like a stone in her shoe:

"*'If this letter is opened and the condition unmet, then the family of Aldersey stands confessed in its silence, and the estate shall pass according to the will of 1994.'*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a moorhen called across the water, that thin hysterical laugh they have.

"The estate passes," I said. "To the beneficiary."

"To whoever the beneficiary is," Nell said. "Yes."

"And the family has spent thirty years failing a test they can't see."

"They've spent thirty years *choosing* to fail it," Nell said. "That's the part people get wrong. You could fail a test without knowing the questions. But Ambrose, Camille, the others — they know why the letter stays sealed. They know what Edith asked. They've decided, decade after decade, that keeping the secret is worth more than keeping the house." She looked at me steadily. "Ask yourself what kind of secret weighs more than Hollow Fen, Mr. Delacroix. Then ask yourself why a stranger would pay double fees to prove a murder hasn't happened yet, six days before the one night the whole family gathers under one roof."

The word landed before I registered she'd said it.

"*Six days,*" I repeated.

"The dinner is Saturday week," Nell said. "The twenty-eighth. It's always the twenty-eighth of June, the date of Edith's first dinner. Invitations went out in April. The caterers come Thursday. The candles arrived Tuesday — forty dozen, same as always." She refilled my cup without asking. "You didn't know. Your client didn't tell you."

My client had told me plenty. Dates of death, dates of probate, dates of filing. What my client had not told me was that the clock I was racing wasn't a legal deadline. It was a dinner party. A ritual. A family standing around a portrait of a dead woman, toasting her, opening a letter they already knew they'd fail.

Unless this year was different. Unless this year, someone had decided the terms would be met — or that the letter would be forced open by other means. A murder scheduled for the anniversary dinner wouldn't be hidden by the gathering. It would be *covered* by it. Forty relatives, candlelight, wine, a house full of noise and grief dressed as celebration. If you wanted a death to disappear into ceremony, you couldn't design a better room.

"When did your client retain you?" Nell asked, too casually.

"Nine days ago."

"And the dinner is in six." She nodded slowly. "So whoever hired you did it inside the window. Close enough to the dinner that you couldn't dig for long, far enough out that you'd say yes before you understood what you'd said yes to." She spread butter on a piece of bread she didn't eat. "That's not a client, Mr. Delacroix. That's a move."

I should have argued. I was paid to argue. Instead I sat in a warm kitchen smelling of bread and damsons and felt the floor of the case shift under me the way the fen ground shifted underfoot — solid until it wasn't.

"Nell," I said. "Who benefits from the will?"

She held my gaze for three full seconds. In those seconds I watched her decide, deliberately, to disappoint me.

"Not mine to say," she said. "But I'll tell you this for free, since you ate my jam. Whoever it is, Edith chose them before she died, and Edith never chose anything carelessly. Not a seal, not a date, not a beneficiary." She rose and began stacking cups, her back to me. "Everything in that woman's will is a sentence she wrote thirty years ago and has been waiting for someone brave enough to read aloud."

* * *

I walked the grounds after, because my legs wanted motion and my mind wanted a room with no witnesses.

The estate in daylight was almost beautiful in a way that felt like being lied to politely. The lawns ran to reed beds; the reed beds ran to open water; the water ran to horizon. A heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a creditor. Gravel paths curved where a straight line would have been cheaper, and I found myself counting the curves, because Vesper Delacroix does not trust a landscape designed to slow you down.

Six days.

I laid it out the way I'd lay out any closing argument. Fact: a will dated 1994 named a beneficiary whose inheritance triggered on a death before the anniversary dinner. Fact: the matriarch whose death anchored the will had died — officially — in 1994, meaning either the will anticipated her death with impossible prescience, or the will's trigger referred to a different death entirely. Fact: my client believed a murder was *scheduled*, future tense, for the dinner six days hence. Fact: the family had gathered for this dinner every ten years for forty years, and every ten years the letter stayed sealed, and every ten years they went home having chosen their secret over their inheritance.

Which meant the pattern predicted safety. Three dinners, no blood. Rituals repeat; that's what made them rituals.

And which meant that if this year broke the pattern, the break wasn't tradition. It was intention. Someone had looked at forty years of ceremony and decided this dinner would be different — and had hired me, nine days out, not to prevent it but to *witness* it. To establish, in advance, with documentation, that as of a certain date, no murder had occurred.

Alibis. My client wasn't buying protection. My client was building a record.

I stopped at the edge of the reed bed. The water lay flat as poured glass, and my reflection floated on it, slightly wrong, slightly darker than the sky deserved. For a moment — a stupid, vertiginous moment — the reflection wore a different face. Older. A woman's face. I blinked and it was only me, a lawyer in a good coat standing at the edge of a marsh, holding a brass key in his pocket like a coin he couldn't spend.

Behind me, gravel crunched.

Ambrose. Of course. He seemed to materialize whenever I approached the water, a man patrolling the border of his own history.

"Enjoying the view?" he asked.

"Learning it," I said. "Six days to your dinner."

Something crossed his face — not surprise. Relief, maybe, that I knew, quickly buried under the family expression, the one they all wore, courteous and closed as a locked drawer. "You've been talking to Nell."

"She talks. I listen. It's called hospitality."

"It's called reconnaissance," Ambrose said, "and she's better at it than you are." He came to stand beside me, hands clasped behind his back, and we regarded the water together like two men at a funeral neither wanted to attend. "Delacroix. A word of advice, free of charge, since you seem to collect those."

"I collect evidence. Advice I take on consignment."

"Whatever you think this dinner is," he said quietly, "it's older than you, and it's heavier, and the last person who tried to open it early regretted it for the rest of her life." His jaw tightened. "Let Saturday come. Let the letter stay sealed. Take your fee and go home."

"*Home,*" I repeated.

He heard it. I saw him hear it — the way the word snagged on something in him, the way his eyes flicked to my face and held there a beat too long, searching for a resemblance he couldn't name and clearly couldn't shake.

"Go home," he said again, softer, and walked back toward the house.

I stayed at the water's edge until the light went long and copper, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the kitchen. Nine days ago, a stranger retained me against a murder that hadn't happened. Six days from now, a family would gather to fail a test they'd failed twice before. And somewhere between those two facts sat a sealed letter with three layers of wax, a will with a hidden name, and a housekeeper who looked at me like a photograph she remembered.

Thirty years ago, Edith Aldersey wrote a sentence and waited for someone brave enough to read it aloud.

In six days, I was going to find out whether that someone was me — or whether I'd been brought here, all along, to be the reason it finally had to be read.
