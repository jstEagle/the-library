## 6. Marisol's Question

The office smelled of cold coffee and old paper when Vesper came back from the fen, and that smell — more than anything else in the world — meant safety.

Marisol was at her desk by the window, cross-legged in her chair the way she was never supposed to sit, a mug of something cinnamon-scented steaming beside a stack of case files she had alphabetized out of pure spite. She looked up when the door opened, and Vesper watched her do the thing she always did: read them in one sweep, top to bottom, like a document she'd already memorized.

"You look like you slept in your car," she said.

"I didn't sleep in my car."

"So you slept badly somewhere else." She uncrossed her legs and stood, taking the file box out of Vesper's hands before they could protest. "Sit down. I'll make tea."

"Marisol—"

"Tea first. Interrogation after. That's the order of operations in this office; you wrote it yourself on the whiteboard in 2019 and never erased it."

Vesper sat. That was the trouble with Marisol Reyes — she remembered everything, including things Vesper wished she'd forget. Seven years ago she'd walked into this office off a temp agency list, twenty-three years old, overqualified and underemployed, and within a month she had reorganized the filing system, renegotiated the lease, and started asking questions no one else had ever thought to ask. Not about the cases. About Vesper. Where they'd gone to school. What their mother's kitchen smelled like. Whether they liked the rain or merely tolerated it.

No one had ever asked those questions. Vesper had built an entire life on the assumption that no one ever would.

She set the tea down — chamomile, because she'd decided years ago that Vesper's nerves were her jurisdiction — and perched on the edge of the desk instead of returning to her chair. That was a bad sign. Marisol only sat on the desk when she was preparing to be inconvenient.

"So," she said. "Hollow Fen."

"What about it?"

"You've been there two days. You called me once, at eleven p.m., to ask me to pull property records for a family you described as 'strangers.' You used the word strangers twice. Then today you come back looking like someone dug up your childhood and made you look at it." She wrapped both hands around her own mug. "So I'm going to ask you a question, and you're going to deflect, and then I'm going to ask it again tomorrow, and the day after, until one of us dies of stubbornness. And we both know I have the stronger constitution."

Vesper lifted the tea. It was too hot. They drank it anyway, buying time in scalded sips.

"It's a strange case," they said finally. "That's all. A client pays double to prove a murder hasn't happened yet. There's a will from 1994 with notarization stamps that shouldn't exist. There's a beneficiary nobody will name. Any lawyer would find it personal."

"Any lawyer would find it *interesting*," Marisol said. "You find it personal. There's a difference, and the difference is currently making your hands shake around that mug."

Vesper looked down. Their hands were, in fact, shaking. They set the mug on the desk with exaggerated care, as if steadiness were a legal argument they could win on procedure.

"The witness line on the will," they said. The words came out before they could stop them, which was how all the true things came out around Marisol. "There's a name in the witness line. Partially water-damaged. But the surname is Delacroix."

Marisol went very still. In seven years, Vesper had told her almost nothing about their past — because there was almost nothing to tell. An orphanage upstate. A state-appointed guardian who changed jobs and vanished from the files. A scholarship, a law degree, a name chosen from a phone book at eighteen because the one they'd been given belonged to a child who no longer existed anywhere except in county records. Vesper Delacroix was self-made in the most literal sense: assembled from paperwork and willpower, with no foundation anyone could inspect.

"That's not possible," Marisol said quietly.

"No. It isn't."

"Unless the will is a hoax."

"Unless the will is a hoax." Vesper turned the mug a quarter-turn on the desk. "But the stamps are real, Marisol. I checked. The notary was real. The paper stock is period-correct. Whoever forged this did it with access to genuine instruments, thirty years ago, for reasons I can't imagine. And the handwriting—" They stopped.

"The handwriting what?"

Vesper had been avoiding this since the fen. Saying it would make it a fact, and facts had weight, and this one felt like it might go through the floor.

"The handwriting looks like mine," they said. "Not similar. Mine. The way I make my lowercase r's. The way I cross my t's left to right instead of right to left, which my first-year contracts professor said was 'a signature defect' he'd never seen in another student." A breath. "I've only ever seen one other person write like that."

Marisol waited. She was good at waiting. It was, Vesper sometimes thought, her actual superpower — not the filing, not the ferocity, but the patience of someone who understood that people were documents too, and some of them opened slowly.

"In the dream," Vesper said. "The one I've had since I was a kid. The woman singing in the reeds. I've never seen her face. But in the dream I always know her hand. She's writing something on the back of mine, letters I can't read, and the pen moves the way I move a pen." They laughed, short and humorless. "Which is insane. It's a dream. Dreams steal from whatever's lying around in your head. I probably saw my own reflection writing once and filed it wrong."

"Probably," Marisol agreed, in the tone of someone agreeing with a client they intended to contradict later.

She slid off the desk and crouched so she was eye-level with Vesper's chair — an undignified position she adopted without embarrassment, because dignity had never once interested her when the truth was on the table.

"Here's my question," she said. "And I want you to actually hear it before you start lawyering it. Why does a case about strangers feel personal? Not the will. Not the stamps. You. What did the fen do to you?"

"Nothing. I got there, I met the family, I—"

"You met the family," Marisol repeated. "Ambrose Aldersey. Camille Aldersey. Nell the housekeeper. And?"

"And a portrait."

"A portrait."

"Edith Aldersey. The matriarch. Dead thirty years, supposedly." Vesper's voice had gone flat, the voice they used for reading adverse testimony aloud. "It hangs at the top of the stairs. Oil on canvas. She's standing at the edge of the water with her hands folded, and the painter gave her eyes a particular color — a gray-green, like the fen after rain. Like—" 

They stopped again. Marisol didn't prompt. She just waited, hands folded now, mirroring the portrait without knowing it.

"Like mine," Vesper finished. "People have commented on my eyes my whole life. Strangers. Teachers. Once a judge, during a recess, who said I had 'fen-water eyes,' which I thought was a strange thing for a man in Detroit to say." Their throat worked. "I stood in front of that painting for four minutes, Marisol. Ambrose found me there. He looked at me looking at her, and he went white, and he said, 'You should go back to the city.' Not angrily. Frightened. Like a man watching a door open that he'd nailed shut himself."

The office clock ticked. Outside, traffic hissed along the wet street; it had started raining while they talked, and neither of them had noticed.

"Okay," Marisol said softly. "That's genuinely unsettling. And here's what I noticed, in case you think I wasn't paying attention: you said 'supposedly dead.' Not 'dead.' Supposedly. You already don't believe them."

"I believe the evidence is incomplete."

"You believe a dead woman has your eyes and your handwriting and possibly your name, and you're calling that incomplete evidence." She reached out and put her hand flat on the case file — the will, the key, the retainer letter. "I've worked for you seven years. You've never once brought a case home inside your body like this. So I'm asking, and you can hate me for it: what happens if this isn't a hoax? What happens if that woman in the portrait is connected to you?"

Vesper looked at her. Marisol Reyes, who kept a photo of her enormous loud family taped inside her desk drawer and showed it to clients' frightened children to make them laugh. Who had learned Vesper's tea preferences in a week and their silences in a month. Who was, though neither of them had ever said it aloud, the closest thing to family the paperwork-version of Vesper Delacroix had ever produced.

"If she's connected to me," Vesper said slowly, "then I'm not who I've spent forty years telling myself I am. I'm not an accident with a law degree. Somebody made me. Somebody chose my name, or let me choose it, and arranged the records, and sent me into the world with nothing — and then either died or watched me from a distance for four decades without ever—" Their voice cracked, just slightly, and they hated it. "Without ever picking up a phone."

Marisol nodded, unhurried. "And if she did watch. If she knew where you were the whole time."

"Then I was never orphaned," Vesper said. "I was abandoned. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire architecture of my personality, so forgive me if I need a moment before I renovate."

That startled a laugh out of her — small, surprised, quickly smothered. "God. You're funny when you're falling apart. Has anyone ever told you that?"

"You tell me constantly."

"Because it's constantly true." She stood, retrieved her own mug, and refilled Vesper's without being asked. "For the record — and you can enter this into evidence — whoever she is, whatever she did, you turned out as the best person I know. So the renovation can't be that extensive. Load-bearing walls are intact."

Vesper looked at the tea, at the rain, at the woman who had asked them about their inner life every year for seven years while everyone else asked only about their verdicts.

"Why do you do that?" they asked. "Ask. Nobody asks. Clients want outcomes. Opposing counsel wants weaknesses. Even friends, such as they were, wanted the version of me that wins. You keep asking what I *feel* about things. It's inefficient."

Marisol considered this with the seriousness she gave to contract clauses.

"When I was nine," she said, "my abuela told me that a person who is only ever asked what they can do starts to believe that's all they are. A function. A tool with good posture." She shrugged. "I decided early I'd never do that to anyone. Especially not to someone whose whole childhood was apparently a filing error." She tapped the will. "Go home. Sleep. Tomorrow we pull the notary's license records, and the adoption court filings, and anything else with that stamp on it. If somebody constructed you, Vesper, there's a paper trail. Paper trails are our whole religion."

"They are," Vesper agreed, and found, unexpectedly, that the idea steadied them. Whatever had been done to them had been done in documents. And documents could be read.

* * *

The dream came the way it always came: not gradually, but all at once, as if Vesper had simply always been there and only now noticed.

Reeds, taller than a person, silver-green in light that had no source. Water beneath — black, still, holding the sky upside down. Somewhere ahead, a woman singing, and the song had no words Vesper could keep hold of, only a rising and falling shape like wind moving through the fen, like grief that had learned to carry a tune.

Vesper walked toward the voice. The reeds parted and closed behind them, erasing the path. This was the part of the dream that never changed: the certainty of being followed, or of following; the sense that the path existed only because someone had walked it first, long ago, and the reeds remembered.

Then the clearing. The woman stood at the water's edge with her back turned, hands folded, exactly as the painter had posed her — and Vesper understood, with the calm horror available only to dreams, that the portrait had not been painted from life. The life had been lived to match the portrait. She had stood like that, at that edge, so many times that the image had worn itself into the world.

She was writing on the air. Or on water. Letters that gleamed and sank, gleamed and sank, and her hand moved the way Vesper's moved — the left-to-right crossing of the t's, the small defiant r's — and Vesper wanted to call out, *that's how I write, where did I get it from, did I get it from you*, but dreams seal the mouth, and the song swallowed everything anyway.

The woman began to turn.

Thirty years of dreams, and she had never once completed the turn. Vesper braced for it now, heart hammering against the ribs of a sleeping body miles away —

—and woke.

Rain on the office-apartment window. 4:17 a.m. by the clock. The dream's last image hung behind their eyes: not the woman's face, which had stayed hidden as always, but her hand — and on the wrist of that hand, catching the sourceless light, a thin old scar shaped like a crescent moon.

Vesper lay in the dark, breathing hard, and did the thing they did instead of praying: they organized.

Facts. The will was genuine or expertly forged. The notary was real. The name Delacroix appeared in a witness line dated 1994, three years before Vesper legally existed under that name. The portrait had their eyes. Ambrose Aldersey had looked at them with fear. And now a scar — a detail no dream should invent, a detail that belonged to bodies, to witnesses, to evidence.

They got up, switched on the desk lamp, and pulled the copy of the will across the blotter. Under the lamp's hard circle of light, they studied the witness line again, magnifier in hand, and this time they stopped looking at the surname and looked instead at the ink — at the pressure of the pen, the hesitation marks where the writer had paused mid-word.

And there, at the end of the damaged line, half-swallowed by a water stain, they saw it for the first time: not a full signature, but the beginning of one. Two letters. An initial and a stroke.

An *E*.

Vesper sat very still while the rain kept time against the glass, and somewhere below the fear, below the vertigo, a colder and older instinct stirred — the hunter's instinct, the one that had never lost a case — whispering that the blank page of their past had just shown them its first line of text.

At dawn, they would go back to the fen.
