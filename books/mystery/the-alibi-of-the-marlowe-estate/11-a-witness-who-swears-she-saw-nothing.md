## 11. A Witness Who Swears She Saw Nothing

The woman Kestrel was looking for had spent twelve years being very good at not being found, which meant that finding her took Kestrel the better part of two days and one small act of professional dishonesty.

Her name was Odette Brandt—or it had been, before she'd traded it in for something quieter. In the newspaper archive, in the yellowed coverage of Lily Marlowe's disappearance, she appeared exactly once: *Odette Brandt, 34, of Miller's Bend Road, told authorities she saw a vehicle near the estate gates on the night in question but could offer no further description.* One sentence. The kind of sentence that reads like nothing and is, in fact, everything—the single thread from which an entire official account was later woven.

Kestrel had pulled the file three times now, reading that sentence the way one reads a will for hidden codicils. *A vehicle near the gates.* Not *no vehicle*. Not *nothing unusual*. A vehicle. Someone had driven to the Marlowe Estate the night Lily vanished, and someone had seen them, and then—for reasons the record did not trouble itself to explain—someone had stopped asking questions.

The trail went cold at Miller's Bend Road because Miller's Bend Road no longer contained Odette Brandt. But county tax records were a language Kestrel spoke fluently, and they said that a parcel adjacent to the old Brandt place had transferred, eleven years ago, to one *D. Ochs*—a name that appeared nowhere else in any registry, any census, any phone directory Kestrel could find. A person who existed only as a line in a deed book.

People who existed only as lines in deed books were, in Kestrel's experience, people worth visiting.

The problem was getting past the gate, and the gate was the problem because D. Ochs had posted signs—not the polite KEEP OUT of weekend farmers, but the serious, galvanized, slightly rusted signage of someone who had thought carefully about trespassers and decided against mercy. NO SOLICITING. NO HUNTING. NO TRESPASSING. And beneath those, in smaller letters, as though added later by a hand that had run out of conviction: *Please.*

"Please," Kestrel murmured, standing at the chain-link fence with her good coat already snagged on a barb. "Well. That's practically an invitation."

She hadn't planned to climb anything. She had planned to knock on the door of the farmhouse visible through the trees and introduce herself as an attorney with some questions about an old matter, which was true, and watch the door close, which would also be true. But the driveway ended at a collapsed carport and the house beyond it was dark, and the only sign of life anywhere on the property was smoke rising thin and gray from a chimney on the far side of a stand of pines—a second structure, a cottage maybe, set back where nobody driving past would think to look.

A person who lived behind her own house, behind her own fence, behind her own signs. Kestrel stood in the November mud and felt something twist in her chest that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite recognition, though it was cousin to both.

She followed the smoke.

* * *

The cottage was small and neat in the way of places maintained by hands that needed the work more than the result. Wood stacked in precise ranks. A garden gone to winter sleep under burlap, tucked in like children. Wind chimes made of flattened spoons, silent in the still air.

The woman on the porch was shelling beans into a bowl and did not look up when Kestrel came around the corner of the woodpile, which meant she had heard her coming for some time and had decided the beans were more interesting.

"Ms. Brandt," Kestrel said.

The shells kept falling. "No such person."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "There isn't. There's a D. Ochs who owns a parcel on Miller's Bend and has never filed a tax protest, never registered to vote, never so much as subscribed to a seed catalog. I checked. It's what I do." She held up her hands, empty, palms out—an old courtroom gesture, *I have nothing concealed*. "My name is Kestrel Galloway. I'm the attorney for the Marlowe Estate. Edmund Marlowe died three weeks ago."

The shells stopped falling.

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the pines, and then the woman set down the bowl with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown, and looked up.

She was perhaps seventy, broad-shouldered, with a face built for weather and eyes that had done their share of enduring it. Her hair was white and cut short and practical, and her hands were enormous, gardener's hands, and they were not quite steady.

"Edmund Marlowe is dead," she said. Not a question. A weighing.

"Three weeks ago. Heart failure, his doctor says. He left a will that—" Kestrel paused, recalibrated. She had learned, these last weeks, how much depended on what you offered first. "He left instructions. About his daughter."

"Don't." The word came out flat and fast, a door slammed. "Whatever you're selling, I bought my silence fair and square and I've paid the installments every month since. Twelve years. You want to know what twelve years of not knowing costs? Come back when you've got a figure."

"I'm not selling anything."

"They all say that." Odette Brandt—Kestrel refused to think of her as D. Ochs; a person who grew spoon chimes deserved her own name—rose from her chair, and she was taller than Kestrel had guessed, and older in the way that fear ages people selectively, leaving the body strong and the spirit worn to lace. "The judge sent flowers when my husband died. Did you know that? Beautiful arrangement. Lilies." Her mouth did something terrible and controlled. "Lilies. So I'd know he knew. That was the whole message. Two hundred dollars of florist's work to say *I see you, Odette, and I can see your kitchen window from everywhere*."

"I believe you," Kestrel said quietly. "And I think you saw something that night. Something you told the sheriff's deputy about, and then stopped telling anyone."

"I saw a car."

"You saw a car."

"I saw a car," Odette repeated, and her voice climbed, cracking along an old fault line, "and I said so, and then a man came to my house who was not a policeman and not a lawyer and not anybody whose business card I ever got, and he sat in my kitchen and drank my coffee and explained to me, very reasonably, the way you'd explain to a child about traffic, that memory is a funny thing. That witnesses get confused. That Mrs. Marlowe had lost her girl and it would be a cruelty to make her relive it with half-remembered cars at the gate. And then he thanked me for my civic cooperation and left, and two days later my husband got a promotion he hadn't applied for, and I understood exactly what had been purchased and at what price, and I have been paying myself back in shame ever since." She drew a breath that shook only at the edges. "That's my statement. Write it down. It won't hold up in court. Nothing I say holds up in court. That was rather the point of me."

The wind moved the pines. Somewhere in the bare garden, a tarp snapped once and settled.

"Ms. Brandt," Kestrel said, "the statute of limitations on whatever happened that night expires in nine days."

Something changed in the woman's face. Not softening—Kestrel watched for softening and didn't get it—but a kind of arithmetic, quick and involuntary, behind the eyes. Nine days. A number landing on a scale.

"That's why he timed it," Odette said slowly. "Old Edmund. That's why the will sat unread until—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, furious at having said even that much.

"Until what?"

"Nothing. I've said nothing. I don't say things. Saying things is how you end up with lilies at your husband's funeral." She picked the bowl back up and resumed shelling with violent precision, beans ricocheting off the rim. "You should go. You seem like a decent young woman and this property is dangerous. There's a hole by the fence. I've been meaning to fill it for years."

"There's a hole by the fence," Kestrel repeated. "How convenient for you, if a trespasser were to fall into it and be unable to leave quickly."

Odette's hands stilled. For the first time she looked at Kestrel directly, and what was in her face was so nakedly exhausted, so tired down to the joists, that Kestrel nearly apologized for existing.

"You're not going to fall in any hole," Odette said. "Because you're going to walk back down the drive and forget my address, like everyone else has managed to do for twelve years."

"Edmund Marlowe spent twelve years paying me to keep documents in order," Kestrel said. "Documents about his daughter. I didn't know that's what I was doing—I thought I was doing my job. When I found out, I wanted to burn my filing cabinets. Instead I started reading them." She let that sit. "I'm not everyone else, Ms. Brandt. Everyone else filed it away. I'm the one who alphabetizes."

A sound escaped Odette then that might have been a laugh in a previous life, before it was buried. "An alphabetizer. Lord help us all. Hale should be trembling."

"He should be," Kestrel said. "Give me a reason to make him."

* * *

Odette did not give her a reason. What she gave her, twenty minutes later, after tea that neither of them drank and a tour of the garden that was clearly a tour of the sightlines—*that ridge there, you can see the road; that window, that's where I watched*—was something smaller and heavier.

They ended up back on the porch because Odette could not be still indoors; Kestrel understood this without being told. Some people paced their cages.

"I recanted," Odette said abruptly. "Officially, I mean. Three weeks after I gave my statement, a man brought papers to the house. An affidavit. Said my eyesight had been called into question, that it would spare everybody embarrassment if I signed a correction. My husband stood next to him while I signed. Not threatening. Just—present. Like furniture that loved me." She stared out at the pines. "I signed it. I've hated myself in four languages since."

"The affidavit would be in the courthouse file."

"It would be in the file, yes."

"And the file," Kestrel said, with the particular dread of a lawyer watching a document burn in slow motion, "is currently sitting in a courthouse annex that Deputy Marsh tells me has all the security of a breadbox."

Odette turned and looked at her then, really looked, the way one looks at a bridge before deciding whether to cross it. Whatever she saw seemed to decide her, because she pushed herself up out of the chair, joints announcing themselves, and went inside without a word.

Kestrel waited. She had learned, in thirty years of practice, that the most important thing a lawyer does is sometimes simply remain in the room.

When Odette came back, she was holding something wrapped in a scrap of flannel, and her hands were shaking now, properly shaking, no more pretending otherwise.

"After the affidavit man left," she said, "after the promotion, after the lilies—I went back. To the estate. Don't ask me how I got in; the boathouse door was broken even then, Edmund never fixed it, and I always wondered if that was carelessness or invitation." She unwrapped the flannel with the tenderness of someone handling a bird. "I'd seen something that night besides the car. Through the window, before I made myself stop looking. And I'd taken—this. God knows why. I was forty feet from my own front door and I picked something up off the ground like a fool, and then I was too frightened to turn it in and too honest to throw it away, and it has lived in my flour tin for twelve years because a flour tin is the one place in this world nobody searches."

She opened her palms.

On the flannel lay a key. Old brass, long-shanked, the teeth worn smooth, with a paper tag wired to the bow so brittle it crumbled at a breath. Kestrel caught the wire before the tag fell and read the faded ink beneath it: a single word, in a hand she recognized—she had spent three weeks steeped in that hand, in ledgers and letters and marginalia.

*Bell.*

"Bell," Kestrel said. "As in—"

"As in the bell pull at the boathouse, if you're asking me, and I'm not saying I am. I never went inside. I stood outside that door for ten minutes arguing with myself and lost." Odette closed Kestrel's fingers over the key with both enormous hands, engulfing them. "But there's a lock on the north door that doesn't match any other lock on that property. Edmund put it in himself, the summer after. I asked the hardware man about it once—he special-ordered it, wouldn't say for what—and the hardware man laughed and said old Mr. Marlowe was finally locking up his fishing tackle. Fishing tackle." Her voice frayed. "That man locked up his daughter and called it fishing tackle, and this whole town helped him carry the lie, and I helped most of all, because I saw the car and I knew whose car it was. I knew. I've known for twelve years. I used to see it parked outside the courthouse every Tuesday and Thursday like it owned the building."

"Whose car, Ms. Brandt."

Odette's chin came up. And here was the thing Kestrel would remember afterward, would carry with her into every hard room that followed: the woman was terrified. Every line of her was terror, hands to spine. And she answered anyway.

"Corwin Hale's," she said. "Black sedan, dented left fender, judge's plate on the dash. I saw it idling at the Marlowe gates at eleven-forty at night, and I saw a girl's coat in the back seat, and then I saw it leave fast with the lights off, and I did what this town does. I looked at my shoes." She released Kestrel's hands. "So there's your witness. A witness who swears she saw nothing. Get it notarized, counselor. Frame it. Hang it in the courthouse where Ambrose Pell can dust it."

The name landed between them like a dropped glass. Kestrel kept her face still, but barely. *Ambrose.* Her mentor. His signature on a death certificate with no body beneath it. She had confronted him six days ago and watched a lifetime of trust come apart in his shaking denial, and she was not ready—would never be ready—to hear his name spoken with that much contempt by a stranger who had earned it.

"You knew Ambrose filed the certificate," Kestrel said.

"I know every piece of it," Odette said wearily. "We all know our own piece. That's how it works. Nobody holds the whole thing, so nobody's guilty of the whole thing, and the whole thing walks free wearing a robe." She stepped back, and the porch light caught her face, and for a moment Kestrel saw the younger woman from the newspaper photograph—thirty-four, uncertain, holding a secret like a hot coal. "Take the key. Find whatever Bell opens. And when you do—" Her voice broke clean through, at last, no more masonry. "When you do, say her name out loud. Say *Lily*. Somebody has to say it out loud. Twelve years, and I swear this town has forgotten the girl ever had one."

Kestrel stood on the step a moment longer. Then she did the only correct thing, the thing no affidavit required: she reached out and took Odette Brandt's flour-dusted, bean-shelling, twelve-years-guilty hand and held it.

"She had one," Kestrel said. "It's in the diary. She wrote it herself. I'll make sure the court hears it in her own handwriting if I have to read it into the record page by page."

Odette nodded, twice, briskly, the way people nod when crying is imminent and dignity is under siege. "Go on, then. Before I change my mind. There's the hole by the fence—mind it on your way out."

* * *

Kestrel drove back toward Marlowe's Hollow with the key in her coat pocket, and it weighed nothing—it was brass, an ounce, less—and it pressed against her hip like a stone.

At the last bend before town, where the road overlooked the valley and the estate's dark fields ran down to the black ribbon of the lake, she pulled over without quite deciding to. The boathouse was invisible from here, but she knew where it sat, at the foot of the sloping lawn, its weathered roof just breaking the treeline. She had walked past it a dozen times since taking the estate files. She had catalogued its contents for the inventory—*rowboat, oars, tarpaulin, workbench, miscellaneous*—and moved on.

*Miscellaneous.*

She took the key out and held it up to the dashboard light. Worn teeth. A dead girl's—or a living girl's—name in a dead man's handwriting. Somewhere behind her, nine days ticked down toward zero, and somewhere ahead of her, in a courthouse annex with a breadbox for a lock, an affidavit of recantation sat waiting to be burned.

Hale's people were tidying. Marsh had called her at midnight to say the archive box was gone. They were moving faster than she was, and they had fire and judges and Tuesday-and-Thursday parking privileges, and she had a key off a flour tin and a witness who swore she saw nothing.

It would have to be enough. It would have to be enough tonight.

Kestrel put the car in gear, and the headlights swept the valley, and for half a second they caught movement at the edge of the estate grounds—a figure on the path by the lake, tall and slight, standing perfectly still beside the boathouse door as though listening to it.

Then the road curved, and the figure was gone, and Kestrel drove on toward town with her heart going like a trapped bird, telling herself it had been Wren checking the property, telling herself it was nothing—

—and knowing, with the certainty of a woman who had just been handed a key labeled *Bell*, that tomorrow she was going to the boathouse. First thing. Before anyone else could.
