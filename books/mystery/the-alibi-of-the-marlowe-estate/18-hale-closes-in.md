## 18. Hale Closes In

Judge Corwin Hale had always believed that civilization was, at bottom, a filing system.

Laws were files. Precedents were files. A man's reputation—the thing he had spent forty years laminating, sheet by careful sheet—was a file, and files could be opened, amended, or sealed. What separated men like him from men like Edmund Marlowe was not morality. Morality was a word juries liked. What separated them was that Corwin Hale understood the filing system, and Edmund Marlowe had merely understood paper.

And now Edmund Marlowe was dead, and his paper had teeth.

Hale stood at the window of his study on Bellamy Street, watching the light come up over Marlowe's Hollow the way it had every morning for sixty-one years—the elms going gold, the courthouse clock ticking its slow municipal heart, the whole town arranged below him like a drawer he had personally organized. Somewhere out past the orchards, at the estate, Kestrel Galloway was awake. He could feel it the way an old dog feels weather. That woman had not slept properly in nine days. He knew because he had made a few calls, and the calls had made a few more, and the picture that came back was of a lawyer who had stopped going home, who had taken to sleeping in Edmund Marlowe's study with her shoes off and her files fanned around her like a card sharp's deck.

She was building something. He could not yet see the shape of it, and that was the intolerable part. Hale had survived four decades on the bench by never being surprised. Surprise was for amateurs and defendants.

He poured his coffee—two fingers, no cream, a discipline he maintained the way other men maintained grudges—and sat at his desk, where three documents waited in a neat row. He had drafted them himself, in longhand, before dawn, because there were things you did not trust to secretaries. Not anymore. Not with Ambrose Pell cold in the ground and his office ransacked and a forged confession sitting in a police evidence bag with Hale's cadence all over it, like lipstick on the wrong glass.

*Amateur work,* he'd thought, when the photographs came. *Amateurs everywhere.*

The first document was a complaint to the State Bar: *In re Kestrel Galloway, Esq.* It alleged—tastefully, with the sorrowful restraint of a man forced to report his own daughter—that Ms. Galloway had engaged in witness tampering, evidence concealment, and the unauthorized practice of probate manipulation, all in service of a "fantastical" claim that a deceased woman was alive. He had attached the diary photocopies. He had attached the affidavit of a groundskeeper named Wren, sworn out by a process server at four o'clock yesterday afternoon, which was a lie, because no such affidavit existed yet—but it would exist by Friday, because the notary who would swear it owed Hale a favor going back to a case in 1998 that no one else remembered, and debts like that did not expire. Statutes of limitations, Hale reflected, were for crimes. Debts were forever.

The second document was a petition to the probate court: *In re Estate of Edmund Marlowe, Deceased.* It alleged that the will under which Ms. Galloway was acting was of questionable authenticity, that the estate's assets were at risk of dissipation, and that a conservator should be appointed immediately to seize and inventory the property. He had a conservator in mind. The conservator had a bank. The bank had a shredder.

The third document was not a legal instrument at all. It was a single sheet of cream stationery, and on it, in Hale's small, precise hand, he had written:

*Ms. Galloway—Some houses are built on foundations. Some are built on the absence of one. You are an intelligent woman; you will know which kind yours is before this letter is a week old. With genuine regard, C.H.*

He read it twice. He almost threw it away. Threats, in his experience, were best delivered as courtesies—the difference between *do this or else* and *I worry for your safety* was the difference between a felony and a fruit basket. But there was a part of him, the part that had been a boy on Bellamy Street watching his own father lose everything to a man with better paperwork, that wanted her to see the teeth. Just once. Just plainly.

He kept the letter. He would decide later.

* * *

At nine, his caller arrived—not through the front door, which was for the town that still believed in him, but through the garden gate, which was for the other town.

Dell Prentiss had been Hale's man for thirty years, back to the days when Hale was a prosecutor and Prentiss was a sheriff's deputy with a drinking problem and a talent for making problems disappear into the marshes. Prentiss had dried out, mostly. His talents remained. He sat now in the leather chair across the desk with his hat on his knee, a big weathered man who looked, as always, like a fence that had been leaned on too long.

"The groundskeeper," Prentiss said. "You want her handled."

"I want her *understood*," Hale said. "There's a difference, Dell, and the difference is the difference between us and the kind of men who end up in my courtroom."

Prentiss nodded the way he always nodded, which was to say without committing to anything.

"Here is what I know," Hale said, rising and moving to the window, because he thought better on his feet and he wanted Prentiss to watch his back while he did it, an old habit of authority. "Edmund Marlowe spent twelve years and a considerable fortune maintaining a fiction. His daughter is alive. I have known this for"—he paused, and the pause was honest, and honesty in that room was itself a kind of performance—"longer than I have admitted to myself. The girl saw something, the night of the Hollis trial. She was in the wrong place with those eyes of hers that missed nothing, and her father, rather than trust the institutions of this town, chose to bury her alive on his own property. Sentimental. Criminal, technically. And now he is dead, and he has left a lawyer, a diary, and a countdown."

"Nine days," Prentiss said.

"Six," Hale corrected. "The hearing on the declaration petition is Thursday. Which means our window is not nine days. Our window is *now*."

He laid it out the way he would lay out a charge to a jury, if his life had gone another way: the bar complaint, filed this morning, which would hang over Galloway like weather and make every judge in three counties look at her sideways. The conservatorship petition, filed an hour later, which would put locks on the estate gates by week's end—*lawful* locks, the best kind, the kind you could not break without becoming the criminal. And then the third thing, which he did not write down.

"The groundskeeper," he said. "Wren. She is not what her file says. Her file says she was hired eight years ago through an agency in Corvallis. The agency says her references came from a farm in the Willamette Valley. The farm burned down six years ago. Do you see the shape of it, Dell?"

"A ghost with a rake," Prentiss said.

"A ghost with a rake, a key to the boathouse, and, if I am right, the last living memory of the night of the Hollis trial." Hale turned. "She has to be found dead again, Dell. That is the phrase I want you to sit with, because I have sat with it and I find it almost elegant. She was declared dead twelve years ago. If she dies again—if there is an accident, a fall, a drowning in the mill pond, something sad and simple and *consistent with the record*—then the declaration petition dies with her. You cannot revive a woman who has, tragically, been confirmed deceased. The law abhors a resurrection twice."

Prentiss was quiet for a moment. Outside, a jay scolded something in the elms.

"And the lawyer?"

"Galloway will be buried in paper," Hale said. "Bar complaint, conservatorship, an ethics inquiry, a malpractice suit from the estate's creditors—by Thursday she will be spending her hours answering subpoenas instead of filing affidavits. Paperwork, Dell, is the great mercy of the law. It lets you destroy a person without ever raising your voice." He smiled, and it was a real smile, warm at the corners, the smile that had charmed four generations of Marlowe's Hollow into calling him *Judge* long after he'd retired from the bench. "I have never once had to shout. That is what I am proudest of."

"And the diary? The recording, if there is one? The boathouse?"

"The conservator will secure the estate," Hale said. "Everything on the property becomes an asset of the estate, inventoried, sealed, and—pending litigation—unavailable. Including, say, a diary. Including, say, a boathouse." He tapped the desk once. "The law will lock the evidence away for me. I only have to ask it politely."

Prentiss put his hat on. At the garden gate he paused, and Hale watched him from the window, and for a moment—half a heartbeat—the two men looked at each other through the glass like two men on opposite banks of a river they'd both crossed too many times.

"Six days," Prentiss said, though it was five now, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a debt accruing interest.

* * *

The bar complaint came back within the day, as Hale knew it would, stamped and docketed and set for preliminary review—thirty days out, but that didn't matter; the *filing* was the weapon, the filing was a stone dropped in a pond, and by evening the ripples had names.

He took the calls in his study with the good bourbon, though he drank less of it than he poured, because a man in his position could no longer afford even the appearance of a loose tongue.

Councilman Boyce called first, voice thin: *Judge, the newspaper people have been asking about the annex fire, and the archivist girl has been pulling boxes, and—* Hale soothed him the way you soothe a horse in a thunderstorm, with a low voice and a firm hand and no sudden movements. *Ralph. Ralph. Sit down. The fire was an electrical fault. The state fire marshal's preliminary report says electrical fault. If you go around looking guilty about an electrical fault, people will begin to suspect you of arson, and then where will the Rotary dinner be?*

The bank called. The bank was already drafting its conservatorship response, eager, the way banks are eager when a judge asks them for a favor and a judge has never once asked them for anything.

And then, at half past nine, when the house was dark and the bourbon had gone warm in his glass, the telephone rang—the garden line, the one only four people alive had the number to—and Hale knew, before he lifted the receiver, before he heard the breathing, that the day had finally come to collect its bill.

He listened. The voice on the other end was low and careful and it told him three things.

It told him that the groundskeeper had not been at the estate since the night of the grave. That she had gone to ground—Prentiss's word, passed along Prentiss's network, and Prentiss's network had gone strangely, unprofessionally quiet, as if the marshes themselves had closed ranks.

It told him that the deputy, Ivo Marsh, had requested the original death file from the state archive by courier, bypassing the county entirely, and that the courier receipt bore the seal of the attorney general's office.

And it told him, in a voice that shook only slightly, that the archivist girl had found something in the annex ruins. A box. A scorched box, pulled from the fire two days before the fire, logged out under a forged signature—but the *logbook itself* had survived, and the logbook had been copied, and the copies had been mailed to three addresses that morning, and one of those addresses was the attorney general's.

Hale set down the receiver with great care, the way a surgeon sets down an instrument.

So. The pond was rippling back at him.

He stood at the window a long time. Below, the town slept, the courthouse clock ticking, the elms holding their gold up to the streetlights. He had built his life here the way a mason builds a wall—one stone, one favor, one buried thing at a time—and he had always believed the wall was load-bearing, that the town itself would hold him up, that Marlowe's Hollow would no more let Corwin Hale fall than a body would let its own spine collapse.

But walls, he knew—he had known since he was a boy on Bellamy Street—were only as good as the mortar, and mortar was only paper and trust, and paper, tonight, was burning all over the county in hands that were not his.

He went back to the desk. He took out the cream stationery, the letter to Kestrel Galloway, and read it once more. *Some houses are built on the absence of a foundation.* He almost laughed. The woman had not even received it yet and already it read like a confession.

He tore it in half. Then in half again. Then he dropped the pieces in the cold fireplace, and watched them curl, and picked up the telephone to make the call he had sworn, thirty years ago on a night in the marshes, he would never have to make—the call to a man in Corvallis who did not use telephones, who did not use names, and who had only ever been paid once, in full, with the understanding that the account was closed.

The account, it seemed, was open.

Outside, the clock on the courthouse struck eleven, each note falling into the dark like a stone into a well, and Corwin Hale counted them the way he had counted everything all his life—carefully, precisely, and with the growing, ice-water understanding that for the first time in forty years, he was counting *down*.
