## 1. The Will That Shouldn't Exist

The rain had been following Sable Winterbourne since the motorway junction at Ashcombe, and by the time his car crunched up the gravel drive of Windrow House it had settled into a steady, businesslike downpour — the sort of rain that seemed less like weather and more like a decision the sky had made about him personally.

He parked beneath a cedar so old and so vast that its lowest branches swept the ground like the hem of a gown, and sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel, looking up at the house.

Windrow House was not beautiful. It was *correct* — grey stone, symmetrical windows, a portico supported by columns that had been added in some Victorian century by someone who believed that dignity could be purchased by the yard. Ivy climbed one wing with an enthusiasm the other wing conspicuously lacked, as though even the plants had taken sides within the family. A single window on the second floor glowed amber against the grey afternoon; every other window in the house was shuttered or dark.

Sable had been executing wills for nineteen years. He had read testaments written on napkins, dictated from deathbeds, and in one memorable case scratched into the varnish of a piano lid. He considered himself difficult to unsettle.

But he had read this file three times on the train, and each time his eye had snagged on the same detail the way a sleeve snags on a nail: Edmund Ambrose Vane of Windrow House had died on the fourteenth of October last year. The will in Sable's briefcase was dated the twenty-second of October *this* year.

A will dated eleven months after its subject's death. Signed, witnessed, notarized, and lodged with his firm by a client who had apparently declined to mention the small matter of being dead.

"Either someone has made an extraordinary clerical error," Sable said to the rain, "or I have been invited to something very interesting."

He took his umbrella and his briefcase and went to find out which.

* * *

The door opened before he reached it — opened by a woman in a plain grey dress with her hair pinned back so severely it looked less like a hairstyle than a verdict. She was perhaps fifty, perhaps sixty; her face gave away nothing except competence.

"Mr. Winterbourne." Her voice was low and level. "Mrs. Pell. The family is waiting in the drawing room."

"Thank you, Mrs. Pell." He offered his hand. She looked at it for a fraction of a second too long — long enough that he noticed, and long enough that she knew he'd noticed — before shaking it once, briefly, like someone returning borrowed money.

"The study has been prepared for you," she said. "There's tea."

"Tea would be wonderful."

"It's already cold," she said, and turned and walked away, leaving him to conclude that this was either a statement of fact or a philosophy.

The hall of Windrow House was dim and smelled of beeswax and old fires and, beneath both, something faintly mineral — wet stone, perhaps, or the sea, though the coast was a mile off. Portraits lined the walls: Vane men, mostly, arranged in descending order of confidence. The most recent portrait showed a lean old man with white hair swept back and eyes that looked directly out of the canvas with an expression Sable could only describe as *amused*. Edmund Vane, painted as a man who knew something you didn't.

Sable had the distinct feeling, standing there dripping in the hall, that the portrait was still doing it.

"This way, Mr. Winterbourne."

Marcus Vane had appeared from somewhere — a tall man in his fifties with his father's height but none of his father's mischief, dressed in a charcoal suit cut well enough to cost more than it looked. His handshake was firm, warm, and precisely calibrated, the handshake of a man who had practiced warmth until it passed inspection.

"Sable Winterbourne. Thank you for coming all this way in —" Marcus gestured vaguely at the windows, where the rain was now auditioning for a larger role. "— all this."

"It's no trouble. Though I confess the circumstances are unusual."

"Yes." Marcus smiled. It was a good smile. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Unusual. We thought it best to have everything handled properly, given the — sensitivities. My father was a private man."

"He sounds it."

"He was a *difficult* man," said a voice from the drawing room doorway. "Private is what we say at funerals."

She was small and silver-haired and upright, wearing black that had clearly been chosen with care and worn with resentment. Constance Vane, the widow. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry, and Sable noted — professionally, without judgment, or telling himself it was without judgment — that her grief had the polish of something rehearsed.

"Mrs. Vane," Sable said, bowing slightly. "My condolences. Belated ones."

"Belated." She laughed — a short, sharp sound with nothing in it. "Yes. Everything about this year has been belated. Come in, Mr. Winterbourne. Sit down. Let us hear what my dead husband has decided to say to us now."

* * *

The drawing room was large enough to echo and furnished heavily enough to muffle everything, including, Sable suspected, certain conversations. A fire burned in a marble fireplace. On the mantel stood a photograph of Edmund Vane in a silver frame, flanked by two candles that had been lit and then, judging by their untouched length, immediately regretted it.

There were four people in the room. Marcus by the fire. Constance on the sofa, spine straight as a poker. And in the corner, folded into an armchair as if trying to be posted through it, a young man of maybe twenty-five who was introduced as Toby — the grandson — and who was either hungover, grieving, or hiding, and whose bloodshot eyes suggested he saw no need to choose just one.

"You're the lawyer," Toby said, by way of greeting.

"I'm the lawyer."

"They sent a lawyer with a name like a weather forecast." He almost smiled. "Winterbourne. What's the forecast, then?"

"Toby," Marcus said mildly.

"What? I'm making conversation. Nobody else is." Toby slumped deeper into the chair. "We've been sitting here for an hour being polite at each other. Gran's counted the candles twice."

Constance said, with terrible dignity, "I have not."

And Mrs. Pell came in with the tea — cold, as promised, or at least aggressively lukewarm — set the tray down with a click that ended all conversation, and withdrew. As she turned, her eyes crossed Sable's for half a heartbeat, and there was something in them he couldn't file under any of his usual headings. Not hostility. Not fear, exactly.

It looked, he thought later, like a warning issued by someone who hoped he wouldn't need it.

"Well," said Constance, when the door had closed. "Read it."

* * *

Sable opened his briefcase and drew out the document, and the room changed.

It was subtle — a stillness, a sharpening, the particular quality of attention that families pay to paper when the paper controls the money. Even Toby sat up. Even the fire seemed to burn more quietly.

"For the record," Sable said, "I'll note the formalities. This instrument bears the date of the twenty-second of October of this year. It is signed by Edmund Ambrose Vane and witnessed by two signatories whose statements I have verified independently. It revokes all prior instruments. It was delivered to my firm with instructions that it be executed upon the first anniversary of Mr. Vane's death — that is, today — in the presence of the family."

He paused. He had rehearsed this part on the train, the professional smoothing-over, the lawyerly equivalent of walking quickly past a graveyard. *Clerical irregularity. Dating error. Nothing to concern yourselves with.*

But Constance was watching him with her dry red eyes, and Marcus was watching him with his calibrated smile, and Toby was watching him with the naked hunger of the young and broke, and Sable found that the smooth words would not come. Perhaps because they all already knew. Perhaps because the date on that page was not an error at all, and everyone in this room knew that too, and the only person pretending otherwise was him.

"A will dated after a death is void on its face," he said instead. "Unless —" and here he heard his own voice drop, as if the room had gotten smaller — "unless the death itself is not what it appears to be."

Silence. Rain against glass. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked and stopped, as if someone had frozen mid-step.

"That," said Marcus pleasantly, "sounds like the beginning of a very unfortunate theory."

"It isn't a theory. It's a jurisdictional question." Sable cleared his throat and looked down at the page. "Shall I simply read the thing? It's shorter than the suspense."

"Please," said Constance.

He read.

The opening was conventional — Edmund Vane, of sound mind and unrepentant disposition (the phrase actually appeared; Sable felt Toby stifle something between a cough and a laugh), did hereby revoke, devise, and declare. There were small legacies: the London flat to Marcus, outright; an annuity to Constance, generous and coldly worded ("to my wife, who always preferred the town"); to Tobias, a sum held in trust until he produced evidence of gainful employment, "a condition I impose in the full expectation that it will never be met."

Toby made a sound like a punctured tire.

"And the residue?" Marcus asked, when Sable paused. "The estate. The house. The rest of it."

Sable turned the page. And there it was — the clause he had read three times on the train and three times in the car, hoping each time that it would resolve into sense the way a word resolves when you stop staring at it.

It did not resolve.

"'As to the whole of the remainder of my estate,'" he read, "'real and personal, including Windrow House, its grounds, endowments, and effects — I leave it entire and undivided to the witness.'"

He looked up. Four faces looked back. None of them moved.

"To the witness," Constance repeated. "What witness?"

"That is rather my question."

"Whose witness?" Marcus's voice was still pleasant, still calibrated, but Sable noticed his hand had found the mantelpiece and was gripping it lightly, the way a man grips a railing on a ship. "Witness to what?"

"The instrument doesn't say." Sable ran his finger down the clause. "It says — and I quote exactly — 'The witness knows who they are, and knows why. If they do not come forward within one year of my death, then let the estate pass to charity, and let my family reflect on the difference between those two outcomes.' End of clause."

"One year," said Constance slowly. "Today is one year."

"So it is."

"Then if nobody comes forward —"

"The estate goes to charity. All of it. House included." Sable closed the folder. "Mrs. Vane, Mr. Vane — I want to be direct with you, because I suspect directness is in short supply here. In nineteen years I have never seen a testament like this. It is validly executed. It is internally coherent. It is also, on its face, impossible, because its author died before it was written. Either this document is a fraud — a very skilled one — or Edmund Vane was alive considerably more recently than any of you have led the world to believe."

The fire popped. Toby had gone very pale and very still, all the sardonic slack gone out of him.

"You're accusing us of something," Marcus said quietly.

"I'm accusing no one. I'm observing a document." Sable stood, tucking the folder under his arm. "I'll need access to Mr. Vane's study. The instructions accompanying the will specify that I execute from his own papers, and that the family provide me with complete freedom of the house."

Something flickered across Marcus's face — quick as a fish turning — and was gone before Sable could name it.

"The study's locked," Toby said suddenly. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, too elaborately. "Well, it is. Has been since — since it happened. Nobody goes in. Dad keeps the key."

"Do you," Sable said to Marcus, "keep the key?"

"I keep *everything*," Marcus said, smiling his excellent smile. "That's what eldest sons are for. You shall have your freedom of the house, Mr. Winterbourne. Tomorrow. Tonight, you've driven a long way in filthy weather, and my mother insists you dine with us. We are, whatever our differences, hospitable people."

"Hospitable," echoed Constance, in a voice that suggested the word had been soaking in vinegar.

Mrs. Pell appeared to show him to his room, materializing in the doorway with the silent inevitability of tide. As Sable followed her out, he glanced back once. Marcus had taken the photograph of Edmund from the mantel and was looking at it — not fondly, not angrily, but the way a chess player looks at an opponent's piece that has just moved where no piece should be able to move.

At the stairs, Mrs. Pell paused. She did not look at him. She spoke to the banister, softly, in that level voice.

"The study key," she said, "isn't where Mr. Marcus thinks it is."

Then she went on up ahead of him, grey dress fading into the dimness of the corridor, leaving Sable Winterbourne standing on the landing with a fraudulent testament under his arm, a cold dinner ahead of him, and — for the first time in nineteen years of quiet, respectable practice — the unmistakable sensation that he had not been hired to execute a will at all.

He had been hired to spring a trap. And he had no idea yet whether he was the trapper, the bait, or the quarry.
