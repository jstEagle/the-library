## 1. The Exhumation

The grave of Aldous Wren had been waiting a hundred years, and it turned out it had been lying the whole time.

Tamsin Kavanagh watched from the back window of his bookshop, the one that faced the churchyard across the lane, through glass he'd been meaning to clean since spring. The crowd had gathered in their best coats—Marrow's End did not often have occasion for its best coats—and they stood in a loose crescent around the open earth, heads bowed against a wind that came off the marsh smelling of salt and turned soil. The vicar's voice carried thin over the distance, words worn smooth by repetition: *dust to dust, ash to ash.* Tamsin had heard those words at too many funerals to find comfort in them. He'd stood at gravesides for thirty years, first as a detective, then as a man who sold books about detectives, which was a different kind of standing at gravesides altogether.

The crane operator eased the coffin up out of the ground with the tenderness of a man lifting something that might still be sleeping. It was oak, or had been oak once; a century underground had softened its edges, darkened it to the color of wet peat. The brass plate on the lid—ALDOUS WREN, 1854–1924, FOUNDER OF MARROW'S END—had gone green at the edges but still caught the grey morning light, and Tamsin saw the mayor lean forward to touch it, almost reverently, before thinking better of himself.

Tamsin should not have been watching. That was the trouble with him; he knew it and watched anyway. He had told himself all week that the centennial reburial was nothing to do with him—a civic pageant, a chance for the town to feel old and important, an excuse for the historical society to sell commemorative tea towels. He had told himself this while dusting the biography shelf, while ringing up Mrs. Pell's order of three large-print romances, while lying awake at two in the morning listening to the shop's pipes knock like someone asking to be let in.

The truth was simpler and worse: when the notice had appeared in the *Marrow's End Gazette*—*CENTENNIAL REBURIAL OF OUR FOUNDER, ALL WELCOME*—something in Tamsin's chest had shifted, the way floorboards shift under weight, quietly, in a house you thought you knew.

He watched them carry the coffin to the trestle table the undertakers had set up beside the new grave—the new grave being a deeper, grander hole lined with fresh stone, because a founder deserved better than what a hundred years ago had provided. The plan was ceremonial: the old coffin would be transferred into a polished casket donated by the Wren family, processed through town behind the brass band, and laid to rest again with proper pomp. Vivian Wren herself would deliver the eulogy. Tamsin could see her even from here, a small upright figure in charcoal wool at the front of the crowd, her silver hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had never once let weather or sentiment rearrange anything.

The undertakers worked the lid loose. There was a moment—Tamsin would remember this moment for the rest of his life, though he didn't know it yet—when the whole scene went ordinary. Wind in the yew trees. A baby crying somewhere near the lychgate. The smell of coffee from the cart the historical society had set up by the gate, because Marrow's End could not hold a ceremony without selling something.

Then the lid came off, and the ordinary moment ended, and it never came back.

From the back window of a failing bookshop, Tamsin Kavanagh saw the crowd change. It happened all at once, the way a flock of starlings turns—not one person reacting but the entire crescent of bodies stiffening, tilting, pressing forward and back at the same time. Someone made a sound. Not a scream; Marrow's End was too composed for screaming. A sound like a held breath being released wrong, a collective *oh* with no end to it.

Tamsin's hands were on the windowsill before he decided to put them there.

He couldn't see into the coffin from where he stood. The angle was wrong, the yew branches in the way. But he could see the faces of the people who could see, and he had spent twenty years reading faces for a living before he'd spent ten reading customers, and what he read now was confusion curdling into horror, and beneath the horror, in some of them, something older and more private. Guilt, maybe. Or recognition.

The vicar stepped back from the trestle. The mayor stepped forward, looked in, and stepped back as if the contents had reached up and touched him. And Vivian Wren—this was the detail Tamsin's mind snagged on and refused to release—Vivian Wren did not step forward at all. She remained exactly where she was, hands folded at her waist, head tilted very slightly, as though she were listening to music no one else could hear. She did not look surprised. She did not look horrified. She looked, if Tamsin had to put a word to it from forty yards and a dirty window away, *patient.*

Then she turned her head, slowly, deliberately, and looked directly at the back window of Ravensworth Books.

It was impossible. Forty yards, dirty glass, a dozen bodies between them, and she was ninety-one years old besides. But Tamsin felt her gaze land on him like a hand on the back of his neck, and he stepped out of the light without deciding to, his heart going hard and stupid against his ribs.

*Old reflexes,* he told himself. *Paranoia. You've been alone too long.* But his body didn't believe him. His body remembered how to be afraid in useful ways.

* * *

By noon the story had spread through Marrow's End the way stories do there—fast, mutated, and with everyone claiming to have been present. Tamsin heard four versions before closing time, delivered by customers who bought nothing and stayed long.

Mrs. Pell said the coffin was full of rocks. "River stones," she said, "smooth ones, like someone collected them special." She said the sexton had fainted. The sexton, in the second version, had not fainted but had sworn loudly and been escorted home. In the third version, told by a young man Tamsin didn't know who claimed to work at the estate, there had been something else among the stones. Paper. A letter, typed, which the coroner had taken away in a plastic sleeve like evidence, though nobody could say evidence of what, because a hundred-year-old coffin wasn't a crime scene, was it? It was a prank. Everyone agreed it was a prank. They agreed so quickly and so uniformly that Tamsin found himself wondering who had suggested the word first.

"A prank," Mrs. Pell repeated, gathering her coat. "Though I don't see the joke myself. Poor Mr. Wren."

"Poor Mr. Wren," Tamsin agreed, and rang up nothing, and watched her go.

When the shop emptied he locked the door and stood in the aisle between history and mystery, surrounded by the smell of paper and dust and the particular sweetness of books that were not going to sell. Three months, his accountant had said. Three months of stock and rent and the heating bill, and then Ravensworth Books would be a memory, and Tamsin would be a retired detective who had failed twice—at the job and then at escaping it.

He looked at the window facing the churchyard. Through it, past the yews, he could see the crowd dispersing, the trestle table empty now, the old coffin covered in a sheet, waiting for whatever the council decided to do next. Small knots of people stood talking in the lanes, and every knot, he noticed, angled itself slightly toward the churchyard, the way bodies orient toward a wound.

He should leave it alone. That was the sensible thing, the thing the last thirty years had been teaching him: that curiosity was a debt collector, that it always came back for payment, that the last time he'd followed it, he'd been ordered off a case so thoroughly and so quietly that the ordering-off had felt like a kind of mercy, and he had accepted it like one, and had been paying interest ever since.

But his feet were already moving. Past the till, down the back stairs, into the cellar where the boxes lived—the boxes he'd promised himself he'd burn, or donate, or simply stop owning. He knelt in the cold and pulled the third box from under the stairs, the one with the masking-tape label in his own younger handwriting: *MARSH, E. — DISCONTINUED.*

Edith Marsh. Town librarian. Thirty-one years old. Last seen on the fourth of November, thirty years ago, walking home from the library with a canvas bag of returned books. The case file had been thin when he'd inherited it and thinner when he'd been forced to hand it back. *Discontinued,* the superintendent had said, meaning closed, meaning dead, meaning *stop looking, Tamsin, and be grateful we're letting you stop looking.* He had kept copies. Of course he had kept copies. Keeping copies was the one dishonest thing he'd ever done, and he'd done it out of grief, which is where most dishonesty is born.

He sat on the cellar steps with the box on his knees and lifted out the folder, and there it was—the typewritten page he'd stared at a hundred times, Edith Marsh's final library requisition form, dated three days before she vanished. Requesting, of all things, a replacement ribbon for the library's typewriter.

He hadn't thought about that ribbon in decades. He hadn't thought about the letter in the coffin either—he'd only heard about it an hour ago, secondhand, garbled. But some part of his mind, the part that had never retired, the part he'd fed and starved and fed again like an animal he couldn't kill, took the two facts and set them side by side and began, without permission, without mercy, to compare them.

A typewriter. A librarian who vanished. A founder whose coffin held stones instead of a man.

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the cellar window, and somewhere across the lane the churchyard lay open and violated and honest for the first time in a century. Tamsin Kavanagh sat in the dark among his unsold books and understood, with the calm dread of a man hearing footsteps on the stairs of an empty house, that the past had not come knocking after all.

It had dug.
