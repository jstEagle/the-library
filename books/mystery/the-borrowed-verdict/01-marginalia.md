## 1. Marginalia

The rain had been at the shop's gutters since Tuesday, and by Thursday afternoon it had worked its way inside, finding the one patch of ceiling above the poetry section that no amount of buckets or optimism could outmaneuver. I moved the Rilke. The Rilke did not deserve to drown. Neither, strictly speaking, did I, though some mornings the distinction felt academic.

My name is Edmund Galloway, and for thirty-one years I was a detective with the city police, a career I ended the way most careers end — not with a bang but with a cardboard box and a colleague who couldn't quite meet my eye. For the six years since, I have been the proprietor of Galloway's Books, a shop whose business model might best be described as *charitable decline*. I sell books the way a lighthouse sells light: rarely, reluctantly, and mostly as an excuse to keep standing in one place.

But I have not entirely retired.

In the back room, behind the till and a curtain of grey wool that had once been a respectable blanket, there is a filing cabinet. In the filing cabinet there is a folder. And in the folder there are three deaths that the coroner called accidents and that I, in the stubborn grammar of my old trade, call murders.

I finished the third file that afternoon, while the rain kept its appointment with the poetry section and the shop stood empty of everyone but me and the smell of damp paper.

* * *

The first death had been Reginald Voss, sixty-two, found at the bottom of his own cellar stairs with a bottle of single malt in his blood and a broken neck. Open-and-shut, said the coroner. Drunken man, steep stairs, gravity doing what gravity does. The police agreed with the enthusiasm of men eager to be elsewhere, and who could blame them? Voss had been nobody. A retired shipping clerk with a tidy pension and no family closer than a nephew in Leeds.

Except that Voss hadn't drunk. His doctor's records — which I obtained through channels I will not describe here, because describing them would constitute a confession — showed a man nine years sober, proud of it in the way only the reformed are proud. And except that the cellar light had been unscrewed a quarter-turn past loose, so that anyone descending after dark descended blind. And except that the stair rail bore two kinds of scuffing, and one of them matched a rubber heel.

I had written all of this in my careful hand across three years of evenings, and on this particular Thursday I wrote the last line of it: *Voss knew something about someone. Find what he carried, and you find why he fell.* Then I underlined it twice, because a conclusion without emphasis is just a sentence.

The second death had been Marion Ellery, fifty-eight, drowned in her bath on a night her neighbours swore they heard nothing, though the house next door had hosted a birthday party loud enough to register on the affections of the entire street. Cardiac event, said the coroner, secondary to drowning. A woman of her age, her medication, her solitary habits — tragic, unremarkable, closed.

Unremarkable, unless you noticed that Marion Ellery had recently withdrawn four thousand pounds in cash from an account she'd otherwise touched only for groceries and church donations. Unremarkable, unless you asked why a woman planning a quiet evening at home had bolted her own front door from the outside — the bolt was engaged when police arrived, and Marion had no key on her person, because Marion had entered through the kitchen window she'd left unlatched for the cat. People do not bolt doors behind themselves when they mean to come back out. Someone else had bolted that door, and whoever it was had walked away into a street full of party noise and never once been looked at.

The third was Thomas Quill, seventy-one, a fall from the sea wall at Halloway Point. Suicide, said the note in his pocket, in handwriting that was almost his. *Almost* is a word I have built a career on. Thomas Quill had been left-handed since birth; the note was written right-handed, competently, by someone who had practised. I knew this because I spent four evenings practising it myself until I could reproduce the forgery's specific hesitations — the way the forger lifted the pen before the Q, the slight backward lean of every descender. You learn a great deal about a hand by imitating its lies.

Three accidents. Three closed files. Three dead people who had, in the last months of their lives, each received or moved or mentioned money — sums too small to make headlines and too regular to be innocent. Voss had a bank draft. Ellery had her cash withdrawals. Quill had a second passbook hidden behind a loose brick in his garden wall, which I found eleven months after they buried him, and which I sat holding on a cold bench for the better part of an hour, feeling like a man reading someone else's mail from inside their coffin.

I did not know yet what connected them. I knew only that three separate tragedies, each examined by professionals and pronounced natural, had all been arranged by hands steady enough to fool doctors and patient enough to fool me for years. That much I had earned. What I didn't have — what I had never had, not once in six years — was anyone to tell.

A detective without a case is a lighthouse with no sea. A detective with a case and no one to tell is something worse: a lighthouse that has begun to doubt the existence of ships.

I capped my pen. The rain went on. Somewhere down the street a delivery van changed its mind about our road, and the silence it left behind was the particular silence of a shop that has sold nothing today and expects to sell nothing tomorrow.

That was when the bell over the door rang.

* * *

Priya Raman came in the way weather comes in — all at once, trailing the street behind her. Twenty-six, maybe twenty-seven, a journalist for the *Courier*'s local desk, which meant she covered parish councils and pub closures with the intensity other reporters reserve for wars. She came in Tuesdays and Fridays, bought almost nothing, read a great deal, and talked to me with the cheerful presumption of someone who has decided I am a character in her life rather than the reverse.

"Mr. Galloway." She shook rain off her coat onto my floorboards, which have suffered worse. "You're still open. I admire that. It's practically heroic."

"The alternative is closing."

"Which you refuse to do out of spite."

"Out of habit. Spite requires energy."

She laughed and made for the history shelves, her usual circuit, and I returned to the till and the small dishonest work of appearing to be merely a bookseller. This is a skill, incidentally. Six years of practice, and I can now discuss shipping costs with a man while a murder file sits twelve feet behind a grey curtain, humming to itself like a wasp in a jar.

It was perhaps ten minutes later that she appeared at the counter holding a paperback — a donated crime novel, one of a box lot I'd taken off the church's hands in autumn, dog-eared and coffee-ringed and worth exactly what I'd paid for it.

"You annotated this," she said.

"I price things in pencil sometimes—"

"Not prices." She set it down and turned it toward me, open to page 117, where in the margin, in my own unmistakable hand, were the words: *No. He'd have used the service door. Check the timing again.*

I looked at it the way a man looks at a photograph of himself asleep — caught somewhere he didn't consent to be seen.

"That's not—" I began.

"It's your handwriting. It's all through it." She flipped pages. Page 43: *Alibi assumes the train ran on time. It didn't that week — strike.* Page 88: *Two witnesses, same sentence, same phrasing. Rehearsed.* Page 201, in the blank space after the final chapter: *Sloppy. The real one was better.* She looked up at me, and her expression had shifted from amusement to something more careful, the way a dog goes still when it catches a scent it wasn't expecting. "You've been solving the book."

"I've been *reading* the book."

"You've been cross-examining it. Mr. Galloway, this author won an award for this novel. There's a quote from a famous reviewer on the cover calling it 'airtight.' And you've found holes in it in pencil."

"Airtight is a word reviewers use. So is watertight. Ships sink anyway."

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and the ceiling above the poetry section dripped its slow metronome into the bucket, one note at a time, like a clock that had given up on hours.

"Why?" she said. Not accusingly. Genuinely. "Why does a retired bookseller read a mystery like a detective?"

And there it was — the question I had spent six years making sure no one asked, arriving on a wet Friday in the mouth of a woman young enough to be my daughter and persistent enough to be my ruin. I could have lied. I am good at lying; it is the family resemblance all investigators share with criminals. I could have said I used to enjoy puzzles, that old habits are just comfort, that marginalia is how lonely men talk to themselves.

All true. None sufficient.

"I was in the police," I said finally. "Thirty-one years. You stop being able to read the way other people read. The story stops being a story and starts being evidence." I slid the paperback back toward her. "Keep it. Consider it a review copy."

She didn't take it. She was looking at me with that particular journalist's attention — the kind that files things away in a place you can't subpoena. "You must miss it," she said. "Having someone to tell things to."

The question landed harder than she could have known. Harder than I had allowed myself to know, if I'm honest, which I generally avoid being.

"There's nothing to tell," I said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly, tucking the paperback under her arm despite my offer. "But you'd be very good at it, I think. Telling things. When there's something." She headed for the door, then paused with her hand on the bell. "Same time Tuesday. If you find any more holes in that author's work, save them for me. I like knowing how things actually happened."

The bell rang. The door shut. The rain resumed.

I stood at the counter for a long moment, listening to the bucket fill, and then I went back through the grey curtain, opened the filing cabinet, and took out the folder — because the truth was I hadn't finished the third file after all. There was one line I'd been avoiding for three days, the line under the question of who had paid Thomas Quill, and why a retired shipping clerk, a churchgoing widow, and a left-handed gardener had all, in the same eighteen months, become worth killing.

I sat down, uncapped my pen, and wrote: *Follow the money past the dead men. Whoever paid them is still paying someone.*

I had no idea, that rainy Thursday, how soon the answer would arrive — or that it would come addressed to me, in two envelopes, on the same morning, in two different hands.

One asking forgiveness.

One promising a fourth name.
