## 3. Ashcombe House

The taxi driver refused to take the last half mile.

"Not that I've got anything against the place," he said, easing to a stop where the gravel drive narrowed into a tunnel of overgrown rhododendron. "It's just that my suspension's got feelings, and those potholes have opinions."

Sebastian paid him in cash and watched his taillights retreat down the coast road with something close to envy. Then she shouldered her bag — recorder, notebook, a thermos of coffee she'd need before noon — and started walking.

The morning was doing its best to be beautiful. Gulls wheeled over the water, the sea lay flat and silver as hammered tin, and the air smelled of salt and wild fennel. It was the kind of coastline that showed up in wedding brochures. Which made it worse, somehow, that thirty years ago a seventeen-year-old girl had walked out of a house on this exact stretch of cliff and never been seen again.

Ashcombe House announced itself gradually, the way bad news does. First a chimney stack rising above the trees like a raised finger. Then a stretch of slate roof gone green with moss. Then the whole gray mass of it, three stories of Victorian stonework crouched at the cliff's edge, its windows reflecting the sea back at itself in flat, blind squares.

Half the shutters were closed. The other half hung at angles that suggested they'd given up sometime around the previous century. Ivy had gotten into the east wing and was dismantling it with the patience of a lawyer.

"Charming," Sebastian said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the other kind of thoughts quiet.

She'd done her homework on the drive up — two hours of Marcus's voice in her earbuds, walking her through the file one more time.

"You want me to say it again?" he'd asked, third time through.

"I want you to say it like you believe it."

"Fine." A sigh, long-suffering, professional. "Vivian Ashcombe, seventeen, last seen the evening of June ninth, 1994, at the family estate. Brother Julian, twenty-two, claims he was at a regatta in Falmouth. Groundskeeper Tom Reilly claims he was at the Anchor & Hope until closing. Tutor Ada Marsh claims she was asleep in her room. Three alibis, three witnesses, zero corroboration from anyone who wasn't on the payroll or in the family. The case went cold by autumn. Eleanor Ashcombe stopped cooperating by Christmas. And now, thirty years later, somebody mails you a confession the day after the old woman's funeral."

"And?"

"And I think you're going to get us sued, and I think you're going to do it anyway, and I'd like the record to show I said so."

"The record shows it every week, Marcus."

"It's a good record," he said, and hung up on her fondly.

Now she stood at the foot of the front steps, looking up at a door tall enough to admit a piano, and pressed the bell. Somewhere deep inside the house, a chime rang out — two notes, descending, like a question being asked twice and not answered either time.

Nothing happened.

She waited. She pressed again. The same two notes, the same silence after. She was reaching for the knocker — heavy brass, shaped like an anchor, which felt less like decoration and more like a warning — when the door opened.

Julian Ashcombe was not what she'd expected, though she couldn't have said what she'd expected instead. Sixty-ish, tall without being imposing, dressed in a linen shirt and corduroys that had once been expensive and were now merely comfortable. His hair was white and swept back, his face was tanned and lined, and his eyes were the pale gray-green of sea glass — pleasant enough at a glance, and utterly unreadable past the glance.

"Ms. Quintana." Not a question. He knew exactly who she was, which meant he'd known she was coming, which meant someone had told him, which meant she'd been discussed. "You found the drive, then. Most people give up at the rhododendrons."

"The rhododendrons and I came to an understanding."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "Come in. Mind the third stair. It complains."

* * *

The hall inside was vast and dim and smelled of dust, beeswax, and something faintly mineral, like the house had been breathing the sea in through its cracks for decades. A staircase swept upward into shadow. Portraits lined the walls — Ashcombes, presumably, generations of them staring down with the particular smugness of people who never had to update their LinkedIn.

Sebastian counted four covered in sheets as Julian led her through to a morning room overlooking the lawn and, beyond it, the cliff and the water.

"Closing up the house?" she asked lightly.

"Selling it, eventually. My mother held on to the whole thing out of stubbornness, and I inherited the gene, but even genes have limits." He gestured her into a chair that tried very hard to swallow her whole. "Tea? I should warn you it's the only hospitality on offer. I don't keep liquor in the house anymore."

"No thank you." She set her bag down but didn't open it. Rule one: let them forget the recorder exists. "I appreciate you seeing me. I know the timing is —"

"Appalling? Yes. My mother is eleven days in the ground, and here you are with your podcast about dead girls and old crimes." He said it evenly, without heat, settling into the chair opposite hers. "I did consider not answering the door."

"But you did."

"I was curious what you actually sound like. On the show you have a very deliberate voice. Measured. Like a woman reading a eulogy she wrote herself and isn't sure she means."

Sebastian blinked. It was, she had to admit, a better piece of criticism than most reviewers had managed.

"That's the craft," she said. "Underneath it I'm mostly just nosy."

"Mm." He laced his fingers over his knee. "Then be nosy efficiently, Ms. Quintana, because I'll tell you now how this goes. You'll ask about Vivian. I'll tell you I don't know anything. You'll ask about the letter. I'll tell you I didn't send it. You'll imply that my family bought its innocence, and I'll point out that you have no evidence beyond an unsigned confession mailed by a stranger, and then you'll leave, and I'll watch from the window until your taxi reaches the rhododendrons, because that's the sort of thing one does in houses like this."

"That's remarkably efficient. Can we skip to the part where you slip and tell me something useful?"

This time the smile arrived properly, thin and dry. "You're not what the internet says you are."

"What does the internet say I am?"

"A vulture with good production values."

"The production values are mostly Marcus. The vulture part is fair."

He almost laughed. She watched him stop himself, watched the discipline come back down over his face like a shutter — one more shutter, she thought, in a house full of them.

"Ask your questions," he said.

So she did. Where were you the night of June ninth, 1994? At the regatta in Falmouth; the club secretary can confirm it, if the club secretary is still alive. Did you see Vivian that day? At breakfast; she'd argued with their mother about something trivial — a horse, of all things. Had Vivian seemed frightened lately? A pause there, half a second too long, and then: she was seventeen; everything frightens a seventeen-year-old. What about the servants' statements? Bought and paid for, he agreed readily, too readily — his mother believed money solved things, and usually she was right, which is its own kind of tragedy.

He answered everything smoothly, everything plausibly, everything like a man who had rehearsed these answers for so long they'd worn smooth as river stones. Sebastian let him talk, made her small notes, and felt the familiar hum under her sternum that meant *he's telling the truth and it doesn't matter, because the truth he knows isn't the truth that counts.*

Outside, the light shifted. A cloud crossed the sun and the room went gray, and in the gray she noticed what she hadn't before: the portrait above the mantel. A young woman, dark-haired, painted in the stiff style of a school commission rather than a proper artist — Vivian, unmistakably, at perhaps sixteen. Someone had hung flowers beneath it. Fresh ones. White roses, not yet dropped a petal.

Eleven days after the funeral, and someone in this house was still changing the flowers.

"Who tends those?" Sebastian asked.

Julian followed her gaze. For the first time since she'd walked in, he looked tired. "I do," he said. "My mother would have hated it. She preferred we remember people the way she decided they should be remembered."

"Which was?"

"Gone." He said it flatly. "Cleanly. My mother did not hold on to things, Ms. Quintana. People, papers, grief — she filed it all and burned the copies."

"Burned the copies," Sebastian repeated, keeping her voice idle as a summer afternoon. "That's an interesting choice of image."

"It's not an image. It's a fact." And then he stopped, and she watched him hear his own sentence, watched the sea-glass eyes go still as he realized what he'd just handed her. The pause stretched. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"Mr. Ashcombe," she said gently, "did your mother burn papers before she died?"

His jaw worked. Whatever war was happening behind his face, it lasted a good five seconds, and she gave it the silence it deserved, because silence is a crowbar if you're patient.

"The week before the funeral," he said finally, "she had the study fireplace lit in June. She sat in there for two evenings burning things. I saw the ash. I didn't look closely at what was in it, because—" He exhaled through his nose. "Because I learned a long time ago that looking closely at my mother's business was how you ended up owing her favors. And her favors always cost more than they appeared to."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to find out anyway. You're the type. And because—" He stood abruptly, crossing to the window, putting his back to her. When he spoke again his voice had lost all its polish. "Thirty years, Ms. Quintana. Do you know what it's like to spend thirty years being innocent of something and unable to prove it? Every time someone looks at me, they're not seeing a man who misses his sister. They're seeing a suspect who aged well."

"I'm sorry," Sebastian said, and found she partly meant it.

"Don't be. Just be accurate." He turned back around, composure reassembled, though it fit worse now, like a jacket borrowed from a broader man. "I didn't send your letter. I don't know who did. But whoever they are, they wanted you here, in this house, asking these questions — and that worries me far more than you do."

"Why?"

"Because the only person who could want this reopened has spent thirty years making sure it stayed shut." He walked her to the door himself, hand resting briefly on the newel post like a man touching an old dog. "Good luck with the episode. Do try to spell our name correctly. My mother spelled it wrong on my birth certificate for six weeks, and I've never fully recovered."

* * *

The door closed behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Sebastian stood on the gravel a moment, letting the wind push at her, running it back. *Burned papers. Two evenings. In June.* Eleanor Ashcombe had died knowing something was coming — the letter? No, the letter was posted *after* the funeral. Something else, then. Something that made an eighty-year-old woman sit down with the family secrets and feed them to a fire one page at a time.

And Julian. Polished, hostile, rehearsed Julian — who had slipped exactly once, and only once, and the slip had sounded suspiciously like a gift.

She was halfway down the drive when the feeling arrived. It came slowly, the way weather does, and she might have missed it entirely if she hadn't spent ten years interviewing people and learning to notice when she was the one being observed.

The windows. The uncovered ones. From inside the house, every one of them had a view of the drive.

She turned and looked back. Gray stone, blank glass, the ivy eating the east wing. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moves, she reminded herself, in the places that are watching you.

Still, she didn't relax until the rhododendrons swallowed the house behind her, and even then her skin kept prickling, the way it does when a melody you can't place keeps playing somewhere just below hearing.

At the coast road, her phone buzzed. An email, no subject line, from an address that was nothing but numbers.

Four words:

*Ask about the fire.*

Sebastian looked back up the drive, at the green tunnel where Ashcombe House had already erased itself, and typed her reply into the empty air of the thread, knowing no answer would come:

*I just left the fire. It's cold.*

Her phone buzzed again almost instantly.

*Not that one.*
