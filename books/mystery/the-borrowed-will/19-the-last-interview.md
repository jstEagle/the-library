## 19. The Last Interview

### Sebastian

I found her the way I should have found her from the beginning: not by chasing clues, but by sitting still.

The print shop in Gullhaven had been a dead end — a woman who fled, a room full of my own face annotated in pencil, a trail that went cold at the harbor. Reyes wanted to put out an APB. Marcus wanted to hire security. I did neither. I went back to the boarding house where Ada Marsh kept her hours, drank terrible tea in a parlor that smelled of lavender and old rules, and said, "She'll contact me again. She always has. The question is whether I'm worth finding."

Ada set down the teapot with the deliberateness of a woman placing a chess piece.

"You've changed," she said. "When you first came here, you asked questions like a scalpel. Now you ask like a stethoscope." She poured my cup. "Wren will notice the difference too."

Two days later, a postcard arrived at my motel. No message on the front — just a photograph of the cove below Ashcombe House, gray water, gray sky. On the back, in that handwriting I'd known was familiar since chapter two of my own nightmare: *Thursday. Low tide. Bring nothing you'd mind losing.*

"Cheerful," Marcus said over the phone when I told him. "That's either an invitation or a threat."

"With this family it's always both."

"You're not going alone."

"I'm absolutely going alone."

"Seb—"

"Marcus." I looked out the motel window at the rain stitching itself down the glass. "Thirty years ago, someone decided Vivian Ashcombe's story belonged to other people — to Eleanor's reputation, to Julian's inheritance, to a father's secrets. Everyone who touched this case made it about themselves. The lawyers, the cops, the family. Even me. Even the podcast." I turned the postcard over in my hand. "If I show up with a producer and a release form and a plan for episode nineteen, I'm just the latest person borrowing her life for my own purposes. So no. Alone. If I'm not back by Friday, publish everything."

"That's a horrible contingency plan."

"It's the only one I've got."

* * *

### Wren

I watched her come down the cliff path at low tide, and I have to admit she was nothing like I expected.

I had studied her for years — every episode, every interview, every photograph. In my head she was a weapon. Sharp, relentless, a woman who took broken people apart and sold the pieces as content. I had built her out of sound bites because it was easier than building her out of a person. People could disappoint you. Weapons couldn't.

But the woman picking her way across the wet rocks was wearing a rain jacket with a torn pocket and arguing quietly with herself about footing. When she reached the flat shelf of rock below the boathouse ruins, she didn't scan the cliffs for me. She sat down on a boulder, opened her bag, took out a thermos, and poured two cups.

Then she waited.

It was such a small thing. Such an absurdly small thing. But everyone who had ever come looking for me had come to take something, and here was this woman who had crossed two hundred miles to sit in the cold and offer tea to nobody.

I stepped out from behind the sea stack.

She didn't startle. That surprised me more than anything else. She just looked up, and there was no triumph in her face, no *gotcha*, none of the hungry light I'd seen in her eyes through a camera lens a hundred times. There was only a kind of tired attention, like a doctor reading a chart.

"Wren," she said. Not a question.

"You're supposed to be afraid of me," I said. "Everyone in your comments section says I'm dangerous."

"I read the comments. Mostly I worry about their cholesterol." She held out the second cup. "Tea? It's the motel's. It tastes like hot regret, but it's warm."

I took it. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me. Thirty years of hiding and my cover nearly blown by a paper cup.

"You found the locket," I said.

"We found the locket."

"The picture's half gone. I tore it myself, the night we left. Half for me, half for her. She said—" My voice caught on something I hadn't planned to say yet. "She said that way we'd always know there was more of us than anyone could see."

Sebastian didn't write that down. I noticed that immediately. No notebook, no recorder, no phone on the rocks between us. Just a woman holding her tea with both hands against the wind.

"I know why you're here," she said. "And before you say anything, I want to tell you what I'm offering. Then you can decide if I'm the enemy or just annoying."

* * *

### Sebastian

I had rehearsed the speech the whole drive down, and then thrown most of it away somewhere around mile ninety, because rehearsed speeches are how journalists lie to themselves.

So I just said it plainly, with the wind trying to steal the words sideways off the rocks.

"I spent my whole career believing the truth belongs to everyone. And mostly I still believe that. But I've learned — late, and expensively — that some truths belong to somebody first. This one is yours. You lived it. You paid for it. Vivian paid for it." I met her eyes. She had her mother's jaw and her aunt's stubbornness and a watchfulness all her own, worn smooth by decades of practice. "So here's the unprecedented part, and my producer already thinks I've lost my mind: you choose what the world hears. Not me. Not the lawyers. Not the algorithm. You sit with me, you tell me everything or almost everything, and then you cut. Anything you don't want broadcast, it doesn't go out. I'll burn the notes myself if you ask. I'll kill the series if you ask. The confession letter started this, but you get to decide how it ends."

Wren stared at me for a long moment. The tide sighed against the rocks, patient as a metronome.

"You're a journalist," she said finally. "You're telling me you'll hand editorial control to your subject."

"I'm telling you I'll hand it to the person the story actually happened to. Turns out those aren't always the same thing. Who knew."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I go home, I tell Marcus the trail went cold, and I take up gardening. I hear hydrangeas are forgiving."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"Your hydrangeas would die," she said. "You'd talk them to death."

"Probably."

She looked out at the water for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped its armor.

"She raised me under a name that wasn't mine. Marian. Marian Cole. She worked double shifts at a diner in Oregon and sewed my Halloween costumes and never once, not once, let slip who we were — until the cancer, until the last month, when she made me promise. *Make them look,* she said. *Not at me. At him.*" Her knuckles whitened around the cup. "I sent the confession letter because I thought making them look meant burning it all down. Then I watched your podcast, and I kept waiting for you to be what I needed you to be — a wrecking ball. And instead you kept... asking better questions than I had answers for."

"That's the nicest insult I've ever received."

"It wasn't an insult."

"No," I said softly. "I know."

She drained the tea like a shot of whiskey and set the cup down on the rock with a click of finality.

"All right," she said. "Tonight. But not here — there's a cabin up the coast, a friend's, off the books. And Quintana?" She stood, brushing grit from her jeans, and for a second I saw the child from the locket photo, the girl erased so thoroughly that even the estate records had filed her death two years late. "If you betray this, I won't run. I've done enough running for three lifetimes. I'll fight. Understand?"

"Understood," I said. "Fair warning: I fight dirty. I once ended a man's career using nothing but his own voicemail greetings and a timeline."

For the first time, she laughed. It was a rusty sound, a door opening after thirty years, and it did something to my chest I wasn't prepared for.

* * *

### Wren

We recorded through the night in a cabin that smelled of cedar smoke and salt, and I learned that truth-telling sounds less like a confession and more like a conversation you're finally allowed to have.

Sebastian set up her recorder on the table between two mugs and a plate of biscuits she'd brought "for morale." Before she pressed record, she showed me the equipment — the voice modulation software, the way she'd scrub any identifying detail, the map with our location marked only as *the northern coast*. She let me test the distortion myself, made me say my own name until it came out as someone else's, until it sounded safe.

"Housekeeping first," she said, pen poised. "Rules of engagement. One: you can pause anytime. Two: you can strike anything, anytime, even after we're done — I keep the raw files encrypted and I'll hand you the key. Three: if at any point you look at me like you're about to bolt, I stop asking questions and make tea. Those are my terms."

"What do you get out of it?"

"A story," she said simply. "The last good one. And maybe — I don't know — a chance to remember why I started doing this. It wasn't for the downloads. It was because I believed nobody's story should end in a drawer." She clicked the record button. "Ready?"

I wasn't. I said yes anyway.

And then I talked. About the house I barely remembered — the father's rages behind closed doors, the mother's elegant arithmetic of denial. About Vivian, seventeen and fierce, teaching me to swim in the cove, telling me our secret like a fairy tale: *you were never born, little wren, and that makes you free.* About the night he came for us, drunk and certain, and Vivian's bicycle going over the cliff path in the dark while I hid in the boathouse, and the world deciding my sister was dead because a dead sister couldn't testify.

About Eleanor, who knew — who had always known — and chose the estate over her granddaughters, choosing silence with the same precision she used to choose china patterns.

Sebastian listened the way I imagine doctors listen to heartbeats. She didn't interrupt. Twice she stopped the recording when I cried, and once when I laughed — really laughed, at the memory of Vivian teaching me to pickpocket bus fare, calling it *survival studies* — and each time she waited without impatience, refilling my mug, letting the moment belong to me before she asked if I wanted to continue.

Around three in the morning, she asked the question I'd been dreading.

"Why me? Why send the confession to a podcaster instead of the police?"

I turned my mug slowly on the table. "Because the police had the file for thirty years and it grew dust and a detective's resignation letter. Because the family bought silence wholesale. Because I needed someone whose entire profession was *making people listen*." I looked up. "And because I'd listened to you. Every episode. You close cases, but you always — you always say the names. The victims' names. Like they were people, not puzzles. I needed someone who would say hers."

Sebastian was quiet for a moment. Outside, wind worried the eaves.

"My mother disappeared when I was nine," she said, and I heard the surprise in her own voice, as if the fact had slipped its leash. "Never found. Never even investigated properly — small town, no money, a woman with a history they didn't like. I became who I am because a drawer ate her." She gave a short laugh. "That's why the handwriting on the confession felt familiar. It wasn't yours I recognized, Wren. It was mine. We write our dead the same way."

* * *

### Sebastian

Dawn came up gray-gold over the water, and we were still at the table, hollowed out and strangely light, two women who had spent the night handing each other the heavy things.

Wren reviewed every segment twice. She struck a detail about the town where Vivian had hidden them; she struck the name of the friend who owned the cabin; she kept, after long deliberation, the account of Eleanor's falsified death certificate, word for word. When she finished, she slid the notes back across the table.

"There," she said. "That's what the world hears."

"That's what the world hears," I agreed, and meant it, and discovered that meaning it cost me less than I'd feared. Marcus was going to have opinions. Let him. Some stories you publish. Some stories you carry.

She walked me out to the car through the cold morning. At the driver's side door she hesitated.

"Quintana. When it airs — people are going to be furious. Him, especially. He still has reach; you know that better than anyone now." Her eyes were steady. "Are you ready for that?"

I thought about Tom Reilly in his trailer, dialing my number with dying lungs. About Julian in his hospital bed. About the boathouse, burned. About my mother's name in a drawer.

"No," I said honestly. "But Vivian wasn't ready either, and she did it anyway. Seems to be a family tradition. Consider me adopted."

Wren smiled — fully, this time, sunrise over water — and pressed something into my hand. A folded page, photocopy paper, creased soft from handling.

"Her letter," she said. "'I'm taking Wren somewhere safe. Don't look.' I think..." She swallowed. "I think it's time someone looked. Broadcast it. Let them see her handwriting. Let them know she chose it — all of it, every hard year. She wasn't a victim who vanished. She was a hero who left."

I drove south with the letter on the seat beside me and called Marcus from the first gas station with a signal.

"Tell the team we go live Friday," I said. "Full series finale. And Marcus — triple the security on the servers. Because when this airs, a very powerful man is going to learn that the daughter he buried is alive, and the sister he mourned chose her over him." I looked at the letter, at Vivian Ashcombe's brave, furious handwriting, thirty years patient. "And he's going to want to stop us. He's running out of tide."
